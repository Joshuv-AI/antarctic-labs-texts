CBL with integrated SDH versus CBL alone in esophageal surgery internship: a randomized controlled trial on clinical competence development
Authors: Zhi Wen, Peirui Chen, Jing Lyu, Jianqiang Wu, Biyuan Qin, Yong Yang, Guofei Huang, Changxue Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEsophageal cancer exhibits significant geographical and economic disparities, with social determinants of health (SDH) playing crucial roles in its occurrence and prognosis. While case-based learning (CBL) is mainstream in clinical internships, traditional CBL predominantly focuses on disease biology, often neglecting sociocultural contexts. At the study institution, medical students receive limited formal SDH education within the preclinical curriculum, and clinical rotations lack systematic SDH training components. Integrating SDH into medical curricula has become an international education reform priority; however, empirical research systematically incorporating SDH into clinical internship settings within China remains limited. This study explored the effectiveness of integrating SDH into CBL on clinical competence development among surgical interns.MethodsA randomized controlled trial with individual-level randomization was employed. A total of 124 medical students completing their surgical internship at a single hospital from March 2024 to January 2026 were randomly assigned to a control group (CBL only, n = 60) or an experimental group (CBL + SDH, n = 64). Teaching was conducted using a standardized "esophageal cancer patient" case. Bias was controlled through blinding of students and outcome assessors, combined with randomization of teaching procedures, confidentiality agreements, and standardized teaching packages. Teaching effectiveness was assessed using objective structured clinical examinations (OSCE), theoretical knowledge tests, case analysis reports, and questionnaires.ResultsThe experimental group scored significantly higher than the control group on objective assessments of history taking, medical record writing, identification and analysis of social factors, and comprehensive treatment planning and humanistic care (P < 0.001), with very large effect sizes. No significant differences were found in theoretical knowledge, physical examination, or diagnosis and medical analysis (P > 0.05). In self-assessments, the experimental group scored significantly higher on six core competency dimensions (P < 0.01), with moderate to large effect sizes. Satisfaction evaluations showed the experimental group rated content and context, thinking training, and competence relevance significantly higher (P < 0.01).ConclusionsIntegrating SDH into CBL significantly enhances surgical interns’ competence to address social health issues without compromising core medical knowledge, offering an effective strategy for biopsychosocial medical education.The observed benefits should be interpreted as the overall effect of the enhanced educational package (SDH-integrated CBL with SP encounters, reflective writing, feedback, and mini-OSCE practice), rather than as the effect of SDH content alone.

Background

Esophageal cancer is a highly prevalent malignant tumor of the digestive system in China, with its incidence and mortality ranking fifth and fourth globally, respectively. It exhibits significant geographical clustering, economic disparities, and dietary cultural dependence [1]. Epidemiological studies have demonstrated that social determinants of health (SDH)—such as pickled food consumption, insufficient intake of fresh fruits and vegetables, poor nutritional status, and occupational exposures such as dust and chemical contact—are closely associated with the occurrence, treatment response, and prognosis of esophageal cancer. Furthermore, the lengthy treatment course, high costs, and strong dependence on family support for postoperative recovery make this disease an ideal vehicle for implementing the biopsychosocial model of medicine in medical education.

Case-based learning (CBL) is currently a mainstream teaching method in clinical internships. Using real or simulated cases as a vehicle, CBL guides students in discussions centering on diagnosis, differential diagnosis, and treatment decisions, effectively training students in evidence-based guideline-driven clinical reasoning [2]. However, traditional CBL predominantly focuses on the biomedical aspects of disease, with insufficient exploration of the sociocultural contexts behind cases, patients’ healthcare-seeking dilemmas, and health equity issues. A prevalent "emphasis on biology, neglect of society" teaching inertia exists. This inertia makes it difficult for medical students to develop a comprehensive clinical reasoning perspective, let alone actively identify and intervene in social factors affecting health outcomes in their future practice.

In recent years, integrating social determinants of health into medical curricula has become an important direction in international medical education reform. Siegel and colleagues argue that SDH are primary drivers of health status and outcome disparities within communities and populations [3]. They call for the ability to identify and mitigate SDH to become an essential component of graduate medical education across all specialties. Numerous international studies have shown that structured SDH teaching modules—such as social history-taking training, community resource navigation exercises, and interdisciplinary case discussions—can significantly enhance medical students’ awareness of health inequalities, their ability to analyze social factors, and their cross-cultural communication effectiveness [4–6]. For example, Roth introduced a case-based SDH flipped classroom in a pediatric clerkship, after which students’ ability to identify and address issues like poverty and food insecurity was significantly enhanced [7]. Similarly, Howell implemented interactive SDH workshops for third-year medical students across multiple clinical rotations, leading to significant improvements in students’ self-assessed SDH cognition and clinical skills [8]. However, research in this area within China is still in its early stages, consisting mostly of theoretical discussions or isolated attempts. There is a lack of empirical research that systematically integrates SDH into authentic clinical internship settings and evaluates its effectiveness using objective structured assessments.

At the study institution, medical students receive limited formal SDH education within the preclinical curriculum, consisting primarily of one or two lecture sessions on health equity concepts without structured clinical application. Clinical rotations lack systematic SDH training components, and students are not routinely assessed on SDH-related competencies. This educational gap provided the rationale for the present intervention, which was designed to complement existing training by embedding SDH instruction within authentic clinical case discussions and providing structured opportunities for practice and feedback.

The key challenge currently hindering the deep integration of medical humanities and professional education is how to organically incorporate SDH into the CBL teaching process while ensuring the efficiency of core medical knowledge transmission, and how to objectively evaluate its impact on enhancing students’ comprehensive clinical competence. To address this, the present study was designed as a randomized controlled teaching trial using a standardized esophageal cancer case. We explicitly embedded three core teaching components—social history-taking, social factor analysis, and individualized support plan development—within the CBL framework, aiming to explore a generalizable and evaluable teaching paradigm for SDH integration. Based on the PRAPARE assessment tool [9, 10] and incorporating the sociodemographic characteristics of esophageal cancer patients in our local region, we incorporated several social determinants as key elements for the standardized patients. A triple-blind design was employed to control bias, with the goal of effectively enhancing students’ clinical competence to identify, analyze, and address social health issues without compromising their mastery of foundational medical knowledge, thereby providing empirical evidence for implementing the biopsychosocial model of medicine in Chinese clinical medical education.

Materials and methods

Research design and participants

This study was a randomized controlled trial. Sample size was calculated using G*Power software. Based on a pilot study, assuming a moderate effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.5) for the primary outcome (OSCE score), with a two-sided significance level of 0.05 and power of 0.80, the required sample size was calculated to be 64 per group. Considering a 10% attrition rate, a total of 140 participants were initially planned. Finally, based on the actual number of eligible students during the study period, 124 participants were included. All participants were fifth-year undergraduate students of clinical medicine who completed their internship at this hospital between March 2024 and January 2026. Inclusion criteria were: completion of core coursework in internal medicine and surgery, and voluntary participation with signed informed consent. Exclusion criteria were: prior rotation in esophageal cancer-related departments, and previous participation in similar educational research. Students were individually randomized using a random number table to a control group (n = 60) receiving case-based learning (CBL) only or an experimental group (n = 64) receiving CBL with integrated social determinants of health (SDH). Allocation concealment was maintained by having a research assistant not involved in enrollment or teaching manage the random number sequence and assign students accordingly.

Description of course design elements for the two groups

Without altering the core case content, total instructional hours, or assessment standards, the teaching process for the CBL + SDH group was systematically redesigned to explicitly incorporate SDH learning principles, while the CBL only group maintained the traditional CBL teaching model focusing on the biomedical aspects of disease. The key elements of the course design for the two groups are compared as follows:

Control group (CBL only)

Students in the control group received only a biomedical information summary before class, including the patient’s chief complaint, history of present illness, physical examination findings, andauxiliary examination results. Classroom discussions centered on disease diagnosis, differential diagnosis, staging, and treatment guidelines, with instructors guiding students to make clinical decisions based on evidence-based medicine. Discussions involving patients’ social background information were entirely excluded. Instructor-student interactions occurred primarily during classroom discussions, where instructors clarified knowledge points, guided technical discussion directions, and summarized diagnosis and treatment standards. Students were required to complete one group discussion and one post-class assignment—writing a multidisciplinary team discussion record—with practice content focusing on the technical aspects of diagnosis and treatment decisions. During the teaching summary, instructors reviewed core knowledge points and emphasized the importance of standardized clinical reasoning.

Experimental group (CBL + SDH)

Students in the experimental group received case materials before class that included comprehensive patient social background information, covering elements such as occupation, economic status, family structure, dietary culture, and health beliefs. Classroom teaching employed a staged information disclosure approach, sequentially presenting clinical symptoms, treatment dilemmas, and community background. Standardized patients, based on predetermined sociodemographic characteristics, authentically reproduced emotional responses and healthcare-seeking dilemmas arising from factors such as financial pressure, insufficient family support, or information asymmetry, requiring students to actively interact with standardized patients to obtain social histories. After teaching, students were required to submit case analysis reports systematically identifying and analyzing social factors influencing the occurrence, development, and prognosis of the case. Standardized patients and instructors used a specially designed social factors assessment checklist to provide immediate feedback on students’ ability to collect social histories and analyze problems. Instructor-student interaction was enhanced through multiple approaches: instructors specifically reserved time during discussions to guide students in considering and discussing the specific impacts of social factors on treatment decisions, including how economic burden influences treatment option selection and how family support affects postoperative recovery; instructors provided class-wide summaries and feedback on common issues students encountered in social factor analysis, and students were encouraged to engage in in-depth discussions with instructors on social case issues through online platforms after class. In addition to completing group discussions, students were required to participate in one mini-OSCE practice focusing on social history collection, using standardized patients and case backgrounds different from the formal assessment, allowing students to repeatedly practice how to inquire about and respond to patients’ non-medical needs in a respectful and effective manner in a low-stakes environment. Following case discussions, instructors guided students in reflective writing, requiring students to write reflection journals focusing on the challenges of social determinants of health in primary care as illustrated by specific cases, reviewing their thought processes when encountering patients, and reflecting on whether certain social factors were overlooked and how these factors influenced their overall understanding of the disease and development of management plans, thereby helping students establish metacognitive awareness of incorporating social context into clinical reasoning and cultivating self-regulated learning abilities.

Outcome measures and assessment tools

Objective assessments

Objective assessments consisted of three components. First, a theoretical knowledge test using a closed-book written examination assessed students’ mastery of foundational knowledge related to esophageal cancer diagnosis and treatment. Second, an OSCE included a dedicated "esophageal cancer patient consultation" station, utilizing standardized patients who were uniformly trained to accurately simulate clinical symptoms and associated psychosocial responses. This assessment focused on evaluating students’ ability to actively inquire about and comprehensively obtain social history information, including dietary habits, occupational environment, and financial burden; the depth of documentation and analysis of social history and related social determinants in medical record writing; and the systematic nature of physical examination and clinical reasoning. Third, a case analysis report required students to provide a written analysis of a new esophageal cancer case containing multiple social determinants. Assessment dimensions encompassed the accuracy of diagnosis and medical analysis, systematic identification and analysis of social factors and their impacts, and the extent to which treatment plans adhered to evidence-based medicine principles while incorporating consideration of patients’ social contexts and demonstrating individualized humanistic care.The OSCE assessment domains were developed through a multi-step process: (1) a literature review of existing SDH assessment frameworks; (2) expert panel discussions involving three senior clinicians and two medical education specialists; and (3) iterative refinement based on pilot testing with five residents who were not part of the study sample. Prior to formal implementation, the scoring rubrics underwent formal pilot testing with the same five residents, and refinements were made to improve clarity and feasibility. All OSCE assessments were performed independently by two trained assessors who were blinded to group allocation, and inter-rater reliability was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for each domain.

Anonymous subjective questionnaires

Course core competency self-assessment questionnaire: A 6-point Likert scale was used, covering eight domains: interest and motivation, knowledge integration ability, individualized treatment planning, ethical and social thinking, communication adaptability, systematic problem-solving, reflection and learning, and case report writing, with an additional attention check item. This questionnaire aimed to assess the impact of the course on students’ cognition and behaviors regarding the integration of biomedical and social factors. Scoring was based on actual item scores, with scores for reverse-coded items transformed accordingly.

Teaching content and format satisfaction questionnaire: A 6-point Likert scale was used to assess student satisfaction across six domains: content and context, teaching format, knowledge construction, thinking training, competence relevance, and overall effectiveness, with an additional reverse-scored item and attention check item.

Study procedures

This study employed a randomized controlled design with individual-level randomization. Students were blinded to group allocation and the teaching method used in the other group, and were informed only that they were participating in a study on improving case-based teaching for esophageal cancer. Before each session, the instructor determined the teaching procedure—case-based learning (CBL) alone or CBL with SDH integration—by drawing lots, after which a research assistant not involved in teaching provided the corresponding standardized manual and case materials. Instructors followed the manual strictly and were not informed whether the assigned procedure belonged to the control or experimental group. A third-party assessor blinded to group allocation evaluated teaching effectiveness.

Quality control for enrollment and teaching implementation

Throughout the study period, eligible students were individually randomized using the random number table as described in Section " Description of course design elements for the two groups". Based on students’ internship schedules, several concentrated teaching sessions were organized, with the study designed as an independent two-day intensive workshop. All enrolled students participated concurrently, and primary outcome assessments were conducted immediately following the teaching activities. This approach shortened the time window between intervention and assessment, minimizing opportunities for communication among students. Confidentiality clauses were explicitly included in the informed consent forms, requiring participants to pledge not to discuss specific course content, procedures, or material details with anyone, including other participants and non-participants, until the study was fully completed with all data collected. It was emphasized that this was part of research ethics and crucial to the validity of the study findings. Teaching was delivered by the same team of uniformly trained instructors, all of whom were familiar with both teaching processes. Before each session, instructors determined the teaching method to be used by drawing lots and strictly adhered to the corresponding standardized teaching packages, which included lesson plans, presentation slides, and guided question lists. Instructors and teaching assistants reminded students of their confidentiality obligations before and after class.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0, with two‑tailed tests and P < 0.05 considered significant. For objective assessments (theoretical knowledge, OSCE dimensions, and case analysis reports), normality was confirmed; data were expressed as mean ± SD, and between‑group comparisons used independent t‑tests, with Cohen’s d effect sizes and 95% CIs. For subjective questionnaires (non‑normal distribution), data were median (IQR), compared by Mann‑Whitney U tests, with r effect sizes. Bonferroni correction was applied for multiple comparisons (α = 0.00625 for 8 competency dimensions; α = 0.0083 for 6 satisfaction dimensions). Internal consistency was assessed by Cronbach’s α (α > 0.7 acceptable). Inter‑rater reliability for OSCE scores used a two‑way random‑effects, absolute‑agreement intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), with ICC > 0.75 indicating good reliability. Invalid questionnaires (incorrect attention checks) were excluded prior to analysis.

Discussion

This study employed a rigorous randomized controlled design and confirmed that systematically integrating SDH into CBL teaching significantly enhances medical students’ comprehensive clinical competence to identify, analyze, and address social health issues without compromising the mastery of core medical knowledge. The intervention complements existing training by addressing a critical gap in the current curriculum—the absence of structured SDH education in clinical settings. While students may have been exposed to SDH concepts in preclinical lectures, the present intervention provides the first opportunity for systematic application of these concepts to clinical cases with active practice and immediate feedback. Regarding internal validity control, random number table allocation was used, and baseline characteristics were comparable between the two groups, reducing selection bias. A triple-blind design—with students unaware of group allocation, instructors randomly determining teaching procedures before each session without knowledge of group labels, and third-party assessors blinded to group assignment—minimized implementation and measurement bias. Additionally, measures such as confidentiality agreements, intensive short-term workshops, uniformly trained standardized patients, and standardized teaching packages effectively controlled for contamination between groups and ensured intervention fidelity. These design details enhance the credibility of the study findings. In terms of instructional design, the study did not simply append SDH content to CBL; rather, integrating the core dimensions of the PRAPARE assessment tool, the identification of social factors was embedded into key components including staged case disclosure, standardized patient interactions, and reflective writing. This design provided students with opportunities to repeatedly practice inquiring about and responding to patients’ non-medical needs in a respectful and effective manner within a low-stakes environment, while establishing cognitive awareness of incorporating social context into clinical reasoning through reflective journals. One study using bibliometric analysis confirmed that integrating SDH into medical education contributes to improving clinical care quality, enabling physicians to make more precise diagnostic and treatment decisions by considering social and economic risk factors [5]. Turgeon and colleagues argue that systematically incorporating SDH into clinical rotations not only helps develop medical students’ ability to identify and address social risk factors but also represents an effective pathway for advancing medical education from conceptual recognition to practical implementation, thereby concretely promoting health equity [11].

A literature survey indicates that medical students’ awareness of social determinants of health (SDH) is generally low, with an average accuracy rate of only 33.3%, and this weak cognitive foundation severely constrains their ability to translate SDH concepts into clinical practice [12]. This situation directly underscores the necessity of the intervention in the present study: students without systematic training often struggle to actively identify social factors when facing real patients, let alone systematically collect and document them in medical records. The highly significant improvement achieved by the experimental group in the "identification and analysis of social factors" dimension (d = 1.39) effectively fills this cognitive gap. Through explicit SDH training integrated with CBL, students not only remediated knowledge deficiencies but also repeatedly practiced how to uncover social background information in authentic case contexts, thereby transforming fragmented awareness into systematic clinical thinking. Furthermore, the significant advantages in the "history taking" and "medical record writing" dimensions (d = 0.54–0.60) further demonstrate that this training successfully translated cognition into behavior: students learned how to actively inquire about social factors during interviews and document them systematically in medical records—a concrete manifestation of the calls in the literature to "integrate SDH into curricula."A SDH education program at a medical college in the United Arab Emirates adopted an experiential learning model combining theoretical study, reflective practice training, and community home visits to provide explicit SDH training for medical students. Study data showed that after completing the course, students demonstrated significant improvements in SDH knowledge, skills, and attitudes, with their reflective narratives also revealing active attention to and systematic consideration of patients’ social background information [13].

Notably, no significant differences were found between the two groups in foundational medical dimensions such as theoretical knowledge, physical examination, and diagnosis and medical analysis (P > 0.05), with effect sizes all below 0.2. This finding corroborates the results of Marrone et al. in emergency medicine teaching: embedding SDH topics within limited instructional time did not produce detectable negative effects on students’ acquisition of intended core clinical knowledge [14]. Both studies collectively demonstrate that systematically integrating SDH teaching within the CBL framework does not come at the expense of reducing or weakening biomedical knowledge transmission; rather, it achieves effective value-added enhancement of social health competencies while maintaining the quality of traditional clinical competence cultivation. This conclusion has important implications for alleviating concerns among some educators that SDH-integrated curricula might "crowd out" core knowledge teaching time, and provides empirical support for the subsequent dissemination of SDH-integrated curricula in medical education.

Regarding self-assessed core competencies, students in the CBL + SDH group scored significantly higher than those in the control group on six dimensions: "knowledge integration ability," "ethical and social thinking," "systematic problem-solving," "individualized treatment planning," "reflection and learning," and "communication adaptability," with effect sizes ranging from 0.48 to 0.67, indicating moderate to large effects. These dimensions directly correspond to the core objectives of SDH education: cultivating medical students’ comprehensive competence to integrate social contextual information into clinical reasoning, balance equity and feasibility in decision-making, and communicate effectively with patients from diverse backgrounds. The "social accountability experiential learning logs" developed by the University of Ottawa’s Faculty of Medicine similarly confirmed the effectiveness of structured reflection tools in SDH teaching—after purposefully documenting and reflecting on social determinants of health during clinical rotations, students’ clinical confidence in addressing social health challenges significantly increased [15]. Kucherepa et al. conducted SAPLCC questionnaire assessments among first-year pharmacy students and demonstrated that following an SDH-integrated curriculum, students’ scores significantly improved across all six domains: knowledge, skills, attitudes, experiences, competence, and awareness, with total scores increasing from 190 ± 29 to 253 ± 33 [16]. This multidimensional, comprehensive improvement precisely mirrors the significant advantages demonstrated by the experimental group across the six dimensions in the present study. Satisfaction evaluations in this study revealed that students in the CBL + SDH group rated the dimensions of "content and context," "thinking training," and "competence relevance" significantly higher than those in the CBL only group, while scoring significantly lower on the reverse-coded item "perceived artificial integration." These results provide strong evidence that students clearly perceived the integration of SDH content with clinical cases as "organic integration" rather than "forced addition." This finding is highly consistent with qualitative evaluations of SDH courses in international medical schools, where students commonly report that cases incorporating social dimensions are more clinically relevant and stimulate deeper clinical reasoning [17]. As noted in medical humanities education research, the key to case-based teaching lies in organically integrating medical knowledge with humanities knowledge, overcoming the drawbacks of mere theoretical indoctrination [18]. This design enhanced the authenticity and intellectual challenge of the cases, while the high scores on the "competence relevance" dimension indicated that students clearly recognized the pathway for transferring learned knowledge to clinical practice. Notably, no significant differences were found between the two groups on general teaching dimensions such as "teaching format" and "knowledge construction," demonstrating that SDH integration did not compromise the foundational teaching experience, achieving the ideal outcome of "enhanced effectiveness without increased burden."

Generalizability considerations warrant careful discussion. This study employed a single esophageal cancer case incorporating locally relevant SDH factors (e.g., pickled food consumption, occupational dust exposure) specific to the Sichuan region. While esophageal cancer is particularly suited for SDH integration due to its strong socioeconomic and dietary determinants, the extent to which these findings generalize to other surgical diseases varies. The approach is likely transferable to diseases with well-established social determinants—such as gastric cancer (dietary factors, H. pylori), lung cancer (smoking, occupational exposures), and colorectal cancer (screening access, dietary patterns)—but may require adaptation for conditions where social determinants are less prominent. For other disciplines, the pedagogical framework (SDH-embedded CBL with SPs and reflective writing) is adaptable to internal medicine, pediatrics, obstetrics/gynecology, and family medicine rotations, where social factors often play critical roles in disease presentation and management. However, the specific SDH content would need to be tailored to each specialty’s relevant social determinants. For implementation in other settings, key adaptations would include: (1) contextualizing SDH factors to reflect local epidemiological and sociodemographic realities; (2) training standardized patients to portray locally relevant social scenarios; and (3) adjusting case complexity to align with learners’ prior SDH knowledge. The single-center, single-case design limits direct generalizability; future multi-center studies across diverse clinical conditions and geographic settings are needed to establish the broader applicability of this approach.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample size (n = 124) fell short of the planned 140, potentially reducing power to detect small effects; although non-significant outcomes had effect sizes below 0.2, suggesting negligible rather than Type II errors, small meaningful effects in other domains may still have been missed, warranting larger multi-center studies. Second, only immediate post-intervention outcomes were measured without delayed follow-up, leaving retention and transfer to clinical practice unknown—a gap that future longitudinal assessments (e.g., 6 or 12 months) with both objective and self-reported measures should address. Third, the experimental group received multiple enhancements beyond SDH content (SP encounters, reflective writing, feedback, mini-OSCE, and more instructor interaction), so the observed benefits reflect the aggregate effect of an enriched educational package rather than SDH alone; factorial designs are needed to isolate its specific contribution, and until then, conclusions should be interpreted cautiously. Fourth, blinding success was not formally assessed, and instructors could plausibly infer group assignment from material differences—an inherent limitation. Fifth, although independent double scoring produced good-to-excellent ICC values (0.82–0.94), these were based on a small pilot sample, so their generalizability to full assessments requires further confirmation. Sixth, despite individual randomization, teaching was delivered to intact class cohorts due to fixed scheduling, creating a natural clustering effect that may reduce observation independence and increase contamination risk; future studies could employ cluster-randomized designs or, if retaining individual randomization, separate venues and schedules to mitigate this.

Conclusions

Using esophageal cancer internship as an example, this study demonstrates that an integrated teaching model combining SDH content with active learning strategies (SP encounters, reflective writing, and structured feedback) within the CBL framework significantly enhances medical students’ social health competencies without compromising mastery of core knowledge. The key to its success lies in embedding SDH into core teaching components and leveraging standardized patients and reflective writing to strengthen contextual immersion and metacognition. This model provides a generalizable teaching paradigm for implementing the biopsychosocial model of medicine.
