Intramedullary osteosclerosis of the tibia with inflammatory features: Multimodality imaging findings
Authors: Juan Felipe Betancur, Melina González Diaz-Granados, Sergio Andrés Arroyave, Luz Eugenia Pérez, Beatriz Ramírez

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Intramedullary osteosclerosis (Ribbing disease), also known as sclerosing dysplasia of the diaphysis of the tibia, is a rare and benign bone disorder characterized by cortical bone thickening of the tibia. The disease was named after the Swedish radiologist Sten Ribbing, who first described it in 1949 [1]. The exact cause of Ribbing disease is unknown; however, it is believed to be related to disturbances in the normal bone remodeling process. This condition is usually asymptomatic and is often discovered incidentally on imaging studies. However, in some cases, it may cause pain and other symptoms. Although typically considered a non-inflammatory sclerosing bone disorder, the clinical and imaging spectrum of Ribbing disease may be broader than traditionally described. This case is notable for bilateral involvement with associated bone marrow edema, periosteal reaction, and contrast enhancement, highlighting potential diagnostic challenges and the need to differentiate this entity from infectious and neoplastic conditions.

Case presentation

A 30-year-old woman with no significant medical history presented with a one-year history of progressive pain in the anterior aspect of the right tibia. The pain was intermittent, paroxysmal, and burning in character, with a visual analog scale (VAS) score of 10/10. Over time, the symptoms progressed to the contralateral tibia. Due to persistent pain, the patient reported prolonged self-medication with diclofenac plus tramadol (25/25 mg daily) for more than one year.

Physical examination revealed localized tenderness and pain elicited by palpation over the anterior aspect of both tibiae, which was more pronounced on the right side. No deformities, swelling, skin changes, gait abnormalities, or neurological deficits were observed.

Laboratory evaluations, including inflammatory markers, metabolic bone profiles, and renal and hepatic function tests, were unremarkable. Serum calcium, phosphorus, alkaline phosphatase, parathyroid hormone, and vitamin D levels were within normal limits and showed no clinically significant abnormalities. Urinalysis and renal ultrasonography revealed no pathological findings.

Computed tomography (CT) of the lower extremities (Fig. 1) revealed endosteal sclerosis and cortical thickening involving the diaphyses of both tibiae, with predominant right-sided involvement, resulting in approximately 60% and 30% obliteration of the medullary canal on the right and left sides, respectively.

CT of both legs, bone window: axial (A) and coronal (B). Bilateral, asymmetric, fusiform, diaphyseal cortical thickening (right > left) with endosteal sclerosis and near-obliteration of the tibial medullary canal, with milder fibular involvement. The metaphyses and epiphyses were spared. No cortical break, aggressive periosteal reaction, or soft tissue collection was observed. The findings are consistent with Ribbing disease (multiple diaphyseal sclerosis).

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the lower limbs (Fig. 2) demonstrated marked cortical thickening of the mid-diaphysis of both tibiae, associated with bone marrow edema and circumferential periosteal reaction. Post-contrast sequences showed enhancement of the medullary cavity and periosteum. No cortical disruption, soft tissue mass, or pathological fracture was identified.

Bone scintigraphy (Fig. 3) showed increased radiotracer uptake in the mid-diaphysis of both tibiae across all three phases, indicating an increased metabolic activity. SPECT-CT confirmed diffuse cortical hyperuptake with focal medullary involvement, corresponding to the areas of cortical hyperostosis identified on CT.

Bone scintigraphy and SPECT/CT (A) Planar 99mTc-MDP images (whole-body anterior/posterior and leg spot views) show intense, linear bilateral uptake along the tibial diaphyses (right-sided predominance), with metaphyseal/epiphyseal sparing; the remainder of the skeleton demonstrates physiologic activity without additional suspicious foci. (B) Leg SPECT/CT (axial, sagittal, coronal; tracer, CT, and fused images): diaphyseal hyper-uptake co-localizes with cortical thickening and endosteal sclerosis, causing medullary canal narrowing with milder fibular involvement. No soft tissue mass or aggressive periosteal reaction was observed. These findings are consistent with those of Ribbing disease.

Based on the characteristic clinical presentation and imaging findings on CT, MRI, and bone scintigraphy, a diagnosis of intramedullary osteosclerosis (Ribbing disease) was made.

Initial medical management included a combination of acetaminophen and caffeine (500/65 mg), pregabalin 75 mg administered every 8 hours, prednisone 60 mg once daily with a gradual tapering schedule, and tapentadol 50 mg every 12 hours. This regimen resulted in partial pain relief, as indicated by a reduction in the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) score from 10/10 to 5/10. After approximately 12 months of supervised conservative management, the patient underwent surgical intervention due to persistent pain and functional limitations. Given the ongoing symptoms and significant obliteration of the medullary canal, surgical cortical fenestration and decortication of the right tibial diaphysis were performed (Fig. 4). The patient is currently undergoing rehabilitation, and surgical management of the contralateral tibia is under consideration.

Lateral radiograph of the right leg. Two small rectangular metallic staples were observed along the anteromedial aspect of the mid-diaphysis, consistent with surgical cortical fenestration/decortication for symptomatic decompression.

Discussion

Intramedullary osteosclerosis (Ribbing disease) is a rare benign bone disorder characterized by progressive diaphyseal cortical thickening and endosteal sclerosis, most commonly involving the tibia. Although its exact etiology remains uncertain, alterations in the TGFB1 signaling pathway have been proposed, and the condition is considered part of the spectrum of sclerosing bone dysplasias [[2], [3], [4]].

Ribbing disease has been described as a possible variant of Camurati-Engelmann disease; however, important clinical and imaging differences allow differentiation between these entities [2,3,[5], [6], [7]]. Ribbing disease typically presents with localized and asynchronous bilateral diaphyseal involvement, sparing the metaphyses and epiphyses, whereas Camurati-Engelmann disease usually shows symmetric involvement, metaphyseal extension, and frequent axial or craniofacial manifestations [7,8].

Patients typically present with progressive, localized bone pain, most commonly affecting the tibia, which may initially be unilateral and later involve the contralateral side of the body. The interval between symptom onset in one limb and subsequent contralateral involvement has been reported to range from 9 to 96 months, with a mean duration of approximately 36 months [9]. Pain is thought to result from increased intramedullary pressure due to endosteal and cortical bone overgrowth or from vascular and Haversian system entrapment within the newly formed lamellar bone [3].

The diagnosis of intramedullary osteosclerosis is primarily one of exclusion, based on characteristic imaging findings combined with the absence of laboratory or clinical evidence of infection, malignancy, or systemic bone disease. Key imaging features include diaphyseal cortical thickening, endosteal sclerosis, medullary canal narrowing, and metaphyseal and epiphyseal sparing. The absence of aggressive periosteal reaction, cortical destruction, or soft tissue mass supports a benign process, even in the presence of non-aggressive periosteal reaction as observed in our case [10,11]. Computed tomography allows for the accurate assessment of cortical hyperostosis and endosteal sclerosis while confirming sparing of the metaphyses and epiphyses. Magnetic resonance imaging usually shows low signal intensity on T1-weighted images corresponding to sclerotic bone, with variable high signal intensity on fluid-sensitive sequences related to bone marrow edema [10,12]. In our case, the presence of bone marrow edema, circumferential periosteal reaction, and contrast enhancement reflects an active and symptomatic phase of the disease, expanding the traditionally described non-inflammatory imaging profile of Ribbing disease.

Bone scintigraphy is useful for evaluating disease extent and activity and may assist in treatment planning. In conjunction with the clinical presentation, these imaging features allow the exclusion of infectious, inflammatory, and neoplastic processes [13].

The differential diagnosis includes chronic osteomyelitis, stress injury, osteoid osteoma, adamantinoma, osteosarcoma, and other sclerosing bone dysplasias.

Chronic osteomyelitis was considered given the presence of bone marrow edema and periosteal reaction; however, it was excluded due to the absence of systemic symptoms, normal inflammatory markers, lack of cortical destruction, and absence of soft tissue collections. Stress fractures usually present with a fracture line and periosteal reaction. Osteoid osteoma is characterized by a nidus and surrounding reactive sclerosis, typically associated with nocturnal pain relieved by NSAIDs. Adamantinoma and osteosarcoma often demonstrate cortical destruction, soft tissue mass, and aggressive periosteal reaction. The absence of these features, along with the characteristic imaging pattern, supports the diagnosis of intramedullary osteosclerosis [3,6,9,10]. Less common conditions, such as van Buchem disease, Worth disease, Nakamura disease, craniodiaphyseal dysplasia, Ghosal hematodiaphyseal dysplasia, and other overlap syndromes, should also be considered in selected cases [14]. Recognition of the characteristic imaging pattern and distribution is essential to avoid unnecessary, invasive procedures.

The management of intramedullary osteosclerosis remains challenging. Analgesics and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs are commonly used for symptom control, particularly in the early stages or in combination with surgical interventions [7,12]. However, bisphosphonate therapy has shown inconsistent results. Although de Rubin et al. [15] reported symptomatic improvement and decreased radionuclide uptake following pamidronate treatment; however, subsequent studies failed to confirm sustained clinical or radiographic benefits, possibly due to the predominance of osteoblastic over osteoclastic activity in this condition [8,16].

Corticosteroids may provide symptomatic relief in selected patients, as reported with intravenous methylprednisolone and low-dose oral regimens [4,16].

In advanced or refractory cases with severe medullary canal obliteration and persistent pain, surgical decompression may be considered as a treatment option. Techniques such as corticectomy, intramedullary reaming, and fenestration have been described, although intramedullary reaming can be technically challenging when the canal is obliterated and may carry a risk of cortical perforation [2,3,6,7,9,17]. Fenestration offers an alternative approach to removing sclerotic cortical and medullary bones in such cases [3,7,9].

This case highlights the importance of recognizing the characteristic imaging features of intramedullary osteosclerosis and considering this rare entity in the differential diagnosis of chronic diaphyseal leg pain, particularly to avoid diagnostic delays and prevent misclassification as infectious or neoplastic disease, which may lead to unnecessary invasive interventions.

A limitation of this case is the absence of histopathologic or genetic confirmation, as the diagnosis was based on clinical and imaging findings.
