From pulmonary embolism to small intestinal neuroendocrine tumor: A new diagnostic perspective
Authors: Zhenping Wu, Hongwei Huang, Angcheng Li, Xiaojuan Zheng, Zhuoqun Zheng, Xianhui He, Ting Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPulmonary embolism is a common and potentially life-threatening acute condition encountered in clinical practice. Neuroendocrine tumors are heterogeneous neoplasms characterized by diverse biological behaviors, and patients with poorly differentiated or advanced neuroendocrine tumors may have an increased risk of developing venous thromboembolism. We report the case of a 60-year-old female who experienced acute chest discomfort and was diagnosed with pulmonary embolism. Comprehensive evaluation revealed no evidence of lower limb thrombosis, autoimmune or hematologic disorders, or other identifiable causes. The emboli resolved following anticoagulant therapy. Seventeen months later, the patient developed upper abdominal pain and underwent a colonoscopy, which revealed a lesion approximately 20 cm proximal to the terminal ileum. Histopathological and immunohistochemical analyses confirmed a grade-1 neuroendocrine tumor. 18F-octreotide positron emission tomography–computed tomography demonstrated increased tracer uptake in the terminal ileum and multiple hepatic foci, consistent with an ileal neuroendocrine tumor with liver metastases. The patient received intramuscular octreotide acetate microspheres (20 mg every 4 weeks) and remained clinically stable during follow-up. This case underscores the importance of considering occult malignancy in older patients presenting with unprovoked pulmonary embolism. Small intestinal neuroendocrine tumors may remain clinically silent for extended periods and should be included in the differential diagnosis when common etiologies have been excluded.Keywords: Pulmonary embolism, neuroendocrine tumor, ileum, venous thromboembolism, case report

Introduction

Venous thromboembolism (VTE), encompassing deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE), is a frequent complication in malignancies, particularly those of the gastroenteropancreatic (GEP) system. Neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) show a comparable thrombotic risk, with reported VTE incidences ranging from 7.5% to 33%. 1 The risk appears to be higher in pancreatic or functioning NETs, possibly because of hormonal activity and associated inflammatory responses. Tumor grade and stage further influence thrombotic potential, with poorly differentiated and advanced NETs showing a greater propensity for thromboembolic events compared with well-differentiated or localized tumors. 2

Case presentation

The reporting of this case is in accordance with the Case Report (CARE) guidelines. 3

Initial presentation: PE

A woman in her 60s initially visited Jinhua People's Hospital in July 2023 with the complaint of chest tightness for a day accompanied with mild cough and scant sputum. She denied hemoptysis, fever, palpitations, or chest pain. Her medical, personal, and family histories were unremarkable. Admission vitals indicated tachycardia (107 beats/min (bpm)) and a blood pressure of 114/78 mmHg, with normal oxygen saturation (98%).

Laboratory investigations revealed a significantly elevated D-dimer level (6201.00 ng/mL). Computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA) performed on 14 July 2023 confirmed PE with filling defects in the right upper and lower lobes (Figure 1). Following anticoagulant therapy with low-molecular-weight heparin and subsequently rivaroxaban, the patient showed complete resolution of the emboli on follow-up computed tomography angiography (CTA) after 1 month. She voluntarily discontinued anticoagulation after 3 months.

Disease progression: Discovery of NET

Seventeen months after the initial PE diagnosis, the patient visited Jinhua Central Hospital in December 2024 with a complaint of upper abdominal pain. A gastroscopy was performed under sedation. Concurrently, a screening colonoscopy was conducted at the patient's request, despite the absence of specific lower gastrointestinal symptoms.

Diagnostic findings

During the colonoscopy procedure, the scope is routinely inserted into the terminal ileum for observation. When the scope is 20 cm away from the terminal ileum, a mucosal protrusion lesion can be seen (Figure 2). Histopathological examination of the biopsy specimen revealed round to oval tumor cells with finely stippled chromatin, exhibiting a characteristic “salt-and-pepper” appearance. Occasional mitotic figures were observed. Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated positive staining for chromogranin A (CgA) and synaptophysin (Syn), with a Ki-67 proliferation index of approximately 1%. Based on the above manifestations, according to the World Health Organization (WHO) 2019 diagnostic criteria, it was diagnosed as a Grade 1 NET (Figure 3).

An 18F-octreotide positron emission tomography–computed tomography (PET–CT) scan was subsequently performed, revealing increased tracer uptake in the terminal ileum (Figure 4)and multiple hypermetabolic foci within the liver (Figure 5). This supported the diagnosis of an ileal NET with hepatic metastases.

Therapeutic intervention and rationale

The management strategy for small bowel NETs relies on assessing functional status and metastatic extent. Although functional tumors require somatostatin analogs (SSAs) to manage carcinoid syndrome, localized nonfunctional disease is typically managed surgically. In this case, the presence of multiple hepatic metastases precluded curative resection.

Although the patient presented with a nonfunctional NET lacking symptoms of carcinoid syndrome, SSA therapy was initiated because the disease was unresectable and metastatic. 4 The patient visited the Neuroendocrine Tumor Center of Fudan University Affiliated Cancer Hospital in January 2025. After a multidisciplinary discussion, based on the patient's tumor grade, stage, treatment efficacy, and adverse effects, the final treatment plan consisted of intramuscular octreotide acetate microspheres administered at a dose of 20 mg once every 4 weeks.

Follow-up

At follow-up in January 2025, the patient did not report any discomfort or adverse symptoms. Due to concerns regarding cumulative radiation exposure, a follow-up PET–CT has not yet been performed.

Discussion

The pathogenesis of thrombosis in NETs is multifactorial. NETs may secrete various hormones and cytokines that can disrupt coagulation cascades, promote inflammatory responses, and induce endothelial injury. 5 Furthermore, components of the tumor microenvironment, such as collagen and fibronectin, interact with coagulation factors and platelets, facilitating thrombus formation. An imbalance between proteolytic enzymes and their endogenous inhibitors further contributes to a prothrombotic state.6,7

Frequent local and distant fibrosis in NETs is driven by serotonin and various growth factors that activate fibroblasts and promote extracellular matrix remodeling.8–10 NETs are highly vascularized neoplasms characterized by the overexpression of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), which collectively enhance angiogenesis and activate coagulation pathways. In the present case, endoscopic examination revealed a lesion with prominent dilated surface vessels, reflecting the tumor's significant vascularity consistent with these biological features (Figure 2). Moreover, NET-derived microparticles enriched with tissue factor facilitate platelet aggregation and endothelial activation, further augmenting the risk of thrombosis. 11

The small intestine is the site for approximately 45% of all NETs, with other common sites including the rectum (20%), appendix (16%), colon (11%), pancreas (5%–10%), and stomach (7%). 12 Within the small intestine, the terminal ileum is the most common site of NET occurrence. Hence, when technically feasible, colonoscopic examination should include intubation of the terminal ileum to enable early detection of such lesions. In the present case, the ileal lesion was discovered incidentally during a gastroscopy, and colonoscopy performed for treating upper abdominal pain. Despite the presence of liver metastases, this patient did not exhibit symptoms of carcinoid syndrome, indicating that it was a nonfunctional NET. 13 Moreover, the patient did not experience ileal obstruction or bleeding. Therefore, early diagnosis of ileal NETs was extremely difficult.

Detection of small intestinal NETs relies primarily on advanced imaging modalities, such as octreotide scintigraphy, capsule endoscopy, and double-balloon enteroscopy, along with biochemical markers, including CgA, 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid (5-HIAA), and serotonin.14–16 However, in the absence of specific clinical manifestations and access to high-resolution diagnostic tools, early detection remains challenging, often resulting in diagnostic delays of several months to years. In the present case, PE occurred 17 months before the diagnosis of the tumor, and no conventional thrombotic risk factors were identified. Although a direct causal relationship cannot be established, the temporal association suggests an association between the thromboembolic event and an occult malignancy-associated hypercoagulable state. This case provides a new diagnostic perspective for small intestinal NETs and highlights the need for careful follow-up in older patients with unexplained PE.

Conclusion

The diagnosis of small intestinal NETs is challenging because of their deep anatomical location and indolent growth pattern. However, venous thromboembolic events may precede typical clinical manifestations. In older patients presenting with unexplained VTE and no identifiable conventional risk factors, clinicians should consider the possibility of an underlying occult malignancy, including small intestinal NET.
