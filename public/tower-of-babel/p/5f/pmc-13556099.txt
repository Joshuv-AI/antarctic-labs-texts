An irradiation-free platform for localized skull bone marrow manipulation to dissect the skull-brain axis
Authors: Yi Lv, Jinmeng Yi, Dongmei You, Yuan Yan, Bo Tang, Yiguo Qiu, Yuzhang Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe skull bone marrow (SBM) has recently emerged as a crucial regulator of brain physiology and pathology. However, its direct impact on the brain remains elusive, as existing methods cannot isolate local SBM functions from systemic confounds. Here, we developed an irradiation-free cranial bone flap transplantation platform that enables localized SBM replacement and niche-preserving engraftment. Our method establishes exclusive chimerism within CNS compartments, achieving a “peripherally clean” profile that cleanly separates SBM–brain communication from systemic variables. Comprehensive characterization of this model revealed age- and sex-dependent chimerism patterns specifically within central nervous system (CNS) niches. In contrast to conventional long bone marrow (LBM) transplantation, our model achieved robust CNS-targeted engraftment with minimal peripheral involvement. Proof-of-concept functional studies further demonstrated its functional utility by attenuating brain aging and neuroinflammation through young SBM transplantation into old recipients. Introduction

Bone marrow, the primary site of hematopoiesis, plays vital roles in systemic immune homeostasis, and its dysregulation is implicated in central nervous system (CNS) disorders [1, 2]. While systemic bone marrow interventions such as young hematopoietic stem cell transplantation can reverse age-related cognitive decline [3] and ameliorate Alzheimer’s pathology in models [4, 5], such approaches introduce systemic variables that obscure local mechanisms. Recent studies highlight the skull bone marrow (SBM) as a unique local immune niche [6], capable of supplying myeloid cells to the CNS under pathological conditions such as experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis, stroke, and brain tumors [7–9]. These findings position SBM as a likely key mediator of brain–immune crosstalk, yet a precise understanding of its direct functions has been hindered by the lack of tools for its targeted manipulation.

Conventional methods to probe bone marrow–brain communication rely on lethal irradiation followed by systemic delivery of long bone marrow (LBM)-derived cells [3–5]. However, these protocols induce systemic immune perturbation [10], whole-body irradiation in particular disrupts blood-brain barrier integrity, leading to recruitment of peripherally-derived donor cells to the CNS [11–14], and compromise the native marrow niche [15–18], making it impossible to distinguish local SBM-specific effects from peripheral confounds. Moreover, the old or compromised SBM microenvironment may further hinder donor cell engraftment and obscure functional readouts [19]. Therefore, there is a clear need for a transplantation model that enables SBM-specific, localized manipulation while avoiding systemic confounds.

Recent efforts to investigate SBM-CNS interactions have employed a range of SBM modulation models. For example, utilization of a modified irradiation protocol with lead body shielding to generate SBM chimeras, has demonstrated the calvarial origin of meningeal B cells in head-reconstituted mice [20]. However, brain irradiation inevitably compromises blood brain barrier (BBB) integrity [21, 22], and systemic effects on reconstituted immune cells remain unavoidable. Alternatively, calvarial window approaches, while useful for cellular tracing [8, 23, 24], are limited by low cell yield and insufficient engraftment for functional studies. A systematic comparison of these methods and an assessment of critical variables such as donor and recipient age and sex are lacking.

To overcome these limitations, we developed an irradiation-free, cranial bone flap-based skull bone marrow transplantation (SBMT) platform that enables anatomically restricted and niche-preserving SBM replacement. We systematically characterized chimerism dynamics across CNS niches and peripheral tissues, identifying pronounced effects of donor–recipient age and sex on engraftment efficiency. The SBMT platform achieved high CNS-targeted chimerism with negligible peripheral leakage, a profile distinct from the widespread systemic engraftment seen in conventional bone marrow transplantation (BMT). Through proof-of-concept young-to-old transplantation, we further demonstrated the platform’s functional utility by ameliorating brain aging phenotypes, thereby establishing causal links between SBM manipulation and CNS outcomes. By uncoupling local SBM–brain interactions from systemic immune effects, the SBMT platform establishes a new standard for the functional dissection of the skull–brain axis and provides a precision-targeted approach for uncovering SBM roles in brain health and disease.

Results

Development of a cranial bone flap-based skull bone marrow transplantation (SBMT) model in mice

We developed a localized skull bone marrow transplantation (SBMT) model by grafting a donor-derived calvarial bone flap containing an intact bone marrow niche into a size-matched cranial window in recipient mice. Following a midline scalp incision, an 8 × 8 mm cranial window was created using an electric skull drill over the parietal and interparietal bones, incorporating the sagittal suture, lambdoid suture, and lambda. Extra caution was taken to avoid damaging underlying meninges. Histological analysis, including whole‑mount H&E staining of the dura mater, Evans Blue vascular labeling, and sagittal H&E sections of the brain, confirmed that the surgical procedure did not compromise the integrity of the dura mater or leptomeninges. Occasional focal detachment observed was attributable to tissue processing artifacts, and the meningeal layers remained otherwise continuous and intact (Fig. 1A and Additional file 2, Figure S1). A corresponding calvarial bone flap was harvested from donor mice after euthanasia, and donor dura mater were meticulously removed prior to implantation (Fig. 1B). The prepared bone flap was then positioned into the recipient cranial window and secured using Vetbond tissue adhesive. The incision was sutured, and mice recovered on a heating pad (Fig. 1C).

Postoperative monitoring revealed minimal adverse effects. Body weight showed minor fluctuations during the first week post-SBMT and subsequently stabilized, with no significant variation by age or sex (Fig. 1D, E). Perioperative mortality rates were age-dependent, increasing markedly in recipients older than 15 months (Fig. 1F). To assess whether the SBMT surgical procedure caused localized injury to the underlying brain parenchyma, we performed immunofluorescence analysis for microglia (Iba-1) and astrocytes (GFAP) on brain sections from SBMT and non‑surgical control mice at 1 month post‑operation, the time point corresponding to the behavioral tests. Multiple regions were examined, including the region directly adjacent to the cranial window cortex, hippocampus, and cerebellum. Quantification of the percentage area positive for Iba-1 and GFAP revealed no significant differences between the SBMT and control groups in any of these regions (Additional file 2, Figure S2). These results indicate that the SBMT procedure did not induce significant reactive microgliosis or astrogliosis, ruling out substantial surgical injury as a confounding factor.

Having established the absence of surgery-induced brain injury, we next assessed whether SBMT affected mouse behavior. General well-being, assessed by nesting behavior, indicated no significant differences in nest scores or nesting percentage between SBMT and control mice, suggesting an absence of surgical brain injury (Fig. 1G). Moreover, general locomotor activity and anxiety level were not affected by SBMT, as judged by comparable open field test parameters between control and SBMT mice (Fig. 1H).

Characterization of chimerism in CNS niches and peripheral tissues in the SBMT mouse model

To evaluate chimerism establishment after SBMT, we employed the CD45.1/CD45.2 congenic system and quantified donor-derived (CD45.1+) and recipient-derived (CD45.2+) cells in CNS niches (including SBM, dura mater and brain containing leptomeninges and choroid plexus) and peripheral tissues at multiple time points (Fig. 2A). Cell viability in donor skull flaps ranged from 65% to 80% at 2 and 4 weeks post-SBMT, confirming graft survival (Fig. 2B). Baseline analysis of a pre-transplantation mixture from donor flap and recipient skull with cranial window indicated that donor-originated CD45.1+ cells constituted ~ 16% of total cells (Additional file 2, Figure S3). A comparable chimerism level was achieved in recipient SBM 4 weeks after SBMT (Fig. 2C).

Within CNS compartments, donor-derived cells were undetectable in the brain parenchyma but accounted for ~ 10% of cells in the dura mater (Figs. 2D, E). In the periphery, deep cervical lymph nodes (dcLNs), draining meningeal (dura mater) lymphatics [25], contained ~ 2% donor-derived CD45.1+ cells at 4 weeks post-SBMT (Fig. 2F), whereas other lymph nodes showed virtually no CD45.1+ donor cells (Additional file 2, Figure S4). Moreover, long bone marrow (femurs/tibias), blood and spleen exhibited only marginal level of CD45.1+ cells (Figs. 2G-I), supporting the model’s suitability for studying CNS-specific bone marrow functions without systemic confounding. Extended analysis at 3 months post-transplantation revealed complete absence of donor‑derived cells in these peripheral compartments, confirming the long‑term maintenance of a “peripherally clean” state after SBMT (Additional file 2, Figure S5).

Flow cytometric analysis of SBM, dura mater, and dcLNs revealed broadly similar immune cell profiles between SBM and dura mater, both predominantly composed of recipient-derived (CD45.2⁺) cells. Notably, donor-derived (CD45.1+) cells contributed approximately 50% of CD8+ T cells in the SBM. A distinct pattern was observed in dcLNs, where B cells, although low in abundance (2.5%), were the exclusively detectable donor-derived population (Fig. 2J-L). The detailed gating strategy is provided in Additional file 2, Figure S6.

Impact of donor and recipient sex on chimerism in SBMT mice

To assess the influence of donor and recipient sex on chimerism, we transplanted SBM from male and female donors into age-matched (3-month-old) male and female recipients, and quantified chimerism in the CNS and peripheral tissues 4 weeks after transplantation (Fig. 3A). Donor sex significantly affected SBM chimerism rates in both male and female recipients, with sex-matched transplants resulting in approximately 7–9% higher chimerism than sex-mismatched transplants. Specifically, female recipients receiving SBM from female donors exhibited markedly higher chimerism (~ 15%) than those receiving male donor SBM (~ 5%). A similar pattern was observed in male recipients, where male donor SBM led to higher chimerism (~ 12%) compared to female donor SBM (~ 6%) (Fig. 3B).

The dura mater displayed a comparable but more pronounced sex-matching effect. Sex-matched transplants yielded significantly higher chimerism rates: male-to-male transplants averaged 16% versus 7% in mismatched pairs, and female-to-female transplants reached 20% compared to 10% in mismatched pairs, suggesting preferential engraftment of sex-matched SBM in dural tissue (Fig. 3C). In contrast, no donor-derived CD45.1+ cells were detected in the brain parenchyma across any experimental group (Fig. 3D). Consistent with meningeal lymphatic drainage, chimerism trends in dcLNs mirrored those in the dura mater; however, the overall low chimerism rates (< 1.5%) did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 3E). These results indicate that sex-matched donor-recipient pairing enhances transplantation efficiency in the SBMT model. Normalization to total SBM chimerism revealed that CD45.1⁺ chimerism in the dura mater and dcLNs were comparable between sex-matched and sex-mismatched groups, with no significant differences in the normalized ratios (Additional file 2, Figure S7A, B), indicating that chimerism in these tissues is primarily driven by overall SBM engraftment rather than tissue‑specific mechanisms. Notably, sex-related differences in CNS chimerism did not extend to peripheral tissues. Donor-derived CD45.1+ cells remained undetectable in peripheral bone marrow (femurs/tibias), blood, and spleen, irrespective of donor or recipient sex (Fig. 3F).

We further characterized immune cell composition and donor-derived cell distribution across sex-matched and mismatched transplantation groups using flow cytometry. Although overall cellular profiles in SBM and dura mater were similar across groups (Additional file 2, Figure S7C, D), dcLNs displayed a clear sex-dependent profile, primarily determined by recipient sex rather than donor sex. Specifically, T cell proportions were substantially higher in female recipients (52–58%) than in males (35–41%), whereas B cell proportions exhibited the opposite trend, being significantly enriched in male recipients (45–51%) relative to females (18–27%) (Additional file 2, Figure S7E).

Analysis of donor-derived immune subsets revealed tissue- and sex-dependent distribution patterns. In SBM, sex-matched transplants supported greater diversity of donor-derived cells, with CD8+ T cells representing the predominant population. Notably, the proportion of donor-derived CD8+ T cells was significantly higher in female-sex-matched groups (60%) than in mismatched groups (34%) (p < 0.05, Fig. 3G). A similar distribution pattern was observed in the dura mater (Additional file 2, Figure S7F, G), where female recipients exhibited substantially higher proportions of donor-derived CD8+ T cells (70% with female donors versus 43% with male donors) compared to only 22% in male recipients (Fig. 3H). In dcLNs, B cells constituted the major donor-derived population (Additional file 2, Figure S7H), with higher proportions in sex-matched groups (2%) than in mismatched groups (1%) (Fig. 3I).

Age-dependent effects on chimerism in SBMT mice

We next evaluated the impact of donor and recipient age on chimerism established by SBMT. SBM from young (3-month-old) or old (15-month-old) male donors was transplanted into age-varied male recipients (Fig. 4A). Donor age markedly affected SBM chimerism, with young donors yielding significantly higher engraftment (~ 14%) than old donors (< 4%). In contrast, recipient age showed no significant effect on SBM chimerism (Fig. 4B). A similar donor-age-dependent pattern was observed in the dura mater, where old recipients receiving young donor SBM exhibited higher chimerism (15%) than those receiving old donor SBM (8%) (Fig. 4C).

Notably, old recipients displayed increased donor-derived CD45.1+ cell infiltration in the brain (~ 3%) compared to young recipients (0.2%), as determined by rigorous gating based on biological (CD45.2 mouse) and FMO (CD45.1 omitted) controls (Additional file 2, Figure S8), consistent with age-associated BBB impairment [26] (Fig. 4D). In young recipients, donor-derived cells were undetectable in the brain regardless of donor age. Interestingly, when donor cell entry into the brain was low (as in young recipients), donor‑derived cells appeared to redistribute to dcLNs, particularly when young donors were used. Accordingly, young recipients showed higher donor-derived cell proportions in dcLNs (1.3%) than old recipients (0.4%) (Fig. 4E). Peripheral tissues, including bone marrow (femurs/tibias), blood, and spleen, remained free of donor-derived cells across all groups, indicating that recipient age does not affect peripheral chimerism in SBMT (Fig. 4F). After normalization to total SBM chimerism, CD45.1⁺ chimerism in the dura mater and dcLNs showed no significant differences between young donor and old donor groups in old recipients (Additional file 2, Figure S9A, B). In contrast, normalized chimerism in the brain parenchyma was significantly higher in old recipients compared to young recipients (p < 0.05), regardless of donor age (Additional file 2, Figure S9C).

We next assessed the effect of donor and recipient age on immune cell composition. In SBM, donor age was the dominant factor shaping cellular profiles. For example, B cell frequency was below 20% in young donor groups but approached 30% in old donor groups (Additional file 2, Figure S9D). In dura mater and dcLNs of recipient mice, groups with old recipients showed similar composition regardless of donor age. In contrast, among groups with young recipients, distinct profiles emerged: recipients that received SBM from old donors exhibited consistently higher T cell proportions compared to those receiving young donors (Additional file 2, Figure S9E, F). Engraftment was observed in both SBM and dura mater across all age‑matched and mismatched groups, with CD8⁺ T cells constituting the major donor‑derived subset. (Fig. 4G, H, Additional file 2, Figure S9G, H). A different distribution pattern was observed in brain parenchyma, with more cells originating from young than old donors. Flow cytometry analysis, validated by detailed gating strategies (Additional file 2, Figure S10), confirmed that CD8⁺ T cell levels were elevated when young donors were used in old recipients, whereas donor age had minimal impact on young recipients (Fig. 4I).

To directly visualize these cells, we performed SBMT using ZsGreen⁺ reporter mice (H11-CAG-ZsGreen-Stop-tdTomato) as donors (Additional file 2, Figure S11A). Immunofluorescence staining confirmed the presence of ZsGreen⁺CD3⁺CD8⁺ cells in the donor skull flap and dura mater (Additional file 2, Figure S11 B, C). Furthermore, ZsGreen⁺CD3⁺ or ZsGreen⁺CD8⁺ cells did not co‑localize with CD31⁺ blood vessels in the brain parenchyma of old recipients (Additional file 2, Figure S11 D, E), providing direct histological evidence for their parenchymal localization. Besides, no donor-derived cells were detected in the brains of young recipients, proving CD8⁺ T cell levels were elevated in old recipients (Additional file 2, Figure S11 F, G).

Moreover, in dcLNs, donor-derived B cells slightly outnumbered T cells in young donor-recipient pairs. This B-cell dominance was more pronounced with old donors. In contrast, T cells surpassed B cells in old recipients receiving cells from young donors (Fig. 4J, Additional file 2, Figure S12).

Comparative analysis of SBMT model and conventional bone marrow transplantation (BMT) model

We sought to know whether donor cell origin, SBM versus long bone marrow (LBM), influences engraftment in conventional BMT. CD45.1+ cells isolated from SBM or LBM were transplanted into lethally irradiated (head-shielded) male CD45.2 mice (Additional file 2, Figure S13A). Both groups showed comparable transient body weight loss (maximum ~ 7.5%) during the first week post-transplantation, followed by gradual recovery (Additional file 2, Figure S13B). Peripheral blood analysis revealed robust and sustained chimerism in both SBM- and LBM-BMT recipients, stabilizing at approximately 60% by 8 weeks, with no significant differences between the two groups (Additional file 2, Figure S13C). These results indicate that donor cell source (SBM vs. LBM) does not significantly affect systemic engraftment in conventional BMT.

We next compared the overall chimerism patterns between the SBMT model and conventional BMT (using either SBM or LBM as donor source) 8 weeks after transplantation (Fig. 5A). Our data showed that conventional BMT led to SBM and dural chimerism rates (~ 16% and ~ 14%, respectively) comparable to those in the SBMT model (Fig. 5B, C). However, conventional BMT resulted in significantly higher donor cell infiltration in the brain parenchyma (~ 2% CD45.1+ cells) relative to SBMT (Fig. 5D). Furthermore, widespread systemic chimerism was observed in conventional BMT recipients, with donor-derived cells detected in dcLNs, long bone marrow, blood and spleen at rates of 40–70% (Fig. 5E-H). No significant differences in chimerism or immune composition were observed between SBM- and LBM-BMT recipients across SBM, dura mater, peripheral tissues or lymph nodes, except for the differences in mono/macro cell populations in the dura mater (p < 0.05, Additional file 2, Figure. S13D-J).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that while donor cell origin does not influence conventional BMT outcomes, the SBMT model uniquely establishes CNS-focused chimerism without systemic dissemination, providing a distinct platform for studying brain-immune crosstalk within local microenvironments.

SBMT as a precise tool for dissecting SBM-brain communication

Based on the observation that SBMT establishes chimerism specifically within CNS niches without systemic immune involvement, we asked whether this model could be used to precisely investigate SBM–brain interactions. As a proof-of-concept, we assessed the effect of transplanting young SBM into old recipients on brain aging-related molecular signatures (Fig. 6A).

Notably, young SBMT significantly reduced P16INK4a expression, a key senescence marker, in both the cortex and hippocampus (approximately 4-fold decrease relative to controls) (Fig. 6B). Moreover, RT-qPCR analysis revealed broad downregulation of senescence-associated genes (e.g., Pai1, P16), inflammatory response-related genes (e.g., Spp1, Spi1, Il-1β, Il-6, Ifnγ, Ccl2 and Ccl5), and oxidative stress-related genes (e.g., P22phox, Nox2, Cebpa) in brain tissue, with reductions ranging from 3- to 10-fold (Fig. 6C–E). To establish an appropriate baseline for interpreting these proof-of-concept data, we included an additional control group of age-matched non-transplanted old mice. Quantitative analysis revealed that old-to-old SBMT groups showed no significant differences in P16INK4a or other gene expression compared with old controls (Additional file 2, Figure S14), indicating that transplantation of old grafts does not alter the normal aging baseline, confirming that young SBMT actively reverses the baseline aging phenotype.

To investigate how young SBMT shapes the newly engrafted bone marrow, we performed bulk RNA‑seq on SBM harvested from old recipients that received young donor SBM and compared the transcriptome with that of age-matched naïve (non-transplanted) old mice. Pathway enrichment analysis of upregulated genes revealed a coordinated transcriptional program indicative of enhanced tissue repair and immunomodulation, including pathways related to bone/connective tissue development, wound healing, extracellular matrix organization, and regulation of angiogenesis, collectively pointing to an improved regenerative capacity of young donor SBM. Enrichment of pathways related to leukocyte migration, myeloid cell differentiation, chemotaxis, and regulation of inflammatory response suggested enhanced immune surveillance and balanced inflammatory control, while the presence of Wnt signaling and ERK1/ERK2 cascades indicated activation of pro‑reparative networks. Together, this pathway signature reflects an enhanced reparative, immunomodulatory, and stress‑resilient state of young donor SBM, demonstrating that it reprograms the engrafted bone marrow niche toward a rejuvenated state that favors tissue repair and immunomodulation (Additional file 2, Figure S15).

In the brain, bulk RNA seq analysis identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between old recipients receiving SBM of young donors versus old donors, as shown in the volcano plot (Additional file 2, Figure S16B, Additional file 1, Supplementary Table 2). Pathway enrichment analysis of genes upregulated in young SBMT revealed a coordinated transcriptional program consistent with brain rejuvenation. Enriched pathways included those governing vascular repair and angiogenesis (e.g., regulation of angiogenesis, endothelial cell proliferation), extracellular matrix organization, cell junction assembly, neurodevelopmental processes (e.g., neurogenesis, axonogenesis), chemotaxis, and metabolic/homeostatic functions (e.g., lipid transport). (Additional file 2, Figure S16C). These transcriptional changes indicate that young SBMT counteracts age‑related molecular alterations by promoting vascular and tissue repair pathways. ELISA of whole brain homogenates confirmed that protein levels of IL‑1β, IL‑6, and IFN‑γ were significantly decreased in recipients receiving SBM from young‑donor (Additional file 2, Figure S16D), demonstrating that young SBMT reduces neuroinflammation in the old brain at the protein level.

In contrast, no significant changes in these proinflammatory genes were detected in peripheral tissues such as dcLNs, long bone marrow, or spleen (Fig. 6F–H, Additional file 2, Figure. S17A–F). Together, these results highlight the potential of the SBMT model as a spatially restricted intervention system, enabling focused study of SBM-derived signals in brain physiology and pathology without confounding systemic effects.

Discussion

In this study, we have established an irradiation-free cranial bone flap transplantation platform that enables precise, localized manipulation of SBM. Our systematic characterization identified donor and recipient age and sex as critical determinants of engraftment efficiency and demonstrated the platform’s unique capacity to isolate CNS-specific effects, showcasing a clear advantage over conventional LBM transplantation. Through proof-of-concept young-to-old SBM transplantation, we further validated the platform’s functional utility by showing significant reduction in brain senescence markers without peripheral effects, underscoring its value for investigating SBM–brain interactions.

Current understanding of bone marrow-brain communication primarily relies on conventional BMT approaches involving intravenous delivery of LBM-derived cells to lethally irradiated recipients [27, 28]. However, whole-body irradiation inevitably induces blood-brain barrier (BBB) disruption [29] and neuroinflammation [30, 31], while systemic cell administration introduces peripheral confounds that obscure CNS-specific interpretations. Although head-shielded irradiation reduces direct brain damage, it remains unclear how partial-body irradiation affects SBM niche reconstitution, and systemic delivery still permits peripheral engraftment. Conversely, head-exposed irradiation strategies, while attempting SBM-restricted chimerism, still cause significant BBB impairment and fail to prevent systemic donor cell circulation [20]. These fundamental limitations hinder resolution of whether bone marrow influences brain through indirect systemic immune modulation [32] or direct SBM niche activity. If the latter is true, the outcome would critically depend on the recipient’s SBM microenvironment [33], a factor that conventional approaches cannot control.

Our irradiation-free SBMT platform addresses these limitations by achieving a “peripherally clean” chimerism profile, with donor cells selectively colonizing CNS niches without systemic distribution. Notably, even without irradiation preconditioning, our model attained SBM chimerism levels comparable to head-shielded irradiation while preserving brain–immune interface integrity [34]. Furthermore, while emerging evidence suggests distinct molecular signatures between SBM and LBM [6], we found that SBM- and LBM-derived cells show comparable distribution in conventional BMT, indicating that cell source may be secondary to delivery strategy in determining engraftment outcomes.

Previous attempts to achieve localized SBMT have been constrained by technical limitations. Approaches using small bone flaps (< 4 mm diameter) or multiple cranial windows are limited by low donor cell yield and procedural complexity [8, 24], while existing implementations have served primarily for cellular tracing without systematic optimization for functional studies [23, 35, 36]. Our optimized SBMT platform overcomes these barriers through several key innovations. First, we introduced a single large-window (8 × 8 mm) design that enhances donor cell delivery, increasing yields by approximately 2-fold over previous methods [8]. Second, by strategically targeting the parietal-interparietal-occipital complex, the most hematopoietically active and vascularized skull region across ages [37], our platform ensures optimal microenvironmental support. Finally, we implemented a niche-preserving strategy that maintains the functional marrow compartment, a microenvironment crucial for bone marrow cell development and differentiation [38, 39]. This represents a fundamental advance over conventional BMT. The combination of high cell input and a physiologically relevant niche establishes a stable platform to probe SBM function and dissect its impact on the brain.

The biological insights generated using this platform further highlight its value. We identified pronounced effects of donor-recipient age and sex on SBM chimerism, with old recipients receiving young donor bone flaps exhibiting substantially enhanced engraftment (approximately 3-fold and 2-fold higher in SBM and dura mater, respectively) and sex-matched pairs showing 7–9% higher chimerism rates. These findings not only provide crucial parameters for experimental design but also attest to the platform’s sensitivity in capturing key biological variables. The age-dependent effects may reflect the compromised supportive capacity of old niches [40], whereas sex-specific differences may stem from minor histocompatibility antigen disparities (e.g., H-Y antigen) [41] and hormonal regulation of the SBM microenvironment [42]. Normalizing brain chimerism to overall SBM engraftment revealed that donor cell presence in the brain remained significantly elevated in old versus young recipients, indicating that recipient age independently determines donor cell entry into the brain parenchyma beyond overall SBM chimerism levels. Notably, among sex-matched transplants, we observed strikingly higher CD8+ T cell chimerism in the CNS of FF compared with MM recipients, despite comparable overall engraftment levels. This compartment-specific difference suggests intrinsic sex-biased properties of CD8+ T cells, potentially driven by androgen-mediated inhibition of CD8+ T cell recruitment [43], as well as fundamental sex differences in CD8+ T cell responses [44, 45]. Collectively, these findings raise the possibility that the male hormonal milieu may limit the entry or retention of donor-derived CD8⁺ T cells within the CNS, contributing to the lower CD8⁺ chimerism observed in MM recipients.

Mechanistically, while the present study focuses on establishing the SBMT methodology, our ongoing investigations aim to identify the cell types and pathways mediating its anti‑inflammatory effects. Preliminary transcriptome and protein analyses point that young donor SBM potentially enhance blood–brain barrier integrity, reducing glial activation, and lowering pro‑inflammatory cytokine levels.

In summary, the SBMT platform provides a phenotypically clean system that directly addresses a central challenge in neuroimmunology: the inability to isolate local SBM–brain communication from systemic confounds. This paradigm shift enables causal interrogation of the skull–brain axis without irradiation artifacts or systemic interference. The SBMT model established in this study provides a unique and “peripherally clean” platform for testing the intervention effects of local bone marrow chimerism on pathological processes in future models of aging and chronic neurodegenerative diseases, thereby laying a solid foundation for such translational investigations. Our work empowers the transition from fundamental investigations into brain aging and disease to the translational evaluation of SBM-targeted therapies, thereby redefining the mechanistic and therapeutic landscape of neuroimmune research.

Methods

Mice

C57BL/6J mice (B6 mice hereafter) and CD45.1 mice (C57BL/6J background) were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory and Cyagen Biosciences, respectively. H11-CAG-ZsGreen-Stop-tdTomato KI/KI reporter mice (C57BL/6J background) were purchased from GemPharmatech. All mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with a 12-12-hour light/dark cycle and ad libitum access to both chew and water. All experiments were performed in compliance with institutional guidelines as approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Chongqing International Institute for Immunology.

Cranial bone flap-based skull bone marrow transplantation (SBMT)

Mice were anesthetized with an intraperitoneal (IP) injection of Avertin (20 µL/g body weight, Dowobio) and placed on a heating pad at 37 °C to maintain body temperature throughout the surgery. Hair removal cream was applied to the recipient mouse followed by gentle wipes with surgical disinfectant. The skulls of recipient mice were exposed by making a skin midline incision with a sharp scalpel. An 8 × 8 mm cranial window was created in the parietal and interparietal bones, encompassing the sagittal suture, lambdoid suture and lambda using a portable electrical skull drill. Sterile 0.9% saline solution was applied for cooling during the intermittent drilling. Concurrently, donor mice were euthanized, and a size-matched calvarial bone flap was carefully harvested. Dura mater was removed under a dissecting microscope using precision tweezers, with the bone flap immersed in ice-cold, sterile 0.9% saline solution throughout the procedure. The bone flap containing skull bone marrow was trimmed as needed and was secured within the recipient’s cranial window using Vetbond tissue adhesive (3 M). Then, the skin was sutured, and the mice were monitored on the heating pad until recovery. Daily monitoring and a single IP injection of ketoprofen (2.5 mg/kg body weight) and enrofloxacin (2.5 mg/kg body weight) was performed for 5 consecutive days after transplantation.

Irradiation and systemic bone marrow transplantation (BMT)

Recipient B6 mice at the age of 3-month were irradiated with a lead head shielding for a single dose of 8 Gy by a gamma source irradiation system (Nordion, Canada) [4] followed by reconstitution with donor-derived bone marrow cells (BMCs) from CD45.1 mice. To prepare donor-derived BMCs, femurs and tibias were harvested and bone marrow was flushed with RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco) into a small petri dish on ice using a 25 G needle. To generate single cell suspensions, a 10 mL syringe with 23 G needle was used to flush the clumps (red cell clusters) through a sterile 70 μm cell strainer. After centrifugation, cell pellets were washed twice and resuspended in PBS.

For collecting the skull bone marrow cells (SBMCs), calvaria of donor mice were cut into small pieces using sterile surgical scissors in ice-cold RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco). SBMCs were released by mechanically squeezing the calvarial fragments using the plunger of a 5 mL syringe. The single cell solution was then passed through a sterile 70 μm nylon cell strainer followed by centrifugation, resuspension, and cell-counting. Subsequently, 3 × 106 donor-derived BMCs or SBMCs in 100 µL PBS were intravenously injected into the tail vein of recipient mice 4–6 hours after irradiation. Mice were monitored daily for sickness and weight loss. Enrofloxacin (0.25 mg/mL)-containing water was provided 3 days prior to irradiation and 2 weeks after BMT. BMCs engraftment was assessed in peripheral blood according to the proportion of CD45.1+ and CD45.2+ cells by flow cytometry (LSRFortessa X-20, BD). Endpoint assays were performed 8 weeks after reconstitution.

Generation of single cell suspensions for endpoint analysis

Following anesthesia with isoflurane, mice were transcardially perfused with ice-cold PBS. Brains, includes the leptomeninges and the choroid plexus, were rapidly dissected and minced on ice using a scalpel. Enzymatic digestion was performed using the papain dissociation kit (Worthington) per the manufacturer’s instructions. Myelin was removed via a 30% Percoll gradient and centrifuged at 700 g (without brakes) for 20 min at 4 °C. Cell pellets were washed and resuspended in 1X PBS before staining.

For calvarial and dural cell isolation, calvaria were dissected and the attached soft tissues were trimmed off. Calvaria were cut into small pieces and dura mater were stripped off the inner skull surface using precision tweezers under a dissecting microscope in ice-cold complete medium composed of RPMI 1640 (Gibco) containing 2% FBS, 1% L-glutamine (Gibco), 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco), 1% sodium pyruvate (Gibco), 1% non-essential amino-acids (Gibco) and 1.5% HEPES (Gibco). Individual dura mater and calvarial fragments were incubated with pre-warmed digestion buffer containing 1 mL of complete RPMI 1640 medium with 1 mg/mL of collagenase VIII (Sigma-Aldrich) and 0.5 mg/mL of DNase I (Sigma-Aldrich) for 15 min at 37 °C with five trituration with a P1000 pipette at 10-minute intervals. The digestion process was neutralized by adding an equal volume of RPMI 1640 with 10% FBS (v/v). Dural and calvarial cell suspensions were filtrated with a 70 μm cell strainer and centrifuged at 500 g for 5 min at 4 °C. Dural cells were pelleted at 500 g for 5 min and resuspend in 1X PBS for further steps. The red blood cells of calvarial suspensions were lysed by 1X RBC lysis buffer (BioLegend) on ice for 5 min, followed by washing with 10 mL 1X PBS. The cells were then resuspended by 1X PBS for staining.

Single cell suspensions from femurs and tibias were generated as described in the “Irradiation and systemic bone marrow transplantation (BMT)” section. After centrifugation, red blood cells were lysed with 1X RBC lysis buffer (BioLegend) and incubated on ice for 5 min, followed by washing with 10 mL 1X PBS. The cells were washed and resuspended in 1X PBS for further steps. Spleens and lymph nodes were isolated and dissociated using frosted glass slides, followed by passing through a 70 μm cell strainers. Red blood cells were lysed with 1X RBC lysis buffer (BioLegend) as abovementioned and were resuspended in 1X PBS for staining.

Flow cytometry

Single cells suspended in PBS were incubated with Zombie NIR solution (1:1000, BioLegend) at room temperature for 15 min in the dark to exclude dead cells. Prior to surface marker staining, cells were blocked with anti-CD16/CD32 antibody (2.4G2, 1:100, BD Biosciences, Cat# 553142) to prevent non-specific binding. Cells were then incubated with appropriate surface marker antibodies for 30 min at 4℃ in the dark, including CD45 (30-F11, BUV395, 1:200, BD Biosciences, Cat# 564279), anti-CD45.1 (A20, FITC, 1:100, Biolegend, Cat# 110705), anti-CD45.2 (104, APC, 1:200, BD Biosciences, Cat# 109813), anti-TCR β chain (H57-597, Percp/cy5.5, 1:200, Biolegend, Cat# 109228), anti-CD4 (RM4-5, BV421, 1:200, Biolegend, Cat# 100563), anti-CD8 (53 − 6.7, BV510, 1:200, Biolegend, Cat# 100752), anti-CD19(6D5, PE-Cy7, 1:200, Biolegend, Cat# 115520), anti-CD11b (M1/70, AF-700, 1:200, Biolegend, Cat# 101222), anti-CD11c (N418, PE, 1:200, Biolegend, Cat# 117308), anti-Gr-1 (RB6-8C5, PE-dazzle, 1:200, Biolegend, Cat# 108452). Flow cytometric data were acquired using LSRFortessa X-20 (BD) and analyzed with FlowJo (BD Biosciences, version 10.8.1).

Immunofluorescence staining

Tissue processing for brain analysis

Mice were anaesthetized by isoflurane and transcardially perfused. Brain was dissected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at 4 ℃ overnight. After rinsing with PBS, the fixed tissues were dehydrated in 30% sucrose solution for 48 h at 4 ℃ and embedded in OCT compound (Sakura Finetek) in -80 ℃. Serial sagittal frozen Sect. (10 μm) were cut using a CryoStar NX50 cryostat (Thermo Scientific).

Tissue processing for SBM and dura mater analysis

For analysis of the transplanted skull flap and dura mater, tissues were collected following transcardial perfusion. The intact cranial bone flap containing SBM and the attached dura mater was fixed in 4% PFA at 4 °C for 24 h. Following fixation, the dura mater was carefully peeled from the bone flap under a dissecting microscope. The isolated dura mater was flattened and embedded in OCT compound (Sakura Finetek) and stored at -80 °C until sectioning. Serial sagittal frozen Sect. (10 μm) were cut using a CryoStar NX50 cryostat (Thermo Scientific).

The remaining SBM-containing bone flap was subjected to additional fixation in 4% PFA for another 24 h at 4 °C, followed by decalcification in 10% EDTA solution at room temperature for 8 days. The decalcification solution was replaced every two days until the bone tissue became soft enough to be easily pierced with a needle tip. Decalcified samples were then processed for paraffin embedding through sequential dehydration in graded ethanol, clearing in xylene, and infiltration with paraffin wax. Embedded tissues were sectioned at 10 μm thickness using a microtome.

Immunofluorescence staining for intracellular markers (p16, Iba-1, GFAP)

Prior to staining, slides were washed with PBS (3 × 5 min) to remove OCT and then permeabilized and blocked with blocking buffer (5% normal donkey serum in PBS, with 0.3% Triton X-100) at room temperature for 1 h. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with rabbit anti-CDKN2A/p16INK4a (1:100, Abcam, Cat# ab211542), or rabbit anti-Iba-1 (1:1000, Wako, Cat# 019-19741) and rat anti-GFAP (1:500, Invitrogen, Cat# 13–0300). Sections were washed 3 times for 10 min each with PBST (0.1% Triton X-20 in PBS), followed by incubation with donkey anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) highly cross-adsorbed secondary antibody Alexa Fluor™ Plus 546 (1:500; Invitrogen, Cat# A10040) or donkey anti-rat IgG (H + L) highly cross-adsorbed secondary antibody Alexa Fluor™ Plus 647 (1:500; Invitrogen, Cat# A48272) at room temperature for 1 h.

Immunofluorescence staining for surface markers (CD3, CD8, CD31)

For detection of cell surface antigens, sections were washed with PBS (3 × 5 min) and blocked with 5% normal donkey serum in PBS at room temperature for 1 h. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against Purified rat anti-mouse CD3 (17A2, 1:200, Biolegend, Cat# 100201), rat anti-mouse CD8α (53 − 6.7, PE, 1:200, BioLegend, Cat# 100708), rat anti-mouse CD31 (MEC13.3, PE, 1:200, BioLegend, Cat# 102507) or CD8 alpha Monoclonal Antibody (53 − 6.7, APC, 1:200, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# A18609). After washing with PBS (3 × 10 min), sections were incubated with appropriate fluorescently conjugated secondary antibodies (donkey anti-rat IgG (H + L) highly cross-adsorbed secondary antibody Alexa Fluor™ Plus 647 (1:500; Invitrogen, Cat# A48272) at room temperature for 1 h.

A 10-minute incubation with DAPI (1:1000 Invitrogen) at room temperature was then performed to stain the cell nuclei. Slides were mounted with Fluoromount-G Mounting Medium (Absin) followed by analysis with a microscope slide scanner Pannoramic MIDI (3DHISTECH). Fluorescent microscopy images were analyzed using ImageJ software.

H&E staining

For whole‑mount analysis, the dura mater was harvested immediately after the SBMT procedure, spread flat on a glass slide, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin without prior fixation or embedding. The stained dura mater was examined under a dissecting microscope to assess structural integrity.

For brain tissue, frozen Sect. (10 μm) were prepared as described in the Immunofluorescence staining section. Briefly, sections were fixed, stained with hematoxylin for nuclei and eosin for cytoplasm, then dehydrated, cleared, and mounted. Images were acquired using a Pannoramic MIDI slide scanner (3DHISTECH).

Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA extraction was performed by MiniBEST Universal RNA extraction Kit (TaKaRa) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Synthesis of cDNA by reverse transcription (RT) was performed using PrimeScript FAST RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (TakaRa). RT-qPCR reaction was performed by TB Green Premix Ex Taq II FAST qPCR kit (TakaRa) using a LightCycler 480 II real time PCR instrument (Roche) with the following program: 95 ℃ or 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 ℃ for 5 s and 60 ℃ for 10 s, and a final step at 95 ℃ for 15 s, 60 ℃ for 30 s and 95 ℃ for 15 s. Gene expression was normalized to a housekeeping gene (Gapdh) and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. Sequences of primers used in this study were listed in Additional file 1, Table S1.

Bulk RNA‑sequencing

Skull bone marrow and whole brain tissues (including leptomeninges and choroid plexus) were collected from recipient mice at 4 weeks post‑SBMT (young-to-old, old-to-old) and non-transplanted old control groups (n = 3 per group). Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). Sequencing reads were quality-trimmed using Trim Galore (v0.6.7). Clean reads were then aligned to the mouse reference genome (GRCm39) with HISAT2 (v2.2.1), and the resulting alignments were sorted and indexed using SAMtools (v1.14). Gene-level read counts were quantified with featureCounts (v2.0.1) based on Ensembl gene annotation (release 114). Finally, a MultiQC (v1.12) report was generated to summarize quality-control metrics across all processed samples. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2, with |log2 fold change| > 0.5 and adjusted p‑value < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Volcano plots were generated to visualize the distribution of differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Pathway enrichment analysis was conducted using clusterProfiler against the Gene Ontology (GO) databases, with adjusted p‑value < 0.05 as the cutoff for significantly enriched pathways.

ELISA

Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and transcardially perfused with ice‑cold PBS. Whole brains (including the leptomeninges and choroid plexus) were rapidly removed and finely minced on ice with a scalpel. The minced tissue was homogenized using a Dounce homogenizer with 1 mL of ice‑cold PBS per brain. The homogenate was filtered through a 70‑µm cell strainer, and the filtrate was centrifuged at 500 g for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected and used for ELISA.

All subsequent steps were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Enzyme Linked Immunosorbent Assay Kit, Elabscience). Briefly, standards and samples were added to a 96‑well plate and incubated for 90 min at 37 °C. The plate was then sequentially incubated with biotinylated detection antibody, concentrated HRP conjugate, substrate reagent and stop solution. Absorbance was immediately measured using a microplate reader (Spectramax i3x, MolecularDevices), and protein concentrations were calculated based on the standard curve.

Behavioural Test

Nest-building activity

Mice were housed individually on corn cob bedding and with access for food and water ad libitum throughout the experiment. Nesting activities were assessed in their “home” cages. The nestlet (SEER) used here is a small cylinder, made of pressed cotton with a diameter of 2 cm and a height of 3 cm. Two identical nestlets with a total of 3 g were placed in the corners of each cage at 7 p.m., and nesting activity was evaluated at 7 a.m. the following morning using established parameters [46, 47]. The dimensions and height of the nest were measured, the weight of torn and untorn nest material in each cage was weighted. Untorn fragments are defined as fragments weighing>0.05 g. The percentage of torn vs. untorn nest material was then calculated. Nesting percentage = torn material/ (torn material + untorn material) %. Score of height was determined as follows: 0, material untouched; 1, material moved but scattered; 2, material torn and flat; 3, walls height < 3 cm; 4, walls height 3–5 cm; 5, wall height>5 cm. Nesting rating scale was evaluated based on the following criteria: 1, nestlet > 90% intact; 2, nestlet 50–90% intact; 3, nestlet < 50% intact; 4, nestlet > 90% torn but the nest is flat; 5, nestlet > 90% torn, and nest walls higher than the mouse’s body.

Open field

Individual mice were placed into a square open field arena (made of opaque white plastic material, 40 cm × 40 cm) and allowed to freely explore for 15 min, as previously described [48]. Total distance traveled was measured to probe the general locomotor activity. Anxiety levels were calculated by measuring time spent in the center, distance traveled in center and center entries.

Statistical analysis

Data was presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0). Quantification of immunofluorescence microscopy images was performed using ImageJ (version 1.54). Flow cytometry data were analyzed by Flowjo (version 10.8). Statistical analysis to compare the mean values for multiple groups was performed using GraphPad Prism by one-way or two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni’s multiple comparisons test. Comparisons between two groups were analyzed with two-tailed unpaired Student’s t test or two-tailed Mann–Whitney U test. P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
