CQD: A Rapid and Accurate Tool for Identifying High-Confidence QTLs and Candidate Genes from GWAS Summary Statistics
Authors: Xiangjian Gou, Shaohong Fu, Jiakun Li, Wanzhuo Gong, Qiaobo Wu, Yun Li, Jin Yang, Qiong Zou, Qin Yu, Lanrong Tao, Haoran Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) are a powerful approach for elucidating the genetic architecture of complex traits. The widespread application of GWAS across diverse crop species has generated vast amounts of GWAS summary statistics, creating a pressing need for effective tools to identify high-confidence quantitative trait loci (QTLs) from these data. Here, we present Candidate QTL Detector (CQD), a tool that enables the rapid and accurate identification of confident QTL regions from GWAS summary statistics. To evaluate the performance of CQD, we reanalyzed 64 phenotypic traits from a previously published maize diversity panel. CQD identified 108 high-confidence QTLs from 4179 significant GWAS signals, and these QTLs harbored several well-characterized genes, including NS2 (a regulator of tassel branch number), DRL1 (a key regulator of plant architecture), and ZmACS6 (an ACC synthase involved in ethylene biosynthesis), demonstrating the reliability of the QTLs detected by CQD. Overall, CQD provides an efficient and flexible framework for extracting robust QTLs from GWAS summary statistics, thereby facilitating the genetic improvement of complex traits and advancing crop functional genomics.

1. Introduction

Understanding the genetic architecture of complex traits is fundamental to crop genetic improvement [1]. Since their introduction in the 1990s, genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have emerged as a powerful approach for dissecting the genetic basis of complex traits [2,3]. By associating genetic variants with phenotypic traits, GWAS enables the identification of genomic loci and candidate genes underlying trait variation. Advances in high-throughput sequencing technologies and the increasing availability of reference genome sequences have greatly facilitated the generation of genome-wide high-resolution single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) variation data, thereby promoting the widespread application of GWAS in crop genetics and breeding [4]. For example, GWAS has been extensively used to improve crop yield [5,6,7] and quality [8,9,10], identify genes conferring drought tolerance [11,12], and elucidate the genetic basis of climate adaptation [13,14]. Consequently, a large volume of GWAS summary statistics has been accumulated [15,16,17]. Meanwhile, the rapid development of omics technologies, including transcriptomics and metabolomics, has further expanded the availability of GWAS summary statistics related to biochemical and molecular traits [18,19]. Therefore, accurately identifying high-confidence quantitative trait loci (QTLs) from large-scale GWAS summary statistics is crucial for advancing our understanding of the genetic architecture of complex traits in crop species.

To date, a variety of state-of-the-art statistical models have been developed to account for population structure and kinship in GWAS, thereby improving statistical power and enhancing the reliability of detected association signals, including mixed linear model [20], multiple loci mixed model [21], and fixed and random model circulating probability unification [22]. Several studies have systematically compared these models in terms of statistical power and control of false positives [1,23,24]. Nevertheless, despite these methodological advances, the large volume of GWAS summary statistics generated inevitably contains many spurious association signals. Therefore, there is a clear need for a lightweight downstream tool that can directly analyze GWAS summary statistics and further extract robust QTLs from these data.

In this study, to address the aforementioned challenges, we developed the Candidate QTL Detector (CQD), a lightweight tool implemented in Perl5. CQD enables the rapid and accurate identification of confident QTL regions from GWAS summary statistics, thereby facilitating the genetic dissection of complex traits and the discovery of candidate genes. Moreover, CQD is compatible with GWAS results generated by different software packages and allows users to flexibly define QTL identification criteria to meet diverse analytical needs. Using previously published phenotypic and genotypic data from a maize (Zea mays L.) diversity panel, we further validated the performance of CQD. The successful identification of several well-characterized genes supports the reliability of the QTLs detected by CQD.

2. Results

2.1. CQD Was Developed to Facilitate the Identification of QTLs and Candidate Genes from GWAS Summary Statistics

To facilitate the rapid identification of reliable QTLs from aggregated GWAS results, we developed CQD, a tool implemented in the Perl programming language. CQD does not rely on any external modules and can be executed on any platform with Perl5 installed. The options -t and -m allow CQD to flexibly parse GWAS summary statistics generated by different software packages (Table 1). The combination of the -p and -s options defines the significance threshold (p/s) for identifying significant SNPs, while the -d and -c options jointly determine the criteria used to define a QTL. To further identify potential candidate genes associated with each QTL, CQD provides the -a and -r options to specify the criteria for candidate gene identification. In addition, the -np and -ns options ensure consistency of chromosome identifiers between the GWAS summary statistics and the genome annotation file. Overall, CQD is a portable, flexible, and broadly applicable tool that enables the rapid and accurate identification of confident QTL regions from GWAS summary statistics.

2.2. Sixty-Four Phenotypic Traits and 9,146,558 SNPs from a Maize Diversity Panel Were Used to Evaluate the Performance of CQD

We employed phenotypic and genotypic data from previous publications to evaluate the performance of CQD [17]. A maize diversity panel consisting of 350 elite inbred lines, representing germplasm from multiple breeding eras in China (CN1960&70s, CN1980&90s, and CN2000&10s) and the United States (Public-US and Ex-PVP), was included in the current analysis. Specifically, we analyzed 64 phenotypic traits, which primarily characterized maize plant architecture and flowering time, such as plant height (PH), lower leaf angle (LAL), and days to anthesis (DTA). Correlation-based cluster analysis indicated that phenotypes of the same category clustered together, confirming the reliability of the phenotypic data (Figure 1A). By performing variant calling on resequencing data from previous publications, we obtained 9,146,558 high-quality SNPs with a minor allele frequency greater than 5% and a missing rate lower than 20%. Principal component analysis revealed clear population structure stratification between Chinese and US inbred lines (Figure 1B,C). Furthermore, genome-wide linkage disequilibrium (LD) decay analysis indicated that when the r2 decayed to 0.2, the LD decay distance reached approximately 90 kb (Figure 1D). This value was subsequently used as a criterion for QTL identification in CQD.

2.3. CQD Successfully Identified 108 High-Confidence QTLs from GWAS Summary Statistics

We first performed GWAS on 64 phenotypic traits. In total, 4179 significant SNPs were detected, which were associated with 53 traits. Notably, a large number of significant SNPs were associated with the traits Cob_color (2312 SNPs) and Kernel_color (1276 SNPs), suggesting that a substantial proportion of spurious associations may be present among these signals. Therefore, we further applied CQD to the corresponding GWAS summary statistics to identify reliable QTLs by setting the parameters (the -d and -c options; see Table 1) such that each QTL contained at least three significant SNPs and the distance between adjacent significant SNPs did not exceed 90 kb. As a result, CQD identified a total of 108 high-confidence QTLs, which were significantly associated with 28 phenotypic traits (Table S1).

Taking kernel color as an example, the original GWAS results identified several well-characterized quality-related genes in maize, including PSY1 (Zm00001d036345) [25] and Zmccr3 (Zm00001d015956) [26]. However, the original GWAS results still contained a large number of individual signals, which substantially hindered the identification of reliable candidate genes (Figure 2A). Application of CQD removed 126 low-confidence individual signals, thereby highlighting its ability to effectively filter GWAS results and retain high-confidence association signals (Figure 2B).

To further demonstrate the reliability of the QTLs identified by CQD, we examined the association mapping results for tassel branch number (TBN), relative ear height (EP), and ear row number (ERN). The genomic inflation factors (λ) for the three phenotypic traits ranged from 0.994 to 1.033, indicating that the GWAS models effectively controlled potential confounding effects and produced well-calibrated association statistics (Figure 3). For the TBN_JL2017 phenotype, three QTLs (QTL1, QTL2, and QTL3) were identified, each containing 4, 3, and 5 significant SNPs, respectively (Figure 3A). Among them, QTL2, located on chromosome 4, harbored three candidate genes. One of these genes, NS2 (Zm00001d052598), is located 29 kb downstream of QTL2 and has been cloned and functionally validated to regulate tassel branch number in maize [27] (Figure 3B,C). For the EP_BLUP phenotype, four QTLs (QTL4, QTL5, QTL6, and QTL7) showed strong associations (Figure 3A). QTL6, located on chromosome 1, contained 50 significant SNPs. A known gene, DRL1 (Zm00001d028216), was identified 72 kb downstream of QTL6 (Figure 3D,E). DRL1 has been previously characterized for its role in regulating plant architecture in maize [28]. Similarly, for the ERN_LF2016 phenotype, one QTL (QTL8) was detected (Figure 3A). QTL8 is located on chromosome 1 and contains 45 significant SNPs. We identified ZmACS6 (Zm00001d033862), located 25 kb downstream of QTL8 (Figure 3F,G). ZmACS6 encodes an ACC synthase, a key enzyme catalyzing the first step of ethylene biosynthesis. Previous studies have demonstrated the important role of ZmACS6 in regulating drought tolerance and grain yield in maize [29,30]. Overall, the identification of these well-characterized genes provides strong support for the reliability of the QTLs identified by CQD.

3. Discussion

Accurately identifying causal variants and cloning key genes are essential for improving crop yield, stress resistance, and climate adaptability, thereby helping to mitigate the challenges posed by population growth and climate change [31,32]. However, the large number of spurious associations present in the rapidly expanding body of GWAS summary statistics severely hampers progress in gene cloning and functional genomics. Although state-of-the-art statistical models have substantially improved the statistical power of GWAS [33], the exponential accumulation of data inevitably leads to an increased burden of false-positive signals [34,35]. In this study, we developed CQD to accurately identify robust QTL regions from large-scale GWAS summary statistics, thereby minimizing the impact of spurious associations and enabling the reliable discovery of causal genes underlying trait variation. Moreover, CQD can be readily integrated into the downstream analysis pipelines of diverse GWAS software, making it a highly versatile tool.

Notably, CQD does not perform statistical hypothesis testing and therefore does not modify the false discovery rate control implemented in GWAS. Instead, CQD consolidates significant SNPs into QTL regions to improve interpretability and reduce signal fragmentation. CQD is designed to complement existing LD-based SNP clustering approaches by providing a region-based consolidation strategy for significant GWAS signals and candidate gene prioritization. The recovery of previously cloned genes further supports that CQD does not increase the false-positive association burden.

In addition to the approaches evaluated here, other computational strategies have been proposed to enhance the interpretation of GWAS and related association data. For example, network-based gene prioritization methods have been developed to integrate GWAS signals with functional interaction networks, thereby improving candidate gene identification from association signals [36]. Similarly, tools originating in biomedical association analysis have addressed large-scale summary statistics processing and modular gene prediction using hybrid clustering approaches [37]. These studies share conceptual parallels with the CQD strategy of clustering significant SNPs and prioritizing candidate genes within consolidated QTL regions and reinforce the value of developing downstream analytical frameworks to extract biologically meaningful signals from dense GWAS datasets.

Tool assessment is necessary to verify practical applicability. In this study, the performance of CQD was assessed using a maize diversity panel. CQD successfully condensed thousands of phenotype-associated SNPs into 108 high-confidence QTLs. Furthermore, several well-characterized genes, including PSY1, Zmccr3, NS2, DRL1, and ZmACS6, were recovered within these QTLs, supporting the reliability of the detected loci. Together, these results demonstrate that CQD can effectively refine GWAS results by filtering low-confidence signals and retaining only robust and biologically meaningful associations.

Although CQD was validated using maize in this study, the algorithm relies on general genomic features, including spatial clustering of significant SNPs, genome annotation, and linkage disequilibrium or physical distance parameters. Therefore, CQD can be readily applied to other crop species where GWAS summary statistics and genome annotations are available. Moreover, CQD is not restricted to specific trait categories and can also be applied to GWAS analyses of root system architecture and other belowground traits, which play critical roles in nutrient acquisition, stress adaptation, and crop productivity. We hope that future studies will integrate CQD into GWAS pipelines, thereby promoting crop genetic improvement and helping to alleviate food security challenges arising from rapid population growth and worsening environmental conditions.

4. Materials and Methods

4.1. Plant Materials and Phenotypic Trait Acquisition

A maize diversity panel was employed to evaluate the performance of CQD. This panel consisted of 350 elite inbred lines representing germplasm from multiple breeding eras in China and the United States. Phenotypic data for this panel were obtained from previous publications, in which multiple traits were measured across four environments (LF2016, LF2017, HN2016, and JL2017). For example, JL2017 represents a field trial conducted in Jilin Province, China, in 2017. Detailed descriptions of the remaining environments are available in the original publication. All field trials were conducted under standard agronomic management without intentional abiotic or biotic stress treatments. These traits included ear row number (ERN), tassel branch number (TBN), tassel length (TL), lower leaf angle (LAL), upper leaf angle (LAU), leaf length (LL), leaf width (LW), plant height (PH), ear height (EH), relative ear height (EP), total leaf number (TLN), upper leaf number (ULN), stem diameter (SD), days to anthesis (DTA), days to silking (DTS), anthesis-silking interval (ASI), cob color, and kernel color. In total, 64 phenotypic traits were compiled from previous studies and used in the present analysis [17].

4.2. Variant Calling and Genotypic Data Acquisition

Genome sequencing reads for all maize lines were obtained from previous publications under the accession number PRJNA609577 [17]. Variant calling was performed following the GATK v4.1.2.0 best-practice workflow [38]. Specifically, raw sequencing reads were subjected to quality control using NGSQC v2.3.3 [39] with the parameters “-l 70 -s 20”. Clean reads were then aligned to the B73 RefGen_v4 maize reference genome [40] using BWA v0.7.13 [41] with default settings (MEM algorithm). The aligned reads were subsequently sorted using SortSam, and PCR duplicates were marked with MarkDuplicates. HaplotypeCaller was then applied to generate a GVCF file for each sample. Next, GVCF files from the 350 samples were merged using GenomicsDBImport, followed by joint genotyping with GenotypeGVCFs. SNPs were further filtered using VariantFiltration with the following criteria: QD < 2.0 || FS > 60.0 || MQ < 40.0 || MQRankSum < −12.5 || ReadPosRankSum < −8.0 || DP < 8.0 || QUAL < 20. Finally, non-biallelic variants and variants with a minor allele frequency <5% or a missing rate >20% were removed, resulting in a final set of 9,146,558 high-quality SNPs retained for subsequent analyses.

4.3. Population Structure and Linkage Disequilibrium Decay Analysis

Principal component analysis was performed on the filtered SNP set using PLINK v1.9 [42] to characterize the population structure of the maize diversity panel. The first three principal components were used to visualize population stratification. Genome-wide linkage disequilibrium decay was estimated using PopLDdecay v3.41 [43]. Linkage disequilibrium was quantified using the squared Pearson correlation coefficient (r2), and the physical distance at which r2 decayed to 0.2 was subsequently used as a criterion for QTL identification and candidate gene determination.

4.4. Genome-Wide Association Study

Using the 9,146,558 high-quality SNPs as the genotype matrix, GWAS of 64 phenotypic traits were conducted using GEMMA v0.98.5 [44] under a linear mixed model [20]. The first five principal components and an intercept term consisting entirely of ones were included as covariates, and the kinship matrix was calculated using GEMMA with default parameters. The effective number (EN = 1,850,299) of independent SNPs was estimated using GEC v0.2 [45], and the significance threshold (1/EN = 5.4 × 10−7) was determined based on the modified Bonferroni correction. Manhattan plots were generated using the CMplot package [46]. To evaluate the calibration of the GWAS test statistics, genomic inflation factors (λ) were calculated as the ratio of the median observed chi-square statistic to the expected median chi-square statistic under the null hypothesis.

4.5. Quantitative Trait Locus Identification

The CQD tool (https://github.com/GouXiangJian/CQD, accessed on 14 February 2026) developed in this study was applied to identify QTLs from GWAS summary statistics. Based on the previously determined genome-wide linkage disequilibrium decay distance and the effective number of independent SNPs, the key parameters for running CQD were set as follows: -p 1 -s 1,850,299 -d 90,000 -c 3 -r 90,000 (Table 1). In addition, CQD generated a list of candidate genes flanking each QTL. These candidate genes were extracted from the B73 RefGen_v4 maize reference genome annotations obtained from the MaizeGDB database.

5. Conclusions

In this study, we developed a portable and flexible tool, CQD, that can be applied downstream of diverse GWAS pipelines to identify reliable QTLs and candidate genes from GWAS summary statistics. CQD provides a region-based framework for consolidating GWAS signals, improving result interpretability, and prioritizing biologically meaningful candidate loci. The performance of CQD was validated using 64 phenotypic traits and 9,146,558 SNPs from a maize diversity panel. Although CQD was evaluated using maize data, the method is species-independent and can be readily applied to other crop species and diverse trait categories where GWAS summary statistics are available. We anticipate that CQD will accelerate research in crop functional genomics and facilitate the discovery and cloning of key genes in crop species.
