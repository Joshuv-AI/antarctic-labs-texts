The global landscape of pediatric hyperuricemia research: a bibliometric roadmap to mechanisms, management, and future therapeutics (1997–2024)
Authors: Liang Xiao, Wei Lu, Junli Wang, Shengyou Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPediatric hyperuricemia has emerged as a significant global health concern, intricately linked to the early pathogenesis of metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular disease, and chronic kidney disease. Despite extensive research in adults, critical gaps persist in our understanding of its unique pathophysiology, age-specific diagnostic criteria, and evidence-based management strategies in children. This study aims to provide a comprehensive, quantitative assessment of the global research landscape on pediatric hyperuricemia, identify pivotal thematic foci, and map emerging frontiers to guide future scientific inquiry and clinical translation.MethodsWe conducted a systematic bibliometric and visual analysis of all relevant articles and reviews indexed in the Web of Science Core Collection (WoS CC) from inception to December 20, 2024. Data were synthesized using VOSviewer, CiteSpace, and the Bibliometrix R package to construct collaboration networks, perform co-citation analyses, and track keyword evolution.ResultsOur analysis identified 189 publications, revealing a trajectory of fluctuating but overall increasing scholarly attention. China (n=47) and the United States (n=43) were the most prolific contributors, with St. Jude Children’s Research Hospital and Harvard University leading institutional output. Authors Simonetta Genovesi and Marco Giussani were the most productive. Keyword co-occurrence analysis delineated three dominant research clusters: (I) core clinical management (“hyperuricemia”, “allopurinol”, “rasburicase”); (II) cardiometabolic comorbidities (“obesity”, “hypertension”, “metabolic syndrome”); and (III) epidemiological and risk factor profiling (“children”, “uric acid”, “prevalence”). Notably, recent years have witnessed an emergent focus on long-term sequelae, including “chronic kidney disease” and “acute kidney injury”.ConclusionsResearch into pediatric hyperuricemia remains nascent yet is rapidly gaining momentum. Our roadmap highlights four critical frontiers: elucidating developmental-stage-specific pathological mechanisms, establishing validated diagnostic thresholds, evaluating the long-term safety and efficacy of both pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions, and defining strategies for lifelong management to mitigate adult-onset complications. We advocate for enhanced international and interdisciplinary collaboration as the cornerstone for accelerating progress in this vital field.

Introduction

In recent years, pediatric hyperuricemia and its associated health sequelae have emerged as a pressing global public health concern (1). Uric acid—the terminal metabolite of purine catabolism—is not only a well-established driver of gout, chronic kidney disease (2,3), and cardiovascular pathology in adults, but also exhibits robust associations with obesity, metabolic syndrome, hypertension, and insulin resistance in children (4) The ongoing shifts in lifestyle and dietary patterns have fueled a sustained rise in childhood obesity (5), which in turn has amplified the burden of hyperuricemia among young populations. Epidemiological evidence indicates that the prevalence of pediatric hyperuricemia now exceeds 10% in several countries and regions, and is closely linked to the early initiation of pathological processes underlying adult-onset chronic diseases—positioning it as a potential early-life biomarker of long-term cardiometabolic risk (6).

Despite this growing clinical significance, research on pediatric hyperuricemia lags far behind its adult counterpart. Critical controversies and substantial knowledge gaps persist regarding its age-specific pathophysiology, standardized diagnostic criteria, and evidence-based therapeutic strategies (7). To date, most investigations have concentrated on the management of hyperuricemia in adults, leaving systematic research in pediatric populations markedly underdeveloped (8,9). Current diagnostic thresholds for children are largely extrapolated from adult standards (e.g., serum uric acid >6 mg/dL), thereby overlooking the dynamic influences of age, sex, and pubertal development on uric acid homeostasis.

Similarly, therapeutic approaches remain inadequately validated in children. While agents such as allopurinol and rasburicase are routinely employed in adult care (10), their safety profiles, efficacy, and long-term developmental impacts in pediatric patients require rigorous evaluation (11). Moreover, non-pharmacological interventions—including dietary modification and lifestyle counseling—lack robust empirical support due to insufficient high-quality evidence (12). Collectively, these limitations underscore an urgent need for a comprehensive, systematic appraisal of the global research landscape in pediatric hyperuricemia (13-15).

Bibliometric analysis offers a powerful quantitative framework for mapping scientific evolution, uncovering collaboration networks, and visualizing knowledge structures within emerging fields—thereby informing strategic priorities for future inquiry (16). Notably, no prior study has applied a comprehensive bibliometric approach to the domain of pediatric hyperuricemia and its treatment. This gap has hindered a synthesized understanding of research hotspots, international cooperation dynamics, and emerging frontiers.

To address this, we present a global bibliometric analysis of scholarly output from 1997 to 2024, integrating data from the Web of Science Core Collection (WoS CC) and leveraging advanced visualization tools—including CiteSpace, VOSviewer, and the Bibliometrix R package—to systematically examine 189 relevant publications. By dissecting research trajectories across dimensions such as country, institution, authorship, keyword co-occurrence, and citation networks, our study provides a multidimensional roadmap of the field’s development. These findings not only offer an evidence-informed foundation for clinical decision-making but also delineate priority areas for future research, policy formulation, and international collaboration—ultimately advancing scientific progress toward improved child health outcomes and the mitigation of lifelong metabolic risk. We present this article in accordance with the BIBLIO reporting checklist (available at https://tp.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tp-2026-0255/rc).

Methods

Data sources and search strategy

This study utilized the WoS CC as its primary data source. Widely regarded by the scientific community as a high-quality, multidisciplinary literature database (17), WoS CC is considered the gold standard for bibliometric analyses due to its rigorous indexing criteria and comprehensive coverage of peer-reviewed research. The search strategy was formulated as follows: TS = (“high uric acid in children”) AND TS = (“treatment”). The temporal scope spanned from 1997 to 2024, with all queries finalized on December 31, 2024. To ensure methodological rigor, we restricted the document types to original research articles and review articles, and limited the language to English. An additional snowballing search—screening reference lists of key publications—was performed to enhance retrieval completeness. This initial search yielded 2,358 records. Full bibliographic records, including cited references, were exported in plain text format for subsequent analysis. The complete search and selection workflow is detailed in Table 1.

Screening criteria

Eligible studies included English-language original articles and reviews directly addressing pediatric hyperuricemia and its treatment. We excluded the following publication types: conference abstracts, early-access articles, editorials, letters, conference proceedings, corrections, retracted papers, book chapters, and expressions of concern. Duplicate records were removed prior to screening. One researcher independently conducted the initial literature screening and data extraction. Data analysis and visualization

Bibliometrics—a quantitative discipline formalized in 1969 (18)—provides a robust framework for mapping the intellectual structure, evolution, and collaborative dynamics of scientific fields (19,20). In this study, we extracted and analyzed metadata pertaining to authors, keywords, journals, countries/regions, and cited references. We employed co-citation analysis, a well-established bibliometric technique wherein two documents are considered related if jointly cited by a third (21). This approach enhances interpretability by revealing latent conceptual linkages within the literature.

All analyses were conducted using an integrated suite of state-of-the-art tools: CiteSpace 6.1.3 (developed by Prof. Chaomei Chen), which applies set-theoretic normalization to construct time-sliced networks and timeline views (22), thereby elucidating the historical trajectory and evolutionary pathways of knowledge clusters; VOSviewer 1.6.19 (Leiden University, the Netherlands) (23), which generates intuitive network, overlay, and density visualizations for co-authorship, institutional collaboration, and keyword co-occurrence; Bibliometrix (v4.3.1) and its interactive Shiny interface (Biblioshiny, http://www.bibliometrix.org) within the R statistical environment, complemented by Microsoft Excel 2019 for data curation. Together, these tools enable comprehensive dissection of publication trends, identification of research frontiers, and assessment of scholarly impact—offering a multidimensional perspective on the global research landscape in pediatric hyperuricemia (24).

Statistical analysis

In this bibliometric study, descriptive statistical methods were applied to quantify publication outputs, citation frequencies, and collaboration intensity. Network metrics (e.g., co-occurrence coefficients, betweenness centrality, and burst strength) were calculated using the default algorithms embedded in VOSviewer (version 1.6.19), CiteSpace (version 6.2.R4), and the Bibliometrix R package (version 4.1.3). No inferential statistical tests (e.g., t-tests or ANOVA) were performed, as this is a descriptive and visual analysis of the scientific literature.

Results

Global research landscape

A total of 189 documents—comprising 159 original articles and 30 reviews—were included in this bibliometric analysis, selected through a systematic search and rigorous screening process. The field of pediatric hyperuricemia and its treatment, though relatively nascent, has demonstrated steady growth in scholarly output since 1997. These publications involved 1,265 authors from 134 countries and regions, affiliated with 797 institutions, and appeared in 141 distinct journals, reflecting the emerging global interest in this clinical domain.

Temporal trends in publications and citations

As illustrated in Figure 2 and Table 2, both annual publication volume and citation counts have increased markedly over the past two decades, albeit from a low baseline. From 1997 to 2002, research activity was minimal, consistent with the field’s formative stage. A sustained upward trajectory followed, punctuated by an unexpected decline in 2018—potentially signaling a methodological bottleneck or a transitional phase toward more refined research paradigms. The peak annual output reached 17 articles (in a single year), while cumulative citations peaked at 499 in 2020. By 2024, 12 new articles had already been published, underscoring ongoing scholarly engagement. Collectively, these trends indicate that pediatric hyperuricemia is transitioning from a peripheral concern to an increasingly recognized focus within metabolic and pediatric nephrology research in the era of big data.

Discussion

This study employed bibliometric tools—CiteSpace, VOSviewer, and R-based Bibliometrics—to map the evolution of research on pediatric hyperuricemia and its treatment over the past 28 years. As an emerging field, this domain remains in its early developmental phase, with a trajectory that can be delineated into distinct chronological stages. From 1997 to 2002, scholarly activity was minimal, characterized by sparse publications and negligible citation impact—reflecting the nascent recognition of hyperuricemia as a pediatric concern. Between 2003 and 2010, growing global emphasis on child health catalyzed modest but steady research expansion. A pronounced acceleration occurred after 2010, culminating in a peak period from 2017 to 2020, during which annual citations reached 499 (in 2020 alone), signaling heightened scientific and clinical interest. Notably, a marked dip in publication output in 2018 may indicate a methodological or conceptual bottleneck, suggesting the need for innovative approaches to overcome current limitations (28). Early investigations primarily focused on establishing normative uric acid reference ranges and elucidating basic pathophysiological mechanisms. In contrast, contemporary research has pivoted toward therapeutic interventions, long-term disease management, and the integration of hyperuricemia into broader metabolic and cardiovascular frameworks. This shift aligns with epidemiological trends: rising childhood obesity—driven by dietary shifts and sedentary lifestyles—has intensified scrutiny of hyperuricemia as a modifiable risk factor for noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) (29). Accumulating evidence now links pediatric hyperuricemia not only to obesity and metabolic syndrome but also to subclinical cardiovascular dysfunction and early renal impairment (30,31). Despite these advances, fundamental gaps persist in diagnostic criteria, therapeutic thresholds, and longitudinal outcomes.

From a translational perspective, the bibliometric trends observed in this study offer several actionable insights for clinical practice. First, the rising cooccurrence of ‘metabolic syndrome’ and ‘hypertension’ with pediatric hyperuricemia supports the integration of serum uric acid monitoring into routine cardiometabolic risk screening for obese or hypertensive children, even without overt gout (32). Second, the emergence of ‘chronic kidney disease’ and ‘acute kidney injury’ as research hotspots suggests that clinicians should maintain a low threshold for evaluating uric acid in children with renal impairment or those receiving nephrotoxic therapies (33). Third, the predominance of pharmacologic keywords (‘allopurinol’, ‘rasburicase’) alongside lifestyle terms underscores the need for dual‑pronged management algorithms; the paucity of high‑level evidence calls for pragmatic trial designs, such as registrybased randomized trials, to inform future guidelines (34). These findings collectively advocate for a staged screening strategy beginning in high‑risk populations and highlight the urgency of developing pediatric‑specific uric acid thresholds rather than extrapolating from adult data.

Epidemiological data confirm a rising incidence of pediatric hyperuricemia, particularly among obese cohorts, yet large-scale studies on pediatric gout remain scarce. Pharmacologically, allopurinol—a xanthine oxidase inhibitor—has demonstrated efficacy in select clinical contexts, such as tumor lysis syndrome (35). However, no consensus exists regarding the indications for initiating urate-lowering therapy in children and adolescents, nor on the optimal agent selection. Concurrently, non-pharmacological strategies—including dietary modification, increased physical activity, and behavioral counseling—are increasingly advocated as first-line interventions. Future research must rigorously evaluate the durability and scalability of lifestyle interventions while advancing the development of safer, age-appropriate pharmacotherapies (34).

Pathophysiological mechanisms: elucidating uric acid metabolism in children requires distinguishing between primary genetic disorders (e.g., Lesch-Nyhan syndrome, Kelley-Seegmiller syndrome, UMOD-associated kidney disease) and secondary hyperuricemia driven by obesity, hypertension, or metabolic syndrome (29). Our bibliometric data reveal a growing but still insufficient body of research on monogenic causes, which often present with severe early-onset hyperuricemia and nephrolithiasis. Future research should prioritize genotype-phenotype correlation studies and registry-based natural history data to inform personalized management

Identifying pediatric-specific risk factors is a priority for early intervention. While adult risk factors such as obesity and hypertension are also relevant in children, unique determinants in the pediatric population remain underexplored (36). These include pubertal hormonal changes, dietary fructose consumption, gut microbiota composition, and genetic variants affecting urate transporters (e.g., SLC2A9, ABCG2, URAT1). Our keyword analysis confirms that ‘risk factors’ is a prominent theme, but most studies apply adult‑derived risk frameworks. Future pediatric cohort studies should aim to develop age-stratified and sex‑specific risk prediction models that capture earlylife exposures and developmental trajectories (37).

Keyword co-occurrence and burst detection analyses identify several active frontiers. Core thematic clusters center on “hyperuricemia”, “risk factors”, “management”, and “metabolic syndrome”. Recent attention has expanded to include the epidemiology of pediatric hyperuricemia, its role in chronic kidney disease progression, and its potential as a cardiovascular risk marker. Novel therapeutic modalities—particularly agents targeting uric acid synthesis (e.g., febuxostat) or enzymatic degradation (e.g., recombinant uricase)—have generated considerable interest. Nevertheless, their application in pediatric populations remains constrained by insufficient safety and efficacy data from large-scale randomized trials (34,38). The limited representation of genetic causes, medication‑induced hyperuricemia, and nephrolithiasis in our keyword analysis likely results from both our treatment‑focused search strategy and the genuine paucity of pediatric interventional studies on these topics. Future research should prioritise these areas, and subsequent bibliometric reviews with broader search queries will help verify whether this gap reflects a true knowledge deficit or merely a methodological artifact. The next phase of research must embrace interdisciplinary integration—spanning nephrology, endocrinology, cardiology, and public health—and foster global collaboration. Only through coordinated data sharing, standardized protocols, and joint clinical trials can the field achieve consensus on diagnostic thresholds and evidence-based treatment algorithms.

Conclusions

Pediatric hyperuricemia and its treatment represent a dynamic yet underexplored frontier in child health. By integrating bibliometric mapping with multi-platform visualization (CiteSpace, VOSviewer, and R), this study offers a focused synthesis of treatment‑related global research trends across dimensions of geography, institution, authorship, journal impact, keyword evolution, and co-citation networks. Our analysis not only charts historical progress but also illuminates high-potential avenues for future inquiry. To maximize scientific impact, researchers should leverage collaborative networks, prioritize mechanistic and interventional studies, and advocate for unified global guidelines. Advancing this field demands a concerted, transnational effort to translate emerging insights into effective prevention and management strategies for affected children worldwide.

This first bibliometric analysis of pediatric hyperuricemia research (1997–2024) has several limitations. First, restricting to the WoS CC may have omitted relevant studies from PubMed, Scopus, or regional databases, introducing selection bias. Second, limiting to English-language publications may have excluded important non-English findings. Third, recently published high-quality papers may have artificially low citation counts due to insufficient time for academic digestion, potentially affecting impact assessments. Fourth, the small sample size (189 documents) limits the statistical power of certain network analyses. Fifth, our focused search strategy (TS = “high uric acid in children” AND TS = “treatment”) was deliberately designed to capture treatment-oriented literature, consistent with our emphasis on management and future therapies; however, it underrepresents mechanistic, genetic, epidemiological, and purely diagnostic studies. Consequently, topics such as hereditary hyperuricemia (e.g., Lesch-Nyhan syndrome), drug-induced hyperuricemia, and nephrolithiasis are less visible in our keyword analysis—a limitation we openly acknowledge. Sixth, changes in database coverage and indexing over the 28-year period may have introduced temporal inconsistencies. Future studies should employ broader search terms to cover the full spectrum of pediatric hyperuricemia research (including genetic, metabolic, and non-therapeutic aspects) and integrate multiple databases with sensitivity analyses to enhance comprehensiveness and validity.
