Chronic Myeloid Leukemia: Historical Perspective, Pathophysiology, and Treatment Advances
Authors: Songphol Tungjitviboonkun, Lea Daran, Sorrawit Unsuwan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic myeloid leukemia (CML) was the first leukemia to be described and remains one of the most well-studied hematologic malignancies. Since its initial description in 1845, CML has evolved from a fatal hematologic disease into a manageable chronic condition.SummaryThis review traces the historical evolution of CML research, from its first clinical recognition in the mid-19th century to modern molecular diagnostics and targeted therapy. Key milestones include the discovery of the Philadelphia chromosome in 1960, identification of the BCR::ABL1 fusion gene in the 1980s, and the subsequent development of tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs). The introduction of imatinib in the early 2000s revolutionized CML treatment, transforming a fatal disease into a chronic condition with near-normal life expectancy for most patients. Second- and third-generation TKIs have since been introduced to overcome drug resistance and target specific BCR::ABL1 mutations, such as T315I. Recently, research has focused on mechanisms of TKI resistance, novel signaling pathways, and strategies to achieve treatment-free remission (TFR). Emerging therapies such as vamotinib, KF1601, and combination regimens are being explored. Furthermore, new insights into non-kinase functions of BCR::ABL1 and the role of microRNAs in resistance open additional therapeutic avenues.Key MessagesCML remains the most successful example of how understanding molecular pathogenesis can directly translate into targeted therapy. Strategic shifts toward multi-generational TKIs and combination regimens are essential to address the challenges posed by clonal evolution and TKI resistance. Modern clinical goals have shifted to sustained TFR, aiming to transform CML from a chronic to a curable condition.

Introduction

In 1845, Bennett first described chronic myeloid leukemia (CML). Since then, our understanding of CML has evolved from basic clinical observation to detailed chromosomal and molecular characterization. CML has become a model disease for targeted therapies, illustrating the translation of molecular discoveries into effective treatments. This article aims to summarize key milestones in the development of knowledge about CML, spanning the evolution of disease characterization, diagnostics, prognostic models, therapeutic strategies, and treatment response evaluation. A chronological overview of these landmark achievements is presented in Figure 1.

Key milestones in CML history.

Development of Knowledge

What is now recognized as CML was described by John Hughes Bennett (1812–1875) in the “Edinburgh Medical and Surgical Journal” in October 1845. It was the first form of leukemia ever reported, preceding Nikolaus Friedreich’s description of acute lymphatic leukemia (ALL) by over a decade, in 1857 [1]. Bennett recorded a 28-year-old man who had splenomegaly, hepatomegaly, and lymphadenopathy, who ultimately died from “suppuration of the blood.”

Four years earlier, David Craigie (1793–1866), Bennett’s mentor and journal editor, encountered a patient who presented with weakness, abdominal distention, and fever. Craigie did not explore further, but when he came across a second patient in November 1844, Bennett recognized the similarity between the 2 patients and published a case report with details of Craigie’s case in the same issue [2]. They initially thought the blood change was secondary to an infectious process in the spleen or the blood itself rather than a leukemic process. It took more than a century to clearly understand the pathophysiology of CML.

A few months after Bennett’s publication, Rudolf Virchow (1821–1902), a 24-year-old medical graduate in Berlin, published the second report, describing a 50-year-old woman who presented with fatigue, epistaxis, an enlarged spleen and liver [3]. This was well before Paul Ehrlich’s discovery of aniline dye-based blood staining in 1877. Therefore, leukocytosis had to be inferred from visible changes in the ratio of red cells to “colourless corpuscles” (white cells) in unstained blood. In 1847, Virchow used the term “leukämie” for the first time for the disorder, and in 1856, he further hypothesized that the disease arose not from infection, but from tissue responsible for white blood cell production [4, 5]. Although Bennett published earlier, Virchow introduced and defined the term leukemia; therefore, both authors are generally acknowledged equally.

In 1853, Bennett described leukemia as comprising splenic and lymphatic types. In 1869, Ernst Neumann (1834–1918), a professor of pathology at Königsberg (now Kaliningrad, Russia), discovered changes in bone marrow and called this third type myelogenous leukemia. The development of staining techniques, especially after James Homer Wright (1869–1928) invented the Wright stain in 1902, eventually enabled the classification of leukemias based on the morphology of white blood cells. It became clear that the splenic and the myelogenous forms had similar peripheral blood findings, and the term spleno-myelogenous type was increasingly used. In 1904, Robert Hutchinson proposed classifying leukemia into lymphatic, myelogenous, and mixed forms of leukemia based on the dominant cell type observed in the blood [6].

Until the mid-19th century, most pathologists believed the hypothesis proposed by William Hewson (1739–1774) that red blood cells arose from the “colourless corpuscles” in the blood. This belief was challenged by Ernst Neumann, who in 1869 observed changes in the bone marrow of leukemia patients. Later in 1872, he proposed that leukemia was primarily a disease of the bone marrow [7].

In 1951, William Dameshek (1900–1969), an influential hematologist at the time, speculated that “chronic granulocytic leukemia” (a term commonly used between 1950 and 1990) was part of a broader group of myeloproliferative syndromes, due to the frequent observation of erythrocytosis and thrombocytosis in early CML cases [8].

The Philadelphia Chromosome

At the National Academy of Sciences meeting in the autumn of 1960, Peter C. Nowell, then a junior faculty member at the University of Pennsylvania School of Medicine, and David A. Hungerford, a graduate student, presented their discovery of “a minute chromosome” in leukocytes from 7 CML patients but not in acute leukemia. It “replaces” one of the four smallest autosomes. They proposed a causal relationship between this chromosomal abnormality and CML [9]. It was later designated the Philadelphia chromosome (Ph [1]; or just Ph), named after the city where it was discovered [10]. The “1” suffix reflected the initial expectation that many other distinct chromosomal abnormalities would soon be found in leukemia subtypes. However, it was the only chromosomal marker discovered [11]. The identification of the Ph was one of the first indications that genetic alterations can indeed cause malignancy – a view that was not generally shared at the time.

Initially, the Ph1 was identified as “22q-,” a deletion of the long arm of chromosome 22. This was later corrected in 1973, when quinacrine fluorescence and Giemsa staining were used to identify reciprocal translocation between the long arms of chromosomes 9 and 22, designated t(9;22)(q34;q11) [12].

Pathophysiology

Nora Heisterkamp and John Groffen studied retroviruses and viral oncogenes before their pivotal work in CML. Their work was related to Abelson murine leukemia virus, which carried the oncogene “v-Abl.” They focused on how this viral gene induced leukemia in mice through unregulated tyrosine kinase activity. In 1983, they identified the human homolog of v-Abl, known as ABL1, and mapped it to chromosome 9 [13].

In 1984, Groffen reported that the breakpoints on chromosome 22 were clustered within a 5.8-kilobase (kb) DNA segment known as the major breakpoint cluster region (M-BCR) [14]. In 1985, Heisterkamp demonstrated that it was part of a gene spanning at least 90 kb, named the bcr gene (phl gene in some older reports) [15]. The breakpoint occurs between bcr exons 13 and 14 or exons 14 and 15 [16].

In 1987, the ABL1 domain on chromosome 9 was found to comprise 11 exons and span over 230 kb. The breakpoint usually occurs in intron 1 upstream of exon 2 (Fig. 2) [17].

Example of a Philadelphia chromosome resulting from a BCR::ABL1 fusion. In this case, the breakpoint occurs between exons 13 and 14 of the BCR gene and between exons 1 and 2 of the ABL1 gene, producing a fusion transcript known as “e13a2.” Not all the CML cases harbor the classical BCR::ABL1 breakpoint. Other breakpoint variants exist, including e14a2 (p210). The e1a2 (p190) is associated with Ph-positive acute lymphoblastic leukemia.

The BCR::ABL1 gene gives rise to the BCR::ABL1 fusion protein, a 210 kDa oncoprotein first described in 1985, which replaces the normal 145 kDa ABL1 protein [18]. Its central role in pathogenesis was confirmed in 1990, when transfer of the p210BCR/ABL gene into mice induced a disease resembling CML [19].

Under normal conditions, ABL1 proteins contain a tyrosine kinase domain (including an ATP-binding region) and regulatory SH2 and SH3 domains, which control kinase activity. Additionally, a myristoylation sequence at the ABL1 N-terminus helps maintain autoinhibition by binding to the pocket of the kinase domain, thereby forming an inactive ABL1 conformation (Fig. 3) [20]. The myristoylation sequence is replaced by BCR sequences, leading to the loss of ABL1 autoinhibition (Fig. 3). The kinase domain remains active without the myristoyl group binding the internal pocket [22].

BCR::ABL1 activates multiple downstream pathways. The RAS/RAF/MEK/ERK pathway promotes cell proliferation, the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway enhances cell survival and resistance to apoptosis, and the JAK/STAT pathway drives proliferation and cytokine-independent growth. Additionally, CRKL/GRB2 signaling further amplifies mitogenic and anti-apoptotic signals. These combined effects lead to uncontrolled myeloid proliferation that is characteristic of CML [23].

Secondary resistance to tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) often arises from point mutations within the ATP-binding region of the ABL1 kinase domain, a mechanism first reported in 2001 [24]. Among these, the T315I mutation is the most frequent and confers resistance to most TKIs, necessitating alternative therapeutic strategies. In addition, loss of function of regulatory SH2 and SH3 domains can dysregulate kinase activity and has been implicated in resistance to asciminib [25].

The BCR::ABL1 fusion protein is also found in Ph-positive (Ph+) acute lymphoblastic leukemia (Ph+ ALL), but the breakpoints within the BCR gene often differ from those seen in CML. In CML, the fusion usually occurs in the M-BCR (exons e12–e16), producing the p210 BCR::ABL1 protein, whereas in Ph+ ALL, the fusion more commonly involves the m-BCR (exon e1), generating the smaller p190 BCR::ABL1 protein. These differences in breakpoint location result in distinct protein isoforms [26].

Evolution of Diagnostic Approaches

The investigation began with the invention of Ehrlich’s staining technique in the late 19th century. Before bone marrow biopsy was well-established in the mid-20th century, the diagnosis relied primarily on clinical presentation, physical examination, and peripheral blood smears. Notably, diagnosis was based on the presence of immature myeloid cells rather than the leukocyte count (Fig. 4) [27].

Peripheral blood smear in chronic phase CML (CP-CML). The smear shows marked leukocytosis (hyperleukocytosis) with a full spectrum of granulocytic maturation (left shift). Thrombocytosis and normocytic anemia may also be present. These pathognomonic features help distinguish CP-CML from the “hiatus leucaemicus” typically seen in acute leukemia.

Bone Marrow Investigation

The first recorded bone marrow biopsy was performed in 1903 by Pianese in Italy, using a trocar to puncture the epiphysis of the femur [28]. In 1908, Giovanni Ghedini (1877–1959) trephined the anterior-internal upper third of the tibia [29]. The procedure involved making a skin incision with a scalpel, drilling through the cortical bone, and aspirating medullary blood using a cannula and syringe, but proved impractical for routine clinical use due to its invasiveness [30]. In 1923, Seyfarth introduced the sternal biopsy, which rapidly replaced tibial biopsy due to its relative simplicity and better access [31]. Later, in 1929, Mikhail Arinkin (1876–1948) modified this technique through sternal puncture [32]. Despite these advances, bone marrow biopsy was not widely adopted in clinical practice until the 1940s, due to the lack of established standards for normal marrow. In 1934, William Dameshek concluded in the “medical progress” article published in the New England Journal of Medicine that bone marrow biopsy was important, especially in patients with leukemia who lacked peripheral leukocytosis (aleukemic leukemia) [33].

Leukocyte Alkaline Phosphatase Activity

Differentiating between leukemoid reactions and leukemia was challenging at the time due to similar blood smear findings. Physicians attempted to use the presence of thrombocytopenia, anemia, and basophilia, assuming these features pointed to leukemia. It was later shown that leukemoid reactions could also manifest with any of these. Thus, bone marrow examination was the only reliable method [34]. In the 1950s, the development of histochemical and biochemical assays added further tools. By 1955, it was well-established that LAP activity was typically low in CML, whereas elevated in myeloid metaplasia and pyogenic infections. While this was helpful, it lacked specificity for diagnosis, as various factors could influence LAP levels [35, 36].

Human Cytogenetics Study

For nearly 50 years, scientists incorrectly believed that humans had 48 chromosomes, until Joe Hin Tjio and Albert Levan correctly identified it as 46 in 1956 [37]. The first cytogenetic analysis of bone marrow cells, without the need for prior culture or colchicine administration, was reported by Tjio and Whang [38]. This innovation enabled routine cytogenetic studies, which became standard in the 1960s for CML diagnosis and monitoring treatment response.

Among these methods, RT-PCR proved to be the most sensitive, becoming the preferred method for monitoring minimal residual disease (MRD) following therapy, particularly after bone marrow transplantation in the 1990s [43].

Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) in CML was reported in 1990 [44]. FISH allows for visualization of the BCR::ABL1 fusion at the DNA level in both dividing (metaphase) and non-dividing (interphase) cells, providing a rapid and quantifiable assessment that can be applied to peripheral blood samples [45].

Currently, the diagnosis is primarily based on the detection of the BCR::ABL1 fusion gene, either through the presence of the Ph chromosome by cytogenetics or FISH, or directly by RT-PCR. Peripheral blood and bone marrow morphology support the diagnosis and help determine the disease phase, but molecular confirmation of BCR::ABL1 is required to establish CML.

Disease Phase Definitions

The MD Anderson Cancer Center (MDACC), particularly by Kantarjian et al. [46], was the first to formally define CML’s three-phase model (chronic, accelerated, blast phase) in the late 1980s [47]. This was emphasized by Sokal's review in 1988 [48]. The 2008 WHO classification was the first to formally include CML phase definitions for global recognition. However, it was later changed from triphasic to biphasic disease in the 2022 WHO classification (excluding the accelerated phase) based on the concept of disease biology rather than using an arbitrary cutoff blast percentage. Current criteria (as of 2026) are primarily based on the 2025 European LeukemiaNet (ELN) recommendations, with the WHO 2022 classifications for pathology reporting (online suppl. Table 1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000551493) [49]. Newer studies have shown that accelerated phase CML (AP-CML) can achieve outcomes comparable to those of chronic phase CML (CP-CML) when treated with TKIs, particularly second-generation agents [50, 51]. The WHO 2022 classification removes the AP-CML definition, instead focusing on high-risk features and molecular criteria rather than morphology.

Development of Treatments

Pre-TKI Era

Arsenic has been used in medicine since 1786, when Thomas Fowler introduced a 1% solution of arsenic trioxide (ATO) in potassium carbonate. In 1865, David Lissauer (1836–1892) described the use of Fowler’s solution in 2 patients with leukemia [52]. The patients recovered with spleen shrinkage, a fall in leukocyte count, and an improvement in anemia [53]. ATO then became the established leukemia treatment for 40 years until it was superseded by splenic irradiation.

Spleen radiation was first reported beneficial in 1903 by Nicholas Senn (1844–1908), a surgeon in Chicago. Effects of roentgen-ray (X-ray) therapy include decreased leukocyte count, reduced spleen size, and an improvement in the patient's general condition [54]. An average survival duration was reported to be 3.5 years vs 3.0 years in non-irradiated [55]. Though it became apparent that the disease process had not been abrogated, radiotherapy had been considered the most effective treatment for decades [56].

Benzol and radioactive substances were also explored as alternatives in the 1910s. Benzol usage was first reported by Korányi [57]. Radioactive substances, including radium, were also experimented with during this period [58]. In 1932, Eben M. Byers, a prominent American socialite, died from radiation poisoning after prolonged consumption of Radithor, a commercially available radium- and mesothorium-containing tonic prescribed for chronic pain [59]. It marked a turning point in raising public awareness about the risks of radiation exposure.

Busulfan, an alkylating agent, was reported to be effective in 19 CML patients in 1956 [60]. It had become the treatment of choice by the mid-20th century [61]. It resulted in longer survival times than radiotherapy, although the onset of blast crisis was not delayed. Busulfan, sometimes used with 6-thioguanine, was the mainstay of treatment for the next 35 years. Adverse effects are lung fibrosis and long-lasting bone marrow aplasia. Myeloblastic transformation was typically resistant to chemotherapy [62].

Hydroxyurea, a century-old compound, was reintroduced into cancer chemotherapy in 1963 [63]. It reduces leukocyte count and spleen size. It became the treatment of choice due to fewer side effects and, compared with busulfan, offers longer survival (58.2 months vs. 45.4 months) in a randomized controlled trial in 1993 [64].

Allogeneic bone marrow transplantation using donor stem cells was the first potentially curative treatment. A group from the University of Washington in Seattle reported in the 1970s the disappearance of the Ph in CML patients who underwent allotransplant [65]. Complications include graft versus host disease (GVHD), graft failure, and late infectious complications. While around 10% of patients will relapse after 5 years, the risk is reduced in patients who develop GVHD, probably due to the graft versus leukemia effect. This led to a report of using donor leukocyte infusion (DLI) in early relapse after transplantation in 1990 [66]. A potential complication of DLI is graft failure or GVHD.

Interferon-alpha (IFN-α): The problem with chemotherapy agents in the CP-CML was they could not prevent progression from a chronic phase to a blastic phase, leading to studies of other treatments. IFN-α was introduced in 1983 after the observations that it had significant antiproliferative activity on normal myeloid progenitor and CML progenitor cells [67, 68]. Treatment in doses of 3–9 × 106 IU/m2 daily subcutaneously until hematological remission resulted in a complete hematological response (CHR) in 70–80% of patients and a cytogenetic response up to 60%. Complete suppression of the Ph+ clone in the bone marrow was achieved in less than 10% of patients [69]. It induced durable complete cytogenetic responses (CCyR) and long-term survival in only a small fraction of patients. A randomized controlled trial conducted in West Germany, comparing IFN versus busulfan or hydroxyurea, showed a higher survival benefit compared with busulfan but not with hydroxyurea (median 5.5 years, 3.8 years, and 4.7 years, respectively) [70]. Although no longer commonly used, it did not cross the placenta and was considered a helpful option for young women with CML who are pregnant or planning pregnancy [71, 72].

TKI Era

Imatinib (Gleevec), formerly STI-571, is a selective BCR::ABL1 TKI and the first targeted therapy in hematology. Levitzki [73] first proposed the use of ABL1 inhibitors in 1992. Early studies demonstrated that ABL1 inhibitors block the growth of BCR::ABL1-positive cells in vitro by inducing apoptosis [74, 75]. Subsequent work confirmed this effect in vivo, showing that ABL1 inhibitors could eradicate leukemic cells in animal models while sparing normal progenitors [76, 77]. The phase 2 study in CP-CML patients who failed IFN reported major cytogenetic response (MCyR) in 60% of patients and CHR in 95% [78]. The dose was 400 mg orally daily. The US FDA approved imatinib usage in IFN failure on May 10, 2001, only 10 weeks after submission of a New Drug Application, marking the FDA’s fastest review period of any cancer drug [79]. It was later approved as the first-line treatment for newly diagnosed CML on December 20, 2002. The landmark phase 3 IRIS study in newly diagnosed CML patients was reported in 2003 [80].

Second-generation TKIs were developed in the 2000s to overcome resistance to imatinib, often caused by single-nucleotide mutations in the gene that alter protein conformation and impair drug binding. A third-generation oral TKI was developed in 2010 due to resistance to imatinib. Ponatinib, formerly AP24534, was granted US FDA approval for intolerance/resistance to prior TKIs on December 14, 2012. It showed potency in CML patients resistant to multiple TKIs and/or T315I-mutated patients. In the phase 2 PACE trial, ponatinib 45 mg oral daily demonstrated durable responses in CP-CML patients resistant to prior TKIs or with T315I mutations. Among 267 evaluable patients, MCyR, MMR, and MR4.5 rates were 60%, 40%, and 24%, respectively, with 82% maintaining MCyR at 5 years. After dose reductions to mitigate arterial occlusive events (AOEs), >90% maintained their responses. 5-year OS was 73%, with AOEs accumulating to 31% [85].

Asciminib, formerly ABL001, was introduced in 2017. The drug targeting the myristoyl-binding pocket of the ABL1 kinase induces the formation of an inactive kinase conformation. The US FDA initially approved it in October 2021 for ≥2 TKI failures or with the T315I mutation, and later, in October 2024, for newly diagnosed CML [86]. Asciminib in CML after TKI failure showed 92% CHR, 54% CCyR, and 48% MMR by 12 months in the ASCEMBL trial [87]. The phase 3 ASC4FIRST trial in newly diagnosed CML patients using asciminib vs investigator-selected TKI showed better MMR at week 48 (67.7% vs. 49%) [88]. Loss of the SH2 and SH3 domains can contribute to asciminib resistance by destabilizing the autoinhibited ABL1 conformation [89].

Olverembatinib, formerly HQP1351, a third-generation TKI, was developed in the late 2010s. Phase 1/2 studies conducted between 2016 and 2019 in China, showed efficacy and tolerability in TKI-resistant CP-CML and AP-CML. Among 127 CP-CML patients, 3-year cumulative MCyR, CCyR, and MMR rates were 79.0%, 69.0%, and 56.0%, respectively, with the highest responses in T315I-mutated patients. In 38 AP-CML patients, response rates were lower but still clinically meaningful. Common adverse events included skin hyperpigmentation, hypertriglyceridemia, proteinuria, and thrombocytopenia [90]. Another phase 1b trial conducted between 2020 and 2024 showed good tolerability and efficacy in patients with CP-CML or Ph+ ALL resistant or intolerant to at least two prior TKIs, including T315I mutations and prior ponatinib or asciminib failure. The CCyR and MMR rates were 61% and 42%, respectively. Adverse events included elevated creatine phosphokinase and thrombocytopenia. The recommended phase 3 dose is 30 mg every other day for patients without T315I variants [91]. Currently, it is approved in China but has not been approved by the US FDA for any indication.

One of the most significant adverse effects of TKIs is their impact on cardiovascular health, first reported in 2011 [92, 93]. Notably, there are currently no validated cardiovascular risk scores specifically developed for this patient population. According to NCCN guidelines, patients with cardiovascular risk factors should be referred to a cardiologist for further evaluation and monitoring. Moreover, some studies have demonstrated that a multidisciplinary management approach can effectively help control cardiovascular risk factors [94].

Prognostic Determination

In 1984, Joseph E. Sokal used multivariable regression analysis to create and test a prognostic model in 813 Ph+ CML patients. Four variables, (1) percent blasts, (2) spleen size, (3) platelet count, and (4) age, could determine the survival in non-blastic patients. Patients were categorized into low-risk, intermediate-risk, and high-risk groups (online suppl. Table 2). It was the first article to describe the relationship between the leukocyte count and spleen size in CML [95].

Jörg Hasford (1950–2021), a professor at the University of Munich, later found that Sokal’s score worked well in patients treated with busulfan or hydroxyurea but not with IFN [96]. In 1998, he proposed a new prognostic score, the Hasford score, using data from 1,573 CML patients treated with IFN-α [97]. He added percent eosinophils and percent basophils to his model (online suppl. Table 2).

After the TKI revolution, Hasford knew the score needed revisiting. In 2011, led by Hasford, the European Treatment and Outcome Study (EUTOS) score was proposed (online suppl. Table 3). This score was used in CML patients who received imatinib treatment [98].

In 2016, the EUTOS long-term survival (ELTS) score was proposed, which better predicted overall survival in the TKI era (Table 1) [99]. It is used in current clinical practice.

TKI withdrawal syndrome occurs in 30–40% of patients after stopping TKIs. It is more frequent in females [107]. The symptoms typically develop within 1–3 months and are characterized by new musculoskeletal pain, joint pain, or stiffness. They can last up to 6 months and are generally managed with analgesics [108].

Ongoing Research and Advancements

Recent research continues to elucidate the genetic abnormalities and expand into treatment options for drug resistance.

Next-generation sequencing (NGS) has expanded CML risk stratification beyond cytogenetic study. It enabled high-sensitivity detection of BCR::ABL1 kinase domain mutations and other somatic mutations. The additional chromosomal abnormalities, classified as major (e.g., +8, +Ph, i(17q), +19, +17, +21) and minor (e.g., −7/7q-, 11q23, 3q26.2), lead to diminished survival probabilities. These co-occurring alterations may reflect genomic instability, particularly in patients with suboptimal responses or disease progression [109]. Compared with conventional Sanger sequencing, NGS provides greater sensitivity in identifying low-level kinase domain mutations that may predict TKI resistance. Early detection, such as the T315I mutation, can inform therapeutic decision-making, including the selection of third-generation TKIs. In this way, NGS has the potential to optimize individualized treatment strategies [110].

Beyond kinase domain analysis, NGS panels have identified high-risk molecular features associated with adverse outcomes. Recurrent mutations in ASXL1, RUNX1, TP53, IKZF1, and epigenetic modifiers such as DNMT3A and TET2 have been linked to inferior molecular response, increased risk of progression, and reduced overall survival [111]. The presence of ASXL1 mutations at diagnosis has emerged as a potential independent adverse prognostic marker in CP-CML [112, 113]. TP53 and RUNX1 mutations, in particular, are frequently observed in advanced-phase disease and are associated with aggressive biology [114].

In advanced-phase CML, NGS may provide additional prognostic stratification and support therapeutic decision-making. Identification of high-risk molecular profiles or complex mutational patterns may prompt earlier consideration of allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, especially in patients with suboptimal response to TKIs. Furthermore, NGS can help distinguish true leukemic evolution from age-related clonal hematopoiesis [115]. As molecular profiling is increasingly integrated into clinical practice, NGS is a key tool for advancing precision medicine in CML.

The integration of AI-driven mutation prediction into clinical practice is still in its early stages, but initial studies show AI can help identify high-risk patients at diagnosis, monitor mutation dynamics during therapy, and potentially predict the emergence of resistance before clinical relapse occurs [116]. LEukemia Artificial intelligence Program (LEAP), an extreme gradient boosting-based machine learning model, helps improve survival outcomes by recommending the optimal TKI for patients with CP-CML [117].

KF1601, a dual BCR::ABL1 and FLT3 inhibitor, demonstrated potent preclinical efficacy in blastic phase CML (BP-CML). It effectively inhibited both BCR::ABL1 signaling, including T315I-mutated models, and FLT3-driven proliferation, which occurs in about 50% of BP-CML cases. KF1601 is effective in imatinib-resistant K562/FLT3/TAZ cells, and achieved complete tumor regression in K562 xenograft models and significantly prolonged survival in orthotopic mouse models. In contrast to ponatinib, which caused AOEs and severe vascular damage, KF1601-treated mice showed no vascular occlusion, with intact vessel walls in thrombosis models. This suggests that KF1601 is a candidate for drug-resistant BP-CML [126].

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T- cell therapy has been studied. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that CAR T-cells targeting specific antigens expressed on CML stem cells (e.g., IL1RAP and CD26) can selectively eliminate leukemic cells in vitro and in animal models, suggesting a potential role for CAR T-cell therapy in eradicating TKI-resistant leukemic stem cells [127, 128]. These studies provide a potential role for CAR T-cell in the future.

Emerging Therapeutic Approaches

Omacetaxine mepesuccinate, formerly known as homoharringtonine, is a natural plant alkaloid derived from Cephalotoxus fortunei and first described in Chinese studies in the 1970s [129]. In 2012, the US FDA approved omacetaxine for the treatment of CP-CML and AP-CML in patients who had failed ≥2 prior TKIs. Approval was supported by data from 111 pretreated patients, demonstrating a MCR in 18% of CP-CML (median 12.5 months) and a MHR in 14% of AP-CML patients (median 4.7 months). It was administered subcutaneously twice daily for 14 consecutive days in a 28-day cycle. Conclusion

Since 1845, CML has evolved from a clinically described but poorly understood disease into a landmark example of precision oncology, driven by the discovery of the BCR::ABL1 fusion gene and the development of imatinib. This molecularly defined framework has transformed CML from a fatal leukemia into a chronic, manageable condition for the vast majority of patients. Despite these transformative advances, important challenges remain, particularly in patients with drug resistance or suboptimal response.

The integration of NGS is expanding our understanding beyond classic ABL1 kinase domain mutations to encompass additional somatic mutations and epigenetic dysregulation in disease progression. In the future, comprehensive genomic profiling at diagnosis may refine risk stratification, guide more therapeutic selection, and enable earlier intervention in high-risk subsets.

Looking ahead, progress in translational research and rational drug development will be essential to achieving deeper, more durable molecular responses. Strategies that integrate next-generation TKIs, novel targeted agents, and potential combination approaches aimed at leukemic cell eradication may further expand the proportion of patients eligible for sustained TFR. While TFR has emerged as a meaningful and achievable clinical endpoint, definitive cure remains an aspirational goal.

For the hematology community, the evolution of CML continues to serve as both a model and a benchmark for mechanism-driven therapy. The ongoing convergence of molecular insight, precision diagnostics, and innovative therapeutics will ultimately determine whether CML becomes not only a controllable disease, but a curable one.
