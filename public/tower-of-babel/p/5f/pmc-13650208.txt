Back to the roots: Cannabis sativa L. root metabolism, microbiomes, and biotechnological potential
Authors: Marta Libik-Konieczny, Natalia Hordyńska-Tomsia, Zofia Mazur, Kamil Zieliński, Mateusz Bibro, Jakub Kurczab, Aneta Gerszberg, Katarzyna Hnatuszko-Konka, Paulina Supel, Piotr Waligórski, Paweł A. Rodziewicz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCannabis sativa L. roots have been less studied than aboveground organs, despite their key role in plant physiology, metabolism, and interactions with biotic and abiotic factors. Metabolomic and phytochemical analyses reveal that roots synthesize a diverse array of bioactive compounds with antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and cytotoxic properties, highlighting their biotechnological potential. Root exudation patterns and interactions with endophytic microorganisms modulate rhizosphere microbial networks that support nutrient uptake, stress tolerance, pathogen resistance, and whole-plant physiology. Root-derived phytohormones and other signalling molecules may participate in coordinating biochemical pathways between belowground and aboveground tissues, with potential effects on secondary metabolism in aerial tissues. Recent advances in metabolomics, transcriptomics, microfluidic rhizosphere systems, and root-specific genetic engineering now enable detailed investigation of root metabolism in Cannabis sativa L. This review synthesises current knowledge on the metabolic roles of Cannabis sativa L. roots, their interactions with the rhizosphere microbiome, and root-derived systemic signalling. It emphasises aspects of root biology that are central to fundamental plant processes and to the development of sustainable strategies for optimising phytochemical yields. By placing roots at the forefront, this synthesis underscores the need to expand research beyond aerial tissues to fully understand and harness the biotechnological potential of Cannabis species.Key points• Root metabolism and signalling regulate whole-plant-metabolic pathways• Root-associated microbiomes influence nutrient dynamics and phytochemical profiles• Root culture systems provide a scalable platform for biotechnological manipulation aimed at the production of bioactive compounds.

Introduction

For millennia, Cannabis sativa L. species have been cultivated for multiple purposes, including fibre, seeds, and a diverse array of pharmacologically important compounds produced particularly by female inflorescences (Russo 2007). Despite ongoing debate surrounding the efficacy, safety, and legal status of cannabinoid-based products, their use and commercial relevance in the pharmaceutical industry continue to expand (Hoch et al. 2025). Consequently, contemporary research has predominantly focused on secondary metabolites synthesised mainly in glandular trichomes, with emphasis on their chemical characterisation, extraction, and regulation across developmental stages and cultivation conditions. Overall, more than 550 metabolites, including cannabinoids, terpenoids, flavonoids, stilbenes, sterols, and triterpenoids, have been identified across Cannabis species and cultivars (Rock And Parker 2021; Tang et al. 2023). The phytochemical analyses are mainly based on Nuclear Magnetic Resonance Spectroscopy (NMR), chromatographic and spectrometric techniques, such as Gas Chromatography coupled with Mass Spectrometry (GC–MS) and Liquid Chromatography–Mass Spectrometry (LC–MS) (Bernstein et al. 2019a, 2019b; Livingston et al. 2020; Kotiranta et al. 2024). In contrast to the well-characterized aerial tissues, roots have remained relatively underexplored, despite their unique metabolite profiles and significance for plant growth and development.

Historical records from ancient Chinese, Roman, and Persian medical traditions describe the use of Cannabis roots for treating inflammation, pain, and infections, suggesting bioactive properties that modern research is only beginning to elucidate (Russo 2007; Ryz et al. 2017; Brand And Zhao 2017). The earliest scientific characterization of Cannabis root-specific phytochemicals dates back to 1971 and includes identification of several triterpenoid and amide compounds (Slatkin et al. 1971). Recent advances in cannabinomics, defined as the comprehensive profiling of Cannabis metabolites, have revealed the extraordinary chemical diversity and organ specificity (Aliferis And Bernard-Perron 2020). Phytochemical analyses of hemp root extracts have identified a diverse spectrum of monoterpenes, triterpenoids, alkaloids, phenolic amides, amines and sterols, several of which display important bioactive properties (Singh et al. 2025; Sá et al. 2026). For instance, alkaloids cannabisativine and anhydrocannabisativine, as well as triterpenoids like epifriedelanol, friedelin and β-amyrin, exhibit anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antimicrobial, hepatoprotective, angiogenic, and estrogenic activities (Ryz et al. 2017; Elhendawy et al. 2018; Kornpointner et al. 2021; Huang et al. 2023; Kobtrakul et al. 2023; Araújo et al. 2024; Gagné et al. 2024; Jang et al. 2025; Jin et al. 2026; Sá et al. 2026). Moreover, friedelin was detected in Cannabis roots at levels comparable to those found in the leaves of Azima tetracantha and Shorea robusta, plants commonly used in traditional Indian medicine (Jin et al. 2020). The latest discovery by Brownstein et al. (2025) expanded the known chemical diversity of Cannabis roots by identifying neolignans with cytotoxic effects against various cancer cell lines, including neuroblastoma, hepatocellular carcinoma, and Hodgkin lymphoma. Cannabinoids were also detected in roots, but only at low levels (Wang et al. 2024; Park et al. 2025b). These findings highlight new opportunities to investigate both the therapeutic potential of Cannabis roots and the potential role of root metabolism in shaping secondary metabolite production, underscoring the value of comprehensive metabolomic analyses. Although somewhat overlooked, roots are increasingly recognized as metabolically dynamic organs that provide key chemical precursors, signals, and physiological inputs influencing plant metabolism, including those associated with specialized metabolic pathways (Ryan et al. 2016; Lynch 2021; Araujo et al. 2025).

This review addresses critical knowledge gaps concerning the functions of Cannabis roots and their contribution to the whole-plant metabolic capacity. Here, we present Cannabis roots as an integrated regulatory centre connecting belowground processes with aboveground plant functioning. Particular attention is given to root architecture, exudation, and root-microbe interactions, which influence nutrient exchange, stress responses, and metabolic regulation. Within this framework, root traits and root-associated microbiota jointly shape metabolic fluxes and the spatiotemporal regulation of secondary metabolism, providing a model for how root-associated factors influence whole-plant fitness. We further discuss emerging biotechnological approaches that harness root metabolism, including root culture systems, metabolic engineering, and synthetic biology strategies aimed at redirecting carbon and nitrogen fluxes toward the production of valuable metabolites. By combining metabolic, microbial, and biotechnological evidence, this review also highlights future research directions that position Cannabis sativa L. roots as a promising and sustainable platform for the production of pharmacologically and industrially relevant compounds. This perspective moves beyond descriptive synthesis and establishes a conceptual basis for targeted manipulation of Cannabis productivity and metabolite biosynthesis.

Root system and microbial interactions

The root system serves as the primary interface between a plant and its subterranean ecosystem. Traits such as lateral root density, root hair abundance, and root tip number influence plant establishment, stress tolerance, and the recruitment of beneficial rhizosphere microbes (Araujo et al. 2025). Interactions between rhizosphere microbiomes and plant are broadly recognised as being crucial for the regulation of plant physiological processes through nutrient acquisition or increased stress resistance, and also for sustainable crop design and production (Shi et al. 2024). Root exudates shape microbial communities, whose metabolic activities can modulate hormonal and redox signalling in plants (Buirs And Punja 2025).

In parallel, small peptide-mediated signalling represents a conserved regulatory module in root development, integrating hormone pathways and developmental control across model plants such as Arabidopsis and several crop species (Zhang et al. 2024). This provides a broader mechanistic framework for interactions between roots and associated microbes and whole-plant processes, although it has not yet been specifically characterised in Cannabis. Tracking root growth and development, as well as rhizosphere interactions and exudate production and release pattern in natural habitats remains challenging. Despite their functional relevance, root-derived compounds are difficult to characterise due to low concentrations, rapid microbial turnover, and limited availability of analytical standards. However, recent advances in microfluidic and hydroponic platforms combined with LC–MS and GC–MS metabolomics have improved detection sensitivity and spatial resolution (Atoloye et al. 2025). In Cannabis, for instance, Root-TRAPR (Root-Transparent, Reusable, Affordable three-dimensional Printed Rhizo-hydroponic) enabled dynamic monitoring of root growth, whereas RhizoChip (Rhizosphere-on-a-Chip) facilitates controlled analysis of root-microbe interactions and exudate release (Suwanchaikasem et al. 2022; Atoloye et al. 2025). More recently, RhizoMAP, a rhizosphere metabolite mapping platform, has been developed as a non-destructive approach that combines membrane imprinting with mass spectrometry imaging. The device has already been successfully used to determine the spatial distribution of metabolites in Populus trichocarpa L. rhizosphere (Veličković et al. 2024). This system provides a scalable platform for high-resolution metabolite imaging and can be applied to other plants species. Integration of such approaches with isotopic labelling strategies, including steady-state ^13C metabolic flux analysis (MFA) (Shih And Morgan 2020), and transcriptomic profiling can further help to elucidate metabolic fluxes underlying exudate production in Cannabis sativa L.

Applying methodological solutions and insights from studies on other plant species enables us to address even more complex biological questions regarding Cannabis metabolism. Emerging evidence indicates that root architecture, exudate composition, endophytic and rhizosphere microbiome diversity contribute to nutrient efficiency, stress adaptation, and plant productivity also in this species. However, the precise integration of these processes and their quantitative contribution to metabolic outcomes still remain not fully resolved in Cannabis sativa L.

Root architecture

Cannabis roots range from 20 to 50% of the total plant dry biomass, depending on cultivar, growing conditions, physico-chemical properties of the growth medium, and cultivation system, e.g. field, pots, hydroponics, aeroponics (Amaducci et al. 2008; Gagné et al. 2024; Wee et al. 2025). Their primary functions include water and nutrient uptake, storage and transport. Roots also participate in establishing biotic and abiotic interactions, maintaining physical positioning and overall performance of the plant. Cannabis sativa L. root system consists of a long primary root (taproot), secondary (lateral roots) and tertiary roots (Fig. 1a). The taproot grows vertically downwards to a different depth depending on soil parameters and cultivation system. It consists of a single cell layer of epidermis, hypodermis and endodermis (Fig. 2a). The cortex, located between epidermis and endodermis, consists predominantly of parenchyma cells which store reserve substances (e.g. starch) and facilitate the transverse movement of water and minerals toward the vascular tissues. Vascular bundle is located centrally and is composed of diarch xylem with two groups of primary phloem, arranged radially within the stele surrounded by a layer of pericycle cells (Fig. 2a) (Hayward 1938; Raman et al. 2017). Secondary roots are formed from the pericycle of the primary root, and together with tertiary roots, grow horizontally, typically at the 15–20° angle, exploring large volumes of soil in search of water and nutrients. They form a rhizosphere by establishing interactions with soil microorganisms (Hayward 1938). In plants propagated vegetatively from cuttings, adventitious roots originate from internal cells of stems which acquired meristematic activity and resemble a fibrous root system (Fig. 1b). Their anatomical structure differs from primary roots. Rhizodermis of AR is early replaced by a periderm developed from pericycle to form a protective outer layer. Root-related traits, including length, density, diameter, surface area, branching, and biomass, show significant heritable variation in Cannabis (Morales et al. 2026). These parameters characterise root basic morphology and give insight into its potential for water and nutrient uptake, architecture, maintenance, longevity, and are also helpful in rhizosphere modelling and crop design strategies aimed at optimising root system architecture (Amaducci et al. 2008; McGrail et al. 2025; Sui et al. 2024).

In field conditions, in well-cultivated mineral soil the taproot can penetrate up to 2 m deep, with lateral roots arising from the upper 20–40 cm depth of the soil and spreading up to 80 cm in length. In richer humus soil, however, the taproot reaches a depth of only 30–40 cm, and the lateral secondary roots are formed just around 10–20 cm depth of the soil making the root system more compact. For instance, in industrial hemp cultivated in loam and loam-slit-clay soil the highest root length density and root biomass were found within the first layer of soil, reaching as much as 5 cm/cm3, which together with taproot, constituted up to 50% of root biomass. Also, just below the ground the diameter of the roots was found to be finer (ca. 190 μm) but increased linearly up to approximately half of the root length, reaching around 300 μm, and remaining constant at around 80% of root length. However, root length density and biomass decreased gradually along with increasing soil depth (Amaducci et al. 2008). Cannabis female and monoecious plants tend to have roots with smaller diameters than male plants, but their total mass and surface area can be up to two times bigger (McGrail et al. 2025). The adjustable nature of Cannabis roots makes it a system well-adapted to water limiting conditions. The increased root zone area positively influences water and nutrient uptake dynamics, facilitates gas exchange, affects the number of biotic interactions, and the overall plant fitness (Gill et al. 2023). Also, the ability of a specific variety to develop higher root biomass may be a key factor determining overall vegetative fitness of the plant, including the performance of secondary metabolism in tissues other than roots. This has been demonstrated in grafting experiments involving joining scions and rootstocks from different Cannabis sativa L. varieties with distinct chemotypes (Purdy et al. 2022; Bitežnik et al. 2024; Lazare et al. 2024). Genetically controlled root traits, such as branching, root diameter, length, root-to-shoot biomass ratio, can also serve as targets for Genomic-Assisted Breeding to improve plant performance (Araujo et al. 2025; Morales et al. 2026).

Root exudates

Root exudates released into the rhizosphere, comprise primary metabolites, secondary metabolites, and also proteins such as enzymes, and signalling peptides (Taghinasab And Jabaji 2020). The composition and spatiotemporal dynamics of root exudation are regulated by nutrient availability and environmental conditions, and are integrated into broader root signalling networks controlling development, stress responses, and rhizosphere interactions (Shi et al. 2024). While direct experimental evidence in Cannabis remains limited, studies in several model plants including Arabidopsis thaliana L., Oryza sativa L., Zea mays L., Glycine max L. and Medicago truncatula L. indicate that small signalling peptides may act as key regulatory factors linking root development, hormone signalling, and microbial interactions (Zhang et al. 2024).

In Cannabis sativa L., more than 170 compounds have been identified in root exudates, including carbohydrates, organic acids, amino acids, dipeptides, nucleotides, lipids, polyketides, phenolics, alkaloids, nitrogen-containing compounds, and other secondary metabolites (Suwanchaikasem et al. 2022; Atoloye et al. 2025). The exudates influence soil pH, chelate minerals, and act as both nutritional substrates and chemical signals that shape microbial community (Taghinasab And Jabaji 2020; Buirs And Punja 2025; Park et al. 2025a). In Cannabis species chemically diverse exudation strategies root exudates may actively modulate its rhizosphere. For instance, the profiles of carbohydrates, amino acids, and organic acids have been associated with the stimulation of plant growth-promoting bacteria, whereas fractions rich in phenolic and terpenoid compounds may selectively influence fungal community composition and microbial antagonism, although the underlying regulatory networks remain only partially resolved (Feng et al. 2024; Atoloye et al. 2025). Experimental evidence also indicates on strong plasticity of root exudation under various nutrient conditions. For example, phosphorus deficiency induces a shift in organic acid secretion profiles, including increased citrate exudation, which in turn enhances phosphorus mobilization in soil (Islam et al. 2023). Also, elicitors such as chitosan have been shown to induce secretion of proteins, including pathogenesis-related proteins, chitinases, and peroxidases, along with elevated levels of phenolic and carbohydrate compounds (Suwanchaikasem et al. 2022). Under abiotic stress conditions, such as heavy metal contamination, Cannabis sativa L. has been shown to recruit particular microbial species into its rhizobiome and benefit from microbe-produced compounds, such as melatonin, which contribute to stress mitigation (Sun et al. 2023; Feng et al. 2024).

Cannabis root exudation is a dynamic process linking environmental sensing, metabolism, and microbial recruitment. However, recognising chemical diversity in the rhizosphere through metabolomic and microbiome data, which show associations, is not sufficient to demonstrate a direct cause-and-effect relationship between exudates and microbial recruitment or function. Elucidation of the regulatory mechanisms underlying these processes requires more scientific attention.

Root endophytes

Root endophytes are widely recognised across plant species as a key component of the plant microbiome that colonises internal root tissues and contributes to growth regulation, nutrient acquisition, and stress adaptation (Luo et al. 2025; Shi et al. 2024). In many plant taxa, including both dicot and monocot species and major agricultural crops, endophytic communities form dynamic associations with the host that are closely linked to root development and environmental responses (Shi et al. 2024; Vimal et al. 2024; Luo et al. 2025). Although the underlying mechanisms are not fully resolved, root endophytes are increasingly viewed as an extension of plant adaptive capacity, with implications for crop improvement.

The bacterial community of Cannabis roots is dominated by the phyla Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, and Firmicutes, with recurrent genera including Pseudomonas, Bacillus, Burkholderia, and Streptomyces (Kusari et al. 2017; Buirs And Punja 2025). Some bacterial taxa can be vertically transmitted through seeds, contributing to early root colonization and potentially influencing the establishment of a beneficial plant-associated microbiome (Lobato et al. 2025). Seed-borne endophytes form a core microbiome that initiates root colonization, whereas soil microbes subsequently expand the diversity and functional potential of the seedling’s endophytic community (Dumigan And Deyholos 2024). These bacteria are associated with plant growth-related functions, including nutrient solubilization, nitrogen fixation, phytohormone production, and siderophore release, which collectively support nutrient acquisition and root development, particularly under stress conditions (Taghinasab And Jabaji 2020; Tariq et al. 2025a). Genera such as Pseudomonas, Bacillus, and Mucilaginibacter have also been linked to improved plant performance and altered secondary metabolite profiles in Cannabis female inflorescences (Lyu et al. 2023; Tanney et al. 2023).

Fungal endophytes of the Cannabis sativa L. root microbiome encompass both non-mycorrhizal and arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF). Frequently reported non-mycorrhizal taxa include: Trichoderma, Metarhizium, Pochonia, Penicillium, Clonostachys species, and yeast species, such as Rhodotorula and Candida (Buirs And Punja 2025; Hu And Bidochka 2025). In some model species, these fungi have been associated with improved plant performance, including promotion of root branching, enhanced biomass and increased tolerance to abiotic stress conditions, primarily through effects on nutrient mobilisation, antioxidant activity, and modulation of plant hormonal and immune signalling pathways (Contreras-Cornejo et al. 2009; Buirs And Punja 2025; Hu And Bidochka 2025). In Cannabis sativa L., presence of Trichoderma endophyte has been associated with improved growth and altered cannabinoid content under greenhouse conditions (Kakabouki et al. 2021). In parallel, Cannabis sativa L. forms symbiotic associations with AMF, predominantly belonging to the phylum Glomeromycota, which colonise root cortical cells and form arbuscules and vesicles that facilitate bidirectional nutrient exchange. This interaction facilitates plant uptake of phosphorus, nitrogen, zinc, and other micronutrients in exchange for photosynthetically derived carbon, and has been associated with changes in plant growth and secondary metabolism (Saloner and Bernstein 2021; Seemakram et al. 2022). For instance, Cannabis plants inoculated with Rhizophagus aggregatus BM‑3 g3 showed significantly higher biomass and enhanced accumulation of major cannabinoids than non‑mycorrhizal controls (Seemakram et al. 2022). Beyond nutrient exchange, AMF interact with bacterial and other fungal endophytes, and are suggested to contribute indirectly to root signalling and microbiome community establishment (Taghinasab And Jabaji 2020).

Endophytic community formation and its functional potential in Cannabis are shaped by the integrated effects of host genotype, ploidy level, developmental stage, and environmental conditions (Taghinasab And Jabaji 2020; Buirs And Punja 2025; Tariq et al. 2025b; Lobato et al. 2025). Among host-derived determinants, root architecture and exudation chemistry act as key selective filters governing microbial recruitment and colonization success (Taghinasab And Jabaji 2020; Shi et al. 2024; Tariq et al. 2025a). Polyploidization, explored in Cannabis sativa L. for improved stress tolerance and cannabinoid production, further modifies root structure and signalling processes, thereby altering endophytic community composition and interactions (Fernandes et al. 2023; Srivastava et al. 2024). Environmental conditions are also important determinants of endophytic community structure. Organic and biologically active soils are generally associated with higher diversity and greater stability of endophytic assemblages, whereas hydroponic or sterile systems tend to result in reduced microbial complexity but can facilitate the establishment of more defined and experimentally tractable microbial communities (Tariq et al. 2025a; Ahmed And Hijri 2021). Experimental inoculations with synthetic microbial communities (SynComs) have shown that defined microbial consortia can influence plant growth and cannabinoid profiles under controlled conditions (Ahmed et al. 2023; Tariq et al. 2025a; Greetatorn et al. 2025; Tonolo et al. 2026). Accordingly, integrated management of endophytes and AMF has been proposed as a strategy to optimise root microbiome structure and improve both productivity and phytochemical outcomes in Cannabis (Ujvári et al. 2025).

Despite these advances, current understanding remains largely derived from descriptive and correlation-based studies (Comeau et al. 2020; Ahmad et al. 2024; Taghinasab And Jabaji 2020; Pagnani et al. 2018; Scott et al. 2018; Davies et al. 2024; Ahmed et al. 2021). These approaches provide valuable insight into microbial diversity but do not establish causal relationships between endophytes and plant traits. Accordingly, observed associations of taxa such as Pseudomonas, Bacillus, Streptomyces, or AMF with Cannabis roots may reflect host-driven selection mediated by genotype, physiology, root architecture, and exudation chemistry rather than direct functional effects (Shi et al. 2024; Wiszpolski And Stolarski 2026). To go beyond correlation, experiments are needed that can show cause-and-effect relationships. Synthetic microbial community (SynCom) approaches provide controlled platforms for testing defined microbial communities under reproducible conditions (Shi et al. 2024; Greetatorn et al. 2025). Gnotobiotic systems, in which sterile plants are inoculated with single strains or simplified communities, allow direct linking of phenotypic effects to specific microorganisms, although they reduce environmental complexity compared to natural soil systems. Complementary stable isotope tracing approaches enable quantification of carbon and nutrient exchange between host and endophytes (Haichar et al. 2016). In addition, mutant- and transformation-based approaches in both plants and microbes can provide mechanistic insight into genetic and molecular pathways underlying colonisation and function (Aunkam et al. 2024). Collectively, these approaches may contribute to a hierarchical framework in Cannabis research that helps distinguish microorganisms that may actively influence plant performance from those that are only associated with plant traits (Wiszpolski And Stolarski 2026; Lyu et al. 2023).

Root metabolic integration

Roots are metabolically active organs that integrate nutrient uptake, primary metabolite biosynthesis, and long-distance transport, thereby contributing to whole-plant metabolic homeostasis (Uribe-Acosta et al. 2025). At the interface with the soil, roots coordinate carbon and nitrogen assimilation in association with microbial nutrient turnover, thereby influencing the production and distribution of primary metabolites (Cai et al. 2023). Root-derived metabolites are transported via xylem and phloem and function as resources for growth and metabolism in plant aerial tissues. The insights into these and related processes originate from studies on model plants such as Arabidopsis thaliana L., Nicotiana spp., and Solanum lycopersicum L., and many others (Zhang et al. 2010; Geng et al. 2023). In contrast, in Cannabis sativa L., the extent, regulation, and physiological relevance of these transport pathways remain largely unresolved.

Primary metabolism in Cannabis sativa L. roots is expected to follow conserved pathways characteristic of higher plants, and provides energy, carbon skeletons, nitrogen carriers, and membrane components required for plant development (Erb And Kliebenstein 2020). Major classes of primary metabolites include carbohydrates, organic acids, amino acids, nucleotides, fatty acids, sterols, and polyols. Central carbon metabolism, encompassing glycolysis and the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, supports root energy demands and generates metabolites such as malate, citrate, and 2-oxoglutarate that link carbon metabolism with nitrogen assimilation. This functional coupling between carbon and nitrogen metabolism is well established in higher plants (Zhang And Fernie 2023) and is reflected in metabolite profiles of Cannabis sativa L. roots (Malík et al. 2022; Song et al. 2023). Nitrogen assimilation in Cannabis sativa L. roots is associated with nitrate and ammonium uptake, followed by reduction and incorporation into amino acids via the glutamine synthetase–glutamate synthase (GS–GOGAT) cycle. This process relies on TCA cycle-derived carbon skeletons, tightly coupling carbon and nitrogen metabolism (Malík et al. 2022; Song et al. 2023).

Transcriptomic analyses of Cannabis sativa L. roots show enrichment of genes involved in nutrient transport, redox regulation, central carbon metabolism, and phytohormone signalling, consistent with the active role of roots in systemic metabolic regulation (Hu et al. 2025). In addition, plastid-localized fatty acid biosynthesis and the mevalonate pathway are expected to contribute to the production of membrane lipids and sterols, including β-sitosterol, a major sterol detected in Cannabis roots (Ferrini et al. 2021).

Root metabolic fluxes and long-distance allocation of primary metabolites can be quantitatively analysed using stable isotope-assisted metabolomics and isotopically nonstationary metabolic flux analysis, which enable tracing of carbon and nitrogen from root uptake into downstream metabolic pools (Allen And Young 2020; Arroo et al. 2021; Kambhampati et al. 2024). For instance, in Sorghum bicolor L., root-feeding studies demonstrated efficient transport and incorporation of labelled metabolites into systemic metabolic networks, highlighting the suitability of roots as experimental entry points for whole-plant metabolic flux analysis (Simpson et al. 2021). Application of these approaches to Cannabis could also provide valuable insights into whole-plant metabolic integration.

Root-derived signalling networks

Roots synthesize and translocate signalling molecules that coordinate root-to-shoot communication, transmitting environmental and stress cues via vascular tissues to regulate growth, development, and adaptive responses (Zhang et al. 2010, 2024; Cai et al. 2023; Geng et al. 2023; Munné-Bosch 2026).

Roots produce diverse classes of mobile signals, including phytohormones such as cytokinins (CYT), abscisic acid (ABA), strigolactones (SLs), and the ethylene (ET) precursor 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylic acid (ACC), as well as secondary metabolites and stress-associated compounds including β-aminobutyric acid, γ-aminobutyrate, mobile RNAs, small peptides, salicylic acid (SA), and pipecolic acid (Pip) derivatives such as N-hydroxypipecolic acid (NHP), which participate in systemic acquired resistance (Cai et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2024). Root-associated microbial communities further contribute to signalling dynamics by producing phytohormones and hormone-related compounds, including auxins (AUX), CYT, gibberellins (GA), SA, jasmonates (JAs), ET-related compounds, trans-zeatin riboside (T-ZR), and ABA. These microbial metabolites influence local hormone pools and modify plant developmental and physiological responses (Shi et al. 2024; Tripathi et al. 2025). Understanding these belowground signalling networks is therefore important for improving plant performance, metabolic engineering strategies, and approaches to the production of specialized metabolites under diverse cultivation systems. Much of our understanding of systemic root signalling comes from studies on model species including Arabidopsis thaliana L., Oryza sativa L., Nicotiana benthamiana L., Nicotiana tabacum L., Glycine max L., and Solanum lycopersicum L., together with several non-model plants (Cai et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2024). In this context, Cannabis sativa L. represents an emerging system in which systemic signalling processes are only beginning to be elucidated.

Root-derived signalling can be conceptualised as a sequential framework (Fig. 3). The process begins with local environmental perception in roots and associated changes in root architecture. These responses alter root exudation profiles and influence recruitment and activity of rhizosphere microorganisms. Hormonal crosstalk and transcriptional regulation integrate these local responses and establish belowground signalling networks. Studies in model plants indicate that microRNAs (miRNAs) contribute to these processes by regulating hormone signalling pathways and coordinating gene expression networks involved in root development and environmental adaptation (Liu et al. 2014; Meraj et al. 2020; Barrera-Rojas et al. 2021; Halshoy et al. 2025; Rabeh et al. 2025). Following local signalling events, mobile molecules are transported systemically through xylem and phloem to aerial tissues. Evidence from model systems demonstrates that hormones such as ABA, CYT, and ACC can function as long-distance signals that regulate shoot physiology (Cai et al. 2023). For instance, ethylene biosynthesis and perception occur in roots across many plant species, and ACC may act as a transportable precursor that moves from roots to shoots and is subsequently converted into ethylene under stress conditions (Van de Poel And Van Der Straeten 2014). Once systemic signals reach aerial tissues, they may activate transcriptional networks involving APETALA2/ethylene-responsive factor (AP2/ERF), WRKY, basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH), basic leucine zipper (bZIP), MYB, and NAM/ATAF/CUC (NAC) transcription factors, integrating hormonal and environmental cues to regulate defence responses and secondary metabolite biosynthesis (Meraj et al. 2020).Fig. 3Schematic overview of a conceptual rhizosphere-centred regulatory network linking Cannabis root metabolism and microbiome interactions with aboveground plant functions. In Cannabis sativa L. transcriptomic and physiological studies suggest partial conservation of these regulatory components; however, functional validation of signalling molecules remains limited (Suwanchaikasem et al. 2022; Hu et al. 2025). And, many studies on Cannabis have focused on practical horticultural applications rather than analysis of systemic signalling. Research on phytohormone function in Cannabis was initially developed through studies of clonal propagation and root development. During vegetative propagation, elevated AUX concentrations and their accumulation in root primordia promote adventitious root formation, whereas high CYT concentrations inhibit this process in stem cuttings (Clarke 1981; Campbell et al. 2021; Sae-Tang et al. 2024). Root-derived hormonal regulation has also been implicated in reproductive development. Classical experiments demonstrated that removal of adventitious roots promoted male flower formation, whereas CYT application reversed this effect, indicating a root-dependent hormonal influence on sex expression (Chailakhyan And Khryanin 1978). More recent metabolomic analyses quantified endogenous AUX, ABA, JAs, and SA in Cannabis roots, providing an initial framework for understanding hormonal regulation of root physiology (Suwanchaikasem et al. 2022).

Among these pathways, jasmonate signalling has received particular attention because of its association with defence and secondary metabolism (Luo et al. 2023). Although most evidence is derived from exogenous foliar methyl jasmonate (MeJA) treatments, environmental and microbial stimuli perceived belowground may contribute to jasmonate accumulation and systemic responses in plants (Marqués-Gálvez et al. 2025).

In Cannabis exogenous application of MeJA treatment upregulated genes associated with glandular trichome development and cannabinoid precursor biosynthesis, as well as increased trichome density and cannabinoid accumulation, supporting a regulatory role for JA signalling in secondary metabolism (Hahm et al. 2024; Huang et al. 2024; Da Cunha Leme Filho et al. 2025). Jasmonate signalling also interacts with ET pathways, which influence stress responses and floral development. In Cannabis sativa L., inhibition of ET perception using silver thiosulfate induces male flower formation in genetically female plants and is widely applied in feminized seed production (Owen et al. 2023; Kim et al. 2024; Timoteo Junior And Oswald 2024). Although ET biosynthesis and ACC transport from roots to shoots are established in model systems, equivalent root-to-shoot signalling mechanisms have not yet been experimentally demonstrated in Cannabis.

Root signalling is further linked to interactions with rhizosphere and endophytic microorganisms. Microbial communities associated with roots produce phytohormones and hormone-like metabolites that may alter plant hormone homeostasis and influence systemic responses (Shi et al. 2024; Tripathi et al. 2025). Emerging evidence further suggests that roots may generate additional mobile defence-related compounds beyond classical phytohormones. For example, Xu et al. (2025) proposed that metabolites associated with systemic acquired resistance, including Pip derivatives such as NHP, may participate in long-distance signalling. Although these mechanisms were established in Arabidopsis thaliana L., their presence and functional significance in Cannabis sativa L. remain unresolved. Overall, current evidence supports the existence of conserved root-derived signalling components in Cannabis, but causal links between belowground signalling, root–microbiome interactions, and aboveground cannabinoid or terpenoid accumulation remain largely unexplored.

Root-centred Cannabis biotechnology

Recent advances in plant biotechnology and cultivation platforms, including hydroponic and aeroponic systems, application of synthetic microbial communities, and integrated multi-omics approaches, now enable investigation and manipulation of root-centred biochemical processes in Cannabis sativa L. (Huang et al. 2024; Hahm et al. 2024; Da Cunha Leme Filho et al. 2025). RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) has transformed plant transcriptomics by enabling high-resolution gene expression analysis without a prerequisite of a fully annotated genome, making it a particularly valuable tool for studying medicinal plants, including Cannabis sativa L. (Wang et al. 2009). A notable example is the transcriptomic atlas of this species (Van Bakel et al. 2011; Braich et al. 2019; Barbosa‐Xavier et al. 2024). In parallel, genomic resources for Cannabis sativa L. have advanced from draft assemblies to chromosome-scale reference genomes, and, more recently, to a global pan-genome covering diverse cultivars and landraces (Van Bakel et al. 2011; Laverty et al. 2019; Braich et al. 2019; Gao et al. 2020; Grassa et al. 2021; Cai et al. 2023). For instance, Cannabis roots exhibit expression of genes associated with biosynthetic pathways supplying isoprenoid precursors for secondary metabolite biosynthesis (Braich et al. 2019). This transcriptional signature is reflected in metabolomic profiles of terpenoid compounds identified in Cannabis roots, confirming their biosynthetic capacity and biotechnological potential (Jin et al. 2020).

One of the key tools used in Cannabis sativa L. biotechnology and genetic engineering is Rhizobium rhizogenes-mediated transformations and the resulting transgenic hairy root (HR) cultures (Kobtrakul et al. 2023) (Fig. 4). HR cultures are characterized by genetic stability, rapid growth and hormone-independent development, which makes them a suitable model for omic studies and an exploration platform for the discovery of new bioactive compounds (Ingvardsen And Brinch-Pedersen 2023). Although establishing HR cultures in Cannabis sativa L. is experimentally challenging, several trials have demonstrated notable success in metabolite production using this approach (for a review see Injamum-Ul-Hoque et al. 2025). For instance, a comprehensive NMR and LC–MS profiling of hemp hairy-roots led to identification of a diverse set of triterpenes, and also, for the first time, a group of lignanamides (cannabisins) with antioxidant and antifungal activities, which were previously thought to be restricted to fruits and seeds. It is worth noting that approximately 38% of detected metabolites in the study were left unannotated, which only highlights the biotechnological potential of that in vitro root system (Kaminsky et al. 2024). Furthermore, optimization of HR culture conditions and elicitation strategies, e.g. by MeJA or SA treatments, has been shown to significantly enhance the accumulation of secondary metabolites, such as friedelin and epifriedelanol in Cannabis sativa L., underscoring the potential of this in vitro platform for sustainable biotechnological production of valuable phytochemicals (Mahendran And Vimolmangkang 2024).Fig. 4Schematic representation of in vitro induction of hairy roots and adventitious roots from leaf explants of Cannabis sativa L. The first examples of genome-edited Cannabis sativa L. lines obtained with new genomic techniques (NGTs) come from callus in vitro cultures generated via Agrobacterium tumefaciens-mediated transformations (Galán-Ávila et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2021; Holmes And Punja 2023). However, HR cultures also represent a platform compatible with recent genome-editing approaches, such as CRISPR/Cas. The delivery of CRISPR/Cas genetic construct via Rhizobium rhizogenes will enable targeted mutagenesis and gene regulation in Cannabis roots, facilitating characterization of genes involved in root growth and development, signalling, and metabolism (Injamum-Ul-Hoque et al. 2025; Liu et al. 2025; Ajdanian And Torkamaneh 2025). Recent methodological improvements, including optimised infection conditions and graft-assisted transformation strategies, have substantially increased HR induction efficiencies, in some cases approaching even 90% of transformation success (Ingvardsen And Brinch-Pedersen 2023). Nevertheless, the translation of genome-edited Cannabis plants into breeding practice still requires further improvements, especially in the efficiency of plant regeneration.

In addition to HR in vitro cultures, adventitious root (AR) in vitro cultures represent a complementary, non-transgenic model system for Cannabis research and biotechnology (Fig. 4). Under in vitro culture conditions, adventitious roots are induced from leaf or stem explants through the exogenous application of auxins. AR cultures provide a genetically stable platform for studying root physiology without introducing any genetic modifications. Cannabis AR cultures are known to activate root-specific metabolic programs, including secondary metabolite biosynthesis, and also exhibit long-term stability and scalability in their production (An et al. 2024; Ajdanian And Torkamaneh 2025; Park et al. 2025b). Also, elicitation of Cannabis AR cultures with signalling compounds, such as MeJA and SA, may lead to enhanced biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, and particularly flavonoids and cannabidiol (Wang et al. 2024). In addition, these types of Cannabis root cultures have been shown to produce plant-derived nanovesicles exhibiting physicochemical stability and specific metabolite profiles, with demonstrated potential as immunotherapeutic agents and vaccine delivery platforms (Park et al. 2025a).

Both hairy root and adventitious root in vitro cultures benefit from growth under highly controlled conditions, ensuring reproducibility in experimental setups. Their utility would be particularly significant if efficient and reproducible protocols for whole-plant regeneration, especially of transformed lines, were established, enabling integration of these root systems with hydroponic and aeroponic cultivation platforms. These types of plant cultivation enable precise control over growth conditions, such as temperature, nutrient availability, oxygenation, and also modulation of secondary metabolite accumulation by application of hormones or elicitors (Ferrini et al. 2021; Weingarten et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2024; Subah et al. 2025; Trono 2025).

Across the preceding sections, Cannabis sativa L. root biology has been discussed in terms of root architecture, exudate chemistry, endophytic and rhizosphere microbial associations, central metabolic integration, and emerging biotechnological approaches. Comparison and synthesis across these research areas highlight major findings, current limitations, and future research directions. Although these components collectively suggest a highly interconnected belowground regulatory system, current research on Cannabis roots remains largely descriptive and correlation-based, in contrast to model plants and major crop species, where underlying biological mechanisms are better understood. This gap highlights the need to integrate structural, biochemical, microbial, and molecular findings into a more coherent, multilevel understanding.

Targeted manipulation of root systems provides practical opportunities to improve nutrient use efficiency and enhanced secondary metabolite production, including flavonoids, cannabinoids and terpenoids. Nutrient regime optimisation, root-applied elicitors, and microbiome management employing beneficial endophytes or defined microbial consortia can redirect metabolic fluxes toward specialized metabolism while improving stress resilience and cultivation sustainability. Root architecture traits, including branching patterns, root diameter, and exudation capacity strongly influence microbial recruitment and metabolic efficiency. Selection for these traits, together with polyploidy-based modifications of root morphology and exudate composition, represents a feasible breeding strategy for developing cultivars adapted to environmentally challenging conditions, such as resource-limited soils (Sui et al. 2024). Despite the importance of robust regeneration systems for advanced biotechnologies, Cannabis sativa L. remains notably recalcitrant to de novo whole-plant regeneration from somatic tissues. Multi-genotype studies have shown that while callus induction is often achievable, subsequent shoot organogenesis still requires further optimisation (Monthony et al. 2021). Root in vitro culture systems, including HR and AR cultures, as well as protoplast-derived callus cultures, provide tractable biotechnological platforms that partially overcome whole-plant regeneration limitations (Stelmach-Wityk et al. 2025). These platforms enable functional genomics, metabolic flux studies, and rhizosphere interaction analyses, as well as support functional studies of root metabolism and microbial interactions. CRISPR/Cas-mediated genome editing in roots enables functional validation of biosynthetic genes, targeted metabolic flux redirection, and modulation of exudation profiles to shape rhizosphere composition. Also, HR and AR cultures offer stable, scalable platforms for the production of root-derived bioactive metabolites, which could be harnessed for pharmacological or industrial purposes.

Despite substantial progress in understanding signalling-mediated regulation of secondary metabolism in Cannabis sativa L., current research remains largely focused on aboveground tissues. Many studies have examined the effects of exogenously applied phytohormones, demonstrating their roles in glandular trichome development and regulation of secondary metabolism, including cannabinoid biosynthesis (Burgel et al. 2020; Oultram et al. 2024; Dimopoulos et al. 2025; Fayaz et al. 2025). In contrast, root contribution to systemic signalling and metabolic regulation remains poorly characterised, despite its role in signalling molecules biosynthesis, perception, and long-distance transport. Evidence from model plants indicates that roots produce metabolites involved in plant–microbe interactions and immune regulation, which can drive metabolic reprogramming and coordinated induction of secondary metabolism (Afrin et al. 2015). For instance, in Arabidopsis thaliana L., the signalling metabolite N-hydroxypipecolic acid is produced in roots and transported to shoots, where it regulates systemic immunity and growth (Xu et al. 2025). Although analogous pathways have not yet been identified in Cannabis sativa L., these findings highlight the importance of root research for understanding plant metabolism, plant–microbe interactions, and cultivation outcomes.

Given the economic and medicinal relevance of Cannabis sativa L., elucidating the root-derived signalling networks is essential for optimising plant performance under diverse cultivation conditions. Addressing these knowledge gaps will require integrated multi-omics approaches, including transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics, isotope-based flux analysis, and metagenomics. These methods can help explain how signals move from roots to shoots and how they may affect glandular trichome development and secondary metabolite production. However, progress in this area is limited by several challenges. These include the lack of validated metabolite standards and incomplete spectral databases for accurate metabolite identification and measurement, difficulties in collecting root samples, instability of root-derived metabolites during extraction and processing, and limited ability to study changes in the rhizosphere across space and time. In addition, the shortage of integrated multi-omics datasets and the limited availability of reliable genetic and transformation tools make it difficult to confirm the function of systemic signalling pathways in Cannabis sativa L. In the near future, AI-based root phenotyping and targeted microbiome design are expected to support more reliable trait improvement, microbial engineering, and sustainable cultivation strategies (Tariq et al. 2025a, 2025b; Narisetti et al. 2026). Making root-focused research a central approach that connects plant physiology, biochemistry, biotechnology, applied microbiology, and genetic and metabolic engineering could help overcome current challenges in improving secondary metabolite production in Cannabis sativa L.
