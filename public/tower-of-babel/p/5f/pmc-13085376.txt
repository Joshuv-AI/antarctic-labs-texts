Residual inflammatory risk as a predictor of cardiometabolic multi-morbidity: results from three prospective cohorts
Authors: Shihai Wang, Xiaobin Zeng, Jianjun Lan, Shiyang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInflammation and its dynamic changes are intricately linked to cardiovascular diseases. This research aimed to explore the predictive significance of residual inflammatory risk (RIR) in evaluating the likelihood of developing cardiometabolic multi-morbidity (CMM).MethodsData were sourced from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), the Health and Retirement Study (HRS) and panzhihua central hospital. Inflammation status was assessed using at least two high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP) measurements taken at baseline and follow-up, with a minimum interval of four weeks. High RIR was characterized by an hsCRP level exceeding 2 mg/L. Participants were categorized into four groups: persistent high RIR, increased RIR (initially low, subsequently high hsCRP), attenuated RIR (initially high, subsequently low hsCRP), and persistent low RIR. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression and subgroup analyses were employed to examine the relationship between RIR and CMM.ResultsThe study included 5,572 participants from the CHARLS (56.26% female, mean age 57.87 years), 3,856 participants from the HRS (60.35% female, mean age 65.74 years), and 2,446 participants from the panzhihua central hospital (54.17% female, mean age 58.65 years). Utilizing Cox proportional hazards regression, participants with persistent high RIR exhibited a significant association with an increased risk of developing CMM compared to those with persistent low RIR (CHARLS: HR = 2.2, 95% CI = 1.79–2.69, P < 0.001; HRS: HR = 1.79, 95% CI = 1.47–2.18, P < 0.001; panzhihua cohort: HR = 3.97, 95% CI = 2.52–6.27, P < 0.001). This association persisted after comprehensive adjustment for potential confounders (CHARLS: HR = 1.81, 95% CI = 1.47–2.23, P < 0.001; HRS: HR = 1.69, 95% CI = 1.38–2.08, P < 0.001; panzhihua cohort: HR = 3.74, 95% CI = 2.34–5.99, P < 0.001). Subgroup analyses further confirmed the robustness of the association between persistent high RIR and CMM across all examined subgroups.ConclusionPersistent high RIR was correlated with an increased risk of CMM. Future research is needed to determine whether anti-inflammatory strategies targeting RIR reduction could mitigate CMM risk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41043-026-01269-y.

Introduction

Cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) refers to the simultaneous occurrence of at least two cardiometabolic diseases (CMD), which encompass conditions such as diabetes, heart disease, and stroke [1, 2]. Those who have been diagnosed with CMM tend to have a heightened risk of mortality and experience a markedly shorter life expectancy compared to individuals with a singular CMD [3]. The global prevalence of CMM is on the rise, presenting substantial challenges to healthcare systems due to its intricate management requirements and the associated high rates of morbidity and mortality [4, 5]. Identifying modifiable indicators for predicting CMM and facilitating early intervention are essential strategies in addressing this issue.

High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP), an indicator of inflammation, has been closely associated with cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) [6]. For instance, a study conducted by Lee et al. indicated that an early rise in serum hsCRP could serve as an independent predictor for the occurrence of CVDs and all-cause mortality [7]. In a cohort study involving 387 patients with schizophrenia, an hsCRP level of ≥ 2.13 mg/L was identified as an independent predictor of elevated cardiovascular risk [8]. However, most prior studies have focused on single-time point hsCRP measurements as predictors of individual cardiometabolic diseases, such as myocardial infarction or stroke [7, 8]. Less attention has been given to how sustained inflammatory activity over time contributes to the accumulation of multiple cardiometabolic conditions—i.e., multimorbidity. Residual inflammatory risk (RIR) is an emerging concept that captures persistent, low-grade inflammation despite standard risk factor management. It is typically defined as hsCRP ≥ 2 mg/L, a threshold supported by cardiovascular risk stratification guidelines [9, 10]. Unlike transient inflammatory spikes, RIR reflects chronic, subclinical inflammation that may continuously promote endothelial dysfunction, metabolic dysregulation, and plaque instability [11–13]. While RIR has been linked to adverse outcomes in patients with established CVD [9, 10, 14], its role in the development of cardiometabolic multimorbidity in general populations remains poorly understood. Importantly, studying RIR in a multimorbidity framework offers added clinical and mechanistic value. First, CMM represents a more severe disease burden than single conditions and is driven by overlapping pathological pathways, where chronic inflammation may act as a common biological driver [15]. Second, a multimorbidity perspective aligns with real-world clinical complexity, where patients often present with multiple interrelated conditions rather than isolated diseases. Third, dynamic assessment of RIR (e.g., persistent vs. transient elevation) may better capture longitudinal inflammatory exposure, which could be more informative for predicting multimorbidity progression than a single baseline measurement [16, 17]. Despite this rationale, several key gaps remain: (1) the prevalence of persistent high RIR among middle-aged and older adults in China and Amerian, as well as its association with CMM are not well characterized; (2) whether RIR trajectories predict the clustering of cardiometabolic conditions (i.e., CMM) beyond single-disease events is unclear; and (3) evidence from diverse cohorts, including both Western and Asian populations, is limited. Therefore, the present study aimed to: (1) Determine the prevalence of persistent high RIR in three prospective cohorts (CHARLS, HRS, and a hospital-based Chinese cohort). (2) Investigate whether persistent high RIR is associated with an increased risk of developing CMM, independent of traditional risk factors. (3) Explore whether this association is consistent across key demographic and clinical subgroups.

We hypothesized that individuals with persistent high RIR would have a significantly higher risk of CMM compared to those with persistent low RIR, and that this association would be observed across diverse populations.

.

Materials and methods

Ethics committee statement

The CHARLS study was granted approval by the Ethical Review Committee at Peking University (IRB00001052–11,015), and all participants provided written informed consent during their enrollment. Similarly, the Research Ethics Review Board of the University of Michigan approved the HRS protocols, which also involved acquiring informed written consent from all participants. The panzhihua cohort was approved by the Review Board at Panzhihua Central Hospital (Sichuan, Panzhihua, China), and it complied with the principles set forth in the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was secured in writing from all participants.

Study population

CHARLS and HRS are two nationally representative cohorts conducted in individuals aged 45 years and older in China and in individuals over the age of 50 years in the United States, respectively. The detailed methodologies of both cohorts have been previously documented in the literature [18, 19]. For the present study, baseline data were derived from wave 1 (2011) of CHARLS and wave 8 (2006) of HRS. The residual inflammatory risk (RIR) was assessed using data from wave 1 (2011) and wave 3 (2015) of CHARLS, as well as wave 8 (2006) and wave 10 (2010) of HRS. Subsequent follow-up surveys were employed to monitor the incidence of CMM until the final follow-up surveys, namely wave 4 to wave 5 for CHARLS and wave 11 to wave 15 for HRS. The Panzhihua cohort consists of individuals who undergo routine physical examinations at the Physical Examination Department of Panzhihua Central Hospital between March 2014 and July 2019. Participants were retested for hsCRP 1 month after inclusion. We conducted follow-up every 3 months through outpatient interview or by telephone contact. Participants were excluded based on the following criteria: (1) presence of CMM at baseline or incomplete information on new CMM incidence during follow-up; (2) lack of complete data on RIR; (3) age below 45 years or missing age data; and (4) loss to follow-up. Ultimately, the study included a total of 5,572 individuals from CHARLS, 3,856 individuals from HRS, and 2,446 individuals from the panzhihua cohort (Fig. 1).

Definition of RIR

Participants were divided into four groups based on a cut-off level of hsCRP > 2 mg/L: (1) persistent high RIR: high values of hsCRP in both laboratory tests; (2) attenuated RIR: high hsCRP in the first test, and low hsCRP in the second test; (3) increased RIR: low hsCRP in the first test, and high hsCRP in the second test; and (4) persistent low RIR: low hsCRP values in both tests. The classification methodology employed in this study was selected based on established cardiovascular risk stratification guidelines and prior RIR studies, ensuring consistency with existing literature and facilitating cross-cohort comparison [10, 17].

Clinical outcomes

Cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) was defined as the concurrent presence of at least two of the following three cardiometabolic diseases: diabetes, heart disease, and stroke. These conditions were selected because they represent major cardiometabolic disease entities with shared inflammatory and metabolic pathways, high clinical burden, and validated ascertainment methods in large cohort studies. Incident CMM was determined at the time of diagnosis of the second qualifying condition. Participants with any CMD at baseline were excluded to ensure the analysis captured new multimorbidity development. Disease status was ascertained as follows:

Heart disease and stroke: Based on self-report of physician diagnosis using standardized questionnaires (e.g., “Has a doctor ever told you that you have coronary heart disease, heart attack, angina, heart failure, or stroke?”) [20].

Diabetes: Defined by self-reported diagnosis, fasting plasma glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, random glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L, HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, or use of glucose-lowering medication, consistent with ADA criteria [21].

Ascertainment of covariates

We gathered demographic details, anthropometric data, behavioral traits, health status, and laboratory test results as potential covariates that might impact the relationship between persistent high RIR and CMM. Demographic variables comprised age, sex, educational level (categorized as primary school or lower, and middle school or higher), and marital status (classified as married or others). Anthropometric assessments included the calculation of body mass index (BMI), defined as BMI (kg/m2) = weight (kg) / height2 (m2). Behavioral characteristics encompassed smoking and drinking. Smoking status was classified as “No” for individuals who had never smoked and “Yes” for those who were former or current smokers. Drinking behavior was categorized as “No” or “Yes”, depending on whether the participant had consumed alcohol within the preceding year. Health status indicators included hypertension, diabetes, heart disease, and stroke. Participants were classified as having hypertension if their systolic blood pressure (SBP) was ≥ 140 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure (DBP) was ≥ 90 mmHg, they reported a history of hypertension, or they were currently undergoing treatment with antihypertensive medications. The biochemical parameters evaluated included a range of crucial health indicators, including glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c), total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP).

Statistical methods

Statistical analyses in this research were conducted using RStudio version 4.2.1. A significance threshold was set at bilateral p-values below 0.05. Continuous variables were represented in two distinct forms: either as the mean ± standard deviation or as the median along with the interquartile range, contingent upon the normal distribution of the data. Categorical data were clearly presented in terms of numbers and percentages to ensure clarity in interpretation. To compare demographic and clinical characteristics across various subgroups, different statistical methods were employed. One-way analysis of variance was utilized for data that followed a normal distribution, while the Kruskal-Wallis test was used for those that did not adhere to normality. For categorical data, the chi-squared test was applied to facilitate comparisons. The cumulative incidence of CMM was evaluated using the Kaplan–Meier method, with comparisons made through the log-rank test. Furthermore, Cox proportional hazards regression was chosen to model time-to-CMM development, given the prospective design and censored nature of the data. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals and was met. Three sequential models were fitted: Model 1 (unadjusted), Model 2 (adjusted for age and sex), and Model 3 (further adjusted for hypertension, smoking, drinking, marital status, education, and total cholesterol). These covariates were selected based on prior literature and their association with inflammation and cardiometabolic outcomes in exploratory analyses. Subgroup analyses were exploratory in nature and were conducted to assess the consistency of the association between RIR and CMM across key demographic and clinical factors. No adjustment for multiple comparisons was made in subgroup analyses, given their hypothesis-generating nature. Competing risks (e.g., death before CMM) were not explicitly modeled, as the focus was on first CMM incidence and non-cardiometabolic mortality was low during follow-up. Given differences in follow-up duration, sampling frames, and population characteristics across CHARLS, HRS, and the Panzhihua cohort, all analyses were performed separately for each cohort rather than pooling data. This preserves cohort-specific context and avoids inappropriate homogenization. Consistency of findings across cohorts was interpreted as supporting generalizability, rather than through formal meta-analysis. Sensitivity analyses were conducted within each cohort to assess robustness to exclusion of baseline conditions.

Results

Characteristics of study population

The participant screening process is illustrated in Fig. 1. The mean age of participants were 57.87 ± 8.67 years in the CHARLS cohort, 65.74 ± 9.51 years in the HRS cohort, and 58.65 ± 9.10 in the panzhihua cohort (Tables 1, 2 and 3). In all cohorts, the proportion of female participants exceeded that of male participants, with females comprising 56.26% of the CHARLS cohort, 60.35% of the HRS cohort, and 54.17% of the panzhihua cohort. Within the CHARLS cohort, 3,087 participants (55.4%) exhibited persistent low RIR, 605 (10.9%) showed attenuated RIR, 1,112 (20.0%) demonstrated increased RIR, and 768 (13.7%) had persistent high RIR. In the HRS cohort, 1,551 participants (40.2%) had persistent low RIR, 520 (13.5%) exhibited attenuated RIR, 454 (11.8%) showed increased RIR, and 1,331 (34.5%) had persistent high RIR. In the panzhihua cohort, 1,284 participants (52.5%) had persistent low RIR, 266 (10.9%) exhibited attenuated RIR, and 527 (21.5%) showed increased RIR, and 369 (15.1%) had persistent high RIR.

Association of RIR groups with CMM

In the CHARLS, the distribution of CMM events across the different RIR categories was as follows: 278 events (9.01%) in the persistent low RIR group, 68 events (11.24%) in the attenuated RIR group, 134 events (12.05%) in the increased RIR group, and 139 events (18.10%) in the persistent high RIR group (Table 1). In the HRS, the incidence of CMM was 172 (11.09%) in the persistent low RIR group, 76 (14.62%) in the attenuated RIR group, 59 (13%) in the increased RIR group, and 243 (18.26%) in the persistent high RIR group (Table 2). In the panzhihua cohort, the incidence of CMM was 36 (2.8%) in the persistent low RIR group, 18 (6.77%) in the attenuated RIR group, 32 (6.07%) in the increased RIR group, and 38 (10.3%) in the persistent high RIR group (Table 3). In absolute terms, the cumulative incidence of CMM in the persistent high RIR group exceeded that in the persistent low RIR group by 9.09% in CHARLS, 7.17% in HRS, and 7.50% in the Panzhihua cohort. This translates to a substantial relative increase in risk and underscores the population-level impact of sustained inflammation, suggesting that a significant proportion of CMM cases could be associated with persistent high RIR. The cumulative incidence of CMM was evaluated utilizing the Kaplan-Meier survival curve. As depicted in Fig. 2, there was a progressive increase in the cumulative incidence of CMM from the persistent low RIR group to the persistent high RIR group among all cohorts (log-rank = 61, P < 0.001 for CHARLS; log-rank = 36, P < 0.001 for HRS; (log-rank = 20, P < 0.001 for panzhihua cohort).

Table 4 provides a comprehensive summary of the association between the four distinct RIR groups and the risk of CMM, as analyzed using the Cox proportional hazards regression model. In the CHARLS, individuals with persistent high RIR demonstrated a markedly increased risk of CMM compared to those with persistent low RIR across all models: model 1 (HR = 2.2, 95% CI = 1.79–2.69, P < 0.001), model 2 (HR = 2.11, 95% CI = 1.72–2.59, P < 0.001), and model 3 (HR = 1.81, 95% CI = 1.47–2.23, P < 0.001). In the HRS, similar findings were observed, with participants in the persistent high RIR group demonstrating an elevated risk of CMM, both without and with adjustment for confounding variables: model 1 (HR = 1.79, 95% CI = 1.47–2.18, P < 0.001) and model 3 (HR = 1.69, 95% CI = 1.38–2.08, P < 0.001). Similarly, in the panzhihua cohort, we also observed that individuals in the persistent high RIR group had an increased risk of CMM occurrence than those with persistent low RIR (model 1: HR = 3.97, 95% CI = 2.52–6.27, P < 0.001; model 3: HR = 3.74, 95% CI = 2.34–5.99, P < 0.001). Notably, the hazard ratios for persistent high RIR indicated a 1.8- to 3.7-fold increase in the risk of developing CMM compared to persistent low RIR, even after full adjustment for potential confounders. This gradient of risk—strongest in the hospital-based Panzhihua cohort—was consistently observed across all three cohorts, reinforcing the robustness of the association.

Subgroup analyses

In this study, subgroup analyses combined with interaction tests were performed to assess whether the association between four distinct RIR groups and the risk of CMM was influenced by potential risk factors. The stratification factors considered included age (< 60 years, ≥ 60 years), sex (female, male), hypertension (no, yes), education (Primary school or lower, Middle school or higher), marital status (no, yes), smoking (no, yes), and drinking (no, yes). As illustrated in Figs. 3, 4 and 5, individuals with persistent high RIR demonstrated an elevated risk of developing CMM across all subgroups compared to those with persistent low RIR (P < 0.05), with the exception of individuals with a primary school education or lower in the HRS cohort and unmarried individuals in the panzhihua cohort. In the CHARLS cohort, a significant interaction was observed in the subgroup analyses for sex. Specifically, in the male subgroup, the hazard ratio (HR) was 2.6 (95% CI: 2.01–3.37) for persistent high RIR vs. persistent low RIR, whereas in the female subgroup, the HR was 1.68 (95% CI: 1.19–2.36) for persistent high RIR vs. persistent low RIR (Fig. 3). This suggests that the association between persistent high RIR and CMM may be more pronounced in men within this population, a finding that warrants further investigation in light of differing lifestyle and risk factor profiles. In the HRS cohort, a significant interaction between hypertension and RIR was observed concerning the risk of CMM development (Fig. 4). Participants with persistent high RIR exhibited higher HR value in the non-hypertension group compared to those in the hypertension group.

Sensitivity analyses

To verify the strength of our results, we conducted several sensitivity analyses. Within the CHARLS cohort, participants with pre-existing heart disease at baseline (N = 497) were excluded, yet the association between RIR groups and CMM persisted (persistent high RIR vs. persistent low RIR: HR = 1.98, 95% CI = 1.56–2.52) (Supplementary file, Table S1). Similarly, the exclusion of participants with diabetes at baseline (N = 667) did not significantly alter the association (persistent high RIR vs. persistent low RIR: HR = 1.68, 95% CI = 1.31–2.16) (Supplementary file, Table S1). A comparable association was observed upon excluding participants with a history of stroke at baseline (persistent high RIR vs. persistent low RIR: HR = 1.80, 95% CI = 1.46–2.23) (Supplementary file, Table S1). Furthermore, when participants with either heart disease, diabetes, or stroke at baseline (N = 1230) were excluded, the results remained consistent with the primary analysis (persistent high RIR vs. persistent low RIR: HR = 1.65, 95% CI = 1.18–2.30) (Supplementary file, Table S1). The same analytical approach was applied to the HRS and panzhihua cohort, yielding analogous results (Supplementary file, Table S2-S3).

Discussion

In this study, we conducted an extensive longitudinal analysis that utilized three different datasets to assess the relationship between RIR groups and the risk of CMM. Our results revealed a significant correlation between consistently high RIR levels and an elevated risk of CMM. Subgroups analysis indicated that individuals with persistent high RIR were at a greater risk of developing CMM across all subgroups compared to those with persistent low RIR. Furthermore, sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of the connection between persistent high RIR and CMM. Notably, the hazard ratios for persistent high RIR were higher in the hospital-based Panzhihua cohort than in the population-based CHARLS and HRS cohorts. This may reflect selection effects, as individuals undergoing routine health checks may differ in health awareness, baseline inflammatory status, or access to care. Additionally, more frequent follow-up in the Panzhihua cohort (every 3 months) could have led to more sensitive outcome detection. Despite these differences, the direction and significance of the association were consistent across all three cohorts, supporting the generalizability of the link between persistent high RIR and CMM risk. The absolute risk increase of CMM associated with persistent high RIR (approximately 7–9% across cohorts) is clinically meaningful, especially given the rising prevalence of multimorbidity and the potential for anti-inflammatory interventions to modify this risk.

High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP) belongs to an acute-phase protein that is produced by hepatic aortic endothelial cells and coronary artery smooth muscle cells. Our findings align with and extend prior work linking systemic inflammation to cardiometabolic health [7, 22, 23]. Previous studies have established single-time point hsCRP as a predictor of individual cardiovascular diseases like myocardial infarction or heart failure [7, 24]. For instance, a comprehensive nationwide prospective cohort study conducted by Xu et al. revealed that elevated levels of hsCRP were significantly correlated with an increased risk of CMM [25]. In a study involving 225 asymptomatic diabetic patients, hsCRP was found to correlate with silent myocardial ischemia [26]. Furthermore, a systematic review indicated that hsCRP is linked to the incidence of HF in both general and high-risk populations [24]. However, a key distinction of our study lies in its focus on inflammatory persistence—operationalized as RIR assessed at two time points—rather than a single measurement. This approach aligns with emerging evidence that inflammatory trajectories are more informative than baseline values alone [16, 17]. While our results converge with the broader theme of inflammation as a risk factor, they specifically suggest that the chronicity of low-grade inflammation may be particularly relevant for the clustering of multiple conditions, a hypothesis less explored in prior multimorbidity research focusing on static biomarkers.

The concept of residual inflammatory risk (RIR) has gained significant attention in recent years, particularly in the context of primary and secondary prevention of cardiovascular diseases [17, 27]. The high RIR was usually defined as an hsCRP ≥ 2 mg/L. Previous clinical trials have reported the prevalence of elevated RIR to range from 29% to 37% [28, 29]. In our research, we found that the proportion of individuals with elevated RIR was 24.6%. A retrospective cohort analysis involving 7026 patients who underwent PCI revealed that persistent high RIR correlated with higher rates of all-cause mortality and myocardial infarction [10]. Nevertheless, the aforementioned study was retrospective in nature with a follow-up duration limited to one year, thereby constraining the ability to establish a causal relationship. In contrast, the prospective design and extended follow-up period of our study significantly enhance the reliability of our findings. Roland et al. demonstrated that persistent high hsCRP after acute coronary syndrome was linked to the greatest risk of a combination of adverse outcomes (myocardial infarction, clinically necessary coronary revascularization, or cerebrovascular incidents) [9]. Their study was limited to 1,209 participants with a restricted follow-up period. In contrast, our research incorporated a larger, multi-center cohort, thereby enhancing the reliability and generalizability of our findings. Additionally, a prospective study involving 4562 patients diagnosed with coronary artery disease suggested that persistent high RIR was linked to an increased risk of all-cause mortality and ischemic episodes [16]. The endpoint events defined in their study were limited to cardiovascular and cerebrovascular occurrences. In contrast, our research expanded the evaluation of inflammatory risk beyond the prediction of conventional cardiovascular events to encompass the more intricate and burdensome clinical outcome characterized by the concurrent presence of diabetes, coronary heart disease, and stroke. This study offers significant population-based evidence supporting “inflammation” as a potentially targetable common pathway for cardiometabolic multimorbidity, thereby suggesting novel directions for future research endeavors. Importantly, we similarly observed that persistent high RIR was correlated with an increased risk of CMM among middle-aged and elderly populations in China and American, thereby corroborating the findings of previous research. It is noteworthy that investigations into the predictive capability of RIR for CMM risk have been limited, as earlier studies have primarily concentrated on individual cardiovascular diseases. This research is the first to examine the relationship between RIR and the likelihood of developing CMM by utilizing two nationally representative prospective cohorts.

Cardiometabolic multimorbidity is increasingly recognized as a significant public health issue [30]. Prior studies have identified a variety of risk factors linked to CMM. For instance, a cohort study conducted by Lu et al. identified several independent predictors of CMM, including waist-to-height ratio, waist circumference, waist divided by height to the power of 0.5, and body mass index (BMI) [31]. Nevertheless, the study’s cross-sectional design poses limitations on establishing causative relationships. In contrast, the prospective framework of our research bolsters the strength and dependability of the results. Additionally, a study by Guo et al. reported an association between impaired fasting glucose and CMM [32]. Nevertheless, the limitation of their research stemmed from the exclusion of individuals with diabetes mellitus and cardiovascular conditions at the onset of the study, which potentially restricted the applicability of their conclusions to the wider population. Our study revealed a correlation between persistent high RIR and CMM risk in the broader population, regardless of diabetes mellitus and cardiovascular disease status. Furthermore, Liu et al. identified both the visceral adiposity index and handgrip strength as the predictors of CMM [33]. However, their study did not include sensitivity analyses based on different types of CMDs, which might have provided more detailed insights. In contrast, our sensitivity analysis demonstrated a consistent link between persistent high RIR and CMM across populations that excluded heart disease, stroke, diabetes, or any CMD at baseline.

The correlation between RIR groups and CMM was significantly moderated by sex in the CHARLS. Specifically, individuals with increased RIR and persistent high RIR showed increased risk of CMM within male group compared female group. Notably, male participants exhibited higher rates of smoking and alcohol consumption than female in the present study (P < 0.05). Prior research has established a link between smoking and alcohol consumption and an increased risk of cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) [34, 35], which may partially explain the heightened risk of CMM observed among male participants. Besides, the hypertensive status also affects the relationship between RIR and the risk of LVA in the HRS. One possible explanation is that hypertensive participants are more prone to receiving treatments such as beta blockers, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors/angiotensin receptor blockers, and medications to lower lipid levels, which could aid in preventing CVDs.

The present study employs data from the CHARLS, HRS, and panzhihua cohorts, three large-scale prospective cohorts. Our study effectively controlled for confounding variables and assessed the relationship between RIR groups and the risk of CMM, thereby enhancing the credibility of the findings. Nevertheless, several constraints should be acknowledged when evaluating these findings. First, the primary focus was on individuals aged 45 years and older in China and the United States, which could restrict the applicability of the results to younger demographic groups and those from various racial backgrounds. Second, the CMM diagnosis relied on self-reported evaluations from physicians and could not be further stratified by specific etiology (e.g., ischemic vs. non-ischemic), which might introduce misclassification or information bias, despite the use of standardized questionnaires common in large cohort studies. Although objective verification (e.g., medical record review) was not feasible for the entire cohort, this method has been employed in numerous prior publications from CHARLS and HRS. Future studies with adjudicated clinical endpoints would be valuable to confirm our findings. Third, it is crucial to emphasize that our study design is observational. While we report a strong and consistent association between persistent high RIR and incident CMM across three distinct cohorts, this finding does not establish a causal relationship. Besides, our study did not include additional inflammatory markers or pathway-specific biomarkers; thus, the biological mechanisms underlying the RIR–CMM association remain to be elucidated in future mechanistic studies. Fourth, despite adjusting for a range of covariates, residual confounding cannot be ruled out. Factors not consistently measured across all cohorts—such as detailed medication use (e.g., statins, anti-inflammatory drugs such as colchicine, interleukin-1β inhibitors, or high-dose corticosteroids), dietary patterns, physical activity, psychosocial stress, and environmental exposures—may influence both inflammation and CMM risk. Future studies should attempt to collect and incorporate these variables. Fifth, RIR was defined using only two hsCRP measurements, which may not fully capture longer-term inflammatory variability or more dynamic patterns. Future studies with more frequent serial hsCRP assessments could further refine the classification of persistent inflammatory risk. Sixth, while we used a standardized hsCRP > 2 mg/L threshold, biomarker variability across assay platforms and intra-individual biological fluctuation could lead to some misclassification of RIR status. Seventh, subgroup analyses were not adjusted for multiple comparisons and should be interpreted as exploratory.

Emerging evidence suggests that artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) approaches may enhance risk stratification for complex conditions such as osteoporosis and cardiovascular diseases by integrating multi-dimensional clinical and biomarker data. For instance, AI models have been employed to diagnose osteoporosis based on clinical risk factors and to recommend tailored exercise protocols [36]. Future research could explore integrating longitudinal inflammatory biomarkers (e.g., hsCRP trajectories) with AI/ML algorithms to develop more precise risk prediction tools for cardiometabolic multimorbidity. Such integrative models may facilitate early identification of high-risk individuals and inform targeted interventions, including lifestyle modifications and anti-inflammatory therapies.

Conclusion

The present study revealed a significant association between persistent high residual inflammatory risk (RIR) and an increased risk of cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) across three prospective cohorts. These findings extend prior research on inflammatory risk by focusing on the clinically relevant outcome of multimorbidity, defined as the co-occurrence of diabetes, heart disease, and stroke. However, due to the observational nature of our study, this association does not establish causality. Important considerations include the implications of measurement limitations (e.g., self-reported outcomes, two-time-point RIR assessment), potential residual confounding, and cohort heterogeneity. Therefore, our results highlight persistent high RIR as a promising and consistent risk marker associated with CMM development, forming a basis for a risk stratification hypothesis. Clinical translation or application is not yet warranted. Future research must prioritize: (1) external validation of this association in additional, diverse populations; (2) longitudinal studies with more frequent biomarker assessments to better characterize inflammatory trajectories and their variability; and most critically, (3) large-scale, randomized controlled trials to investigate whether anti-inflammatory interventions that lower RIR can effectively reduce the incidence of CMM and alter multimorbidity trajectories. The biological pathways linking sustained inflammation to multimorbidity also require further elucidation in mechanistic studies.
