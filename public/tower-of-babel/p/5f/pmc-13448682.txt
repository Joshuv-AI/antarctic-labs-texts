gKFA: a geometric framework for kurtosis fractional anisotropy
Authors: Sumit Kaushik, Avinash Bansal, Jan Kubíček, Martin Haváček, Amit Khairnar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diffusion kurtosis imaging (DKI) extends diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) by incorporating non-Gaussian diffusion effects through a fourth-order kurtosis tensor. In this work, we investigate the three-dimensional geometric structure of the kurtosis tensor and introduce a geometry-inspired anisotropy descriptor based on mapped symmetric second-order quadratic forms. This representation enables kurtosis anisotropy to be studied through lower-order tensors whose geometry is easier to analyze and whose positive definiteness can be assessed more conveniently. Based on this framework, we define geometric kurtosis fractional anisotropy (gKFA) as the geometric mean of the fractional anisotropies computed from the mapped quadratic forms. The proposed descriptor was evaluated using simulations, physical phantoms, and in vivo human and rodent brain data. In simulations and phantom experiments, gKFA showed sensitivity to directional heterogeneity and tissue-complexity changes that were only weakly reflected by conventional KFA. In the human brain dataset, additional analyses compared gKFA with conventional kurtosis fractional anisotropy (KFA), mean kurtosis (MK), axial kurtosis (AK), radial kurtosis (RK), and anisotropy measures derived from the mapped quadratic forms. These results indicate that gKFA behaves as a kurtosis-anisotropy descriptor that is closely related, but not identical, to KFA, and distinct from standard scalar kurtosis measures such as MK, AK, and RK. In both human and rodent data, gKFA showed better tissue contrast and complementary behavior relative to KFA, including sensitivity to regional differences in the rodent brain.

Introduction

Compared to diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), diffusion kurtosis imaging (DKI) incorporates non-Gaussian diffusion effects by introducing a kurtosis term into the Taylor series expansion of the diffusion-weighted signal1–4. This non-Gaussian component captures deviations from Gaussian diffusion caused by tissue heterogeneity, cellular barriers, and complex microstructural organization. As a result, DKI provides access to additional tissue microstructure descriptors that are not available from second-order diffusion models alone. Due to this increased sensitivity, DKI has been shown to be valuable for detecting and monitoring microstructural alterations associated with a range of neurological and neurodegenerative disorders5–10. Several scalar metrics have been derived from the kurtosis tensor to summarize non-Gaussian diffusion behavior, including mean kurtosis (MK), axial kurtosis (AK), radial kurtosis (RK), and kurtosis fractional anisotropy (KFA).

Among these, kurtosis fractional anisotropy (KFA) is commonly used to characterize the directional variability of diffusional kurtosis. While fractional anisotropy (FA) derived from DTI summarizes anisotropy of Gaussian diffusion based on second-order tensors, KFA reflects anisotropy of non-Gaussian diffusion encoded by the fourth-order kurtosis tensor. Thus, both FA and KFA summarize directional properties, but they arise from fundamentally different diffusion models and capture complementary aspects of the diffusion structure.

Despite its widespread use, FA has shown ambiguous behavior in several clinical and experimental settings. In particular, FA values can be influenced by multiple factors, including fiber crossings, partial volume effects, and proportional changes in tensor eigenvalues, which can reduce its specificity to microstructural alterations. For example, studies that examine FA changes in the substantia nigra (SN) have reported inconsistent findings between cohorts and disease stages11–14.

Interpretation of FA becomes especially challenging in white matter regions with complex fiber configurations15. Because FA is a relative measure of eigenvalue dispersion, microstructural tissue changes, such as demyelination or axonal loss, may lead to an increase, decrease, or no measurable change in FA, depending on how the eigenvalues are affected. This limitation is less pronounced in highly coherent fiber bundles, such as the corpus callosum, the corticospinal tract, and the spinal cord, but remains problematic in regions with crossed or fanning fibers.

However, metrics derived from the kurtosis tensor have demonstrated improved sensitivity to microstructural alterations in gray matter and deep gray matter structures. For example, a rodent study16 showed that mean kurtosis was more sensitive than FA to microstructural changes in the substantia nigra and was able to detect early-stage Parkinson’s disease. Subsequent work further demonstrated that MK exhibits more pronounced differences in the major regions of the brain compared to FA17.

KFA has also been reported to exhibit elevated values in deep gray matter regions such as the thalamus and lenticular nucleus, where FA typically indicates low anisotropy18. In addition, KFA reductions have been associated with increased tissue density in tumors, aging-related changes, and myelin damage19,20. In the context of carbon monoxide poisoning, KFA was shown to be sensitive to white matter injury and microstructural damage in both white matter and gray matter, demonstrating greater diagnostic precision and stronger correlations with neuropsychiatric scores than other kurtosis-derived metrics21.

Despite these advantages, the kurtosis tensor possesses a higher number of degrees of freedom than the diffusion tensor22. This increased dimensionality makes kurtosis-derived quantities harder to analyze and more sensitive to estimation instability, particularly in heterogeneous tissues and under low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) conditions. Rather than addressing noise alone, the present work is motivated by the need to investigate the three-dimensional geometric structure of the kurtosis tensor and to derive a complementary anisotropy descriptor from lower-order geometric representations. We introduce a geometric framework that interprets the fourth-order kurtosis tensor in terms of mapped second-order quadratic forms. These quadratic forms visually approximate the dominant directional structure of the kurtosis (Orientation Distribution Function) ODF, as their principal axes tend to align with the ODF maxima observed in the visualizations, while providing a stable and interpretable geometric representation of kurtosis anisotropy. Based on this framework, we propose a descriptor, geometric kurtosis fractional anisotropy (gKFA), derived from the anisotropy of the mapped quadratic forms. We systematically evaluated the behavior of gKFA using numerical simulations, physical phantom experiments, and in vivo datasets from human and rodent brains. These analyzes included comparisons with conventional KFA and standard DKI metrics such as MK, AK, and RK. Our results indicate that gKFA is closely related, but not identical, to conventional KFA, and behaves more like a kurtosis-anisotropy descriptor than like a scalar kurtosis measure. In the human dataset, gKFA showed strong white-matter–gray-matter contrast and complementary behavior relative to KFA, while in the rodent data it showed sensitivity to regional tissue differences.

Because positive definiteness is more difficult to assess directly for fourth-order tensors than for second-order matrices, we adopt the representation proposed by Song et al.23, which maps the fourth-order kurtosis tensor to a set of symmetric second-order forms. This mapping exploits the structure of the kurtosis tensor when evaluated along diffusion gradient directions and allows sufficient positivity conditions to be assessed through lower-order tensors that are easier to interpret and analyze.

In diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), FA is derived from the eigenvalues of the second-order diffusion tensor and provides a rotationally invariant measure of the dispersion of eigenvalues. Kurtosis fractional anisotropy (KFA) is defined analogously at the level of the fourth-order kurtosis tensor as a normalized Frobenius-norm deviation from the isotropic kurtosis tensor, and is rotationally invariant under orthogonal changes of coordinate system. Biophysical interpretation of the mapped quadratics. The three mapped quadratic forms, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{p}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{q}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{r}$$\end{document}, are not arbitrary algebraic constructs, but direction-conditioned projections of the fourth-order kurtosis tensor evaluated on the unit sphere. Each quadratic captures how non-Gaussian diffusion varies along distinct geometric combinations of spatial directions, thereby decomposing the kurtosis tensor into complementary second-order representations. From a biophysical perspective, these quadratic forms reflect anisotropy in higher-order diffusion moments associated with orientation dispersion and compartmental heterogeneity. Although the 2nd order diffusion tensor describes the second moment of molecular displacement, the kurtosis tensor characterizes deviations from Gaussian diffusion caused by barriers, restrictions, and structural complexity. Experiments

Angular resolution

We used the adaptive kernel method24,25 to estimate ODFs for the second and fourth-order tensors that represent kurtosis. The experiment used a diffusion gradient strength b=1500 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s/mm^2$$\end{document} with 81 gradient directions.

Fig. 1 a) shows a configuration in which two linear fibers intersect a curved fiber, forming three (heterogeneous) intersections. In three regions, the maxima of ODFs for fourth-order tensors do not align with the underlying fiber directions. Therefore, a proper rotation is usually needed for alignment. However, Fig. 1 b) shows a DTI reconstruction. The principal directions of the corresponding second-order tensors resolve the ground truth direction in regions where only one fiber is present or where no fiber is present (background). Figs. 1 c), d) and e) represent the second-order (ellipsoid) reconstruction of the quadratics. In the crossing regions, the principal directions of the quadratics p and q follow the actual path of the underlying fibers.

Diffusion-weighted signals were simulated for two-fiber configurations at b = 0, 1000, 2000, and 3000 s/mm² using 33 uniformly distributed gradient directions. The ground-truth fibers were modeled with angle differences ranging from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0^{\circ }$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$90^{\circ }$$\end{document}. Appropriate eigenvalues were selected and DKI fitting was performed following a work26, assuming equal volume fractions.

Angular differences are computed from the principal eigenvectors of the tensors p and q. Figure 2 shows the estimated angles (y-axis) against the ground truth fiber angles (x-axis), along with the identity line x = y. The plot indicates that the estimation errors are lower for both small and large crossing angles. These results improve on a previous study, which estimated directions by computing the ODFs maxima27. That showed a reasonable estimate only for angles above \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$55^{\circ }$$\end{document} and failed for the set of angles below \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$35^{\circ }$$\end{document}.

Sensitivity to Kurtosis Orientation

The microstructure of the tissue affects the direction dependence of diffusional kurtosis (non-Gaussian). To compare how scalar metrics from quadratics, and KFA respond to orientation changes in the kurtosis component, we used a setup with two crossing fibers. Together, these fibers are aligned with the x-axis at increasing angles \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0^{\circ }-90^{\circ }$$\end{document} as shown in Figs. 3 a) and b), the respective line plots, depicting their sensitivity. gKFA and FAs of quadratic forms are sensitive to orientation change of kurtosis, behave as a geometry-sensitive anisotropy metric, unlike conventional KFA.

Kurtosis metrics and correlations

Figure 11 shows FA and kurtosis-derived metric maps from in vivo human brain data. The FA map exhibits dark, curved bands (shown by a red arrow) in regions of heterogeneous white matter, which arise because FA, under the Gaussian diffusion assumption, tends to treat crossing or complex fiber configurations as more isotropic. As a result, anisotropy can be underrepresented in these regions. Both gKFA and KFA show elevated values in such areas, consistent with increased directional variability of non-Gaussian diffusion associated with fiber crossings. The gKFA map follows the overall spatial pattern of KFA but appears with higher contrast, suggesting complementary sensitivity to directional heterogeneity in such regions.

For the in vivo noise robustness experiment, we used the previously described human cfin_multib dataset. Prior to the addition of synthetic Rician noise, we applied brain masking using the DIPY median_otsu procedure (vol_idx=[0,1], median_radius=4, numpass=2, autocrop=True, dilate=1). We then restricted the analysis to the b=0, 1000, 2000, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$3000\ \mathrm {s/mm}^{2}$$\end{document} shells and used a subsampled voxel mask for computational efficiency during fitting.

The baseline DKI fit was computed on this masked and shell-selected data set, and subsequently Rician noise was added to evaluate the SNR dependence of the correlations between the baseline and noisy estimates of KFA and gKFA. We applied no motion correction, eddy-current correction, Gibbs-ringing correction, denoising, smoothing, or additional spatial filtering before adding synthetic Rician noise. For illustration, gKFA and KFA maps at different SNR levels are shown in Fig. 12. Negative kurtosis values observed in conventional kurtosis-derived scalar maps arise primarily from noise sensitivity and the unconstrained estimation of the fourth-order kurtosis tensor.

The positivity of the fourth-order tensor W can in principle be assessed directly from its 15 independent coefficients through the quartic form \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W({\textbf{g}})$$\end{document}, but this requires checking the positivity in all directions and is therefore less straightforward than eigenvalue-based validity checks for the mapped quadratic forms.

To analyze tissue-specific correlations, three tissue classes were defined according to FA values: gray matter (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0 \le \textrm{FA} \le 0.3$$\end{document}), white matter (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{FA} \ge 0.6$$\end{document}), and intermediate tissues comprising the remaining voxels.

Figure 13 shows the Pearson correlation between the scalar maps computed from a reference in vivo human brain dataset and those obtained after adding synthetic Rician noise at increasing SNR levels. The reference data set corresponds to the original acquisition, while noisy datasets were generated by adding Rician noise with specified SNR values, where the noise scale was determined using the mean signal intensity of the volumes b=0 within the analysis mask. These results indicate that in this experimental setting, gKFA and KFA show tissue-dependent behavior, with gKFA performing more favorably in white matter.

Positive-definiteness under noise: To quantify the effect of noise on the tensor positive definiteness, we performed an SNR-dependent simulation using DKI-consistent synthetic signals with repeated Rician noise realizations. For each fitted tensor, positive definiteness of the mapped quadratic forms \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{p}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{q}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{r}$$\end{document} was assessed using their minimum eigenvalues, and positivity of the fourth-order tensor W was assessed numerically by evaluating the quartic form over a dense sampling of directions on the unit sphere.

Figure 15a shows that the frequency of non-positive-definite cases systematically decreased with increasing SNR. In particular, the proportion of numerically non-positive-definite tensors W decreased markedly from low to high SNR, while the proportion of cases in which at least one of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{p}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{q}$$\end{document}, or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{r}$$\end{document} was non-positive-definite also decreased, although it remained consistently higher than the corresponding rate for W. The conventional KFA remained numerically defined throughout the tested SNR range.

Individually mapped quadratic forms also showed distinct sensitivity to noise (Fig. 15b). Among them, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{r}$$\end{document} exhibited the highest non-positive-definite rate at moderate-to-high SNR, whereas the invalidity rates of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{p}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{q}$$\end{document} decreased more rapidly as SNR increased. These results indicate that non-positive-definite mapped quadratic forms are most common under low-SNR conditions, and support the use of explicit validity checks when computing gKFA.

Sensitivity to regional tissue differences

As an illustrative example of its behavior, we evaluated the sensitivity of gKFA to regional differences in brain tissue using a data set of control rodents previously reported in16. Such analyses may help assess whether gKFA captures spatial variations in tissue microstructure that could be relevant for studies of neurodegenerative changes.

For rodent regional analysis, the regions of interest (ROIs) were defined manually in native diffusion space following the procedure used in our previous study30. ROIs were selected in anatomically relevant regions involved in Parkinsonian pathology, namely the thalamus, substantia nigra, hippocampus, striatum and sensorimotor cortex. ROIs were delineated on the images b=0 with the aid of FA maps using ImageJ software, according to the Paxinos and Franklin mouse brain atlas31. No separate atlas registration step was applied; instead, ROIs were drawn directly on the diffusion data with reference to the anatomical atlas and the corresponding diffusion contrast. An atlas-guided illustration of the rodent ROI delineation procedure is provided in Supplementary Figure S1.

The sensitivity to metrics was examined in five anatomically and functionally relevant regions: thalamus (th), substantia nigra (sn), hippocampus (hc), striatum (str), and dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus (DMV). For each region, the mean scalar values were calculated and compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA).

ANOVA revealed a marginal overall effect for conventional KFA (F = 2.21, p = 0.085), while gKFA demonstrated a statistically significant regional effect (F = 3.44, p = 0.016), indicating a greater sensitivity to inter-regional differences in the present dataset. The Post-hoc Tukey HSD analysis identified significant pairwise differences for gKFA between DMV and substantia nigra, as well as between substantia nigra and thalamus (p < 0.05). KFA did not produce significant pairwise differences. These results suggest that gKFA provides an improved discrimination of regional differences compared to conventional KFA in this data set. Consistent with previous diffusion kurtosis imaging studies in transgenic mouse models, the thalamus emerged as a particularly sensitive region. Following three months of chronic exposure to rotenone, gKFA showed a significant alteration in the thalamus relative to controls (p < 0.039), while KFA did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.067). Given the central role of the thalamus in the motor circuitry of the basal ganglia, this finding is anatomically and functionally plausible and suggests that gKFA is sensitive to regional differences in the structure of directional kurtosis in a region implicated in neurodegeneration.

In general, this regional analysis suggests that gKFA may provide complementary sensitivity to regional differences compared to conventional KFA in biologically relevant brain structures.

Discussion

Conventional kurtosis fractional anisotropy (KFA) summarizes the anisotropy of the fourth-order kurtosis tensor into a scalar, analogous to FA in DTI. As such, it provides a compact measure of kurtosis anisotropy but necessarily collapses the full three-dimensional directional structure of the kurtosis tensor. This dimensional reduction may limit sensitivity to directional heterogeneity in tissues with a complex diffusion organization. By comparison, gKFA is derived from a geometric decomposition of the kurtosis tensor into multiple symmetric second-order quadratic forms. Each mapped quadratic form encodes directional combinations of kurtosis-tensor components in a lower-order representation whose geometry is easier to analyze than that of the full fourth-order tensor. The motivation of the present work is therefore to study non-Gaussian diffusion behavior in 3D through these reduced-order geometric representations. By retaining coordinate dependence, gKFA captures aspects of directional kurtosis structure that are averaged out in rotationally invariant summaries. In this sense, gKFA should be interpreted as a geometry-inspired descriptor of anisotropy across the mapped quadratic forms rather than as a replacement for conventional rotationally invariant kurtosis measures. The proposed framework may also be useful for constrained DKI simulation, since mapped quadratic forms provide lower-order geometric objects with explicit matrix-based validity checks. This could support synthetic-data generation for training and evaluating deep-learning methods for kurtosis-parameter estimation. However, this should be understood as a future direction.

A key limitation of the present study is that no direct biophysical validation of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{p}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{q}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{r}$$\end{document}, or gKFA is provided. Although metrics are derived systematically from the kurtosis tensor and appear sensitive to tissue complexity and directional heterogeneity, the present results do not establish a one-to-one correspondence with specific microstructural features such as axonal diameter, myelin content, fiber crossing angle, or water exchange. In addition, confounding factors such as T2 relaxation, partial-volume effects, and model mismatch can influence the kurtosis tensor and therefore the mapped quantities derived from it. Consequently, gKFA should currently be interpreted as a descriptor of the structure of directional kurtosis in 3D. A dedicated Monte Carlo study varying controlled microstructural parameters would be required to establish such a specificity.

The regional behavior of gKFA, for example in the thalamus, is interpreted more appropriately as reflecting differences in the structure of directional kurtosis than a uniquely identified underlying tissue mechanism. Thus, gKFA may provide complementary information in tissues where conventional measures such as FA or KFA offer a less differentiated description of non-Gaussian diffusion behavior. More generally, many pathological processes, including neurodegeneration, demyelination, inflammation, tumor infiltration, and synaptic loss, can alter tissue complexity in ways that are not captured solely by directional coherence. Geometry-based descriptors derived from the kurtosis tensor may therefore be useful to probe such changes, although their biological specificity remains to be established32,33.

Several methodological aspects also need to be considered. We expanded the set of benchmarks to include the standard DKI metrics mean kurtosis (MK), axial kurtosis (AK), and radial kurtosis (RK). These measures quantify important but different aspects of the kurtosis signal, which reinforces the interpretation of gKFA as a kurtosis-anisotropy measure rather than a scalar kurtosis-magnitude measure. KFA remains the most direct comparator to gKFA, since both are intended to summarize kurtosis anisotropy. By contrast, MK, AK and RK are not direct analogues of the proposed metric, but rather complementary measures of kurtosis magnitude and directional averaging. At the same time, the current set of benchmarks remains incomplete. In particular, the study has not yet included a comparison with noise-corrected KFA or with generalized fractional anisotropy (GFA). Accordingly, gKFA is best viewed as a geometrically motivated kurtosis-anisotropy descriptor that is closely related, but not identical, to KFA, while its relationship to other established measures remains an important topic for future work.

In the current work, we also rely on region-of-interest definitions based on FA thresholds and angular criteria, which may introduce selection bias. In addition, the physical phantoms with idealized orthogonal configurations cannot fully capture biological complexity such as fiber dispersion, partial-volume contamination, or inter-compartmental exchange. Future work should therefore evaluate gKFA under more realistic experimental conditions, examine how acquisition design, including b-value sampling and gradient-direction schemes, may affect its precision and sensitivity, and test the metric in larger datasets and disease cohorts. Broader benchmarking against noise-corrected KFA, GFA, and microstructure-informed simulations will be necessary to establish the robustness and biological interpretability of gKFA. In the present study, a direct comparison with the noise-correction scheme of Glenn et al. was not possible because the required repeated independent acquisitions were not available18,22.

Conclusion

We presented a geometric framework for diffusion kurtosis that maps the fourth-order kurtosis tensor to a set of symmetric quadratic forms, enabling a geometric interpretation of the kurtosis anisotropy. Based on this formulation, we defined geometric kurtosis fractional anisotropy (gKFA) as a scalar descriptor of orientation-dependent non-Gaussian diffusion.

The results of simulations, physical phantoms, and in vivo human and rodent data indicate that gKFA is sensitive to directional heterogeneity of non-Gaussian diffusion in both white matter and deep gray matter regions such as the thalamus. These findings suggest that gKFA captures aspects of kurtosis anisotropy that are not fully reflected by conventional scalar summaries of the kurtosis tensor. The gKFA is intended as an additional descriptor that complements existing diffusion and kurtosis measures by providing a geometric view of non-Gaussian diffusion anisotropy.
