Confronting finitude and treatment adherence in older adults with chronic diseases: a qualitative study
Authors: Leovaldo Alcântara, Luís Midão, Elísio Costa, Constança Paúl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdherence in older adults with chronic conditions is shaped not only by clinical demands but also by how individuals interpret illness, ageing, and the time they perceive they have left. Understanding these meaning‑making processes is essential for developing person‑centred strategies that respond to the realities of multimorbidity and functional decline.MethodsA qualitative descriptive study was conducted using semi‑structured interviews with 32 older adults (mean age = 77.0 years) living with chronic illnesses in the metropolitan area of Porto, Portugal. A maximum‑variation sampling strategy captured diverse social, clinical, and functional contexts. Data were analysed using Reflexive Thematic Analysis within a constructivist paradigm.ResultsFive themes described how participants navigated treatment in daily life: (1) Finitude as a catalyst for meaning and therapeutic action: awareness of vulnerability and limited time informed an existential positioning toward self‑care, ranging from protective engagement to ambivalence; (2) Identity in metamorphosis: treatment adherence was negotiated through tensions between preserving autonomy and accommodating the “sick self”; (3) The relational basis of care: family, social contact, and loneliness shaped motivation and confidence in managing treatment; (4) Recognition in care: feeling listened to and respected reinforced trust, whereas rushed or dismissive encounters undermined engagement; (5) Systemic precariousness: access barriers, financial strain, and service shortages constrained what adherence was practically possible. Across themes, adherence emerged as a contextual, relational, and existential process shaped by personal priorities, support networks, communication quality, and structural feasibility.ConclusionsAdherence in later life reflects how older adults position themselves toward self‑care in the face of vulnerability, changing identities, and real‑world constraints. Clinical practice may benefit from linking recommendations to what matters most to patients, simplifying regimens, strengthening relational support, and addressing structural barriers. Tailoring care to patients’ life contexts may foster more sustainable and meaningful engagement with treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-026-07763-6.Keywords: Patient Compliance, Aged, Chronic Disease, Comorbidity, Primary Health Care, Social Support

Introduction

The rapid rise in the older adult population is a global trend driven by declining birth rates and increasing life expectancy. The United Nations predicts that by 2050, the number of individuals aged 60 and over will surpass 2. 1 billion, marking one of the most significant social shifts of the century [1]. This growth will be especially evident in low- and middle-income countries, where health systems are already under considerable pressure [44].

Driven by advances in public health and living standards, people are living longer. However, this longer life expectancy also increases chronic non-communicable diseases (NCDs), such as cardiovascular problems, diabetes, respiratory diseases, and mental disorders, which are now the leading causes of death and represent a considerable burden on health services [39]. Chronic non-communicable diseases account for approximately 74% of all annual deaths worldwide [37].

In Portugal, the situation is particularly concerning. The country has one of the highest rates of chronic diseases among older adults within the European Union (EU) [51]. In 2024, an alarming 68.1% of older adults (65 and older) reported having at least one chronic condition, with low back pain (37.3%), high blood pressure (26.4%), and depression (12.1%) being the most common [51]. While the EU average for chronic illnesses is 35.1%, Portugal's rate reached 44.5% in 2023, highlighting a more serious issue than the European average [21].

Even with effective treatments available, adherence to therapy remains critically low. The World Health Organization defines adherence as the extent to which an individual’s behaviour aligns with agreed clinical recommendations, and consistent estimates indicate that only around 50% of patients with chronic diseases in developed countries follow their prescribed regimens, with even lower rates reported in low- and middle-income settings [34, 45, 50, 56] . In older adults, adherence tends to be particularly compromised due to multimorbidity, polypharmacy, functional limitations, and psychosocial vulnerabilities [28, 39, 49]. In Portugal, studies report that cognitive decline, limited health literacy, social isolation, and regimen complexity are among the main contributors to non-adherence in older populations [25, 42, 48]. This scenario underscores the need to better understand the factors shaping adherence in later life and to develop strategies that respond not only to clinical complexity but also to the lived experiences of older adults.

Background

Despite extensive research on treatment adherence, much of the existing literature is quantitative, focusing on sociodemographic, clinical, and behavioural predictors [31, 39]. While these studies provide important insights into the prevalence and determinants of non‑adherence, they offer a limited understanding of how older adults experience and interpret the daily demands of managing chronic illness. Qualitative research is therefore essential to explore the meanings, emotions, and contextual barriers that shape adherence behaviours in later life, particularly among those living with complex social and health realities [20, 28, 49].

Emerging evidence suggests that adherence among older adults is not shaped solely by clinical or behavioural factors but is also influenced by existential aspects of living with chronic illness. Ageing and illness frequently confront individuals with vulnerability, changes in autonomy, and the need to renegotiate identity and purpose [4, 35]. Although quantitative studies have explored predictors and mediators of adherence, little is known about how treatment intersects with older adults’ existential concerns, emotional worlds, and perceived life priorities [22, 33].

The onset or progression of chronic illness often intensifies reflections on temporality, loss, meaning, and finitude. According to psychological theories of ageing, awareness of limited time shapes motivation, emotional regulation, and health‑related decisions [15, 41]. However, the interplay between these existential dimensions and adherence remains underexplored in qualitative research. Understanding how older adults make sense of illness and treatment within the context of ageing, loss, and changing priorities may be crucial to informing more person‑centred and clinically meaningful strategies [5, 6].

In this study, finitude is understood as a lived perception of temporality, an experiential awareness that life is limited [14, 15, 36]. Ageing and chronic illnesses can intensify this sense of limitation, which can lead to different orientations toward self-care: for some, a renewed motivation to preserve life and maintain meaningful relationships,for others, resignation, avoidance, or reduced autonomy [14, 36, 47]. This existential dimension provides a useful lens to explore how older adults engage with their treatments and navigate the challenges of chronic disease management.

Aim of the study

This study aimed to explore how older adults living with chronic diseases interpret their treatment experiences in the context of ageing, multimorbidity, and perceptions of finitude. Specifically, the study sought to understand how existential concerns, temporal perceptions, and relational contexts shape their engagement with treatment and influence adherence-related behaviours.

Methodology

Study design

Descriptive qualitative approaches are particularly suitable for studies in the health field, as they allow for a deeper understanding of the phenomenon, based on the analysis of the experiential descriptions of individuals who experience the phenomenon, leading to more effective and practical actions guided by this understanding [19, 52].

This study was informed by a constructivist paradigm, which posits that reality is socially constructed, with meaning emerging from interactions and knowledge being co-constructed [26].

To ensure methodological rigour and transparency in reporting, this study was conducted and reported in accordance with the consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ): a 32-item checklist for interviews and focus groups [54]. A completed COREQ checklist is provided as a supplementary file (Supplementary File 1).

Participants and recruitment

Participants were older adults (≥ 60 years) with at least one diagnosed chronic non-communicable disease undergoing treatment for a minimum of six months. A purposive maximum-variation sampling strategy was used to capture diversity in gender, socioeconomic background, illness profiles, and living contexts [43, 46]. Participants were recruited across multiple settings in the Porto metropolitan area, including a military hospital, day centres, social centres, residential care institutions, community organisations, and the wider community.

The institutional coordinators served as intermediaries, identifying potentially eligible individuals who met the inclusion criteria and informing them about the study. Individuals who expressed interest communicated their availability to the intermediaries, who then contacted the researcher to schedule the interview. Community-dwelling participants were recruited through local organisations and informal networks.

The lead researcher had no prior relationship with any of the participants or institutions involved in recruitment. Participation was voluntary, with no financial or material incentives. Sample size was guided by the principle of information power [40], acknowledging that sample adequacy depends on data richness rather than numerical thresholds. Such sampling methods are especially recommended for qualitative exploratory studies, in which the emphasis is on obtaining a deep theoretical understanding of a phenomenon rather than statistical generalisation of the results [12, 32, 43]. A total of 32 interviews were conducted, allowing for sufficient diversity and information depth.

Data collection

Data collection was conducted through semi-structured interviews [18], between December 2024 and May 2025. The interview guide was developed by the research team based on existing literature on ageing, chronic diseases, and treatment adherence, as well as previous literature reviews conducted by the authors [2, 3, 39]. It also incorporated key concepts from existential and lifespan psychology (e.g., temporality, autonomy, identity), without adopting a phenomenological analytic framework. The guide was pilot tested with three older adults with characteristics similar to the desired sample to improve clarity, fluency, and emotional usability. The three older adults who participated in the pilot testing did not form part of the final study sample. The final version covered ten thematic areas, including perceptions about health and treatment, social support, beliefs about medication, and reflections on finitude. An example question was: “How does the awareness that life has an end influence your health decisions?” The interview guide was developed specifically for this study and is provided as Additional file 1 (English version).

The interviews were conducted in private rooms within the participating institutions or in private locations previously agreed upon with the community participants. Only the researcher and the participant were present. The interviews lasted an average of 30 min and were audio-recorded with the participants' consent. Given the emotional sensitivity of topics such as ageing, loss, and finitude, the researcher adopted an empathetic approach, employing active listening, sensitive questioning, and reflective silence. Participants were encouraged to speak at their own pace, with pauses always offered when discomfort or more prominent emotions arose.

Ethical approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Pharmacy of the University of Porto (CEFFUP 14–12–2024) and the University's Data Protection Office (A-16/2025). All participants received written and verbal information and provided free and informed written consent. They were reminded that they could skip questions, pause, or withdraw at any time. Anonymity and confidentiality were ensured through coded identifiers (e.g., P1, P2). Emotional well-being was monitored throughout the process; although referrals were not necessary, support options were available.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using Reflexive Thematic Analysis (RTA), following the six-phase approach outlined by Braun and Clarke [8–10]. RTA was used as a flexible and theoretically informed analytic approach to identify and develop patterns of meaning across participants’ accounts, while acknowledging that meaning is co-constructed through engagement with the data and reflexive analytic decision-making. The first phase involved familiarisation with the data, during which the lead researcher conducted repeated readings of all transcripts to gain an in-depth understanding of the content. In the second phase, initial codes were generated across the dataset, focusing on features of the data relevant to participants’ experiences of chronic illness, treatment, ageing, and daily self-care. Coding was conducted iteratively, allowing continuous refinement of codes as analysis progressed.

In the third phase, codes were examined and collated into provisional themes based on conceptual and semantic similarities. The fourth phase involved reviewing these provisional themes in relation to both the coded data and the entire dataset, with attention to internal coherence, clear boundaries between themes, and alignment with the study aims. During this phase, themes were refined, merged, or reorganised as necessary.

The fifth phase focused on defining and naming the final themes, clarifying their scope and analytic focus. In the sixth and final phase, an analytic narrative was constructed, integrating the final themes with illustrative data extracts to produce a coherent and transparent account of the findings. This phase involved ongoing reflexive engagement and discussion within the research team to support analytic clarity and coherence.

All interviews were transcribed verbatim in Portuguese. Transcripts were subsequently translated into English by the lead researcher and reviewed by a second researcher to ensure semantic accuracy and consistency across languages.

Researcher positioning and reflexivity

The lead researcher, trained in clinical psychology and psychogerontology, had no prior relationship with participants or institutions. Reflexivity was maintained throughout the study using a reflexive journal and regular supervisory discussions, supporting transparency and making explicit the interpretative lens shaping the analysis.

The research team brought complementary disciplinary perspectives to the study. Co‑authors have extensive research experience in ageing, gerontology, chronic diseases, and treatment adherence, using both qualitative and quantitative methodologies. The senior authors provided methodological supervision and contributed to critical discussions at multiple stages of the study, including decisions regarding study design, interview conduct, analytic procedures, and the interpretation and refinement of themes. These iterative discussions supported analytic rigor, transparency, and reflexive awareness throughout the research process.

Results

Thematic refinement occurred through several iterative rounds of revision, aiming for coherence, distinction, and theoretical richness. The resulting themes were interpretative constructions that captured the existential dimensions of living with chronic illness and navigating treatment adherence. This analytical process led to the identification of five central themes, each representing a facet of participants’ existential experience regarding health, autonomy, identity, and finitude.

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of the sample

The sample consisted of 32 participants aged between 60 and 99 years (M = 77.0; SD = 10.1). The sample was evenly distributed by gender, with 16 women (50.0%) and 16 men (50.0%). Educational levels were heterogeneous, ranging from “no formal education” to “doctorate”, with a predominance of basic education (e.g., 4th grade or less). Regarding family arrangement, most participants lived alone (n = 21; 65.6%), although most reported frequent contact with family or friends.

Multimorbidity was a central characteristic of the sample, with participants presenting a wide range of chronic conditions of varying severity and prognosis. The most frequently reported conditions included high blood pressure, diabetes, heart disease, and high cholesterol. However, the sample also encompassed individuals with more severe or life-limiting diagnoses, such as cancer, chronic kidney disease, neurodegenerative disorders, lymphoma (in remission), liver cirrhosis, multiple strokes, advanced heart failure and severe respiratory conditions. This clinical diversity reflects the heterogeneity of existential challenges and health trajectories among participants, which informed the thematic analysis of finitude and treatment adherence. Perceived financial conditions were evenly divided, with 16 participants (50.0%) classifying them as “insufficient” and the other 16 (50.0%) as “sufficient”, often with the caveat “with adjustments” or “with support”. Thematic findings

The thematic analysis identified five central themes that articulate the existential experience of ageing with chronic illness: (1) Finitude as a Catalyst for Meaning and Therapeutic Action; (2) Identity in Metamorphosis: The Tension Between Autonomy and the ‘Sick Self’; (3) The Relational Basis of Care: Connection, Loneliness, and the Desire to Belong; (4) Recognition in Care: Between Invalidation and Therapeutic Partnership; and (5) Systemic Precarity and Care as an Act of Resistance. Together, these themes illustrate how living with ongoing treatment intertwines with reflections on time, mortality, the meaning of life, and the restructuring of identity. Finitude as a catalyst for meaning and therapeutic action

A majority of participants described that ageing, episodes of worsening health, and serious illnesses heightened their awareness of death and the fragility of life. For many, this perception served as a motivator to maintain medication, avoid risks, or adopt healthy behaviours. Some participants explicitly stated that they “want to live a few more years” or “see their grandchildren grow up,” associating treatment with the preservation of life and autonomy.

A minority of participants expressed resignation or ambivalence, considering death inevitable and therefore questioning the value of certain therapeutic efforts. These participants expressed greater difficulty in maintaining lifestyle changes or demanding therapeutic routines. Despite the differences, finitude consistently appears as a personal framework that influences the motivation to adhere to treatment or not.

Across accounts, finitude emerged as a personal interpretative framework shaping treatment engagement; sometimes mobilising protective health behaviours, sometimes eliciting avoidance or fatalism. For example, P24 (male, 60 years old) described feeling particularly worried before clinical reassessments, perceiving them as moments in which negative developments about his condition might be communicated.

“Ever since that issue of the three-month life expectancy they gave me, I think I always think about it. I wonder if I'll live a bit longer, or if my organs will shut down. I'm very worried about the state of my pancreas. I get scared every time I have a phase of reassessing my condition; I always think the worst. This affects me deeply. I get sad, worried, regretful about the life I led, but I hope that I still last a few more little years.” (P24, male, 60 years old)

In contrast, other participants channelled their awareness of finitude into a proactive reorientation of their priorities. For P5 (male, 61 years old), the notion that time is precious motivates him to manage his health deliberately.

“Life is very good — at least, I think so. That's why I want to avoid doing things that harm me or speed up ageing and the worsening of my illness. I live with it naturally, but I know I need to avoid certain things. It's necessary to make different decisions and not simply let things happen.” (P5, male, 61 years old)

Some participants described conflicting feelings after experiences of loss or deterioration in health. Although these events increased their awareness of the possibility of further decline, they also reported uncertainty between maintaining recommended self-care behaviours and continuing habits they recognised as harmful.

“I've thought about that many times, especially with the deaths of my brother and my mother. I question the point of doing everything right if we're going to die anyway. I think it's better to drink and smoke and live a fun life.” (P19, male, 65 years old)

Some participants expressed a wish to live longer in order to accompany the growth of younger family members, describing this as an important motivator to maintain their health and follow treatment recommendations.

“Ah, sometimes, I think… I have a granddaughter who’s only 3 years old, you know? I think she came very late; I sometimes say she came too late, because I’m already 70 and she’s only 3. I sometimes wonder, will I see her grown up? And I start thinking, look, when I’m 80, she’ll only be 13 — still so young, you understand. And then I think… I hope God lets me last longer so I can see her all grown up, you know? I try to take care of myself, so I can make it there.” (P11, female, 70 years old)

Some participants explained that having relatives who experienced serious illnesses, such as cancer, increased their concern about their own health. In these cases, this family history was described as a reason to adopt certain health-related behaviours more consistently.

“I feel fear and anxiety when I think about death. This motivates me to take care of my health. I quit smoking, changed my diet, my routines and my habits. I am very afraid of dying, or of getting cancer; in my family, everyone has had cancer.” (P30, male, 77 years old)

Identity in metamorphosis: the tension between autonomy and the "sick self"

A majority of participants described challenges in maintaining independence while managing chronic illness. Many reported tensions between wanting to remain autonomous and needing help with daily activities or treatment-related tasks. Strategies to cope with these changes varied: some participants tried to maintain independence for as long as possible, while others expressed resistance to aspects of medical treatment or difficulty accepting new routines.

Several participants explained that the progression of physical limitations led to feelings of frustration or resignation. Although many acknowledged these changes as part of their health condition, they also described the emotional difficulty of adjusting to reduced functional capacity.

“I do feel limited. I would like to do more, but my body won’t allow it. I feel that if I push myself to do the things I want to do, I’ll end up hurting myself. I resign myself to doing what I can, and that’s how we get by.” (P7, female, 92 years old)

Some participants who experienced serious illness earlier in life described mixed feelings regarding those periods. They expressed appreciation for the treatments that allowed their recovery; while also acknowledging sadness about the activities and opportunities they were unable to experience due to prolonged illness.

“When I was a child, I had a disease in my hip bones. They did grafts with bones from my legs. I spent a long time in bed; I only started walking properly when I was 20. I didn't have a youth like other people – I spent more time in hospitals than living like others. But thank God, since the surgery I have been well.” (P9, female, 88 years old)

Some participants explained that they followed medical recommendations closely because they believed this would help them maintain independence in the long term. Although they did not always feel involved in treatment decisions, they reported adhering to the prescribed plan to avoid future dependence on others.

“I take them and follow the treatments because I know I must. If they are prescribed, it's because I need them. I don't want to become debilitated and dependent on other people, much less go into a nursing home. So, if I want to continue managing my life independently, I must do what the doctors say.” (P18, female, 80 years old)

Some participants described that the illness caused a sudden interruption of important social roles, such as working or providing care to family members. They reported sadness and frustration related to losing activities that had been central to their daily lives and sense of usefulness.

“Since I became ill, I stopped working; I stopped doing many things after I got the heart condition. I am forbidden from going near the stove, and that is something that saddens me deeply. The kitchen was always an important part of my life, and not being able to be there leaves me with a sense of longing and sadness.” (P23, female, 80 years old)

Some participants reported that following treatment recommendations meant giving up long-standing dietary or social habits. They described these changes as particularly difficult, noting that such practices had been part of their routine for many years and were not easy to modify.

“My mouth doesn’t accept diets. I eat everything. I’d rather eat as I always have. I can’t live with diets; I won’t make it that way. I’d die if I had to diet.” (P13, male, 80 years old)

Across accounts, illness-related limitations prompted an ongoing renegotiation of autonomy, as participants balanced their desire for independence with the realities imposed by the ‘sick self’.

The relational basis of care: connection, loneliness, and the desire to belong

A majority of participants highlighted the importance of family support, social connection, and, for some, spiritual beliefs in coping with illness and maintaining treatment routines. Emotional or practical support from relatives or close contacts was described as helping them continue with their care, whereas the absence of such support contributed to feelings of being alone or having to manage the illness without assistance.

In some cases, the illness revealed limitations within existing support networks, with participants reporting that family members were not always able or available to help. A few participants also mentioned that moments of greater health vulnerability led to renewed contact with people from whom they had been distant, describing these reconnections as unexpected but helpful during periods of greater need.

“It all appeared at the same time, right after a divorce. And then, it was as if the world collapsed. It was a really bad patch; I had no one around me. Family didn't help. But curiously, people I had known for years and hadn't seen appeared, and in that moment, they were the ones who helped me the most.” (P4, female, 66 years old)

Participants who reported having consistent and supportive family relationships described this support as helpful in managing their illness. Sharing daily tasks or treatment-related responsibilities with family members made it easier for them to keep up with care routines and contributed to a greater sense of security in dealing with the disease.

“My daughter heard me complaining and that I needed to see a doctor, and she said, ‘Of course, let's go.’ On another occasion, I was feeling unwell and she immediately booked an urgent appointment for me. So, for my needs, I have my daughter, my wife, my family—I have four other siblings as well.” (P12, male, 71 years old)

In some cases, participants explained that support from family members encouraged them to continue their treatment and take care of their health. They described this support as a motivating factor in maintaining treatment routines.

“I have always had support and encouragement from my family. I have always been concerned about my family; everything I have ever done was to be able to give them a good life. I have always taken care of my health so I can be with my family and support them too. Family is the most precious thing there is.” (P31, male, 99 years old)

Some participants noted that being open to talking about their difficulties and maintaining social contact helped them feel less isolated and better able to manage their illness.

“I don't think I'm the most qualified person to speak on this, but I believe that not acquiring vices or committing excesses is a good start. However, what I think is most important is for a person to seek to talk about their problems, not to close themselves in a shell and brood over them. There is always someone who can listen to us; I think that is fundamental. Isolating yourself is the worst thing you can do.” (P25, male, 61 years old)

Participants who reported having little or no formal or informal support described feeling isolated while managing their illness. They explained that dealing with health problems alone made day-to-day management more difficult and increased their sense of insecurity in relation to their condition.

"I am alone, completely alone, I have no support from anyone." (P17, male, 65 years old)

Some participants who felt they had little support reported that these experiences made them more aware of the value of having people around them during periods of illness. They explained that the absence of support highlighted the importance of maintaining social contacts that could offer help when needed.

"I believe the main thing is to have people around you, to be with friends who can support you in your worst situations." (P17, male, 65 years old)

Across accounts, relationships shaped how participants coped with illness, either by providing emotional and practical stability or, when absent, by intensifying feelings of vulnerability and insecurity.

Recognition in care: between invalidation and therapeutic partnership

Almost all participants described a wide range of experiences in their interactions with healthcare professionals. Several reported feelings listened to and supported during consultations, noting that clear communication and a sense of being taken seriously increased their confidence in following treatment recommendations.

By contrast, some participants recounted fewer positive encounters, including perceptions that their concerns were dismissed or that consultations were too brief to address their questions. These experiences contributed to feelings of frustration and, in some cases, uncertainty about their treatment plans.

In accounts where participants felt heard, they described these interactions as helpful for understanding their condition and staying engaged in their care. Conversely, when they perceived a lack of attention or insufficient explanation, they reported greater difficulty trusting or adhering to therapeutic guidance.

“Ah, they listen to me beautifully, and they learn. They still learn from me; they ask me things that, often, for them is also a learning experience, and they thank me for it.” (P1, female, 92 years old)

Some participants reported feeling that their concerns were not given sufficient attention in healthcare encounters. They described situations in which consultations felt rushed or their symptoms were dismissed, which contributed to feelings of frustration and of not being adequately supported during their care.

“This business with family doctors... we are in a very bad situation. We are in a very bad situation because they just want to rush us through. They don't give us much attention, and when we show them the test results, everything is always fine. Well, for them, it's always fine, so they don't have to deal with it. It's always fine. But it's not! We are the ones who feel it, aren't we?” (P18, female, 80 years old)

In other cases, participants described having a trusting and supportive relationship with their healthcare professionals. They reported that feeling listened to and respected during consultations increased their confidence in the treatment and made it easier to discuss concerns related to their care.

“My relationship with the healthcare professionals is very good. We have built a bond of trust; I don't hide anything from them, and they don't hide anything from me. I feel that I can talk about and discuss aspects of my treatment. They listen to me, they respect me, and they show a genuine interest.” (P25, male, 61 years old)

Some participants said that although they wished to be involved in decisions about their treatment, they felt that their views were not always considered. They described situations in which their attempts to express concerns or preferences were overlooked, which left them feeling frustrated and insufficiently included in the decision-making process.

“My treatments began while I was still in the Navy, after a nervous collapse. Several exams were done; the medical board raised hypotheses, conducted more tests, and have prescribed many medications ever since. I participated actively. I even resorted to the legal system to challenge diagnoses and seek more precise examinations, because I suspect there was negligence in my case. I did not want to be medically discharged; they did this against my will, based on assessments I never agreed with.” (P30, male, 77 years old)

Across accounts, clinical encounters either reinforced participants’ sense of trust—when marked by listening, clarity and respect—or intensified feelings of vulnerability when concerns were dismissed or insufficiently addressed.

Systemic precariousness and care as an act of resistance

A majority of participants mentioned structural and organisational barriers within the healthcare system that made it difficult to follow their treatment plans. These included long waiting times, limited access to medical appointments, bureaucratic procedures, and shortages of healthcare professionals or resources. Such constraints created challenges in maintaining continuity of care, particularly for those with multiple chronic conditions.

In particular, participants with an immigrant background described additional obstacles in accessing healthcare services, such as difficulties obtaining a family doctor or accessing routine examinations. They reported that these situations caused delays in receiving care and made the management of their illness more complex.

“Here in Portugal, I faced many barriers. When I arrived, I tried to schedule an appointment for my routine exams and was immediately blocked. They told me: ‘You don't have a family doctor, you don't have a resident card, you cannot access care.’ I have been in the country for over two years, contributing with taxes and formal work, but I have still not managed to get access to a family doctor — who seems to be the only gateway to getting tests and follow-up care.” (P5, male, 61 years old)

Several participants mentioned that the progression of their illness led them to stop working. They reported that this loss of employment created financial difficulties and increased their reliance on external support, making the management of daily life and treatment more challenging.

“At the start of the treatment, everything was difficult. I lacked the willpower, the money, and I didn't always have the means. I even reached a point where I didn't have money for food—I ate from social support vans. I almost became homeless.” (P13, male, 80 years old)

Some participants reported facing significant financial constraints, noting that their limited income made it difficult to cover basic living expenses as well as treatment-related costs. They explained that these financial limitations added to the challenges of managing their illness and increased their dependence on external support.

“No, I receive a social integration support payment of 250 euros. I pay 200 for a rented room, and I have to manage everything else in my life with the 50 euros that are left after rent. I cannot have an extra coffee; every single cent must be counted.” (P25, male, 61 years old)

Some participants explained that financial hardship made it difficult to manage both their daily living expenses and treatment-related costs. They reported that limited income sometimes required them to prioritise basic needs over medication purchases, which added to the challenges of living with multiple chronic conditions.

“Scheduling appointments should be easier; getting specialist consultations needs to be more accessible. We need more doctors. There should also be home support for people in my situation. Besides, the pension is miserable and doesn't cover anything – sometimes I have to skip buying food to afford the medications I need.” (P27, male, 80 years old)

Across accounts, participants portrayed adherence not only as a health-related task, but as a daily act of negotiation within conditions of systemic precariousness; in which financial strain, bureaucratic barriers and limited access to care shaped the possibilities of managing chronic illness.

Discussion

This qualitative study explored how older adults living with chronic conditions understand and manage their treatments in everyday life. Across the sample, adherence was not a single behaviour but a situated process shaped by (i) perceptions of health vulnerability and possible decline, (ii) the continuous negotiation between autonomy and functional limitations, (iii) the availability and quality of family and social support, (iv) the content and tone of clinical encounters, and (v) structural conditions that enable or hinder access and continuity of care. These findings align with and extend current evidence on multimorbidity, self‑management and adherence in later life.

Across accounts, awareness of finitude and life’s temporal limits surfaced as a quiet, practical undercurrent that shaped how older adults positioned themselves toward self‑care. We refer to this stance as an “existential positioning”: a situated orientation (more proactive or more resigned) through which people make sense of why, when, and how to continue caring for themselves in later life. Clinically, this lens helps to explain why similar regimens can be lived so differently across patients, even when clinical severity is comparable, and why aligning treatment with personally valued goals (e.g., independence, being present for family) can sustain adherence beyond abstract risk information.

In this context, participants often associated health crises, news of diagnoses, or ageing itself with greater awareness of potential deterioration. For many, this coincided with a greater commitment to medication and preventive routines; for some, it coexisted with ambivalence and difficulty in maintaining demanding lifestyle changes. This pattern is consistent with research showing that treatment decisions in older adults are influenced by future time perspective and prioritisation of goals perceived as more valuable (e.g., preserving function or being present for family), as proposed by the Socio-Emotional Selectivity Theory (SST) and related work on ageing and motivation [14–16, 36]. Our findings add qualitative detail to this evidence, illustrating how older adults translate awareness of vulnerability into concrete routines (e.g., more regular use of medications) and when that awareness can also lead to hesitation when changes threaten long-standing habits.

Clinically, these results support discussing person-specific goals (e.g., maintaining independence to perform family roles) when agreeing on treatment plans, which may help maintain motivation for adherence beyond generic messages about risks [29, 30].

Our data indicate that awareness of vulnerability and limited time rarely functions as a purely cognitive assessment; instead, it is adopted as an “existential positioning” in daily self-care. For many participants, this translated into a protective orientation, such as more regular medication routines or proactive risk avoidance. For a minority, it coexisted with ambivalence or deliberate non-adherence when changes threatened identity-laden habits. This practical perspective complements socio‑emotional selectivity accounts of ageing: the focus on meaningful goals and limited future time helps clarify how people align treatment with what they still consider worth preserving (autonomy, relational presence, dignity) and disengage when care is perceived as something that erodes identity or is practically unfeasible.

It is important to emphasise that this is not an appeal to abstract philosophy in the clinic. Instead, it is a brief, value-based investigation (e.g., "What is most important for you to continue doing going forward?"/"How does this plan help you continue being the person you want to be?"). It can reveal the patient's existential positioning and guide treatment adaptation. A practical takeaway is simple: ask what is important, link one treatment element to that answer, and simplify one burdensome step.

This existential positioning became particularly visible in how participants negotiated autonomy in everyday care. All participants described some degree of functional loss that affected daily tasks and treatment routines. For many, regular medication was understood not only as a clinical requirement but as a practical means of preserving independence. For others, dietary restrictions or rigid routines conflicted with lifelong habits and personal identity, generating frustration or ambivalence.

These experiences echo classic descriptions of biographical disruption in chronic illness, where bodily changes interrupt established roles and ways of living [11, 17], and align with contemporary syntheses on the daily burden of multimorbidity [28, 49]. Our findings extend this work by showing that, in later life, autonomy acts as a central value through which treatment decisions are filtered: people adhere not only to control symptoms, but to avoid future dependence and maintain activities that anchor their sense of self.

From a clinical perspective, care plans should aim to protect independence wherever possible. This includes simplifying regimen schedules (e.g., once‑daily dosing; deprescribing when clinically appropriate), reducing unnecessary complexity, and negotiating pragmatic dietary or activity goals that respect identity‑laden routines. Such adjustments may reduce friction between treatment demands and what older adults perceive as essential for maintaining autonomy, thereby supporting more sustainable adherence [24, 39].

Participants’ existential positioning toward self-care was also shaped by the social environments in which they managed their illness. Most participants who reported consistent family support described it as helpful in organising medication, attending appointments, and maintaining routines. This support provided both emotional stability and practical assistance, reinforcing a sense of safety and continuity in care.

On the other hand, those who lived alone or with limited support reported more difficulty in maintaining care and greater insecurity in managing their health. Feelings of loneliness — whether due to widowhood, family estrangement, or strained relationships — have been associated with reduced motivation, emotional fatigue, and difficulties in coping with self-care demands. These findings are consistent with prior evidence showing that social support predicts better self‑management, whereas loneliness in older adults is associated with poorer health behaviours, greater service use, and increased vulnerability [23, 38, 55, 57].

Our data also illustrate that support networks are dynamic, not static. Some participants described re-establishing ties with people from whom they had been estranged for a long period, particularly during acute illnesses or emotional stress. Informal contacts, such as neighbours, religious communities, community centre staff, and friends, sometimes compensated for gaps in family support and helped stabilise treatment routines when health deteriorated.

These findings suggest that clinicians should routinely assess levels of social support and isolation during medication reviews, especially in multimorbidity. When limited networks are identified, interventions may include involving family or caregivers in medication organisation, linking patients with community resources (e.g., day centres, mutual‑aid associations), and providing brief caregiver‑directed guidance. Strengthening relational supports can help sustain adherence by reducing emotional burden and anchoring treatment within everyday routines.

This relational landscape also influenced how participants interpreted clinical communication and continuity of care, shaping their trust in treatment and confidence in navigating the health system. Communication quality also influenced participants’ existential positioning, either reinforcing a protective orientation toward self‑care or intensifying resignation when interactions felt dismissive. Feeling listened to and taken seriously increased trust in treatment decisions and sustained motivation to continue. In contrast, brief consultations, perceived disregard for symptoms, or difficulty accessing a family doctor/specialist contributed to frustration and uncertainty. These reports echo previous studies showing that clear information, shared understanding of the regimen, and relational trust are fundamental to adherence in long-term conditions [29, 53]. In a context of multimorbidity and polypharmacy, continuity and consistency of messages between healthcare providers are particularly relevant [31, 39].

In practice, this implies that services should prioritise communication practices that actively check comprehension (e.g., routine use of teach‑back), explicitly negotiate treatment adjustments, and ensure continuity through structured follow‑up and medication reconciliation. Strengthening relational continuity can help stabilise treatment routines and reduce uncertainty, especially for older adults with multiple chronic conditions.

These interpersonal dynamics were experienced and often exacerbated in conjunction with systemic and socioeconomic constraints that shaped what adherence was practically possible. Participants frequently pointed to system‑level barriers such as long waits, limited appointments, bureaucratic steps, shortages of professionals, and financial constraints that forced difficult choices between medication and other essentials. Those with immigrant backgrounds described additional access obstacles, such as difficulties obtaining a family doctor or navigating eligibility pathways for routine examinations. These structural conditions often shaped not only the feasibility of treatment but also participants’ existential positioning toward self‑care: scarce resources and restricted access could reinforce a protective orientation (“I have to care for myself because the system won’t”), or, conversely, a resigned stance when the effort required felt overwhelming.

These reports echo earlier work on structural vulnerability and social determinants of health, highlighting how adherence depends not only on motivation but also on material and organisational feasibility [7, 13, 27]. Within the Portuguese setting, prior studies also note how regimen complexity, health literacy and limited resources interact to undermine sustained adherence in older adults [25, 48].

These results highlight the importance of treatment adherence support combining clinical optimisation (e.g., regimen simplification, medication alignment) with social and financial support measures. These may include cost‑conscious prescribing, generic substitutions, social work referral, and clearer pathways for individuals without an assigned family doctor. At the service level, strengthening primary care access, continuity, and capacity is likely to produce substantial benefits for adherence in multimorbidity.

These structural constraints contextualise and sometimes limit the relational, motivational and autonomy‑driven processes described above, underscoring the need for adherence strategies that address both clinical and material realities.

How these findings extend current knowledge

Taken together, our results reaffirm that adherence in later life is better understood as a relational and contextual process than as an isolated individual behaviour. They extend current knowledge in five keyways: First, introducing a practice‑ready lens of “existential positioning” that helps explain why older adults prioritise emotionally significant goals due to a perception of limited time. Interventions that emphasise immediate emotional benefits and strengthen bonds promote greater adherence than those focused solely on future gains. Understanding these socio-emotional priorities allows for more personalised and effective care strategies [14, 15]. Second, showing that protecting autonomy is a central driver of adherence, with many older adults following treatment not only to control symptoms but to avoid loss of independence and preserve valued roles and activities. This extends existing work on multimorbidity by demonstrating how autonomy operates as both a practical and existential anchor in treatment decision‑making. [28, 49]. Third, detailing the practical weight of social ties and the risks of loneliness for day‑to‑day treatment management. Our findings expand prior evidence by illustrating the dynamic nature of support networks,how fragile ties may be reactivated during crises, and how informal contacts (neighbours, day‑centres, friends) can compensate for family gaps and stabilise routines [23, 55]. Fourth, highlighting that communication quality and continuity of care function as relational determinants of adherence, especially in the context of multimorbidity and polypharmacy. By showing how feeling listened to, respected, and taken seriously sustains engagement, these findings add experiential depth to established evidence on relational trust in chronic illness care [29, 53]. Finally, underscoring that adherence is inseparable from structural feasibility, including access pathways, financial constraints, and service capacity. By embedding treatment decisions within wider contexts of precariousness, our study reinforces and extends frameworks on structural vulnerability and emphasises the need for adherence strategies that address both clinical and material realities [7, 27].

Together, these contributions integrate psychological, relational and structural perspectives and emphasise the practical relevance of tailoring adherence support to the lived contexts of older adults with chronic illnesses.

Clinical implications

These implications integrate the clinical, relational and structural elements that shaped participants’ existential positioning toward self‑care. Based on the five domains above, we outline actions at three levels: At the clinical encounter: Elicit patient-prioritised goals (e.g., staying mobile to care for grandchildren) and link each recommendation to those goals [14, 15, 36]. Use teach-back and provide written summaries for complex regimens,schedule early follow-up when changes are made. Simplify regimens (e.g., once-daily dosing, deprescribing) and negotiate pragmatic diet/activity targets that preserve valued routines where possible [24, 39]. Around the patient: Screen for social support and loneliness,involve family/caregivers in medication organisation when appropriate [23, 55]. Refer to community resources (day centres, home support, mutual-aid organisations) to bolster routines and reduce isolation. At system level: Facilitate access and continuity in primary care (assignment to a family doctor,structured recall systems). Address economic barriers with cost-conscious prescribing, generic substitutions, and linkage to social benefits; coordinate with social workers when financial constraints jeopardise adherence [25, 27].

Strengths and limitations

This discussion should be read alongside the Limitations section, which outlines issues related to context, sample characteristics, interpretive approach and retrospective accounts. Strengths of the study include a diverse sample drawn from multiple care settings and a reflexive thematic analysis that allowed attention to variation across participants’ experiences. The researcher’s perspective, understood as a unique and situated interpretive lens within Reflexive Thematic Analysis, was managed through systematic reflexive practice and supervisory discussions, ensuring transparency in how interpretations were developed. Limitations include the single-country setting and the resulting constraints on transferability to other health systems or cultural contexts. In line with the epistemology of Reflexive Thematic Analysis, transcripts were not returned to participants for checking, as this practice is not considered necessary for ensuring rigour in this approach. The findings are intended to illuminate processes that shape treatment engagement in later life and inform clinical practice, rather than support statistical generalisation.

Future research directions

Building on these results, future work could: (1) Test multicomponent interventions that combine regimen simplification, goal-concordant communication, and social-support linkage for older adults with multimorbidity. (2) Examine service redesign (e.g., pharmacist-led medication reviews; social prescribing) on adherence and functional outcomes. (3) Explore immigrant older adults’ access pathways to primary care and their effects on continuity and adherence. (4) Use longitudinal qualitative designs to track how motivation and routines evolve with health events and changing support networks. Such work would help translate the relational, motivational and structural processes identified in this study into scalable, person‑centred strategies for older adults living with chronic conditions.

Conclusion

Treatment adherence in later life cannot be viewed solely from a behavioural perspective or as an individual decision about whether to adhere to treatment, but rather as a relational, existential, and emotionally embedded process. In later life, life history and accumulated experience shape a temporal awareness grounded in both time lived and perceived time remaining. Given this, self-care behaviours are permeated by individual subjectivity and by how the individual positions themselves existentially in relation to the disease and the available treatment options.

Treatment adherence is dynamically negotiated between the desire for autonomy and the acceptance of the limitations imposed by ageing and concomitant diseases. The concept of existential positioning offers a useful lens for understanding how these dimensions converge in everyday decision-making. It highlights that adherence is not just about following medical recommendations, but about maintaining a coherent sense of self in the face of ageing, illness, and uncertainty. Through this positioning, older adults actively negotiate whether and how treatment aligns with what they value, what they can sustain, and what they perceive as meaningful in their remaining time.

Therefore, improving adherence in later life requires moving beyond individual-level explanations and interventions. Instead, it calls for approaches that recognise the relational and structural contexts in which self-care takes place, while also engaging with patients’ values, priorities, and lived experiences. Grounding treatment in what matters most to older adults and ensuring that care remains feasible within real-world constraints supports more sustainable, meaningful, and person-centred engagement with treatment in the context of chronic illness.
