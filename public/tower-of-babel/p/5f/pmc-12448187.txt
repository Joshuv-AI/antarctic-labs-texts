Feasibility and efficacy of pH-integrated ultrathin endoscopy for ambulatory gastroesophageal reflux monitoring (with videos)
Authors: Xiaoyu Hu, Yaoping Zhang, Jinyong Hao, Bofu Tang, Yifan Zhang, Xiaojun Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCurrent techniques for monitoring gastroesophageal reflux are limited to measuring physicochemical parameters. We presented a novel pH-integrated ultrathin endoscope (the EndoMonitor System) for simultaneous acquisition of reflux images and pH data. This preclinical study evaluated the performance of the latest version of the EndoMonitor System.MethodsThe stability and hydrogen ion selectivity of the pH electrode in the EndoMonitor System were evaluated. Subsequently, six types of reflux events were simulated in an isolated organ (porcine esophagus-stomach). Complete duodenal ligation was used to create porcine gastroesophageal reflux models. The number of reflux episodes and acid exposure time were assessed and compared with the pH-impedance catheter results.ResultsThe detection rates for distal acid reflux, distal mixed reflux, and horizontal acid reflux events of the EndoMonitor System were significantly higher than those of the pH-impedance catheter (100% vs 18.6%, 100% vs 32.7%, and 100% vs 49.1%, respectively; all P < 0.001). Furthermore, the EndoMonitor System demonstrated superior detection performance in proximal acid and mixed reflux events compared with the pH-impedance catheter (100% vs 90.9% and 100% vs 88.1%, respectively; both P < 0.05). In vivo reflux monitoring demonstrated statistically higher detection rates of reflux events with the EndoMonitor System in two-thirds of the experimental groups (P < 0.05), with a more remarkable overall detection advantage (100% vs 82.0%; P < 0.001). The acid exposure time measurement results for these two devices were comparable (mean ± standard deviation: 2.62 ± 1.82 vs 2.52 ± 1.74 min; P = 0.161).ConclusionsThe EndoMonitor System improves the accuracy of identifying reflux events and simplifies the monitoring process. It was also highly operable. This study provided important preclinical experience for clinical trials.Keywords: ultrathin endoscope, gastroesophageal reflux, ambulatory reflux monitoring, real-time visualization, dual-mode monitoring device

Introduction

Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) is a common and economically burdensome public health issue with a variable global prevalence ranging from 2.5% to 33.1% in different regions [1, 2]. In addition to endoscopy, ambulatory reflux monitoring carries a strong weight in diagnosis [3]. In 1974, Johnson and DeMeester [4] proposed the gold standard for the methodology of esophageal dynamic reflux monitoring. In the 1990s, multichannel intra-luminal pH-impedance monitoring was applied to dynamic esophageal reflux monitoring [5]. A study reported a sensitivity of 77% and a specificity of 92% for transnasal intra-luminal pH-impedance testing [6]. The number of reflux episodes and acid exposure time (AET) are considered important parameters to differentiate physiological reflux (<40 times, <4%) from pathological reflux (>80 times, >6%) in the Lyon Consensus [7, 8]. However, the results are not displayed in real-time, and manual analysis is subsequently needed [9]. Therefore, this examination is time-consuming and subjective. Even though different versions of automated analysis systems have been introduced, they still suffer with occasional duplicate counts of reflux episodes [10]. Other events related to esophageal movement (e.g. burping and feeding) and factors such as sensor position may also affect the values [11, 12].

To circumvent the above problems caused by subjective differences, existing studies have applied real-time magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to the anti-peristalsis imaging of the gastroesophageal junction [13, 14]. Despite the fact that real-time imaging is efficient and secure, it is susceptible to resolution and imaging quality limitations. In another solution, wavelet transform and wavelet-based fractal analysis were employed to develop efficient computer methods to assist the diagnostic process. The sensitivity and specificity of this approach are 93% and 75%, respectively [15]. This method just follows the pH waveform exclusively without requiring direct observation of reflux episodes, so the reliability of outcomes is fundamentally dependent on the precision of pH measurements. Therefore, we have developed a pH-integrated ultrathin endoscope, called the EndoMonitor System, to enable simultaneous acquisition of reflux imaging and pH. Such a device is envisaged to make reflux measurements more accurate. This preclinical study investigated the performance, operability and safety of ambulatory reflux monitoring using the latest version of the EndoMonitor System.

Methods

EndoMonitor system

The EndoMonitor System is a transnasal visualized pH catheter system designed for dynamic gastroesophageal reflux monitoring, comprising a camera module, pH electrodes, catheter body, and data output terminal (Figure 1A and D). The catheter body, measuring 2.8 mm in outer diameter and 1.5 m in length, is constructed from polyurethane medical materials. A miniature camera module with an integrated CMOS sensor (model OV6946) providing 160 kilopixel resolution is mounted at the distal end of catheter (Figure 1B). The camera module is sealed on the periphery to prevent liquid infiltration. From distal to proximal on the distal catheter wall, the pH electrodes are reference and indicator electrodes. The reference electrode consists of Ag/AgCl material, while the indicator electrode is fabricated from antimony metal. A seal is provided in the gap between the electrode and the catheter to prevent fluid ingress into the catheter lumen. To ensure optimal contact with refluxate, the pH indicator electrode protrudes slightly from the catheter surface (Figure 1C). The catheter body possesses a parallel double-channel configuration in which the camera transmission cable and pH electrode wires thread through separately. The device is continuously powered by connecting to a mobile phone or computer through a USB interface (such as Type-C or USB-A) (Figure 1D). The system outputs two types of reflux data: dynamic videos and pH values. The reflux videos are displayed in real-time on mobile terminals (e.g. tablets, smartphones). The pH measurements are stored on a portable device, then the data will be transmitted to the terminal software (Figure 1E). This device design has been granted a utility model patent by China National Intellectual Property Administration (patent holder: The Second Hospital of Lanzhou University; patent No. CN 222398271 U).

The EndoMonitor System. (A) Full view of the equipment. (B) Camera module. (C) pH working/reference electrode. (D) Section view of the equipment. (E) Equipment in operation.

Performance testing of pH electrode

The standard buffer solutions (SBS) with pH values of 1.68, 4.01, and 9.18 were accurately prepared and sub-packed. In the first phase, the stability of pH electrode was evaluated. The pH electrode was sequentially immersed in SBS following an ascending pH order (1.68, 4.01, and 9.18). A sequential immersion was performed in five sets, each lasting 3–5 s (Figure 2A). The selectivity of the pH electrode was evaluated in the second stage. The electrode was first immersed in SBS at pH 1.68 until the measured values were stable. The electrode was then transferred to human gastric juice for 5 s before removal. Finally, the electrode was re-immersed in the original SBS to observe whether the measured values recovered. The procedure was repeated for five cycles. Subsequently, the same test procedure was performed in pH 9.18 SBS to fully characterize electrode selectivity (Figure 2B).

Performance testing of pH electrode. (A) Stability test. (B) Interference resistance test.

In vitro experiment

Acidic reflux fluid (pH ∼1.4) was prepared using 2.0 g NaCl, 3.2 g pepsin, 80 mL 1 M HCl, and distilled water. Meanwhile, 292.5 mg of bilirubin, 50 mL sodium carbonate solution, and acid reflux solution were used to prepare a mixed reflux solution (pH ∼6.0). The upper gastrointestinal tracts (from the esophagus to the stomach) isolated from porcine were cleaned and used as an in vitro reflux model. Prepared acidic or mixed reflux fluids were injected into the gastric cavity, followed by pyloric ligation. Gastric fluid reflux into the esophagus was induced by compression of the gastric fundus. The patterns of reflux were classified as proximal (<5–6 cm), distal (>5–6 cm) and horizontal (Figure 3A), depending on the height and angle at which the reflux enters the esophagus [16] (Supplementary Video 1). The horizontal reflux was set up to correspond to the supine reflux in clinical practice. The EndoMonitor System and a pH-impedance catheter (Chongqing Jinshan Technology Co., Ltd) were simultaneously positioned 6 cm above the gastroesophageal junction and secured for reflux monitoring. The pH-impedance catheter featured eight impedance rings forming six measurement channels, with a pH electrode integrated between the second and third rings. Monitoring experiments for each type of reflux were repeated for four sets.

Schematic diagram of gastroesophageal reflux simulation experiment. (A) In vitro experiment. (B) In vivo experiment.

In vivo experiment

The experimental protocols were reviewed and approved by the Ethical Committee of Lanzhou University Second Hospital (No. D2024-625). Three 2-month-old Bama miniature pigs (approximately 10 kg, sex-neutral) were obtained from the Veterinary Research Institute, Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Lanzhou, P. R. China). Preoperative sedation was achieved via intramuscular injection of midazolam (0.1–0.15 mg/kg) and atropine (0.1 mg/kg). Subsequently, venous access was established through the marginal ear vein, and anesthesia was induced by continuous intravenous infusion of propofol (20–40 mg/h). Anesthesia was maintained with inhalation of isoflurane (1% to 3%) and oxygen (30%–60%). The animals were then stabilized in the supine position, and complete ligation of the duodenum [17] was carried out to establish the GERD model (Figure 3B). Vital signs and anesthetic depth were continuously monitored by a veterinarian. Animals were fasted but had free access to water after surgery. In vivo reflux monitoring experiment would be carried out unless anything untoward occurred within 24 h of observation.

The second stage was in vivo reflux monitoring experiment. Three pig models of GERD were treated with fasting and water restriction. The anesthesia protocol was consistent with the methods described in the modeling experiments. All animals were stabilized in the left recumbent position, and a gastroscope bite block was placed in their mouth. Guided by gastroscope, the EndoMonitor System and pH-impedance catheter were placed 5–6 cm above the gastroesophageal junction. The external parts of the two monitoring devices were secured to the cheek with tape. The state of cardia and inflammation of lower esophageal mucosa were observed and recorded by the EndoMonitor System. What was carried out next was reflux monitoring. Two sets of in vivo reflux monitoring were performed for each animal, and the duration of a single reflux monitoring experiment was limited to 15 min (Supplementary Video 2). The animals were sacrificed at the end of experiment. The lower esophageal mucosal tissue specimens were collected for hematoxylin and eosin.

Assessments

The primary outcomes of pH electrode performance test were response capability and accuracy. Response capability refers to the duration required for the pH electrode to stabilize the measured pH output from immersion into the test solution. It was classified into three grades: excellent (<3 s), good (3–5 s), and poor (>5 s). The measurements were categorized into three pH ranges: acidic (pH <4), weakly acidic (pH 4–7), and weakly alkaline (pH >7). Accuracy was classified into three grades: excellent (measured values within the same pH range as the reference value with an error <1.00), good (same pH range with an error ≥1.00), and poor (values in divergent pH ranges).

The primary outcomes of the reflux monitoring experiment were the detection rate of reflux events and esophageal AET. Esophageal AET was defined as the cumulative time of pH below 4.0 in the esophagus. The data obtained by the pH-impedance catheter were preprocessed by an automated analysis system, and then the corrected results were provided by at least two physicians with more than 5 years of clinical experience. Surgical complications (e.g. wound infection or disruption, intestinal fistula), anesthesia-related adverse events (such as coughing, aspiration, and hypoxemia) as well as device-related defects including infection or mechanical injury, were recorded.

Statistical analysis

Categorical data are presented as frequencies and percentages. Intergroup rate comparisons were analyzed using χ2 tests or Fisher’s exact tests (applied when sample sizes <40 or expected frequencies <5), with statistical significance defined at P < 0.05. Differences in mean esophageal AET between the two groups were evaluated via paired-sample t-tests, preceded by Shapiro–Wilk normality testing of the difference data. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA).

Results

Performance of pH electrode

In the first phase of the stability test, the pH electrode showed a fast response (rated as “excellent”) when in contact with each SBS. No signal drift occurred, and excellent accuracy was achieved (Figure 4A). In the second stage of the human gastric juice interference experiment, the electrode still had excellent responsiveness and accuracy in the pH 1.68 SBS, and no signal drift was observed (Figure 4B). In contrast, in the SBS measurement at pH 9.18, the pH electrode was evaluated as having a “good” responsiveness and accuracy grade after being disturbed by gastric juice, with no significant data drift (Figure 4C). Collectively, although the interference effect caused by human gastric juice in SBS at pH 9.18 was slightly larger than that in SBS at pH 1.68, the performance of the pH electrode was still within the acceptable operating range for practical applications.

Results of pH electrode performance test. (A) Stability of pH electrode. (B and C) Anti-interference ability of pH electrode.

In vitro reflux monitoring

The reflux visualization and pH data recorded by the EndoMonitor System are shown in Supplementary Video 1 and Figure 5, respectively. The EndoMonitor System achieved 100% detection rates for horizontal acid reflux and distal acid and mixed reflux episodes, significantly outperforming the pH-impedance catheter (49.1%, 18.6%, and 32.7%, respectively, all P < 0.001; Table 1). Superior proximal reflux detection was also observed (acid reflux: 100% vs 90.9%, mixed reflux: 100% vs 88.1%, both P < 0.05). No significant difference emerged in horizontal mixed reflux detection between the two devices (100% vs 96.8%; P = 0.496).

In vivo reflux monitoring

Three Bama miniature pigs were successfully constructed as GERD animal models by complete duodenal ligation. Postoperative observation of the animals revealed that activity was normal, no signs of infection were observed in the surgical wound, and no adverse events were reported. The EndoMonitor System documented gastroesophageal junction laxity and patchy mucosal erythema in the distal esophagus (Figure 6A). Histopathologic change showed mild inflammation characterized by scattered inflammatory cell infiltration within the superficial lamina propria and intraepithelial regions as well as focal epithelial denudation (Figure 6B).

Evaluation of gastroesophageal reflux animal modeling effect. (A1) Relaxation of cardia. (A2) Circumscribed areas of erythema in the lower esophagus. (B1) Epithelium exfoliate (HE, ×200). (B2) Infiltration of few inflammatory cells (HE, ×400). HE = hematoxylin-eosin staining.

The EndoMonitor System could be used to detect reflux events across all six in vivo experimental groups with 100% accuracy. In contrast, the MII-pH catheter exhibited variable detection rates in these groups (Table 2). Statistically significant differences (P < 0.05) were observed in reflux episode detection rates between the EndoMonitor System and MII-pH catheter across four of six comparative groups. However, the overall reflux episode detection rate showed a statistically significant advantage for the EndoMonitor System (100% vs 82.0%; P < 0.001). Although lower esophageal AET values were recorded by the EndoMonitor System compared with the MII-pH catheter (mean ± standard deviation: 2.62 ± 1.82 vs 2.52 ± 1.74 min), this difference lacked statistical significance (P = 0.949) (Table 3). That is, the two devices were comparable in their capabilities of AET monitoring.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a pH-integrated ultrathin endoscope to enable visual-physiological reflux monitoring. Following confirmation of pH electrode performance, the efficacy and operability of the EndoMonitor System were validated by in vitro and in vivo experiments. In vitro subgroup analyses showed that the EndoMonitor System was superior to the pH-impedance catheter in its ability to capture comprehensive reflux data, especially for distal reflux events that are not accessible with a conventional catheter. The delayed transmission of pH-impedance data resulted in less precise measurements, whereas real-time imaging by EndoMonitor provided esophageal pH <4 duration values closer to actual conditions. Thus, the EndoMonitor system exhibited superior efficiency and feasibility over traditional pH-impedance approaches.

Esophageal AET in patients with GERD exhibits inherent day-to-day variability [18]. Refluxate pH levels critically correlate with clinical symptoms, therapeutic responses and prognosis [19]. Clinically, the symptoms of non-erosive reflux disease are mainly caused by acidic reflux, while reflux hypersensitivity is highly correlated with non-acidic reflux [20], which makes pH electrodes a key component in ambulatory reflux monitoring equipment. Although the pH electrode was slightly less accurate in alkaline SBS than in acidic SBS, it was still effective in distinguishing acidic from non-acidic reflux in subsequent in vitro and animal experiments. The pH electrode induces a larger signal drift in alkaline SBS than in acidic SBS, which is attributed to the characteristics of the antimony electrode. The hydroxylation reaction (Sb2O3 + H2O → 2SbO(OH)) of antimony oxide film in alkaline medium will change the surface electronic state of the electrode. In addition, unlike the measurement of flowing liquid, prolonged contact with the stationary liquid may also cause an additional slight drift due to changes in the surface electronic state [21]. However, refluxate pH ≥9 is extremely rare in clinical practice. Therefore, pH electrodes have been suitable for reflux monitoring applications.

Although pH-impedance monitoring remains the preferred method for dynamic assessment of GERD, the clinical utility of quantifying reflux episodes was initially unvalidated for a long time, caused largely by marked interobserver variability in pH-impedance interpretation [9, 22]. Rogers et al. [7] later clarified the value of total reflux count in predicting prognosis in a reflux-dominated patient population. The efficacy of total reflux count in predicting symptom relief was comparable to that of AET (69% sensitivity, 70% specificity) [23], which supported its clinical use [3, 7]. Recent evidence further links supragastric belching frequency to preceding reflux episodes [24], emphasizing the need for meticulous interpretation of pH-impedance data in specific clinical contexts. Consequently, we selected total reflux episodes and AET as primary endpoints for EndoMonitor evaluation. Although automated analysis of monitoring metrics has been of high clinical value, several challenges and questions arise, including the occasional repeat counts of reflux episodes [10] and the interference of signal artifacts, tolerance limitations, dietary factors and the influence of hiccups [25]. Post-procedural manual analysis of reflux data (rather than real-time visualization) introduces discrepancies in identifying total reflux episodes, particularly distal events [9]. Anatomically, pH-impedance catheter positioning is based on the 5–6 cm high-pressure zone between the squamocolumnar junction and the lower esophageal sphincter [26]. Distal reflux occurs in 22% to 37% of healthy individuals [27], but its pathological significance is not well defined.

For free of these constraints, we designed the EndoMonitor System enables real-time visual recording of reflux events. In vitro experiments indicated that the EndoMonitor system was superior to the MII-pH catheter in detecting all reflux subtypes except for supine mixed reflux, for which its performance was comparable (P = 0.496). A likely explanation for this involves the fact that the mixed refluxate contains more multivalent ions and mucus, which allows the refluxate to remain on the esophageal wall for longer, and the increased electrical conductivity makes the impedance ring more sensitive. Thus, the results were more similar for both monitoring devices. In vivo reflux monitoring proved that the EndoMonitor System could completely detect reflux events, and the detection rate was significantly higher than that of the pH-impedance catheter in four experimental groups (P < 0.05). The remaining two groups exhibited low statistical power due to restricted monitoring durations. The system’s overall detection efficacy was further validated by total reflux event rates (P < 0.001). Notably, post-reflux swallow-induced peristaltic waves, a novel diagnostic modifying index in the MII-pH [28, 29], are visualized in Supplementary Video 2. Post-reflux swallow-induced peristaltic waves can assess the chemical clearance efficiency of both acidic and non-acidic reflux [8, 30] and is more closely linked to episodes of proximal air-containing reflux during wakefulness [31]. Therefore, the panoramic visualization capability of the EndoMonitor System may improve the diagnostic utility of reflux parameters by optimizing interpretation.

Previous studies have mainly focused on imaging-based reflux visualization techniques. Real-time MRI showed promising diagnostic performance for reflux monitoring, with a sensitivity of 0.78 (95% CI: 0.66–0.87), the specificity was 0.67 (0.45–0.84), and the positive predictive value was 0.87 (0.75–0.94), which was in good agreement with the results of pH value and impedance measurement [13]. Although the evaluation process of real-time MRI was noninvasive [14], it was not able to evaluate esophageal motility and mucosal conditions under direct vision like endoscopy [32]. It has been well established that there are obvious differences in the degree of mucosal damage caused by different reflux subtypes [19]. Endoscopy should still serve as a gold standard for the diagnosis of erosive esophagitis (Los Angeles grades B, C, and D), Barrett’s esophagus or peptic stricture [18]. However, it is impractical to apply endoscopy to continuously monitor mucosal dynamic changes during short-term evaluation. The intuitive design and easy operation of the EndoMonitor System make it potentially a valuable tool for tracking the progression of reflux-related lesions.

Still, this study is not devoid of limitations. First, it was a preclinical study for medical product evaluation. Symptom-related indicators such as symptom index and symptom association probability [33] could not be evaluated, which requires future implementation in clinical trials. Second, it was impractical to perform transoral device placement in conscious animals, which limited the number of simulated reflux episodes and the duration of monitoring. Anesthesia-induced reduction in tone of the lower esophageal sphincter increases the risk of aspiration [34]. Therefore, the duration of the experiment can be shortened as much as possible on the premise of ensuring that sufficient data can be obtained. Whether anesthesia or modeling was the main cause of lower esophageal sphincter relaxation was uncertain, the validity of the modeling protocol has been histopathologically confirmed in distal esophageal tissues. Future clinical trials will align EndoMonitor System’s monitoring duration with MII-pH catheter protocols in conscious volunteers, likely amplifying its comparative advantages. Third, simulated reflux events fundamentally differ from clinical pathophysiology. GERD primarily originates from transient lower esophageal sphincter relaxations, characterized by variable reflux composition, frequency and height. In vitro acid reflux events and mixed reflux events were separately grouped and simulated in a single form. Mixed reflux events of in vivo experiment were induced by restricting gastric emptying and increasing gastric retention. These approaches failed to simulate lower esophageal sphincter dysfunction and replicate GERD pathophysiology. Future investigations will be prioritized to evaluate the EndoMonitor System’s applicability in dynamic gastroesophageal reflux monitoring under physiologically authentic conditions.

Conclusion

This study documented the effectiveness and feasibility of the novel EndoMonitor System in simultaneously capturing dynamic reflux imaging and pH data. Although further clinical validation is required, the system holds promise for elucidating the relationships among reflux events, esophageal motility and mucosal injury.
