Introduction

Psoriasis is a chronic immune-mediated, inflammatory skin disease, typically presenting with silvery scaling plaques on erythematous background predominantly on knees, elbows and gluteal cleft.1

Prevalence estimates range from 0.1% to 11.4%, with higher rates reported in older individuals and high-income populations (Western Europe and North America).2 Among the comorbidities associated with psoriasis, psoriatic arthritis is one of the most common, affecting 20–30% of patients.3 Cardiometabolic and psychiatric conditions also occur frequently,1 whereas Crohn’s disease4 and, more rarely, uveitis5 are less prevalent.

The burden of psoriasis is determined by its chronic, disabling and disfiguring nature, often leading to pain, impaired daily functioning and reduced quality of life. It includes prevalence, incidence, morbidity, mortality and health costs and is often measured by disability-adjusted life years.6,8 Key contributors to disease burden include costly treatment, cardiovascular comorbidities and stigmatisation resulting in psychosocial distress.6

Recent advances in psoriatic treatments with non-biologics, biologics, biosimilars and new targeted therapies have significantly improved outcomes for patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis. Patients treated with these therapies achieve an average of 90% reduction in Psoriasis Area and Severity Index measures (PASI 90) and experience enhanced quality of life.9 10 However, the access and availability of modern therapeutics vary considerably across countries and socioeconomic groups, with limited availability in low-resource settings and among disadvantaged populations.11,13

Currently, our knowledge on global psoriasis epidemiology, treatment options, access to and availability of treatment, comorbidities as well as burden of disease is lacking. In many parts of the world, real-world data are scarce, and efforts to map the characteristics of psoriasis are challenged by the absence of standardised data collection methods globally. In response to the WHO’s14 call to close global data gaps through standardised comparable data collection, the Global Healthcare Study on Psoriasis (GHSP) was initiated to provide harmonised evidence on psoriasis and its burden of disease worldwide.

Significant inconsistencies in study results across populations have made it difficult to compare findings and fully understand the global burden and heterogeneity of psoriasis. A large-scale, international study conducted under real-world conditions is essential to address these limitations. The GHSP was designed to generate standardised, globally comparable data and to evaluate the clinical, epidemiological and socioeconomic impact of psoriasis worldwide.

Beyond quantifying disease characteristics and severity, the GHSP aims to identify disparities in access to care, treatment availability and the management of comorbidities across diverse healthcare settings. By capturing data from patients around the world, the study seeks to inform evidence-based strategies in dermatology, public health and policy—while accounting for gender, ethnicity and regional differences.

Cohort description

The GHSP, founded in 2018, is an independent, investigator-initiated, multicountry observational cross-sectional study; accordingly, each patient was assessed once at a single study visit only. This cross-sectional phase serves as the foundation for the ongoing transition into our longitudinal registry—the GHSSD, which will enable repeated visits and follow-up assessments in future research phases. Its primary aim is to assess data on psoriasis management, disease characteristics, severity, diagnostic standards, treatment approaches, comorbidities, quality of life and socioeconomic impact across different countries and continents.1112 15,17

To close the geographical gap in the understanding of psoriasis, particularly between low- and middle-income countries, the study applies a standardised data collection tool, allowing direct comparisons of disease burden and healthcare access.11

Originally established as a cross-sectional study, the GHSP is currently transitioning into a global, real-world registry of patients with psoriasis and other inflammatory skin diseases. This gives us the possibility to collect longitudinal data. Ethics approval has already been granted for this expanded phase under the name ‘Global Healthcare Registry of Skin Diseases’.

Primary objectives

The primary objective of the GHSP is to conduct a comprehensive analysis of global variations in psoriasis characteristics, severity, diagnostic approaches, treatment standards and comorbidities, as well as their impact on quality of life and socioeconomic status. This includes investigating dermatological healthcare utilisation worldwide, environmental factors, regional availability of treatments, urban versus rural healthcare disparities and socioeconomic influences. By comparing patient populations across different world regions and healthcare systems, the study seeks to identify patterns, disparities and key barriers to effective disease management. Ultimately, these insights will help develop strategies to improve access to and the quality of dermatological healthcare globally.

Endpoints include healthcare utilisation patterns, barriers and disparities in access to care and the impact of environmental and socioeconomic factors on treatment outcomes and patient satisfaction.

Secondary objectives

The secondary objectives are to provide detailed analysis of additional factors influencing dermatological healthcare outcomes.

Environmental factors

Examines how climate, pollution and ultraviolet exposure affect inflammatory skin diseases, focusing on severity, symptoms and treatment outcomes. Endpoints include correlations between environmental data and disease severity, symptoms and treatment effectiveness.

Regional access to and availability of treatment options

Investigate the distribution and accessibility across different regions and challenges faced in introducing new therapies. By identifying these barriers, the study seeks to provide a comprehensive understanding of the obstacles that hinder effective dermatological care. Endpoints include regional treatment availability, access barriers and their impact on treatment uptake.

Rural versus urban disparities

Explores differences in dermatological healthcare access and availability, barriers to access and differences in service utilisation. By identifying these disparities, the study aims to develop strategies to enhance healthcare access in underserved regions. Endpoints include travel distance to care, visit frequency, availability of specialists, patient satisfaction and cost-related barriers.

Socioeconomic impact

Investigate how social and economic factors influence healthcare access, treatment adherence, disease burden, disease progression and quality of life outcomes.

Together, these objectives support a comprehensive understanding of global dermatological health disparities, with the ultimate goal of developing strategies to improve dermatological healthcare while taking regional and cultural aspects into account.

Data collection is organised at three hierarchical levels—country, study centre and patient—to ensure coverage of all aspects of research and results in more robust data.

At the country level, investigators’ annual surveys capture access to treatment, approval of drugs, insurance coverage, prevalence of psoriasis, comorbidities and treatment options as well as national healthcare infrastructure.

At the study centre level, specific data about the characteristics of the study centre, its catchment area, patient volume and available treatment modalities offered are collected annually by study site investigators.

At the patient level, anonymised clinical and demographic data are collected, including disease subtype, comorbidities, prior treatments and socioeconomic background among others.

Recruitment

GHSP employs a secure, intuitive, web-based data capture platform that is easily accessible from any electronic device. Health-related data are collected from routine clinical care and medical records using psoriasis-specific questionnaires by trained healthcare professionals. Participants are enrolled globally throughout these centres participating in the GHSP.

The data entry platform features user-friendly workflows to facilitate rapid, consistent and efficient data recording with standardised psoriasis-specific case report forms across all participating sites.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All adult patients (≥18 years) with a dermatologist-confirmed diagnosis of psoriasis are eligible for inclusion. Data collection is conducted both retrospectively and prospectively. Informed consent is required prior to enrolment and was obtained during regular dermatological consultation. Exclusion criteria include inability or unwillingness to provide written informed consent.

Questionnaire

The standardised GHSP case report form includes 88 items consisting of binary, categorical and continuous variables and is completed by trained physicians at referral centres. The standardised questionnaire aligns with European registries, such as the Swiss Dermatology Network for Targeted Therapies18,25 and the German Psoriasis Registry (PsoBest).26 Data points include clinical scores (eg, PASI and affected body surface area (BSA), Physician Global Assessment), psoriasis subtypes, joint and nail involvement and patient-reported outcomes (eg, Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI)). Sociodemographic data, such as age, sex, smoking, alcohol and drug consumption, educational level, relationship and employment status are assessed. Comorbidities are documented. All study visits are conducted on-site at participating GHSP referral centres by a qualified healthcare professional (eg, medical doctor or a trained nurse).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics have been used to summarise patient data. Categorical data are presented as frequencies (absolute and relative), while numerical data are reported as mean and SD or median and IQR. Violin plots were used to visualise the distributions of PASI and DLQI stratified by country, including all countries with at least 50 patients enrolled. Countries with fewer than 50 enrolled patients were excluded from graphical displays to reduce instability and overinterpretation of sparse country-level estimates. Data from these countries remain part of the overall cohort and should be interpreted with caution. The statistical analysis was carried out with Python V.3.9.2.

Findings to date

Overall cohort characteristics

As of 26 October 2024, the GHSP included cross-sectional data from 3950 patients enrolled across 130 study centres in 22 countries spanning six continents (Argentina (n=32 patients), Australia (n=2), Brazil (n=944), Canada (n=11), Chile (n=649), China (n=313), Colombia (n=12), Croatia (n=83), Germany (n=7), Greenland (n=6), Italy (n=57), Nigeria (n=4), Peru (n=131), Philippines (n=111), Poland (n=26), Portugal (n=161), Singapore (n=153), Switzerland (n=808), Thailand (n=238), Tunisia (n=80), USA (n=121) and Zambia (n=1)). Data were collected by 160 investigators. At the time of analysis, 142 additional study centres had pending onboarding requests (figure 1).

Overview of participating countries, with included number of patients.

The study population consisted of 55.4% males and 44.3% females. The median (IQR) body mass index (BMI) was 26.5 (23.7–30.1) kg/m2. Overall, 16.5% of patients were current smokers, while 9.4% were former smokers. Current alcohol consumption was reported in 26.4% of patients, whereas 6.9% had previous alcohol consumption. The median (IQR) PASI was 5.0 (2.0–11.0), the median (IQR) BSA was 5.0 (1.0–12.0) and the median (IQR) DLQI was 7.0 (2.0–14.0). Psoriatic arthritis was present in 20.2% of the patients, and nail psoriasis was observed in 36.7% (tables1 2).

Socioeconomic characteristics

Health insurance reimbursement was available for 91.6% of the patients, with 33% having private and 58.6% public insurance, where 7.5% identified as self-payers. Insurance coverage was recorded as a binary variable (insured vs not insured) and without capturing the scope of coverage, reimbursement level or access to specific therapies such as systemic treatments or biologics. Most (66.8%) were employed, while 1.8% were homemakers and 6.3% unemployed. A total of 0.7% of patients had no formal education, 5.5% and 34.9% had primary and secondary education as their highest completed education and 30.2% had a university degree. Ethnic distribution was 40.3% Hispanic, 40.2% white, 10.2% Asian and 4.0% black (tables3 4).

Psoriasis severity

The overall median (IQR) PASI was 5.0 (2.0–11.0) corresponding to mild psoriasis. Among countries with at least 150 patients, Portugal had the lowest median (IQR) PASI with 1.0 (0.0–4.0), followed by Singapore with 3.5 (2.0–5.9), Switzerland with 4.0 (1.8–8.0), Thailand with 4.2 (2.1–7.9) and Brazil with 6.0 (2.0–12.0). China and Chile showed the highest median PASI with 8.7 (4.0–13.0) and 8.0 (3.0–15.0), respectively. Chile had the largest variation in PASI values, followed by Brazil and China. Notably, the median PASI in all these countries was below 10 (figure 2), a trend also observed in countries with <50 patients.

Dermatology life quality index

The overall median (IQR) DLQI was 7.0 (2.0–14.0). Among countries with at least 150 patients, Portugal had the lowest median DLQI at 1.5 (0.0–5.0), followed by Singapore at 3.0 (1.0–8.0) and Switzerland at 6.0 (2.0–14.0). Brazil with 10.0 (3.0–15.0) and China with 9.0 (5.0–14.0) showed the highest values. Chile and Thailand had similar median DLQI values at 7.0 (3.0–20.0) and 7.0 (3.0–14.0), respectively. Chile exhibited the widest IQR, followed by Switzerland and Brazil.

PASI distribution by treatment modality

Median PASI scores varied across treatment categories. For patients without treatment or on topicals, the median (IQR) PASI was 4.0 (2.0–9.0), while for those receiving phototherapy or conventional systemic therapies, it was 6.0 (3.0–12.0). Patients treated with biologics had a median (IQR) PASI of 3.9 (1.0–11.0). Across countries with sufficient data, PASI scores among patients receiving no or topical therapy ranged from 2.5 (1.2–5.0) in Switzerland to 7.5 (3.0–12.9) in Brazil. For phototherapy/systemic treatment, Switzerland showed a median PASI of 9.0 (4.0–15.1), while Chile reported 5.0 (3.0–8.5), whereas for biologic-treated patients, PASI ranged from 1.0 (0.0–3.0) in Chile to 12.0 (5.7–19.6) in China (table 5).

Discussion

The GHSP is the first global study to systematically map psoriasis with the intention of improving the understanding of the disease by highlighting inequalities in access to care and the availability of treatment. The aim is to improve treatment options worldwide, highlighting disparities, raising awareness and driving future change to reduce the burden of disease. All country-level comparisons and treatment-stratified analyses presented in this cohort profile are descriptive in nature. They are intended to illustrate the analytical potential of the dataset rather than to support causal inference or definitive cross-country comparisons.

While median PASI and DLQI scores remain below 10 in all countries with≥50 patients, wide IQRs suggest substantial heterogeneity in disease severity and quality of life impact. The lack of consistent correlation between PASI and DLQI17 indicates that psychosocial burden may be shaped more by sociocultural factors than clinical severity. Similarly, BMI distributions reaffirm the higher prevalence of overweight in psoriasis patients compared with the general population.27 28

This observation likely reflects multiple physiological and psychological influences including social isolation, depression, poor dietary habits, reduced physical activity and increased alcohol consumption.29 Alcohol consumption has been reported in 20–25% of psoriasis patients, compared with 14% in healthy controls,29,31 with higher intake of saturated fats also frequently observed.30 31 In the GHSP cohort, 26.4% reported current alcohol use, consistent with prior findings and reinforcing the link between psoriasis and modifiable lifestyle factors.

Beyond behavioural factors, chronic systemic inflammation in psoriasis may also contribute to overweight and metabolic disturbances.32,36 Elevated inflammatory cytokines have been associated with both psoriasis and obesity, supporting a bidirectional relationship in which systemic inflammation may promote weight gain and, in turn, be further exacerbated by it.

Although PASI and DLQI often correlate—particularly in response to biologics37,39—this association was not observed in a previous study based on this data set.17 Greater DLQI variability in ethnically diverse countries such as Brazil and Switzerland underscores the need to contextualise quality of life metrics within sociocultural frameworks when evaluating the impact of psoriasis on patients’ quality of life.

Despite a high overall rate of health insurance coverage, large disparities exist between the compulsory benefits provided by public health insurance schemes, approval procedures and access to treatment across countries.12 In underserved regions, access to systemic therapies—including biologics and phototherapy—remains limited, often due to lack of reimbursement or regulatory approval. Patients with darker skin tones and individuals from minority ethnic groups face additional barriers such as delayed diagnosis, underestimation of disease severity, undertreatment and reduced access to clinical trials.

In contrast to existing disease atlases such as the Global Psoriasis Atlas and the Global Hidradenitis Suppurativa Atlas, which primarily focus on estimating prevalence and typically rely on retrospective data sources such as healthcare records or insurance databases from individual countries, the GHSP was designed to prospectively collect primary clinical data across diverse settings. Traditional registries offer valuable real-world data but often face barriers in under-resourced settings due to follow-up demands and regulatory complexity. By adopting a pragmatic, cross-sectional approach with simplified, one-time data collection, the GHSP enabled broader inclusion of countries and centres that previously lacked the capacity to systematically document psoriasis care. This low-threshold entry model not only facilitated participation from underrepresented regions but also laid the foundation for the study’s current transition into a longitudinal global registry.

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of the GHSP is its international, multicentre design, which enabled the collection of standardised, real-world data across a geographically and socioeconomically diverse patient population. The large sample size ensured sufficient statistical power for between-country comparisons and subgroup analyses.

The cross-sectional design does not allow longitudinal follow-up of individual patients, as participants are recruited at different timepoints of disease course and treatment. Interpretation of PASI and DLQI values stratified by treatment category should therefore be approached with caution, as treatment modality and disease severity are not temporally linked; higher PASI values among biologic-treated patients may reflect recent treatment initiation or delayed access rather than treatment failure.

Additionally, some countries contributed relatively few patients, limiting stratified analyses. Recruitment predominantly from reference dermatology centres may introduce selection effects, and insurance coverage was captured as a binary variable, which does not reflect differences in reimbursement scope or effective access to therapies. This limitation may partly explain why substantial disparities in access to care persist despite high nominal insurance coverage. As the GHSP transitions into a global registry—the GHRSD with continuous patient inclusion, these limitations are expected to diminish, thereby enhancing generalisability and scientific value.

Collaboration

Establishing the Global Healthcare Registry of Skin Diseases marks a key milestone towards enabling longitudinal analyses across countries and extending the research scope to additional inflammatory skin conditions.

We encourage collaboration with dermatology centres around the world to strengthen and standardise the data collection to obtain generalisable results. New study centres can be enrolled at (https://www.dermastudy.org). After enrolment, researchers from the study centres can obtain relevant data and are encouraged to participate in research articles comparing data across geographical regions.

With ethics approvals in place and patient recruitment underway, this evolving platform will play a central role in advancing global dermatology research and practice.

Conclusion

Our findings highlight global differences in psoriasis presentation, quality of life impairment and access to care. Significant disparities persist in psoriasis management and treatment availability across different geographical regions and underscore the urgent need for equitable, system-level interventions.

The GHSP offers a novel, pragmatic approach to closing knowledge gaps through standardised data from diverse settings. By capturing clinical and socioeconomic aspects, it provides a robust basis for identifying treatment barriers and informing policy, guidelines and advocacy to improve equitable psoriasis care.
