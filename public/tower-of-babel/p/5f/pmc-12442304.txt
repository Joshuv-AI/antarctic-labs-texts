Prevalence of antibodies against rubella virus, cytomegalovirus, hepatitis b, and Toxoplasma gondii in women of reproductive age prior to conception in Iran
Authors: Nasser Hajipour, Ehsan Mohammady, Gholamreza barzegar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Toxoplasma gondii (TOX), Rubella virus (RUV), Cytomegalovirus (CMV) and herpes simplex virus (HSV) are major TORCH pathogens that can cause serious congenital infections and adverse pregnancy outcomes if primary infection occurs during pregnancy. This cross-sectional study assessed the serological status of TORCH infections among 388 women of reproductive age prior to conception referred to a clinical laboratory in Tabriz, Iran, between April 2024 and April 2025. The objective was to determine the prevalence of IgG and IgM antibodies against Toxoplasma gondii, Rubella virus, CMV, and the presence of Hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg). Serum samples were analyzed using a chemiluminescence immunoassay, and data were processed with SPSS software version 22. Results showed that IgG seropositivity for Toxoplasma gondii was 28.4% (95% CI: 23.7–33.5%), significantly increasing with age (p = 0.004), with the highest rate (40.2%, 95% CI: 31.5–49.4%) observed among women aged 36–45. Rubella IgG antibodies were detected in 95.6% (95% CI: 92.7–97.6%) of participants, with the highest prevalence (98.3%, 95% CI: 94.4–99.8%, p = 0.000) in the 26–35 age group. CMV IgG seropositivity was found in 99.7% (95% CI: 98.4–100%) of women, with no statistically significant differences across age groups. HBsAg was present in 1% (95% CI: 0.3–2.7%) of the participants, and although slightly higher in older women, the association with age was not statistically significant (p = 0.384). These findings highlight a high level of immunity against Rubella and CMV, moderate exposure to Toxoplasma gondii, and a low prevalence of active Hepatitis B infection. Routine TORCH screening in women of reproductive age is essential as part of preconception care to prevent congenital infections and associated complications.

Introduction

The infections caused by Toxoplasma gondii (TOX), as well as other infections such as hepatitis B virus (HBV), rubella virus (RUV), cytomegalovirus (CMV), and herpes simplex virus (HSV), collectively known by the acronym TORCH, are among the most critical maternal infections, posing serious risks to women of reproductive age, particularly during the preconception period [1]. These pathogens are responsible for approximately 2–3% of all congenital anomalies worldwide. The “O” in TORCH refers to “Other” infections, which may include Parvovirus B19, hepatitis B virus (HBV), varicella-zoster virus (VZV), human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), and Zika virus [2]. These infections are of particular concern due to their typically asymptomatic nature, high seroprevalence, and ability to cross the placental barrier, leading to vertical transmission and potentially severe fetal outcomes such as miscarriage, stillbirth, congenital malformations, and long-term neurodevelopmental disorders [3]. Notably, the seroprevalence of TORCH-related pathogens varies significantly across different geographical regions [4]. Therefore, early identification of maternal serostatus, particularly before pregnancy, is crucial and provides an opportunity for implementing timely preventive strategies to reduce both maternal and neonatal complications.

Toxoplasma gondii, an obligate intracellular protozoan, is widely distributed and capable of infecting virtually all warm-blooded animals, including humans. The primary mode of transmission to humans is through the ingestion of oocysts from contaminated food or water, or tissue cysts in undercooked meat [5]. Congenital toxoplasmosis can lead to severe fetal damage, including hydrocephalus, chorioretinitis, and intracranial calcifications, particularly when primary infection occurs during early pregnancy [6]. Globally, the seroprevalence of T. gondii infection varies considerably depending on dietary habits, environmental hygiene, and climate, ranging from 10% to over 60% in different populations. In Iran, various studies have reported T. gondii seroprevalence in women of reproductive age between 25% and 50%, with regional differences reflecting local risk factors [7, 8].

Rubella, a vaccine-preventable viral illness, poses a serious threat to the developing fetus when maternal infection occurs during the first trimester of pregnancy [1, 9]. Congenital rubella syndrome (CRS) is characterized by a triad of sensorineural deafness, eye defects (such as cataracts and retinopathy), and congenital heart disease, along with other anomalies. Although universal vaccination programs have significantly reduced the burden of rubella and CRS in many countries, gaps in immunity persist among women of childbearing age in regions where rubella immunization coverage is suboptimal or where the vaccine has been introduced recently [10–12]. In Iran, rubella vaccination was incorporated into the national immunization program in 2003 as part of the measles-mumps-rubella (MMR) vaccine, but seronegativity among some cohorts of women of reproductive age prior to conception continues to pose a risk for fetal infection [12–15].

Cytomegalovirus (CMV), a ubiquitous herpesvirus, is the leading cause of congenital viral infection worldwide. Most CMV infections in immunocompetent adults are subclinical or present with mild flu-like symptoms [9]. However, primary maternal infection during pregnancy, or reactivation in seropositive individuals, can result in intrauterine transmission. Congenital CMV infection may cause sensorineural hearing loss, microcephaly, intellectual disability, and visual impairment. Due to the absence of a licensed CMV vaccine and the limited efficacy of antiviral therapy during pregnancy, prevention relies heavily on behavioral interventions and early detection [16]. The seroprevalence of CMV is known to increase with age and socioeconomic factors. In developing countries and among low-income populations, rates of CMV seropositivity are often above 90% by early adulthood [17, 18].

Hepatitis B virus (HBV), a major global cause of chronic liver disease, remains a significant public health challenge, particularly in regions with intermediate to high endemicity [1]. Maternal HBV infection can result in vertical transmission to the neonate, especially in the presence of high maternal viral load or hepatitis B e-antigen (HBeAg) positivity [19]. In the absence of prophylactic measures, perinatal transmission leads to chronic HBV infection in up to 90% of affected infants, increasing the risk of cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma later in life. Universal HBV screening during pregnancy is recommended in most national guidelines, and timely administration of hepatitis B immunoglobulin (HBIg) and HBV vaccine to the newborn can significantly reduce transmission rates [20, 21]. In Iran, the implementation of the national hepatitis B vaccination program has led to a decline in HBV prevalence; however, pockets of high-risk groups remain, including women of reproductive age prior to conception without prior vaccination or incomplete immunity [22, 23].

Despite the availability of effective vaccines for rubella and HBV, and preventive measures for T. gondii and CMV, many women enter pregnancy without knowledge of their serological status. This ignorance not only poses a risk for vertical transmission but also reflects missed opportunities for public health intervention. Screening women prior to conception allows for timely vaccination, education on infection prevention, and monitoring of seronegative individuals. Furthermore, it contributes to broader maternal and neonatal health goals by reducing the incidence of congenital infections and improving pregnancy outcomes [24, 25].

The present study aims to investigate the seroprevalence of antibodies against T. gondii, rubella virus, CMV, and HBV among women in Tabriz, a major metropolitan area in northwest Iran. By assessing immunity levels and exposure history to these pathogens, this research seeks to identify vulnerable subgroups, guide educational and immunization campaigns, and ultimately contribute to the reduction of congenital infections. Our findings may also serve as a baseline for evaluating the effectiveness of existing preventive programs and informing future policy decisions in reproductive health.

Materials and methods

Study population

This cross-sectional study involved a total of 388 women of reproductive age prior to conception who presented for preconception screening at a clinical laboratory in Tabriz, Iran, over a two-year period from April 2024 to April 2025. All participants were enrolled voluntarily after providing written informed consent, with the objective of evaluating their serological status regarding TORCH agents. Venous blood samples were obtained from each participant and immediately processed to isolate serum. Samples were centrifuged at 1200 × g for 15 min to ensure complete separation of serum from cellular components.

The sample size for the study was 388 women of reproductive age prior to conception, determined using the Transfield formula [26]. The formula for calculating the sample size is: n = Z2.P (1-P)/E2; where n is the required sample size, Z is the Z-value for the desired confidence level (1.96 for a 95% confidence level), P is the prevalence rate of the infection, and E is the margin of error (0.05 for 5%). The sample size was determined based on the prevalence rates for each infection: 27% for T. gondii, 94% for Rubella, 99% for Cytomegalovirus (CMV), and 2% for Hepatitis B.

Serological testing

Serum samples were analyzed for the presence of IgG and IgM antibodies against cytomegalovirus (CMV), T. gondii (TOX), and rubella virus (RUB), as well as for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg). The detection procedure utilized a chemiluminescence immunoassay system (Liaison® XL, Italy). All tests were performed in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols, and results were interpreted based on the provided cut-off index values. Routine internal quality controls were conducted on both the equipment and diagnostic kits, in compliance with laboratory accreditation standards.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics software, version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages, were used to summarize the prevalence of IgG and IgM antibodies against Toxoplasma gondii, Rubella virus, Cytomegalovirus (CMV), and Hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) among the study population. To assess the association between seropositivity rates and age groups, the Chi-square test was applied. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Seroprevalence data were presented stratified by age groups (15–25, 26–35, and 36–45 years) to investigate potential age-related trends in exposure or immunity. Confidence intervals (95% CI) were calculated for all main prevalence estimates to provide a measure of precision.

Discussion

The present study provides a comprehensive assessment of the serological status of women of reproductive age prior to conception in northwest Iran, revealing important patterns in immunity and exposure to T. gondii, rubella virus, cytomegalovirus (CMV), and hepatitis B virus (HBV). Our findings show an overall IgG seropositivity rate of 28.4% for T. gondii, with a significant age-related trend that highlights cumulative exposure over time, consistent with the parasite’s lifelong persistence following initial infection. This pattern has been observed in similar studies in Iran and other regions, supporting the notion that seropositivity increases with age due to greater likelihood of past exposure through undercooked meat, unwashed vegetables, or contact with contaminated soil or cat feces [27, 28]. The low IgM positivity (0.5%) indicates that acute or recent infection is relatively rare in this population, a finding aligned with previous reports that suggest most infections in reproductive-age women are latent and asymptomatic [27, 28].

It is important to note that the presence of IgM antibodies alone may not reliably distinguish between primary and reactivation infections, particularly in the case of CMV. IgM antibodies can persist for several months after the initial infection and may also reappear during viral reactivation without indicating a new acute infection. In our study, although the overall IgM seropositivity was low, suggesting a low rate of recent or active infections [29, 30].

In this regard, published data have reported a wide range of prevalence rates across different geographical regions worldwide, including France (51%) [31], Poland (43.7%) [32], Germany (20–77%) [33], Norway (9.3%) [34], China (less than 10%) [35], the United States (22.5%) [36], Turkey (85%) [37, 38], and Spain (18.8%) [39], India (45%) [40], as well as specific regions within Iran such as Tabriz (26.9%) [28], Yazd (20.85) [41], Amol (69. 91%) [42], Birjand (8.3%) [43], Sanandaj (25.7%) [44]. Additional reports include Pakistan (38.7%) [45], Guatemala (71.6%) [46], Ghana (55.95%) [47], Sub-Himalayan Region (51.5%) [48], Western Romania (39.6%) [49], and Sweden (14–25.7%) [50]. These discrepancies may be attributed to differences in dietary practices—such as consumption of thoroughly cooked or frozen meat—along with socioeconomic conditions, regional climatic variations, and advancements in food hygiene and meat production standards [51].

The findings of the present study, which show a rubella-specific IgG seroprevalence of 95.6% among Iranian women and 84.6% among those aged 15–25 years, fall within an acceptable global range but still exhibit some differences compared to international studies. For instance, the immunity rate in Iran is higher than that reported in India (83.4%) [52] and southern Italy (85.8%) [53], yet lower than the rates observed in Turkey (96.3%) [54] and Nigeria (97.9%) [55].

In this context, reported seroprevalence rates of Rubella infection have varied across different regions, including 100% in the USA [56], 98% in Mozambique [57], 95–96.2% in Turkey [58–60], 93.3–94% in Saudi Arabia [61], 91.2% in Hodeidah [62], 90% in El-Beida [63], 83% in the Sub-Himalayan region [48], and 75–96% in Iran [28].These discrepancies may result from various factors, including differences in the implementation of national immunization programs, the timing and extent of vaccination coverage, the quality of health surveillance systems, the presence or absence of premarital vaccination programs, and variations in immunity assessment methods [12]. Furthermore, the lower immunity rate among younger Iranian women may reflect waning immunity over time or gaps in vaccine coverage in this age group. Differences in study design, sampling methods, socioeconomic conditions, and cultural factors may also contribute to the observed variations [14]. Considering these elements, the results of the current study, while affirming the relative success of Iran’s immunization efforts, underscore the need to review and strengthen vaccination strategies, particularly among young women, to prevent congenital rubella and improve reproductive health outcomes [11].

In the present study, the prevalence of IgG antibodies against cytomegalovirus (CMV) among pre-pregnancy women was remarkably high at 99.7%, while IgM antibodies were detected in only 1% of participants, with no significant age-related differences observed for either marker (p = 0.83 and p = 0.892, respectively). These findings indicate widespread prior exposure to the virus in the studied population. Compared to a study conducted in Iran in 2015, which reported IgG and IgM prevalence rates of 98.8% and 5%, respectively [64], during the first trimester of pregnancy, the results of the 2022 study by Nabizadeh et al. (2022) show an increase in IgG prevalence to 99.7% and a decrease in IgM prevalence to 0.6%. These findings suggest that while IgG levels have remained consistently high over time, the rate of active infection (IgM) has decreased, possibly reflecting a reduced incidence of recent CMV infections. Additionally, when compared to international studies, the IgG prevalence in our study is higher than those reported in other countries: 84.5–95% in Turkey, 84% in Spain, 56.8% in Australia, and 39–94.7% in the United States [4, 55], Turkey (95–96.2%) [58–60], northern Kosovo and Metohija (96.2%) [65], Saudi Arabia (93.3–94%) [61], and the Sub- Himalayan Region (79.8%) [48]. These differences may be attributed to variations in socioeconomic conditions, public health standards, population awareness, diagnostic methods, and transmission patterns across regions [66]. Taken together with other studies, the present findings emphasize the importance of continued surveillance and health education in the pre-pregnancy period to help prevent primary CMV infections, which could result in adverse fetal outcomes [66, 67].

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection is considered one of the major public health challenges worldwide, and according to some reports, approximately 325 million people globally are infected with this virus. HBV is a contagious disease that can also be transmitted vertically to newborns [68]. Based on global classifications, the prevalence of HBV in populations is categorized into four levels: high (≥ 8%), high-intermediate (5–7.9%), low-intermediate (2–4.9%), and low (< 2%) [69]. Studies conducted in different countries have shown that the prevalence of this infection among women of reproductive age before pregnancy varies: 0.38% in the United States, 2–8.7% among Asian-born immigrant women [21], and 7.7% in Ghana [70]. In the present study, the prevalence of HBs Ag was 1%, which is slightly lower than the 1.2% reported by Nabizadeh et al. (2022). Moreover, the prevalence in our study was higher than the rates reported in some Iranian cities, including Babol (0.18%), Dehloran (0.59%), and Amol (0.62%) [71, 72], but was approximately comparable to the findings of Tanrıverdi et al. (2018) in Turkey, who reported a 1.2% prevalence among pre-pregnancy and reproductive-age women [73]. These similarities may suggest a potential role for genetic factors and ethnic diversity in the distribution of the disease, although further studies are required to confirm this hypothesis [74].

Iran initiated a national hepatitis B vaccination program in 1993 targeting neonates, which has been widely implemented across the country. Several studies have demonstrated a marked decline in HBV prevalence among vaccinated cohorts, highlighting the program’s effectiveness in reducing vertical and horizontal transmission [75, 76].

Considering the significant role of TORCH infections in causing congenital anomalies and serious complications for the fetus, identifying these infections in women before pregnancy and during reproductive age is highly important [9, 77]. These diseases are often asymptomatic in non-pregnant women but can be vertically transmitted to the fetus and lead to severe outcomes. Since access to molecular diagnostic methods is limited, serological testing remains the primary approach for detection [1]. Moreover, the prevalence of TORCH infections varies by geographic location and regional conditions, and higher prevalence rates may exist in certain parts of the country; therefore, conducting epidemiological studies is essential to accurately assess the situation [78]. The findings of the present study showed a relatively low prevalence of acute TORCH infections in the study population. However, due to the risk of vertical transmission and the resulting complications, screening and preventive measures in women of reproductive age are of critical importance [79].

Conclusion

Based on the results of this study, it can be concluded that among women of reproductive age prior to conception, the seroprevalence of IgG antibodies against rubella and CMV is very high, indicating a desirable level of immunity in the population against these infectious agents. In contrast, the moderate seroprevalence of IgG against T. gondii (28.4%), particularly in younger age groups, highlights the need for increased awareness and preventive strategies targeting this infection. Additionally, the low prevalence of IgM antibodies for all three TORCH agents suggests a low rate of recent or acute infections in the studied population. The relatively low prevalence of HBsAg (1%) observed in this study also reflects successful implementation of national hepatitis B vaccination programs, though continued immunization and surveillance remain essential.

To improve maternal and neonatal outcomes, the integration of specific public health interventions is recommended. These include: (1) Routine screening for T. gondii among prepregnancy women to identify those at risk; (2) Rubella booster vaccination for seronegative women of childbearing age to prevent congenital rubella syndrome; and (3) Incorporation of TORCH panel testing into national preconception care programs to enable early detection and timely management of infections. These measures can play a critical role in reducing the burden of congenital infections and should be considered as part of comprehensive reproductive health policies in Iran and similar settings.

Limitations

This study has several notable limitations. First, it lacks data on critical sociodemographic variables such as education level, employment status, residential setting (urban vs. rural), socioeconomic status, and ethnicity or regional background of participants. Tabriz is home to diverse populations, and ethnicity may influence exposure risks to TORCH infections; the absence of such data limits the comprehensiveness of our risk assessment. These factors are known to significantly influence the risk of TORCH infections, particularly in developing countries where inequalities in healthcare access, hygiene, and nutrition can affect both exposure and susceptibility. Furthermore, the retrospective design limited data collection to existing medical records, which did not include key behavioral and environmental risk factors. Specifically, information on dietary habits (e.g., consumption of undercooked meat or unwashed produce), contact with domestic or stray animals (especially cats), personal hygiene practices, and vaccination history was unavailable. This lack of comprehensive exposure data hindered our ability to conduct a robust risk assessment, especially for T. gondii and cytomegalovirus (CMV). Additionally, one limitation of this study is the lack of data on gravidity and parity, which are relevant variables in the epidemiology of TORCH infections. Future studies are recommended to include these factors to allow for more detailed risk stratification and analysis. Another important limitation is the inability to distinguish between primary and reactivation infections based solely on IgM seropositivity, particularly for CMV. IgM antibodies may persist for months following primary infection or reappear during viral reactivation, complicating the interpretation of acute infection. Thus, reliance on IgM without confirmatory testing—such as IgG avidity assays or molecular techniques like PCR—may result in misclassification of infection phases. This is especially relevant for epidemiological evaluations and public health decision-making. Future research should incorporate such confirmatory diagnostic tools to enhance the accuracy of infection staging and associated risk assessment.
