Long-Term Reproducibility of OCT Angiography Parameters in Patients with High Myopia
Authors: Yun-Sang Roh, Sung-Chul Lee, Min-Woo Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the long-term reproducibility of OCT angiography parameters in patients with high myopia.DesignA retrospective study.SubjectsPatients with high myopia and normal controls.MethodsSubjects were divided into 2 groups: patients with high myopia (HM group) and normal controls (control group). Intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) and coefficient of variation (CV) values were calculated.Main Outcome MeasuresIntraclass correlation coefficient and CV of SVD, DVD, and CVD.ResultsA total of 194 eyes were enrolled: 99 in the HM group and 95 controls. For SVD, values were 0.636/15.9% in HM group and 0.827/9.7% in controls; for DVD, 0.590/15.7% and 0.731/9.8%; for CVD, 0.672/9.8% and 0.842/7.2%, respectively. The global prevalence of high myopia has been steadily rising, and its ongoing structural alterations can contribute to visual impairment through various mechanisms.1 Therefore, in patients with high myopia, continuous follow-up is essential to observe and monitor their anatomical changes. The development of OCT angiography (OCTA) has enabled noninvasive evaluation of the retinal microvascular architecture, and several studies have reported microvascular changes in patients with high myopia using this technique. Shuaib et al2 reported that vessel densities (VDs) of the superficial and deep capillary plexuses were significantly lower than normal controls in children with high myopia. Wang et al3 also reported that superficial and deep macular VDs, as well as peripapillary flow density, were significantly decreased in the high myopia group compared to the mild or moderate myopia group.

Thus, although numerous studies have investigated high myopia using OCTA, it should be noted that, due to the elongated axial length and irregular anatomical contours, highly myopic eyes are more likely to exhibit poor image quality or segmentation errors. In their study on the association between myopia and VD in glaucoma patients, Suwan et al4 noted that unavoidable segmentation errors related to myopia represented a limitation. Spaide et al5 further highlighted the importance of assessing cross-sectional images, as segmentation errors in high myopia can result in significant interpretative errors. Therefore, it is essential to ensure the reliability of OCTA examinations in patients with high myopia. However, only a few studies have addressed this issue, and its long-term reproducibility has not yet been investigated.

This study aimed to evaluate the long-term reproducibility of OCTA parameters in patients with high myopia.

Methods

Patients

The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Konyang University Hospital, Republic of Korea (no. 2025-08-020). Medical records from January 2018 to December 2024 were retrospectively reviewed, including best-corrected visual acuity, intraocular pressure, spherical equivalent, axial length, and detailed histories. Owing to the retrospective design, the requirement for informed consent was waived, as confirmed by the institutional review board/ethics committee approval. High myopia was defined as axial length ≥26.0 mm, consistent with commonly used criteria in previous studies.6,7 Axial length was measured using partial coherence interferometry (IOLMaster; Carl Zeiss Meditec) as part of routine preoperative or diagnostic evaluation. Subjects were divided into 2 groups: patients with high myopia (HM group) and normal controls (control group). The control group consisted of patients diagnosed with unilateral epiretinal membrane, macular hole, or intraocular lens dislocation, with fellow eyes without any clinical ophthalmic pathology included for comparative analysis. Only fellow eyes without any structural abnormalities on spectral-domain OCT and OCTA imaging were included for analysis. Exclusion criteria comprised a history of ophthalmic diseases other than high myopia—including retinal detachment, inflammatory eye disorders, full-thickness macular hole, retinal vessel occlusion, and glaucoma—as well as any previous intraocular surgery other than cataract extraction. We also excluded patients with severe myopic maculopathy, such as choroidal neovascularization or severe foveoschisis, as well as those with marked chorioretinal atrophy that could cause significant segmentation errors during OCTA measurements; eligibility was therefore determined based on the presence of myopic macular changes within the analyzed macular region rather than on overall myopic classification. If both eyes met the inclusion criteria, 1 eye was selected to avoid within-subject correlation, and the eye with higher image quality was chosen to ensure measurement reliability, rather than to favor better structural integrity.

OCTA Measurements

OCT angiography was performed using a Spectralis OCTA2 device (Heidelberg Engineering). The device automatically captured images of the superficial vascular complex (internal limiting membrane to inner plexiform layer), deep vascular complex (DVC; inner plexiform layer to the outer border of the outer plexiform layer), and choriocapillaris (CC; 20 μm thick layer of the Bruch membrane). The projection artifact removal mode was applied for DVC and CC analyses. Each scan covered a 6.0 × 4.5 mm area centered on the fovea. Littman and the modified Bennett formulas were used to correct the magnification of OCTA images based on the axial length.8,9 Vessel density was measured using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health). Eight-bit images were processed with the default threshold settings to distinguish target features from the background in segmented grayscale images. The binarized images were then used to calculate VD, defined as the ratio of the area occupied by white pixels to the total pixel area. Two graders (Y.-S.R. and M.-W.L.) manually delineated the foveal avascular zone (FAZ), defined as the central avascular region of the fovea and measured its area using the built-in program. The mean of the 2 measurements was used for analysis. Image quality was automatically graded by the built-in software on a scale from 0 (no signal) to 40 dB (excellent). To assess the long-term reproducibility of these parameters, all measurements were analyzed 3 times at 6-month intervals. Scans with quality <25 dB, as well as those affected by fixation loss, poor foveal centration, motion artifacts, or segmentation errors, were excluded.

Statistical Analysis

Demographic characteristics and ocular parameters were compared using independent t-tests for continuous variables and chi-squared tests for categorical variables. Repeated-measure analysis of variance was used to identify a significant change in the 3 measurements within each group. The reproducibility of each OCTA parameter was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), coefficient of variation (CV), and reproducibility limit. Intraclass correlation coefficient was calculated as the ratio of between-subject variance to total variance, with values interpreted as poor (<0.40), fair (0.40–0.59), good (0.60–0.74), or excellent (0.75–1.00). Coefficient of variation (%) was calculated as (standard deviation/overall mean) × 100, with values <10% indicating good reproducibility. The reproducibility limit was calculated as 1.96√2 × standard deviation, representing the range within which 95% of repeated measurements are expected to fall. Agreement among the 3 measurements was assessed using Bland–Altman plots for each pairwise comparison. Given that CV is inherently influenced by the mean value, reproducibility was not interpreted based on CV alone. Instead, CV was primarily used to assess relative variability and to explore factors associated with measurement reproducibility. Accordingly, univariable and multivariable linear regression analyses were performed for the CV of VD values in each layer to identify factors associated with the reproducibility of VD measurements. All statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS Statistics software (ver. 18.0; IBM Corp).

Results

Patients

A total of 194 eyes were enrolled: 99 eyes for the HM group and 95 eyes for the control group (Table 1). The mean age was 48.0 ± 13.5 years in the HM group and 50.4 ± 12.3 years in the control group, respectively (P = 0.573). The mean spherical equivalent was –5.75 ± 4.88 and –0.28 ± 1.79 diopters (P < 0.001), and the mean axial length was 27.5 ± 1.4 and 23.8 ± 1.1 mm (P < 0.001) in each group. The choroidal thickness was 195.4 ± 83.6 and 283.0 ± 96.9 μm (P < 0.001) in each group. Hypertension (P = 0.001) and diabetes mellitus (P < 0.001) were significantly more prevalent in the control group than in the HM group. Other factors, including sex, lens status, best-corrected visual acuity, intraocular pressure, and central macular thickness, were not significantly different between the 2 groups.

OCTA Measurement

The mean OCTA quality of the 3 examinations was 31.8 ± 1.9 dB in the HM group and 32.9 ± 2.6 dB in the control group (P = 0.001). In the HM group, the FAZ area was 0.32 ± 0.18, 0.31 ± 0.16, and 0.30 ± 0.16 mm2 at the first, second, and third measurements, respectively (P = 0.284) (Table 2). In the control group, the corresponding values were 0.40 ± 0.15, 0.41 ± 0.16, and 0.41 ± 0.15 mm2 (P = 0.120) (Fig 1). Intergrader agreement for FAZ area measurements was good, with an ICC of 0.898 in the HM group and 0.923 in the control group. In the HM group, the superficial vessel density (SVD) was 22.0 ± 6.7, 22.2 ± 6.3, and 21.8 ± 6.3% at the 3 measurements (P = 0.738), whereas in the control group, the corresponding values were 23.4 ± 6.4, 23.3 ± 6.4, and 23.1 ± 6.0% (P = 0.849) (Fig 2). For the deep vessel density (DVD), the HM group showed values of 20.1 ± 5.7, 20.2 ± 5.4, and 19.9 ± 5.4% (P = 0.815), while the control group showed 23.0 ± 4.8, 23.0 ± 4.9, and 22.8 ± 4.8% (P = 0.857) (Fig 3). For the CC vessel density (CVD), the HM group showed 29.4 ± 6.1, 29.8 ± 6.1, and 29.3 ± 6.4% (P = 0.484), and the control group showed 30.7 ± 4.3, 30.7 ± 4.4, and 30.7 ± 4.5% (P = 0.968) (Fig 4).

Long-Term Reproducibility of OCTA Parameters in Each Group

In the HM group, the ICC of the FAZ area was 0.783, and the CV was 20.8 ± 12.8%. In the control group, the corresponding values were 0.931 and 9.5 ± 6.2%, respectively (Table 3). For the SVD, the ICC and CV were 0.636 and 15.9 ± 9.1% in the HM group, and 0.827 and 9.7 ± 6.6% in the control group. For the DVD, the ICC and CV were 0.590 and 15.7 ± 12.1% in the HM group, and 0.731 and 9.8 ± 6.1% in the control group. Factors Associated with the CV of VD Measurement in Each Layer in the HM Group

In univariable analysis, only the image quality was associated with the CV of the SVD (B = –1.38, P = 0.004) and DVD (B = –1.86, P = 0.004) (Table 4). In CVD, best-corrected visual acuity (B = 17.02, P < 0.001), intraocular pressure (B = 0.50, P = 0.025), axial length (B = 2.25, P < 0.001), the quality of OCTA image (B = –1.76, P < 0.001), and choroidal thickness (B = –0.03, P = 0.001) were significantly associated with the CV in univariable analysis. In multivariable analysis, axial length (B = 1.93, P < 0.001) and the quality of OCTA image (B = –1.13, P = 0.004) remained significant factors (Fig 5). No factors showed a significant association with the CV of the FAZ area.

Discussion

We investigated the long-term reproducibility of OCTA parameters in highly myopic patients. The study population consisted of patients with HM without overt macular structural abnormalities that could affect OCTA measurements, as well as a control group of fellow eyes without clinical ophthalmic pathology, predominantly from a middle-aged population. The FAZ area demonstrated relatively good reproducibility, while SVD and CVD showed only moderate reproducibility, with DVD exhibiting the poorest reproducibility among the parameters. These parameters all showed significantly lower reproducibility compared with the control group. OCT angiography image quality showed a significant association with the CVs of SVD, DVD, and CVD, while axial length was significantly associated with the CV of CVD.

Our findings indicate that the long-term reproducibility of SVD and DVD measurements was reduced in highly myopic patients compared with controls, with DVD showing particularly poorer reproducibility. It is also noteworthy that, despite the control group demonstrating excellent reproducibility for all other parameters, the reproducibility of DVD measurements was somewhat lower than that of the others. This could be explained by the clear boundary provided by the internal limiting membrane in the superficial vascular complex, whereas in the DVC, segmentation must be performed within the retinal layers, making it more susceptible to subtle errors or minor discrepancies between examinations. A steeper retinal contour in highly myopic eyes may further exacerbate such errors, which could partly account for the poorer reproducibility observed in DVD measurements in this population. Accordingly, analyses of retinal VD changes in highly myopic patients should consider these factors, with special attention to the interpretation of VD in the DVC, where reproducibility is particularly limited.

Among highly myopic patients, the CVs of both SVD and DVD showed significant associations with OCTA image quality, suggesting that decreased image quality is linked to poorer long-term reproducibility. A previous report has shown that the long-term reproducibility of VD in normal individuals is influenced by signal strength (corresponding to image quality), and our findings suggest that this may also be the case in high myopia.10 It is noteworthy that, although this study included only OCTA image quality of ≥25 dB, which is indicative of a reasonably acceptable quality, the results nevertheless remained influenced by image quality. In clinical practice, OCTA image quality is often lower in highly myopic eyes, partly attributable to anatomical factors such as elongated axial length and irregular retinal contour; accordingly, although the present study included OCTA images with relatively acceptable quality, image quality remained significantly lower in the HM group. Therefore, in a clinical setting—where OCTA image quality is often poorer—the reproducibility of OCTA measurements in highly myopic eyes may be more strongly dependent on image quality and consequently lower than that observed in the present study. In this context, when comparing OCTA parameters across visits or between individuals, consideration of image quality or restriction to images meeting predefined quality criteria may be warranted in research settings. In routine clinical practice, OCTA findings in highly myopic eyes should be interpreted cautiously, taking image quality into account rather than relying solely on absolute quantitative values.

Choriocapillaris vessel density measurements demonstrated a moderate long-term reproducibility, which was also lower than that observed in the control group, similar to retinal VD. The CV was lower in CVD than in SVD and DVD, a finding that can be explained by the higher mean value of CVD, since the CV is inherently affected by the mean. In addition, as with retinal VD, the reproducibility of CVD was also influenced by OCTA image quality. Therefore, even when the overall image quality appears relatively high, caution is warranted in interpreting CVD if the quality differs between examinations performed over a longer interval. It is noteworthy that, unlike retinal VDs, which showed no significant association between axial length and their reproducibility, CVD demonstrated a significant association with axial length. This finding is thought to reflect the exclusion of patients with pathologic myopia who had structural abnormalities in the macular region. In the absence of macular structural damage, retinal VD appears to be less affected by axial length even in highly myopic eyes, whereas CVD requires more cautious interpretation with respect to long-term changes.

Previous studies have reported that manual measurement of the FAZ area in highly myopic eyes showed good repeatability, with an ICC of 0.891 and a CV of 8.8%.11 In the current study, the long-term reproducibility of manual FAZ area measurement demonstrated lower values, with an ICC of 0.783 and a CV of 20.8%. The relatively elevated CV is considered to reflect the dependence of CV on the mean value, given the small absolute FAZ area. Because CV is inherently influenced by the mean, particularly for parameters with small absolute measurements, it should be interpreted alongside ICCs and Bland–Altman analyses rather than in isolation. Despite lower reproducibility than in the control group, manual FAZ area measurement demonstrated a relatively higher ICC compared with other retinal layer VDs, indicating a slightly greater measurement reliability.

There are some limitations to our study. First, the retrospective nature of this study inevitably introduces selection bias, including potential bias related to the selection of 1 eye per patient and image quality. Second, because this study was based on real-world clinical data, repeated OCTA measurements were not obtained within the same imaging session; therefore, short-term repeatability of OCTA parameters, including intragrader repeatability, could not be separately evaluated. Third, patients with pathologic myopia who exhibited evident macular structural changes that could induce segmentation errors were excluded from this study; therefore, the applicability of our findings is limited in highly myopic eyes with macular structural alterations, which are commonly accompanied by high myopia. Fourth, although no structural abnormalities were detected on OCT or OCTA imaging, the control group was not a population-based normal cohort, and subclinical retinal or systemic factors cannot be completely excluded. Another limitation is that this study was conducted at a single tertiary referral center, which may limit the diversity of the study population and the generalizability of the findings. A strength of this study is that it analyzed the long-term reproducibility of OCTA parameter measurements—widely used both clinically and academically in the context of high myopia, whose prevalence continues to rise—based on a considerable number of cases. To our knowledge, such an analysis has not yet been reported.

In conclusion, in highly myopic eyes, manual FAZ area measurement exhibited relatively good long-term reproducibility, SVD and CVD showed moderate reproducibility, and DVD demonstrated poor reproducibility; all of these were inferior to those observed in the control group. The reproducibility of VD measurements across all layers was influenced by OCTA image quality, even when the quality appeared acceptable, with lower image quality being associated with reduced reproducibility. Moreover, unlike SVD and DVD, the long-term reproducibility of CVD was affected by axial length, suggesting that in highly myopic eyes without macular structural changes, VD measurements are more susceptible to axial length in the CC than in the retina. These factors should be taken into account when interpreting OCTA parameters in high myopia, both in clinical practice and in academic research.
