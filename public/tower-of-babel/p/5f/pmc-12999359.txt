Physical Activity Levels of Early Childhood Educators: A Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Myranda Hawthorne, Bayley V. Inniss, Rachel Heydon, Shauna M. Burke, Patricia Tucker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundEarly childhood educators (ECEs) play a key role in fostering healthy behaviours, such as physical activity (PA), in young children, yet their own PA levels are not well characterized. Understanding ECEs' PA is important given their potential to model active behaviours in childcare settings.MethodsThis systematic review and meta‐analysis synthesized ECEs' PA levels, focusing on (1) time spent in moderate‐to‐vigorous PA (MVPA); (2) the proportion of ECEs meeting the World Health Organization's (WHO) PA recommendation for adults; and (3) associations between ECEs' and children's PA. Seven databases were systematically searched using terms related to PA, childcare/education and childhood. Eligible studies reported quantitative data for at least one objective. Data for objectively measured MVPA and WHO guideline adherence were pooled using meta‐analyses, and associations with children's PA were narratively synthesized. Twenty‐one studies (5291 participants) met inclusion criteria; 18 reported on MVPA (11 in meta‐analysis), 11 on guideline adherence and 6 on associations.ResultsECEs accumulated an average of 223.6 min/week of MVPA (95% CI: 83.6–363.5), exceeding WHO guidelines but with high variability (I 2 = 97.6%). Overall, 52.6% (95% CI: 35.9%–68.8%; I 2 = 97.3%) of educators met the adult 24‐h PA guidelines, with study‐level proportions ranging from 21% to 92%. Narrative findings indicated modest but generally positive associations between ECEs' and children's PA.ConclusionsMany ECEs meet or exceed WHO PA guidelines, underscoring their potential as active role models in childcare settings. Strengthening ECEs' capacity to support movement‐rich environments could further enhance benefits for both educators and children through consistent, collaborative approaches.

Introduction

Regular physical activity (PA) is critical for overall health, including physical, mental and occupational wellbeing (Dhuli et al. 2022; Kemel et al. 2022; Kokkinos 2012; Stanton et al. 2014). Higher levels of PA in adults are associated with reduced risks of morbidity, mortality and the development of noncommunicable diseases such as cardiovascular disease, Type II diabetes and certain cancers (Dhuli et al. 2022). In 2020, the World Health Organization (WHO) released a global set of Guidelines on Physical Activity and Sedentary Behaviour (Bull et al. 2020). According to these guidelines, adults (18 to 64 years) should engage in at least 150 min/week of moderate‐to‐vigorous physical activity (MVPA) (MacIntosh et al. 2021). But, according to a globally representative analysis (Strain et al. 2024), approximately one third of all adults are insufficiently physically active, a global prevalence that continues to increase.

Early childhood educators (ECEs) play a dual caregiving and educational role supporting young children's developmental, emotional and physical needs (Cassidy et al. 2019; Tamblyn et al. 2024; Parker et al. 2021). Their occupational role involves frequent standing, bending, lifting and active interactions with children, yet it is unclear if ECEs, as a workforce, achieve the recommended amounts of MVPA to support their long‐term health (Holtermann et al. 2020; Prince et al. 2021). Their work is physically, intellectually and emotionally demanding (Rasmussen et al. 2018). And they often face systemic challenges including low wages, limited job security, minimal benefits and high emotional labor—placing many educators within lower socioeconomic status (SES) groups (Johnson et al. 2020). Individuals with low SES experience greater barriers to engaging in healthy behaviours, including PA (Foster et al. 2023). Ensuring that ECEs engage in sufficient PA is important for maintaining health, reducing chronic disease, and sustaining job capacity; as an added benefit, active educators may also foster more active behaviours among children in childcare.

Children's PA behaviours are shaped by interacting influences across multiple levels of the socioecological system (Bronfenbrenner 1977). The adults in children's immediate environments play a central role in shaping behaviours through modelling and encouragement (Lindsay et al. 2017). Early childhood settings form a particularly important setting, where ECEs directly influence movement opportunities by structuring active play and role modelling (Hinkley et al. 2016; Lugossy et al. 2022). The interactions between educators' PA behaviours and practices reinforce opportunities for children's activity (Kilanowski 2017); for example, higher educator activity levels are associated with more frequent use of strategies that promote movement in childcare (Wright et al. 2024). Further, centre‐level policies, training requirements, and cultural norms around PA also shape how educators can support children's movement (Lee and Park 2021). These interconnected factors highlight the central role of ECEs as facilitators and gatekeepers of children's early PA experiences.

Reported associations between teacher PA and preschoolers' MVPA are mixed within the literature. For example, an observational study conducted in the United States identified a significantly positive association (R 2 = 0.643, p = 0.005) between teacher modelling and preschoolers' MVPA measured via accelerometry (Carroll et al. 2022). In contrast, a study conducted in England identified a negative association (log [OR] = −0.434, p = 0.042) with MVPA (Woodfield et al. 2022). Two research groups from Canada have shown that ECEs who are more active are more confident and motivated to integrate movement into daily programming (Bruijns et al. 2021; Lu and Montague 2016). Bruijns et al. (2021) found that PA training had a positive influence on ECEs' self‐efficacy to engage children in PA, potentially increasing the quality and quantity of active play opportunities in childcare settings. Similarly, Lu and Montague's (2016) review highlighted that PA requires renewed attention and recognition as a core component of practice in early childhood education settings. Moving forward, promoting regular, high‐quality PA should be recognized as a fundamental priority for ECEs (Lu and Montague 2016).

Given that early childhood is a critical window for establishing lifelong movement behaviours, ensuring that ECEs are well positioned to model and promote PA represents an important public health opportunity (Jackson et al. 2021; Lioret et al. 2020). However, most research in childcare settings has prioritized children's PA outcomes (Bourke et al. 2023; O'Brien et al. 2018; Okely et al. 2021; Vanderloo and Tucker 2017), with educators' PA often reported only incidentally (or not at all) (Wright et al. 2024; Lum et al. 2022). Few studies have placed specific focus on objective measures of ECEs' PA, and there is limited evidence across diverse childcare settings (e.g., centre‐based vs. home‐based care) (Chen et al. 2023; Gustafsson et al. 2023). Collectively, these gaps leave our understanding of ECEs' PA level fragmented. Although individual studies provide useful insights, a systematic review and meta‐analysis are needed to synthesize the literature, identify broader patterns among ECEs' PA levels and clarify the overall relationship between ECE and child PA.

The purpose of this review was to explore ECEs' PA levels, specifically (1) ECEs' MVPA levels (min/week), (2) the proportion of ECEs meeting the WHO's PA recommendation and (3) the strength of the association between ECEs' and children's PA levels.

Methods

This review was guided by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) (Page et al. 2021) statement and was prospectively registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO CRD420251070706). Objectives 1 and 2 were both examined via meta‐analysis and narrative synthesis. To examine Objective 3, only a narrative synthesis was employed.

Search Strategy

Seven electronic databases were searched on 10 June 2025: MEDLINE, PsycINFO, EMBASE, CINAHL, Scopus, EBSCO Sports Medicine Database (SPORTDiscus) and Education Database. The search terms covered three main concepts, early childhood, education and PA (operational definitions in Table 1).

Search terms were guided by keywords and MeSH terms used in existing literature on similar topics, combined using Boolean operators and adjusted for each database. Reference lists of included studies were reviewed, using forward citation tracking, and additional literature was retrieved based on the eligibility criteria. A complete list of search terms can be found in Appendix S1.

Eligibility

Primary research articles using quantitative or mixed‐methods designs, published in peer‐reviewed journals, were included. No restrictions were placed on year of publication. Included studies had to be available in English or French and measure a population of educators working in early childhood/childcare settings. Eligible studies reported at least one of the three PA‐related outcomes of interest, including (1) average min/week of MVPA or another unit of time that could be converted into a study wide mean number of min/week; (2) the percentage or proportion of the sample adhering to the WHO PA guidelines of at least 150 min/week of MVPA; and/or (3) the association between ECEs' PA levels and those of young children in childcare. Studies that reported either objectively or subjectively measured PA levels were eligible for inclusion; however, only those using objective measures of PA were included in the meta‐analysis of means for Objective 1.

Articles were excluded if they provided only qualitative measures of MVPA, did not distinguish or separate MVPA from other PA intensities (such as total or light PA) or did not include ECEs as the population. Studies that presented data on ECEs' PA behaviours but did not include total time spent in MVPA as a mean number of min/week or a convertible unit of time (i.e., min/day) were excluded. Articles that were reviews and articles without full‐text availability were also excluded.

Screening Process and Study Selection

Articles were screened in two stages: first, title and abstract screening, followed by full text screening, both of which were conducted by two independent researchers. All screening processes were completed using Covidence. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion until consensus was achieved.

Data Extraction

Data extraction was performed by the primary author and confirmed by a second reviewer in Covidence. After consensus, the complete data extraction table was exported to Microsoft Excel. The extracted data included (1) study details (authors, country, year of publication and study design); (2) population characteristics (number of participants, age and sex/gender) and ECE characteristics (i.e., type of childcare workers included in the sample and type of childcare centre in which educators were working); (3) data collection approaches (accelerometry/survey), data collection setting (i.e., at work, during leisure time or both) and unit of measurement (e.g., min/week, min/day and h/day); and (4) outcomes, including time spent in MVPA per week (mean and SDs), adherence to the WHO PA recommendations for adults (proportion or number) and/or association between ECE and child PA.

Risk of Bias in Individual Studies

The Quality Assessment Tool for Quantitative Studies, developed by the Effective Public Health Practice Project (EPHPP), was used to assess the quality of the studies in this review (Thomas et al. 2004). This tool, previously used in systematic reviews of PA and early childhood interventions (de Sousa et al. 2022; Jylänki et al. 2022), provides a structured assessment of six domains. Each component was rated strong (1), moderate (2) or weak (3) along with a global rating calculated for each study. The quality assessment was conducted by the primary author and verified by a second reviewer. Given the limited number of studies available on this topic, those rated as weak were not excluded from the primary analyses but interpreted with caution. Sensitivity analyses tested the effects of excluding these studies in the pooled estimates.

Analysis

Meta‐Analysis

Where studies reported results separately for intervention and control groups, or across multiple subgroups, combined means and SDs were calculated in R studio (Version 4.5.1) using the metafor and metaHelper packages to obtain a single study‐wide value. For Objective 1, studies were included in the meta‐analysis if PA was measured objectively using accelerometry and the authors reported time spent in MVPA across the entire day. Because WHO PA guidelines are expressed as min/week of MVPA, when individual studies reported MVPA as daily means and standard deviations, values were extrapolated to weekly totals by multiplying by 7. This approach has been used in similar PA research (Brickwood et al. 2019) and allows consistent comparison across studies, while maximizing the inclusion of available data (Sharp et al. 2022). This method represents the most transparent and reproducible option given reporting formats in the literature. If studies reported data using multiple epoch lengths, only the 60‐s data were retained, as this was the most commonly reported epoch length used for adult PA measurements. In the meta‐analysis of means, random‐effects models were applied to account for anticipated heterogeneity arising from differences in measurement protocols and study populations. Effect estimates were expressed as mean differences with 95% confidence intervals.

For Objective 2, all studies reporting on adherence to the WHO's PA guidelines for adults were included in the meta‐analysis of proportions, where a generalized linear mixed‐effects model (GLMM) was used, the most appropriate method for proportion data (Schwarzer et al. 2019). Effect measures were expressed as pooled proportions with 95% confidence intervals.

All pooled effect estimates were calculated in R studio (Version 4.5.1) using the meta and metafor packages. Statistical heterogeneity was quantified using the I 2 statistic and the Q (Cochran's chi‐squared) test (Higgins et al. 2003; Schulzke 2021). I 2 values were interpreted as ≤ 40% minimal heterogeneity, 30%–60% moderate, 50%–90% substantial and 75%–100% considerable (Lin 2020). Sensitivity analyses were conducted by excluding studies identified as statistical outliers and studies rated as weak in methodological quality. However, given the small number of studies included in the meta‐analyses, subgroup and sensitivity analyses were considered exploratory and interpreted with caution. Where sufficient studies (≥ 10) contributed to a pooled analysis, publication bias was explored using funnel plots and Egger's regression test (Ioannidis and Trikalinos 2007).

Narrative Synthesis

All studies we narratively synthesized, conducted independently by the primary author, followed SWiM (Synthesis Without Meta‐Analysis) reporting recommendations (Campbell et al. 2020). Studies were organized by outcome, with patterns and trends identified, and key findings highlighted. A table was used to organize results for each of the three outcomes.

To examine the association between ECEs and children's PA (Objective 3), studies had to report a statistical measure quantifying the relationship between educator and child PA levels. Standardized betas and correlation coefficients were interpreted against conventional effect size benchmarks (Cohen 2013; Gignac and Szodorai 2016), and unstandardized betas were reported descriptively (Nieminen 2022; Aiken and West 1991). Because the studies that reported on this outcome used heterogeneous measures of association that could not be readily standardized, a quantitative meta‐analysis was not possible. Instead, these results were synthesized narratively to summarize the direction and strength of reported associations.

Results

The initial searches yielded 9679 articles. After removal of duplicates (n = 3314), 6381 articles were title and abstract screened. During title and abstract screening, 6306 studies were excluded, leaving 75 studies for full text review. Fifty‐four further articles were excluded, leaving 21 unique studies. Of these included studies, 11 provided necessary information to be included in the meta‐analysis examining mean weekly MVPA, and a separate group of 11 were included in the meta‐analysis exploring the proportion of ECEs meeting the WHO guidelines. Study Characteristics

Studies (n = 21) were published between 2016 and 2025, from the United States (n = 8) and Norway (n = 6), followed by Denmark (n = 2). Most studies were cross‐sectional (n = 12), randomized controlled trials (n = 4) and quasi‐experimental pre–post designs (n = 2). Participants worked within early childhood education and care settings with position titles including childcare workers, preschool teachers, assistants and directors. Study settings were most often daycare/childcare centres, with others in preschools or mixed settings. Sample sizes in studies ranged from 8 to 1434 participants (mean = 262.8; median = 159). The proportion of female participants ranged from 55% to 100% (median = 97%), and mean ages ranged from 34.0 to 49.4 years (mean = 40; median = 39.5 years). See Table 2 for study details.

Most studies examined time in MVPA (n = 18; 86%), followed by the proportion of participants meeting the WHO adult PA guidelines (n = 11; 52%) and a smaller subset investigated correlations between ECEs' and children's PA levels (n = 6; 29%). PA was most frequently assessed using accelerometry (n = 17). The most common devices were wrist‐worn ActiGraph monitors. A smaller number of studies used self‐report methods, such as online questionnaires (n = 2), or validated survey instruments such as the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (n = 1) and portions of the Behavioural Risk Factor Surveillance Survey (n = 1).

Quality Appraisal

Strong global ratings were assigned to four studies (Kippe and Lagestad 2024; Linnan et al. 2020; Ward et al. 2018; Ward et al. 2020), which used rigorous designs (cluster‐randomized or quasiexperimental), employed validated tools and demonstrated acceptable handling of withdrawals and confounders. Nine studies (Chen et al. 2020; Fossdal et al. 2018; Kippe 2025; Lagestad and Kippe 2023; Linnan et al. 2017; Nilsen et al. 2023; Schmidt et al. 2021; Tonge et al. 2021; Wester et al. 2025) received a moderate global rating, typically due to limitations in study design (predominantly cross‐sectional) and incomplete control for potential confounders. By contrast, eight studies (Broďáni and Lipárová 2016; Carson et al. 2020; Chaudhary et al. 2024; Kippe and Lagestad 2020; Petrić et al. 2022; Randall et al. 2021; Rech et al. 2023; Tovar et al. 2017) were rated weak, most often due to selection bias, reliance on self‐report measures and insufficient adjustment for confounders. Data collection methods were frequently rated strong, reflecting widespread use of validated accelerometer protocols. The full risk of bias assessment can be found in Appendix S2.

Objective 1—Time Spent in MVPA

The meta‐analysis of studies reporting on weekly time spent in MVPA included findings from 11 studies (n = 2617 participants) objectively reporting mean min/week of MVPA among ECEs (Figure 2) (Kippe and Lagestad 2024; Linnan et al. 2020; Ward et al. 2018; Ward et al. 2020; Fossdal et al. 2018; Lagestad and Kippe 2023; Linnan et al. 2017; Nilsen et al. 2023; Schmidt et al. 2021; Chaudhary et al. 2024; Kippe and Lagestad 2020). The random‐effects model yielded a pooled mean of 223.6 min/week (95% CI: 83.6–363.5). Reported means across individual studies ranged widely from approximately 92.7 min/week (Kippe and Lagestad 2024) to 881.9 min/week (Chaudhary et al. 2024). Heterogeneity was substantial (I 2 = 97.6%, 95% CI: 96.8%–98.2%), with a highly significant test for heterogeneity (Q[10] = 423.48, p < 0.0001). Given this degree of variability, the pooled mean should be interpreted with caution, as it reflects a broad central tendency across highly diverse study estimates rather than a consistent underlying effect.

Forest plot of ECEs' mean weekly time spent in MVPA (n = 11).

Visual inspection of the funnel plot for studies reporting mean MVPA suggested some asymmetry (Figure 3). Most studies were clustered around the lower mean values with relatively small standard errors, whereas one study (Chaudhary et al. 2024) appeared as an outlier, reporting a much higher mean MVPA than all other studies. Egger's regression test indicated statistically significant funnel plot asymmetry (t = 2.41, df = 9, p = 0.0389), suggesting the presence of small‐study effects. The intercept was significantly different from zero (bias = 6.997, SE = 2.8977), suggesting that smaller studies tended to report higher MVPA estimates than larger studies.

Funnel plot of studies included in meta‐analysis of mean time spent in MVPA.

Outlier detection using the random‐effects model identified the study by Chaudhary et al. (Randall et al. 2021) as a statistically influential outlier. When this study was excluded, the pooled estimate of MVPA with the remaining 10 studies was substantially reduced (169.3 min/week, 95% CI: 116.5–222.1) compared with the original analysis (223.6 min/week, 95% CI: 83.6–363.5); however, it still surpassed the minimum amount of MVPA specified in the WHO PA Guidelines of 150 min/week. Heterogeneity remained very high (I 2 = 97.5%, τ 2 = 5184.5), although the between‐study variance decreased relative to the full model (τ 2 = 35878.1).

Restricting the analysis to studies rated as strong or moderate quality from the risk of bias assessment (n = 9 studies; 2549 participants) produced an even lower pooled estimate of 153.9 min/week (95% CI: 109.2–198.6) compared with the overall meta‐analysis yet still exceeds the recommended 150 min/week as outlined by WHO. Although the reduction in the pooled mean suggests that weaker studies contributed to higher MVPA estimates, the degree of inconsistency across studies remained pronounced (I 2 = 97.3%, τ 2 = 3211.6). The persistence of heterogeneity, even after excluding lower quality evidence, indicates that other methodological and contextual factors likely account for the substantial variability observed between studies.

Together, these analyses suggest the true average for MVPA of ECEs likely falls between 154 and 170 min/week, considerably lower than the 224 min/week. This contrast indicates that the inclusion of weaker or outlier studies may substantially overstate typical activity levels.

Narrative synthesis (n = 18) revealed weekly time spent in MVPA ranged widely from 76.0 (Tovar et al. 2017) to 881.9 min/week (Chaudhary et al. 2024), with a median of 138.8 min/week and an unweighted mean of 221.7 min/week. Part of this wide variability is due to one study in particular, which reported an extremely high mean of 881.9 min/week of MVPA, measured with GTX9 accelerometers (Chaudhary et al. 2024), which is over 550 min greater than the second highest mean of 325.0 min/week (Wester et al. 2025). Although this study did measure PA with validated protocols, the small sample size of only 25 ECEs suggests that this outlying finding may reflect a disproportionately active group rather than being representative of the broader ECE population. The three largest studies, all of which included over 550 participants, reported very similar values, between 120.0 and 125.0 min/week. Of the three studies that used self‐reported estimates of MVPA, two were below 150 min/week (Petrić et al. 2022; Tovar et al. 2017), whereas the third is more than double the benchmark (Broďáni and Lipárová 2016). Taken together, the evidence does not provide a consistent pattern in ECEs' MVPA levels, with findings varying widely across study samples.

Objective 2—Adherence to the WHO Adult PA Guidelines

The second pooled analysis included 11 studies with a total of 3928 participants (Linnan et al. 2020; Ward et al. 2018; Fossdal et al. 2018; Lagestad and Kippe 2023; Linnan et al. 2017; Nilsen et al. 2023; Chaudhary et al. 2024; Kippe and Lagestad 2020; Petrić et al. 2022; Randall et al. 2021; Tovar et al. 2017), examining the proportion of ECEs who met the WHO recommendation of at least 150 min/week of MVPA. Under a random‐effects model, an estimated 52.64% of ECEs (95% CI: 35.9%–68.8%) were classified as sufficiently active (Figure 4). Considerable variability was evident, with study‐specific proportions ranging from less than 25% (Linnan et al. 2017) to more than 90% (Chaudhary et al. 2024). The degree of heterogeneity was extreme (I 2 = 97.3%, τ 2 = 1.2782), and both the Wald test (Q = 371.6, df = 10, p < 0.0001) and the likelihood ratio test (Q = 439.0, df = 10, p < 0.0001) indicated significant residual heterogeneity across studies, indicating that differences in prevalence estimates were unlikely to be due to sampling error alone. This suggests that the overall pooled estimate should be interpreted as a broad indication of average prevalence rather than a precise reflection of the proportion of ECEs achieving guideline‐consistent MVPA.

Forest plot of adherence to the WHO's adults PA guidelines (n = 11).

Outlier detection under the random‐effects model identified six studies as statistically influential (Chen et al. 2020; Fossdal et al. 2018; Tonge et al. 2021; Randall et al. 2021; Rech et al. 2023; Tovar et al. 2017). After excluding these, the pooled prevalence of ECEs meeting the WHO PA guidelines was 59.1% (95% CI: 46.6–70.4) across the remaining five studies (n = 1921). Heterogeneity remained considerable (I 2 = 96.8%), though the between‐study variance was markedly reduced (τ 2 = 0.29), suggesting that the excluded studies contributed disproportionately to the observed variability.

Removal of the studies rated as weak (n = 5) yielded a revised pooled prevalence of 46.5% (95% CI: 23.9–70.6) of ECEs achieving WHO PA recommendations. This was lower than the overall pooled prevalence of 52.6%, suggesting that weaker quality studies may have inflated estimates in the full model. Nevertheless, heterogeneity remained extreme (I 2 = 98.3%; τ 2 = 0.91), underscoring that differences in study quality alone do not account for the observed variability across studies.

Together, these sensitivity analyses suggest that the true prevalence of ECEs meeting WHO guidelines likely lies between 45% and 60%, depending on analytic approach. The persistence of high heterogeneity across both models indicates that additional methodological and contextual differences continue to drive variability in reported prevalence.

When examined via narrative synthesis, the proportion of ECEs meeting the WHO adult PA guidelines was highly variable (Table 2). Across studies, the proportion ranged from 21% (Linnan et al. 2017) to 92% (Chaudhary et al. 2024), with a median of 48.2% and an unweighted mean of 52.3%. The four studies with the highest adherence rates of 68.7%, 72.2%, 86.0% and 92.0% were those with the four smallest sample sizes: 64, 72, 43 and 25 participants, respectively (Fossdal et al. 2018; Lagestad and Kippe 2023; Chaudhary et al. 2024; Kippe and Lagestad 2020). The studies that used self‐report estimates, rather than device‐based methods, all reported adherence proportions that were at or below the median value for all 11 studies (Petrić et al. 2022; Randall et al. 2021; Tovar et al. 2017). The high level of variability of adherence rates among these 11 studies suggests that the true prevalence of ECEs meeting WHO guidelines is likely difficult to ascertain with confidence given the current evidence base.

Objective 3—Associations Between ECEs' and Children's MVPA

Six studies investigated the relationship between educators' and children's MVPA (Table 2). Kippe (2025) reported a small‐to‐moderate effect (r = 0.26; according to Cohen 2013, large effect under the more recent benchmarks; Gignac and Szodorai 2016) between ECEs' and children's MVPA. By contrast, Rech et al. (2023) reported a correlation of r = 0.98, which represents a near‐perfect association. Further, Chen et al. (2020) reported (β = 0.01) a trivial association (Cohen 2013); however, this result was not statistically significant (p = 0.93). Unstandardized betas were reported by Tonge et al. (2021) (B = 1.26) and Carson et al. (2020) (B = 1.27), and Fossdal et al. (2018) reported a statistically significant association between ECEs' and children's MVPA (t = 2.57, p = 0.021), but with a very small effect size (f 2 = 0.013), according to Cohen's (2013) threshold. These associations were all observed in accelerometer‐based studies conducted within preschool and childcare settings, and although effect magnitudes varied, the direction of association was consistently positive.

Discussion

The purpose of this systematic review and meta‐analysis was to examine ECEs' PA levels, with respect to (1) time spent in MVPA; (2) the proportion meeting the WHO's PA guidelines; and (3) associations between ECEs' and children's activity levels. For Objective 1, the meta‐analysis pooling 11 studies indicated that ECEs accumulate an average of 224 min/week MVPA. However, the wide confidence interval and statistical heterogeneity suggest substantial between‐study variation, and therefore, these results should be interpreted with caution. Meta‐analytic pooled proportions of ECEs who met the WHO guidelines (Objective 2), also including 11 studies, revealed that about half (53%) of ECEs met recommendations, with study‐level estimates ranging from 21% to 92%. These two findings reflect different approaches to evidence synthesis: one estimating the average weekly minutes across studies, the other capturing the proportion of individuals in each study achieving guideline thresholds. Finally, evidence examining associations between ECEs' and children's activity levels (Objective 3) suggests a positive relationship that supports the notion that ECEs' activity levels may influence children's active engagement during care hours. This underscores the potential for ECEs to serve as role models and facilitators of children's activity (Kilanowski 2017; Stanley and Kuo 2022). However, the heterogeneity of findings suggests the role of different contextual factors—such as centre policies, physical environments and staff training—also shapes these associations. Taken together, the evidence suggests that many educators achieve or surpass recommended PA levels and that these educators' PA levels can positively influence PA levels of young children.

Although our review did not differentiate between PA accumulated during preschool hours versus outside of work, it is possible that broader occupational and environmental factors, rather than individual motivation alone, play a role in shaping ECEs' activity levels. Prior research has shown that low‐wage, female‐dominated professions often provide limited opportunities for structured PA (Shaw et al. 2016), and similar constraints may apply to childcare contexts. The adherence rates observed among ECEs varied widely across studies. In some cases, they mirrored the compliance seen in the general adult population (Strain et al. 2024), whereas others were substantially higher. This variability suggests that sector‐specific barriers, such as low pay, high stress and limited workplace resources (Rasmussen et al. 2020), may differentially influence opportunities for health‐enhancing PA across childcare settings. These barriers can reduce access to affordable fitness facilities (Koh et al. 2022), limit time and energy to participate in leisure‐time PA after demanding workdays (Child Care Aware of America 2017) and create workplace environments that do not prioritize or support employee wellness initiatives (Arandia et al. 2020; Vaughn et al. 2020). Collectively, these factors may constrain some ECEs' ability to consistently engage in sufficient health‐enhancing PA despite the active demands of their job.

As there are no previously published formal syntheses summarizing the PA levels of ECEs, the present review offers a unique contribution to the literature. The finding that half of ECEs adhere to the WHO's PA guidelines is broadly consistent with evidence from the general adult population (Huang et al. 2025; Kracht et al. 2024; Liangruenrom et al. 2020). Evidence from European research identifying insufficient MVPA among preschool teachers and childcare providers (Gutiérrez Caballero et al. 2022; Hoffmann et al. 2013) has highlighted systemic challenges, including long working hours, lack of institutional supports and the perception that time for structured activity is a luxury (Randall et al. 2021; Jerebine et al. 2024). Although these challenges may be problematic for some, over half of ECEs in the current review were sufficiently active. Although fewer studies in this review focused specifically on associations between ECEs' PA and children's PA, the overall conclusion of a modest but positive relationship is consistent with broader literature. Specifically, researchers have shown that children are more active when led by teachers who are themselves more active (Cheung 2020; Copeland et al. 2012; Kyhälä et al. 2021). This aligns with theoretical frameworks such as the socioecological system theory, which emphasizes the importance of different parts of the environment in shaping children's health behaviours (Bronfenbrenner 1977).

Several steps were taken in this review to mitigate the common concern of potential lack of comparability across highly heterogeneous studies. Eligibility criteria restricted inclusion to studies that reported MVPA separately from LPA and total PA and to those using accelerometer‐based measures collected across the full day. Where studies reported multiple subgroups, data were combined into a single study estimate, and baseline values were extracted to reflect typical activity levels. All data were harmonized into a common unit (min/week), and random‐effects models were employed to account for expected between‐study variability. Sensitivity analyses further examined the influence of outlier studies and those rated as weak in methodological quality. Despite these efforts, heterogeneity remained high, reflecting genuine differences in measurement protocols (e.g., accelerometer cut‐points, epoch lengths, wear‐time criteria and device type) as well as possible contextual variations in geographic location, national policy environments, socioeconomic contexts and childcare setting characteristics (e.g., staff‐to‐child ratios, outdoor space and daily schedules). Accordingly, the pooled estimates presented here should be interpreted as descriptive summaries that highlight broad patterns rather than precise benchmarks of ECEs' PA levels.

The strengths of this systematic review and meta‐analysis include the systematic search strategy, the dual independent review process and the strict adherence to predetermined eligibility criteria, which were used in the selection of studies. The a priori specification of procedures for data extraction, analysis and synthesis, as outlined in our PROSPERO registration and consistent with PRISMA guidelines, is also a strength. This review represents the first pooled picture of ECEs' PA levels, which will advance this body of literature and help inform future practice, policy and research.

However, this study is not without limitations. One limitation of this review was the small number of studies suitable for meta‐analysis, which limited the precision of pooled estimates and prevented fulsome investigations into subgroup and sensitivity analyses. A second limitation was the heterogeneity among studies included. With a larger number of studies included, it would be valuable to investigate the effects of various confounding factors such as age, race, job title, education level or type of childcare setting, on PA level and adherence to the guidelines. Additionally, several studies reported MVPA in min/day, so to harmonize these outcomes with the weekly WHO guidelines, MVPA times reported as daily means and SDs were extrapolated by multiplying them by seven. This approach assumes uniformity of daily activity levels and neglects potential intraindividual variability (e.g., differences between weekdays and weekends). Consequently, the computed weekly estimates may be subject to systematic overestimation or underestimation, depending on actual day‐to‐day fluctuations in PA. However, the narrative synthesis ensured that evidence unsuitable for pooling was still considered, capturing contextual nuances and patterns that might have been overlooked by the meta‐analysis alone.

Future Directions

The findings of this review have practical and policy relevance. Given the inconsistency among MVPA estimates and the proportion of ECEs attaining guideline‐recommended PA levels, workplace wellness initiatives and professional development programmes may further promote active lifestyles among this population. At a policy level, investment in supportive environments and resources could enhance both ECEs' health and their capacity to model active behaviours for children (Lafave et al. 2024). Supporting ECEs' PA has the potential for dual benefits: improving educators' own health and well‐being, while simultaneously enhancing their ability to foster positive movement behaviours in young children. Embedding PA promotion into professional standards, childcare regulations and workforce development strategies could contribute to healthier trajectories for both educators and the children under their care.

Future research should prioritize longitudinal intervention designs, adopt standardized measurement protocols for PA and make efforts to include underrepresented groups such as male educators, home‐based childcare providers and educators of ethnic and racial minorities. Considerable variability in measurement tools and analytic approaches currently limits comparability across studies (Jake‐Schoffman et al. 2019; Sattler et al. 2020). Moving forward, research would benefit from the use of standardized outcome measures, validated accelerometer cut‐points, transparent reporting of wear‐time protocols and the open sharing of accelerometer datasets to enable pooled analyses and strengthen the cumulative evidence base in this field.

Conclusion

ECEs' activity levels are associated with children's PA during childcare hours, underscoring their potential influence on shaping early movement behaviours. Approximately half of ECEs engage in sufficient PA—positing that this cohorts PA levels are on par with those of the general public. By prioritizing ECEs' health alongside their caregiving role, policies and practices can create more supportive childcare environments and ensure that both educators and children are positioned to thrive.
