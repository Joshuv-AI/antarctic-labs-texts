Ninety-day venous thromboembolism incidence and associated risk factors after total and reverse shoulder arthroplasty: a national database analysis
Authors: Lina H Elias, Marisol Allen, Olufunmilola Adeleye, Anup A Shah, Midhat Patel, Evan S Lederman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVenous thromboembolism (VTE), including deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism, is an uncommon but potentially serious complication following total shoulder arthroplasty. This study evaluated patient factors associated with post-operative (PO) VTE after anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty (aTSA) and reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA).MethodsA retrospective cohort study was performed using the PearlDiver database. Patients undergoing primary aTSA or rTSA with at least 120 days of follow-up were identified using International Classification of Diseases, 10th edition procedure codes. VTE within 90 days post-operatively was the primary outcome. Logistic regression evaluated demographic characteristics, comorbidities, length of stay, Charlson Comorbidity Index, and prior VTE history as predictors of PO VTE.ResultsA total of 42,014 patients underwent aTSA and 93,358 underwent rTSA. PO VTE occurred in 256 (0.61%) patients after aTSA and 767 (0.82%) after rTSA. Increased length of stay, higher Charlson Comorbidity Index, obesity, congestive heart failure, prior valve replacement, renal disease, hepatobiliary disease, and pulmonary disease were associated with increased odds of VTE following both procedures. Diabetes, coronary artery disease, hypertension, malignancy, and rheumatoid arthritis were additional risk factors following rTSA. Among 4,576 patients with a prior VTE, 464 (10.14%) experienced recurrent PO VTE, corresponding to a 25-fold increase in odds compared with patients without prior VTE (odds ratio, 25.68; P < .001).ConclusionPO VTE occurred in fewer than 1% of patients after primary shoulder arthroplasty. Several comorbidities were associated with increased VTE risk, while prior VTE was the strongest predictor of recurrence. These findings may assist in identifying patients who warrant heightened perioperative VTE risk assessment.Keywords: Anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty, Reverse total shoulder arthroplasty, Total shoulder arthroplasty, Venous thromboembolism, Risk factors, Post-operative complications

The utilization of anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty (aTSA) and reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA) has substantially increased within the last 2 decades.4,8 While aTSA and rTSA surgeries have revolutionized the management of debilitating shoulder conditions, the incidence of venous thromboembolism (VTE) remains a concern, impacting PO outcomes and patient morbidity. Understanding the prevalence, risk factors, and management strategies for VTE in the context of these surgical procedures can help optimize patient care and surgical outcomes. The reported incidence of VTE events following total shoulder arthroplasty (TSA) procedures has shown rates ranging from 0.2% to 16%.7,15,16 Many of these studies were performed at small or single hospital institutions.

Furthermore, certain patient factors and demographics have been evaluated for an association with VTE including body mass index (BMI), cardiovascular conditions, smoking, hospital length of stay (LOS), operation time, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), prior history of VTE, and diabetes. When these are then applied to patients who also undergo shoulder replacement surgery, an added risk for PO VTE can be compounded.9,11,13 Outside of arthroplasty, obesity, congestive heart failure (CHF), malignancy, chronic kidney disease, and prior VTE are established independent risk factors for VTE in the general population. Consequently, distinguishing baseline thrombotic risk from surgery-related risk remains challenging in observational database studies.

VTE remains a concern in orthopaedic surgery, with in-hospital rates of deep vein thrombus (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE) occurring in approximately 1 in 100 and 1 in 200 patients, respectively, after total knee arthroplasty and total hip arthroplasty (THA) despite recommended prophylaxis.6 While established guidelines have helped reduce VTE rates in lower extremity joint replacements, no formal protocols exist for upper extremity procedures including shoulder arthroplasty.

VTE events following orthopaedic surgery lead to significant clinical and economic burdens, with costs nearly doubling for patients who develop complications due to longer hospital stays and increased inpatient care.14 Mortality is highest in the first three months, and recurrent events further increase health-care expenditures.4,12 As shoulder procedures become more common, understanding these economic implications is essential for informed decision-making.

The present study aims to compare the incidence of VTE rates as the primary outcome. The secondary outcome is to identify associated factors and the degree of potential PO risk related to VTE complications between aTSA and rTSA procedures.

Materials and methods

A retrospective review was performed using PearlDiver, a Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act–compliant proprietary database containing de-identified and anonymized medical insurance billing claims from over 165 million orthopedic patients with Commercial, Medicare, and Medicaid insurance in the United States. This database does not contain identifiable protected health information and informed consent was not required due to the anonymized nature of the data.2 Therefore, an institutional review board exemption was obtained. Patient cohorts and demographic information were identified using International Classification of Diseases, 10th edition (ICD-10), procedural and diagnoses codes, as well as Current Procedural Terminology codes. This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-collected health Data statement.3

Patient selection

The database was queried to identify patients who underwent primary aTSA (ICD-10-P-0RRJ0JZ, ICD-10-P-0RRK0JZ) and rTSA (ICD-10-P-0RRJ00Z, ICD-10-P-0RRK00Z) from 2007 to 2023. A separate query of a combined TSA cohort was also used to group patients based on prior history of VTE. For both searches, patients who underwent revision shoulder arthroplasty were excluded along with those who did not have at least 1 year of pre-operative management and 120 days post-operative (PO) follow-up to ensure complete capture of 90-day VTE events (Fig. 1).

Primary outcomes and variables

The primary outcome variable was a VTE event within 90 days of the shoulder arthroplasty procedure. There were 3 cohorts as follows: all total shoulder arthroplasty, aTSA, and rTSA. ICD-10 codes for DVT and PE were grouped together to create the grouped outcome VTE. The comorbidities and patient factors assessed included gender, CCI score, LOS, tobacco use, obesity, and type 1 and 2 diabetes mellitus. Cardiovascular conditions included CHF, coronary artery disease (CAD), hypertension (HTN), and valvular disease. Renal diseases such as chronic kidney disease and renal failure were considered, along with liver diseases ranging from mild to severe. Malignancies included lymphoma, metastatic cancer, and solid tumors. Pulmonary conditions assessed included chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and obstructive sleep apnea.

Data analysis

Arthroplasty type and outcome variables were summarized with frequency count and percentage. The Chi-square test was used to compare the incidence rates of VTE events in the aTSA and rTSA groups within 90 days after the procedure as well as those with a pre-operative history of embolism in a combined TSA cohort. Logistic regression analysis was performed to evaluate the association between patient characteristics and type of shoulder arthroplasty performed. Odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals were calculated to assess the relative weights of each factor. Statistical analyses were performed using R Studio software embedded within the PearlDiver platform. A significance threshold of P ≤ .05 was selected because this study was designed as an exploratory observational analysis. Given the number of predictors evaluated, findings were interpreted cautiously due to multiple comparisons and should be considered hypothesis-generating because no formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was performed.

In the combined primary aTSA and rTSA cohort, 4,576 (3.4%) of patients had a prior history of a VTE and 130,796 (96.6%) were negative. Of the patients with a positive VTE history, 464 (10.14%) had a recurrent VTE event after their shoulder arthroplasty procedure. Five hundred sixty-one (0.43%) of patients in the negative VTE history group had a VTE event post-operatively. Discussion

In this large retrospective study using a national administrative claims database, we found multiple patient factors to be associated with increased odds of thromboembolic event after shoulder arthroplasty. These findings identify patient characteristics associated with PO VTE and may inform future risk stratification studies.

Previous studies have reported low but variable rates of VTE following shoulder arthroplasty, with incidence ranging from 0.2% to as high as 2.6% depending on patient selection, institutional practices, and prophylaxis protocols. Saleh et al15 conducted a systematic review reporting VTE rates between 0.2% and 16%, though they noted considerable heterogeneity across studies and inconsistent reporting of prophylaxis regimens, limiting firm conclusions regarding true incidence and prevention strategies. Similarly, Tashjian et al16 identified a 2.6% VTE rate in a single-institution series, with increased risk associated with raised CCI, pre-operative thrombotic event, lower pre-operative hemoglobin and hematocrit levels, diabetes, lower PO hemoglobin level, use of general endotracheal anesthesia without inter-scalene nerve block, higher BMI, and revision shoulder arthroplasty. More recent analyses, including those by Lung et al11 and Na et al,13 utilizing national databases and systematic reviews, have consistently reported VTE rates between 0.6% and 0.8% and identified key risk factors such as hypoalbuminemia, elevated BMI, prolonged hospital stay, African American race, and higher comorbidity burden. However, these prior studies were limited by smaller sample sizes, single-center cohorts, or reliance on short-term PO windows.

The present study expands upon previous work by evaluating more than 135,000 shoulder arthroplasty procedures using multivariable regression to identify patient characteristics independently associated with PO VTE. Notably, a prior history of VTE emerged as the strongest associated factor, where 10% of shoulder arthroplasty patients had a recurrent thromboembolic event post-operatively. Additional significant factor included elevated CCI scores, obesity, CHF, longer hospital stays, and obstructive sleep apnea—all of which are consistent with systemic contributors to VTE identified in the existing literature. Interestingly, comorbidities such as diabetes, CAD, HTN, and rheumatoid arthritis were associated factors of VTE only within the rTSA cohort. Although several comorbidities demonstrated statistically significant associations with PO VTE, many of the observed odds ratios were modest in magnitude. These findings likely reflect overall medical complexity and frailty rather than strong procedure-specific determinants of thromboembolic risk. Accordingly, these associations should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, particularly given the potential for residual confounding and the absence of adjustment for multiple comparisons.

VTE remains a significant complication following orthopedic procedures, particularly in lower extremity arthroplasty. Januel et al6 reported that despite the use of recommended in-hospital prophylaxis, symptomatic VTE still occurs in approximately 1 in 100 patients following total knee arthroplasty and 1 in 200 patients following THA, underscoring that prophylaxis reduces but does not eliminate risk. In response to these persistent events, the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons and the American College of Chest Physicians have developed comprehensive, evidence-based guidelines that emphasize individualized prophylaxis based on patient-specific factors, pharmacologic options, duration, and surgical considerations. Lieberman and Heckmann10 further highlight the shift toward risk-adapted, multimodal prophylaxis strategies, balancing efficacy and safety. Recent national trends reported by Agarwal et al1 demonstrate that VTE rates following THA have significantly declined from 2011 to 2019, coinciding with increased aspirin utilization and more selective use of potent anticoagulants. However, unlike lower extremity arthroplasty, there are currently no formal VTE prophylaxis guidelines specific to upper extremity joint replacements, particularly shoulder arthroplasty. In these cases, prophylactic strategies rely primarily on individualized risk assessment and surgeon preference without the support of standardized protocols. Whether prophylaxis strategies should differ according to these associated factors cannot be determined from the present database.

VTE and its complications pose not only significant clinical challenges but also considerable economic and societal burdens. The initial health-care costs associated with acute VTE events are substantial and can escalate significantly with recurrent DVT, PE, or the development of chronic complications such as post-thrombotic syndrome. MacDougall et al12 estimated that the average total health-care costs for managing VTE, including its long-term sequelae, can reach tens of thousands of dollars per patient, driven primarily by prolonged hospital stays, extended outpatient care, and recurrent events. These findings are echoed by Cohen et al,5 who emphasized the considerable socioeconomic consequences of VTE, particularly in the first three months postevent when the risk of mortality, disability, and productivity loss is highest. In the orthopedic surgical population, Ruppert et al14 systematically reviewed the economic burden of VTE and found that patients who developed VTE after orthopedic procedures incurred nearly double the total health-care costs compared to those without complications, largely due to increased hospitalization duration and associated inpatient care expenses. As the volume of shoulder arthroplasty procedures continues to rise, particularly among aging, comorbid patients undergoing rTSA, even modest increases in VTE rates could have profound clinical and economic repercussions. A comprehensive understanding of the economic impact of VTE is critical for health-care providers, patients, and policymakers, especially as the demand for shoulder arthroplasty expands to address a growing population with complex medical needs.

Although the overall incidence of VTE following shoulder arthroplasty remains low, the potential for significant morbidity and increased health-care utilization necessitates continued emphasis on optimizing risk assessment and prophylaxis strategies. Our findings, together with prior reports, highlight the importance of individualized VTE risk assessment. Importantly, the present study identifies associations rather than causal relationships, and the observed findings should not be interpreted as evidence that modification of these patient factors or perioperative management strategies will necessarily reduce PO VTE risk. Prospective studies are needed to validate these associations and determine their clinical applicability.

Limitations

This study is not without limitations, especially those inherent to utilizing a billing-based database. These include reliance on the accuracy of administrative data, which may lack detailed clinical information such as perioperative protocols, thromboprophylaxis regimen, and intraoperative factors like surgical duration. Additionally, the use of ICD-10 and Current Procedural Terminology coding introduces the potential for misclassification due to clerical errors or varying interpretations. Patients who had a revision arthroplasty were excluded and not analyzed in this study. A critical limitation of this study is the inability to account for perioperative VTE prophylaxis, baseline anticoagulation status, malignancy activity, and immobility. Additionally, the study did not capture the type, timing, duration, or patient adherence to perioperative thromboprophylaxis or anticoagulation therapy. Consequently, differences in PO VTE rates may reflect variation in prophylaxis practices rather than intrinsic differences in patient or procedural risk. Another limitation is the lack of clinical context and details on the status of persistent risks. For example, a diagnosis of HTN and diabetes is available, but appropriate laboratory values are not available; hence, it is not feasible to determine whether these conditions are well-controlled or poorly controlled. Finally, due to the retrospective design, there is a possibility of selection bias and residual confounding. Despite these limitations, the large national cohort provides broad representation of contemporary shoulder arthroplasty patients and permits evaluation of relatively uncommon PO events such as VTE.

Conclusion

VTE occurred infrequently following shoulder arthroplasty. Several patient characteristics were associated with PO VTE, with prior VTE demonstrating the strongest observed association. Because important variables including thromboprophylaxis, anticoagulation status, malignancy activity, and other clinical factors were unavailable, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than evidence of causal relationships. Prospective studies are needed to validate these findings and determine whether they can improve perioperative risk stratification.
