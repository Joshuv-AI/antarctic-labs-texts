Diammonium phosphate supplementation in low-protein diets improves nitrogen utilization and feed efficiency without impairing carcass traits or meat quality in finishing pigs
Authors: Xianglin Zeng, Yan Tang, Siqi Li, Qiye Wang, Huansheng Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLow-protein (LP) diets improve nitrogen utilization efficiency and mitigate environmental pollution, however, excessive reductions in crude protein (CP) result in total nitrogen (TN) deficiency, thereby compromising growth performance. This study investigated the effects of replacing calcium hydrogen phosphate with diammonium phosphate (DP) in an LP diet on growth performance, carcass traits, meat quality, plasma biochemical parameters, hepatic nitrogen-metabolizing enzyme activities, and amino acid (AA) profiles in finishing pigs under TN restriction. Additionally, 15nitroge (15N)-isotope tracing was employed in suckling piglets to track ammonia nitrogen assimilation by analyzing 15N enrichment in plasma and hepatic AA following 15N-DP administration. Sixty-three barrows (Duroc × Landrace × Large White; initial body weight: 74.3 ± 3.8 kg) were randomly allocated to one of three dietary treatments: a normal protein (NP) diet (CP 13.47%), a LP diet (CP 9.06%), or a LP (CP 10.47%) diet supplemented with 1.07% DP (LDP). All pigs had ad libitum access to feed and water for 51 d. The ratio of essential AA nitrogen to TN was 0.43, 0.50, and 0.43 in the NP, LP, and LDP diets, respectively. Pigs fed the LDP diet exhibited higher gain-to-feed (G:F) ratio (P = 0.014), plasma urea nitrogen concentrations (P = 0.001), hepatic non-essential amino acids (NEAA) concentrations (P = 0.028), and carbamoyl phosphate synthetase 1 (CPS-1) activity (P = 0.018), but lower average daily feed intake (ADFI; P < 0.001) compared with those fed the LP diet. The free AA profile of the longissimus dorsi muscle (LM) in pigs fed the LDP diet showed reduced concentrations of Arg and Phe (P < 0.050), but increased the concentration of Pro compared with those fed the NP diet (P = 0.037). To trace nitrogen fate, three suckling piglets received a 15N-DP-supplemented diet. 15Nitrogen enrichment in plasma and hepatic AA at multiple time points confirmed that dietary ammonia nitrogen was absorbed and incorporated into endogenous AA. In summary, dietary supplementation with DP in the LP diet improved nitrogen metabolism and AA synthesis, thereby enhancing G:F ratios without compromising carcass traits or meat quality. However, excessive DP supplementation may suppress feed intake, underscoring the need to optimize its dietary inclusion level to ensure efficient pig production.Keywords: Low-protein diet, Diammonium phosphate, Amino acid, 15Nitrogen-isotope, Finishing pig

Introduction

The formulation of low-protein (LP) diets emerged as a pivotal strategy to enhance nitrogen utilization efficiency and mitigate environmental impacts in swine production, particularly in China, where the industry was strongly dependent on imported protein sources and faced increasing pressure from nitrogen pollution (Wang et al., 2018; Rocha et al., 2022). However, when crude protein (CP) levels fall below a critical threshold, growth performance often declined, even when dietary requirements for essential amino acids (EAA) were met through crystalline amino acid (CAA) supplementation (Fang et al., 2019; Xu et al., 2022b; Gomes et al., 2023). This impairment was largely attributable to a concomitant reduction in total nitrogen (TN) intake, which limited the endogenous synthesis of non-essential amino acids (NEAA), thereby hindering efficient protein accretion and metabolic homeostasis (Wu et al., 2013).

To overcome this limitation, previous studies proposed the supplementing LP diets with non-protein nitrogen (NPN) sources to promote the de novo synthesis of NEAA and thereby meet the nitrogen demands for protein synthesis (Mansilla et al., 2015, 2017a, 2017c, 2018). Among various NPN sources, ammonia nitrogen was considered more bioavailable than urea because of its rapid absorption and efficient assimilation (Rose et al., 1949; Deguchi et al., 1978). In our previous research, we demonstrated that supplementing LP diets with graded levels of diammonium phosphate (DP) and optimizing the essential amino acid nitrogen (EAA-N)-to-TN (EAA-N/TN) ratio to 0.48 significantly enhanced growth performance in growing pigs without compromising carcass traits or meat quality (Zeng et al., 2025). Notably, previous studies focused on NPN supplementation in diets severely deficient in TN, where the EAA-N/TN ratio was often intentionally raised well above the optimal value of 0.48 to enable effective utilization of the supplemented ammonia nitrogen by pigs (Mansilla et al., 2015, 2017a, 2017b, 2018). However, the metabolic and physiological responses of pigs to NPN supplementation under conditions in which the EAA-N/TN ratio slightly exceeded 0.48 had not been systematically investigated.

The liver plays a key role in ammonia detoxification and nitrogen metabolism through carbamoyl phosphate synthetase 1 (CPS-1), glutamine synthetase (GS), and glutamate dehydrogenase (GDH) (Qvartskhava et al., 2015; Hakvoort et al., 2017; Karaca et al., 2018; Dai et al., 2022). Under dietary TN limitation, supplemental ammonia nitrogen is preferentially directed toward NEAA synthesis via the GS and GDH pathways, rather than being diverted into ureagenesis through CPS-1 (Brosnan and Brosnan, 2009; Mansilla et al., 2017b). Therefore, the activities of CPS-1, GS, and GDH, together with plasma urea nitrogen (BUN) concentrations, serve as indicators of whether DP supplementation in LP diets exceed the physiological capacity for nitrogen retention.

In previous studies, the utilization of dietary NPN was evaluated using two indirect approaches: 1) inferring nitrogen utilization by monitoring changes in circulating nitrogenous metabolites (Nordgren et al., 2006; Mansilla et al., 2017c, 2018), and 2) quantifying total 15N accumulation in tissues following ingestion of 15N-labeled NPN (Deguchi et al., 1978; Bergner et al., 1984). However, these methods neither elucidated the specific metabolic forms of NPN-derived nitrogen nor confirmed its incorporation into individual amino acids (AA).

To address these issues, this study supplemented TN restricted LP diets with DP to maintain an EAA-N/TN ratio comparable to that of the normal protein diet. We evaluated growth performance, carcass traits, meat quality, plasma biochemical parameters, hepatic nitrogen-metabolizing enzyme activities, and AA profiles. Additionally, 15N-labeled DP was administered to suckling piglets to trace the incorporation of ammonia nitrogen into specific AA. We hypothesized that supplementing an LP diet with DP would restore TN supply and yield production performance comparable to that of a normal-protein diet, and that suckling piglets would utilize 15N-labeled DP for the de novo synthesis of NEAA.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

All experimental protocols were reviewed and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Hunan Normal University (Approval number 2023-614).

Animals and experimental design

Sixty-three crossbred barrows (Duroc × Landrace × Large White), with a mean initial body weight (BW) of 74.3 ± 3.8 kg, were randomly assigned to three dietary treatments with seven replicate pens per treatment (three pigs per replicate pen). The dietary treatments consisted of a normal protein (NP) diet (CP 13.47%), a low protein (LP) diet (CP 9.06%), and a LP diet supplemented with 1.07% DP (LDP, CP 10.47%). The nutrient composition of the experimental diets (Table 1) was formulated based on the Chinese Feed Composition and Nutrient Value Tables (China Feed Database, 2020) and was designed to meet the nutrient requirements recommended by the NRC (2012), including all EAAs but excluding NEAAs. The calculated net energy (NE) values of all diets were 10.47 MJ/kg, exceeding the NRC (2012) recommendation of 10.36 MJ/kg and thereby ensuring adequate dietary energy supply. The NP diet was formulated with nitrogen supplied exclusively from intact proteins and CAA. The EAA-N/TN ratio in this diet was 0.43, which was lower than the optimal value of 0.48 proposed by Heger et al. (1998) for achieving maximal nitrogen retention. An EAA-N/TN ratio below 0.48 generally indicated that the TN supply was adequate, whereas a ratio above 0.48 reflected a relative shortage of TN. The LP diet was formulated to be deficient in NEAA-N while meeting all EAA requirements, resulting in an EAA-N/TN ratio of 0.50, which exceeded the optimal ratio of 0.48, indicating a relatively limited supply of TN. The LDP diet was prepared by adding DP to the LP diet to achieve an EAA-N/TN ratio of 0.43.

Throughout the experiment, all pigs had ad libitum access to water and were fed their respective experimental diets. Daily feed intake was recorded for each pen, and the total feed intake was calculated for the entire 51 d feeding period. Pigs were weighed at the beginning and end of the trial, and growth performance was assessed by calculating average daily gain (ADG), average daily feed intake (ADFI), and gain-to-feed (G:F) ratio using standard formulas.

Feed chemical composition analysis

Representative feed samples were collected, ground using a small grinder, and dried at 65 °C for 72 h to a constant weight. Gross energy was determined using an intelligent calorimeter (MP-C2000, Mingpeng Technology Co., Ltd., Changsha, Hunan, China) according to the bomb calorimetric method (GB/T 45104-2024). Crude protein content was determined using the Kjeldahl method with a fully automatic azotometer (KDN-103F) and a digestion furnace (HYP-320) (Shanghai Xianjian Inspection Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) following standard procedures (GB/T 6432-2018). Amino acids were quantitatively analyzed using a fully automatic amino acid analyzer (L-8900, Hitachi, Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) following the acid hydrolysis method (GB/T 18246-2019).

For calcium and total phosphorus determination, 2 g of dried feed was weighed and ashed in a crucible. The sample was heated in an electric furnace (1000W, Hangzhou Fuxing Instrument Equipment Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China) at low temperature until smokeless, and then ashed in a muffle furnace (MFLXD433-12, Shanghai Muffle Furnace Technology Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) at 500 °C for 5 h until it turned off-white. After cooling, 5 mL of nitric acid (10014518, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) was added, and the digested ash was transferred to a volumetric flask and diluted to 25 mL. Calcium was determined by disodium ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid titration (GB/T 6436-2018), and total phosphorus was measured by spectrophotometry (GB/T 6437-2018).

Sample collection

At the end of the trial, after an 8 h fasting period, one pig from each pen (seven pens per treatment), selected to match the average BW of pigs in the respective dietary treatment group, were euthanized by exsanguination after electrical stunning. Blood samples were collected from the anterior vena cava, then centrifuged at 1237 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. Subsequently, the abdominal cavity was opened, and organs including the stomach, liver, spleen, and kidneys were removed and weighed. Following the removal of the hair, head, hooves, tail, and viscera, the carcass weight (including the kidneys and leaf fat) was recorded. The longissimus dorsi muscle (LM) from the right side was excised from the anterior portion to the 10th rib and analyzed for meat color, pH, drip loss, cooking loss, and water-holding capacity (WHC). All samples were stored at −80 °C until further analysis.

Carcass traits and meat quality measurements

Following the method described by Wang et al. (2022), the straight and oblique lengths of the carcass, loin muscle area, back fat thickness, and leg-to-hip ratio were measured. Subsequently, the lean meat, fat, bone, and skin were separated and weighed to determine their respective proportions.

The pH of the LM was measured at 45 min postmortem using a pH meter (PH-828, Dongguan Wanchuang Electronic Products Co., Ltd., Dongguan, Guangdong, China) with automatic temperature compensation. Measurements were taken at an ambient temperature of 4 °C, and the pH meter was calibrated with standard buffer solutions (pH 4.0 and 7.0) prior to use. To assess the color of the LM from finishing pigs at 45 min post-slaughter, a portable colorimeter (CR-410; Konica Minolta, Inc., Tokyo, Japan) was calibrated with a standard white plate. The recorded color parameters included lightness (L∗), redness (a∗), and yellowness (b∗).

To assess WHC, approximately 20 g of samples were initially weighed, then covered with gauze and filter papers. The samples were placed in a Tenove Meat-1 system (Tenovo International Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) and subjected to 35 kg of pressure for 5 min. Plasma biochemical parameters

Plasma biochemical parameters, including total protein (TP), albumin (ALB), BUN, glucose (GLU), total bile acids (TBA), non-esterified fatty acids (NEFA), and blood ammonia (NH3L), were measured using a biochemistry analyzer (TBA-120FR, Toshiba Medical Systems Corporation, Tokyo, Japan).

Enzyme activity

Liver samples were homogenized in ice-cold phosphate buffered saline (P1020, Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) and centrifuged at 2200 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected, and the activities of CPS-1 (YJ1251640, Shanghai Enzyme-linked Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), GS (YJ1011571, Shanghai Enzyme-linked Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), and GDH (AKAM005M, Beijing Boxbio Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) were measured using an Agilent BioTek Synergy HTX (Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA), following the kit instructions.

Determination of free amino acids profile

Free AA concentrations in plasma, liver, and LM were measured using a L-8900 fully automatic amino acid analyzer (Hitachi, Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) with lithium buffers. A 0.20 g sample of liver or LM was weighed, and 10 mL of 0.02 mol/L HCl (10011018, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) was added for ultrasonic extraction, which was performed for 30 min. For plasma, liver or LM homogenates, 1 mL was mixed with 1 mL of 8% sulfosalicylic acid (M80069101, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) and left to stand for 15 min. The mixture was then centrifuged at 1237 × g for 20 min. The supernatant was separated and defatted by extraction with n-hexane (1:1, vol/vol; 80068618, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). After transferring the aqueous phase, it was diluted five-fold with 0.02 mol/L HCl and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane before being analyzed using an amino acid analyzer. The AA content of the sample was quantified by comparing the peak profile obtained from the sample with the corresponding standard AA profile.

Determination of the hydrolyzed amino acids profile of longissimus dorsi muscle

To determine the muscle AA profile, meat samples were freeze-dried under vacuum. A 0.10 to 0.15 g sample of dried muscle was placed in a hydrolysis tube, to which 10 mL of 6 mol/L HCl was added. The ampoule was sealed and placed in an electric thermostatic oven at 110 °C for 22 h for hydrolysis, followed by cooling. The hydrolysis tube was opened, rinsed several times with deionized water, and the hydrolysate was brought to a final volume of 50 mL in a volumetric flask. A 1 mL of the filtrate was taken, evaporated in 65 °C water bath, dissolved in 1 mL of 0.02 mol/L HCl, diluted five-fold, and filtered through a 0.22-μm membrane. The resulting solution was analyzed for the HAA profile of the LM (excluding tryptophan) using a automatic amino acid analyzer (L-8900, Hitachi, Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The AA content of the muscle samples was determined by comparing the peak profiles obtained from the samples with those of standard AA profiles.

15Nitrogen labeled diammonium phosphate tracing

Three 10-d-old Duroc × Landrace × Large White lactating piglets (initial BW: 4.33 ± 0.02 kg) were surgically fitted with indwelling urinary catheters (outer diameter: 1.4 mm, length: 20 cm; CVC Set, Yangzhou Huaxing Instrument Equipment Co., Ltd., Yangzhou, Jiangsu China) according to the procedure described by Yen and Killefer (1987). After recovery, the piglets were housed individually in non-restrictive metabolic cages for 3 d and fed a control diet (artificial milk powder, CP: 20%, mixed with 75 mL of ddH2O). They were fed 15 g of milk powder every 2 h. On d 4, baseline blood samples were collected from the jugular vein to determine initial 15N enrichment (control group). Subsequently, piglets were orally administered a 16 g of a 15N-DP diet consisting of 15 g control feed and 1 g 15N-DP (99% 15N; 287488-13-5; ISOTOPE, Wuhan, Hubei, China), dissolved in 75 mL ddH2O. Blood samples were collected from the mesenteric vein at 15 min post-feeding and from the jugular vein at 2 and 4 h post-feeding. At each collection point, the first 2 mL of blood was discarded to clear the catheter, followed by collection of 5 mL of blood into sodium heparin tubes. The equal volume of heparinized saline was infused to maintain blood volume. Blood samples were centrifuged at 1237 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the resulting plasma was stored at −20 °C until analysis. After 6 h, the piglets were euthanized, and liver tissues were collected, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C for subsequent analysis.

For sample preparation, 10 μL of plasma or 10 mg of liver tissue was homogenized with 90 μL of chilled methanol to precipitate proteins. After vortexing, the samples were centrifuged at 13,200 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. A 10 μL aliquot of the resulting supernatant was mixed with N-ethylmaleimide (NEM, ML341155GMCN, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) in phosphate buffer (0.1 mol/L, pH 7.0, containing 10 mmol/L ascorbic acid and 10 mmol/L ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid) and vortexed for 1 min to derivatize free thiol groups. Subsequently, 10 μL of 0.23 mol/L 4-tert-butylbenzenethiol (tBBT, TB074125G, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) in dimethyl sulfoxide was added to quench excess N-ethylmaleimide, followed by the addition of borate buffer (0.2 mol/L, pH 8.8, containing 20 mmol/L tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine hydrochloride and 5 mmol/L ascorbic acid). After vortexing for 30 s, 5-aminoisoquinolyl-N-hydroxysuccinimidylcarbamate solution was added to tag amino groups. The reaction mixture was incubated at 55 °C for 10 min, acidified with formic acid, centrifuged again, and filtered through a 0.22-μm membrane prior to instrumental analysis.

Amino acid metabolites and their 15N-labeled isotopomers were quantified using ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (1290-6470 UHPLC-MS/MS, Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA). Chromatographic separation was performed on an Agilent ZORBAX Eclipse Plus C18 column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.8 μm) at 50 °C. The mobile phase consisted of water with 0.1% formic acid and methanol with 0.1% formic acid, utilizing gradient elution at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min. Mass spectrometry was performed in positive electrospray ionization mode using multiple reaction monitoring (MRM). Key source parameters included a nebulizer pressure of 50 psi, drying gas at 315 °C and 10 L/min, sheath gas at 350 °C and 10 L/min, capillary voltage of 4000 V, and nozzle voltage of 500 V. Data acquisition and analysis were performed using MassHunter software (version B.08.00, Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA). Metabolite identification was achieved by comparing retention times and MRM ion pairs with corresponding reference standards. The distribution of isotopologues (e.g., M+0, M+1, M+2) were quantified as percentages based on relative signal intensities.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 26.0, IBM Co., Ltd., Armonk, NY, USA). Data normality was verified using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was assessed using Levene's test. Differences among groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA. When significant effects were detected, post hoc comparisons were performed using Duncan's multiple range test. Differences are considered statistically significant at P < 0.050. Plasma biochemical parameters

Table 6 showed that the LP diet increased plasma ALB concentration in finishing pigs compared with the NP diet (P = 0.044). Discussion

In this study, finishing pigs fed the LDP diet exhibited a tendency toward reduced ADG and a significant reduction in ADFI compared with those fed the NP and LP diets. Notably, pigs receiving the NP and LDP diets exhibited significantly greater G:F ratio than those fed the LP diet. These findings were partially consistent with the observations of Li et al. (2021), who reported that supplementing DP in LP diets reduced both ADFI and ADG in finishing pigs. However, the reduction in ADG observed in the present study did not fully align with our expectations. Our previous study demonstrated that the addition of DP to LP diets improved ADG and G:F ratio in growing pigs, when the EAA-N/TN ratio reached a threshold of 0.48 following DP supplementation (Zeng et al., 2025). The discrepancy between the observed and expected results may have been attributed to the decrease in ADFI following DP supplementation of the LP diet, rather than to impaired nutrient absorption or utilization. Previous studies have shown that increased ammonia nitrogen levels in LP diets were associated with decreased ADFI, and the reduction in ADFI observed in the LDP diet in the present study may have resulted from excessive DP supplementation (Mansilla et al., 2017a). Furthermore, previous studies showed that pigs fed TN-deficient diets increased voluntary feed intake in an attempt to compensate for the insufficient nitrogen supply (Brumm and Miller, 1996; Mansilla et al., 2017a). This behavior may help explain the significant increase in ADFI observed in pigs fed the LP diet compared with those fed the NP diet in our study. Based on these findings, the G:F ratio appeared to be a more reliable indicator of nutrient utilization efficiency than ADG, as it accounted for both feed intake and growth. Therefore, it is essential to systematically evaluate the effects of varying DP inclusion levels in LP diets on ADFI to validate their capacity to enhance G:F ratio without compromising ADG in swine.

Organ indices provide valuable insights into the functional status of different organs under physiological conditions (Cui et al., 2022). In the present study, pigs fed the LDP diet showed a significantly higher stomach weight than those fed the LP diet. However, no significant differences in organ indices (liver, spleen, kidney, and stomach) were observed among the three dietary treatments. The liver plays a crucial role in nitrogen metabolism, and previous studies have shown that the addition of ammonium salts to TN-restricted diets often resulted in increased liver weight (Mansilla et al., 2017b; Zeng et al., 2025). However, a comparable hepatic response was not observed in this study, which may have been attributable to differences in the EAA-N/TN ratios and the physiological stage of the animals. Specifically, the LP diet used in this study had an EAA-N/TN ratio of 0.50, which was closer to the optimal value of 0.48 proposed in previous studies, whereas the LDP diet was adjusted to 0.43. In contrast, earlier studies used diets with higher EAA-N/TN ratios, which may have amplified the liver's response to supplemental ammonia nitrogen. These differences suggested that both AA balance and growth stage played critical roles in modulating hepatic adaptations to NPN supplementation. The stomach plays a vital role in feed storage, mixing, secretion of gastric juice for initial digestion, and defense against pathogens invasion (O'Connor and O'Morain, 2014; Schop et al., 2020; Rimbara et al., 2021). In this study, DP supplementation in the LP diet significantly increased gastric weight, suggesting that DP may have enhanced gastric function. However, our previous study did not observe any effect of DP supplementation on stomach weight in pigs fed LP diets (Zeng et al., 2025). This discrepancy highlighted the need for further research to fully elucidate the potential effects of DP supplementation on gastric function in LP diets.

In addition to growth performance, carcass traits and meat quality are also crucial indicators for evaluating the efficiency of feed formulations in swine nutrition. In this study, feeding both the LP and LDP diets significantly increased the a∗ values of meat in finishing pigs compared with the NP diet. No differences in carcass traits and meat quality were observed between the LDP and LP diets, which was consistent with previous studies showing that the addition of DP to LP diets does not negatively affect these parameters (Li et al., 2021; Zeng et al., 2025). Additionally, the observed increase in a∗ values in the LP and LDP diets may have been attributable to the reduced dietary CP levels, which was consistent with previous findings indicating that lowering dietary CP improves meat color (Suarez-Belloch et al., 2016; Li et al., 2018). Therefore, the increase in a∗ values observed in our study was likely attributable to the reduction in dietary CP levels rather than to DP supplementation itself.

Blood concentrations of TP, ALB, and BUN served as biochemical markers reflecting dietary CP levels and the efficiency of nitrogen metabolism, including digestion, absorption, and utilization processes in pigs (Mansilla et al., 2017c; Zhao et al., 2021). In the present study, plasma ALB concentrations were significantly higher in pigs fed the LP diet compared with those fed the NP diet, suggesting that reducing dietary CP levels may have enhanced plasma protein synthesis. This was consistent with a previous study showing that moderate reductions in dietary CP levels could increase ALB concentrations (Zhao et al., 2021), whereas other studies reported that reducing dietary CP levels either decreased or had no effect on ALB concentrations (Yu et al., 2017; Camp Montoro et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2022a). Meanwhile, both the LP and LDP diets significantly reduced plasma BUN concentrations in pigs compared with the NP diet, indicating that reduced dietary CP levels effectively decreased nitrogen waste. Notably, plasma BUN concentrations in pigs fed the LP diet decreased more markedly than those in pigs fed the LDP diet. Previous studies have demonstrated that nitrogen supplemented with DP in TN limited diet was preferentially utilized for synthesizing NEAA prior to conversion into BUN (Mansilla et al., 2017a,c). This suggested that the elevated BUN concentration observed in the LDP diet may have resulted from excessive DP supplementation. To further investigate this response, we measured the activity of hepatic CPS-1, the rate-limiting enzyme in the urea cycle (Meijer et al., 1990). Pigs fed the LDP diet exhibited significantly higher hepatic CPS-1 activity than those fed the LP diet, indicating that excess ammonia nitrogen generated through DP supplementation was eliminated via the urea cycle. Additionally, we assessed the activities of GDH and GS, two key enzymes involved in hepatic nitrogen metabolism. Glutamate dehydrogenase catalyzed the reversible conversion between ammonia and Glu and contributed to the biosynthesis of NEAA, whereas GS catalyzes the synthesis of Gln from ammonia and Glu (Brosnan and Brosnan, 2009; Levitt and Levitt, 2018). In this study, pigs fed the LP and LDP diets exhibited significantly elevated hepatic GDH activity compared with those fed the NP diet. This upregulation likely reflected a compensatory metabolic adaptation aimed at enhancing endogenous NEAA production in response to dietary NEAA restriction. In contrast, no significant differences in GS activity were observed among the dietary treatments. These findings suggested that while appropriate supplementation of DP enhanced nitrogen cycling and AA metabolism, supplementing the LP diet with DP to match the EAA-N/TN of the NP diet may have led to excessive DP intake, thereby increasing nitrogen excretion via the urea cycle. Therefore, further research is needed to determine the optimal DP supplementation level that minimizes nitrogen waste and its environmental impact, while still meeting the nutritional requirements of finishing pigs fed LP diets.

Nitrogen adequacy in swine diets was traditionally evaluated using the standardized ileal digestible Lys-to-CP (SID Lys:CP) ratio, with the NRC (2012) recommending a ratio of 6.0 for finishing pigs. In this study, the calculated SID Lys:CP ratios for the NP, LP, and LDP diets were 5.5, 8.2, and 6.7, respectively. Based solely on this metric, both the LDP and LP diets appeared to be limited in TN. However, plasma BUN concentrations and hepatic CPS-1 activity indicated that pigs fed the LDP diet obtained adequate TN, suggesting that the EAA-N/TN ratio provided a more accurate assessment of nitrogen adequacy than the SID Lys:CP ratio.

The distribution of AA in plasma and liver served as a crucial indicator of nitrogen metabolism and utilization efficiency under the different dietary treatments. While plasma concentrations of total EAA and NEAA remained largely consistent across the dietary groups, distinct patterns emerged in the hepatic AA profiles. Notably, pigs fed the LDP diet exhibited significantly higher concentrations of NEAA in the liver, particularly Glu, Gly, and Ser, compared with those fed the LP diet. These results were consistent with previous findings demonstrated that ammonia nitrogen supplementation enhanced the hepatic Glu release (Mansilla et al., 2018). Glu played a pivotal role in nitrogen metabolism, serving as a precursor for Gln, Gly, Ser, and other nitrogen-containing compounds such as glutathione and Arg (Wang et al., 2013; Broer and Gauthier-Coles, 2022). Interestingly, despite similar hepatic GDH activity in pigs fed the LP and LDP diets, the LDP diet resulted in higher hepatic Glu concentrations. This phenomenon was likely attributable to the additional ammonia nitrogen derived from DP supplementation, which enriched the substrate pool for GDH-catalyzed reactions, thereby favoring Glu synthesis over its catabolism. As GDH catalyzes a reversible reaction that was sensitive to substrate and product concentrations, the increased ammonia supply likely shifted the equilibrium toward Glu synthesis (Hou and Wu, 2018). Taken together, these findings further supported that the improved G:F ratio observed in pigs fed the LDP diet, compared with those fed the LP diet, could be attributed to the additional ammonia nitrogen provided by DP under TN-limited conditions. Exogenous ammonia nitrogen may have served as a critical nitrogen donor for the hepatic de novo synthesis of NEAA, particularly through the Glu pathway. This metabolic mechanism could have compensated for dietary NEAA deficiencies and thereby supported the maintenance of growth performance under TN-restricted conditions.

Skeletal muscle served as the major AA reservoir in pigs, and its free AA contributed substantially to meat flavor by providing key taste attributes such as umami, sweetness, and bitterness (Ma et al., 2020; Liao et al., 2025). In the present study, the concentration of Arg among free AA in the LM was higher in pigs fed the LP diet than in those fed the LDP diet. In contrast, pigs fed the NP diet exhibited elevated concentrations of both Arg and Phe, but a reduced concentration of Pro, relative to those fed the LDP diet. Given that Arg and Phe were associated with bitterness, whereas Pro contributed to sweetness, these changes suggested that the LDP diet may have enhanced meat flavor by modulating the concentrations of key flavor-related AA (Ma et al., 2020). In contrast, the LM HAA profile remained unchanged across all dietary treatments, indicating that neither reducing CP levels nor supplementing DP altered the composition of protein-bound AA in muscle, a finding that was consistent with our previous results (Zeng et al., 2025). These results suggested that DP supplementation in LP diets may have enhanced meat flavor by altering the concentration of free flavor-related AA in the LM, without affecting the HAA profile.

To further elucidate the metabolic fate of ammonia nitrogen derived from dietary DP, we conducted 15N isotope tracing experiments in suckling piglets, providing an intuitive depiction of the spatiotemporal dynamics of nitrogen absorption and utilization across key metabolic organs. Previous studies showed that dietary ammonia nitrogen reached its initial absorption peak in the portal circulation approximately 15 min post-ingestion, peaks in the liver around 2 h, and gradually stabilized systemically within 4 h (Bos et al., 2005; Mansilla et al., 2017c). Fifteen min after feeding, significant 15N enrichment in Arg (M+1), Leu (M+1), Ala (M+1), Asp (M+1), Glu (M+1), Gln (M+1), Ser (M+1), and Cit (M+1) was observed in the mesenteric venous plasma, indicating that dietary ammonia nitrogen was efficiently absorbed in the intestine and promptly utilized for the de novo synthesis of NEAA. This finding was consistent with previous reports demonstrating that the gastrointestinal tract possessed a high capacity to assimilate ammonia nitrogen for the de novo synthesis of NEAA (Mansilla et al., 2017c). The liver's pivotal role in nitrogen metabolism was further evidenced by the broader range of 15N-labeled AA detected in jugular venous plasma at 2 h post-feeding compared with mesenteric plasma at 15 min. In contrast, the number of significantly enriched 15N-labeled AA declined at 4 h post-feeding. This reduction likely resulted from decreased ammonia availability in the portal circulation, as previous studies showed that portal ammonia concentrations typically peaked between 2 and 3 h after feeding and subsequently declined (Bos et al., 2005; Mansilla et al., 2017c). The attenuated hepatic AA synthesis, together with the redistribution of previously synthesized 15N-labeled AA to peripheral tissues, may have accounted for the diminished labeling observed at this time point. Importantly, 15N enrichment in hepatic AA increased markedly at 6 h post-feeding, providing direct evidence that ammonia nitrogen from dietary DP was not only metabolized but also incorporated into endogenous AA pools, thereby supporting tissue protein synthesis and nitrogen retention (Bos et al., 2005; Spinelli et al., 2017). These findings offered a mechanistic explanation for the improved growth performance observed in pigs fed the LDP diet, confirming that the additional nitrogen provided by DP effectively supported anabolic processes under TN-restricted conditions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, supplementing DP into the LP diet, thereby aligning its EAA-N/TN ratio with that of the NP diet, improved G:F ratio of finishing pigs without adversely affecting carcass traits or meat quality. Furthermore, the elevated plasma BUN concentration and the enhanced hepatic CPS-1 activity suggested that excessive DP had been added to the LDP diet, which likely contributed to the reduction in ADFI. Notably, DP improved pork flavor by modulating key free AA concentrations in the LM. These findings suggested that DP represented a promising NPN source for optimizing LP diets; however, further research is warranted to define its optimal inclusion level to maximize its nutritional benefits while minimizing potential adverse effects on feed intake.
