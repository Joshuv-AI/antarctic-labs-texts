GmSNAP14 : a key contributor to soybean cyst nematode resistance in soybean
Authors: Vinavi A. Gamage, Mariola Usovsky, Anser Mahmood, Elizabeth De Meyer, Bishnu Dhital, Clinton G. Meinhardt, Qijian Song, Lesa J. Beamer, Andrew M. Scaboo, Melissa G. Mitchum
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary Soybean (Glycine max) plants counteract soybean cyst nematode (SCN, Heterodera glycines Ichinohe) infection through an impairment of soluble N‐ethylmaleimide‐sensitive factor (NSF) attachment protein (α‐SNAP) – NSF interactions and vesicular trafficking leading to cellular toxicity in response to nematode feeding.Through the use of a bi‐parental mapping population from a cross between the SCN‐resistant soybean cultivars Pickett × Peking, a major QTL on chromosome 14 was mapped to a confidence interval containing the GmSNAP14 gene.SCN‐resistant genotypes were found to carry one of two variant GmSNAP14 alleles harboring either a deletion or an insertion in GmSNAP14. Expression of full‐length transcripts was absent or markedly lower in plants carrying these alleles when compared to susceptible plants. Additionally, the generation of deleted and/or alternatively spliced isoforms coding for GmSNAP14 C‐terminal variant proteins was pronounced in resistant plants, suggesting that SCN resistance may result from a combination of diminished GmSNAP14 expression and GmSNAP14 protein variants.CRISPR/Cas9‐mediated knockout of GmSNAP14 enhanced resistance to SCN, consistent with susceptibility gene behavior indicating GmSNAP14 as a potential nematode virulence target. Our findings can be leveraged through the use of genome editing and conventional breeding techniques utilizing native alleles to develop resistant soybean cultivars.

Introduction

Vesicular trafficking has been increasingly recognized for its role in plant responses to abiotic and biotic stresses. Under such conditions, trafficking pathways are reprogrammed to deliver stress‐related proteins and metabolites to specific cellular locales (Collins et al., 2003; Leshem et al., 2006; Asrat et al., 2014; Kwon et al., 2020). Vesicle formation, movement, docking, and fusion are crucial for maintaining cellular homeostasis, coordinating development, and mediating responses to environmental cues (Bonifacino & Glick, 2004). Central to this machinery are SNARE (Soluble N‐ethylmaleimide‐sensitive factor Attachment Protein Receptor) complexes, which are positioned on opposing membranes and utilize free energy to drive membrane fusion. To sustain vesicular trafficking, the SNARE complex must be disassembled to regenerate free acceptor SNAREs, a process mediated by N‐ethylmaleimide‐sensitive factor (NSF) and facilitated by SNAPs (soluble NSF attachment proteins) that bind to the surface of the SNARE complex and recruit NSF (Jahn & Scheller, 2006). SNAP proteins belong to the tetratricopeptide repeat (TPR) domain‐containing family with key roles in hormone responses, signal transduction, and diverse cellular functions. Each TPR motif is c. 34 amino acids long and arranged in tandem to form a super helical structure that enables protein–protein interactions (D'Andrea & Regan, 2003). Mutations within the TPR domains, along with sequence differences in the C‐terminus and other regions of SNAP proteins, likely influence their interaction potential and subcellular localization (Blatch & Lässle, 1999; Lakhssassi et al., 2017). SNARE disassembly by NSF and SNAP is critical for ensuring the continuity of membrane trafficking within the secretory pathway (Brunger, 2005; Jahn & Scheller, 2006).

In soybean (Glycine max), α‐SNAP proteins have emerged as critical players in conferring resistance to the major pathogen of soybean, the soybean cyst nematode (SCN, Heterodera glycines Ichinohe) (Cook et al., 2012; Shaibu et al., 2022; Usovsky et al., 2023). The soybean genome contains five annotated α‐SNAPs located on chromosomes 2 (GmSNAP02/Glyma.02G260400), 9 (GmSNAP09/Glyma.09G279400), 11 (GmSNAP11/Glyma.11G234500), 14 (GmSNAP14/Glyma.14G054900), and 18 (GmSNAP18/Glyma.18G022500). GmSNAP11 and GmSNAP18 are paralogs sharing 98% amino acid similarity, while GmSNAP02 and GmSNAP14 are paralogs sharing 96% amino acid similarity. The GmSNAP11/GmSNAP18 and GmSNAP02/GmSNAP14 pairs share roughly 87% amino acid similarity (Lakhssassi et al., 2017). GmSNAP18 and GmSNAP11 are ubiquitously and highly expressed in all soybean tissues, whereas GmSNAP02 and GmSNAP14 are expressed at low levels and display overlapping as well as distinct expression patterns during soybean growth and development (Libault et al., 2009). GmSNAP09 shares only c. 50% amino acid identity with the other GmSNAP proteins (Lakhssassi et al., 2017).

Interestingly, three out of the five α‐SNAP genes in soybean, including GmSNAP18 (Rhg1) (Cook et al., 2012; Bayless et al., 2016; Lee et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2017), GmSNAP11 (Rhg2) (St‐Amour et al., 2020; Basnet et al., 2022; Shaibu et al., 2022), and GmSNAP02 (Usovsky et al., 2023), have been demonstrated to play a role in conferring SCN resistance. Resistant GmSNAP18 alleles (GmSNAP18‐a and GmSNAP18‐b) code for atypical α‐SNAP variants defined by polymorphisms in C‐terminal residues at a conserved functional site. SCN‐triggered hyperaccumulation of atypical SNAPs that are unable to engage the typical NSF proteins disrupts vesicular trafficking and leads to cellular toxicity specifically in nematode feeding sites (Bayless et al., 2016), thereby preventing the nematode from completing its life cycle. In SCN‐resistant soybean germplasm, the presence of an atypical NSF, GmNSFRAN07, that can bind with atypical GmSNAP18, preserves plant fitness and viability, and prevents cytotoxicity (Bayless et al., 2018). By contrast, the resistant GmSNAP11 splice variant allele (GmSNAP11‐sp) carries a single‐nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) at a splice donor site that leads to retention of a 17 bp intronic fragment, causing a frameshift resulting in a truncated protein that may not accumulate (Bayless et al., 2018; Shaibu et al., 2022). Similarly, GmSNAP02 insertion and deletion alleles either completely abolish GmSNAP02 expression or cause severe protein truncation (at the 8th amino acid), respectively, resulting in a loss of GmSNAP02 functionality (Usovsky et al., 2023).

The soybean cultivar Peking (PI 548402) has served as one of the main sources of SCN resistance genes for the development of SCN‐resistant cultivars, specifically GmSNAP18‐a and GmSHMT08 (serine hydroxymethyltransferase), two major genes known to confer bigenic resistance to SCN HG type 0 (Race 3). (Hartwig, 1981; Meksem et al., 2001; Liu et al., 2017). Pickett belongs to the first generation of SCN‐resistant cultivars developed in the United States by introgression of the known resistance genes found in Peking. Pickett originated from the cross of BC4 (‘Lee’ × Peking) × BC6 (D49‐2491 × ‘Dorman’) (Brim & Ross, 1966). Forrest belongs to the second generation of SCN‐resistant cultivars and was selected from the cross of ‘Dyer’ × Bragg (Hartwig & Epps, 1973). Dyer carried SCN resistance and was developed from the cross ‘Hill’ × BC2 (Lee × Peking) (Hartwig & Epps, 1968). Although Forrest and Pickett display a similar phenotypic response to SCN, both are moderately susceptible to SCN HG type 1.2.5.7 (Race 2), while Peking is moderately resistant to HG type 1.2.5.7, which indicates that Peking carries additional resistance gene(s) to this SCN population that were lost during the development of both Pickett and Forrest. In this study, a cross between Pickett × Peking, both of which carry the same alleles for the known major resistance genes (GmSNAP18‐a, GmSNAP11‐sp, GmSHMT08, and GmNSF RAN07 ), but exhibit either moderately susceptible or moderately resistant phenotypes to virulent SCN HG type 1.2.5.7 (Race 2), respectively, identified GmSNAP14 as a candidate resistance gene. We provide multiple pieces of evidence to demonstrate that GmSNAP14, the close paralog of GmSNAP02, acts as a susceptibility gene when combined with GmSNAP18‐a and GmSNAP11‐sp, but independent of GmSHMT08, to provide resistance to SCN HG type 1.2.5.7 (Race 2) in Peking.

Materials and Methods

Mapping population development

A soybean (Glycine max (L.) Merr.) recombinant inbred line (RIL) population composed of 168 F4:5 lines was developed from a cross between cultivars Pickett × Peking. Cross‐hybridizations were made at the Farm for Soy Innovation in Columbia, Missouri, during the summer of 2021, and populations were advanced using single‐seed descent at winter nurseries in Hawaii and/or Puerto Rico before single plant harvest in the F4 generation and subsequent composite line establishment in the F5 generation. Source data is provided in Supporting Information Dataset S1.

Nematode bioassays

The soybean cyst nematode (SCN, Heterodera glycines Ichinohe) inbred population TN22 used in this study was previously developed (Colgrove & Niblack, 2008) and is maintained on PI 88788 at the University of Georgia and Williams 82 at the University of Missouri. This SCN population is an HG type 1.2.5.7 (Race 2) (Niblack et al., 2002). Average female indices (FI) on the HG type and race indicator lines were as follows: Pickett (FI = 58), Peking (FI = 19), PI 88788 (FI = 75), PI 90763 (FI = 0), PI 437654 (FI = 0), PI 209332 (FI = 76), PI 89772 (FI = 0), and PI 548316 (FI = 67). Williams 82 was used to calculate the FI (mean cyst count = 198.7). In June 2024, SCN resistance bioassays were conducted in a University of Missouri glasshouse following established procedures in accordance with the Standardized Cyst Evaluation 2008 Protocol (Niblack et al., 2009) as described by Usovsky et al. (2023).

DNA extraction, genotyping, and linkage mapping

A modified cetyl trimethyl ammonium bromide (CTAB) method was used for high‐quality total genomic DNA extraction for both genotyping and confirmation of CRISPR/Cas9‐induced edits (Porebski et al., 1997). From each F4:5 RIL developed from the cross between Pickett × Peking, tissue punches from up to 16 young trifoliate leaves were bulked. DNA samples extracted from all RILs were submitted to the Soybean Genomics and Improvement Laboratory, USDA‐ARS, for genotyping using the Illumina Infinium BARCSoySNP3K BeadChip (Song et al., 2024). SNP data obtained from the Illumina Infinium BARCSoySNP3K BeadChip genotyping platform were filtered using tassel 5.0 software (Bradbury et al., 2007). The R package ‘abhgenotyper’ (Furuta et al., 2017) was used to conduct imputation of the missing genotypes based on flanking alleles. Linkage mapping was performed using mapqtl 6.0 software (van Ooijen, 2009). Interval mapping at 1 cM intervals along the chromosomes was used to detect quantitative trait loci (QTL) based on the initial logarithm of odds (LOD) threshold of 3.0. The permutation test was conducted in analyzed lines 1000 times, and the initial logarithm of odds (LOD) threshold was established under type I error at alpha = 0.05. Markers closely linked to positions with the highest LOD scores were taken as cofactors for multiple‐QTL modeling (MQM) analysis. Graphical presentation of detected QTL was drawn using mapchart 2.32 software (Voorrips, 2002).

Detection of GmSNAP14 alleles using WGRS

The short‐read fastq files for PI 437654, Peking, and PI 90763 accessions, re‐sequenced at an average of 14–17× depth, were sourced from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Sequence Read Archive database project numbers SRP045129 and SRP062245. The raw short‐reads of GmSNAP14 (Glyma.14G054900) from these accessions were aligned to the reference genome Wm82.a2.v1 using Burrows–Wheeler Aligner (Li & Durbin, 2009). The short‐read BAM file alignments for each of the accessions were visualized using integrative genomics viewer (IGV) software (Robinson et al., 2011).

High Molecular Weight DNA extraction and HiFi sequencing

Single plants of Peking, Forrest, and PI 437654 were grown in a glasshouse and a dark treatment of 30 h was applied before tissue collection. High molecular weight (HMW) gDNA was extracted from 1 g of leaf tissue using the Nanobind plant nuclei kit (PacBio) in two steps: (1) isolation of nuclei using TissueRuptor disruption; (2) DNA extraction from plant nuclei. HMW DNA was sent to the Genome Technology Access Center (GTAC) at Washington University in St. Louis, MO. The samples were proceeded with the creation of SMRTbell libraries, and PacBio® HiFi sequencing technology was used to obtain highly accurate long reads (HiFi reads) across two SMRT cells for a target of c. 30× coverage for each sample (Eid et al., 2009; Wenger et al., 2019). CLUSTAL multiple sequence alignment (MSA) was conducted using muscle (3.8) software (Edgar, 2004) to compare the GmSNAP14 genomic and coding sequences of the accessions to the Williams 82 (Wm82.a2.v1) genomic and coding sequences. Raw genomic sequences and CLUSTAL MSA of Forrest, Peking, and PI 437654 were compared with Williams 82 (Wm82.a2.v1) and presented in Notes S1.

Effect of GmSNAP14‐del and QTL12 on resistance to HG type 1.2.5.7

RILs in the population Pickett × Peking were genotyped twice with MU‐SNAP14‐DEL‐WT and MU‐SNAP14‐DEL‐MUT assays. In addition, three markers of the Illumina Infinium BARCSoySNP3K BeadChip, Gm12_14775930, Gm12_17122701, and Gm12_18028431, were used to select for QTL12. The RILs were separated into four categories of allele combinations at the homozygous stage: (1) GmSNAP18‐a + GmSNAP11‐sp + GmSHMT08; (2) GmSNAP18‐a + GmSNAP11‐sp + GmSHMT08 + QTL12; (3) GmSNAP18‐a + GmSNAP11‐sp + GmSNAP14‐del + GmSHMT08; and (4) GmSNAP18‐a + GmSNAP11‐sp + GmSNAP14‐del + GmSHMT08 + QTL12. The violin plots representing the resistance for each allelic class to HG type 1.2.5.7 were drawn in RStudio, and a post hoc Tukey HSD test was performed using RSstudio.

RNA extraction and real‐time qRT‐PCR analysis

Nematode infection of soybean seedlings was performed according to Ithal et al. (2007). Excised root pieces were immediately flash frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C until RNA extraction. Five biological replicates consisting of 8–10 seedlings/treatment were used for each genotype. Total RNA was isolated from soybean root samples using the RNeasy Plant Mini kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer's protocol. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from total RNA using the PrimeScript first‐strand cDNA synthesis kit (Takara, Kusatsu, Japan). Real‐time quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT‐PCR) analysis was carried out using PowerUp™ SYBR™ green master mix (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA) in a CFX96 C1000 touch thermal cycler (Biorad, Hercules, CA, USA). The gene‐specific primer sequences used in qRT‐PCR analysis are listed in Table S1. Gene expression levels were normalized to two soybean reference genes GmUbiquitin (accession no. D28123) (Kandoth et al., 2011) and GmTUA5 (accession no. AY907702) (Miranda et al., 2013). Relative expression was calculated by the Pfaffl method (Pfaffl, 2001).

Amplification of full‐length GmSNAP14 transcripts and protein modeling

Forward and reverse primers F1/R1 (Table S1) designed to the untranslated sequence immediately upstream of the start and stop codons for GmSNAP14 were used to amplify the corresponding transcripts from Williams 82, Forrest, Pickett, Peking, PI 90763, and PI 437654 cDNA using Q5® High‐Fidelity 2× Master Mix (NEB, Ipswich, MA, USA) in a BioRad C1000 touch thermocycler with the following cycling parameters: 30 s at 98°C, 40 cycles of 10 s at 98°C, 30 s at 60°C, 1.5 min at 72°C followed by 7.5 min at 72°C. The PCR products were separated by electrophoresis on a 2% agarose gel and purified using the QIAquick® gel extraction kit (Qiagen) following the manufacturer's protocol. Purified fragments were cloned into the pGEM®‐ T Easy Vector (Promega) for sequencing. GmSNAP02 and the three GmSNAP14 isoforms T1 (289 aa), T2 (246 aa), and T3 (175 aa) were modeled using AlphaFold3 (Abramson et al., 2024).

GmSNAP14 promoter analysis

Putative cis‐elements were detected in the GmSNAP14 promoter region (2000 bp upstream) of PI 437654, Peking, and Williams 82 using the ‘Promoter Analysis’ module in plantpan 4.0 (https://plantpan.itps.ncku.edu.tw/plantpan4/promoter_analysis.php, Chow et al., 2024). The GmSNAP14 promoter sequence for Williams 82 was obtained from Phytozome (Goodstein et al., 2012), while Peking and PI 437654 promoter sequences were extracted from PacBio HiFi long reads. The query was performed against transcription factor binding motifs in the Glycine max plantpan 3.0 database, and the results were compared between Williams 82 and Peking, and Williams 82 and PI 437654 using a Python script.

GmSNAP14 CRISPR guide RNA (gRNA) and construct design

A dual single guide RNA (sgRNA) plasmid construction system was employed as previously described (Kang, 2016). The construct CRISPR/Cas9‐GmSNAP02‐T4 + T3 used for knocking out GmSNAP02 was previously published (Usovsky et al., 2023). The gRNA sequences targeting GmSNAP14, as well as those targeting both GmSNAP02 and GmSNAP14 simultaneously, were designed using the CHOPCHOP web tool (Labun et al., 2016). Complementary oligonucleotides for selected gRNAs were synthesized, annealed to form double‐stranded gRNA inserts, and cloned into the AtU6‐26‐SK vector. The Cas9 cassette from 35S‐Cas9‐SK and the sgRNA expression cassettes were subcloned into the binary vector pcamGFP‐CvMV‐GWOX. This includes a strong CvMV promoter driving GFP for transgene selection. Final constructs were introduced into Rhizobium rhizogenes strain K599 via the freeze–thaw method (Höfgen & Willmitzer, 1988). The resulting vectors were named pcamGFP‐CvMV‐GmSNAP14‐T5 + T3, and pcamGFP‐CvMV‐GmSNAP02&14‐T1 + T2. The empty vector (EV) control consisted of the pcamGFP‐CvMV‐GWOX backbone without any gRNA. Constructs confirmed for editability were used to generate composite soybean plants. Primer sequences used for vector construction are provided in Table S1.

Generation of composite soybean plants

For CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing, Forrest and the Forrest‐derived Gmshmt08 mutant F234, which harbors a nonsense mutation at position Q226* (Kandoth et al., 2017), were used. F234, previously developed through ethyl methane sulfonate mutagenesis of Forrest, was backcrossed twice to Forrest. Plants exhibiting a female index (FI) > 60 for SCN HG type 0 and homozygous for the mutation in GmSHMT08 were selected. Both Forrest and F234 exhibit FI > 60% in response to SCN inbred population HG type 1.2.5.7.

Composite soybean plants with GmSNAP02, GmSNAP14, and GmSNAP02/GmSNAP14‐edited roots were generated by R. rhizogenes (K599)‐mediated transformation according to the method of Fan et al. (2020) with modifications for SCN bioassays (Gamage et al., 2025). Two independent biological replicates, each containing 5–6 plants for GmSNAP02, GmSNAP14, both GmSNAP02 and GmSNAP14 simultaneous knockout events, and EV were used for SCN phenotyping. The soybean composite plants were inoculated with 1000 SCN HG type 1.2.5.7 eggs. Inoculated plants were kept in the glasshouse at 26°C with a 16 h : 8 h light : dark period for 25 d. Cysts from roots were extracted and counted using a stereoscope. A Kruskal–Wallis statistical test was used to determine significant differences in cyst counts between Forrest and F324 plants transformed with different CRISPR/Cas9‐gRNAa+gRNAb constructs, followed by a pairwise comparison using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test. The analysis and dot plot generation were done using the R program v.4.2.2 using ggplot2 v.3.5.1 (Wickham, 2016).

Confirming CRISPR/Cas9 edits and gRNA off‐target analysis

To confirm CRISPR/Cas9‐induced edits, PCR amplification was conducted using GmSNAP02 and GmSNAP14‐specific primers flanking target sites in Forrest and F234. Q5® High‐Fidelity 2× Master Mix (NEB) in a BioRad C1000 touch thermocycler with the following cycling parameters: 30 s at 98°C, 40 cycles of 10 s at 98°C, 30 s at 60°C, 1.5 min at 72°C followed by 7.5 min at 72°C. PCR amplicons were gel‐extracted and sequenced directly. SNAPGene viewer was used to decode the chromatograms.

Results

Phenotypic evaluation of Pickett × Peking mapping population

Peking, Pickett, and Forrest were genotyped for the presence of all currently known SCN resistance alleles (Table S2) (Usovsky et al., 2025). All three accessions were determined to carry the same combination of resistance genes, including GmSNAP18‐a + GmSNAP11‐sp + GmSHMT08 + GmNSF RAN07 . The parental lines Pickett and Peking scored FI = 63.2 and FI = 24.1, respectively (Fig. S1a; Table S3). The Shapiro–Wilk test showed a significant departure from normal distribution with w = 0.89 and P‐value of 7.634 × 10−10. Skewness indicated asymmetrical distribution with datapoints skewed toward lower FI values. The negative excess kurtosis score indicated that the distribution is platykurtic, where the tails of the distribution are shorter and thinner than those of a normal distribution. The distribution frequency for SCN HG type 1.2.5.7 (Race 2) is shown in Fig. S1(a). This population displayed transgressive segregation, where 56 out of the 168 lines that were phenotyped fell into a category of high resistance (FI < 10). This means that over 30% of the lines showed a lower FI score than the moderately resistant parent accession Peking.

Genetic linkage map and QTL mapping

A total of 1165 polymorphic SNPs were utilized to construct the linkage map (Fig. S1b). Simple interval mapping detected five peaks on chromosome (Chr.) 5 (Gm05_36046937), 6 (Gm06_47452526), 12 (Gm12_17122701), 14 (Gm14_4100 480), and 15 (Gm15_48211214). Subsequently, a permutation test set the LOD significance threshold at 3.2. The MQM mapping detected one major and one minor QTL (Figs 1a, S1c; Table S4). The major QTL on Chr. 14 (QTL14) was mapped between Gm14_3945360 and Gm14_4655885 (Wm82.a2.v1) with a LOD score of 41.8, which accounted for 62.4% of the phenotypic variation (R 2). The additive (Add) effect was 20.2, which signified that the resistance allele of QTL14 was contributed by Peking. The minor QTL was mapped to Chr. 12 (QTL12) between markers Gm12_14775930 and Gm12_18028431 with a LOD score of 6.5 and R 2 of 5.6. The Add effect of −5.6 indicated that Peking was associated with increased FI at QTL12, and Pickett was the donor of resistance derived from QTL12. These two QTL explained 70.8% of the total phenotypic variation. A total of 80 annotated genes were present within the QTL14 confidence interval (CI) of 710 kb, positioned between markers Gm14_3945360 and Gm14_4655885 (Wm82.a2.v1). Among the annotated genes in the QTL14 CI, 54 genes had functional ontologies based on the Arabidopsis orthologs, including the GmSNAP14 gene (Glyma.14G054900) that codes for α‐SNAP14 (Fig. S2a). Because GmSNAP14 is a paralogue of GmSNAP18, GmSNAP11, and GmSNAP02, it became the top priority candidate gene for further analysis.

Discovery of GmSNAP14‐del and GmSNAP14‐ins alleles

The GmSNAP14 gene was further analyzed by aligning whole‐genome re‐sequencing (WGRS) raw reads from three SCN‐resistant soybean accessions including Peking, PI 437654, and PI 90763 to the SCN‐susceptible soybean Williams 82 using the Wm82.a2.v1 reference genome (Fig. 1b). In Peking, a pattern of increased distance between short‐read pairs spanning exons 7 and 8 was observed, which is indicative of a deletion. The deletion size was estimated to be c. 300 bp, and the name GmSNAP14‐del was assigned to this allele. In PI 437654 and PI 90763, a differential pattern of read alignment and clipping was observed in the third intron of GmSNAP14, which is indicative of an insertion. The insertion size was large and not possible to assess using short‐read WGRS. The name GmSNAP14‐ins was assigned to this allele.

Peking, Forrest, and PI 437654 were used to obtain highly accurate long reads (PacBio HiFi reads) of the whole genome. From long‐read HiFi sequencing of these accessions, reads containing the complete GmSNAP14 genomic sequence were extracted and compared with the Wm82.a2.v1 genomic and coding sequence (Fig. S2). Through MSA analysis using muscle software, the deletion size of the GmSNAP14‐del allele in Peking was estimated to be c. 400 bp (~Gm14:4379951–4380 351). The deleted region included part of intron 6, exon 7, intron 7, exon 8, and part of intron 8. The insertion size of the GmSNAP14‐ins allele in PI 437654 was predicted to be c. 9725 bp (~Gm14:4378888 insertion in intron 3). A blast search of this insertion sequence against the NCBI Core nucleotide BLAST database indicated that the insertion sequence had high similarity to the family of copia/Ty1‐related retroelements. Additionally, different SNP and indel variants were observed within intron five of Peking, Forrest, and PI 437654 (Fig. S2b).

TaqMan assays for detection of GmSNAP14‐del and GmSNAP14‐ins alleles

Modified TaqMan assays were designed to detect GmSNAP14 alleles for high‐throughput genotyping purposes, similarly to the detection of GmSNAP02 alleles (Usovsky et al., 2023). Five assays were developed to target the GmSNAP14 alleles (Table S5), and their primers and probe sequences are shown in Table S6. Two assays, MU‐SNAP14‐DEL‐WT and MU‐SNAP14‐DEL‐MUT, detect the GmSNAP14‐del allele. Their performance is shown in Fig. S3. The MU‐SNAP14‐DEL‐WT amplifies only the sequence of wild‐type Williams 82 that is deleted in Peking (WT; GmSNAP14‐WT) and heterozygotes and serves as an amplification control, whereas the desirable mutant (MUT; GmSNAP14‐del) allele clusters with no template control (NTC, i.e. no amplification). To separate the MUT allele from NTC, the MU‐SNAP14‐DEL‐MUT assay is needed. It amplifies only desirable MUT alleles and heterozygotes, whereas the non‐desirable WT allele clusters with NTC. For these reasons, these assays behave like a dominant marker.

Three assays, MU‐SNAP14‐INS‐WT, MU‐SNAP14‐INS‐MUT1, and MU‐SNAP14‐INS‐MUT2 detect the GmSNAP14‐ins allele. Their performance is shown in Fig. S4. MU‐SNAP14‐INS‐WT amplifies only non‐desirable wild‐type (WT; GmSNAP14‐WT) alleles and heterozygotes, and serves as an amplification control, whereas the desirable mutant (MUT; GmSNAP14‐ins) allele clusters with NTC (no amplification). To separate the MUT allele from NTC, the MU‐SNAP14‐INS‐MUT1 or MU‐SNAP14‐INS‐MUT2 assay is needed. These assays could be used interchangeably, as they target different regions of the same insertion and amplify only desirable MUT alleles and heterozygotes, whereas the non‐desirable WT allele clusters with NTC. For these reasons, these assays behave like a dominant marker. Table S7 summarizes the genotypes of the SCN HG type and race indicator soybean lines. The genotypes were juxtapositioned with their phenotypes, shown as a female index (FI) for each of the five SCN populations. Peking, PI 89772, and PI 209332 carry the GmSNAP14‐del allele, whereas PI 90763, PI 437654, and ‘Cloud’ carry the GmSNAP14‐ins allele. Forrest, Pickett, and PI 88788 carry the GmSNAP14‐WT allele.

Effect of detected GmSNAP14‐del and QTL12 on SCN resistance

The F4 : 5 RILs derived from the cross Pickett × Peking were separated into four allelic groups based on the presence/absence of homozygous GmSNAP14‐del and QTL12. All lines were also homozygous for GmSNAP18‐a, GmSNAP11‐sp, and GmSHMT08. FI to SCN HG type 1.2.5.7 were used to build the violin plots (Fig. S5). The one‐way ANOVA test showed a significant difference between group means (F‐value = 199; P‐value = 2e−16). A post hoc Tukey's HSD test determined that all four allele combinations showed differences in their means except the combinations of GmSNAP18‐a + GmSNAP11‐sp + GmSNAP14‐del + GmSHMT08 and GmSNAP18‐a + GmSNAP11‐sp + GmSNAP14‐del + GmSHMT08 + QTL12 (Table S8).

GmSNAP14 expression in response to SCN infection

To assess the expression of GmSNAP14 in response to SCN infection, six soybean genotypes were inoculated with SCN HG type 1.2.5.7, including Williams 82 (susceptible control; GmSNAP14‐WT), Forrest (GmSNAP14‐WT), Pickett (GmSNAP14‐WT), Peking (GmSNAP14‐del), PI 90763 (GmSNAP14‐ins), and PI 437654 (GmSNAP14‐ins). Full‐length GmSNAP14 transcripts were amplified from cDNA of both mock‐inoculated (M) and SCN‐inoculated (I) roots at 3 d post‐inoculation (dpi) with gene‐specific F1/R1 primers positioned immediately upstream of the start codon and downstream of the stop codon (Fig. 1c). Fig. 2(a) shows the results from PCR amplification of GmSNAP14 transcripts (T) in all genotypes in five biological replicates. PCR‐generated products resolved into three discrete sizes and were sequenced: 870 bp (T1, all exons), 741 bp (T2, lacking exons 7 and 8), and 581 bp (T3, lacking exons 6, 7, and 8) (Fig. 2b). The complete nucleotide and amino acid alignments for all isoforms are provided in Figs S6 and S7. GmSNAP14 and its closely related paralogue GmSNAP02 code for 289 amino acid (aa) proteins sharing 96% sequence similarity, each with four predicted TPR domains (Lakhssassi et al., 2017). The GmSNAP14 T2 and T3 transcripts code for 246 and 174 aa proteins, respectively (Fig. 2c). All four TPR domains remained intact in the T2 GmSNAP14 variant, but the deletion in the T3 GmSNAP14 variant truncated the fourth TPR domain and caused a frameshift (Figs 2c, S7). Two SNPs were detected in exon 6 among genotypes. A synonymous SNP was detected at nucleotide position 503 (C, cytosine) in the Forrest, Pickett, and Peking cDNAs, whereas Williams 82, PI 90763, and PI 437654 had a thymine (T) at this position. The Forrest cDNA harbored a non‐synonymous SNP at nucleotide position 653 (T, valine) in exon 6, whereas all other genotypes had a C (alanine) at this position. In Williams 82, the full‐length T1 transcript dominated, with T2 and T3 detected at negligible levels. Forrest and Pickett consistently produced only the full‐length T1 transcript across the five biological replicates. Peking predominantly produced T2 and negligible levels of T3. Low levels of T1, T2, and T3 were detected in PI 90763 and PI 437654, resulting in inconsistent amplification across biological replicates (Fig. 2a). For genotypes where splice variants were detected, these occurred in both mock‐ and SCN‐inoculated roots, indicating that these alternative splicing events are independent of genotype and SCN infection.

For relative quantification of GmSNAP14, it was not possible to design gene‐specific qRT‐PCR primers that would specifically amplify each isoform; therefore, a forward primer (F2) was designed to bind within exon 1 and a reverse primer (R2) was designed to span and bind the junction of exons 2 and 3, ensuring amplification of all transcript isoforms (Fig. 2b). Overall, Cq values in genotypes carrying the GmSNAP14‐ins (PI 90763 and PI 437654) alleles were consistently higher than genotypes carrying the GmSNAP14‐WT allele (Williams 82, Forrest, and Pickett) (Fig. S8). Due to the low expression, significant variation was observed in mock to infected expression ratios across the five biological replicates; therefore, neither uniform upregulation nor downregulation could be established (Fig. S9).

Taken together, these data showed that genotypes carrying GmSNAP14‐WT consistently accumulate higher levels of GmSNAP14 full‐length transcripts, whereas expression of full‐length transcripts is absent or markedly lower in genotypes with GmSNAP14‐del and GmSNAP14‐ins alleles; a reduced expression pattern that is independent of SCN infection. The generation of deleted and/or alternatively spliced isoforms coding for GmSNAP14 C‐terminal variant proteins was pronounced in resistant lines, suggesting that SCN resistance might result from a combination of diminished GmSNAP14 expression and loss‐of‐function GmSNAP14 protein variants.

GmSNAP14 promoter analysis reveals differences in cis‐elements among genotypes

A comparison of GmSNAP14 promoter sequences from PI 437654, Peking, and Williams 82 revealed several SNPs in PI 437654 relative to Williams 82, whereas Peking harbored several SNPs, two insertions, and two deletions relative to Williams 82. Comparison of cis‐elements between PI 437654 and Williams 82 showed a loss of HD‐ZIP cis‐element in PI 437654 (Glyma10g07440.1; Glyma13g21330.1, Wm82.a1.v1.1 reference genome version) (Dataset S2). This loss of HD‐ZIP cis‐element in PI 437654 resulted from a SNP (T to A at Gm14_4377488, reference genome version Wm82.a2.v1) in the PI 437654 promoter region relative to Williams 82 (Fig. S10a). Additionally, a comparison of cis‐elements between Peking and Williams 82 revealed several cis‐elements unique to Williams 82 including NAC, NAM, WRKY, and TBP. Interestingly, two SNPs (G to A at Gm14_4377984 and G to A at Gm14_4377986, reference genome version Wm82.a2.v1) in Peking relative to Williams 82 resulted in a gain of HD‐ZIP cis‐element in Peking (Fig. S10b). The presence of HD‐ZIP cis‐acting elements in the soybean cultivars, Williams 82 and Peking, which display a higher GmSNAP14 gene expression and its absence in soybean accession, PI 437654, which exhibits a low level of gene expression may indicate a potential role of this cis‐element in regulation of GmSNAP14 gene expression that warrants further investigation.

GmSNAP14 loss‐of‐function confers SCN HG type 1.2.5.7 resistance

To directly test whether a loss‐of‐function of GmSNAP14 contributes to resistance to SCN HG type 1.2.5.7, Forrest composite plants were generated using a dual‐gRNA CRISPR/Cas9 construct (GmSNAP14‐T5 + T3) for targeted editing of this gene in transgenic roots. Prior work established that knocking out GmSNAP02 in Peking using the dual‐gRNA construct CRISPR/Cas9‐GmSNAP02‐T4 + T3 enhanced resistance to SCN HG type 1.2.5.7 (Usovsky et al., 2023). Therefore, three editing treatments, GmSNAP02, GmSNAP14, and the double knockout of both GmSNAP02 and GmSNAP14 were evaluated for their individual and combined effects on SCN infection in the Forrest background.

The dual‐gRNA construct CRISPR/Cas9‐GmSNAP02‐T4 + T3 generated double‐strand breaks in exon 1 and exon 5 of GmSNAP02, which lie 871 bp apart (Fig. 3a), whereas CRISPR/Cas9‐GmSNAP14‐T5 + T3 targeted double‐strand breaks in exon 5 and exon 6 of GmSNAP14, which lie 489 bp apart (Fig. 3b). A dual‐gRNA construct CRISPR/Cas9‐SNAP02&14‐T1 + T2 was used for simultaneous targeting of GmSNAP02 and GmSNAP14 to evaluate the combined function of these two genes in SCN resistance. CRISPR/Cas9‐SNAP02&14‐T1 + T2 targeted two sites in exon 1 of both GmSNAP02 and GmSNAP14, which lie 36 bp apart (Fig. 3c). R. rhizogenes strain K599 carrying either construct or an EV control was used to transform Forrest to generate composite plants with edited roots (Fig. 4a).

Forrest roots transformed with either CRISPR/Cas9‐GmSNAP02‐T4 + T3 or CRISPR/Cas9‐GmSNAP14‐T5 + T3 showed a significant reduction in cyst numbers relative to roots transformed with EV in two biological replicates (Figs 4b, S11). Due to the chimeric nature of hairy roots, we expect the cyst count would be even lower if these edits were made in stable transgenic plants.

Editing of the individually targeted GmSNAP02 and GmSNAP14 genes was verified in roots by PCR with flanking primers F3/R3 and F4/R4, respectively (Fig. 3a,b). EV samples yielded the expected 1160 bp product for GmSNAP02 and 783 bp product for GmSNAP14. Because the dual‐gRNA construct CRISPR/Cas9‐GmSNAP02‐T4 + T3, previously validated for editing GmSNAP02 in Peking, had a single‐nucleotide mismatch relative to Forrest in the T4 gRNA, cleavage at its exon‐1 target was inefficient. By contrast, the T3 gRNA was a perfect match, resulting in efficient editing of exon 5 in both Forrest and the Forrest Gmshmt08 F234 mutant. For this reason, the full 891 bp deletion in GmSNAP02 was rarely detected, whereas the complete 512 bp deletion in GmSNAP14 induced by CRISPR/Cas9‐GmSNAP14‐T5 + T3, and the complete 48 bp deletion in both GmSNAP02 and GmSNAP14 generated by CRISPR/Cas9‐GmSNAP02&14‐T1 + T2 were frequently seen in samples (Fig. 4c–e). The edits were confirmed by direct sequencing of the corresponding PCR amplicons (Figs S12–S15). All GmSNAP02‐ and GmSNAP14‐edited roots of Forrest exhibited a reduced number of cysts (Fig. 4c,d), except for unedited plant #1 in replicate 02 (Fig. 4b indicated by green arrow, Fig. S11), transformed with CRISPR/Cas9‐GmSNAP02&14‐T1 + T2, which had cyst counts similar to the Forrest EV. Taken together, these results demonstrated that targeted knockout of GmSNAP14 enhanced the resistance of Forrest to SCN HG type 1.2.5.7, confirming GmSNAP14 as a susceptibility gene.

Discussion

In the present study, we unequivocally demonstrate a role for GmSNAP14‐del/ins alleles in conferring resistance to SCN. Prior studies located regions on chromosome 14 that were associated with SCN resistance in bi‐parental mapping populations using restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP) markers and simple sequence repeat markers (Qiu et al., 1999; Yue et al., 2001; Guo et al., 2006). Most recently, one of the largest SCN resistance genome‐wide association studies to date identified a quantitative trait nucleotide (QTN) at Gm14:4100480 (Wm82.a2.v1) associated with resistance to multiple SCN populations and proposed GmSNAP14 as a candidate gene because the gene was located 278 kb away from the associated QTN (Mahmood et al., 2023). In our current study, GmSNAP14 was again identified as a candidate gene associated with SCN resistance within QTL14 facilitated by linkage mapping in a RIL population from a cross between Pickett and Peking, both carrying the same alleles for the known major resistance genes GmSNAP18‐a, GmSNAP11‐sp, GmNSF RAN07 , and GmSHMT08 but exhibiting moderately susceptible and moderately resistant phenotypes, respectively, to an SCN HG type 1.2.5.7 (Race 2) population. Aside from the major QTL14 harboring the GmSNAP14 gene, this QTL mapping study revealed an additional minor resistance QTL12 that was derived from Pickett with the potential to cause an additional additive effect on resistance to HG type 1.2.5.7 and likely explains the observed transgressive segregation. More research is needed to locate, identify, and characterize the gene(s) underlying QTL12 and investigate their independent effect on SCN resistance.

Our prior work identified GmSNAP02 insertion and deletion alleles, causing either a loss of GmSNAP02 transcript or a severely truncated protein (due to a frameshift causing a premature stop codon at the 8th amino acid), respectively, resulting in the loss‐of‐function of GmSNAP02 and enhanced resistance to SCN (Usovsky et al., 2023). Here, we also identified GmSNAP14 insertion and deletion alleles, yet both alleles exhibited distinctly different loss‐of‐function mechanisms from those of GmSNAP02. Unlike GmSNAP02, the GmSNAP14‐del allele harbored an in‐frame genic deletion of two exons (6 & 7) predicted to produce a smaller GmSNAP14 isoform, and the GmSNAP14‐ins allele carried a large intronic insertion, which differed from the large exonic (> 6 kb) insertion observed in GmSNAP02. Three different GmSNAP14 transcripts (T1–T3) were confirmed by expression analysis in roots of the SCN‐susceptible cultivar Williams 82; the full‐length GmSNAP14 transcript T1 predominated, with T2 and T3 alternatively spliced transcripts detected at low levels. These findings are supported by soybean reference genome annotations that predict GmSNAP14 may be subject to alternative splicing (Fig. S27).

By contrast, we did not detect alternative splice variants of GmSNAP02, suggesting functional divergence in post‐transcriptional regulation between the two paralogs (Usovsky et al., 2023). In Pickett and Forrest, which also carry the GmSNAP14‐WT allele, only the full‐length GmSNAP14 transcript (T1) was detected. In Peking, which carries the GmSNAP14‐del allele, only the T2 transcript (lacking exons 7 and 8) and an alternatively spliced T3 transcript (lacking exons 6 to 8) were detected. For the accessions PI 90763 and PI 437654 harboring the GmSNAP14‐ins allele, a mix of full‐length (T1) and alternatively spliced transcripts (T2–T3) was produced that are identical to those produced in Peking, which has the GmSNAP14‐del allele. Collectively, these data suggest that exons 7 and 8, and possibly 6, code for protein domains that may directly serve as nematode effector target sites.

Alternative splicing is a crucial regulatory layer known to introduce a dynamic post‐translational dimension to plant immunity by fine‐tuning gene expression and modulating gene function. Alternative or unconventional splicing of canonical R genes, non‐canonical or S‐genes, may broaden the immunity repertoire in soybean. For some R genes, resistance can require intron retention (Zhang & Gassmann, 2003) or the combined accumulation of full‐length and alternatively spliced isoforms (Dinesh‐Kumar & Baker, 2000). Beyond R genes, there is evidence that plant elicitor peptides trigger the production of truncated transcript isoforms of negative regulators of immune responses, leading to enhanced resistance to pathogens (Dressano et al., 2020). Moreover, the balance of different transcript isoforms of non‐canonical resistance genes has been shown to boost immune responses against pathogens (Duan et al., 2016). Plants also exploit alternative splicing to control the activity of S‐genes by shifting the transcript pool from full‐length to truncated isoforms. Similar splicing switches can delete effector‐binding motifs or trigger nonsense‐mediated decay of S‐gene mRNAs, adding an extra, rapidly deployable layer of immunity (Liu et al., 2016; Arai et al., 2022). There is evidence in plants that large intron insertions, especially transposable‐like elements, can remodel splice‐site architecture. These changes can be used differently during pathogen attack, producing alternatively spliced transcripts that contribute to resistance (Kuang et al., 2009; Ganie & Reddy, 2021; Sun et al., 2024). Large insertions in introns can promote upstream and downstream exon skipping via disrupting the recognition of distant exons (Varagona et al., 1992). However, in the case of GmSNAP14, the intronic insertion is unlikely to explain the exon skipping pattern in PI 90763 and PI 437654 because skipping was observed in Peking and Williams 82, soybean accessions that do not carry the insertion.

Additionally, the accessions (PI 90763 and PI 437654) carrying the GmSNAP14‐ins allele displayed a low level of GmSNAP14 expression in both mock‐ and SCN‐inoculated samples relative to other cultivars (Peking, Forrest, Pickett, Williams 82). This pattern indicated that reduced basal expression, rather than SCN‐specific downregulation, may be a key component of the resistance phenotype in these cultivars. Intronic insertions are known to reduce gene expression. A gene may continue to produce correctly spliced mRNA, yet the transcript accumulates at lower levels because the inserted sequence interferes with transcription. Such insertions might contain RNA processing signals in their central region that can reduce expression (Luehrsen & Walbot, 1990). The foreign DNA can pause, stall, or prematurely release RNA polymerase II, introduce cryptic splice or polyadenylation signals, or recruit small‐RNA–directed chromatin modifiers that establish repressive marks. Any of these mechanisms can reduce the overall mRNA pool despite an otherwise intact coding region (Rowland & Strommer, 1985; Vayda & Freeling, 1986; Liu et al., 2004). Therefore, the identified insertion present in the third intron of GmSNAP14‐ins in PI 90763 and PI 437654 could explain the lower expression observed in these lines, consistent with reports for other plant genes (Strommer et al., 1982). The low expression level might also be explained by promoter SNPs and InDels that may impact GmSNAP14 expression. Interestingly, a comparative analysis of the promoter region of GmSNAP14 among resistant and susceptible soybean lines revealed the loss of an HD‐ZIP cis‐element in the GmSNAP14 promoter in PI 437654. Class III HD‐ZIPs are well known for their role as key regulators of plant growth and development and have been shown to play a major role in promoting cyst nematode parasitism (Liu & Mitchum, 2024). Importantly, here we demonstrated that the observed low level of GmSNAP14 gene expression and alternative splicing events in SCN‐resistant lines are independent of SCN infection.

Targeted knockout of GmSNAP14 reduced infection by SCN, consistent with S‐gene behavior, marking GmSNAP14 as a potential virulence target for nematodes to induce infection. Additionally, the observed reduction in infection when either GmSNAP02 or GmSNAP14 is knocked out suggests that the proteins encoded by these genes might be targeted independently in the nematode population.

The hypothesis that SCN deploys stylet‐secreted effectors that interact with WT α‐SNAPs to promote cellular vesicular trafficking activity necessary for feeding cell formation is strongly supported by our findings. In response to nematode infection pressure, SCN‐resistant plants have evolved to fight back by modifying or completely disrupting α‐SNAP function, causing local disruption to cellular vesicular trafficking, which inhibits SCN's ability to successfully form a feeding cell (Cook et al., 2012; Bekal et al., 2015; Bayless et al., 2016, 2018; Lakhssassi et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2017; Usovsky et al., 2023). A model summarizing our findings is presented (Fig. 6). We propose that the inbred SCN HG type 1.2.5.7 (Race 2) population used in this study contains a heterogeneous mix of genetically distinct individuals that deploy one or more stylet‐secreted effectors that target α‐SNAPs to promote their activity. In Pickett and Forrest, the atypical GmSNAP18‐a baits an effector, but its interaction with NSF is locally disrupted within the nematode feeding cell, which impairs vesicular trafficking. A truncated GmSNAP11‐sp baits an effector, but its interaction with NSF is disrupted or GmSNAP11‐sp fails to accumulate in the feeding cell (Bayless et al., 2018), completely evading an effector and further impairing vesicular trafficking. However, a large portion of nematodes within the population are secreting effectors targeting GmSNAP02‐WT and GmSNAP14‐WT, which sustains vesicular trafficking activity to compensate for the impaired function of GmSNAP11‐sp and GmSNAP18‐a, resulting in the observed moderate susceptibility to SCN HG type 1.2.5.7 (Race 2) in Pickett and Forrest. In Peking, however, a C‐terminal GmSNAP14‐del variant either baits an effector but can no longer interact with NSF, or evades an effector completely through deletion of the effector‐binding site, preventing a portion of the nematodes in the population from promoting GmSNAP14 activity, which acts in conjunction with GmSNAP11‐sp and GmSNAP18‐a dysfunction, leading to further impairment to vesicular trafficking and moderate resistance to SCN HG type 1.2.5.7 (Race 2).

In PI 90763 and PI 437654, the complete loss of GmSNAP02 evades an effector secreted by the remaining portion of the nematodes within the population that are targeting GmSNAP02, and this, coupled with the low level of GmSNAP14‐WT expression in PI 90763 and PI 437654, provides the full scope of resistance to the SCN HG type 1.2.5.7 (Race 2) population. Currently, the number and identity of effectors targeting SNARE recycling machinery remain unknown. Preliminary evidence for an interaction between an SCN SNARE‐like effector and GmSNAP18 was previously reported (Bekal et al., 2015). In addition, more recent genome scans have identified genomic regions associated with candidate effector genes with signatures of selection that may also be responsible for SCN virulence on resistant soybean (Kwon et al., 2024).

In summary, GmSNAP14 can be used in gene editing and native variant allele breeding strategies to enhance soybean resistance to SCN. Importantly, GmSNAP14 is a critical component of the foundation of genetic architecture conferring resistance in Peking, along with GmSNAP18‐a, GmSNAP11‐sp, GmSHMT08, and GmNSF RAN07 . Peking, along with PI 88788, is one of the two most widely used sources of SCN resistance in modern soybean cultivars, and understanding the complete suite of genes that control broad‐spectrum resistance is crucial for efficient cultivar development programs. It should be noted, however, that although composite plants with transgenic roots confirmed for GmSNAP02 and GmSNAP14 double knockout were generated, it remains to be evaluated whether a complete deletion of GmSNAP14 in the presence of either GmSNAP02‐ins or GmSNAP02‐del alleles permits whole plant regeneration due to possible divergent expression patterns in aboveground tissues of soybean. GmSNAP14 editing may require a more precise strategy that targets the promoter to reduce rather than abolish expression, or editing that involves replication of the native alleles, to avoid unintended effects on soybean growth and development. Additionally, we demonstrated that targeted knockout of GmSNAP02 and/or GmSNAP14 in Essex, a susceptible soybean genotype (similar to Williams 82), did not have a major effect on susceptibility to SCN, indicating that the enhanced resistance provided by GmSNAP02‐ins/del and/or GmSNAP14‐ins/del requires the presence of GmSNAP18‐a and GmSNAP11‐sp. As such, genome editing strategies and selection of native resistance alleles when developing SCN‐resistant cultivars must be pragmatic with specific intentional objectives relative to the SCN HG type being targeted to protect against. To facilitate this approach, the introgression of GmSNAP14‐del from Peking and GmSNAP14‐ins from PI 90763/PI 437654 into elite soybean cultivars that carry a GmSNAP18‐a, GmSNAP11‐sp, and GmSNAP02‐del/ins gene stack is achievable and can be facilitated by detection assays developed in this study. Ultimately, deploying S‐genes hijacked by SCN to establish infection in soybean may provide more durable resistance because the nematode must evolve an entirely new strategy to overcome the missing host function; a strategy that may come with a cost.
