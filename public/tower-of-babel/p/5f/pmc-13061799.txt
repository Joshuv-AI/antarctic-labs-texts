Phenotypic characterization of treatment-naïve childhood asthma with obesity: features of lung function, inflammation, and lipid profiles
Authors: Aliya Maimaitiniyazi, Zhiyuan Wang, Yanan Wang, Qianqian Dai, Menghua Li, Junlin Zhao, Liang Ru
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo characterize the multidimensional phenotype of childhood asthma associated with obesity at initial diagnosis, prior to controller therapy.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study of 130 treatment-naïve children with asthma, comprising 67 with normal weight and 63 with obesity. All participants underwent spirometry, assessment of inflammatory biomarkers, and immune cell profiling. Lipid profiles were measured solely in the obese subgroup (n = 63) to evaluate metabolic comorbidity. The Type 2 (T2)-high inflammatory phenotype was defined using standard clinical cut-offs (blood eosinophil count ≥ 0.3 × 10⁹/L and/or FeNO ≥ 20 ppb); dyslipidemia was defined according to the 2012 National Cholesterol Education Program (NCEP) criteria.ResultsCompared to children with normal-weight asthma, those with obesity showed early airflow obstruction, with reductions in forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV₁%), the FEV₁/forced vital capacity (FVC) ratio, mid-expiratory flow (MMEF%), and peak expiratory flow (PEF%), but preserved FVC. Multivariable analysis confirmed this obstruction was independently associated with obesity after adjusting for age, sex, and T2-inflammatory status. The obese asthma group also presented elevated neutrophil counts, reduced regulatory T-cell (Treg) percentages, and a lower prevalence of the T2-high phenotype, despite a similarly high burden of clinical atopy and elevated serum total immunoglobulin E (IgE). Furthermore, over half (56%) of the obese group had dyslipidemia at diagnosis.Conclusion: At asthma diagnosis, obesity defines a phenotype characterized by early airflow limitation, a T2-low inflammatory tendency, and frequent metabolic dysregulation. Obesity itself is a primary, independent determinant of this lung function impairment. These findings establish a critical phenotypic baseline for future longitudinal studies aimed at enabling personalized management of childhood asthma complicated by obesity. What is known:• The association between obesity and asthma in children is recognized as a distinct clinical entity associated with more severe disease and poorer outcomes. Comprehensive baseline data integrating lung function, inflammatory profiles, and metabolic status are scarce for children with obesity related asthma in a treatment-naïve state.What is new:• At asthma diagnosis, treatment-naïve children with obesity present a distinct phenotype characterized by 1) early airflow obstruction, 2) a frequent T2-low inflammatory signature (with elevated neutrophils), and 3) a high prevalence of dyslipidemia—despite a high background of clinical atopy. Obesity itself is a primary, independent determinant of this early lung impairment. These findings establish a critical baseline profile, underscoring the need for multidimensional assessment to stratify risk and guide clinical decision-making in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00431-026-06926-2.Keywords: Pediatric asthma, Obesity, Dyslipidemia, Type 2 inflammation, Lung function, Phenotype

Introduction

Asthma and childhood obesity are major, intersecting global health challenges. Asthma affects millions of children globally, with high burden persisting in many regions [1, 2]. Concurrently, childhood obesity prevalence has risen dramatically, with an eightfold increase among 5–19-year-olds from 1975 to 2016 [3]. Robust evidence establishes obesity as a key risk factor for asthma development and severity, defining a distinct obesity-related asthma phenotype with substantial clinical burden [4, 5]. This is critically relevant in China, where childhood obesity surged from 0.1% (1985) to 6.4% (2014), creating an urgent need to understand this comorbidity in specific, high-burden populations [6].

The association between obesity and asthma in children is now recognized as a distinct clinical entity, characterized by a more severe disease course and reduced response to standard therapy. Its pathophysiology extends beyond allergic inflammation to encompass mechanical constraints, systemic inflammatory alterations, and metabolic dysregulation [4, 5]. To effectively manage this complex condition, a clear understanding of its initial presentation in treatment-naïve children is essential, particularly given the dynamic context of childhood development.

Critically, the pulmonary function signature in children diverges from the classic adult pattern. Instead of a restrictive deficit typical of obese adults, early airflow obstruction—characterized by a reduced forced expiratory volume in 1 s to forced vital capacity (FEV₁/FVC) ratio with preserved lung volumes—emerges as a hallmark. This pediatric pattern is consistent with the concept of developmental dysanapsis, which may be uniquely exacerbated by obesity [7]. Beyond mechanics, functional deficits are increasingly linked to the proinflammatory and metabolic milieu of obesity. For instance, metabolic syndrome and insulin resistance correlate with lower lung function in adolescents, particularly those with asthma [8]. Emerging evidence further characterizes this milieu in obese asthma as involving altered systemic inflammation and lipid metabolism, which may contribute to pulmonary impairment distinct from classic atopic inflammation [9].

In parallel, evidence suggests that obesity can fundamentally alter the inflammatory basis of asthma. Studies report a shift away from a classical Type 2 (T2)-high pattern toward non-T2 or mixed endotypes [5]. However, the clinical relevance and consistency of this inflammatory shift at the initial diagnosis of childhood asthma, prior to any controller therapy, remain poorly clarified. A comprehensive profile that integrates pulmonary function, inflammatory status, and metabolic health at this critical diagnostic time point is lacking.

Therefore, this study aimed to characterize the integrated baseline phenotype of asthma in treatment-naïve children, with a specific focus on delineating features associated with obesity and their interrelationships. Establishing this comprehensive phenotypic profile at diagnosis serves as a critical first step, providing the essential foundation for future longitudinal studies to understand disease trajectories and guide personalized management in this high-risk population.

Methods

Study population and ethical consideration

This single-center, prospective, cross-sectional observational study aimed to characterize the untreated, baseline phenotypic features of childhood asthma at the point of initial diagnosis. Consecutive children aged 5–16 years with a new asthma diagnosis were recruited from the pediatric respiratory and allergy outpatient clinics of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University between January and November 2025. Key inclusion was treatment-naïve status. Exclusions included major cardiopulmonary, vascular, immune disorders, or relevant medical history. Written informed consent was obtained from all parents or guardians. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University (Approval No: K202509-54) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. To address the primary aim of comparing obesity with normal weight, the final analytical cohort consisted of treatment-naïve children with normal weight or obesity, as detailed in the study flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Definitions

Asthma diagnosis and criteria for the treatment-naïve cohort

Asthma was diagnosed by a pediatric pulmonologist according to Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) guidelines, based on characteristic symptoms and objective evidence of variable expiratory airflow [10]. In this cohort, asthma was confirmed by bronchoprovocation testing in 105 children (81%). The remaining 25 children (19%) were diagnosed by bronchodilator reversibility (≥ 12% increase in FEV₁ with an absolute increase of ≥ 200 mL). Peak expiratory flow (PEF) monitoring was listed as a diagnostic option in accordance with GINA guidelines but was not used as the sole diagnostic criterion for any child.

To capture the pre-treatment state, all enrolled children met the following criteria: (1) documented variable airflow, defined as positive bronchial provocation test or positive bronchodilator reversibility; (2) no history of regular use of asthma controller medications, including inhaled corticosteroids or leukotriene receptor antagonists; (3) no use of short-acting bronchodilators within 72 h prior to assessments; and (4) clinical stability with no acute exacerbation at enrollment. For the participants whose diagnosis relied on bronchodilator reversibility, all research procedures (lung function, blood sampling, FeNO) were performed prior to bronchodilator administration to ensure that all baseline data reflected the true treatment-naïve state. This approach aligns with the GINA recommendation to confirm asthma before initiating inhaled corticosteroid treatment [10].

Childhood obesity definition

Childhood obesity was defined using the Chinese Expert Consensus on the Diagnosis, Assessment, and Management of Childhood Obesity (2022), which provides age- and sex-specific body mass index (BMI) percentile cut-offs [11]. Participants with a BMI ≥ 95th percentile for their age and sex were classified as having obesity and constituted the primary exposure group.

Clinical assessments

Anthropometric, pulmonary function, and airway inflammation assessment

Standard anthropometry (height, weight) was used to calculate BMI. Spirometry (Jaeger MasterScreen IOS) followed American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society (ATS/ERS) standards; the maneuver with the highest sum of forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV₁) and forced vital capacity (FVC) was selected for analysis [12]. Parameters included FVC, FEV₁, the FEV₁/FVC ratio, maximal mid-expiratory flow (MMEF), peak expiratory flow (PEF), and the instantaneous flows at 75%, 50%, and 25% of FVC (MEF₇₅, MEF₅₀, MEF₂₅). All values are presented as percent predicted (%) using Global Lung Function Initiative (GLI) 2012 equations [13]. Fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) was measured at 50 mL/s (NIOX VERO®) and reported in parts per billion (ppb).

Blood sample collection and laboratory analysis

In all children, fasting peripheral blood (≥ 12-h overnight fast) was collected for analysis in the hospital’s clinical laboratory. A complete blood count provided absolute neutrophil and eosinophil counts (× 10⁹/L) and percentages (%). Serum total immunoglobulin E (IgE) levels were measured by chemiluminescent immunoassay (IU/mL). Flow cytometry was used to quantify the percentages of helper and regulatory T cells (Tregs) (%) among lymphocytes. In the obese group only, a full lipid profile—including triglycerides (TG, mmol/L), total cholesterol (TC, mmol/L), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C, mmol/L), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C, mmol/L)—was additionally measured by enzymatic assay to assess metabolic comorbidity.

Definition of key phenotypic variables

Type 2 inflammation phenotype

The T2-high phenotype was defined by elevated blood eosinophil count (≥ 0.3 × 10⁹/L) and/or elevated FeNO (≥ 20 ppb), using established clinical cut-offs applied in pediatric studies and severe asthma biologic eligibility criteria [10].

Atopy

Atopy was defined as the presence of physician-diagnosed allergic rhinitis, atopic dermatitis, allergic conjunctivitis, and/or food allergy, based on a combination of clinical history and objective assessments where available: (1) Allergic rhinitis was confirmed by nasal endoscopy, performed in all children with asthma at enrollment. (2) Atopic dermatitis was diagnosed using the UK Working Party criteria, which are validated for population studies and remain widely used [14]. Severity was assessed by the SCORAD score, a standardized tool endorsed by the Harmonising Outcome Measures for Eczema (HOME) initiative [14, 15]. (3) Allergic conjunctivitis was diagnosed based on typical symptoms and clinical history. (4) Food allergy was defined by a history of immediate hypersensitivity to specific foods, supported by positive specific IgE (≥ 0.35 kU/L) when available. Specific IgE levels to aeroallergens were not systematically measured in all participants; therefore, atopy status in the main analysis was based on clinical diagnoses.

Dyslipidemia

Lipid profiles were measured only in the obese subgroup (n = 63). Dyslipidemia was defined according to the 2012 National Cholesterol Education Program (NCEP) Expert Panel on Integrated Guidelines for Cardiovascular Health and Risk Reduction in Children and Adolescents [16, 17]. Diagnosis required meeting one or more age-specific thresholds: TC ≥ 5.2 mmol/L, LDL-C ≥ 3.4 mmol/L, HDL-C < 1.0 mmol/L, or TG ≥ 1.1 mmol/L (< 10 years) or ≥ 1.5 mmol/L (≥ 10 years). The overall prevalence was calculated as the proportion of participants meeting the diagnostic threshold.

Exploratory inflammatory-metabolic phenotype analysis

To explore phenotypic heterogeneity, children were cross-classified by T2 status (high/low) and dyslipidemia (present/absent), forming four subgroups: 1) T2-high with dyslipidemia, 2) T2-high without, 3) T2-low with dyslipidemia, and 4) T2-low without. This framework examined potential additive effects of these dimensions on outcomes. Given its exploratory nature, analyses are considered hypothesis-generating.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.5.1. Normality was assessed via Shapiro–Wilk tests. Continuous variables are presented as median (IQR) and were compared using Mann–Whitney U tests; categorical variables are presented as n (%) and were compared using Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. Multivariable linear regression models were used to quantify the independent association of obesity with lung function parameters (FEV₁%, FVC%, FEV₁/FVC, MMEF%, PEF%), adjusting for age, sex, and T2 status. Collinearity was assessed (all Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) < 5), and results are reported as standardized beta (β) coefficients with their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and the unique variance explained by obesity (ΔR2). Within the obese subgroup, Spearman correlations between lipids and lung function were computed in T2 strata, with p-values corrected for Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR). Interaction testing assessed effect modification by T2 status. For exploratory phenotyping, children were cross-classified by T2 status and dyslipidemia. Between-group comparisons used Kruskal–Wallis tests; trends were assessed with Jonckheere-Terpstra tests. A retrospective power analysis confirmed that primary analyses were adequately powered (> 95%), while exploratory subgroup analyses are considered hypothesis-generating due to limited sample size.

Lung function assessment revealed early impairment in obese asthma, characterized by an obstructive pattern with significantly lower spirometric values, particularly in FEV₁%, FEV₁/FVC ratio, and MMEF% (all P < 0.05), while FVC% remained preserved. They also exhibited a distinct immune profile, with elevated neutrophil counts and percentages and reduced Treg percentage (all P < 0.05).

Measures of classical T2 biomarkers—blood eosinophil counts and FeNO levels—did not differ between groups (all p > 0.05). However, applying clinical cut-offs (blood eosinophils ≥ 0.3 × 10⁹/L or FeNO ≥ 20 ppb) revealed a significantly lower prevalence of the T2-high phenotype in the obese group (p = 0.015). Consequently, most children with obese asthma were classified as T2-low. The prevalence of comorbid atopy was similarly high in both groups. Supporting this clinical picture, levels of IgE, a biomarker of atopic predisposition, did not differ between groups (p = 0.397), and the proportion of children with elevated IgE (≥ 100 IU/mL) was comparable (normal-weight: 73%; obese: 78%).

Independent contribution of obesity to lung function impairment

Multivariable linear regression, adjusted for age, sex, and T2 status, quantified the unique effect of obesity on lung function. Obesity emerged as the strongest independent predictor of impairment, uniquely explaining 13.2% of the variance in FEV₁/FVC ratio, 5.8% in FEV₁%, and 5.3% in MMEF% (all P < 0.01), while its association with FVC% was not significant (P = 0.255). Within the obese subgroup, T2 status did not predict spirometry after adjusting for age and sex (all P > 0.05). Collectively, these analyses indicate that obesity had a substantially stronger and independent association with airflow limitation than Type 2 inflammation in this treatment-naïve cohort, as summarized in Fig. 2.

We next explored whether these lipid abnormalities were associated with lung function. In the overall obese asthma cohort, exploratory Spearman correlations between lipid profiles and lung function parameters (20 pairs) revealed no significant associations after FDR correction (all FDR-p > 0.1). The strongest uncorrected correlation was between HDL-C and PEF% (ρ = 0.326, uncorrected p = 0.009; FDR-p = 0.183). The full correlation matrix is in Supplementary Table S1. Analyses within T2-high and T2-low subgroups showed nominally significant uncorrected correlations (e.g., HDL-C with MMEF% in T2-high: ρ = 0.425, p = 0.021; HDL-C with PEF% in T2-low: ρ = 0.362, p = 0.035), but none survived FDR correction (all FDR-p > 0.37). Bootstrap resampling for the HDL-MMEF correlation in T2-high yielded a stable estimate (95% CI: 0.093 to 0.677). Formal interaction testing found no evidence of effect modification by T2 status. A linear regression model for MMEF% including an HDL-C × T2-status interaction term (adjusted for age and sex) was non-significant (β = 14.86, 95% CI: −25.33 to 55.06, p = 0.469). Sensitivity analyses using robust standard errors and rank-based models supported this conclusion (all p > 0.22). A post-hoc power analysis indicated adequate power (83%) to detect a moderate interaction effect.

Toward an integrated phenotyping framework: Coexistence of altered inflammation and metabolic dysregulation

Building on the above, we employed an integrative framework by cross-classifying the 63 obese children based on T2 status (high/low) and dyslipidemia (present/absent). The cross-classification yielded four subgroups with proportions as shown in Fig. 3.

We examined whether the co-occurrence of these two distinct physiological disturbances was associated with worse lung function. A consistent gradient of worsening mean lung function was observed across the subgroups: children with both T2-high status and dyslipidemia had the lowest values, those with either condition had intermediate values, and those with neither had the highest values. This pattern was evident for all measured parameters (Fig. 3).

While formal comparisons across subgroups were limited by sample size, children with both T2-high status and dyslipidemia consistently showed the lowest mean lung function, a pattern warranting validation in larger cohorts.

Discussion

This study characterizes the phenotype of childhood asthma at diagnosis, prior to therapy. Compared to their normal-weight peers, children with obesity showed: 1) early lung function impairment (airflow obstruction and reduced small airway flows); 2) a distinct inflammatory signature, characterized by a lower prevalence of the T2-high phenotype despite a high atopic burden (reflected by both clinical allergies and elevated IgE), elevated neutrophil counts, and a reduced Treg percentage; and 3) within the obese group, a high prevalence of dyslipidemia. Collectively, these features delineate a distinct clinical entity and prompt a central question: what are the principal drivers of this presentation?

Our multivariate analysis confirms obesity as a primary, independent determinant of this early obstructive physiology. This finding highlights a critical age-dependent heterogeneity in the obesity-asthma link: in adults, obesity-related asthma often presents a restrictive ventilatory pattern (reductions in FEV₁, FVC, and FVC), largely attributed to the mechanical load of adiposity on the chest wall and diaphragm [5, 18–20], whereas in children—including our cohort—it manifests primarily as an obstructive pattern (reduced FEV₁/FVC with preserved FVC) [20, 21]. This pediatric-specific pattern, consistent with the concept of dysanapsis, is uniquely exacerbated by obesity, supported by meta-analytic evidence linking childhood obesity to declines in lung function [8, 20, 22]. Thus, obesity exerts a substantial and developmentally distinct impact on airway physiology in children.

Beyond its mechanical effects, obesity influences asthma pathophysiology in both allergic and non-allergic individuals, demonstrating that these conditions are not mutually exclusive [23]. In our cohort, obesity was associated with an inflammatory shift away from classical Type 2 immunity, evidenced by elevated neutrophils, reduced Tregs, and a T2-low tendency despite a high atopic burden. This profile aligns with evidence that obesity can alter inflammatory endotypes in asthma, potentially promoting Type 3 (T3) or mixed pathways [24, 25]. This alteration may be particularly relevant in atopic individuals [5]. Supporting this, studies show that in atopic asthmatics, obesity is associated with increased sputum macrophages and a tendency toward lower eosinophils and IL-5 – a pattern not seen in their non-atopic counterparts [26]. Similarly, in models of atopic dermatitis, obesity converts a Th2-predominant disease into a more severe, Th17-driven condition [24]. Mechanisms such as nucleotide oligomerisation domain-like receptor protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome activation and IL-1β/IL-17 signaling provide plausible pathways for these changes [5, 27, 28]. Considering the pediatric context, a developmental immunology perspective is instructive. The early-life immune system exhibits a physiological Th2 and regulatory bias, which underpins the high baseline prevalence of atopic sensitization in children [29, 30]. Within this normative developmental setting, our finding of a dissociated phenotype—high atopic burden yet low T2-high inflammation—in children with obesity suggests that obesity may act as a significant modifier. This observed dissociation aligns with the broader concept that obesity can alter immune endotypes [24]. It underscores a critical implication for clinical phenotyping: in children with obesity, the pathophysiology of asthma may involve inflammatory pathways not captured by classical T2 biomarkers, necessitating a more comprehensive assessment that accounts for the dynamic context of childhood development.

Beyond inflammation, metabolic dysregulation was a prevalent feature, with dyslipidemia affecting over half (56%) of our obese cohort. This aligns with established links between childhood obesity, metabolic syndrome, and adverse respiratory outcomes [31]. Our exploratory analyses did not reveal strong linear correlations between individual lipid levels and lung function—consistent with the complex nature of “immunometabolic” crosstalk [9]. The high prevalence of dyslipidemia nevertheless defines it as a core phenotypic component, even if its mechanistic link to lung function is complex and potentially immune-mediated. Our study, limited to a basic lipid panel, cannot capture the full metabolic state (e.g., insulin resistance) or detailed immune cell activation that may underlie this heterogeneity [9]. Given this complexity, we employed a pragmatic framework, cross-classifying children by T2 status and dyslipidemia. Exploratory analysis revealed a consistent trend: the subgroup with both T2-high status and dyslipidemia exhibited the lowest mean lung function across all parameters. Although limited by subgroup size and requiring validation, this pattern suggests that inflammatory and metabolic disturbances may jointly identify a functionally impaired subgroup at diagnosis—a finding consistent with the notion of “immunometabolic” asthma pathways [9].

However, additional factors not captured in this analysis—such as diet, early-life antibiotic exposure, and alterations in the gut microbiome—may further shape the phenotype observed in obese children with asthma. As reviewed by Machado Menegati et al. [32] and references therein, obesity and early-life antibiotic use are associated with reduced gut microbial diversity—including decreased abundance of beneficial genera such as Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus—and altered production of short-chain fatty acids. These microbial changes can influence immune development via the gut-lung axis, promoting Th17/neutrophilic inflammation and impairing regulatory T cell function, which aligns with the T2-low tendency, elevated neutrophils, and reduced Treg percentage observed in our obese asthma subgroup. These mechanistic pathways provide a compelling framework for understanding the immune deviation seen in this population. As highlighted in the same review [32], the inflammatory and dysbiotic components of obesity are likely to act in synergy, highlighting the complexity of disentangling their individual contributions. A detailed analysis of the gut microbiome in this cohort, while not the focus of the present study, has been completed and will be reported separately.

Our study has several limitations that should be considered. Its cross-sectional, single-center design establishes associations but not causality, though it successfully characterizes the phenotype prior to treatment. The sample size, while adequate for primary analyses, had limited power for subgroup and exploratory comparisons. While clinically practical, our reliance on peripheral biomarkers may not directly reflect airway inflammation. The use of Chinese pediatric criteria for obesity strengthens internal validity for the Chinese population but may affect generalizability to other populations.

This study provides a comprehensive, integrated phenotypic profile at asthma diagnosis, encompassing pulmonary function, inflammatory patterns, and metabolic status. This baseline assessment defines the essential reference point for measuring future disease evolution. The logical and necessary next step is to validate the longitudinal significance of this framework. Specifically, prospective studies must track how these baseline features co-evolve with lung function growth and exacerbation risk over time [33, 34]. This is particularly crucial in children, where asthma unfolds within the context of ongoing immunological and metabolic development [29, 34]. Determining whether the identified phenotypes predict distinct trajectories is the key to advancing from a one-size-fits-all approach to personalized, dynamic management strategies [33].
