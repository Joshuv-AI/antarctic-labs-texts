Inferring stochastic dynamics by biophysical Neural ODE using single-cell transcriptomics
Authors: Jingyu Dou, Wentao Lyu, Feng Chen, Qing Nie, Chunhe Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSingle-cell RNA sequencing has revolutionized our ability to dissect cellular heterogeneity and study cell fate mechanisms, yet inferring stochastic dynamics from static snapshots remains a fundamental challenge. Current approaches face a critical trade-off: mechanistic models impose rigid assumptions limiting biological realism, while data-driven methods sacrifice interpretability for deeper mechanistic explorations. Here, we present DynNet, a deep learning method that integrates Neural ODEs with biophysical models and prior knowledge of gene expression dynamics. DynNet learns the stochastic dynamics of gene regulatory systems for cell fate decisions. Benchmarking on synthetic data shows DynNet’s ability to infer stable cell states, reconstruct dynamical trajectories, and characterize multi-stable cell fate transitions. Using hepatocyte differentiation data, DynNet demonstrates its capability to infer developmental trajectory and the underlying cell fate landscape, revealing the stability and transition probabilities among distinct cell states. Applied to Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) data, DynNet further captures critical gene regulations and transition paths during EMT.Subject terms: Dynamical systems, Gene regulatory networks

Introduction

Stochastic dynamical transitions are fundamental in biological, physical, and chemical systems1–5 and are especially crucial for understanding cell fate determinations6,7. The existence of multiple stable steady states allows cells to transition between distinct fates in response to external or internal cues. This mechanism is particularly important in stem cell differentiation8, immune cell activation9, and cancer cell phenotypic switching10,11.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) technology has enabled the study of cellular functions12,13, including lineage decision, gene regulatory networks, and disease mechanisms at cellular resolution14–16. However, scRNA-seq involves the destruction of cells to measure their molecular state, making it impossible to directly track the specific molecular trajectories of individual cells over time17. This limitation in capturing temporal dynamics has spurred the development of computational approaches to reconstruct the dynamics of system behaviors.

Various computational methods have been developed, including Markov-model-based approaches and deep learning methods, to reconstruct cell fate trajectories and model gene expression dynamics18–22. These methods are now widely used to reconstruct developmental processes22–26 and disease progression27,28. Markov-model-based methods focus on reconstructing cell state trajectories and ordering cells in pseudo-time. Nevertheless, these types of methods lack a quantitative, interpretable dynamic model explicitly linked to underlying gene regulatory mechanisms18,19,29. Neural ordinary differential equations (Neural ODEs) use neural networks and differential equations, allowing reconstruction of gene expression dynamics continuously from data20,21,30–32. However, classic deterministic Neural ODEs fail to capture the multi-stable dynamics as only a definite direction of dynamics is allowed at any given point20,33–35. Moreover, it is difficult for Neural ODE frameworks to include prior knowledge, for example, specific gene regulatory interactions, and often the dynamics on low-dimensional latent spaces is derived from the high-dimensional gene expression data, limiting the direct interpretability of the inferred dynamics at the level of individual genes. RNA velocity-based methods like scVelo are effective for inferring cellular dynamics along trajectories by capturing the rates of spliced and unspliced transcripts16,24,31,36. Multi-omics data, such as metabolic markers or lineage information, might be used to estimate unspliced RNA sequences37. In the absence of additional measurements, inferring interpretable dynamics involving underlying gene regulatory mechanisms solely from scRNA-seq data remains a challenge. As gene regulatory network (GRN) models provide interpretable frameworks for understanding transition dynamics between different stable states38–42, it’s important to bridge mechanistic modeling and data driven approaches43–47. In addition, energy landscape methods provide effective ways to characterize stability, transitions, and stochastic dynamics for cell fate decision processes in gene regulatory networks41,44,48.

Here we present DynNet (Stochastic Dynamics Inference Network) to infer the latent gene regulatory dynamics. Crucially, DynNet leverages scRNA-seq data collected at multiple, discrete, and known time points, utilizing the temporal information as constraints to model the underlying continuous process. Our core idea is to employ stochastic differential equations (SDEs), the natural mathematical framework for stochastic simulation, to mechanistically decouple the dynamics into a deterministic gene regulatory drift term and a diffusion term representing stochastic fluctuations, allowing for interpretable, single-cell level inference. In this approach, the model jointly optimizes biophysical parameters and network weights by incorporating prior regulatory knowledge via evidence-tiered constraints. To capture the inherent stochasticity of gene expression dynamics, DynNet employs a neural network to estimate the noise variance dynamically, allowing for a more accurate description of single-cell data. DynNet also integrates landscape reconstruction within its framework to infer the underlying dynamical structure of gene regulatory systems. By embedding energy landscape analysis directly into its modeling process, DynNet provides a tool to identify transition driver genes and important regulations, through perturbation analysis.

To evaluate DynNet, we apply it to representative synthetic datasets, including mutually inhibited self-activation (MISA) model and cell differentiation gene network. DynNet can efficiently reconstruct steady state distributions, cell state evolution vector field, and cell fate landscape, compared with ground truth data. We further apply DynNet to realistic biological datasets: the epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) process with external signaling modulation42 and the hepatocyte differentiation system exhibiting bifurcation behavior49. Through DynNet, we reconstruct the developmental trajectory and underlying dynamic landscape for the hepatocyte differentiation. We also quantify the most probable state transition paths and identify key regulators driving EMT.

Results

The overview of the DynNet

We developed a hybrid modeling framework, DynNet (Fig. 1), based on dynamical systems theory to reconstruct continuous cell fate transition dynamics. DynNet is based on discrete snapshot data in scRNA-seq across multiple time points. We define the cell data as being measured at a total of T time points, t=1,2,3,…,T. For any given time point t, the number of cells is Nt, and the number of genes is M. We assume that the initial time point (t=1) represents the starting point of the biological process, intermediate time points capture the transition states, and the final time point (t=T) corresponds to the terminal phenotype, such as fully differentiated cells or stable states. First, by preprocessing the raw data (Fig. 1A), we transform the sequencing data of Nt cells at each time point into a gene expression matrix Xt∈RNt×M={xi}, where xi∈RM represents a feature vector characterizing the state of the i-th cell. The core of this work is to model the unobservable transitions between time points based on dynamical systems theory. To ensure that the learned dynamics align with known biological network structures and to prevent overfitting of sparse time-sampled data, we introduce two constraint mechanisms. Hard constraints mask neural connections that do not exist in the GRN, restricting parameter updates only to existing edges. Soft constraints penalize spurious regulatory effects via regularization (Methods). For the stochastic fluctuations in the SDE system, we employ an additional noise estimation network. The diffusion coefficient is estimated by an independent neural network, Dϑ (Fig. 1C, right). This network constructs the diffusion matrix, which is constrained to be a diagonal matrix to model biochemical fluctuations.

The training process follows an alternating optimization strategy (Fig. 1E). First, the regulatory parameters of the velocity estimator are set as θ=A,B,S. Here, A is activation interaction matrix, with its element aij representing the activation strength from gene j to gene i. B is inhibition interaction matrix, with its element bij, representing the inhibition strength from gene j to gene i. S is threshold vector, with its component Sj denoting the half-maximal effective concentration (threshold level) corresponding to gene j. These parameters are optimized by minimizing a composite loss function Lv. This composite loss consists of a dynamical consistency loss ET∣fxT∣2, a weight-matching loss 1T∑t=1TEt∣wt^−wt∣2, and a constraint term Cθ. The expectation Et⋅ in the loss definition is computed as the empirical average over the samples at time t. The dynamical consistency loss minimizes the drift term when the system is close to stable state, ensuring that the learned dynamics meet the stable state conditions. To guarantee consistent distribution weights among multiple stable states throughout the developmental process, we introduced a weight-matching loss (Fig. 1D). This term minimizes the discrepancy between the empirical weights w, which represents the distribution weights estimated from the data using a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM), and w^, which is estimated from simulated data generated by the DynNet model. We provide the theoretical derivation and verify the stability of our designed loss function on multi-stable datasets (see Supplementary Information Section A.4 and Figure S2-S4). The constraint term imposes restrictions on the parameters in accordance with the prior GRN, thereby preventing overfitting to sparsely sampled temporal data (see Methods). The stable state probability distribution Pssx is derived using moment equations and subsequently the energy landscape is calculated as Ux=−lnPssx (Fig. 1F), which reveals the attractor structure and cell state stability41,44. In this landscape, local minima correspond to stable cellular phenotypes, while saddle points indicate the critical points of fate transitions. The landscape not only elucidates the global stability of the attractors but also estimates the transition pathways between them. In summary, DynNet reconstructs continuous dynamical trajectories from discrete scRNA-seq data by integrating biophysical modeling with deep learning techniques. Its output includes reconstructing cell fate transitions, constructing cell type transition networks, analysing gene regulatory relationships, and predicting cell fates (Fig. 1G). In the following sections, we demonstrate the effectiveness of DynNet by applying it to a variety of synthetic and realistic scRNA-seq datasets.

DynNet accurately recovers multi-stable dynamics in simulation data of MISA model

The Mutual Inhibition and Self-Activation (MISA) model54,55 is a classical gene regulatory motif in which two genes mutually inhibit each other while self-activating themselves (Fig. 2A and Figure S5). The system’s multi-stability property makes it a mathematically tractable benchmark for dynamic inference methods. We generated synthetic data comprising five temporal snapshots (2000 samples per time point), capturing transitions from initial states to equilibrium. Phase space analysis of the simulated data revealed three stable states: an X1-dominant differentiated state (S1), an X2-dominant differentiated state (S2), and a balanced pluripotent state (S3) with moderate expression levels (Fig. 2B). To evaluate DynNet’s performance in multi-stable systems, we established a comprehensive analysis framework assessing three key aspects: stable state localization accuracy, single-cell fate prediction reliability, and global dynamic reconstruction capability. This framework allows for a comprehensive verification of the model’s capacity to resolve multi-stable configurations, track single-cell developmental paths, and recover dynamical landscapes.

To assess the ability of DynNet to recover and quantify the properties of the system’s stable states, we compared its predictions against the ground truth simulation data of MISA. DynNet correctly identified the presence of three stable states at the last time point. We then quantitatively compared DynNet’s estimated positions and variances of these stable states to those derived from ground truth data. This comparison indicates that DynNet accurately estimates stable state centers, showing close agreement with observed data (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, the covariance regions, reflecting stable state variability, also show alignment with the simulation data. Beyond position and covariance, DynNet’s estimates of the marginal distributions for variables X1 and X2 also closely match those observed in the ground truth data (Fig. 2D and Figure S6). The reconstructed energy landscape shows agreement with the overall distribution and shape of the data (Fig. 2E). Consistent performance in reconstructing stable state properties was also observed when applying DynNet to bistable data (Figure S7 and S8). Collectively, these findings demonstrate DynNet’s capacity to accurately identify and quantify key stable state properties, including their number, locations, associated uncertainties and marginal distributions. This demonstrated capacity is crucial for precisely defining distinct cellular states and confirms DynNet’s reliability in accurately predicting and comprehensively characterizing the stable states of multi-stable systems.

To validate single-cell fate predictability, we assessed DynNet’s performance by formulating stable state prediction as a classification task based on decision boundaries. As a quantitative measure of prediction reliability, decision boundaries delineate the regions of phase space leading to different stable states (see Supplementary Information Section D). The grid-structured dataset produced by the MISA model delineates the ground truth decision boundaries, serving as a benchmark for our comparative analysis (Fig. 2F left). We then applied the trained DynNet model to predict the stable state for each point in the grid-structured dataset and derived its corresponding decision boundaries (Fig. 2F right). DynNet’s predicted decision boundaries demonstrate consistency with those obtained from the simulation data, accurately dividing the phase space into the three stable state basins of attraction. This agreement further demonstrates the effectiveness of DynNet in delineating the region of attraction in phase space. We also compare the decision boundaries between DynNet and Neural ODE method as benchmark method (see Supplementary Information Section C). The decision boundaries predicted by DynNet are more accurate than those predicted by the classic Neural ODE method. These results demonstrate that for some cells with an undetermined fate, DynNet can effectively predict their future fate (Figure S9 and S10).

To evaluate DynNet’s ability to reconstruct the system’s global dynamic behavior, we first assessed its performance in reconstructing the overall velocity field. We computed the velocity field of the simulated data as a benchmark (Fig. 2H and Figure S11). The velocity field estimated from DynNet not only captured key features such as decision boundaries and stable states but also demonstrated better performance in approximating the true dynamics compared to the Neural ODE method across datasets with varying numbers of time points (2, 3, and 5) (Fig. 2G and Figure S11). Unlike the traditional Neural ODE, which struggled to accurately reconstruct dynamics with fewer time points and required more data for comparable accuracy (Figure S11), DynNet consistently provided more accurate predictions across all time points tested. These results underscore the robustness of DynNet in reconstructing the underlying dynamics of the system with limited temporal data. Beyond reconstructing the overall velocity field, we also evaluated DynNet’s ability to capture dynamic transitions between stable states. To specifically validate this ability, we used a perturbed bistable system. Data was simulated from this system by modifying parameters to alter stable state weights without changing their positions, thereby capturing a clear transition from the low-X1/high-X2 state to the high-X1/low-X2 state (Figure S7 and S12). DynNet accurately identified this transition when inferring dynamics from the simulated data (Figure S12). These results support that DynNet can robustly reconstruct the global dynamics and state transitions of multi-stable systems.

To validate DynNet’s reliability in practical applications, we evaluated its robustness against violations of modeling assumptions and its optimization stability. First, regarding the robustness to noise structures, we verified that DynNet maintains high fidelity even when the underlying data exhibits non-diagonal covariance (Figure S13). Notably, explicitly modeling the full non-diagonal diffusion matrix yielded negligible performance gains on these simulated datasets. These results indicate that the diagonal diffusion assumption provides a robust approximation, effectively balancing computational efficiency with modeling fidelity. To further bridge the gap between continuous SDE modeling and the discrete, sparse nature of scRNA-seq data, we validated DynNet on Gillespie-simulated trajectories with binomial dropout (Figure S14). The model successfully recovered the sparse regulatory topology (with near-zero off-diagonal terms) and accurately quantified cell fate biases, demonstrating robustness to realistic measurement noise. We further evaluated the stability and efficiency of the optimization process, finding that the model exhibits high parametric consistency by converging to the same set of regulatory parameters across independent random initializations (Figure S15). Additionally, a comparison of initialization strategies reveals that incorporating the GRN prior significantly accelerates loss convergence compared to random initialization, empirically validating the computational necessity of our prior-guided training strategy (Figure S16).

DynNet accurately recovers simulation data for developmental branching process

To further evaluate DynNet’s performance, we constructed a gene regulatory network comprising 7 genes (Fig. 3A and Figure S17). In this network, red arrows denote activation, while blue arrows indicate repression. Here, G1, G2 and G3 progressively activate G4 and G5, which mutually inhibit each other, forming a toggle switch, and G6 and G7 are downstream targets of G4. BoolODE, a stochastic differential equation-based simulator was used to simulate the multi-stable dynamics56. Training data comprise six temporal snapshots capturing the transition from the initial state to equilibrium, which are reduced in dimensionality using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) (Fig. 3B). This system exhibits differentiation-like dynamics, as the system state evolves from a stem-cell-like state (I) into two distinct differentiation states (D1 and D2) over time. Comparing the gene expression in the two differentiation states, we found that genes G4, G5, G6, and G7 showed significant differences between the two stable states, which is consistent with the gene regulatory relationships (Fig. 3C).

DynNet successfully reconstructed the system’s dynamics from simulated data. The marginal distributions of the estimated stable states demonstrate agreement between DynNet’s estimates and the data (Fig. 3D). The estimated velocity field accurately captures gene expression trajectories toward stable states (Fig. 3E). However, we also noted that the estimated velocity fields do not fully align with the cell trajectories before cell fate bifurcation. This apparent misalignment may arise because the overlap between pre-bifurcation states and the bifurcation points in the projection space (Fig. 3B) makes it challenging to accurately assess the dynamics in this visualization. Despite using data generated by a simulator based on a different dynamic modeling framework from being used by DynNet, the model successfully estimated the dynamics. Using the gene expression dynamics inferred by DynNet, we analysed the temporal changes in individual genes. We specifically observed the cascade activation dynamics involving gene G1, G2, and G3 (Figure S18), which showed characteristic transient expression profiles with levels first increasing and then decreasing over time. Among the downstream targets, we focused on G6 and G7, which serve as marker genes distinguishing different cell fates. The mutual inhibition between G4 and G5, acting as cell fate switches, then causes a significant and synchronous bifurcation in the expression levels of both G6 and G7, clearly marking the system’s bistable fate choice (Fig. 3F). Consistent with the MISA validation, DynNet accurately recovered the bifurcation dynamics and steady-state distributions from Gillespie simulation data (Figure S19), further confirming its robustness to realistic molecular fluctuations. We also validated the performance of DynNet on this dataset with an extra non-diagonal noise as environment noise (Figure S20 and S21). Despite being trained solely with the diagonal diffusion matrix, we confirmed the DynNet’s ability to reconstruct the attractors with accurate noise estimation. Furthermore, quantitative accuracy showed minimal relative error in inferred stable state weights and agreement in the marginal density plots (Figure S22). These results support our modeling choice with the diagonal diffusion matrix assumption.

We next investigated the impact of prior knowledge constraints on model inference. Using hard constraints where the regulatory relationships in the simulation data were predefined, the interaction type between two genes remains fixed during optimization, and crucially, parameters for non-existent interactions (not present in the prior) are rigidly masked or fixed to zero. For example, if the prior dictates that gene j has no inhibitory effect on gene i, the corresponding inhibitory parameter is set to zero and is not optimized. In contrast, soft constraints do not rigidly mask parameters but instead penalize them during optimization, driving them to be as small as possible (Figure S23 and S24). Fig. 3H illustrates the training loss trajectories, and this flexibility comes at a cost: soft constraints lead to slower convergence compared to hard constraints due to the expanded parameter search space. Despite the computational cost, this difference allows soft constraints to explore the parameter space and potentially discover new interactions. However, we noted that when using soft constraints, the estimated parameters occasionally diverged from the true underlying network (Fig. 3G and Figure S24). The edges not present in the original network yet still show nonzero estimated values after optimization (Fig. 3G), such as the activating edge from G5 to G7. This phenomenon is biologically explicable by the indirect interaction in the true network: G5 inhibits G4, and G4 in turn inhibits G7. This double-inhibition induces a positive correlation between G5 and G7 expression, which the model interprets as a direct regulatory edge under soft constraints. Nonetheless, for edges present in the prior network, DynNet produced reasonable weight predictions. While soft constraints require careful interpretation of such indirect relationships, they offer the significant advantage of allowing the model to infer potential regulatory relationships, a capability not possible with rigid hard constraints. Notably, this implies that the soft constraint approach may be viewed primarily as a strategy to enhance the reconstruction of system dynamics by capturing functional dependencies, rather than as a dedicated tool for precise structural network inference. Building upon this capacity to capture functional dynamics, DynNet naturally supports in silico perturbations. Simulated suppression of the driver gene (G4) in the 7-gene network correctly predicted a macroscopic shift from a developmental bifurcation to a monostable trajectory (Figure S40).

To investigate this discovery capability when prior knowledge is incomplete, we performed experiments on the 7-gene network with only self-activation terms as priors, leaving all cross-gene regulatory interactions to be inferred. In this case, hard constraints did not lead to the desirable dynamics because of the loss of prior information. But DynNet successfully reconstructed the underlying dynamics with a possible GRN structure based on the dynamic data under soft constraint (Figure S25). Furthermore, we demonstrate that DynNet can learn dynamics through a two-step method of topology-hard constraint inference parameters in a soft constraint learning network (see Supplementary Information Section E.2 and the Figure S36–38). To validate this structural identifiability and the utility of data-driven priors, we evaluated DynNet across 50 synthetic networks (see Supplementary Information Section E.2). Initialization with a correlation network acted as an optimization accelerator, speeding up convergence while enabling the soft-constraint to robustly correct false prior edges (Figure S46). Consequently, DynNet demonstrated reliable de novo topological recovery, maintaining robust accuracy under purely data-driven settings (correlation network as priors) and even when guided by a 30% corrupted prior (Figure S39).

Given these advantages, we further tested DynNet’s inference capability under limited observational data. We designed a 9-dimensional gene network and considered the scenario where only a subset of genes could be observed and performed dynamical estimation based on this limited information (Figure S26). The simulated data evolved into three discrete stable states, each corresponding to distinct cell types characterized by the expression of genes G, H, and I. We selected a subset of genes for training DynNet to fit the multi-stability data generated by the model and applied soft constraints for regularization. DynNet’s estimated stable states show alignment with the simulation data in terms of their centers, marginal distributions, and covariance regions. Although some deviations were observed between the estimated results and the data, particularly in the marginal distributions of genes E and F where two low-expression states were less distinctly separated in the simulation data compared to the estimates, the overall consistency and biological interpretability remained evident (Figure S26). The test results on the synthetic gene network demonstrate that DynNet effectively handles complex gene regulatory dynamics of cell differentiation, even when data is incomplete, especially through indirect regulation (Figure S27). This capability to reconstruct dynamics from incomplete node observations is particularly crucial given DynNet’s fundamental design philosophy. Rather than attempting to model the entire transcriptome, DynNet focuses on capturing the continuous dynamics of a targeted set of core driver genes governing cell fate decisions. Because the complete biological network inevitably involves unobserved intermediate regulators, the ability to effectively absorb these hidden effects into the core network via inferred indirect interactions is paramount. By doing so, DynNet ensures the recovery of macroscopic system dynamics even when the analysis is strictly confined to a limited core gene set.

We also investigated the impact of sparse temporal sampling by determining the required temporal resolution on synthetic datasets. We evaluated the impact of sparse temporal sampling across multiple dimensions. While sparse sampling yielded high error and variance, prediction accuracy, loss convergence, and structural recovery all optimally plateaued between 4 and 6 time points (Figures S41–S44). This confirms that a practical resolution of 4 to 6 time points, which aligns well with standard scRNA-seq experimental designs, provides sufficient temporal anchors for robust continuous dynamic inference.

DynNet deciphers bifurcation dynamics and fate determination in hepatocyte differentiation

To assess DynNet’s capability in inferring stochastic dynamics in real biological systems, we applied DynNet to a bifurcating dataset from hepatocyte differentiation. In vitro differentiation of pluripotent stem cells bifurcates into distinct cell fates, such as hepatocyte-like cells or liver bud (LB) formation, depending on the culture conditions49. Recent studies have systematically analyzed the differentiation trajectory from induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) to mature hepatocyte-like (MH) state and LB state. During early differentiation, iPSCs first transition to the definitive endoderm (DE) stage before progressing to the hepatic endoderm (HE) state. At this stage, distinct culture conditions drive two divergent pathways: conventional conditions yield immature hepatoblast-like (IH) cells that mature into hepatocyte-like cells, whereas organoid cultures induce the formation of LB tissues (Fig. 4A). Here, we evaluate the feasibility of reconstructing this bifurcation process using DynNet.

The estimated gene-gene interactions were visualized using a circular plot, with red edges indicating activation and blue edges indicating inhibition. This network serves as the reference framework for DynNet analysis (Fig. 4B and Figure S29). Dimensionality reduction of the key genes revealed the underlying developmental trajectory, where the first principal component primarily captured differentiation progression, and the second one reflected cellular heterogeneity. Notably, the two terminal cell states exhibited clear separation (Fig. 4C).

To analyze this bifurcation dataset, we employed DynNet to characterize its dynamic properties and regulatory interactions. The dynamic modeling allowed us to validate the branching trajectories and molecular mechanisms that regulate hepatocyte differentiation. Fig. 4D presents a comparison between the expression density distributions of several genes in the estimated stable states and the corresponding empirical data. This comparison demonstrates that DynNet accurately estimates the marginal expression profiles observed in both terminal differentiation states. Concurrently, we computed the velocity field and visualized it on the energy landscape to reveal the global dynamic properties of the system. The system showed two stable attractors corresponding to MH and LB states, with velocity streamlines converging towards these attractors. Additionally, the velocity fields at the HE cluster exhibits bifurcation dynamics, consistent with experimental observations of HE cells displaying different differentiation trajectories under varying culture conditions (Fig. 4E). To investigate developmental transition pathways, we quantified transition actions between different cell states and estimated their associated probabilities57. The results show that intermediate states lowered transition actions, for example, the DE→HE→ early LB path required less action than a direct DE→LB transition (Fig. 4F). This is consistent with our previous findings showing that intermediate states can accelerate cell fate transitions42. Reverse transitions consistently exhibit higher action costs than forward developmental transitions. Additionally, transitions to MH states require less action than those to LB states, suggesting a developmental bias toward MH. First passage time (FPT) analysis58 further supports this finding, showing that MH states have shorter FPTs than LB states, aligning with their lower transition action costs. To assess robustness, we evaluated DynNet across different simulated timescales and found that FPT estimates remain consistent across stable states, suggesting that DynNet effectively captures the system’s global dynamical features (Fig. 4G).

Finally, we performed in silico knockout experiments in DynNet’s dynamic model. We targeted highly expressed genes in distinct cell states to assess their impact on differentiation. Knocking out these lineage-specific markers redirected differentiation, highlighting their role in cell fate determination (Fig. 4H). Eliminating MH-specific genes such as TOB1, which maintains mature hepatocyte function, accelerated the transition toward LB states, indicating their critical role in preserving mature cell identity, consistent with existing biological evidence59,60. In contrast, removing LB markers such as RANBP1 promoted differentiation toward the MH state. This result suggests that these genes play a crucial role in regulating the balance between progenitor and differentiated states, consistent with previous findings on lineage commitment in hepatocyte development61. Similarly, knockout of stem cell markers such as SRGAP2 did not affect the final outcome but altered the speed of differentiation, likely due to the reduced stemness of the cells. This observation aligns with studies indicating that stem cell markers regulate pluripotency and proliferation rather than directly determining cell fate62. First passage time (FPT) analyses further support these findings: knocking out MH markers led to significantly reduced LB FPTs, reflecting a faster transition, and vice versa (Fig. 4I and Figure S30). For genes whose mechanisms are not yet well understood, DynNet can predict their impact on the differentiation process through knockout experiments, as demonstrated with CHP1, SRGAP2 and SRSF9. Overall, the knockout simulations provide valuable insights into how intracellular interactions and gene regulatory mechanisms drive cell fate decisions.

More broadly, our findings demonstrate that DynNet enables a detailed reconstruction of hepatocyte differentiation dynamics at the single-cell level. The enhanced resolution provided by DynNet allows us to capture the evolving dynamics and regulatory interactions, revealing the gradual transitions between distinct cell fates. Although cell fate bifurcation is a complex process, DynNet reliably recapitulates both the stable state distributions and dynamics observed in experimental data. These results underscore DynNet’s potential for revealing the mechanistic underpinnings of cellular differentiation and fate determination.

DynNet decodes EMT landscape switching

To see the effectiveness of DynNet in different biological data, we applied it to a TGF-β perturbation lung cancer time-course dataset for EMT. We analyzed dynamics of this single-cell dataset measuring 36 genes at seven time points (Day 0, 2, 6, 10, 12, 16, and 20) in over 100,000 cells. TGF-β was applied for the first 10 days to induce EMT and then withdrawn for the next 10 days to promote the mesenchymal-to-epithelial transition (MET), a reversal in cell fate. We estimated the data-driven energy landscape from the real data, representing it in the projection space (Fig. 5A, Figure S31–S33 and Methods) and performed K-means clustering to identify four clusters, corresponding to terminal states E, M, IM (MET), and pEMT (partial EMT state), in agreement with previous work63. First, we focused on the period from Day 10 to Day 20, when cell fate transitions are driven solely by gene regulations.

The MET process begins at Day 10 and stabilizes by Day 20. To investigate these dynamics, we first calculated the data-driven landscape, estimated from the distribution of scRNA-seq data (Methods). This landscape was visualized by projecting the high-dimensional data onto a one-dimensional axis using PCA (Fig. 5B). The position along this axis represents the progression through the MET process, and the height of the potential landscape at each point indicates the potential (or energy) of the corresponding cell state. At Day 10, the M state exhibits low energy, reflecting its stabilization following the TGF-β-induced transition from the E state (Fig. 5B, Top). After TGF-β removal, the M state’s energy increases and the E state’s decreases (Fig. 5B, Bottom). These shifts suggest that the energy landscape underlies the transition from the M state to the E state. The global stability of this system converges to the E state.

To simplify DynNet’s parameters and reconstruct the MET dynamics, we focused on a subset of key genes driving the cell fate transition. As DynNet can capture indirect regulatory interactions, reducing the gene to a subset should still reveal the main dynamic trajectories. We performed PCA using PCE and PCM, derived from E and M markers respectively, and calculated each gene’s contribution along these axes (Figure S33). This analysis revealed that only a few genes distinguish the E and M states. Based on the bimodality coefficient of gene expression from Day 10 to Day 20, we selected five core genes (Figure S33 and S34). For the selected core genes, we investigated currently known potential gene interactions and constructed a prior network (see Supplementary Information Section F.2). Since the regulatory relationships among genes are not fully understood, we applied soft constraints as prior information.

We trained DynNet on the MET data to estimate the dynamics governing cell fate transitions. Fig. 5C displays the model-based energy landscape, derived from DynNet’s learned dynamics, with gene expression data at Day 20 overlaid. The alignment of low-energy regions with high-probability areas indicates that DynNet accurately captures the system’s dynamic behavior. Stable state simulations reveal that the potential distributions of E and M markers clearly distinguish the two states, a finding further supported by the marginal gene expression distributions (Fig. 5D), which show that DynNet effectively estimates both the width of gene expression peaks and the intrinsic noise levels. In addition, cells in the E state exhibit high E marker expression, while cells in the M state show high M marker expression, and intermediate cells express both (Fig. 5E). The velocity field calculated in the projected space (Fig. 5F) confirms the stability of the E and M states. However, not all cells revert to the E state, suggesting that the M state retains some stability. Furthermore, we estimated a weighted gene regulatory network based on the prior gene network (Fig. 5G). The estimated results align well with biological evidence, revealing mutual activation among characteristic genes within the same cell type, while characteristic genes of different cell types predominantly exhibit inhibitory relationships.

Identify transition paths and critical genes for EMT

As DynNet reconstructs the underlying dynamics of EMT, we performed several downstream analyses. Based on the stable state positions on the landscape, the M-to-E transition likely occurs via the intermediate states IM and pEMT, consistent with previous findings63. To verify this, we applied the minimum action path method based on Freidlin-Wentzell theory57 to compute the transition actions (Fig. 6A, B). Without TGF-β (Day 10-20), the transition action for MET is significantly lower than that for the reverse E-to-M transition, indicating that MET is more probable under these conditions. Moreover, Fig. 6A shows that EMT follows a direct path, while MET passes through IM, which corresponds to the lowest energy barrier and ensures system stability. Landscape analysis confirms a lower total action for MET, reinforcing the tendency toward the E state. Additionally, the lower action from IM-to-M compared to M-to-IM suggests that IM is a metastable state, with cells often reverting to the more stable M state. Finally, transition network analysis reveals that indirect paths have higher probabilities and lower actions than direct ones, further supporting the role of intermediate states in MET (Fig. 6C and Supplementary Information Section F.2).

To further explore the effects of different genes on the system’s dynamics, we performed a sensitivity analysis of DynNet’s regulatory parameters. This analysis computes the changes in transition actions, ΔSE→M and ΔSM→E after individually perturbing model parameters. Specifically, we perturbed activation constants within groups and inhibition constants between groups by ±5% (Fig. 6F and Supplementary Information Section F.2). The results indicate that enhanced activation between CD44 and VIM significantly increased the M→E transition action while slightly decreased the E→M action, suggesting that stronger activation of M-state markers stabilizes the M state. Conversely, increased activation of E-state markers (e.g., MUC1 and CDH1) raised the E→M transition action and lowered the M→E action, favoring MET and stabilizing the E state. Moreover, stronger mutual inhibition revealed that increased inhibition of E markers reduced the likelihood of MET, whereas enhanced inhibition of M markers made cells in the M state more prone to reverting to the E state. These sensitivity results are qualitatively consistent with known experimental observations64.

To identify key genes driving the EMT, we conducted simulated knockout experiments. Our results show that knocking out CD44 in M-state cells caused a reversion of cell fate back to the E state (Fig. 6H, right), indicating that CD44 is crucial for maintaining the M state. In contrast, enforcing low expression of CDH1 in E-state cells promoted their transition to the M state (Fig. 6H, left), consistent with the critical role of E-cadherin loss (encoded by CDH1) in EMT65. Furthermore, perturbing the regulatory parameters to assess the influence of CD44 and CDH1 on other genes revealed that changes in these two genes produce significant effects, underscoring their importance in EMT regulation. We analyzed gene expression trends along the MET transition pathway to determine the roles of different genes in the MET process. We found that the transition from the M state to the IM state is primarily driven by MUC1, while the expression of VIM (Vimentin) undergoes significant changes before cells reach the E state, consistent with its role as a downstream gene66,67 (Fig. 6G).

To investigate cell transitions during EMT, we incorporated exogenous TGF-β into our model, reflecting its role in regulating gene expression. We treated the EMT process as a perturbation of the underlying dynamics trained on the MET process and tracked the EMT dynamics by fine-tuning the parameters of DynNet. TGF-β is present during the first 10 days of EMT but absent in the subsequent MET phase63. It inhibits E-cadherin expression via degradation mechanisms involving SMURF1 and SMURF264,68. To capture this, we added TGF-β as a regulatory node affecting CDH1 and modeled its inhibitory effect as a function of its concentration69. The degradation rate constant k was trained solely on the EMT process. We then compared the stable state distribution estimated by DynNet with real data (Figs. 6D, E). The marginal distribution of the TGF-β-induced EMT process estimated by DynNet shows good agreement with the data at stable state distribution. This indicates that although the parameters of DynNet were trained on MET data, it can still successfully simulate the EMT process through fine-tuning with TGF-β. In addition, state transition probabilities were calculated (see Supplementary Information Section F.2). Our analysis revealed that the original E state (Day 0) comprises multiple sub-states, E1, E2 and E3, with E1 characterized by high expression of epithelial markers and E2/E3 by partial mesenchymal features. Notably, in the model retrained by adjusting only the degradation rate parameters, cells in the E1 state first switched to the E2 state before undergoing EMT (Figure S35). Transition probability calculations further supported a lower transition action for the E1-to-E2 pathway, indicating a higher likelihood for this route. Overall, these results suggest that our original dynamical model effectively captures the essential characteristics of the EMT dynamics. By training the regulatory parameters using MET data and fine-tuning only the degradation rate parameters, we accurately described the dynamics of the EMT process, in line with the known role of TGF-β in modulating degradation rates, particularly for E-cadherin.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that DynNet effectively captures the essential characteristics of the EMT process by integrating MET training data, fine-tuning degradation rates, and incorporating TGF-β effects.

Discussion

Existing methods for inferring dynamics involve trade-offs between interpretability, data requirements, and scalability18,20,21. DynNet overcomes these limitations by integrating mechanistic biophysical modeling with data-driven neural SDE frameworks. Unlike purely model-driven approaches constrained by oversimplified assumptions or black-box deep learning models that lack interpretability, DynNet explicitly parameterizes gene regulatory interactions using Hill functions while employing a dynamic noise estimator to capture the effects of stochasticity. By parameterizing causal regulatory mechanisms directly within the continuous drift function, it moves beyond traditional models that rely on post-hoc, local Jacobian matrix approximations, thereby granting the inferred dynamics robust global interpretability. Importantly, DynNet is fundamentally designed as a continuous dynamics modeling framework rather than a standalone GRN inference tool. This continuous-time integration inherently mitigates interval-induced spurious correlations that typically plague discrete-time models. Balancing prior biological information with data evidence, its integrated biophysical component captures both direct and indirect regulatory relationships within the gene network, facilitating dynamics inference from a minimal set of key genes directly in the original gene expression space.

On simulation datasets (MISA and branching process), we evaluate the capability of DynNet to infer stable state properties, predict cell fates, and track underlying dynamics, highlighting the value of integrating prior knowledge via constraints. On the realistic hepatocyte differentiation dataset, DynNet reconstructs a dynamic landscape featuring two attractor states (MH and LB) and captures bifurcation dynamics at the HE cluster, consistent with experimental observations. On EMT dataset, DynNet quantitatively characterizes the dynamics of EMT and MET processes, predicts transition pathways and identifies key genes as fate determinants, bridging molecular mechanisms with cellular decision-making. This capability allows for direct gene perturbation analysis and facilitates the identification of key genes and mechanistic rules driving cellular state changes. Ultimately, this framework presents a valuable tool for studying functions and mechanisms of complex biological systems, controlling cell fate transitions and proposing therapeutic strategies for disease28,70.

To ensure the practical applicability and robustness of this mechanistic framework, several specific strategies and design choices have been incorporated into DynNet. First, to ensure computational feasibility in high-dimensional stochastic modeling, we adopted a diagonal diffusion matrix. Both theoretical and empirical validations confirm the robustness of this simplification: the continuous drift function effectively captures underlying gene-gene correlations, while the learned state-dependent noise dynamically compensates for omitted non-diagonal terms. Second, to facilitate the optimization process, we strategically integrated prior knowledge. While purely data-driven priors are susceptible to spurious correlations, our framework strictly utilizes them as optimization accelerators rather than validation metrics. To mitigate these inherent structural inaccuracies during de novo discovery, we employ an L2 soft-constraint, enabling continuous data dynamics to stably override false prior edges.

Nevertheless, there are several limitations related to structural assumptions and extreme computational scaling. First, while employing Hill functions significantly enhances mechanistic interpretability, it imposes a rigid structural assumption on the system’s dynamics. This restricts the functional flexibility of the drift equation and constrains the optimization landscape. Second, the framework operates under the assumption that the biological system eventually converges to quasi-steady states, which limits its utility in analyzing highly oscillatory or cyclical processes, such as the cell cycle. Finally, despite our mathematical simplifications, the numerical integration of SDEs and the optimization of non-linear drift functions remain computationally demanding. Because this optimization is intensive, DynNet is inherently constrained to targeted sets of core driver genes and cannot currently process genome-wide transcriptomic data. Given these boundaries, DynNet is optimally positioned as a downstream analytical tool for deep mechanistic dissection rather than broad de novo network discovery.

To enhance DynNet’s applicability, flexibility, and scalability, several strategies may be utilized. First, a gene encoding method may be adapted to analyze co-expression modules of high-dimensional gene data. Furthermore, a mixed modeling architecture that utilizes lightweight response functions for non-core gene modules can be used. To increase mechanistic modeling flexibility, it may be useful to incorporate a hybrid neural network architecture that combines the core mechanistic Hill function assumption with an assumption-free component (such as a general MLP), allowing DynNet to better describe complex system characteristics.

Neural network architecture

The proposed network structures are illustrated in Fig. 1. Given that cell fate evolution can be expressed by SDEs

the network structure was designed based on the principle of using nonlinear functions to model system dynamics and to account for fluctuations within the biological system.

In the proposed Neural SDE framework, the time variable t corresponds strictly to the experimental sampling time (i.e., the elapsed physical time between measurements) rather than an algorithmically inferred pseudotime. In biological developmental systems, cells sampled at a single experimental time point often exhibit significant stage heterogeneity, such as the coexistence of progenitor and differentiated cells. The SDE framework does not assume developmental synchrony within a given time point; rather, it explicitly models this within-timepoint heterogeneity as stochastic fluctuations, allowing individual cells to progress at varying rates. Utilization of experimental real time preserves the temporal asynchrony necessary for estimating true kinetic quantities, such as transition rates and barrier-crossing timescales.

Noise estimator

Stochasticity in gene expression plays vital roles in many of the real biological processes, including differentiation and cell type transition73,74. A Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) Dϑ is implemented to approximate the diffusion matrix Dx, where 2D(x)=σ(x)σ(x)T in Eq. 5. Specifically, we assume D(x)∈RM×M is a diagonal matrix with diagonal elements Dii(x)=dix and zero off-diagonal elements. The MLP takes the gene expression profile of each cell as input and outputs the diffusion term for each gene at the cell’s current state. This design enables adaptation to intricate patterns in noise generation, offering a biologically plausible representation of noise intensity across varying expression states.

A detailed analysis of the time complexity is essential to demonstrate the scalability of the DynNet model on large single-cell datasets. Let M be the number of genes, N be the average number of cells at each time point, and L be the number of time steps. The total complexity is dominated by the optimization phases within Algorithm 1 and Algorithm 2.

GRN reconstruction

Although DynNet is fundamentally designed for continuous dynamics modeling rather than standalone GRN inference, the constraint method provides utility of GRN reconstruction under purely data-driven settings. Specifically, when the model is optimized using soft constraints without any external prior knowledge, this procedure enables the de novo generation of a reliable reference GRN. This extracted topology can subsequently be utilized as a structural constraint in subsequent training phases to further stabilize and guide the inference of the continuous vector field. To discretize the continuous learned weights into a final sparse network topology without relying on arbitrary, hard-coded cutoffs, we implemented a data-driven thresholding strategy based on GMM (see Supplementary Information Section A.5). Under L2 regularization, the distribution of these normalized weights typically exhibits a dense component near zero (representing regularized, non-essential edges) and a long tail (representing data-supported interactions). We fitted a two-component GMM to this distribution. The component with the lower mean was designated as the background noise model, parameterized by a mean (μnoise) and a standard deviation (σnoise). The specific cutoff for retaining a structural edge was defined as the 99.7% confidence upper bound of this noise distribution (μnoise+3σnoise). Edges with absolute weights exceeding this threshold were retained in the final discrete GRN.

Energy landscape construction

Understanding the stable states of a system is crucial in the study of cell fate decisions. Dimension reduction for landscape

Gene regulatory networks often involve a large number of genes, resulting in a high-dimensional state space that can be challenging to interpret directly. To facilitate visualization and analysis of the energy landscape, it is often necessary to project it onto a lower-dimensional subspace. We followed the method in our previous work to perform the dimension reduction for the high-dimensional landscape41. A common and effective technique for this dimensionality reduction is based on the principal components of the system’s variability, which can be extracted from the covariance matrix of the data.

Specifically, a projection matrix V∈RM×C is constructed, where M is the original high dimensionality and C<M is the desired lower dimensionality. The columns of V are formed by the C eigenvectors corresponding to the first C largest eigenvalues of the covariance matrix Σ of the system’s data. These eigenvectors represent the directions of maximum variance in the data.

Statistics and reproducibility

To evaluate the robustness of the DynNet framework, all computational findings were validated through multiple independent simulation runs using different random seeds. For the MISA and branch synthetic datasets, individual cells were simulated independently; the robustness of these simulations was verified across various random seeds, with the exact number of simulated cells detailed in respective figure legends. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. Rather, sample sizes for stochastic simulations were chosen based on standard practices in computational systems biology to ensure the stability of the inferred dynamic distributions and sufficient statistical power for transition analysis. Specifically, for the hepatocyte dataset, FPT estimations involved 100 independent stochastic simulations per temporal resolution condition to properly elucidate differentiation kinetics. For the simulated gene knockouts, 50 independent cell trajectories were simulated for each knockout and wild-type control group, with statistical significance between transition delays determined using a two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Similarly, for the EMT dataset, the perturbation-induced changes in the minimum action path (ΔS) were calculated as the average over 50 independent simulation replicates. All successfully completed simulation runs were included in the reported results, and no data were excluded from the analyses. While inherent stochasticity is introduced via the simulation algorithms, the experiments were not randomized in terms of group allocation. Furthermore, the investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment, as the group assignments—such as specific in silico gene knockouts—were mathematically predefined by the computational models.

Datasets and prior knowledge

This study utilizes both synthetic and experimental single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data. Synthetic data, generated using the MISA and BoolODE models, was used to evaluate the method’s performance56. These data were split into training and testing sets (see Supplementary Information Section E.1 and E.2). The MISA model simulates multi-stable gene regulatory networks, while the BoolODE model combines Boolean logic with ordinary differential equations. Experimental scRNA-seq data from publicly available sources, specifically an EMT dataset63 and a hepatocyte differentiation dataset49, were preprocessed using Scanpy.

Prior knowledge about gene regulatory relationships was integrated into the model. This information, extracted from databases like STRING and EVEX, was used to construct a prior gene regulatory network. In addition, we also consider using correlation as an estimation of the gene regulatory network. Both hard and soft constraints are employed to guide the learning process. Hard constraints enforce consistency with highly reliable prior knowledge, while soft constraints allow the model to adapt when prior information is less certain or incomplete. These constraints help to improve the biological interpretability of the model by encouraging it to learn regulatory patterns consistent with existing knowledge.
