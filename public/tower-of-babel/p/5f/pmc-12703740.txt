Discovery of a Novel Sucrose 6F-Phosphate Phosphorylase and Its Application for Isomelezitose Production
Authors: Manon Steynen, Jorick Franceus, Davy Van de Walle, Koen Dewettinck, Vladimír Křen, Helena Pelantová, Tom Desmet
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Rare sugars have gained attention as attractive alternatives to conventional sugars due to their potential health benefits and lower caloric value. However, their scarcity restricts widespread application, highlighting the need for efficient biosynthetic production strategies. In this study, a novel sucrose 6F-phosphate phosphorylase from Sphaerochaeta globosa strain Buddy was identified through sequence analysis within the glycoside hydrolase family GH13_18. Substrate screening revealed that this enzyme is capable of producing isomelezitose, a rare trisaccharide naturally found in honey, known for its osmoprotective and potential prebiotic properties. A proof-of-concept production at a 1 L scale, starting from cheap bulk sugars sucrose and isomaltulose, yielded 68 g of high-purity isomelezitose (>99%), which enabled a first evaluation of its properties as a food ingredient. Isomelezitose exhibited limited digestibility and a relative sweetness of 27% compared to sucrose. Moreover, its physicochemical properties were comparable to those of sucrose, including similar glass transition temperature, crystallinity, and flow/tribological behavior. To this end, isomelezitose might form an interesting opportunity as a sugar alternative in the food industry.

Introduction

Carbohydrates serve as the primary source of energy, with sucrose, commonly known as table sugar, being the best-known example. However, in 2015, the World Health Organization (WHO) issued guidelines recommending that the intake of free sugars should be limited to less than 10% of total energy intake for adults and children. These measures are aimed at tackling the rampant epidemic of overweight and obesity, which contribute significantly to the risk of various noncommunicable diseases, such as diabetes and cancer. , However, despite the negative connotation often associated with carbohydrates, certain sugars have been reported to show attractive health-promoting properties. Allulose, for example, has only 10% of the caloric content with respect to sucrose (0.4 kcal·g–1), while retaining approximately 70% of its sweetness. In addition, allulose has several beneficial physiological properties such as antihyperglycemic, antihyperlipidemic, and antiobesity effects, − making it a promising low-calorie sweetener for the food industry. Furthermore, the disaccharide kojibiose (2-O-α-d-glucopyranosyl-d-glucopyranose) can stimulate the growth of beneficial microorganisms such as bifidobacteria and is not metabolized by common oral bacteria, making it a promising prebiotic and noncariogenic food ingredient. − These sugars occur naturally in trace amounts in sources such as wheat, Itea plants, and honey.

These carbohydrates are often categorized as rare sugars, defined by the International Society of Rare Sugars as monosaccharides and their derivatives that are rare in nature. ,, Unfortunately, this scarcity hinders their widespread exploitation in industry, and efficient biosynthetic processes need to be developed to unlock their full potential. To that end, enzymatic syntheses using sucrose phosphorylases (SP, EC 2.4.1.7), belonging to subfamily 18 of glycoside hydrolase family 13 (GH13_18), have previously proven to be very effective. Using a double displacement mechanism, SPs catalyze the reversible phosphorolysis of sucrose into α-d-glucose 1-phosphate (αGlc1P) and fructose. However, their broad substrate promiscuity also allows SPs to transfer the glucosyl moiety from sucrose (i.e., glucosyl donor) to a variety of glycosyl acceptors other than phosphate. The transglucosylation potential of SPs has already been widely explored and the SP from Bifidobacterium adolescentis (BaSP) has successfully been used for the biocatalytic production of kojibiose, , nigerose, and their analogues. Here, we focus on novel protein sequences that differ from known SPs from GH13_18 based on deviations in highly conserved residue motifs.

In this study, we report the discovery of a novel sucrose 6F-phosphate phosphorylase (SPP) originating from Sphaerochaeta globosa, strain Buddy (SphSPP), a Gram-negative bacterium that was isolated from black, anoxic river sediments. To elucidate the wild-type activity as well as the promiscuity of this enzyme, a broad screening of putative acceptor substrates was performed.

Material & Methods

Chemicals and Reagents

Unless stated otherwise, all chemicals were of analytical grade and were obtained from Merck. Carbohydrates were purchased from Biosynth. The SphSPP sequence (UniProt: F0RRL2) was codon-optimized for Escherichia coli and then synthesized and subcloned into a pET21a vector at the NheI and XhoI sites by Life Technologies (Belgium). E. coli BL21 (DE3) electrocompetent cells were transformed with the resulting plasmid.

Sequence Analysis

Protein identifiers classified within GH13_18 were extracted from the CAZy database (https://www.cazy.org), and their corresponding amino acid sequences were retrieved from GenBank. Duplicate entries were then systematically eliminated. Subsequently, a multiple sequence alignment was made with Clustal Omega using default parameters. A phylogenetic tree was constructed with RAxML v8.2.12 (LG+I+G substitution model) and then visualized using iTOL v5. , Homologues of SphSPP were identified by BLAST analysis using the default settings of the NCBI server (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov). Sequence logos were generated using WebLogo3. No signal peptides were present for the sequences analyzed in this study.

Enzyme Production and Purification

Precultures of 5 mL were grown overnight at 37 °C and 200 rpm in LB medium (Lysogeny broth; 500 mL; 5 g·L–1 NaCl, 10 g·L–1 tryptone, 5 g·L–1 yeast extract) supplemented with 100 μg·mL–1 ampicillin. Subsequently, 500 mL LB, containing 100 μg·mL–1 ampicillin, was inoculated with the precultures (1%) and again incubated at the same conditions. At OD600 0.6, expression was induced by the addition of isopropyl β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside to a final concentration of 0.1 mM. Cells were harvested by centrifugation after 16 h of incubation at 20 °C. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was frozen at −20 °C.

SphSPP was extracted by cell lysis and sonication. The obtained pellet was thawed and resuspended in 8 mL lysis buffer (10 mM imidazole, 0.1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, 1 mg·mL–1 lysozyme, 50 mM phosphate-buffered saline; pH 7.4) and incubated on ice for 30 min. Using the Branson sonifier 450, the lysate was then sonicated three times for 2 min (level 3, 50% duty cycle) after which the soluble fraction was obtained by centrifugation (18,000g for 30 min). The clarified lysate was His6-tag purified by nickel-nitrilotriacetic acid chromatography following the instructions of the supplier (HisPur Ni-NTA; Thermo Fisher Scientific). After elution the purified enzyme was exchanged to 50 mM 2-(N-morpholino)­ethanesulfonic acid (MES) at pH 5.5 using an Amicon Ultra-15 centrifugal filter unit with a 30-kDa cutoff (Merck). The protein concentration was measured using a NanoDrop ND-1000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with the extinction coefficient (93,170 M–1cm–1) and molecular weight (56.4 kDa) as determined by the ProtParam tool (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/). The molecular weight of SphSPP was verified by sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE; 12% gel; Bio-Rad).

Substrate Screening and Kinetic Characterization of SphSPP

The specificity of the putative SphSPP was evaluated qualitatively using 25 mM αGlc1P as the glucosyl donor and 50 mM acceptor substrate (50 mM MES pH 6.5, 30 °C). In total, SphSPP was screened on 59 potential acceptors. Here, the release of inorganic phosphate was detected with the colorimetric phosphomolybdate assay in accordance with Gawronski et al. Optimal reaction conditions were determined in triplicate with 100 mM αGlc1P, 50 mM d-fructose and 0.02 mg·mL–1 purified enzyme. Reactions were monitored for 5 min with sampling at regular intervals. The pH optimum was assessed at 37 °C using 50 mM acetate (pH 3.0–4.0), 50 mM MES (pH 4.5–6.5) and 50 mM 3-(N-morpholino)­propanesulfonic acid buffer (MOPS; pH 7.0–7.5). By analogy, the temperature influence on enzyme activity was determined using 50 mM MES buffer at pH 5.5.

The apparent kinetic parameters for SphSPP were determined at 37 °C and pH 5.5 for sucrose, αGlc1P, inorganic phosphate, fructose 6-phosphate, fructose and isomaltulose. In the phosphorolytic direction of the reaction, the release of fructose from sucrose was measured using the colorimetric bicinchoninic acid reducing sugars assay. Reactions were performed using varying concentrations of sucrose (0–150 mM) or inorganic phosphate (0–24 mM), with 100 mM of the respective cosubstrate. In the synthetic direction, the release of inorganic phosphate from αGlc1P was detected using the colorimetric phosphomolybdate assay. Reactions were performed using varying concentrations of αGlc1P (0–80 mM), fructose 6-phosphate (0–50 mM), fructose (0–100 mM), or isomaltulose (0–50 mM), with 100 mM of cosubstrate. The reactions were monitored for 5 min with regular sampling after which the specific activity was calculated. All reactions were performed in triplicate and the final kinetic parameters were obtained by nonlinear regression of the Michaelis–Menten equation using Sigma Plot 15. The molecular weight was used to calculate the turnover number k cat. All specific activities in this study were determined by monitoring initial reaction rates (<10% conversion).

The enzyme’s kinetic stability was evaluated by incubating the purified protein (77 μg·mL–1) at 37 °C. At regular time intervals, the residual activity was determined at 30 °C in 50 mM MES, pH 5.5 by measuring the phosphate release with the phosphomolybdate assay. Assuming first-order inactivation kinetics, the experimental data were fitted to the following equation: f = a.e–b.x, from which the half-life value was then calculated.

Optimization of Reaction Conditions

Isomelezitose synthesis was performed in Eppendorf tubes (500 μL) at optimal reaction conditions (50 mM MES pH 5.5; 37 °C). Purified SphSPP (0.02 mg·mL–1) was incubated with varying concentrations of sucrose (200–600 mM) and isomaltulose (200–400 mM) and reactions were monitored for 24 h. Samples were taken regularly and terminated by heat inactivation (5 min, 95 °C). Subsequent analysis was carried out using high-performance anion exchange chromatography (HPAEC; Dionex ICS-600; Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a CarboPac PA20 column and pulsed amperometric detection (PAD) at a flow rate of 0.5 mL·min–1. An isocratic elution with 90% eluent A (ddH2O) and 10% eluent B (100 mM NaOH) was performed for 12 min, followed by a 1 min linear gradient to 100% eluent B, which was maintained for 10 min. The initial eluent composition was then restored in 1 min and run for an additional 3 min. Isomelezitose production was quantified by reference to the fructose release that occurs when sucrose is converted while taking into account the glucose release caused by the competing hydrolysis reaction.

Proof-of-Concept: 1 L scale production

A proof-of-concept production process was performed on 1-L scale with 400 mM sucrose and 200 mM isomaltulose in 50 mM MES buffer (pH 5.5, 37 °C). His6-tag purified SphSPP was added to the mixture at a final concentration of 0.02 mg·mL–1. Isomelezitose synthesis was then monitored for 9 h with sampling at regular time points, and samples were subsequently inactivated at 95 °C for 10 min. The obtained solution was cooled to 45 °C before adding invertase to remove the remaining substrates. After 4 h, invertase was inactivated by heating the solution to 95 °C for 10 min. For removal of all ions, the reaction mixture was treated with a mixed bed ion-exchange resin (AG501- X8 (D), Bio-Rad), after which all suspended solids were removed by vacuum filtration with a 0.2 μm poly­(ether sulfone) filter. Isomelezitose was finally isolated from the monosaccharides through preparative liquid chromatography (Sugar Purification System, Knauer, Eurokat Ca-resin, mesh 25–56 μm), after which the different fractions were concentrated with a rotavapor (Rotavapor R-200, Büchi) at 40 °C to a final Brix value of 20° (°Bx). A highly pure, powdered product was obtained after lyophilization (VaCo5 lyophilizer, Zirbus technology).

Analysis was performed by HPAEC-PAD as described above. NMR Analysis

The structure of isomelezitose was confirmed by nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy of highly pure isomelezitose (>99%) derived from the 1-L scale production. NMR data were acquired on a Bruker Avance III 700 MHz spectrometer (Bruker BioSpin, Rheinstetten, Germany) in D2O at 30 °C. All experiments were performed using Bruker TopSpin 3.5 software. The 1H NMR spectrum was referenced to the residual signal of D2O (δH 4.732 ppm), carbon signals were referenced to the signal of acetone (δC 30.50 ppm). Spin systems of individual monosaccharide units (assigned by COSY, HSQC, 1D-TOCSY, and HSQC-TOCSY experiments) were linked using diagnostic correlations extracted from the HMBC experiment. The final NMR data were in agreement with spectra previously reported in the literature. ,

Product Evaluation

Food Formulation Properties

Isomelezitose and sucrose in powdered form were characterized for their dry matter content via oven drying according to the official method AOAC 931.04. Their thermal characteristics were analyzed via a Q1000 DSC (TA Instruments) equipped with a refrigerated cooling and calibrated with indium (enthalpy and temperature), azo-benzene (temperature), and undecane (temperature). Melting of isomelezitose and sucrose enclosed in aluminum cups was recorded by heating from 20 °C to beyond 200 °C at 5 °C·min–1. Preliminary results (absence of a sharp melting peak) for isomelezitose suggested a relatively low dry matter content and necessitated a predrying step of 1.0 g sample for 2 h at 140 °C. The melting temperatures (Tm, onset, Tm, max and Tm, offset) were retrieved as described in Beerens et al. Glass transition temperature characteristics were determined after conditioning predried isomelezitose and sucrose at their peak maximum temperature in the DSC cell followed by cooling and reheating at 5 °C·min–1. Using Universal Analysis software, onset glass transition temperature (Tg, onset), midpoint glass transition temperature (Tg, mid) and offset glass transition temperature (Tg, offset) were determined. Solubility (°Bx) of isomelezitose was determined by measuring the Brix value (MA 871 refractometer, Milwaukee Instruments) of a saturated solution separated from crystals following the preparation of a highly concentrated solution at 80 °C and conditioning for a couple of days at ambient temperature (20.5 ± 0.5 °C) in a closed falcon tube. Next, 30 °Bx solutions of isomelezitose and sucrose were prepared to determine their water activity (Aqualab 4 TEV aw meter) at ambient temperature, and viscosity η (at 20 and 37 °C), and friction (at 37 °C). A flow curve was recorded from 0.5 s–1 to 158 s–1 under steady-state conditions using an AR2000ex rheometer (TA Instruments) equipped with concentric cylindrical geometry (conical bob with 14.00 mm radius and cup with 15.00 mm radius) at 5.920 mm gap. Newtonian flow behavior was confirmed, so the slope of the curve corresponded to the viscosity η (mPa·s). A Stribeck curve was recorded from 1.10–8 m·s–1 to 1 m·s–1 under 1 N normal force using an MCR302 rheometer (Anton Paar) equipped with a ball-on-three-pins geometry (glass ball and polydimethylsiloxane pins). As friction factor μ is considered a system property, the first measurement of the sucrose solution was followed by the first measurement of the isomelezitose solution, and so on. The area under the Stribeck curve was calculated in Excel by applying the trapezoidal rule after the conversion of the x values to LOG10­(x). All of the analyses, listed in this section, were done in triplicate. An independent t test using SPSS Statistics 29.0 was used to compare the average values of the isomelezitose and sucrose powder and solution at a p-value of 0.05.

To evaluate the sweetness of an isomelezitose solution (30 g isomelezitose/100 g solution), panelists were recruited among the PhD students and staff at the Faculty of Bioscience Engineering (Ghent University). The candidates were subjected to different training sessions, namely two ranking tests comprising 4 sucrose solutions (three-digit coded) and three scoring tests in which a sucrose solution (three-digit coded) had to be scored from 0 to 10 and supported by four sucrose solutions with a given score (1, 4, 7, and 10 for respectively 1.0, 4.0, 7.0, and 10.0 g sucrose per 100 g solution, respectively). The results of the ranking tests and the scoring tests of the 12 recruits were satisfactory. Finally, 11 panelists evaluated the sweetness of the three-digit coded isomelezitose solutions supported by the above-mentioned reference solutions and scores. A boxplot was created using SPSS Statistics 29.0 software package to exclude outliers. The relative sweetness to sucrose was calculated based on the score and dry weight of the isomelezitose solution. The training courses and the evaluation of the sweetness of the isomelezitose solution were approved by the Ethics Committee of Ghent University (No. EC/2024/0125).

In vitro Digestion with Rat Intestinal Extract

Isomelezitose and standards (including sucrose, isomaltulose, maltose, and maltotriose) were incubated for 90 min with rat intestinal extract (Sigma-Aldrich), prepared according to Hodoniczky et al. Each reaction (200 μL) contained 40% (v/v) rat intestinal extract and 20 mM substrate in 100 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7). The reactions were shaken at 500 rpm and maintained at 37 °C. All experiments were performed in triplicate. To determine the hydrolysis percentage, appropriate dilutions were analyzed using the glucose oxidase-peroxidase (GOD-POD) assay to measure the amount of released glucose.

Results & Discussion

Search for Novel Glycoside Phosphorylases

The transglucosylation potential of SPs has previously been exploited for the synthesis of a series of valuable sugars and glucosides. ,,, In an effort to further expand this product portfolio, the GH13_18 family of the Carbohydrate Active Enzyme Database was investigated to identify new phosphorylases that may have divergent substrate specificities. Earlier studies found that a specific sequence stretch that corresponds to a flexible loop in subsite +1 can act as a reliable specificity determinant. ,, In SPs, this specificity fingerprint is found in positions 337–341 (numbering of Leuconostoc mesenteroides SP; LmSP) where it is characterized by a highly conserved (L/V)­D­(I/L/V)­YQ motif. In comparison, glucosylglycerol phosphorylases contain a significantly different conserved GAIYQ motif at the equivalent positions (Figure ).

Phylogenetic tree of the GH13_18 family. Clades are distinguished based on their specificity fingerprints, highlighting the correlation between sequence logos of the conserved motifs (positions 337–341 in Leuconostoc mesenteroides SP; LmSP) and the corresponding substrate specificity. The newly identified fingerprint is highlighted in dark blue and characterized GPs with black dots. Sequence logos were generated using WebLogo3.

The CAZy database was accessed and all sequences belonging to GH13_18 were extracted and aligned, rendering ∼ 1900 unique sequences (June 2021). Subsequently, this multiple sequence alignment was searched for sequences containing an atypical sequence motif at the aforementioned specificity fingerprint positions. This resulted in the discovery of a divergent glycoside phosphorylase (GP) sequence originating from Sphaerochaeta globosa strain Buddy, characterized by an FDTYQ motif and sharing 34–58% sequence similarity with known, characterized GPs (Supporting Information Figure S1). A BLAST search of this sequence revealed 29 more sequences with the same FDTYQ motif, as well as 15 sequences where D or T was substituted for N or A, respectively. In the phylogenetic tree, these sequences all reside in a single subclade that has evolved out of the clade of LmSP-like sequences.

Expression, Purification, and Biochemical Characterization of SphSPP

The putative GP from S. globosa str. Buddy (SphGP) was recombinantly expressed in E. coli , with a C-terminal His6-tag enabling its purification by nickel-nitrilotriacetic acid metal affinity chromatography (Supporting Information Table S1). SDS-page analysis revealed a single protein band in the soluble fraction, with a molecular weight of 55 kDa which is consistent with the theoretical value of 56.4 kDa (Supporting Information Figure S2). From a 500 mL culture, approximately 5 mg of protein could be obtained. The optimal pH and temperature of SphGP was determined using αGlc1P and d-fructose as the donor and acceptor substrate, respectively. The enzyme exhibited maximum activity at pH 5.5 and 37 °C (Supporting Information Figure S3). To assess kinetic stability, the enzyme was incubated at 37 °C, and residual activity was periodically measured at 30 °C and pH 5.5, revealing a half-life of 1.6 h based on first-order inactivation kinetics (Supporting Information Figure S4).

The specificity of SphGP was evaluated through an extensive screening on a range of putative glycosyl acceptor substrates, which included mono- and disaccharides as well as phosphate sugars (Supporting Information Table S2). GPs are able to transfer the glucosyl moiety from αGlc1P to various acceptor substrates, releasing inorganic phosphate. Phosphate can subsequently be detected by the colorimetric phosphomolybdate assay and serve as a measure for synthetic activity. The highest phosphate release was observed in reactions with d-fructose or d-fructose 6-phosphate as the acceptor substrates. Interestingly, the specificity motif of SphGP (FDTYQ) is a combination of the motifs found in SPs ((L/V)­D­(I/L/V)­YQ) and some SPPs (FDVHQ), which could explain the somewhat promiscuous behavior toward both substrates. Furthermore, l-sorbose and isomaltulose also resulted in significant phosphate release. While the former activity was previously reported by Aerts et al., the activity toward the disaccharide isomaltulose (6-O-α-d-glucopyranosyl-d-fructofuranose) has not been reported in GPs to date. Isolation and structural elucidation of the transglycosylation product by NMR spectroscopy confirmed the formation of isomelezitose (α-d-glucopyranosyl-(1,6)-β-d-fructofuranosyl-(2,1)-β-d-glucopyranoside), with chemical shifts in accordance with the rounded values previously reported in the literature. , (Supporting Information Table S3). As such, SphGP appears to transfer the glucosyl unit derived from αGlc1P to the reducing end of isomaltulose, thereby forming an α-(1,2)-β-glycosidic bond and leading to the synthesis of the nonreducing trisaccharide (Figure ). Isomelezitose is naturally present in honey and first reported as a minor transglycosylation product of α-glucosidase from brewer’s yeast. Studies have shown that this nonreducing trisaccharide is noncariogenic, low in calories, and a promising candidate ingredient for diabetic foods. ,,, Moreover, it exhibits potential as an osmolyte as it significantly reduces losses in cell viability and activity during air drying and storage. Nevertheless, the potential of isomelezitose as a pharmaceutical excipient, osmoprotectant, and diabetic food ingredient remains underexploited because of its high cost and low availability. In this regard, its synthesis from sucrose may offer an economically feasible solution.

Reactions catalyzed by SphSPP. (a) Native activity, reversible phosphorolysis of sucrose 6F-phosphate; (b) promiscuous activity, reversible phosphorolysis of isomelezitose.

The kinetic parameters were determined in both the synthesis and phosphorolysis direction (Table ). Michaelis–Menten kinetics were observed for each of the substrates tested. The Michaelis constants (K M) for sucrose (54 mM) and fructose (40 mM) were significantly higher than previously reported for SPs, with their K M values typically ranging between 1–15 mM and 8–22 mM, respectively. Furthermore, SphGP exhibited a K M of 7 mM for d-fructose 6-phosphate, indicating that this may be its preferred acceptor substrate. SPPs were previously discovered in GH13_18 by analyzing the gene landscape of a putative GP from Thermoanaerobacterium thermosaccharolyticum, where the GP gene was found in an operon with a putative phosphofructokinase, hinting at its activity on phosphorylated sugars. Interestingly, previous research has shown that in certain Clostridiae species from the human microbiome, the gene landscape may also include genes encoding α-galactosidase and sucrose kinase. This arrangement suggests an alternative pathway in which sucrose 6F-phosphate is translocated via the AgaE PTS component, potentially indicating a broader physiological role. However, in the gene landscape surrounding SphGP, no such genes could be identified. Nevertheless, the kinetic parameters obtained here are fully in line with those observed with the T. thermosaccharolyticum SPP. Therefore, we propose that SphGP should be designated as SphSPP, regardless of its origin from the phylogenetic clade of LmSP-like sequences.

The sucrose 6F-phosphate phosphorylase from S. globosa str. Buddy (SphSPP) also shows a relatively high affinity for isomaltulose (K M = 13 mM). This likely reflects the fact that both fructose 6-phosphate and isomaltulose share a fructose moiety substituted at the C6 position, albeit with different substituents, i.e. a phosphate group and a glucosyl group, respectively (Figure ).

Process Optimization for Isomelezitose Production

The activity on isomaltulose and resulting isomelezitose synthesis has not yet been reported for phosphorylases. However, a number of biocatalytic processes for the synthesis of isomelezitose from sucrose involving various glucansucrases ,,, have already been developed. Unfortunately, these processes are typically characterized by low substrate conversions or elongation and branching, leading to the formation of higher oligosaccharides. A second enzymatic approach involves the use of an isomelezitose synthase, engineered from a sucrose isomerase of Protaminobacter rubrum. However, this reaction needs to be stopped prematurely in order to limit the synthesis of undesired side products. Finally, several synthesis pathways using α-glucosidases or cellular systems have been described. ,− Notably, a process using cell extracts from Metschnikowia reukaufii in the presence of high sucrose concentrations yielded 81 g·L–1 isomelezitose. Nevertheless, the synthesis includes the formation of alternative transglucosylation products, such as trehalulose, melezitose, erlose and theanderose, resulting in a more complex product mixture, which can complicate downstream processing.

The potential of isomelezitose in applications such as pharmaceutical excipients, osmoprotectants and diabetic foods is still limited by its high cost and low availability in nature, making extraction from natural sources economically unfeasible. Therefore, the discovery of SphSPP could provide an efficient alternative route for the biocatalytic synthesis of this rare sugar starting from the low-cost bulk sugars sucrose and isomaltulose, aiming for maximum conversion with minimal hydrolysis (Figure ).

Isomelezitose production from bulk sugars sucrose and isomaltulose, catalyzed by SphSPP.

To that end, SphSPP (0.02 mg·mL–1) was incubated with varying concentrations of sucrose and isomaltulose during 6-h reactions at 37 °C and pH 5.5 (Table ) in order to evaluate the conversion of the limiting substrate. A 2-fold excess of the acceptor substrate isomaltulose only resulted in a 28% conversion of the limiting substrate sucrose. However, a 2-fold and 3-fold excess of the donor substrate sucrose resulted in isomaltulose conversions of 96% and 57%, respectively. Using a 2-fold excess of sucrose thus offers significant advantages, as it ensures near-complete conversion of the limiting substrate, which greatly simplifies downstream processing. Additionally, it reduces the use of isomaltulose as a starting material, which is more expensive than sucrose (being produced from the latter through isomerization).

The double displacement mechanism of glucoside phosphorylases in GH13_18 inherently poses the risk of water molecules entering the acceptor site and intercepting the glucosyl-enzyme intermediate. The resulting irreversible hydrolysis of the donor substrate releases glucose, which in turn can serve as a competing acceptor substrate, leading to the synthesis of undesired other transglycosylation products. Therefore, it is noteworthy that only a minimal amount of glucose (<1%) was detected under each of the tested reaction conditions, indicating that the common hydrolytic side-activity is largely absent in SphSPP (Figure ).

Proof-of-Concept: 1 L Scale Production

Our small-scale optimization indicated that a 2:1 ratio of sucrose to isomaltulose yielded the highest conversion of the limiting substrate within just 6 h. Consequently, the same reaction conditions were used in a proof-of-concept production process on a 1-L scale to determine the final yield of isomelezitose, as well as the substrate utilization efficiency and selectivity toward the final product (Table ). Our process reached a final isomelezitose concentration of 159 mM with 73% conversion of the limiting substrate and minimal hydrolysis.

To obtain isomelezitose as a pure product, the residual disaccharides need to be removed from the reaction mixture. We hypothesized that the disaccharides may be enzymatically removed from the mixture by an invertase that hydrolyzes them into their highly soluble building blocks glucose and fructose. However, invertase displayed only limited activity on isomaltulose during downstream processing, necessitating an alternative method to isolate isomelezitose from the residual substrates. This additional purification step was accomplished by preparative liquid chromatography (Supporting Information Figure S5), rendering a highly pure isomelezitose solution, which was concentrated by vacuum evaporation and lyophilized. As such, 68 g of white, powdered isomelezitose was obtained with a purity exceeding 99% (Supporting Information Figure S6).

The isomelezitose sample (92.61%) had significantly lower dry matter than sucrose (99.75%) motivating the application of a predrying step prior to thermal analyses. A melting peak in the thermal profiles indicated that sucrose and dried isomelezitose are in a crystalline state. The peak maximum (200.8 °C) and offset temperature (205.7 °C) of the latter was significantly (p < 0.05) higher than that of sucrose (191.7 and 196.1 °C, respectively) in contrast to the offset temperature, which is likely due to the presence of low levels of impurities introduced during predrying of isomelezitose. These impurities likely impacted the reproducibility of the glass transition temperature range as well. Still, a small but significant shift (p < 0.05) of the glass transition temperature range (65.7–80.5 °C) to higher temperatures was observed for isomelezitose compared to sucrose (57.1–65.0 °C). In fact, isomelezitose was expected to have higher glass transition temperatures because of its higher molecular weight.

The solubility of isomelezitose was about 36% (w/w) at ambient temperature, which is lower than that of sucrose (>65%). Variations in solubility among sugars have been primarily attributed to the predominance of water-sugar interactions over sugar–sugar associations. Hereby, the number and three-dimensional orientation of hydroxyl groups are key. Notably, equatorial hydroxyl groups exhibit a greater capacity for hydration compared to their axial counterparts, due to their more favorable spatial alignment with the tetrahedral structure of water. ,

The relative sweetness of isomelezitose to sucrose was about 27%, which is higher than that of inulin (∼10%), a commonly used sugar alternative in food products.

The 30° Brix solutions of isomelezitose and sucrose were sampled for water activity, viscosity, and tribological measurements. Water activity at ambient temperature and viscosity at both 20 °C and

37 °C were significantly higher (p < 0.05) for the isomelezitose solution, although the increases can be considered as minor (<1%, 7% and 5%, respectively). Regarding the lubricating behavior of the solutions at 37 °C, no significant differences (p > 0.05) in area under the Stribeck curve between the two solutions were observed. This is an important observation as research in oral processing has acknowledged that besides the flow properties of food, mouthfeel is also influenced by frictional interactions within the oral cavity. Despite its substantially lower sweetness compared to sucrose, isomelezitose has a clear potential for use in food, as it has quite similar properties in terms of glass transition temperature, relevant for hard-boiled candy and sugar-snap cookies, crystallinity and flow/tribological properties, which are important for chocolate , among others.

In vitro Digestion with Rat Intestinal Extract

The WHO has previously emphasized the critical need to combat the growing prevalence of noncommunicable diseases such as cancer, cardiovascular diseases, and diabetes. To that end, the WHO advises adults to consume at least 25 g of dietary fiber per day, i.e. carbohydrates with a DP ≥ 3 that are resistant to enzymatic digestion in the human small intestine. ,

The hydrolysis of carbohydrates into their constituent monosaccharides can be examined using rat intestinal colonocyte extracts, which lack pancreatic or other secreted enzymes. This approach is currently the standard method for evaluating the resistance of novel carbohydrates to enzymatic degradation. ,,, Isomelezitose and a few reference carbohydrates were incubated for 3 h with rat intestinal extract, following the protocol described by Hodoniczky et al. The degree of hydrolysis was quantified by measuring the released glucose using the GOD-POD assay after appropriate sample dilution. Complete hydrolysis of maltose and maltotriose was observed within 3 h, aligning with previously reported data (Table ). , In contrast, the disaccharides sucrose and isomaltulose exhibited slower degradation rates, with hydrolysis reaching 65% and 24%, respectively. Remarkably, isomelezitose showed strong resistance to enzymatic breakdown, with less than 1% hydrolysis after 3 h. Additional experimental evidence is needed to verify to what extent isomelezitose can be digested in the human mouth, stomach, and small intestine. However, these preliminary findings suggest that this rare sugar might be considered a dietary fiber, provided that it demonstrates a beneficial physiological effect on human health by fermentation in our large intestine. Interestingly, previous research indicated that this trisaccharide may indeed convey such prebiotic effects, supporting the growth of beneficial bacteria such as Lactobacillus and Enterococcus species. Its presence in bacterial cultures has enhanced the production of butyrate, acetate, and lactate, which are short-chain fatty acids and key metabolites essential for gastrointestinal health. However, additional research involving more complex microbial communities, including commensal species, as well as a broader analysis of metabolite profiles, is needed to convincingly demonstrate that isomelezitose is a prebiotic. Moreover, the cellular impact should be further assessed through incubation with gastrointestinal cell lines, (e.g., Caco-2 cell line).

In conclusion, the discovery of SphSPP facilitated the highly efficient biocatalytic synthesis of the rare trisaccharide isomelezitose with high purity (>99%), opening up a promising route for its cost-effective production on an industrial scale, starting from two bulk sugars. With 27% of the sweetness of sucrose, limited digestibility, and potential prebiotic properties, isomelezitose may serve as a valuable addition to the current portfolio of sugar alternatives. The process described here may be further improved by generating isomaltulose in situ from sucrose, which would reduce or remove the reliance on isomaltulose, which, although an easily accessible bulk substrate, remains considerably more expensive than food-grade sucrose. This approach would further lower substrate costs and enhance the industrial relevance of the process. It would also improve the overall atom efficiency of the process, as the excess sucrose used in the reaction would be largely consumed, thereby minimizing waste. This can be accomplished by adding sucrose isomerase to the process as a second enzyme to catalyze the conversion of sucrose into isomaltulose. A promising candidate would be the highly active and specific sucrose isomerase from Serratia plymuthica engineered by Pilak and colleagues, which is capable of efficiently producing isomaltulose with minimal trehalulose byproduct.
