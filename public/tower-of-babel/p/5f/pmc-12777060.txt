Stress Distribution Pattern of a Calculated Standardized Force on Primary Second Molar Restored with 3D-printed Resin NextDent Crown and Prefabricated Zirconia Crown: A 3D Finite Element Analysis
Authors: Ritesh R Kalaskar, Utkarsha S Kadam, Nidhi Sharma, Snehal D Jagtap

ABSTRACT
AbstractAims and backgroundRestoring grossly destructed primary molars requires esthetic and durable materials that can withstand masticatory forces. Although preformed zirconia crowns are strong and durable, they require extensive tooth preparation and are brittle. The three-dimensional (3D)-printed resin NextDent crowns are nonbrittle and offer better or similar esthetic alternatives, but their ability to withstand intraoral stresses remains untested. Introduction

Dental caries is the most common dental disease affecting children worldwide. Deciduous teeth play a crucial role in preserving the arch length, for mastication, esthetics, and for a smooth transition to permanent dentition.1 It is crucial to maintain the natural teeth in the mouth, particularly when it comes to primary dentition.

While restorative techniques indeed involve removing decayed tooth structure, it's crucial for enduring the increased masticatory demands. Following the removal of the damaged tooth structure, it is reinforced using various materials like amalgam, stainless-steel crowns, resin-modified glass ionomer cement, and resin-filled composites.

Prefabricated zirconia crowns have emerged as a promising alternative for treating deciduous teeth, offering durability, translucency, anatomical conformity, and esthetic appeal. Nonetheless, drawbacks like high cost and potential wear on adjacent teeth need consideration. Hence, continuous efforts are essential to develop esthetic restorative materials for primary teeth capable of addressing the limitations of previous crowns while meeting esthetic demands.2

The advent of computer-aided design/computer-aided manufacturing (CAD/CAM) has propelled the emergence of three-dimensional (3D) dental printing systems as remarkable technologies within the dental field. These digital processes in dental laboratories offer enhanced precision and reproducibility in a quicker, more cost-effective manner compared to traditional methods.3 The ability to customize the size and form of the 3D-printed resin crown for each patient sets it apart from conventional prefabricated crowns made of zirconia or stainless steel. Utilizing 3D printing technology also makes it possible to produce large quantities of crowns in different sizes, which is advantageous when using prefabricated resin crowns.4

Fracture resistance of 3D-printed resin NextDent crown was assessed by using a universal testing apparatus. Fractures were identified both mechanically and acoustically during loading procedures. As bite force exhibited an increasing trend with age, commencing from childhood, within the scope of one study, the mean force values required to induce fracture in a 3D-printed resin crown were >1262.5 N for the resin NextDent variant. This observation indicates that the fracture resistance of resin crowns exceeded the force exerted by natural occlusal loads across all age-groups. Consequently, 3D-printed resin crowns manifested a fracture resistance comparable to that of prefabricated zirconia crowns.2

The development of finite element analysis (FEA) made it possible for the demonstration of the stress propagation across the entire tooth and its restoration.3 Studies have evaluated the fracture resistance of 3D-printed resin NextDent crowns up to this point, but none have used 3D FEA to investigate the stress distribution patterns of masticatory forces over these crowns and prefabricated zirconia crowns, including their occlusal stress on dentin, periodontal ligament, cancellous, and cortical bone. Furthermore, no study has been done to assess the fracture and deformation tendencies of 3D-printed resin NextDent when restored on the primary second molars.

Methodology

This in vitro study evaluated the stress distribution patterns in two different restorative crown materials for primary mandibular second molars. FEA models were developed based on pulpotomy- or pulpectomy-treated deciduous second molars with comparable remaining tooth structures. The experimental groups included:

Group I: Primary mandibular second molar restored with a 3D-printed NextDent C&B MFH resin crown.

Group I: Primary mandibular second molar restored with a 3D-printed NextDent C&B MFH resin crown.

Group II: Primary mandibular second molar restored with a prefabricated zirconia crown.

Group II: Primary mandibular second molar restored with a prefabricated zirconia crown.

Materials and Methods

To construct the FEA models, a cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) scan was obtained from a 9-year-old patient who had previously undergone endodontic treatment (pulpotomy or pulpectomy) on a primary mandibular second molar. The CBCT data were acquired in Digital Imaging and Communication in Medicine (DICOM) format. Using the Materialise Interactive Medical Image Control System (MIMICS), a 3D model of the dentoalveolar structure was reconstructed. Mesh generation was performed in Hypermesh, and FEA was conducted using ANSYS software, version 11.0 (Swanson Analysis Systems, Houston, PA) (Fig. 1).

Hypermesh indicating nodes and elements

Model Preparation

A virtual geometric model (VGM) of the mandibular jaw, incorporating the pulpotomy- or pulpectomy-treated deciduous second molar, was created from the CBCT scan. The 3D models included essential anatomical features, such as enamel, dentin, and the pulp chamber. A periodontal ligament of 0.25 mm thickness surrounded the tooth, and the surrounding bone structure consisted of cortical bone (2 mm thick) encasing a spongy bone core (14 mm diameter and 22 mm height for spongy bone, 16 mm diameter and 24 mm height for cortical bone).5

The pulpectomy-treated tooth was obturated using zinc oxide eugenol, and resin-modified glass ionomer cement (GC Fuji Type II) was used as the restorative material. To simulate a significantly damaged tooth, 30% of the dentin was retained after crown preparation, with crowns cemented using resin-modified glass ionomer luting cement (GC Fuji Type I).

Finite Element Mesh Generation

Mesh generation was performed in ANSYS, where the geometric models were converted into a finite element mesh, consisting of nodes and elements forming the grid structure for FEA. Material properties, including Poisson's ratio and modulus of elasticity, were assigned to each component, assuming that materials were homogeneous, isotropic, and linearly elastic (Table 1).

Boundary Conditions

To simulate masticatory forces, a vertical occlusal load of 500 N was applied along the long axis of the tooth at 0°. The load was distributed across three points—two on the inner inclines of the lingual cusps and one on the outer inclines of the buccal cusps. Stress distribution and magnitude were evaluated using the von Mises criterion to determine stress patterns within the tooth structure under load.

Results

The stresses were visualized in color coding ranging from dark blue (minimum stress) to red (maximum stress) (Fig. 2).

(A) Stress distribution of 3D-printed resin NextDent crown on surrounding tooth structure; (B) Stress distribution of zirconia crown on surrounding tooth structure

Stress areas are the cuspal inclines in contact with the adjacent tooth, and maximal stresses are restricted to the crown with very little penetrating to the underlying tooth. There is an overall increase in stress with decreasing tooth structure, though it remains well below the ultimate tensile strength of each material. The stresses dissipate gradually from the point of loading to the rest of the crown.5

The von Mises stress analysis shows that the 3D-printed crown reaches a maximum stress of 383.2 MPa, exceeding the 351.12 MPa observed in the zirconia crown. The underlying dentin experiences a stress level of 57.67 MPa with the resin crown, significantly greater than the 19.195 MPa recorded for the zirconia crown (Fig. 3 and Tables 2 and 3).

The stress analysis reveals that the periodontal ligament exhibits comparable stress levels of 9.49 MPa for the 3D-printed resin crown and 9.38 MPa for the prefabricated zirconia crown. Similarly, the cortical and cancellous bone show nearly identical stress values of 23.6 and 8.73 MPa, respectively, for both the 3D-printed resin and zirconia crowns (Fig. 2 and Table 2).

Under identical loading conditions, the 3D-printed resin NextDent crown exhibited a deformation of 0.296 mm, while its underlying dentin displayed a deformation of 0.0305 mm. In contrast, the zirconia crown displayed a deformation of 0.0311 mm, with its underlying dentin demonstrating a deformation of 0.0265 mm (Fig. 3).

Notably, the periodontal ligament, cortical bone, and cancellous bone showed consistent deformation values across both the 3D-printed resin NextDent and zirconia crowns, measuring 0.0266, 0.0169, and 0.0180 mm, respectively (Fig. 3 and Tables 2 and 3).

Discussion

Clinical studies and reviews have established the efficacy of stainless steel crowns (SSCs) as reliable semipermanent restorations for primary teeth affected by caries, hypoplasia, or postpulp therapy, and as anchors in orthodontic appliances.6–12 However, growing demand for esthetics in pediatric dentistry has led to alternatives like prefabricated zirconia crowns, which offer superior appearance and durability. Brands such as EZ-Pedo and NuSmile highlight their biocompatibility, low thermal conductivity, and strength. Yet, their drawbacks include extensive tooth preparation, brittleness, potential wear on opposing teeth, and marginal adaptability, prompting the need for more advanced materials.13

The rise of digital dentistry and 3D printing technology has introduced customized crowns that ensure precise fit and ideal contacts while allowing multiple restorations to be fabricated simultaneously. Initially used for interim prostheses, 3D printing resins are now being explored for pediatric applications, offering minimal tooth preparation and enhanced adaptability.14 In this study, comparing stress distribution patterns in 3D-printed resin crowns and prefabricated zirconia crowns using FEA provides valuable insights into their biomechanical performance, guiding advancements in pediatric restorative techniques.

The finite element method (FEM) is a powerful computational tool widely used in dentistry to evaluate stress and strain distribution in restored primary teeth. By creating a digital model of the tooth and crown, FEM allows simulation of chewing forces through the application of loads and constraints, providing detailed insights into stress behavior within complex dental structures.15 Originally, introduced by Courant in 1943, FEM was applied to dentistry by Farah et al. in 1973, enabling comprehensive assessments of stress distribution in dental models. This technique has since become instrumental in analyzing the biomechanical performance of restorative materials.16 Chiang et al. highlighted the utility of FEM in comparing the responses of different crown materials to functional forces, facilitating the development of optimized restorative solutions.18 This study aims to leverage FEM to evaluate the stress distribution and deformation of 3D-printed NextDent crowns and zirconia crowns, contributing to evidence-based recommendations for their clinical application.

This study employed von Mises stress analysis, a widely accepted method for identifying areas prone to structural degradation. It provides a comprehensive measure of stress distribution and is particularly useful for evaluating the risk of failure in brittle materials. Under vertical loading conditions, von Mises stress values were calculated to assess the mechanical behavior of the restorative crowns and underlying tooth structures. The study highlights that maximal stresses are localized within the crowns, with minimal stress propagation to the underlying dentin and bone structures. This is consistent with previous research by Reddy et al., 2020, indicating that the material properties and crown design significantly influence stress concentration within the restoration.19 The higher stress values observed in the 3D-printed resin NextDent crowns compared to zirconia crowns align with their slightly lower modulus of elasticity, leading to increased flexibility and stress absorption.20

Stress levels in the dentin, periodontal ligament, cortical bone, and cancellous bone for both crown types were well below the ultimate tensile strength of these materials. This finding corresponds with research by Magne and Belser, which demonstrated that restorative materials effectively dissipate forces to the surrounding structures when appropriately bonded.5 The resin crown exhibited significantly higher deformation (0.296 mm) compared to the zirconia crown (0.0311 mm). The underlying dentin exhibited greater deformation under resin crowns. The increased deformation of the resin crown suggests reduced tooth fracture, as the crown absorbs the stress and deforms under the same load. Similarly, Prabhakar et al. reported that preformed zirconia crowns experienced lower maximal von Mises stresses, including in the underlying dentin. This indicates that preformed zirconia crowns provide superior protection to primary teeth.5 According to Alamoudi et al., a 3-year interventional study (2014–2017) examined 232 primary teeth that underwent pulpectomy. The study evaluated the clinical performance of NuSmile primary zirconia crowns, including 172 primary incisors and 60 primary molars, over a 2-year follow-up period. The findings highlighted that these crowns effectively maintain gingival health, demonstrate high long-term survival rates, and exhibit good retention and marginal integrity, thereby reducing the risk of secondary caries.21

This study contributes to a better understanding of the biomechanical performance of these materials, providing valuable insights into fracture resistance and material performance for clinical decision-making in pediatric dentistry. However, this is an in vitro study and may not fully replicate clinical conditions or long-term oral environment factors like moisture, temperature changes, or bacterial interactions. Future research exploring the long-term clinical performance and cost-effectiveness of these crowns can further refine their applications in restorative dentistry.

Clinical Significance
