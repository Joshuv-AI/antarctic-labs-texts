Association of Device‐Measured Habitual Sedentary Activity and Standing Posture With Blood Pressure and Hypertension in Danish Adults: The Lolland‐Falster Health Study
Authors: Sofie Rath Mortensen, Jan Christian Brønd, Therese Lockenwitz Petersen, Randi Jepsen, Fernando Domínguez‐Navarro, Søren T. Skou, Anders Grøntved
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLimited evidence exists on the link between sedentary activity and blood pressure (BP), especially using combined thigh‐ and back‐worn accelerometry, which offers accurate posture classification. This study examined the association between device‐measured sedentary activity patterns and BP levels and hypertension and explored whether obesity explained these associations.MethodsThis cross‐sectional study included 3127 adults (≥18 years) from the Danish Lolland‐Falster Health Study (2017–2020). The outcome was participants' systolic BP and diastolic BP in mm Hg measured by a health care professional. Exposure was time spent on sedentary activity (duration, postures, prolonged bouts and breaks in sedentary activity) during the day assessed with thigh‐worn and back‐worn accelerometers. Multiple regression models were used to investigate if sedentary activity was associated with BP levels.ResultsParticipants with hypertension, standardized for age and sex, were more sedentary (9.4 hours/day), had fewer sedentary breaks (54 breaks/day), and more prolonged sedentary bouts (5 bouts/day) compared with those without hypertension. Daily number of sedentary breaks between ≥50 to <65 and ≥65 was associated with lower systolic BP (−1.8 mm Hg [95% CI, −3.3 to −0.3] and −2.3 mm Hg, [95% CI, −3.8 to −0.7]) when compared with participants with daily breaks <50. Higher total daily sedentary time was associated with higher diastolic BP (0.3 mm Hg per hours/day [95% CI, 0.05–0.5]).ConclusionsHigher total sedentary time and prolonged sedentary bouts were associated with higher diastolic BP, independent of lifestyle factors. Conversely, a higher number of sedentary breaks was associated with lower systolic BP, diastolic BP, and lower odds of hypertension.RegistrationURL: https://www.clinicaltrials.gov; Unique identifier: NCT02482896.

Hypertension, characterized by elevated blood pressure (BP) levels, is the second largest risk factor contributor to the global burden of disease 1 and is estimated to affect 33% of adults between 30 and 79 years worldwide. 2 Regular physical activity (PA) is recommended to reduce BP in people with hypertension or prehypertension as well as to prevent the onset of hypertension and the development of hypertension‐related complications. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 PA is increasingly recognized as a target to improve health in the population, 7 whereas evidence supporting recommendations on reductions in time spent sedentary and increasing time spent standing are limited. 8 , 9 , 10 This reflects the lack of evidence for the association of sedentary activity patterns, sitting, standing or prolonged bouts and breaking up sedentary activity with BP levels. 10

A recent study showed benefits of a 6‐month sitting reduction intervention on resting BP in older adults. 11 Additionally, regular interruptions of light breaks in prolonged sitting in adults with overweight and obesity demonstrated reduced resting BP over a few hours. 12 Although recent standing and activity have been shown to directly influence ambulatory BP, 13 the broader implications of cumulative standing duration and resting BP remains less well understood. 14

Managing hypertension poses challenges where traditional approaches centered on PA may be less feasible, for example, in people with obesity and high levels of sedentary time. 15 , 16 Thus, targeting reductions in sedentary time and promoting more frequent standing may offer a feasible alternative to reduce elevated BP. However, to advance our understanding and refine recommendations in this area, population‐based analyses with accurate and detailed assessment of habitual sedentary patterns are needed. Such studies hold the potential to furnish timely and comprehensive insights into sedentary patterns among people with hypertension and are important to prevent a continuously rising public health issue.

The aims of this study were to examine the association of sedentary activity, measured by thigh‐ and back‐worn accelerometry, patterns in terms of duration, intensities, postures, and prolonged bouts and breaks in sedentary activity during the day, with BP levels and hypertension in a population‐based sample of Danish adults. Further, we examined whether habitual standing posture was associated with BP and hypertension and to what extent the associations were explained by obesity.

METHODS

Data Availability

All data used in this study were derived from the LOFUS (Lolland‐Falster Health Study) study. Use of LOFUS data requires approval from LOFUS and must comply with Danish legislation.

Setting and Data Sources

The data underlying this article cannot be shared publicly, in compliance with the General Data Protection Regulation. This cross‐sectional study used from the Danish household‐based population study the LOFUS that was initiated to establish baseline information on health status among inhabitants in a mixed‐rural provincial area of Denmark from February 2016 to February 2020. Inhabitants ≥18 years were randomly invited to participate in the data collection that encompassed questionnaires, a site visit with physical examinations and biological samples. Detailed information about the study design and methodology is available elsewhere. 17 , 18 A subsample of participants were invited to wear accelerometers in continuation of the physical examinations. 19 Between February 2017 and November 2018, inclusion criteria for participation in device‐measured PA were at least 1 adult and 1 child from the same household agreed to accelerometer assessment. From December 2018 to February 2020, all LOFUS participants were eligible for inclusion. 20

The LOFUS study was approved by the Region Zealand's Ethical Committee on Health Research (SJ‐421) and the Danish Protection Agency (p‐2024‐16 360). Further, LOFUS is registered in clinicaltrials.gov (NCT02482896). Data storage and management for this study were approved by the Danish Data Protection Agency through the University of Southern Denmark (Journal nr.: 11.396). Written informed consent from participants was obtained at the site visit. 17 The study was reported according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.

Study Population

Among adults (>18 years) participating in the LOFUS study, 7208 were eligible to wear accelerometers. The present study included LOFUS participants with valid accelerometer data and information on hypertension status and body mass index (BMI).

Outcome

Participants' systolic BP (SBP) and diastolic BP (DBP) were measured 3 times at the health examinations by a health care professional and were used to calculate participants' mean SBP and DBP. Participants were instructed to lie still in the supine position and avoid talking before the BP measurement. Self‐reported use of BP medication was obtained from the questionnaire.

Participants' hypertension status was based on their mean BP and classified according to the American Heart Association and American College Cardiology. 21 Participants were defined as hypertensive if their measured SBP ≥130 mm Hg or DBP ≥80 mm Hg. Additionally, if participants reported use of BP‐lowering medication and their SBP was <130 mm Hg and DBP <80 mm Hg, they were also defined as ‘hypertensive.’ Being nonhypertensive was defined as SBP <130 mm Hg and DBP <80 mm Hg and no self‐reported use of BP‐lowering medication. This classification method aligns with established guidelines and ensures accurate identification of hypertensive individuals, including those effectively managing their BP with medication. Continuous SBP and DBP and the prevalence of hypertension were used as outcomes in the present study.

Exposures

PA and sedentary activity were assessed with 2 Axivity AX3 (Axivity, Newcastle, UK) accelerometers that were attached to the skin using adhesive plaster. One was placed on the right side of the lower back, and the other on the front of the right thigh. Participants were instructed to wear the accelerometers for 7 consecutive days at all times (24 hours/day), including during sleep and water activities. If the accelerometers fell off, participants were instructed to reapply with supplemental adhesive tape. 19 Raw valid wear periods were identified by evaluating acceleration and temperature data in combination as previously described. 20 , 22 Criteria for a valid day was minimum 22 hours of wear time per day. Participants providing at least 3 valid weekdays and 1 valid weekend day were included. Time spent in PA intensities was determined by generating ActiGraph counts using 10‐second epochs from the raw acceleration measured at the back. 23 Classification of the postures sitting and standing were determined using the algorithm described by Skotte et al, 24 which is based on a simple decision tree in combination with 6 different signal features generated from the thigh and the back raw triaxial acceleration data. The algorithms for these postures and activity types have a very high sensitivity and specificity in both adults and children. 24 , 25

Descriptive Variables and Covariates

Information about participants' age and sex was derived from the Civil Registration System and other background information came from the LOFUS questionnaire. 26

Civil status was dichotomized into “married or living with a partner” or “widow/separated/divorced/unmarried.” Highest level of education was collapsed into 4 categories: (1) primary or lower secondary education (≤10 years), (2) upper secondary or vocational education (10+ 2–3 years), (3) higher education (>13 years), and (4) other. Participants reported their occupational status, which was categorized into (1) employed (responses: employed, student, other), (2) unemployed (responses: unemployed, absent due to sick leave, early retirement), and (3) retired (voluntarily retirement: special pension or old‐age pension). Participants' height and weight were measured at the physical examinations and used to calculate their BMI (kg/m2). For descriptive presentation, BMI was categorized into the following: normal weight (BMI < 25), overweight (BMI≥25 and <30), and obesity (BMI≥30), and for the analyses, the variable was categorized into nonobese (BMI<30) and obese (BMI≥30) as defined by the World Health Organization. 27 Cohen's 10‐item Perceived Stress Scale was used obtain participants' self‐reported psychological stress, which was categorized into low perceived stress (scores <18) and high perceived stress (scores ≥18). 28 The World Health Organization‐5 Well‐Being Index was used to obtain participants' self‐reported mental well‐being. Scores <50 were categorized as low mental well‐being. 29 Self‐reported long‐lasting chronic pain was reported with yes/no, and self‐reported information about cardiovascular diseases and diabetes among participants was included in this study. Smoking status was categorized into 3 groups: (1) current, (2) former, or (3) never. Information about participants' self‐reported binge drinking was obtained and categorized into (1) never, (2) rarely, (3) monthly, or (4) weekly, almost daily, or daily. Self‐estimated diet habits were categorized as follows: (1) healthy, (2) somewhat healthy, and (3) unhealthy.

Statistical Analysis

Primary analyses of the present study were preplanned, and the primary goals of the analyses were explorative on the associations between sedentary activity and BP.

Characteristics and distribution of sedentary activity variables across categories of hypertension were presented as numbers and proportions for categorical variables and means±SD or medians and interquartile range (25th and 75th percentile) for continuous variables depending on the distribution. We used the “dstat” function in STATA to describe the distributions across hypertension with standardization of age and sex to equalize differences across the groups. 30

Multiple linear regression models were used to investigate the association between total sedentary activity, categories of sedentary bouts and prolonged sedentary bouts, and time standing with levels of SBP and DBP. Multiple logistic regression models were performed to investigate the odds ratios (ORs) of having hypertension with the same exposures as discussed previously. When investigating these associations, 4 models of each exposure were performed. Model 1 was adjusted for age and sex. Model 2 was additionally adjusted for diet, smoking, and alcohol intake as these variables are believed to influence BP and exposures. Model 3 was additionally adjusted for MVPA, and Model 4 included adjustment for the obesity prevalence to assess the extent to which the associations may be influenced by obesity, considering that obesity could act as a confounder or mediator.

We also investigated the extent to which the associations of total sedentary time and time spent standing with levels of SBP and DBP were dependent on levels of MVPA. This was done by including an interaction term in the model. Specifically, the interaction term was between tertiles of MVPA, standardized for age and sex, and total sedentary time or time spent standing, with the main effects included in the model. Similarly, an interaction term was added to the model to investigate to what extent the associations were dependent on age (<65 years versus ≥65 years) and sex.

Overall, 35.5% of the total number of eligible participants had missing data on either exposures, outcomes, or covariates (see flow chart in Figure S1). To prevent selection bias due to missing values in specific variables, we used a multivariate chained equation imputation approach including all covariates and respective outcomes to impute missing values. 31 Regression coefficients and 95% CIs were obtained using Rubin's rules 32 based on 50 imputed data sets including 3128 study participants; analyses of participants with complete data (n=2048) are represented in supplements.

All regression models were estimated using ordinary least squares. Residuals were assessed and found to be approximately normally distributed, and heteroskedasticity‐robust SEs were used to account for potential heteroskedasticity.

All statistical analyses were performed using the software STATA BE 17.0 and R statistical (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria) software version 4.3.1 (2023‐06‐16), RStudio (RStudio Inc., Boston, MA, USA) version 2023.06.0.+421.

RESULTS

Out of 3127 LOFUS participants with data on hypertension, 1182 (38%) did not have hypertension and 1945 (62%) had hypertension. Median (interquartile range) age among participants with hypertension was 65.6 (50.5–71.6) and 43.8 (36.1–55.3) among participants without hypertension. When standardized on age and sex, a larger proportion of individuals with hypertension were more highly educated, were more binge drinkers, and had more unhealthy diet habits compared with individuals without hypertension (Table 1).

Participants with hypertension were found to have higher total daily sedentary time (9.4 hours/day), fewer sedentary breaks (54 breaks/day), and more prolonged sedentary bouts (5 bouts/day) when compared with participants without hypertension (Table S1), Total sedentary time was 9.1 hours/day; sedentary breaks totaled 58 breaks/day; prolonged boutswere 4 bouts/day with standardization on age and sex.

When compared with participants with <50 daily sedentary breaks, those with 50 to <65 breaks (−1.8 mm Hg [95% CI, −3.3 to −0.3]) or ≥65 breaks (−2.3 mm Hg [95% CI, −3.8 to −0.7]) were associated with lower SBP in the fully adjusted model (model 4). This association was also observed between those with ≥65 breaks and DPB when compared with those with <50 breaks. Higher total daily sedentary time was associated with higher DBP (0.3 mm Hg per hour/day [95% CI, 0.05–0.5]). Additionaly, for every additional sedentary break per hour sedentary time, there was an associated lower SBP of −0.3 mm Hg and DBP of −0.2 mm Hg in the fully adjusted model. Prolonged sedentary bouts of ≥4 to <6 and ≥6 were associated with higher DBP when compared with those with <50 breaks, after adjusting for age, sex, diet, alcohol, smoking, and MVPA. Similarly, an association was found between time spent standing and higher DBP. However, when obesity was included in the model, only the association between prolonged sedentary bouts of ≥4 to <6 and DBP remained significant. No association between time spent standing and BP was observed in the fully adjusted model (Table 2).

DISCUSSION

We found that participants with hypertension were more sedentary during the day, had fewer daily breaks in sedentary time, and had more prolonged sedentary bouts when compared with age‐ and sex‐matched participants without hypertension. Independent of lifestyle factors including time spent on MVPA, higher total daily sedentary time, prolonged sedentary bouts, and less time spent standing were associated with higher DBP. Conversely, a higher number of breaks in sedentary activity throughout the day, as well as more breaks per hour of sedentary time, were associated with lower SBP, DBP, and prevalence of hypertension. These associations were generally attenuated when accounting for obesity, except for the frequency of breaks in sedentary time and breaks per sedentary hour, where more breaks were consistently associated with lower SBP, lower DBP, and a lower prevalence of hypertension.

In our study, we found that people with hypertension spent an average of 9.4 hours daily on sedentary activity. This is somewhat similar to the results from a Brazilian study by Gerage et al. 33 from 2015, in which they found that people with hypertension had ≈8.9 hours of sedentary time daily (cutpoint based) measured with a belt‐worn accelerometer on the hip. 33 Importantly, our study sample was considerably larger and population‐based (1945 participants with hypertension) as compared with 87 participants with hypertension in the study by Gerage et al 33 A Danish study by Domazet et al. 34 investigated PA behaviors using accelerometers in individuals with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes. As hypertension and type 2 diabetes share many similarities in terms of risk factors and complications, and they often coexist in the same individuals, 35 these populations may be somewhat comparable. Moreover, Domazet et al. revealed that a population of 768 individuals spent ≈10.8 hours on sedentary time daily. The difference in these numbers may be explained by several factors. First, the participants of Domazet et al. 34 were newly diagnosed with diabetes, were less educated, and had higher BMI compared with our study population, which may have influenced the participants' levels of sedentary time. 36 Also, in our study, sedentary time was defined by time spent in different sedentary positions, 24 while Domazet et al. and Gerage et al. defined sedentary time based on intensity (activity counts <100 per minute), 33 , 34 and it is therefore possible that the thresholds identify other behaviors that are not sedentary, such as standing stationary, leading to an overestimation of sedentary time. 37

In our primary analyses, we found that per 1 hour increase in total sedentary activity daily, DBP increased by 0.3 mm Hg in the fully adjusted model, whereas no association was found with SBP (P value=0.675). A systematic review and meta‐analysis by Lee and Wong 38 from 2015 found that every additional hour of sedentary behavior per day was associated with an increase of 0.06 mm Hg for SBP and 0.2 mm Hg for DBP in both adults and children. Additionally, they found that for every additional hour of sedentary behavior per day, there was a 2% increase in hypertension risk (OR, 1.02 [95% CI, 1.003–1.03]). Interestingly, these associations were significant when studies with either self‐reported and device‐measured sedentary behavior were included in the analyses, but when including only studies with device‐measured sedentary behavior, the authors did not find an association with SBP or DBP. 38 As self‐reported sedentary behavior, particularly single‐item questionnaires, tends to be underestimated compared with device measured, this suggests that the findings from Lee and Wong do not confirm ours. 37 , 38

When investigating the association between time spent standing and BP, we found that each additional hour spent standing was associated with a −0.3 mm Hg lower DBP, after adjusting for age, sex, and lifestyle factors. Although standing may offer potential benefits for BP reduction, prolonged standing has shown some adverse effects that are relevant to consider. A review by Gibbs et al 14 from 2021 found that higher all‐day standing was associated with reduced mortality, whereas higher occupational standing had adverse or no associations with subclinical or incident of cardiovascular disease. The duration and frequency of time spent standing seem to be important in relation to BP, likewise with time spent sitting. Also, the benefits of standing may be amplified when it is used to break up sedentary behavior. Therefore, it may be relevant for future studies to include the duration of time spent standing when breaking up prolonged sedentary behavior.

In this study, we examined the extent to which the associations of sedentary activity and standing with BP were influenced by obesity by including obesity as a covariate in our models. Results indicated that the associations between prolonged bouts (≥6 bouts) and DBP, as well as time spent standing and DBP, were no longer significant after adjustment for obesity. This suggests that obesity may account for a substantial portion of these associations. On the other hand, the associations of total sedentary time, number of sedentary breaks, and breaks per hour of sedentary activity with BP remained significant after adjusting for obesity.

In a previous study conducted by the authors using data from the same population, we investigated the extent to which BMI alongside other factors explained differences in PA levels across diabetes status. 39 The study found that differences in PA between those with prediabetes and those without diabetes appeared to be influenced by differences in BMI in the 2 groups. 39 Although that study is not directly comparable to the present study, it suggests that BMI may play a significant role in the associations between both physical and sedentary activity with conditions such as hypertension and diabetes, in which obesity is highly prevalent and related to these conditions. 40 , 41

Clinical Implications

A reduction of ≥5 to 10 mm Hg SBP or ≥3 to 5 mm Hg DBP is proposed to be a clinically relevant difference in BP in trials aiming to treat hypertension. 42 Based on our results from a population‐based sample of individuals with and without hypertension, reduction in sedentary activity does not seem to be an intervention that can stand alone when treating hypertension. Although we acknowledge the cross‐sectional nature of our study, our findings indicate that an increase of approximately 2.5 hours per day in total sedentary time may be associated with a 1 mm Hg higher DBP, whereas an increase of 3 breaks per hour of sedentary time could potentially be associated with about a 1 mm Hg lower DBP. Although these changes in BP might seem modest, population‐wide shifts in the distribution of BP of this magnitude could have a relevant impact on the incidence of hypertension‐related diseases, such as cardiovascular disease, 43 emphasizing the importance of reducing sedentary behavior and increasing breaks in sedentary time in daily routines. Nevertheless, the evidence supporting an association between sedentary activity and BP remains inconclusive. A Finish study by Norha et al 44 from 2024 investigated the effects of reducing sedentary time on resting, exercise, and postexercise BP in 64 inactive adults with a mean resting SBP/DBP of 143/88 mm Hg in a 6‐month randomized controlled trial. The study aimed to reduce sedentary behavior by 1 hour per day; however, the actual mean reduction in sedentary time from baseline to follow‐up in the intervention group was 40 minutes per day. 45 Despite this reduction, the study found no significant impact on BP at rest, during submaximal or maximal exercise, or during recovery from maximal exercise. Additionally, the intervention led to significant increases in MVPA and step count, suggesting that reductions in sedentary behavior were accompanied by increases in other forms of PA. However, these findings may not be directly applicable to observational studies like ours, where isolating the effects of reduced sedentary time proves challenging.

The World Health Organization continues to recommend that adults, including those with chronic conditions such as hypertension, limit time spent sedentary and replace their sedentary time with PA of any intensity. 8 Our study supports maintaining a focus on reducing sedentary time and aligns with the concept “move more, sit less, more often,” which has been proposed as a simple and relevant approach to prevent hypertension and improve BP in people with hypertension. 46 , 47 This approach should be viewed as a supplement in the context of other health behaviors, including, diet, sleep habits, and reducing stress levels. 46

Strengths and Limitations

In this study, participants wore 2 accelerometers (one on the lower back and the other on the right thigh) to assess sedentary activity under free‐living conditions, which is considered to be a strength. 24 The thigh‐worn accelerometer was used to estimate total sedentary time based on nonupright postures, although it did not differentiate between sitting, reclined, or lying position. The back‐worn accelerometer was used for classifying light, moderate, and vigorous physical activity. Another strength of this study is that BP was measured by health care professionals in a controlled standardized setup, and data from use of hypertensive medication were available for the study.

For this study, we chose a fixed time window of waking hours (from 7:00 am to 10:00 pm). Also, participants should have at least 10 hours of awake time on each measurement day to be included in the study. This inclusion criteria and the fixed time window could be both a strength and a limitation of the study. The fixed time window was chosen to avoid including time spent sleeping; however, this may limit the study's ability to capture all sedentary activity during a day. The 10 hours of awake time was chosen to ensure that participants had sufficient data on their daily sedentary behaviors, yet we are aware that people usually have more than 10 hours of awake time daily.

Although this study has some major strengths, there are also some limitations to be discussed. First, participating in both the LOFUS study and the accelerometer measurements were voluntary. Further, the participation rate in the LOFUS study was highest among those who were middle aged, female, Danish citizens, and with high socioeconomic status. 18 This may have introduced selection bias in our study, and the results observed may not be representative of other populations.

A limitation of the accelerometer measurements is that they may misclassify seated activities with higher energy expenditure, such as resistance training, as sedentary time, which may have introduced misclassification of actual sedentary behavior. Furthermore, it is important to remember that 1 week of measurement with a median of 6 valid days may not fully reflect an individual's general sedentary activity behaviors. Although multiple imputation was used to address missing data, potential bias may still exist if data were not missing at random. Nevertheless, sensitivity analyses conducted using complete case data produced consistent results, providing some reassurance regarding the robustness of our findings.

Although we have information about self‐reported diet habits among participants, the variable has its limitations as it does not capture diet habits that are particularly relevant in studies of hypertension, such as intake of salt and processed food. 48

Additionally, although we adjusted for obesity status (BMI ≥30 versus <30 kg/m2), BMI is an indirect measure of adiposity and does not differentiate between fat mass and fat‐free mass. Thus, the observed attenuation of associations after adjusting for obesity status may, at least in part, reflect differences in fat‐free mass rather than adiposity alone. 49 , 50

Lastly, the cross‐sectional study design limits our ability to determine the directionality or underlying nature of the associations observed between sedentary activity and hypertension.

CONCLUSIONS

Our study reveals that people with hypertension tend to be more sedentary during the day, take fewer breaks from sedentary behavior, and engage in more prolonged sedentary bouts compared with those without hypertension. We observed that greater overall daily sedentary time, prolonged sedentary bouts, and less time spent standing were associated with higher DBP independent of lifestyle factors, whereas taking more frequent breaks during sedentary periods, both in absolute numbers and relative to the time spent sedentary, was associated with lower SBP, DBP, and reduced prevalence of hypertension. These associations were generally attenuated when accounting for obesity status.

PERSPECTIVES

Our findings imply that targeting reductions in sedentary time, including total sedentary time and breaking up sedentary time, may be a relevant approach to improve BP in the adult population. However, based on the results from these cross‐sectional data, targeting reductions in sedentary time may not be able to stand alone to clinically affect BP, and promoting PA should therefore still be considered as an important part of prevention and treatment. Future studies should consider longitudinal designs to clarify the nature of the association in the adult population, while also exploring how replacing total sedentary time or taking more frequent standing breaks in sedentary time affects patterns of physical activity and their impact on BP.
