Mitochondrial ribosomal protein MRPL27 supports glioma malignancy through regulation of oxidative phosphorylation
Authors: Jingyao Gu, Boya Zhou, Lin Ji, Haoyang Yuan, Haibo Ji, Mingxiao Zhu, Chunyan He, Xueping Gu, Suji Yan, Yin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlioma is a highly invasive primary brain tumor with a poor prognosis and currently lacks effective treatment methods. Increasing evidence indicates that mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) is crucial for the development of glioma; however, the regulatory mechanisms controlling mitochondrial protein synthesis and energy metabolism are not yet fully understood. Analysis of the TCGA and CGGA databases reveals that MRPL27 is highly expressed in glioma tissues and is significantly associated with poor patient survival. Silencing MRPL27 significantly inhibits the proliferation, migration, and tumor growth of glioma cells, while inducing cell apoptosis. Mechanistically, the absence of MRPL27 impairs the mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation process, reduces ATP production, disrupts redox balance, and increases oxidative stress. Notably, MRPL27 deficiency specifically reduces the protein content of mitochondrial-encoded oxidative phosphorylation components without altering their transcriptional levels, indicating its role in post-transcriptional regulation of mitochondrial protein expression. In summary, these findings suggest that MRPL27 is a key regulator of mitochondrial translation and energy metabolism in glioma, and emphasize the significance of mitochondrial ribosome regulation as a potential metabolic weakness for therapeutic intervention.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-026-06236-8.Keywords: Glioma progression, MRPL27, Oxidative phosphorylation, Mitochondrial translation

Introduction

Glioma is the most common primary malignant tumor of the central nervous system, with high-grade gliomas—particularly glioblastoma (GBM)—exhibiting marked invasiveness, extensive heterogeneity, and extremely poor prognosis [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) classification, gliomas are categorized into grades I–IV, of which grade IV GBM represents the most aggressive subtype and accounts for approximately 70% of high-grade gliomas [2–4]. Despite advances in surgical resection, radiotherapy, and temozolomide-based chemotherapy, patient survival remains limited, and tumor recurrence and therapeutic resistance are largely unavoidable.

The advent of molecular classification has shifted glioma research toward precision medicine based on key genetic alterations, including IDH mutations, EGFR aberrations, PTEN loss, and dysregulated PI3K/AKT/mTOR and MAPK signaling [5, 6]. However, in GBM, therapies targeting single oncogenic drivers have achieved only modest and transient benefits, largely due to pronounced intratumoral heterogeneity, blood–brain barrier–mediated drug delivery limitations, and rapid adaptive resistance under therapeutic pressure [7–10]. These challenges underscore the need to identify more fundamental biological dependencies that sustain glioma malignancy.

In this context, tumor metabolic reprogramming—particularly mitochondrial dysfunction—has emerged as a central driver of glioma progression and therapeutic resistance [11]. Beyond energy production, mitochondria regulate redox homeostasis, biosynthetic metabolism, cell death signaling, and stress adaptation [12]. Increasing evidence indicates that glioma cells dynamically modulate oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and mitochondrial respiratory activity, rather than relying solely on glycolysis, to support rapid proliferation and therapeutic stress. Seminal studies by Peter Dirks and colleagues demonstrated that glioblastoma stem-like cells preferentially depend on mitochondrial OXPHOS, which is tightly linked to tumor initiation and radioresistance [13]. Subsequent work from Jeremy Rich’s laboratory further showed that enhanced mitochondrial metabolism sustains glioma stemness and confers survival advantages under therapeutic pressure [14]. Consistently, Craig Thompson and colleagues highlighted the essential role of mitochondrial oxidative metabolism in supporting tumor biosynthesis and redox balance during cancer metabolic reprogramming [15].

Consistent with these observations, genes involved in mitochondrial translation, respiratory chain assembly, metabolite transport, and mitochondrial quality control have increasingly been recognized as key regulators of glioma progression, metabolic adaptation, and patient prognosis. Positioned at the intersection of oncogenic signaling and metabolic plasticity, dysregulation of these mitochondrial-associated pathways may confer energetic and redox advantages to tumor cells while critically shaping their sensitivity to radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and targeted therapies.

Among these mechanisms, nuclear-encoded mitochondrial ribosomal proteins have gained increasing attention. The mitochondrial ribosome is essential for the synthesis of mitochondrially encoded proteins, respiratory chain assembly, and oxidative phosphorylation efficiency, and its disruption directly impairs cellular energy metabolism and redox homeostasis [16]. Emerging evidence indicates that aberrant activation of mitochondrial translation enhances tumor cell adaptation to high energetic demands and therapeutic stress and is associated with poor clinical outcomes. Studies from Aaron Schimmer’s group demonstrated that tumor cells are highly dependent on mitochondrial protein translation, and that its pharmacological inhibition suppresses oxidative phosphorylation and induces tumor cell death, identifying mitochondrial translation as a critical metabolic vulnerability [17]. In glioblastoma stem-like cell models, Sighel et al. further showed that inhibition of mitochondrial translation using agents such as quinupristin/dalfopristin suppresses proliferation and enhances sensitivity to existing therapies, supporting mitochondrial translation as a viable therapeutic target in GBM [18].

Mitochondrial protein translation is mediated by the mitoribosome, composed of mitochondrial ribosomal proteins (MRPs). Beyond their canonical translational role, MRPs have been implicated in mitochondrial homeostasis, oxidative stress regulation, and tumor progression [19]. Several MRPs have been linked to cancer metabolism and adverse outcomes, including MRPL35 in colorectal cancer [20], the SIRT1/MRPS5 axis in hepatocellular carcinoma [21], and MRPS23 methylation–dependent regulation of OXPHOS in breast cancer [22]. Despite these advances, the functional roles of MRPs in glioma—a tumor characterized by pronounced metabolic plasticity—remain largely unexplored.

Not all MRPs are equally likely to influence mitochondrial translational output in the same way. Structural and biochemical studies have suggested that a subset of mitochondrial ribosomal proteins positioned near the polypeptide tunnel exit may be particularly important because this region is closely linked to nascent peptide emergence, early processing, and membrane insertion of mitochondrially encoded OXPHOS subunits. In yeast mitoribosomes, MRPL3, MRPL13, and MRPL27 have been identified as tunnel-exit-associated, mitochondria-specific ribosomal proteins, highlighting this subgroup as a structurally and functionally distinct module within the large mitoribosomal subunit [23]. Because mitochondrial-encoded respiratory chain proteins are highly hydrophobic and require coordinated translation and membrane insertion, proteins in this tunnel-exit-associated region may be especially relevant to the maintenance of OXPHOS integrity [19].

Among this subgroup, MRPL27 attracted our attention for two reasons. First, its structural location suggests a close link to mitochondrial translational output rather than a peripheral regulatory role. Second, emerging cancer-related evidence indicates that MRPL27 may not be a housekeeping mitoribosomal component only. For example, MRPL27 has been reported to be upregulated and associated with poor prognosis in cholangiocarcinoma, where its expression is linked to mitochondrial translation and respiratory chain–related pathways [24]. These observations raised the possibility that MRPL27 might represent a metabolically relevant and clinically meaningful mitochondrial ribosomal factor in tumors, although its role in glioma remains unknown.

MRPL27 is an integral component of the large subunit of the mitochondrial ribosome, localized within the mitochondrial matrix and involved in mitochondrial-encoded protein translation [19]. Although previous studies have implicated MRPL27 in mitochondrial functional maintenance and tumor-associated mitochondrial programs [19, 23, 24], its expression pattern, biological significance, and mechanistic contribution to glioma progression have not been defined. In the present study, we therefore sought to systematically investigate MRPL27 in glioma, with particular emphasis on its relationship to mitochondrial translation, oxidative phosphorylation, and malignant progression. By addressing these questions, we aimed to clarify whether MRPL27 represents a metabolically important determinant of glioma biology and a potential diagnostic or therapeutic target.

Materials and methods

Ethics

Experiments was approved by the Ethics Committee of Soochow University.

Chemicals and reagents

Puromycin, antibiotics, and cell culture reagents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Fluorescent dyes and assay kits were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Invitrogen, Shanghai, China). Primary antibodies used in this study were sourced from Cell Signaling Technology (Beverly, MA, USA) and Abcam (Cambridge, UK). All primers, sequence information, and viral constructs were supplied by Genechem Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis

Publicly available glioma single-cell RNA-seq datasets generated using Smart-seq2 and 10x Genomics platforms (GSE67835 and GSE131928) were analyzed in R using Seurat. Genes detected in ≥ 3 cells and cells with ≥ 100 detected genes were retained. Low-quality cells were removed using platform-specific thresholds (Smart-seq2: nFeature_RNA > 3000, percent.mt < 20%; 10x: nFeature_RNA > 200, percent.mt < 20%). Data were normalized and integrated using SCTransform, followed by PCA, Louvain clustering, and UMAP visualization.

Cell types were manually annotated based on canonical marker genes and grouped into four major categories: Malignant, Immune, CNS_normal, and Stromal. Malignant glioma cells were identified using established glioma-associated markers, including EGFR, SOX2, OLIG2, PDGFRA, NES, PTPRZ1, PROM1, CDK4, and MKI67 [25, 26], and further validated by inferCNV analysis comparing marker-defined malignant and non-malignant populations. Normalized MRPL27 expression was projected onto UMAP, and mean expression and the fraction of MRPL27-positive cells were calculated across cell categories. Gene co-expression with MRPL27 was assessed by Spearman correlation with Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction, and enrichment analysis of correlated genes was performed using Enrichr.

Cell culture.

The human glioma cell lines U251 and T98G were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Shanghai Institutes for Biological Sciences (Shanghai, China) and authenticated by the same institution. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin, and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO₂. Surgically resected glioma specimens were washed, mechanically dissociated, and enzymatically digested using collagenase I and deoxyribonuclease (Sigma-Aldrich). The resulting single-cell suspensions were collected and washed, followed by the removal of non-malignant cells, including fibroblasts, vascular cells, and immune cells. All cell cultures were routinely tested and confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Soochow University and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The procedures for the isolation and culture of primary human glioma cells and astrocytes have been described previously [27, 28]. All experiments were performed using cells between passages 3 and 10 after thawing.

Human glioma tissues

Human glioma tissues and matched adjacent normal brain tissues were collected from patients who underwent surgical resection at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University between 2018 and 2019, as previously described [27, 29, 30]. All enrolled patients were histopathologically diagnosed with high-grade glioma (WHO grade III–IV). Inclusion criteria comprised patients with primary glioma who had not received radiotherapy or chemotherapy prior to surgery. Patients with a history of other malignancies, recurrent glioma, or preoperative radiotherapy or chemotherapy were excluded from the study. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. To ensure the reproducibility and reliability of the study, key clinicopathological characteristics of the enrolled patients, including age, sex, WHO grade, and IDH mutation status, are summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

Western blotting

Tissues and cells were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors (Beyotime, Wuxi, China). Protein concentrations were determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (Invitrogen, Suzhou, China). Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS–PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (EMD Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany). After blocking, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with appropriate secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein signals were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system. Uncropped immunoblot images are provided in the Supplementary Materials. Trypsin-EDTA digestion solution (FL20011) were purchased from Bioland Biotechnology Co. Ltd.(Hangzhou, China).The pre-stained protein marker is Catalog NO. F4005 (APExBIO, Houston, USA).

Quantitative real-time reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

Total RNA was isolated from cells and tissues using TRIzol reagent (Takara, Otsu, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration was measured, and complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Takara) on a Bio-Rad real-time PCR detection system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA). Relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method, with β-actin serving as the internal reference gene. All primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), and the corresponding sequences are provided in Table 1.

Sequences utilized in this study

MRPL27 shRNA

MRPL27 was silenced in glioma cells using short hairpin RNA (shRNA). Cells at approximately 50% confluence were infected with lentiviral particles carrying MRPL27-targeting shRNA cloned into the GV369 vector (Genechem, Shanghai, China) at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10. After 60 h, stable cell populations were selected with puromycin and subsequently passaged six times. Two non-overlapping shRNA sequences targeting MRPL27 (shMRPL27-S1 and shMRPL27-S2) were used, while control cells were infected with lentiviral particles expressing a scrambled shRNA (shC). MRPL27 mRNA and protein expression levels in stable cell lines were routinely assessed. For in vivo experiments, the shMRPL27-S2 or shC sequences were subcloned into adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors, and viral particles were generated as previously described [30, 31]. All lentivirus-mediated knockdown experiments in other glioma cell lines were performed at an MOI of 10.

MRPL27 knockout

Gene editing of MRPL27 was performed in primary glioma cells at approximately 50% confluency. Cells were first transduced with a lentiviral vector expressing Cas9, as previously described [27, 30, 31], to establish stable Cas9-expressing cell lines. These cells were subsequently infected with lentiviral particles carrying the CRISPR/Cas9-MRPL27-KO puro construct containing MRPL27-specific single-guide RNA (sgRNA) sequences (Genechem, Shanghai, China). Stable knockout cell populations were selected using puromycin. Disruption of the MRPL27 locus was confirmed by Sanger sequencing of PCR-amplified genomic fragments, and knockout efficiency was further validated by Western blot analysis. Two single-cell–derived MRPL27 knockout clones (koMRPL27-sg1 and koMRPL27-sg2) exhibited complete loss of MRPL27 protein expression. Control cells were generated by infection with a lentiviral CRISPR/Cas9 empty vector (koC) [27, 29, 31, 32]. For all glioma cell lines subjected to CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene knockout, lentiviral transduction was performed at a fixed multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10. Successful knockout was additionally confirmed by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR).

MRPL27 overexpression

For MRPL27 overexpression, glioma cells were transduced with lentiviral particles carrying an untagged MRPL27 expression construct (GV369) at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10 for 60 h. Stable cell lines were established by puromycin selection and subsequently passaged six times. MRPL27 mRNA and protein expression levels were routinely assessed to confirm sustained overexpression. All glioma cell lines used for overexpression experiments were infected with lentivirus at an MOI of 10. All lentiviral vectors were purchased from Anzhenbio (China). For viral transduction, lentiviral stocks were serially diluted in 1 mL of maintenance medium containing 1% fetal bovine serum (FBS; JYK-FBS-301, Inner Mongolia Jinyuan Kang Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) and then added to the appropriate wells.

Cell proliferation assays

Cell proliferation was evaluated using colony formation, EdU incorporation, and CCK-8 assays. For colony formation, 800 cells per well were seeded and cultured for 2 weeks, followed by fixation with paraformaldehyde, crystal violet staining, and colony counting. Staining solution purchased from (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., G4740). For EdU assays, cells were plated in 24-well plates, cultured for 48 h, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100, and stained with EdU and DAPI. Fluorescence images were acquired from three random fields per well using a Zeiss microscope. For CCK-8 assays, cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 3 × 10³ cells per well, incubated for 96 h, and then treated with CCK-8 reagent for 2 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Shanghai, China).

Transwell migration and invasion assays

Cell migration and invasion were evaluated using Transwell chambers (Corning). For migration assays, 12,000 cells in 100 µL were seeded into the upper chamber, and 600 µL of medium containing 12% FBS was added to the lower chamber. After 24 h, non-migrated cells on the upper surface were removed with a cotton swab. Migrated cells on the lower surface were stained with 300 µL of staining solution for 10 min, rinsed with PBS, and imaged. For invasion assays, Transwell inserts were pre-coated with Matrigel (100 µg/cm², Sigma) prior to cell seeding.

Apoptosis assays

Apoptosis was evaluated using TUNEL staining. Target cells were seeded at 5 × 10⁴ cells per well in 12-well plates and cultured for 72 h. Cells were stained with TUNEL and DAPI, and images were captured using a Leica fluorescence microscope. The proportion of TUNEL-positive cells was quantified from at least 1,500 nuclei across three randomly selected fields (200× magnification) and expressed as a percentage of DAPI-stained nuclei.Mitochondrial membrane potential was assessed using the JC-1 assay (Biyuntian). Green JC-1 monomer fluorescence (excitation 490 nm) was measured, and representative images integrating both green (490 nm) and red (625 nm) channels were acquired.Caspase-3 activity was measured using a colorimetric assay kit (BioVision, Milpitas, CA). Lysates containing active caspases cleaved the substrate at specific recognition sequences, releasing a chromophore quantified by absorbance at 450 nm using a spectrophotometer (BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT).

Caspase activity assays

Caspase-3 and caspase-9 activities were determined using commercially available colorimetric assay kits (BioVision, Milpitas, CA). Briefly, cells or tumor tissues were lysed in the supplied lysis buffer and clarified by centrifugation. Equal amounts of protein from each sample were incubated with caspase-specific substrates containing the DEVD (for caspase-3) or LEHD (for caspase-9) recognition sequence. Upon cleavage by active caspases, the chromophore p-nitroaniline (pNA) was released and quantified by measuring absorbance at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT).

Measurement of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS)

Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were assessed using fluorescent probes DCFH-DA and CellROX according to standard protocols. Cells were incubated with DCFH-DA working solution or CellROX reagent (final concentration 1–5 µM) prepared in serum-free medium or culture medium, respectively, at 37 °C for 30 min under dark conditions. After incubation, cells were washed three times with PBS to remove excess probe. Fluorescence signals were immediately captured using a fluorescence microscope with consistent exposure settings (DCF-DA: excitation/emission 488/525 nm; CellROX: red fluorescence). Mean fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software and normalized to cell number to reflect intracellular ROS levels.

ATP content and mitochondrial complex I activity

Cellular ATP levels and mitochondrial complex I activity were measured using ATP assay kits and mitochondrial complex I activity kits (Beyotime, Wuxi, China). Cells subjected to the indicated treatments were lysed, and supernatants were collected after centrifugation. ATP content was quantified based on a luciferase-dependent luminescence reaction, while complex I activity was determined by monitoring NADH oxidation–dependent absorbance changes.

Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) analysis

Mitochondrial respiration was evaluated by measuring the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) using an XF24 Extracellular Flux Analyzer (Agilent Seahorse Bioscience), following established protocols. Cells were seeded in Seahorse XF24 plates and equilibrated in assay medium prior to measurement. OCR was recorded under basal conditions and following sequential injections of oligomycin (1 µM), FCCP (0.5 µM), and a mixture of antimycin A and rotenone (0.5 µM each) to assess basal respiration and maximal respiration.

Immunohistochemistry and tissue fluorescence staining

Harvested xenograft tumors were fixed, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at 4 μm thickness. Sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through graded ethanol, and subjected to antigen retrieval in citrate buffer. After blocking to prevent nonspecific binding, sections were incubated with primary antibodies followed by appropriate secondary antibodies. Ki-67 expression was evaluated using a commercial immunohistochemistry kit (Beyotime, Wuxi, China). In parallel, immunofluorescence staining for Caspase-3 was performed to assess apoptosis. TUNEL staining was conducted on adjacent sections using a BioVision kit, with DAPI counterstaining to visualize nuclei. Slides were mounted with antifade medium and imaged using a fluorescence microscope.

Tumor xenograft mouse model

Male and female BALB/c nude mice aged 6–8 weeks (body weight 17.9–18.4 g) were purchased from the Animal Center of Soochow University and housed under specific pathogen–free (SPF) conditions. Animals were maintained in a controlled environment (22 ± 2 °C, 50 ± 10% humidity) with a 12-h light/dark cycle and provided ad libitum access to food and water. For subcutaneous xenograft establishment, 5 × 10⁶ glioma cells were suspended in serum-free medium, mixed with Matrigel to a final volume of 150 µL, and injected into the right axillary region of each mouse. Mice were randomly allocated into experimental groups (n = 8 per group) using a randomization table, and investigators were blinded to group assignment during tumor assessment. Body weight and overall health status were recorded every six days. Humane endpoints included tumor volume exceeding ethical limits, body weight loss greater than 20%, or the appearance of distress or tumor ulceration, at which point mice were euthanized by CO₂ inhalation.

Bioinformatics analysis

Bioinformatics analyses were performed proficiently using R 4.2, ensuring high accuracy and reliability in data processing and interpretation. CNV inference analysis was performed to validate malignant cell annotation in the single-cell dataset, and the inferred CNV profiles were compared between marker-defined malignant and non-malignant cell populations.

Statistical analyses

All in vitro experiments were performed with investigators blinded to group allocation. Data following a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 23.0 (Chicago, IL, USA). Differences among multiple groups were evaluated by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett’s post hoc test, whereas comparisons between two groups were assessed using Student’s t-test. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were analyzed using the log-rank test. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All in vitro experiments were independently repeated at least three times with consistent results.

Results

Upregulation of MRPL27 in glioma and its association with clinical outcomes

To clarify why MRPL27 was prioritized among structurally related mitochondrial ribosomal protein candidates, we first compared the expression and prognostic relevance of MRPL3, MRPL13, and MRPL27 in the TCGA-GBMLGG cohort. All three genes were significantly upregulated in glioma tissues relative to normal brain tissues (Figure S1A), and high expression of each gene was associated with shorter overall survival in Kaplan–Meier analysis (Figure S1B–D). However, when major clinicopathological variables, including WHO grade, IDH status, age, and sex, were incorporated into multivariate Cox regression models, only MRPL27 retained independent prognostic significance, whereas MRPL3 and MRPL13 did not (Figure S1E). These findings indicate that, among these structurally related mitochondrial ribosomal protein candidates, MRPL27 provides the most robust independent clinical signal and therefore represents the most suitable candidate for subsequent investigation.

We first analyzed transcriptomic data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) glioma cohort. MRPL27 mRNA expression was significantly elevated in glioma tissues compared with normal brain tissues (Fig. 1A), and was further increased in high-grade gliomas relative to low-grade gliomas (Fig. 1B). Clinicopathological correlation analysis revealed that MRPL27 expression was significantly higher in IDH wild-type tumors than in IDH-mutant gliomas (Fig. 1C), while no association was observed with patient sex (Fig. 1D). In contrast, MRPL27 expression showed significant associations with patient age and 1p/19q codeletion status (Fig. 1E, F).

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis demonstrated that elevated MRPL27 expression was significantly associated with unfavorable clinical outcomes in glioma patients (Fig. 1G), including shorter overall survival (OS), progression-free interval (PFI), and disease-specific survival (DSS) (Fig. 1H, I). Univariate Cox regression analysis identified high MRPL27 expression as a significant risk factor for poor OS (hazard ratio [HR] = 2.269, P < 0.001) (Fig. 1J). This association remained significant after adjustment for other clinicopathological variables in multivariate Cox analysis (HR = 1.633, P < 0.001) (Fig. 1M), indicating that MRPL27 serves as an independent prognostic factor in glioma.

Time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis showed that MRPL27 consistently achieved area under the curve (AUC) values above 0.6 across 1–5 years of follow-up (Fig. 1K), indicating stable prognostic performance. The AUC values for predicting 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS were 0.671, 0.692, and 0.682, respectively (Fig. 1L). In addition, ROC analysis based on TCGA data demonstrated a high diagnostic accuracy of MRPL27 in distinguishing glioma from normal brain tissues (AUC = 0.866) (Fig. 1K).

To validate these findings, we analyzed an independent cohort from the Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA). Consistent with the TCGA results, MRPL27 expression was significantly upregulated in glioma tissues (Figure S2A) and was associated with poorer overall survival (Figure S2G). Moreover, MRPL27 expression correlated significantly with tumor grade, IDH status, patient age, and 1p/19q status, but not with sex (Figure S2B–F). Collectively, analyses from both TCGA and CGGA cohorts demonstrate that MRPL27 is markedly overexpressed in glioma and closely associated with adverse clinical outcomes.

Single-cell transcriptomic analyses reveal tumor-specific enrichment of MRPL27 in malignant glioma cells

Single-cell transcriptomic analysis was performed to define the cellular distribution and tumor specificity of MRPL27 within the glioma microenvironment using the GEO dataset GSE131928, which comprises glioblastoma tissue samples. After quality control, normalization, and dimensionality reduction, 20 transcriptionally distinct cell clusters were identified. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) analysis revealed clear separation among these clusters (Fig. 2A). Based on established marker genes and functional annotations, cells were classified into four major populations: malignant tumor cells, immune cells, stromal cells, and normal central nervous system (CNS) cells. Malignant cells occupied a discrete and highly enriched region within the UMAP space (Fig. 2B). To further support the robustness of malignant cell annotation, copy number variation (CNV) inference analysis was performed. The cell clusters classified as malignant based on marker expression exhibited widespread large-scale CNV patterns, whereas non-malignant cell populations largely lacked such alterations (Figure S3). These results supported the reliability of the marker-based annotation and confirmed the malignant identity of the tumor cell clusters.

MRPL27 expression mapped onto the UMAP showed a strong enrichment within malignant tumor cells, whereas immune, stromal, and normal CNS cells exhibited consistently low expression levels (Fig. 2C). Quantitative analyses at the cell subtype level further demonstrated that MRPL27 displayed elevated average expression and a higher proportion of expressing cells across multiple tumor-associated subpopulations, with particularly prominent expression in proliferation-associated malignant subtypes (Fig. 2D). Consistently, analysis at the level of major cell populations confirmed that MRPL27 expression was significantly higher in malignant tumor cells compared with immune, stromal, or normal CNS cells (Fig. 2E), indicating a pronounced tumor cell–biased expression pattern.

The tumor specificity of MRPL27 expression was further evaluated by integrating a single-cell RNA sequencing dataset derived from normal brain tissues (GSE67835). In normal brain samples, MRPL27 expression remained uniformly low across all major cell populations. In contrast, GBM samples exhibited markedly increased MRPL27 expression and a higher proportion of MRPL27-positive cells specifically within malignant tumor cells (Fig. 2F). Together, these cross-dataset analyses demonstrate that MRPL27 overexpression is largely confined to malignant glioma cells rather than normal brain cell populations, supporting its tumor-associated and cell type–specific expression pattern in GBM.

MRPL27 is significantly upregulated in glioma tissues from patients receiving local treatment

MRPL27 expression was next examined in glioma tissues obtained from patients receiving local therapy using both clinical specimens and glioma cell models. Tumor tissues (“T”) and matched adjacent normal brain tissues (“N”) were collected from 16 patients with primary high-grade glioma (HGG, WHO grade III–IV). Analysis of tissue lysates showed that MRPL27 mRNA expression was significantly higher in glioma tissues than in matched normal brain tissues (Fig. 3A). Consistently, MRPL27 protein levels were markedly elevated in tumor samples from 10 representative HGG patients (Patients #1–#10) (Fig. 3B). Quantification of Western blot signals across all 16 paired samples, normalized to individual loading controls, further confirmed significant upregulation of MRPL27 protein in glioma tissues (Fig. 3B).

MRPL27 expression was then evaluated in human glioma cell models, including patient-derived primary glioma cells (P1 and P2) and established glioma cell lines T98G and U251. Compared with primary human astrocytes, MRPL27 mRNA levels were significantly increased in all examined glioma cells (Fig. 3C). Normalization to astrocyte expression revealed consistently elevated MRPL27 mRNA levels across both primary and immortalized glioma models. In agreement with these findings, MRPL27 protein expression was also markedly higher in glioma cells than in astrocytes (Fig. 3D).

Immunohistochemical analysis further demonstrated stronger MRPL27 staining in glioma tissues (“T1” and “T2”) than in matched adjacent normal brain tissues (“N1” and “N2”) from representative patients (Fig. 3E). Subcellular localization analysis using immunofluorescence staining revealed that MRPL27 predominantly localized to the cytoplasmic compartment and showed substantial co-localization with the mitochondrial marker MitoTracker Red, indicating mitochondrial enrichment of MRPL27 (Fig. 3F). Line-scan fluorescence intensity analysis along selected regions showed highly coincident peaks for MRPL27 and MitoTracker signals. Together, these results demonstrate that MRPL27 is markedly upregulated in human glioma tissues and cells, displays tumor-specific enrichment, and predominantly localizes to mitochondria, supporting its potential functional relevance in glioma biology.

MRPL27 is associated with oxidative phosphorylation– and cell proliferation–related transcriptional programs

Differential expression analysis of the TCGA glioma cohort stratified by MRPL27 expression identified 3,352 differentially expressed genes (|log₂FC| > 1, p < 0.05), including 2,449 upregulated and 903 downregulated genes (Fig. 4A). In parallel, Pearson correlation analysis using intersected TCGA-GBM and TCGA-LGG datasets identified 2,290 genes positively and 375 genes negatively correlated with MRPL27 expression (correlation coefficient > 0.35, adjusted p < 0.05; Fig. 4B). Integration of these datasets yielded 409 overlapping genes (Fig. 4C), defined as key MRPL27-related genes.

Gene Ontology analysis revealed that these genes were predominantly enriched in chromatin organization and cell proliferation–related processes, including protein–DNA complex assembly and DNA replication–independent chromatin assembly, as well as centromeric and kinetochore components (Fig. 4D). Molecular function analysis highlighted enrichment in cyclin-dependent protein kinase regulator activity, indicating a strong association with cell cycle control. Consistently, gene set enrichment analysis showed that high MRPL27 expression was associated with pathways related to oxidative phosphorylation, mitochondrial translation, DNA replication, and cell cycle progression (Fig. 4E). Given that diffuse infiltration is a defining biological feature of glioma, particularly GBM, and that glioma cell invasion is closely linked to extracellular matrix remodeling [33, 34], integrin-mediated adhesion, and cytoskeletal dynamics, we further asked whether MRPL27 expression was associated with transcriptional programs related to invasive behavior. Supplementary GSEA showed that MRPL27-high tumors were significantly enriched in several invasion-related pathways, including epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), extracellular matrix organization, integrin cell surface interactions, and Rho GTPase effectors (Figure S4). Protein–protein interaction network analysis demonstrated a highly interconnected structure centered on key regulators of cell cycle and mitosis (Fig. 4F). These hub genes were generally upregulated in MRPL27 high-expression tumors (Fig. 4G) and showed significant positive correlations with MRPL27 expression (Fig. 4H). Collectively, these findings indicate that MRPL27 is closely linked to coordinated transcriptional programs governing mitochondrial function and cell cycle progression, supporting its role in glioma malignant progression.

Silencing MRPL27 activates apoptosis in glioma cells

In both primary and established glioma cells, shRNA-mediated depletion of MRPL27 markedly reduced cell viability, proliferation, and migratory capacity. Given these growth-suppressive effects, we examined whether MRPL27 loss compromises glioma cell survival by activating apoptotic pathways. In primary glioma cells (P1), knockdown of MRPL27 using two independent shRNAs (shMRPL27-S1 and shMRPL27-S2) led to pronounced increases in Caspase-3 and Caspase-9 activities (Fig. 6A, B). Consistently, Western blot analyses revealed robust cleavage and activation of both caspases following MRPL27 silencing (Fig. 6C). Apoptotic induction was further confirmed by TUNEL staining, which showed a significant increase in TUNEL-positive nuclei in MRPL27-depleted P1 cells, accompanied by elevated cell death rates (Fig. 6D, E). Pharmacological inhibition of caspase activity partially rescued these effects: treatment with the Caspase-3–specific inhibitor z-DEVD-fmk or the pan-caspase inhibitor z-VAD-fmk significantly restored cell viability and reduced cell death in shMRPL27-treated cells (Fig. 6F). In contrast, caspase inhibition had no appreciable impact on viability or survival in control cells.

MRPL27 knockdown disrupts mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and induces oxidative stress in glioma cells

Consistent with a role for MRPL27 in mitochondrial regulation, its silencing in primary glioma cells (P1) resulted in pronounced mitochondrial dysfunction. MRPL27 knockdown significantly reduced intracellular ATP levels and respiratory chain complex I activity (Fig. 7A, B), accompanied by disruption of redox homeostasis, as indicated by a decreased GSH/GSSG ratio, increased lipid peroxidation (TBAR activity), and accumulation of the oxidative DNA damage marker 8-OHdG (Fig. 7C–E). Seahorse analysis further revealed marked suppression of basal and maximal oxygen consumption rates, indicating impaired oxidative phosphorylation (Fig. 7F). In parallel, JC-1 staining demonstrated mitochondrial membrane depolarization (Fig. 7G), while CellROX and DCF-DA assays showed a robust increase in intracellular ROS levels following MRPL27 depletion (Fig. 7H, I).

MRPL27 maintains oxidative phosphorylation by regulating mitochondrial protein translation

Previous single-cell and TCGA cohort analyses showed that MRPL27 is preferentially expressed in malignant glioma cells and correlates with transcriptional programs related to oxidative phosphorylation and cell proliferation. Consistent with these observations, both shRNA-mediated knockdown and CRISPR/Cas9-mediated deletion of MRPL27 suppressed cell viability, proliferation, and migration/invasion across multiple glioma cell models and triggered caspase-dependent apoptosis. At the mitochondrial level, MRPL27 loss was associated with reduced ATP production, impaired mitochondrial respiration and complex I activity, mitochondrial membrane depolarization, increased ROS accumulation, and disruption of redox balance. Antioxidant treatment with N-acetylcysteine (NAC) partially restored cell viability and alleviated the suppression of malignant phenotypes induced by MRPL27 knockdown, indicating a contribution of mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress to these cellular effects. Given the pronounced impairment of oxidative phosphorylation following MRPL27 loss, we examined the expression of key mitochondrially encoded subunits directly involved in the electron transport chain and ATP synthesis. These included the complex I core subunit MT-ND2, the complex III component MT-CYB, the complex IV catalytic subunits MT-CO1 and MT-CO2, and the ATP synthase subunits MT-ATP6 and MT-ATP8.

In primary glioma cells (P1), shRNA-mediated MRPL27 knockdown did not significantly alter the mRNA levels of MT-ND2, MT-CYB, MT-CO1, MT-CO2, MT-ATP6, or MT-ATP8 (Fig. 10A). In contrast, Western blot analysis revealed a marked reduction in the protein levels of these mitochondrially encoded subunits under MRPL27-silencing conditions, whereas control shRNA had no effect (Fig. 10B). Similar results were obtained in MRPL27-knockout P1 cells. CRISPR/Cas9-mediated deletion of MRPL27 did not significantly affect the transcription of mitochondrially encoded genes (Fig. 10C) but resulted in a pronounced decrease in the corresponding protein expression levels (Fig. 10D). These data indicate that MRPL27 depletion selectively impairs the expression of mitochondrially encoded OXPHOS proteins at the protein level, without affecting their transcription, suggesting a role for MRPL27 in regulating mitochondrial protein translation or stability.

MRPL27 expression is associated with EMT-like invasive phenotypes in glioma cells

Given that MRPL27 overexpression significantly enhanced the migration and invasion of glioma cells in vitro (see Sect. 3.11), we further explored whether MRPL27 overexpression status was accompanied by transcriptional features and phenotypic changes related to EMT-like invasion (Fig. 12). P) < 0.001, and gene expression in at least 20% of cells (pct > 20%) were used as screening thresholds to identify a set of differentially expressed genes associated with high expression status of MRPL27 (Fig. 12A). At the pathway level, gene set enrichment analysis showed that tumors in the MRPL27 high expression group were significantly enriched in invasion-related pathways such as EMT, extracellular matrix organization, integrin-mediated interaction, and focal adhesion (Fig. 12E). Previous studies have shown that TUBB2B can promote glioma cell migration and invasion by interacting with the mesenchymal marker protein Vimentin to induce EMT program [35]. In addition, in triple negative breast cancer, the high expression of TUBB2B is significantly associated with poor prognosis and brain metastasis colonization, and targeted inhibition of TUBB2B can effectively inhibit tumor growth and brain metastasis formation [36]. At the functional level, genetic regulation of MRPL27 expression caused synergistic changes in EMT-related markers, with MRPL27 knockdown or knockdown and overexpression inducing opposite expression trends, respectively (Fig. 12F-H). Taken together with the results that MRPL27 overexpression significantly enhanced the migration and invasion ability of glioma cells, the above data collectively suggest that MRPL27 overexpression is closely related to the EMT-like invasion phenotype of glioma cells.

Silencing MRPL27 inhibits the growth of subcutaneous glioma xenografts in nude mice

To assess the effect of MRPL27 on glioma growth in vivo, primary P1 glioma cells expressing control shRNA (shC) or MRPL27-targeting shRNA (shMRPL27) were subcutaneously injected into the right axilla of nude mice (5 × 10⁶ cells per mouse). Tumor size and body weight were monitored every six days (Fig. 13A, F), and all mice were sacrificed on day 42. Excised tumors were photographed and analyzed (Fig. 13E). Tumors derived from the shMRPL27 group displayed significantly reduced volume and weight compared with those from the shC group (Fig. 13C, D). Consistently, MRPL27 silencing markedly attenuated xenograft growth and decreased the estimated daily tumor growth rate (Fig. 13B). No significant differences in body weight were observed between groups throughout the experiment (Fig. 13F, G).

Tumor tissues were collected for molecular and histological analyses. qRT-PCR and Western blotting confirmed reduced MRPL27 mRNA and protein levels in shMRPL27 xenografts (Fig. 13H, I), which was further validated by immunohistochemical staining (Fig. 13J). Ki-67 staining revealed decreased proliferative activity in tumors from the shMRPL27 group (Fig. 13K). In parallel, immunofluorescence analysis showed increased Caspase-3 expression and apoptotic signals in MRPL27-silenced xenografts (Fig. 13L, M). These findings were supported by Western blot results showing enhanced cleavage of Caspase-3 and Caspase-9 in shMRPL27 tumors (Fig. 13N).

Given the mitochondrial phenotypes observed in vitro, the expression of mitochondrially encoded oxidative phosphorylation–related proteins was examined in xenograft tissues. Protein levels of MT-ND2, MT-CYB, MT-CO1, MT-CO2, MT-ATP6, and MT-ATP8 were markedly reduced in tumors from the shMRPL27 group compared with controls (Fig. 13O). These results indicate that MRPL27 promotes tumor growth and survival in vivo by maintaining the expression of mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation–related proteins.

Discussion

Gliomas, particularly glioblastoma, remain among the most formidable malignant tumors of the central nervous system to treat [37, 38]. Despite advances in molecular classification and the expansion of targeted therapeutic strategies, only a subset of patients derives durable benefit, and overall survival improvements remain modest [39, 40]. The transient nature of therapeutic responses and the near-universal occurrence of recurrence highlight the extraordinary adaptive capacity of glioma cells under sustained treatment pressure [41]. Dissecting the fundamental biological mechanisms that enable this adaptability is therefore essential for understanding glioma malignancy and for identifying more effective therapeutic vulnerabilities.

In recent years, attention has increasingly shifted from individual oncogenic signaling pathways toward tumor metabolic plasticity. Accumulating evidence indicates that glioma cells do not rely on a single metabolic program but instead dynamically regulate mitochondrial function to meet energetic and biosynthetic demands imposed by rapid growth and hostile microenvironmental conditions [42–44]. Mitochondria occupy a central position in this adaptive process, coordinating energy production, redox homeostasis, and cell death susceptibility, thereby conferring metabolic flexibility and therapeutic tolerance [45, 46]. However, the molecular mechanisms that preserve mitochondrial functional integrity in glioma cells remain incompletely understood.

Mitochondrial protein translation represents a core process underlying mitochondrial integrity and bioenergetic competence. This process depends on the mitochondrial ribosome and its associated proteins, which not only sustain oxidative phosphorylation but may also influence mitochondrial homeostasis and stress signaling [47, 48]. Increasing studies across diverse cancer types have linked mitochondrial ribosomal proteins (MRPs) to metabolic reprogramming and adverse clinical outcomes [49–51]. Nevertheless, the contribution of MRPs to metabolic adaptation and malignant progression in glioma has remained largely unexplored, representing an important gap in current knowledge. Not all MRPs are equally likely to influence mitochondrial translational output. We therefore focused on a structurally defined subgroup of large-subunit mitoribosomal proteins located near the polypeptide tunnel exit, a region closely linked to nascent peptide emergence and early membrane insertion of mitochondrially encoded OXPHOS subunits. Previous structural studies identified MRPL3, MRPL13, and MRPL27 as tunnel-exit-associated mitochondrial ribosomal proteins. Among these candidates, only MRPL27 retained independent prognostic significance after adjustment for major clinicopathological variables, supporting its prioritization for subsequent investigation.

MRPL27 is a structural component of the large subunit of the mitochondrial ribosome and plays a key role in the translation of mitochondrially encoded proteins required for respiratory chain assembly and oxidative phosphorylation [52]. Beyond its canonical translational function, emerging evidence suggests a broader role for MRPL27 in mitochondrial homeostasis and cellular metabolism. Notably, aberrant MRPL27 expression has been reported in cholangiocarcinoma and is associated with poor clinical outcomes, implicating MRPL27 as a potential tumor-promoting factor [24].

In this study, we systematically characterized the expression pattern, functional relevance, and mechanistic role of MRPL27 in glioma. MRPL27 was found to be significantly upregulated in glioma tissues and malignant tumor cells, with high expression closely associated with unfavorable patient prognosis. Functional perturbation experiments revealed that MRPL27 is required to sustain glioma cell proliferation, migration, and invasion across multiple primary and established glioma models. MRPL27 silencing resulted in impaired mitochondrial function, as evidenced by reduced ATP production, loss of mitochondrial membrane potential, excessive ROS accumulation, and induction of caspase-dependent apoptosis. Conversely, ectopic overexpression of MRPL27 enhanced mitochondrial bioenergetic capacity and promoted malignant cellular behaviors, underscoring its functional importance in glioma biology. Our single-cell analyses further showed that MRPL27 upregulation was largely restricted to malignant glioma cells rather than broadly distributed across stromal, immune, or normal CNS populations, suggesting that its tumor-associated elevation more likely reflects an intrinsic metabolic adaptation of malignant cells than a simple compositional effect. We note, however, that the upstream mechanisms driving MRPL27 overexpression were not directly addressed in the present study.

The oncogenic role of MRPL27 was further substantiated in vivo using a subcutaneous xenograft model. MRPL27 knockdown significantly suppressed tumor growth, reduced proliferative activity, and increased apoptotic signaling in tumor tissues. These phenotypes were accompanied by marked impairment of mitochondrial respiratory function, supporting a critical role for MRPL27 in sustaining tumor growth through mitochondrial metabolic regulation in vivo.

Mechanistically, MRPL27 deficiency profoundly reduced the protein abundance of multiple mitochondrially encoded subunits of the electron transport chain and ATP synthase, including MT-ND2, MT-CYB, MT-CO1, MT-CO2, MT-ATP6, and MT-ATP8, without affecting their corresponding mRNA levels. This dissociation between transcript and protein expression strongly suggests that MRPL27 regulates mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation primarily at the level of protein translation efficiency or stability rather than transcription. Given its role as a large-subunit mitochondrial ribosomal protein, MRPL27 likely acts as a key determinant of mitochondrial translational output, thereby ensuring adequate respiratory chain function and ATP supply to support glioma cell survival and growth.

Aberrant regulation of MRPs has emerged as an important driver of tumor progression. A recent TCGA-based study in cholangiocarcinoma demonstrated that MRPL27 is markedly upregulated in tumor tissues and that its high expression predicts poor overall and disease-free survival, with enrichment in pathways related to mitochondrial translation and electron transport [24]. Notably, the absence of frequent somatic mutations suggests that MRPL27 exerts its tumor-promoting effects predominantly through dysregulated expression or regulatory mechanisms. Together with our findings, these observations point to a conserved role for MRPL27 in supporting tumor growth across different cancer types by maintaining mitochondrial protein translation and ATP production.

In addition to maintaining mitochondrial bioenergetics, our data suggest that elevated MRPL27 expression is also linked to invasive phenotypes in glioma cells. This is relevant because glioma malignancy is defined not only by proliferative capacity but also by diffuse infiltration. We do not interpret MRPL27 as a direct cytoskeletal regulator; rather, we propose that MRPL27-driven mitochondrial translation and OXPHOS maintenance provide the energetic and redox conditions that support invasive behavior. Consistent with this view, MRPL27 loss impaired ATP production, mitochondrial respiration, and redox balance, whereas NAC partially rescued the migration defect. Integrated single-cell and bulk transcriptomic analyses further supported this interpretation. MRPL27-high tumors were enriched not only in oxidative phosphorylation and mitochondrial translation programs, but also in invasion-related pathways, including EMT, extracellular matrix organization, integrin-mediated interactions, focal adhesion, and Rho GTPase signaling. We also identified TUBB2B as a representative gene consistently co-varying with MRPL27 expression. Since previous studies have linked TUBB2B to EMT-like and invasive behavior in glioblastoma and triple-negative breast cancer [35, 36], the co-occurrence of MRPL27 high expression and TUBB2B upregulation in our dataset suggests that MRPL27-driven metabolic remodeling may coincide with activation of EMT-like invasive programs, rather than implying a direct linear regulatory hierarchy.

From a translational perspective, the low mutational frequency of MRPL27 renders it an attractive candidate as a metabolism-associated prognostic biomarker and potential therapeutic target. Furthermore, multiple MRPs have been implicated in resistance to anticancer therapies, and pharmacological inhibition of mitochondrial ribosome function or mitochondrial protein synthesis has shown promise in restoring treatment sensitivity in several tumor models [51, 53, 54]. In this context, our data suggest that MRPL27 may enhance glioma cell survival under therapeutic stress by preserving mitochondrial translational capacity and metabolic flexibility, thereby buffering energy depletion and oxidative stress. Although direct evidence linking MRPL27 to therapeutic resistance in glioma is currently lacking, this study provides a strong rationale for future investigations into the relationship between MRPL27 expression and treatment response, as well as for exploring combinatorial strategies targeting mitochondrial translation alongside existing therapies.

In summary, this study identifies MRPL27 as a critical regulator of mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and redox homeostasis in glioma. By sustaining the expression of mitochondrially encoded OXPHOS proteins, MRPL27 supports the metabolic demands of malignant glioma cells and promotes tumor progression. These findings highlight mitochondrial translation as an underappreciated but potentially exploitable metabolic vulnerability in glioma.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that MRPL27 plays a critical role in glioma progression by maintaining mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and redox balance. Elevated MRPL27 expression supports mitochondrial protein translation and energy production, thereby promoting tumor cell growth, migration, and invasion, whereas genetic disruption of MRPL27 impairs mitochondrial function, induces oxidative stress–associated apoptosis, and suppresses glioma growth both in vitro and in vivo. These findings identify mitochondrial ribosomal regulation as a key metabolic layer in glioma biology and suggest that MRPL27 may represent a potential target for therapeutic intervention.
