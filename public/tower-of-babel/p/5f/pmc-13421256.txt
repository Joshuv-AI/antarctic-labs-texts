Discriminating inflammation from malignancy with short-dynamic patlak parametric 18 F-FDG PET/CT
Authors: Jelena Jandric, Lorenzo Leonardi, Rossella Barisonzi, Marcello Rodari, Laura Evangelista, Alessia Artesani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeDifferentiating malignant from inflammatory uptake on 18F-FDG PET/CT remains a major diagnostic challenge, as standardised uptake value (SUV) lacks specificity. This study evaluated whether parametric imaging from short-duration dynamic FDG PET/CT provides complementary information beyond SUV for distinguishing malignancy from inflammation.MethodsTwenty-eight patients undergoing oncologic PET/CT (breast, lung, lymphatic or gastrointestinal cancer) were included, yielding 68 lesions (43 malignant, 25 inflammatory). Short dynamic acquisitions (20 min) were motion-corrected and used to generate influx rate (Ki) and distribution volume (Vd) maps. Lesions were segmented on SUV images (40% SUVmax), and radiomic features were extracted from SUV, Ki, and Vd maps, including core and peritumoral regions. Classification performance was assessed both using logistic regression and Random Forest model. ResultsMalignant lesions exhibited higher mean values than inflammatory lesions for both SUV (6.0 vs. 2.7 g/ml) and Ki (2.0 vs. 0.8 ml/min/100 ml), while Vd values largely overlapped between classes (50 vs. 47%). No single parameter provided a reliable discriminative threshold. Restricting the analysis to equivocal SUV values (< 5.2 g/ml), multivariate regression combining SUV Mean, Ki/Vd Variance, and Ki Entropy achieved an accuracy of 0.86 (pseudo-R² = 0.42), outperforming SUV Mean alone (accuracy 0.77, pseudo-R² = 0.26). Core–peritumoral analysis revealed as Ki/Vd Variance the most statistically significant features between the two classes (4.35 ± 3.36 malignant vs. 1.93 ± 1.34 inflammatory; p = 0.006). Random Forest classification confirmed superior performance of parametric features (ROC AUC 0.86 ± 0.10) compared with SUV-only models (0.83 ± 0.08). ConclusionShort dynamic Patlak FDG PET/CT improves differentiation of malignant from inflammatory uptake beyond SUV alone. Decomposing FDG uptake into metabolised (Ki) and unmetabolised (Vd) fractions, components provide physiologically meaningful insights, revealing steeper core-to-peritumoral metabolic gradients in malignancy and more homogeneous tracer distribution in inflammation. These findings support the added value of parametric imaging and motivate prospective validation in larger clinical cohorts.Graphical Abstract Graphical abstract of short-dynamic Patlak parametric 18F-FDG PET/CT for differentiating malignant from inflammatory lesions. Conventional PET metrics, including SUV, Ki, and Vd considered individually, did not provide a reliable threshold for lesion classification because of substantial intercorrelation between features, particularly between mean SUV and Ki. Introduction

[18F] Fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) Positron Emission Tomography (PET) coupled to Computed Tomography (CT) is a cornerstone of oncologic imaging because many tumoral cells exhibit elevated glucose metabolism [1]. However, glucose avidity is not tumour-specific, and some inflammatory cells can also overexpress glucose transporters and hexokinase activity, leading to avid FDG uptake at inflammation sites [2]. Because of this lack of specificity, inflammatory processes may mimic malignancy on FDG PET, posing a diagnostic dilemma [3].

In practice, nuclear medicine physicians rely on uptake patterns, intensity, and relationships to areas of physiological distribution, as well as clinical context to suggest inflammation versus tumour. Common inflammatory patterns on PET/CT include diffuse uptake (as in colitis), spread or segmental uptake (as in vasculitis) or hilar nodal uptake (as in sarcoidosis), whereas malignancies often appear as more focal masses [4, 5]. Nevertheless, such patterns frequently overlap.

Current EANM/SNMMI guidelines for FDG PET in inflammation note that SUV thresholds are not validated in inflammation [2] and should be used with caution. For example, one study advised SUVmax>3 g/ml to improve specificity in spinal infection [6], but such cutoff remain empirical. In fact, relatively high SUV may be seen in inflammation, as well as relatively low SUV may be seen in malignant lesions. False positives are well-documented – for example, post‐surgical inflammatory reactions or uptake around implants (vascular grafts, prostheses) can mimic tumour [7]. In short, no standardised PET criteria exist to distinguish inflammation from cancer, and interpreting equivocal FDG foci often depends on reader experience, additional imaging, or biopsy.

To address this challenge, dynamic PET/CT with kinetic modelling has emerged as a promising tool. In dynamic scanning, tracer activity is recorded over time, and graphical Patlak analysis is applied to compute voxel-wise parameters [8, 9]. This yields quantitative images of the FDG influx rate constant (Ki, related to the Patlak slope) –proportional to the metabolic rate of FDG trapping – and the distribution volume (Vd, related to the Patlak intercept) ‒ associated to free and unphosphorylated FDG in both blood and tissue [10]. These parametric maps separate the metabolic (irreversible) component of uptake from delivery effects [11]. In several studies, Patlak-derived Ki images frequently show consistently higher lesion‐to‐background contrast than conventional SUV images [12–14]. In some cases, combining SUV with Patlak‐derived metrics appears to improve diagnostic accuracy [15]. Recently, simplified protocols like short dynamic acquisitions have been shown to yield reliable Ki estimates while minimising scan time [16]. These developments suggest that kinetic parameters could in principle enhance specificity for tumour versus inflammation, although formal guidelines on their use have not yet been established.

In this study, we investigated whether metabolic information from motion-corrected Patlak parametric FDG PET/CT can enhance discrimination between malignant and inflammatory uptake. Using a short-duration dynamic protocol validated in previous studies [17, 18], we focused on FDG-avid solid tumours in breast, lung, lymphatic and gastrointestinal cancer patients, including inflammatory findings from common comorbid conditions. The primary aim was to assess whether Patlak-derived kinetic parameters provide complementary information beyond SUV, and whether Ki and Vd, alone or in combination, differ between malignant and inflammatory uptake. Radiomic features from SUV and parametric maps were used for estimating the likelihood of malignancy versus inflammation.

Materials and methods

Population

Patients were recruited among individuals referred for 18F-FDG PET/CT examinations for oncological indications at the Nuclear Medicine Department of IRCCS Humanitas Research Hospital. Eligible participants were identified based on the following inclusion criteria: (1) confirmed solid tumours undergoing PET/CT for staging, restaging, treatment planning; (2) written informed consent. Exclusion criteria were: (1) Patients younger than 18 years; (2) pregnant or breastfeeding women; (3) individuals unable to undergo PET/CT for any reason; (4) patients with a diagnosis of heart failure.

PET/CT acquisition

A short-dynamic whole-body (dWB) PET/CT protocol was performed using a SAFOV scanner (Biograph Vision 600, Siemens Healthineers). Patients received a weight-based FDG injection (4.8 MBq/kg), followed by a 50-minute uptake period. A dWB PET/CT scan was then acquired using continuous bed motion over 20 min total (4 × 5-minute passes). A low-dose CT scan was acquired first for attenuation correction and anatomical localization. From each scan, both dynamic PET images and standard SUV image (10 min duration at 60 min post-injection) were derived. Dynamic data were reconstructed as series of temporal PET frames, while static PET were reconstructed for conventional SUV analysis. Standard corrections for attenuation, scatter, random coincidences, dead time, and radioactive decay were applied. No post-reconstruction filtering was used. The reconstructed images consisted of 335 planes (440 × 400 voxels) with a voxel size of 1.65 × 1.65 × 3 mm3.

An image-derived arterial input function (AIF) mask was generated by manually drawing a circular region of interest (approximately 15 mm in diameter) in the descending aorta, in agreement with previously published studies [16, 19–22]. For each patient, the absence of significant motion artifacts affecting the selected vascular region was visually verified.

Before running the Patlak graphical analysis, the dynamic PET frames were corrected for motion, in agreement with previously published work [17]. Specifically, we used the open-source Phyton-based FALCON software (version V2) [23, 24], specifically tailored for correcting motion among PET frames. In this study, we performed deformable registration with 50 × 50 × 50 iterations at each level of the multi-scale registration. The registration started from the 1st frame and used the last frame as reference, to better guaranteeing correct co-registration between parametric results and SUV images [25].

Patlak parametric images were generated using an indirect reconstruction workflow, as motion correction required data export and frame-wise processing, as validated in our previous study [18]. In this study, we used an open-source Python pipeline (pyPatlak) to compute voxel-wise Ki and Vd maps from motion-corrected dynamic PET frames [26]. The code was validated against commercial direct Patlak analysis in a previous study and source code is available on GitHub [27, 28].

Lesion and peritumoral region delineation

Lesions were classified as malignant or inflammatory based on a combination of clinical and imaging information. Malignant lesions were included only if they were confirmed by biopsy and/or clearly identified as malignant on other medical imaging examinations. Inflammatory lesions included conditions such as haemorrhoids, bone fractures, and musculoskeletal inflammation. Lesions that were suspected but not definitively classified were not included in the analysis.

A board-certified nuclear medicine physician segmented malignant and inflammatory lesions on static SUV PET images. Areas of increased FDG uptake relative to the background, corresponding to either malignancy or inflammation, were identified and segmented using a 40% SUVmax threshold in the Siemens Healthineers syngo.via workstation [29, 30]. This threshold defined the volume of interest (VOI) for each lesion.

The resulting VOI masks were exported and applied to both SUV and Patlak parametric images, enabling extraction of quantitative metrics. Each lesion was subsequently expanded by 5 mm, and the resulting external rim was defined as the peritumoral region to assess border behaviour in the two lesion classes.

For all samples, including both intralesional (core) and peritumoral regions (rim), the extracted radiomic features comprised first-order statistics (mean, maximum, variance, skewness, kurtosis, and entropy), and grey-level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM) features (inverse difference moment [IDM], contrast, and joint energy). These 9 radiomic features were calculated for SUV, Ki, Vd, as well as Ki/Vd ratio (i.e., index of the competition between metabolic trapping and membrane washout), for a total of 36 features. Data were stored in CSV format and subsequently analysed.

Data preparation

Data preprocessing involved the exclusion of samples based on the following criteria. To minimise partial-volume effects and ensure adequate signal-to-noise ratio in the parametric images, lesions with a diameter smaller than 15 mm were excluded, corresponding to approximately 200 voxels (voxel size: 1.65 × 1.65 × 3.0 mm). Intestinal lesions were also omitted, as the motion-correction algorithm could not fully compensate for peristaltic motion, potentially compromising the accuracy of kinetic parameters. Liver segments were excluded due to the elevated Vd values observed in this organ for both healthy tissue and malignant lesions in comparison with other segments (Figure S1). This trend was observed in other studies and recently suggested to be modelled using a dual-blood input function to produce more reasonable results [31–33]; these findings were therefore excluded to further observation here and considered to be tackled in a dedicated analysis. The final dataset comprised 68 lesions, classified as malignant (43) or inflammatory (25).

Statistical analysis

Exploratory data analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted in Python (v. 3.10.3). A preliminary exploratory data analysis was performed to characterize the class distribution and descriptive statistics (median and interquartile range). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and differences between malignant and inflammatory lesions were evaluated using the Mann–Whitney U test. Feature interdependence was examined through Pearson correlation analysis. When highly correlated variables were identified (r > 0.80), the least significant feature was iteratively removed to mitigate redundancy. Univariate logistic regression was performed for each feature, and variables with p < 0.10 were included in a multivariate model. In the multivariate logistic regression, all tests were two-sided, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Prior to dimensionality reduction, standard scaler was used to remove intensity differences amongst parameters.

Random forest classifier

A Random Forest Classifier (RFC) was developed and validated to differentiate malignant from inflammatory lesions. Before training the RFC, all features were normalised (‘MinMaxScaler’) and hyperparameter tuning was performed via ‘GridSearchCV’, optimising parameters including the number of estimators, maximum tree depth, minimum samples required to split a node, and minimum samples per leaf.

Model performance was assessed using 5-fold stratified cross-validation, ensuring preservation of the original class distribution across folds and providing an unbiased estimate. 200 independent training and validation iterations were performed, and in each iteration, the dataset was randomly split into balanced training and validation subsets, with 70% of the data used for model training and 30% reserved for validation, ensuring comparable class representation between malignant and inflammatory lesions. Given the class imbalance in the dataset, the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) [34] was applied only to the training data within each cross-validation fold. Finally, the model was evaluated based on the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (ROC AUC).

Exploratory data analysis

A total of 68 samples were included in the analysis following data cleaning, with a class imbalance (63% malignant vs. 37% inflammatory). Above all samples, the SUV distribution was stratified using the 33rd and 66th percentiles (3.14 g/ml and 5.23 g/ml, respectively), yielding three groups of comparable size: low SUV (≤ 3.14 g/ml, n = 22), medium SUV (3.14–5.23 g/ml, n = 22), and high SUV (> 5.23 g/ml, n = 23). Marked differences in class composition were observed across strata. The low-SUV group was predominantly inflammatory (18 inflammatory vs. 4 malignant lesions), the medium-SUV group showed a more balanced distribution (7 inflammatory vs. 15 malignant lesions), and the high-SUV group consisted exclusively of malignant lesions (23/23), resulting in a single-class stratum.

Descriptive statistics (median and interquartile range [IQR]) for all radiomic features are provided in Table S2 (SM). Here, we focus on differences in mean values between the two classes (Table 2). Malignant lesions generally exhibited higher SUV mean values (6.0 g/ml, IQR = 4.2) compared with inflammatory lesions (2.7 g/ml, IQR = 0.8). However, some malignant tumours displayed SUV values as low as those observed in inflammation. A similar pattern was found for parametric images: the mean Ki was 2.0 ml/min/100 ml for malignancies and 0.8 ml/min/100 ml for inflammatory lesions. While malignant lesions tended to show higher SUV and Ki values, these differences did not reach statistical significance. Importantly, not all lesions with SUV greater than 3 g/ml could be confidently classified as malignant (Fig. 1A). Vd demonstrated no specific difference in median values in inflammatory lesions compared to malignant ones (50 vs. 47%), and no clear threshold could be defined to reliably separate the two classes (Fig. 1A).

Overall, these results highlight the diagnostic challenge of differentiating malignant from inflammatory lesions using SUV, Ki, or Vd in isolation. They highlight the need for a more comprehensive, multifactorial approach to improve lesion characterization.

Two-classes strata analysis

Given that no inflammatory lesions were observed above an SUV threshold of 5.2 g/ml, subsequent analyses were restricted to the low- and medium-SUV strata, where malignant and inflammatory lesions coexist (Fig. 1B). This focused approach allowed us to identify radiomic features with the greatest discriminative potential under clinically uncertain conditions. The resulting subset comprised 44 lesions, including 19 malignant and 25 inflammatory samples.

Correlation analysis first revealed strong interdependence among features, especially within SUV- and Ki-derived metrics (r > 0.80), indicating substantial multicollinearity and potential instability in linear models (Figure S2).

Univariate analysis (p < 0.10) identified six candidate features derived from the lesion core—SUV Mean, SUV Contrast, KiVariance, KiEntropy, VdSkewness, and Ki/VdVariance—as well as eight features based on core-to-rim ratios (Table S3). The latter were predominantly related to the Ki /Vd ratio, including Kurtosis, Variance, Entropy, and Mean. No highly correlated features were detected within this reduced subset, allowing them to be considered jointly in subsequent multivariate and Random Forest analyses.

Given the limited size of the minority class (19 malignant lesions), the number of predictors was restricted to three to ensure model stability. The final multivariate logistic regression model therefore included SUV Mean, Ki /VdVariance, and KiEntropy (Table S4). The model converged successfully and demonstrated good overall performance, with a pseudo R² of 0.42 and a statistically significant likelihood ratio test (p = 1.3 × 10⁻⁵). Within the model, SUV Mean and Ki /VdVariance emerged as significant independent predictors (p = 0.05 and p = 0.04, respectively), whereas Ki Entropy did not reach statistical significance. The model achieved an overall accuracy of 0.86. The confusion matrix showed 24 correctly classified inflammatory lesions and 14 correctly classified malignant lesions, with three misclassifications in each class. Performance metrics indicated an imbalance between classes: inflammatory lesions were identified with high recall (0.96), while recall for malignant lesions was lower (0.74), despite comparable precision (0.83 for inflammation and 0.93 for malignancy).

When SUV Mean alone was considered, the univariate logistic regression model also converged successfully and yielded a statistically significant likelihood ratio test (p = 8.3 × 10⁻⁵), but with highly reduced explanatory power (pseudo R² = 0.26). SUV Mean remained a significant predictor of malignancy (p = 0.002), and higher values were associated with increased malignant probability. However, overall classification accuracy decreased to 0.77. The confusion matrix revealed 22 correctly classified inflammatory lesions and 12 correctly classified malignant lesions, with 3 inflammatory and 7 malignant lesions misclassified. In this setting, recall for inflammatory lesions remained high (0.88), whereas recall for malignant lesions dropped substantially (0.63).

Core vs. peritumoral analysis

A core-to-peritumoral pattern analysis revealed several radiomic features with significant differences between malignant lesions and inflammatory findings, highlighting distinct spatial uptake gradients (Fig. 2). SUV Variance showed higher values in malignant lesions (mean 3.18 ± 1.93; median 2.88) compared with inflammatory lesions (mean 2.02 ± 1.72; median 1.44), with a significant Mann–Whitney U test (p = 0.033) and a medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.64). This indicates a steeper gradient from the peritumoral rim toward the lesion core in malignant tissue.

In contrast, Ki Kurtosis was significantly lower in malignant lesions (mean 0.60 ± 0.32; median 0.52) than in inflammatory lesions (mean 0.87 ± 0.31; median 0.88; p = 0.006), with a large effect size (Cohen’s d = − 0.86), suggesting a more uniform net influx rate distribution between core and periphery in malignancy. A similar pattern was observed for Vd Mean, which was lower in malignant lesions (mean 1.21 ± 0.36; median 1.14) than in inflammatory lesions (mean 1.52 ± 0.46; median 1.43; p = 0.015), again indicating reduced core-to-rim contrast in malignancy.

Ratio-based features combining Ki and Vd showed particularly strong discriminatory power. Ki/Vd Mean was higher in malignant lesions (mean 2.68 ± 1.05; median 2.61) than in inflammatory lesions (mean 1.98 ± 1.24; median 1.77; p = 0.014), reflecting a steeper metabolic gradient from periphery to core. Ki/Vd Variance demonstrated the most pronounced difference, with substantially higher values in malignant lesions (mean 4.35 ± 3.36; median 2.95) compared with inflammatory lesions (mean 1.93 ± 1.34; median 1.57; p = 0.006) and a large effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.95), underscoring its strong potential as a discriminative biomarker.

Conversely, Ki/Vd Kurtosis was significantly lower in malignant lesions (mean 0.66 ± 0.38; median 0.55) than in inflammatory lesions (mean 1.12 ± 0.61; median 1.04; p = 0.0026), again consistent with a more homogeneous spatial distribution. Finally, Ki /Vd Entropy was higher in malignant lesions (mean 1.15 ± 0.16; median 1.12) than in inflammatory lesions (mean 1.03 ± 0.18; median 1.01; p = 0.0045), indicating increased heterogeneity concentrated toward the lesion core.

Random forest classification

Three Random Forest classifiers were trained in parallel using distinct feature sets: a SUV-only model, a parametric model incorporating Ki-, Vd-, and Ki/Vd-derived features identified as independent and significant in univariate analysis, and a combined model including all selected significant features. Model performance was evaluated on the validation set using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC AUC), and results were aggregated across iterations to assess robustness and variability.

Across the 200 iterations, the PET-only model achieved an average ROC AUC of 0.83 +/- 0.08 (overfitting 0.11), while the parametric model achieved an average ROC AUC of 0.86 +/- 0.10 (overfitting 0.11). The classification performance was further characterised by precision, recall, and F1-score, averaged over the 200 iterations. The SUV-only model demonstrates a relatively balanced performance across classes, with comparable precision and recall for both inflammation and malignancy. Malignant lesions are identified with a recall of 0.81, indicating good sensitivity, although precision is slightly lower (0.77), reflecting a moderate number of false-positive malignant predictions. Inflammatory lesions show a similar trade-off, with reasonable precision and recall values, resulting in stable F1-scores for both classes. Discussion

The key findings from our exploratory analysis can be summarised as follows:

No statistically significant threshold was identified that could reliably differentiate malignant from inflammatory lesions using a single parameter alone, whether SUV, Ki, or Vd. This indicates that none of these metrics, in isolation, is sufficient to resolve the diagnostic ambiguity between malignancy and inflammation.

Notably, mean values of SUV and Ki were highly correlated, explaining why Ki parametric maps cannot reveal tumours that are not already apparent on SUV images [35–37]. As described by Sadato et al. [10], the linear correlation between SUV and Ki mean values was nonetheless expected.

These findings suggest that, beyond absolute uptake, the relative heterogeneity and spatial distribution of Ki and Vd play a central role in differentiating malignant from inflammatory processes.

Our findings differ from previous studies, reporting only discrimination between malignant and inflammatory lesions using Patlak-derived parameters. For example, Skawran et al. reported a significantly higher Ki,max in malignant targets than in infectious/inflammatory ones (mean 3.0 vs. 2.0 ml/min/100 ml, p = 0.002), although the overlap in Ki and in lesion-to‐background ratios (LBR) was large – e.g. LBR(Ki) was 5.0 vs. 4.4 (p = 0.05) for cancer vs. inflammation [38]. Similarly, Fu et al. found marked differences in Ki in malignant lymph nodes than benign nodes, while Vd showed no significant separation [39]. In these studies, however, analyses primarily focused on global parameter values. In contrast, our approach interrogates the relative contribution and spatial distribution of metabolised and unmetabolised FDG within SUV-defined lesion volumes. This perspective acknowledges that SUV already combines these components, whereas Patlak analysis enables their explicit separation within the same anatomical region.

From a biological standpoint, our findings can be interpreted within a coherent physiological framework. While Vd is less directly interpretable than Ki, it can be regarded as a surrogate of the fraction of free and unphosphorylated FDG retained within the tissue and vasculature. A substantial overlap of Vd values was observed between malignant and inflammatory lesions. This finding is not unexpected from a biological standpoint, as both conditions are characterised by hyperemia and increased vascularization, leading to an enhanced delivery and distribution of unmetabolised FDG within the tissue. Consequently, Vd alone cannot be regarded as a specific marker of malignancy. However, this overlap does not represent a limitation; rather, it highlights the complementary role of Vd within the Patlak framework. While Vd captures tracer delivery and vascular contribution—shared by both inflammation and cancer—Ki reflects irreversible intracellular phosphorylation, which is typically enhanced in malignant tissue. The simultaneous assessment of these components reveals distinct spatial patterns: inflammatory lesions tend to exhibit relatively high and more homogeneous distributions of unphosphorylated FDG, consistent with limited metabolic trapping, whereas malignant lesions show higher Ki values with lower and more spatially heterogeneous Vd, particularly between the lesion core and periphery. This decoupling between tracer distribution and phosphorylation, especially when expressed through ratio-based and heterogeneity features, underpins the improved discrimination achieved by parametric imaging combined with radiomics. While biologically intuitive, this represents, to our knowledge, the first systematic empirical demonstration of such tracer distribution patterns, suggesting a potential new paradigm for image interpretation in oncologic PET.

Some limitations should be acknowledged. The relatively small and imbalanced dataset may limit the generalizability of the present results. We note that 10 of the 43 malignant lesions originated from a single patient. Although the analysis was conducted at the lesion level across different anatomical sites and tumour types, this clustering may have partially overrepresented patient-specific biological characteristics and influenced the distribution of some imaging features. Synthetic oversampling was used to mitigate class imbalance and improved model stability. Nonetheless, these findings should still be interpreted with caution. Our results are further influenced by our inclusion criteria, as they relate only to those imaging feature patterns that are consistent across patients and across different anatomical sites, that share the common biological mechanism of FDG avidity. Therefore, the outcome captures generalizable patterns related to tracer uptake behaviour, rather than focusing on differences driven by specific patients or by tumour or inflammatory subtypes. Future studies involving larger, multi-centre cohorts will be essential to refine model training, improve sensitivity for malignancy detection, and establish robust and generalizable decision thresholds.

As a final remark, we note that this study employed an indirect Patlak reconstruction workflow, primarily to enable frame-wise motion correction, which is not currently implemented in clinical PET workstations performing direct Patlak reconstruction. Nonetheless, previous validation studies demonstrated strong agreement between the pyPatlak implementation used here and commercial direct Patlak software [28], supporting the reliability of the derived kinetic parameters. The authors anticipate that future integration of motion correction into direct Patlak reconstruction workflows may further improve robustness and reduce noise-related variability in parametric imaging.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that short-duration dynamic FDG PET/CT combined with Patlak parametric imaging provides added value over conventional SUV-based assessment for differentiating malignant from inflammatory lesions. While SUV, Ki, and Vd alone showed substantial overlap between lesion types, their combined evaluation—particularly through ratio-based and heterogeneity features—enabled a more physiologically grounded characterization of tracer uptake. Malignant lesions were characterised by enhanced irreversible FDG trapping and steeper core-to-peripheral metabolic gradients, whereas inflammatory processes exhibited more homogeneous distributions of unmetabolised tracer. These findings support the concept that separating tracer delivery from metabolic trapping improves lesion interpretation in equivocal cases. Although further validation in larger, multi-centre cohorts is required, the proposed framework is compatible with routine clinical workflows and holds promise for translation into scalable, parametric imaging–based decision support in oncologic PET.
