Determinants of leprosy among patients visiting Sheshamane comprehensive specialized hospital, West Arsi zone, oromia region, ethiopia: a case-control study
Authors: Kedir Geda Bamedo, Fufa Balcha Tolossa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Leprosy is still a major public health problem in several countries. It is estimated that several million people worldwide suffer from stigma and leprosy-related permanent physical disability. Despite the World Health Organization’s recommended strategy for interrupting transmission, incidence reduction has been minimal in high-burden countries. In the study area, there is a lack of understanding of leprosy transmission risk factors. The study aimed to investigate the determinants of leprosy in patients who visited Sheshamane Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, West Arsi zone, Oromia region. A hospital-based unmatched case-control study was conducted at Sheshamane Comprehensive Specialized Hospital from January to March 2022. Systematic random sampling was used to choose 202 participants (67 cases and 135 controls). An interviewer-administered structured questionnaire was used to gather data. Epi-Info version 7.2 was used to enter the data, and SPSS version 25 was used for analysis. Binary logistic regression analysis is used to identify risk factors for leprosy. P-value < 0.05 and an adjusted odds ratio with a 95% confidence interval were used to determine statistically significant factors. Multivariable analysis showed that a history of contact with a leprosy cases (AOR = 8.92; 95% CI: 3.91, 20.36), Household food shortage experiences (AOR = 2.29; 95% CI: 1.14, 5.42), and lack of latrine (AOR = 2.99; 95%CI: 1.24, 7.25) were significantly associated with an increased risk of having leprosy. Meanwhile, taking BCG (Bacillus Calmette-Guerin) vaccination (AOR = 0.35; 95% CI: 0.14, 0.86) and having good personal hygiene (AOR = 0.40, 95% CI: 0.18, 0.89) were significantly associated with decreasing the risk of developing leprosy. A contact history with leprosy, living in a rural area, household food shortages, and a lack of a latrine at home were all risk factors for leprosy, while good personal hygiene and BCG vaccination decrease the risk of leprosy. Prioritizing interventions to enhance environmental factors such as food security and access to safe water, sanitation, and hygiene in leprosy-endemic areas, along with prompt contact tracing combined with chemoprophylaxis, is recommended.

Background

Leprosy is an infectious disease caused by Mycobacterium leprae (M. leprae) and Mycobacterium lepromatosis, the second known causative agent of Hansen’s disease (leprosy) that causes physical disabilities1. It remains a major public health problem, particularly in low-income countries, including Ethiopia2. Leprosy is one of the 21 neglected tropical diseases (NTDs), and it is estimated that two to three million people worldwide have leprosy-related disabilities3–5. Even though leprosy was declared a public health issue in 2000 (defined as having a prevalence of less than 1 per 10,000 people), both nationally and internationally, new cases are still occurring5. Leprosy can infect people of all ages and genders. 90–95% of the population is immunologically resistant to the bacillus, and the incubation period ranges from 3 to 20 years6. Leprosy is most likely transmitted through droplets from the nose and mouth during prolonged close contact with untreated leprosy patients7.

People with leprosy, often from impoverished backgrounds, face increased poverty due to the disease and experience stigma, hampering diagnosis and societal inclusion, leading to poorer treatment outcomes8. Diagnosis relies on WHO-defined cardinal signs such as skin lesions, nerve enlargement, and positive skin smears. Nerve involvement, lesion count, and bacilli presence determine leprosy classification as paucibacillary (PB) or multibacillary (MB)9.

Approximately 200,000 new cases of leprosy are reported each year, stabilizing the incidence after a previous decline over the past ten years. The recent decrease was attributed to the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic, but there is now a noticeable rebound in new cases, although they have not yet returned to pre-pandemic levels10.

The ongoing rise in new cases poses a significant challenge for national health authorities11. Multidrug therapy (MDT) has been pivotal in treating leprosy, with over 16 million patients globally since its introduction in the 1980s12,13. Early detection and treatment initiation are crucial for preventing disabilities. Following MDT initiation, leprosy patients are no longer infectious. WHO provides free MDT, and since 2018, recommends leprosy chemoprophylaxis with a single dose of rifampicin (SDR) for patient contacts, offering up to 57% protection14–16.

By the end of 2001, Ethiopia had fully integrated leprosy control into general health services, facilitating early diagnosis13.

Leprosy-related disabilities underscore its severity, hindering work opportunities and socioeconomic development while increasing stigma and social isolation3,17. Leprosy persists as a health challenge in low- and middle-income countries like Ethiopia18. The WHO’s Global Leprosy Strategy 2021–2030 targets zero new cases, transmission, and leprosy-related disabilities and discrimination19.

According to the 2021 WHO Weekly Epidemiological Report, global leprosy incidence increased by 8.33% compared to 2020, likely due to improved detection and reporting. In 2021, there were 140,063 new leprosy cases globally, including 8467 (6.05%) with G2D at diagnosis and 8989 (6.4%) newly detected children, indicating ongoing transmission13,20. Ethiopia is one of the 22 leprosy high-burden countries(HBCs), reporting 2589 new cases in 2021, including 307 (11.9%) with G2D and 288 (11.1%) newly detected children1,10,21. Leprosy incidence in Ethiopia varies significantly by region, ranging from 2.4 per 10,000 people in Gambella to 0.1 per 10,000 people in the Somali Region. The number of new cases increased from 3,114 in 2017 to 3,218 in 2018, with Oromia and the Amhara Regional states contributing over 76% of total cases1,22. Most districts in the West Arsi zone are designated as high leprosy burden areas, suggesting targeted prevention and control efforts are needed to understand transmission pathways and direct risk factors21.

Understanding transmission pathways and direct risk factors for leprosy in the study area is crucial for targeted prevention and control efforts17, Various factors such as socio-demographic, socioeconomic status, family history, BCG vaccination status, prior contact with a patient, environmental conditions, and poor water sanitation and hygiene have been associated with leprosy risk23–25. Despite interventions, Ethiopia still faces a high leprosy burden, with control strategies receiving insufficient attention21.

No studies on leprosy risk factors have been conducted in southern Oromia, despite its history as a leprosy treatment center. The risk factors for leprosy transmission in this area, including Sheshamane Comprehensive Specialized Hospital and its surroundings, remain unknown26,27. Variations in socioeconomic status, healthcare coverage, and service quality suggest that leprosy risk factors may differ regionally and over time within the same country. Previous studies in other regions were often limited by cross-sectional designs, leaving gaps in understanding. This unmatched case-control study aims to address these gaps and contribute to understanding potential leprosy risk factors. This study aims to identify leprosy risk factors among Sheshamane Comprehensive Specialized Hospital patients. The findings can inform targeted strategies for early diagnosis, reducing stigma, improving healthcare access, and enhancing public health surveillance to prevent M. leprae transmission in leprosy-endemic areas.

Methods and materials

Study setting

The study was conducted at Sheshamane Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (SCSH), situated 250 km south of Addis Ababa, Ethiopia’s capital. With an estimated population of three million people according to a 2007 survey, the hospital serves 14 rural districts, three town administrations, and houses six district hospitals and 105 health centers in the zone. Initially established in 1951 as a leprosy rehabilitation center, SCSH has evolved to provide a wide range of primary and specialized healthcare services, boasting 267 beds, five outpatient departments, and various healthcare units. Its selection for the study was based on its longstanding history as a leprosy treatment and rehabilitation center, particularly within the West Arsi zone.

Study design and period

A hospital-based unmatched case-control study design was carried out to identify the leprosy risk factors. The study was conducted from January to March 2022.

Population

All leprosy patients who visited public hospitals in the West Arsi zone for MDT treatment were the source population. Whereas, all leprosy patients who visited Sheshamane Comprehensive Specialized Hospital for MDT treatment during the data collection period were the study population.

Case definition

Leprosy patients

All included leprosy patients, aged 18 or older and residing in the West Arsi zone, were newly diagnosed with leprosy and undergoing MDT treatment at the SCSH dermatology outpatient clinic, meeting WHO diagnostic criteria during the data collection period28.

Diagnostic criteria: Presence of one or more of the three cardinal signs of leprosy: thickened pale or reddish skin lesion with definite loss of sensation, enlarged peripheral nerve with or without tenderness, and acid-fast bacilli detected in a slit skin smear28.

Control definition

Control

Controls were individuals aged 18 years or older, residents of the West Arsi zone, newly diagnosed with TB at the SCSH TB clinic on the same day as the cases during the data collection period. They exhibited no signs or symptoms of leprosy or other skin diseases, and these controls were ruled out of leprosy by skin test.

Inclusion criteria for cases and controls

Patients aged 18 years or older, who were newly diagnosed with leprosy and registered at the SCSH dermatology OPD during the data collection period and residents of the West Arsi zone, were included. TB patients who were 18 years or older, residents of the West Arsi zone, and were declared free of leprosy and other skin disease signs and symptoms by a physician on the same day as cases (during the data collection period) were included.

Exclusion criteria for cases and controls

Patients with leprosy who were severely ill or had mental health problems and were unable to communicate, patients who had completed MDT treatment, patients younger than 18 years, and patients who were not residents of the West Arsi zone were excluded from the study. Patients with TB who were severely ill or had mental health problems and were unable to communicate, who had skin diseases or who were previously infected with leprosy, patients under the age of 18, and patients who were not residents of the West Arsi zone were excluded from the study.

Recruitment of cases and controls

Case enrolment

Based on the registration order of the dermatology OPD of SCSH, newly confirmed leprosy cases that fulfilled the inclusion criteria were invited to the study. Those who agreed to participate in the study were then enrolled by systematic random sampling (every second individual was invited from the register to participate in the study).

Control enrolment

A control person was aged ≥ 18 years and visited the SCSH TB clinic for TB treatment from January to March 2022 and met the inclusion criteria for controls. Following the agreement, a systematic random sampling technique was employed (every second individual on the registers was chosen to participate in the study).

Sample size determination

The sample size for an unmatched case-control study was calculated using the double population formula in the Epi-Info version 7.2 software, taking into account a previous study conducted in North Gondar, Ethiopia, which discovered that the most important risk factor for leprosy transmission was poor WASH (water, sanitation, and hygiene)25. With the assumption of a 95% confidence interval (CI), 80% power, a case-to-control ratio of 1:2, and an odds ratio (OR) of 2.6, 20% of controls were exposed, and 39.4% of cases were exposed. After adding a 10% non-response rate, the total sample size was 202 (67 cases and 138 controls).

Sampling technique and procedures

Cases and controls meeting the inclusion criteria were recruited using systematic random sampling from among 1478 patients who visited the SCSH dermatology OPD for skin and other diseases over one year. Among them, 405 cases of leprosy were confirmed. With an average of 34 leprosy cases per month, for the three-month data collection period, 102 cases visited the dermatology OPD. Using a sampling fraction of 1.7, every second case was enrolled for the study from the dermatology OPD registration order during this period.

The estimated number of controls who visited the TB clinic in the 12 months before data collection was 950. With an average of 79.2 TB controls per month, the three-month average was 238. Using a sampling fraction of 1.95, every second member of the control group was selected via systematic random sampling from the new TB case registration order. Additionally, a random number between 1 and 2 determined the selection of the first case and control. Two TB cases were used as controls for each case of leprosy.

Data from clinical and bacteriological examination

Clinical and bacteriological confirmation data of leprosy were obtained from the hospital after the patient was diagnosed.

Study variables

Dependent variable

The dependent variable was defined as equal to one (case) if the study participant was leprosy confirmed and zeros (control) otherwise.

Independent variables

Socio-demographic-related factors

age, sex, educational status, religion, occupation, marital status, and family size.

Socio-economic related factors

food shortage.

Environmental-related factors

residency area, latrine availability, a hand-washing habit with soap, and personal hygiene.

Health determinants related factors

history of previous illness, contact history with known leprosy patients, BCG vaccination status, availability of health facility in residency area, and distance of health facility from residence.

Data collection tool and procedures

Data were collected using a structured interviewer-administered questionnaire, adapted from previous studies30,31. The questionnaire underwent translation into Afan Oromo and back-translation into English for consistency, followed by pre-testing and validation on 5% of the sample size at Gambo Hospital. The main variables included sex, age, residency, education level, socioeconomic status, medical history, contact with leprosy patients, BCG vaccination history, food security, family income, health facility availability, hygiene, and latrine availability. Three BSc nurses and two BSc laboratory technologists underwent comprehensive training on study objectives, sampling techniques, and data collection methods. Daily monitoring of data collection quality was supervised by the primary investigator and a supervisor. Interviews were conducted in private rooms adjacent to the outpatient clinic.

Operational definitions

New leprosy case

a patient with MB or PB leprosy who has never had treatment for leprosy before and is using MDT.

Family size

people in the family who live in the same house as the study participant7.

Food shortage

a period in which a family has reduced (skipped) the number of meals per day. It was considered a food shortage when a participant did not eat three times per day4.

Personal hygiene

defined as the daily self-care that study participants perform to keep their hair, teeth, mouth, skin, hands, and feet clean. Questionnaires were used to collect data, and the scale of measurement was categorical32.

Contact history

an individual who lived with an untreated leprosy person in one house or a neighborhood7.

History of illness

A person who had been diagnosed and required ongoing treatment for more than six months32.

BCG immunization history

defined as all people who have received the BCG vaccine immunization with scars and whose accuracy has been verified7.

Availability of health care

the availability of primary, secondary, or tertiary health care services within nearby of the study participants’ residences32.

Data quality control

Data quality was ensured through the use of a well-designed and pre-tested questionnaire, along with trained data collectors and a supervisor. Before entry into the database, data underwent cross-checking for accuracy and were subsequently entered, coded, cleaned, and checked for completeness, missing values, and outliers using frequency distributions. Standard protocols were followed to ensure quality in laboratory specimen collection and handling, with internal quality control procedures verifying laboratory reagent quality33. Trained laboratory technologists examined each slide, supervised by a senior technologist.

Data processing and analysis

Data was entered into Epi-Info Version 7.2 and exported to SPSS version 25 for analysis. Descriptive statistics, including proportions, means, and standard deviations, summarized the characteristics of cases and controls. Cases were coded as one, while controls were coded as zero. Bivariable and multivariable binary logistic regression models tested the association between dependent and independent variables. Bivariable analysis assessed the strength of the association between potential risk factors and leprosy, while multivariable logistic regression controlled for confounding effects. Factors significant at a P-value less than 0.25 in bivariate analysis were included in the multivariable model. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test examined model fitness, while the multicollinearity diagnostic assessed variable correlation. In the final model, the Hosmer-Lemeshow test P-value = 0.264, which shows it was a good fit, and the VIF for all variables fitted in the final model was VIF < 5, which shows there is no multicollinearity between independent variables. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals evaluated the association magnitude between leprosy risk factors and variables of interest, with a significance cutoff at P < 0.05.

Ethical approval

The study was approved by the institutional review board committee (IRB) of St. Paul’s Hospital Millennium Medical College with Protocol number (PM23/344). The Oromia Regional Health Bureau also gave written permission for the study to be conducted. A statement about the purpose of the study was read to each study participant, and those who agreed to participate signed the informed consent form and were interviewed. To ensure confidentiality, study participants were interviewed in a private room, and their names were not written on the questionnaire. All methods performed in this study are per the relevant guidelines and regulations of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Risk factors associated with leprosy

Socio-demographic characteristics, health determinants, environmental factors, and hygiene factors were analyzed using Bivariable binary logistic regression for the possible association. Then, Sex, residency area, Age category, contact history with leprosy cases, BCG vaccination status, Eat balanced diet, Eat food three times per day, experienced food shortage, nearby availability of health facility, personal hygiene, and household latrine availability were variables that showed a possible association with leprosy infection at a P-value ≤ 0.25 and fit for multivariable logistic regression analysis.

In multivariable logistic regression analysis, having a contact history, having BCG vaccination, practicing personal hygiene, household food shortage experiences, and having no latrines had a statistically significant association with developing leprosy at P-value < 0.05. The study found that Patients who had contact with leprosy patients were 8.92 times more likely to develop leprosy than patients who had no contact with leprosy (AOR = 8.92; 95% CI: 3.91, 20.36). The BCG vaccine was found to be protective against leprosy; those who received BCG vaccination were lowering risk by 65% than non-vaccinated (AOR = 0.35; 95% CI: 0.14, 0.86). Similarly, keeping regular personal hygiene was found to be a protective factor against leprosy by reducing the risk of developing leprosy by 60% for individuals who could / had possibilities for practicing personal hygiene than those who did not have these possibilities (AOR = 0.40, 95% CI: 0.18, 0.89). Food shortage at home was found to be a risk factor for developing leprosy, increasing the risk more than two times (AOR = 2.29; 95% CI: 1.14, 5.42). Households that had no latrines were found to increase the risk of developing leprosy by 1.24 times that of households that had latrines (AOR = 2.99; 95%CI: 1.24, 7.25) (Table 4).

Discussion

This hospital-based case-control study found that leprosy patients face unfavorable environmental, socioeconomic, and health conditions. Key risk factors included contact with leprosy patients, lack of a latrine, and household food shortages. BCG vaccination and proper personal hygiene were protective factors.

The study participants’ socio-demographic characteristics showed that 43.3% of cases were aged 45 to 60 years, while 48.1% of controls fell in the economically active age group of 30 to 44 years. This aligns with previous studies in Ethiopia, ALERT, and Brazil, indicating that 37.7% of the cases were among individuals aged 45 to 60 years17,34. Prolonged incubation periods and extensive social interactions in this age group contribute to the elevated risk of infection7.

Our findings indicate that People who come into contact with someone who has leprosy are at an 8.92 times higher risk of contracting the disease than those who do not. This is in line with other research that found a strong correlation between contact with leprosy cases and an increased risk of contracting the disease, such as findings from Indonesia7,35. Close contacts of leprosy patients, especially household members, face the highest risk of infection, which increases with the patient’s bacillus load. Chemoprophylaxis is recommended for these contacts36.

Another important factor linked to leprosy was BCG vaccination status, which, according to our analysis, reduced disease risk by 65%, exceeding earlier protection rates of 26 to 61%35. Lack of BCG vaccination increases leprosy risk and transmission. The BCG vaccine is vital in reducing morbidity, disability, and stigma, especially in high-burden areas. While WHO recommends BCG at birth, the efficacy of revaccination remains unclear due to mixed findings. Additionally, relying on scar presence to confirm vaccination may be inaccurate, as up to 10% of infants may not develop one15.

Our study found that individuals who experienced household food shortages had over two times the risk of developing leprosy compared to those who did not, consistent with similar research highlighting the association between food shortages and leprosy occurrence37. However, a study in Indonesia found no such association38. Prolonged food shortages can lead to deficiencies in essential nutrients crucial for a robust immune response against infectious agents, thereby increasing the susceptibility to contracting infectious diseases like leprosy.

In this study, personal hygiene has also been identified as an important protective factor against developing leprosy, and a person who was able to maintain personal hygiene was shown to reduce the transmission of leprosy by 60%39. This was consistent with another study that indicated personal hygiene is regarded as one of the most important protective factors against leprosy transmission30. The relationship between hygiene and disease transmission is crucial for health protection and disease control. Further research is required to explore whether this connection is associated with poor hygiene habits or hygiene poverty, where individuals cannot afford essential hygiene items such as soap, menstruation products, and clean water. Access to safe water, sanitation, and hygiene is vital not only for preventing infections and diseases but also for preventing leprosy-related disabilities40,41.

According to this study, leprosy was more likely to infect households without latrines than those with them. This result is consistent with earlier studies carried out in North Gondar and Indonesia, which also showed a strong correlation between a higher incidence of leprosy and the lack of sanitary facilities25,42. This might be due to the absence of latrines may increase exposure to contaminated soil or water, where M. leprae can survive, facilitating transmission, and Unsanitary environments may contribute to indirect transmission by compromising immunity or through contact with contaminated surfaces or soil43.

Our study had several limitations. Firstly, recall bias was present as some information relied on participant memory. Secondly, only patients who visited Sheshamane Comprehensive Specialized Hospital were included, potentially limiting the representativeness of leprosy risk factors. Thirdly, the unmatched case-control design might introduce bias by not accounting for confounding factors. Addressing these limitations requires large-scale studies with diverse samples. Incorporating qualitative research could offer deeper insights into living conditions and socio-economic factors.

Conclusion

The study confirms that those with a leprosy contact history, those experiencing food shortages, and those lacking household latrines face significantly increased leprosy risk. Conversely, BCG vaccination status and personal hygiene serve as significant protective factors against leprosy development. To address leprosy, there is a need for increased political commitment and investment in researching its social determinants. Prioritizing interventions to improve environmental factors like food security and access to safe water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) can enhance health outcomes in leprosy-endemic areas, potentially lowering disease risk. Implementing efficient contact tracing, active case finding, and offering chemoprophylaxis, alongside maintaining BCG vaccination, are crucial strategies for preventing and controlling leprosy.
