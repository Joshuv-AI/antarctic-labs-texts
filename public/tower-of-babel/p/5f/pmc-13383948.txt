Gallic acid-usnic acid complex alleviates necrotic enteritis in broilers by remodeling gut microbiota and strengthening intestinal barrier
Authors: Xunyu Guo, Ziqi Zhao, Shujun He, Zengpeng Lv, Yuming Guo, Wei Nie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the effects of a dietary supplement containing a gallic acid-usnic acid complex (GU) on broilers challenged with necrotic enteritis (NE). A total of 324 one-day-old Arbor Acres broilers were randomly assigned to three groups with 6 replicates of 18 broilers each: (1) basal diet (NC); (2) basal diet + NE challenge (PC); (3) basal diet + NE challenge + 500 mg/kg GU (GU). The NE challenge was induced by oral administration of a coccidial quadrivalent vaccine on day 12, followed by Clostridium perfringens type A on days 17-20. The results showed that GU supplementation significantly improved growth performance, as evidenced by increased body weight and average daily gain (ADG) compared to the PC group at 21, 28, and 42 days of age (P < 0.05). GU effectively inhibited key pathogenic microbes through its antimicrobial and antiparasitic actions, as evidenced by reduced fecal coccidial oocyst shedding and decreased cecal Clostridium perfringens colonization compared to the PC group. It enhanced intestinal barrier function via improved ileal villus structure, increased goblet cell density, and upregulated the expression of the tight junction protein Claudin-1. GU significantly reduced the concentrations of interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) in the ileum and serum (P < 0.05), downregulated the mRNA expression of nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) and myeloid differentiation primary response 88 (MyD88) (P < 0.05). Furthermore, GU modulated the gut microbiota by increasing α-diversity and enriching beneficial bacteria including Cyanobacteria, Bacteroides coprocola, and Parabacteroides merdae. In conclusion, GU alleviates NE in broilers by modulating the gut microbiota, inhibiting pathogenic microbes, strengthening the intestinal barrier, and mitigating inflammation and oxidative stress, thus presenting a promising nutritional strategy for sustainable broiler production.Keywords: Gallic acid, Usnic acid, AA broiler, Necrotic enteritis, Intestinal health

Introduction

Necrotic enteritis (NE), a severe intestinal disease caused by Clostridium perfringens (Gaucher et al., 2015), is highly prevalent in poultry production and causes substantial economic losses to the global poultry industry, with an annual estimate of up to 6 billion US dollars (Li et al., 2017). NE exists in two primary forms: clinical and subclinical. Clinical NE is characterized by a mortality rate of up to 50%, accompanied by intestinal ulcers, erosions, and bloody feces (Alnassan et al., 2014). The subclinical form is often undetected and persistent, posing serious food safety risks and leading to significant economic losses in poultry production (Abd El-Hack et al., 2022).

Intestinal health is critical for the overall health and product quality of poultry. The gut harbors a complex microbial community that symbiotically interacts with the host and exerts a profound influence on poultry health (Feng et al., 2023). Accumulating evidence indicates that intestinal barrier dysfunction is closely associated with the pathogenesis of NE (Lv et al., 2024; Fathima et al., 2022). Several antibiotics have shown significant efficacy in the prevention and control of NE (Agunos et al., 2012). However, with the comprehensive ban on antibiotic growth promoters in feed, the development of green feed additives has become essential for the sustainable development of animal husbandry (Lu et al., 2022). Feed additives such as plant extracts, probiotics, prebiotics, acidifiers, and enzymes have attracted widespread attention due to their safety and functional properties (Hussein et al., 2019). Numerous studies have demonstrated that plant extracts exert positive effects on intestinal health in animals by virtue of their antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and gut-modulating properties (Du et al., 2016). Gallic acid, a natural plant phenolic compound, alleviates inflammation, reduce oxidative stress, and regulates intestinal health in livestock and poultry (Jiang et al., 2022). Usnic acid (lichen acid), one of the most extensively studied lichen secondary metabolites, exhibits diverse bioactivities (Lauterwein et al., 1995) and mitigates NE-induced intestinal impairments in broilers by regulating gut immunity, modulating microbial composition, and enhancing intestinal barrier function (Xu et al., 2025). Both gallic acid and usnic acid improve intestinal health in livestock and poultry through anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and other biological mechanisms. However, no studies have investigated the combined effects of these two compounds, especially in the prevention of NE induced by C. perfringens and coccidia co-infection in broilers.

Therefore, we hypothesized that the gallic acid-usnic acid complex (GU) alleviates NE in broilers by modulating the gut microbiota and reducing inflammation and oxidative stress. To verify this hypothesis, we employed 16S rRNA gene sequencing to characterize the intestinal microbial community, and evaluated the effects of GU on growth performance, antioxidant capacity, intestinal barrier structure and function, and mitochondrial ultrastructure in NE-challenged broilers. The findings of this study are expected to provide novel insights for the prevention and control of NE in broilers.

Materials and methods

The study was approved by China Agricultural University Laboratory Animal Welfare and Animal Experimental Ethical Inspection Form (Issue No: Aw 31015202-1-1).

Animals, diets and experimental design

A total of 324 one-day-old, healthy Arbor Acres (AA) broilers with similar body weights were randomly assigned to one of three treatment groups based on a completely randomized design: (1) the basal diet group (NC); (2) the basal diet + NE challenge group (PC); and (3) the basal diet + NE challenge + 500 mg/kg GU group (GU). Each group consisted of 6 replicates, with 18 broilers per replicate. Broilers were housed in cages. The basal diet was formulated according to the (NY/T 33-2004), and its composition and nutritional concentrations are detailed in Table 1. The gallic acid-usnic acid complex (GU) used in this experiment was provided by Shengtaier Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). The complex contained 1.3% gallic acid and 3.2% usnic acid (w/w), with gallic acid extracted from Cirsium setosum and usnic acid extracted from the lichen Usnea. GU was supplemented into the diet at the designated concentration of 500 mg/kg. The dose of 500 mg/kg was selected based on our preliminary dose‑response trials (300–500 mg/kg). To ensure uniform distribution, the GU complex was first thoroughly mixed with the vitamin and mineral premixes using a stepwise dilution method to create a concentrated premix. This premix was then blended with the remaining basal diet ingredients (corn, soybean meal, etc.) in a horizontal ribbon mixer for 15 minutes to achieve the final concentration of 500 mg/kg GU in the complete feed. Throughout the trial period (days 1 to 42), broilers had ad libitum access to feed and water. All husbandry practices and vaccination protocols followed standard operating procedures. The experiment lasted for 42 d, including 1 to 21 d in the first period and 22 to 42 d in the second period.

NE challenge

NE was induced in broilers according to the method described by Wu et al. (2018). Following a 12-h feed withdrawal, on the morning of day 12, broilers in the PC and GU groups received an oral challenge with a 20×dose of a coccidial quadrivalent vaccine (Foshan Standard Bio-Technology Co., Ltd., China), which contained Eimeria acervulina, E. maxima, E. tenella, and E. necatrix. Subsequently, from days 17 to 20, these broilers were orally administered 1 mL of a freshly prepared suspension of Clostridium perfringens type A (10⁸−10⁹ CFU/mL) daily for four consecutive days. In contrast, broilers in the NC group received 1 mL of sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) orally on day 12 and were orally administered 1 mL of sterile reinforced clostridium medium (Qingdao Haibo Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) daily from days 17 to 20.

Sample collection

On days 21 and 28, from each replicate, one broiler with a body weight nearest to the mean was randomly selected. Blood was collected from the wing vein into vacuum tubes and centrifuged at 1006 ×g. The supernatant (serum) was collected and stored at −20°C. Broilers were subsequently euthanized via exsanguination from the jugular vein. Following laparotomy, intestinal segments were separated and lesion scores were recorded for each segment. Ileal tissues collected from 28-day-old broilers were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde for transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis of mitochondrial ultrastructure, and in 4% paraformaldehyde for assessment of intestinal morphology and histopathology. Additionally, cecal digesta collected on day 28 was placed into sterile centrifuge tubes and stored at −80°C for subsequent microbial analysis.

Growth performance

The average body weight of broilers per replicate and the initial feed weight were recorded on day 1. On days 21, 28, and 42, all broilers were fasted for 6 hours. Body weight and residual feed weight were then measured per replicate. Feed conversion ratio (FCR), average daily feed intake (ADFI), and average body weight gain (BWG) were calculated for 1-21 d, 22-42 d, and the entire trial period (1-42 d). Throughout the experiment, broiler health status was monitored daily. Mortality was recorded and used to calculate corrected feed intake.

Coccidia oocysts count in feces

Fecal coccidial oocyst counts were determined on days 21 and 42 post-infection. Fresh fecal samples were collected per replicate and thoroughly homogenized. Oocyst counts were performed using the McMaster counting technique and expressed as oocysts per gram of feces (OPG), following the method described by Xu et al. (2023a). The specific methods are as follows:

We placed approximately 2 g of fresh feces into a conical flask. We gradually added 60 mL of saturated sodium chloride solution to the feces in multiple aliquots and mixed the suspension thoroughly. We then aspirated the supernatant fecal suspension and charged the chambers of a McMaster counting slide (manufactured by Shanghai Veterinary Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences). After allowing the slide to stand for 5 minutes, we examined it under a light microscope (Leica DM 750, Shanghai, China) and counted the oocysts. During counting, we included oocysts touching the left and upper grid lines while excluding those touching the right and lower lines. We calculated the final oocyst count by multiplying the average number of oocysts in both chambers by 200 and the dilution factor.

Clostridium perfringens count in the cecum

We quantified Clostridium perfringens (C. perfringens) in cecal digesta from broilers on days 21 and 28 according to the method of Wu et al. (2018). We accurately weighed 0.2 g of cecal digesta and placed it into a 5 mL sterile centrifuge tube. Then added 1.8 mL of pre-chilled sterile saline solution (0.85% NaCl) to achieve a 1:9 (w/v) sample-to-saline ratio. We vortex-mixed the suspension thoroughly. We then performed serial tenfold dilutions from 10 to 1 to 10–5. Inoculated plates were immediately transferred to a 37°C anaerobic chamber and incubated for 24 hours prior to colony enumeration. We expressed the results as colony-forming units per gram of digesta (CFU/g) after log₁₀ transformation, with the final unit reported as log10 CFU/g (lgCFU/g).

Intestinal lesion scoring

Scoring was based on the severity of intestinal pathology, with lesions graded on a scale from 0 to 4 to enable statistical analysis as numerical data (Dahiya et al., 2005), where: Score 0 indicates no intestinal lesions, an intact intestinal wall structure, normal color, and no abnormalities; Score 0.5 indicates severe congestion of the serosa and mesentery without significant compromise of the intestinal wall structure; Score 1 indicates thinning and fragility of the intestinal wall with small red petechiae (>5 petechiae) but no necrosis; Score 2 indicates the presence of focal necrotic foci with relatively small necrotic areas; Score 3 indicates the presence of patchy necrosis, typically involving 1-2 necrotic plaques (approximately 1-2 cm in length); and Score 4 indicates the presence of typical, extensive diffuse necrotic areas with severely compromised intestinal function. This quantitative representation allows for a more precise evaluation of the graded severity continuum, rather than simply categorizing lesions as "present" or "absent," and facilitates meaningful comparisons of lesion severity across different experimental groups.

Serum inflammatory cytokines and hepatic function parameters

Serum samples were analyzed using commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits according to the manufacturers' protocols. Specifically, chicken interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) kits were obtained from Jiangsu Enzyme-linked Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China). The interleukin-6 (IL-6) kit was sourced from Shanghai Enzyme-linked Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China). Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) kits were purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China). Throughout the handling process, serum samples were maintained on ice. All kits were equilibrated at room temperature for 30 minutes prior to use. All procedures strictly followed the respective kit instructions, and all assays were performed as single measurements per sample.

Ileal morphometric analysis and goblet cell quantification

Ileal tissue collected from broilers at 28 days of age was immediately fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution. Following fixation, tissues were embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and Alcian Blue-Periodic Acid Schiff (AB-PAS) staining. Stained sections were examined under a light microscope (Leica DM 750, Shanghai, China). Representative fields of view displaying clear ileal tissue structures were identified by adjusting the magnification, and images were captured. For each sample, 10 villi and their associated crypts were randomly selected for measurement. Morphometric analyses included measurements of ileal villus height (VH), villus width (WD), crypt depth (CD), and intestinal wall thickness. The villus height to crypt depth ratio (VH/CD) was calculated. Additionally, the number of goblet cells per 100 μm of villus length was quantified. Histopathological assessment was performed to analyze intestinal lesions. The analyses were performed at 28 days of age, which is one week after the final C. perfringens challenge (day 20), allowing for the assessment of intestinal recovery and the sustained effects of GU treatment.

Reactive oxygen species and endotoxin concentrations

The Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) ELISA Kit and Endotoxin ELISA Kit (Jiangsu Meimian Industrial Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China) were removed from refrigeration in advance and equilibrated at room temperature for 30 minutes to ensure reagent activity and stability. Previously stored ileal tissue, cecal tissue, and serum samples were retrieved from the −80°C freezer and immediately placed on ice. ROS concentrations in ileal tissue were determined using the ROS ELISA Kit, while lipopolysaccharide (LPS) content in cecal tissue and serum was measured using the Endotoxin ELISA Kit. All procedures were performed strictly according to the manufacturers' protocols. All assays were performed as single measurements per sample.

Gene expression of intestinal immunity and tight junction markers

Prior to analysis, RNAiso Plus reagent (Takara, Tokyo, Japan), PrimeScript™ RT reagent kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara, Tokyo, Japan) and TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ kit (Takara, Tokyo, Japan) were equilibrated to room temperature. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed on ileum content to assess expression concentrations of genes associated with: (1) inflammatory mediators (TNF-α; IL-1β); (2) inflammatory signaling pathways (Toll-like receptor 4, TLR-4; Myeloid Differentiation Primary Response Protein 88, MyD88; Nuclear Factor Kappa-B, NF-κB); and (3) intestinal tight junctions (Claudin-1; Occludin; zonula occludens-1, ZO-1). Total RNA was extracted from broiler cecal samples (day 28) using RNAiso Plus reagent according to the manufacturer’s protocol, followed by quantification of RNA purity and concentration. Following the manufacturer's protocol for the PrimeScript™ RT reagent kit with gDNA Eraser, extracted RNA was combined with reaction reagents to synthesize cDNA through reverse transcription. Synthesized cDNA was combined with TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ kit and gene-specific primers for qRT-PCR analysis on an Applied Biosystems 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System. All procedures strictly adhered to kit specifications. All assays were performed as single measurements per sample. The β-actin gene served as the endogenous control, and relative mRNA expression concentrations were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are detailed in Table 2.

Mitochondrial ultrastructural examination

Ileal samples were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution. Following fixation, specimens underwent dehydration and embedding procedures. Ultrathin sections were prepared according to standard protocols by Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). The sections were subsequently examined under a transmission electron microscope (Hitachi TEM system). Mitochondrial ultrastructure was imaged at varying magnifications (5.0 k×, 15.0 k×, and 40.0 k×), with electron micrographs randomly acquired for analysis.

16S rRNA sequencing for microbiota analysis

Total genomic DNA was extracted from cecal digesta of 28-day-old broilers. The purity and concentration of the DNA were assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis to ensure quality for downstream applications. Barcoded specific primers targeting the sequencing regions were designed. Using diluted genomic DNA as template, PCR amplification was performed with Phusion® High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix with GC Buffer and high-fidelity DNA polymerase (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) to ensure amplification efficiency and accuracy. PCR products were verified by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis, followed by purification using magnetic beads to remove impurities and non-specific products. Samples were quantified fluorometrically and pooled in equimolar amounts. And were re-evaluated by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis to confirm uniform quality and mixing. Libraries were constructed using the TruSeq® DNA PCR-Free Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). After passing quality control assessments via Qubit fluorometer and quantitative PCR (Q-PCR), libraries underwent high-throughput sequencing on the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina) to generate high-quality sequencing data.

Sample data were demultiplexed from the raw sequencing output based on barcode and PCR primer sequences, followed by removal of barcode and primer regions. Quality control of demultiplexed reads was performed using fastp (v0.22.0) to filter high-quality reads. Subsequently, FLASH (v1.2.11) was employed to assemble high-quality reads, generating optimized Tags. These Tags were aligned against a reference database using vsearch (v2.22.1) to detect and filter out chimeric sequences, yielding final effective data. The validated data were analyzed and visualized through the Majorbio Cloud Platform to characterize microbial community structure and diversity.

Statistical analysis

All data in this trial were processed using SPSS statistical software (version 20.0). A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) procedure was applied to analyze the data, followed by Duncan's multiple-range test for significant differences. Differences were considered significant at P < 0.05. Data visualization was performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0).

Results

Growth performance

The effects of GU on the growth performance of AA broilers are presented in Table 3. Broilers in the GU group had significantly higher average body weights at 21, 28, and 42 d of age compared with the PC group (P < 0.05). During the period of 1-21 d, ADG in the GU group was significantly higher than that in both the NC and PC groups, and FCR was significantly lower than that in the PC group (P < 0.05). From 22 to 42 d, dietary GU supplementation significantly improved ADG in broilers compared with both the PC and NC groups (P < 0.05). Over the entire trial period (1-42 d), ADG in the GU group was significantly higher than that in the PC group (P < 0.05).

Intestinal lesion scoring

The intestinal lesion scores of broilers are presented in Table 5. The NE challenge in this study induced relatively mild intestinal lesions, with scores generally below 1.1 on a 0-4 scale, which is characteristic of a subclinical NE model (Van der Klein et al., 2024). Despite this low severity, NE challenge numerically increased intestinal lesion scores at all ages compared with the unchallenged NC group, and dietary GU supplementation reduced these lesion scores. Specifically, at 21 d, the duodenal, jejunal, ileal, and cecal lesion scores in the PC group were significantly higher than those in both the NC and GU groups (P < 0.05), and the lesion scores in the GU group were significantly lower than those in the PC group (P < 0.05). At 28 and 42 d, no significant differences were observed among the three groups, suggesting that intestinal damage was most pronounced during the acute phase of infection (around 21 d) and that GU exerted its protective effects primarily during this critical period. Collectively, GU supplementation ameliorated the severity of intestinal lesions and improved intestinal health in NE-challenged broilers.

Serum biochemistry and inflammatory factors

As shown in Table 6, compared with the PC group, broilers in the GU group had a highly significant reduction in serum IL-1β concentrations (P < 0.01) and a significant decrease in serum TNF-α concentrations (P < 0.05), indicating that GU supplementation effectively attenuated the systemic inflammatory response in NE-challenged broilers. NE challenge caused highly significant elevations in serum AST and ALT activities in the PC group compared with the NC group (P < 0.01). GU supplementation significantly reduced serum AST activity (P < 0.05) compared with the PC group, suggesting that GU alleviates inflammatory progression and ameliorates hepatocellular damage induced by NE. In addition, the GU group had a highly significant reduction in ileal IL-1β and IL-6 concentrations (P < 0.01) and a significant decrease in serum IL-6 concentrations (P < 0.05) compared with the PC group.

Discussion

NE represents a major cause of economic losses in the global poultry industry, posing a significant challenge to intestinal health. It is particularly important to find a novel plant extract to alleviate the impact of NE on broilers. Samuel reported that dietary supplementation with gallic acid (75-100 mg/kg) enhanced feed efficiency during the overall production cycle (1-42 d), findings consistent with our results (Samuel et al., 2017). Specifically, our study demonstrated that GU supplementation significantly increased average body weights at 21, 28, and 42 d compared to NE-infected broilers without intervention (PC). These improvements may be attributed to GU's capacity to promote intestinal development, enhance barrier function, increase nutrient digestion and absorption efficiency, mitigate inflammation and oxidative stress induced by co-infection with Coccidia and Clostridium perfringens, and modulate gut microbiota composition. Collectively, GU exerts beneficial effects on growth performance in broilers under both healthy and diseased conditions.

This study demonstrates the combined inhibitory efficacy of usnic acid and gallic acid against Coccidia and Clostridium perfringens—the two primary pathogens implicated in NE. Co-infection with these pathogens represents the most established model for inducing experimental NE (Dierick et al., 2021). Prior research indicates that usnic acid may suppress coccidial activity through disruption of tocopherol biosynthesis (Balanco et al., 2019), interference with syncytium-to-kinete transition, and impairment of pre-flagellar structures (Da Luz et al., 2015), while concurrently exhibiting inhibitory properties against C. perfringens. Samuel et al. (2017) reported 75-100 mg/kg gallic acid's direct anti-parasitic efficacy in reducing oocyst shedding in infected chickens. Findings consistent with our results when 500 mg/kg of a gallic acid/usinic acid complex was fed. Furthermore, GU supplementation induced a transient reduction in cecal C. perfringens colonization at 21 d and profound suppression at 28 d, confirming the compound's marked anti-clostridial efficacy. These collective findings validate the combinatorial efficacy of the gallic acid-usnic acid complex (GU) in countering coccidial invasion and restricting pathogenic bacterial proliferation, highlighting its prophylactic potential against NE pathogenesis.

The intestine serves as the central organ for nutrient absorption in poultry and concurrently functions as a critical defense barrier (Gao et al., 2025). Its multilayered barrier systems operate synergistically to effectively prevent invasion by pathogens and harmful substances, thereby safeguarding intestinal and systemic health (Fathima et al., 2022). Multiple studies have documented that dietary supplementation with various plant extracts significantly reduces intestinal lesion scores in broilers, with reported decreases from>2.0 in control groups to<1.0 in treatment groups (Zhao et al., 2013). In the present trial, GU complex supplementation significantly reduced intestinal lesion scores in 21d broilers compared to the PC. This indicates a beneficial effect of GU on restoring intestinal function and health, likely attributable to its anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and gut-barrier enhancing properties. These findings are substantiated by numerous previous reports demonstrating the positive effects of gallic acid or usnic acid on maintaining intestinal health (Xu et al., 2025; Deng et al., 2022).

Hepatic dysfunction represents a key extraintestinal manifestation of NE (Gao et, al., 2024b). This occurs because NE compromises intestinal barrier integrity, facilitating the infiltration of toxic substances from the intestinal lumen. Gut-derived endotoxins are established as critical mediators of liver injury (Xia et al., 2021). When hepatocytes undergo pathological damage—such as inflammatory infiltration or necrotic changes-disruption of cell membrane integrity leads to leakage of intracellular enzymes. This is clinically reflected in elevated serum concentrations of AST and ALT (Gu et al., 2024). In our trial, NE challenge induced highly significantly increases in AST and ALT activities in the PC group versus controls, indicating substantial hepatocellular damage where enzyme release resulted from inflammatory or necrotic processes. GU supplementation significantly attenuated AST elevation compared to the PC, demonstrating its capacity to mitigate inflammatory responses and ameliorate the severity of hepatocellular injury. This hepatoprotective effect may be attributed to GU's ability to preserve hepatocyte membrane integrity, reduce oxidative stress, and inhibit pro-inflammatory cytokine production. Supporting evidence includes gallic acid's documented inhibition of caspase-9 activity to reduce apoptosis (Rahimifard et al., 2020) and its potent hepatoprotective effects in acetaminophen-induced liver injury models (Rasool et al., 2010). These collective findings substantiate the gallic acid component's cytoprotective properties and highlight GU's therapeutic potential in alleviating NE-associated hepatic damage.

The interplay between inflammatory responses and oxidative stress plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of necrotic enteritis. Following NE infection, broilers experience compromised immune function and significantly diminished antioxidant capacity (Tang et al., 2022). Mitochondria, serving as cellular powerhouses, constitute the primary site of ROS generation. Their structural integrity is intrinsically linked to systemic oxidative stress and inflammation (Hirose et al., 2023). Mitochondrial damage triggers the release of oxidized and fragmented mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) into the cytosol. This mtDNA subsequently enters the circulation and initiates inflammatory cascades (Lv et al., 2024). The ensuing exacerbation of oxidative stress and inflammation damages mitochondrial structure, prompting mitochondria to generate increased concentrations of ROS. These elevated ROS concentrations further inflict damage on mitochondria, establishing a vicious cycle (Lv et al., 2024). Additionally, ROS directly compromise intestinal epithelial cells, disrupting barrier function and facilitating bacterial/endotoxin translocation, thereby intensifying intestinal inflammation. Consequently, mitigating mitochondrial damage and preserving functional integrity effectively alleviates oxidative injury in animals (Jin et al., 2025). In this study, dietary GU supplementation induced a numerical reduction in intestinal ROS concentrations compared to the PC, accompanied by restoration of normal mitochondrial morphology. These findings demonstrate GU's efficacy in attenuating oxidative stress and ameliorating mitochondrial damage in the ileal tissues of NE-challenged broilers.

The intestinal mucosal barrier, comprising physical, chemical, immune, and microbial components, constitutes a critical defense against luminal pathogens (Wu et al., 2021). The physical barrier primarily consists of intestinal epithelial cells interconnected by tight junctions (Zhang et al., 2024). Villus height and crypt depth serve as key morphological indicators of intestinal digestive-absorptive capacity in broilers (Jayaraman et al., 2013). Post-challenge, our study observed significantly reduced ileal VH, increased CD, and a profoundly diminished VH/CD ratio, indicating severe intestinal damage. GU supplementation markedly reversed these pathological alterations, demonstrating its ability to enhance intestinal development. These findings align with Samuel et al. in which gallic acid improved jejunal morphology by reducing CD and increasing VH/CD (Samuel et al., 2017) and Xu et al. (2025) where usnic acid promoted villus elongation and VH/CD optimization. Intestinal barrier dysfunction correlates with inflammatory bowel disease pathogenesis, characterized by structural and functional tight junction abnormalities (Turner et al., 2009). Consistent with Xu et al.'s observations in NE-infected broilers (Xu et al., 2023b), the PC group exhibited significant downregulation of Claudin-1 and ZO-1 gene expression versus NC. Crucially, GU supplementation significantly upregulated Claudin-1 expression, indicating its capacity to restore tight junction integrity. Goblet cells—specialized secretory epithelial cells—establish chemical barriers through mucin and antimicrobial protein secretion, preventing direct pathogen-epithelial contact (Peterson et al., 2014). Increased goblet cell density enhances mucosal protection and intestinal homeostasis (Duangnumsawang et al., 2021). The increased goblet cell density observed in the GU group suggests a potential for enhanced mucin production and mucosal protection, which are critical for maintaining the chemical barrier. This finding, combined with the improved mucosal and villous integrity, supports the role of GU in reinforcing intestinal barrier defenses.

NE compromises intestinal barrier integrity and increases permeability in broilers. Consequently, inflammatory markers in serum and intestinal mucosa serve as established indicators of inflammatory severity (Liu et al., 2018). IL-1β and TNF-α are key pro-inflammatory cytokines whose reduced concentrations signify attenuated inflammation. This study demonstrated highly significantly reductions in serum IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α concentrations in GU-treated broilers compared to the PC, indicating potent anti-inflammatory properties of the GU complex and its therapeutic efficacy against NE. NF-κB, a master transcriptional regulator of inflammation, becomes activated via the MyD88 adaptor protein in Toll-like receptor (TLR) signaling pathways. Significantly downregulated expression of NF-κB and MyD88 in the GU group further suggests that GU mitigates intestinal inflammation and barrier dysfunction by suppressing inflammatory pathway activation. LPS, an immunodominant component of Gram-negative bacterial membranes, potently triggers host immune responses (Moraes et al., 2016). Studies have demonstrated that broilers infected with NE exhibit significantly elevated serum concentrations of LPS and a marked upregulation in the expression of genes associated with pro-inflammatory cytokines within the intestine (Tang et al., 2022). Our findings are in alignment: cecal and serum LPS concentrations were markedly elevated in the PC group, confirming barrier impairment. Crucially, GU supplementation significantly reduced serum LPS, demonstrating its capacity to reinforce immune barrier function, inhibit LPS translocation, and attenuate systemic inflammation.

Gut microbiota enhances animal performance by facilitating nutrient absorption and supporting immune function (Hirose et al., 2023; Gao et al., 2024a). Disruption of microbial communities—manifested as dysbiosis or population imbalances—predisposes poultry to digestive disorders, reduced growth performance, and increased disease susceptibility (Polansky et al., 2015). Firmicutes, Bacteroidota, and Proteobacteria constitute the dominant phyla in avian intestines (Zhou et al., 2021). Our study observed significantly reduced α-diversity in NE-infected broilers, likely attributable to gut homeostasis disruption promoting pathogenic colonization—consistent with multiple reports (Song et al., 2022, 2023; Xu et al., 2023c). Crucially, GU supplementation significantly restored microbial α-diversity, indicating its capacity to reverse NE-induced microbial segregation and modulate broiler gut microbiota. Bacteroides possesses enzymatic machinery for digesting complex carbohydrates (cellulose and resistant starch), generating nutrients for host and co-microbiota. Its key metabolites propionate and butyrate regulate inflammatory responses and gut homeostasis (Joat et al., 2023). Thus, increased Bacteroides abundance benefits intestinal equilibrium and immune balance. Our data revealed significant Bacteroides depletion post-challenge, while GU supplementation effectively restored its prevalence—demonstrating GU's efficacy in promoting microbial ecosystem health.

LEfSe analysis revealed significant enrichment of Cyanobacteria, Bacteroides coprocola, Rhodospirillales, and Parabacteroides merdae in the GU group. Cyanobacteria, photosynthetic bacteria, synthesize organic compounds through photosynthesis, potentially enhancing gut microbial diversity and barrier function. Their proposed mechanisms include modulating host immune responses and metabolism via short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production (García-Sanmartín et al., 2022). Bacteroides coprocola and Parabacteroides merdae represent beneficial poultry gut commensals (Kollarcikova et al., 2020), and these two bacterial species ferment organic substrates to generate SCFAs and other nutrients, thereby supporting energy metabolism and maintaining microbial equilibrium (Murphy et al., 2010). Certain Parabacteroides species further stabilize the gut ecosystem by inhibiting pathogens and promoting beneficial bacteria proliferation (Liu et al., 2022). Rhodospirillales bacteria exhibit phototrophic capabilities, utilizing light energy and organic matter for metabolism. Within the gut, they facilitate organic matter decomposition, enhance nutrient absorption, and likely influence host health through bioactive metabolites (Villena et al., 2023). Collectively, these data indicate that GU modulates avian immunity and metabolism by enriching functional microbial populations-including SCFA producers, bile acid metabolizers, and phototrophic bacteria. This restructuring enhances intestinal barrier integrity, ameliorates gut health, and counteracts necrotic enteritis pathogenesis. Our findings demonstrate GU's efficacy in restoring microbial diversity and rebalancing the gut ecosystem.

Conclusions

This study reveals the protective effects of GU against intestinal damage in broilers with NE. Dietary supplementation with 500 mg/kg GU alleviates NE pathogenesis by modulating gut microbiota balance, inhibiting pathogen proliferation, enhancing anti-inflammatory and antioxidant capacities, strengthening intestinal barrier function, and improving mitochondrial structure and function.
