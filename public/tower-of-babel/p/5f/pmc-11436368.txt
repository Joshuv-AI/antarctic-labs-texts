Efficacy and safety of bosutinib in previously treated patients with chronic myeloid leukemia: final results from the BYOND trial
Authors: Carlo Gambacorti-Passerini, Tim H Brümmendorf, Elisabetta Abruzzese, Kevin R Kelly, Vivian G Oehler, Valentín García-Gutiérrez, Henrik Hjorth-Hansen, Thomas Ernst, Eric Leip, Simon Purcell, Gerald Luscan, Andrea Viqueira, Francis J Giles, Andreas Hochhaus
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis final analysis from the phase 4 BYOND trial reports outcomes with bosutinib in patients with previously treated chronic myeloid leukemia (CML); 163 patients with CML resistant/intolerant to previous tyrosine kinase inhibitors received bosutinib (starting dose: 500 mg QD). At study completion (median follow-up, 47.8 months), 48.1% (n = 75/156) of patients with Philadelphia chromosome–positive chronic phase CML were still receiving treatment. Among evaluable patients, 71.8% (95% CI, 63.9–78.9) and 59.7% (95% CI, 51.4–67.7) attained or maintained major molecular response (MMR) and molecular response (MR)4, respectively, at any time on treatment. The majority of patients achieved a deeper molecular response relative to baseline while on bosutinib. Kaplan-Meier probabilities (95% CI) of maintaining MMR and MR4 at 36 months were 87.2% (78.0–92.7) and 80.7% (69.4–88.1), respectively. At 48 months, the Kaplan-Meier overall survival rate was 88.3% (95% CI, 81.8–92.6); there were 17 deaths, including 2 that were considered CML related. Long-term adverse events (AEs) were consistent with the known safety profile of bosutinib, and no new safety issues were identified. The management of AEs through dose reduction maintained efficacy while improving tolerability. These results support the use of bosutinib in patients with previously treated CML.ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT02228382Subject terms: Chronic myeloid leukaemia, Phase IV trials

Introduction

Bosutinib is an orally bioavailable, potent, multi-targeted, dual Src-Abl tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) that has been approved for patients with Ph-positive (Ph+) chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) that is resistant or intolerant to prior therapy and for patients with newly diagnosed Ph+ chronic phase (CP) CML [1]. Approval of bosutinib 500 mg once daily (QD) for patients with CML previously treated with ≥1 TKI was based on the results from a phase 1/2 study that demonstrated durable efficacy and a tolerable safety profile in this patient population [2, 3]. The phase 4 BYOND trial further evaluated the efficacy and safety of bosutinib in patients with previously treated CML [4]. Initial results of BYOND, which began in 2014, found that response rates and depth of response were better than those reported in the initial phase 1/2 study, which began in 2006 when fewer therapy options were available for patients with CML [2–4]. In recent years, treatment options as well as knowledge of treatment management have increased, contributing to improved outcomes for patients currently starting treatment after ≥1 prior TKI. The aim of the present analyses was to further investigate outcomes with bosutinib in patients with CP CML, as well as to assess the effect of therapy management with dose reductions on safety and efficacy. Final results from BYOND after ≥3 years of follow-up are reported.

Methods

Study design and patients

BYOND (ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT02228382) was a single-arm, open label, non-randomized phase 4 study of bosutinib in patients with chronic or advanced Ph+ CML who had failed prior treatment with TKIs. The complete methods for the study have been published previously [4].

In short, patients aged ≥18 years with CML whose disease had previously failed prior treatment with commercially available TKIs due to resistance or intolerance, or who were otherwise not candidates for treatment with commercially available TKIs, received bosutinib 500 mg QD. Dose escalation to a maximum of 600 mg QD was permitted in cases of an unsatisfactory response or signs of disease progression in the absence of any grade 3/4 or persistent grade 2 adverse events (AEs). Dose reduction to 400, 300, or 200 mg QD due to toxicity/tolerability was permitted.

On-study bosutinib treatment was continued for up to 4 years (208 weeks) from the time of first dose, or until disease progression, unacceptable toxicity, withdrawal of consent, death, or study discontinuation. Patients completing at least 4 years of bosutinib with continued benefit could be switched to commercially available therapy after 4 years. Patients who discontinued bosutinib prior to completing 4 years of therapy were followed for survival until they completed 4 years on study or study termination. This final analysis was based on the final database lock, after ≥3 years of follow-up.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients provided written informed consent before study procedures began, and the protocol was approved by institutional review boards at each study site.

Efficacy and safety assessments

Full details of efficacy evaluations have been described previously[4]. Analyses of cytogenetic response were based on data from local laboratory assessments, whereas MR and mutational analysis were assessed at an independent central laboratory. Mutational analysis was to be performed at baseline, in case of treatment failure or disease progression, and at the end of treatment; however, mutational analysis was not performed when BCR::ABL1 levels were considered too low (<1%). Safety evaluations and AEs of special interest clusters have also been defined [4].

The primary endpoints of the BYOND study have also been reported previously [4]. These included cumulative confirmed major cytogenetic response (MCyR) by 1 year in patients with Ph+ CP CML treated with one or two prior TKIs and with three prior TKIs, and cumulative confirmed overall hematologic response by 1 year in patients with accelerated phase (AP) or blast phase (BP) CML. Secondary and exploratory endpoints included cumulative response rates, duration of response, on-treatment transformation to AP or BP CML, progression-free survival (PFS), overall survival (OS), newly observed BCR::ABL1 mutations, and safety. Post hoc analyses included efficacy and safety in patients who dose reduced (without further reduction) to 400, 300, and 200 mg QD.

Statistical analysis

All bosutinib-treated patients with Ph+ CML with a valid baseline efficacy assessment for the respective endpoint (evaluable population) were included in the cytogenetic and molecular efficacy analyses. Per the study protocol, complete cytogenic response (CCyR) was imputed on any date where major MR (MMR) was achieved, and no valid cytogenetic assessment was available. The impact of dose reduction on efficacy was assessed by evaluating response >6 months after the first dose reduction to 400, 300, and 200 mg, respectively. Patients were counted as responders if they had an assessment demonstrating response >6 months after the dose reduction.

Time-to-event endpoints, except for duration of response (DOR) and OS, were estimated using cumulative incidence, adjusting for the competing risk of treatment discontinuation without the event (PFS analyzed as time to progression or death). DOR and OS were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method.

Duration of response for CCyR and MR was defined as the time from first response to confirmed loss, progression of disease as assessed by the investigator, or on-treatment death due to any cause. For CCyR, confirmed loss was defined as 2 consecutive assessments with >0 Ph+ metaphases at least 28 days apart. For MMR, confirmed loss was defined as 2 consecutive assessments at least 28 days apart with a <3-log (>0.1%) reduction in transcripts from standardized baseline, one of which with a ≤2-log reduction (≥1%). Loss of MR4 was defined similarly to loss of MMR but required <4-log (>0.01%) reduction in transcripts from standardized baseline, one of which with a ≤3-log reduction (≥0.1%). Patients without confirmed loss, progression, or death were censored at the last valid assessment where response could be assessed.

PFS was defined as the time from first dose to objective progression or relapse as assessed by the investigator and noted as the reason for permanent treatment discontinuation or on-treatment death due to any cause. For CP, progression was defined as (at least one of the following): progression to AP or BP CML, loss of MCyR, loss of complete hematologic response or doubling of white blood cells to >20 ×109/L on 2 occasions at least 2 weeks apart (after the first 4 weeks of treatment) in a patient without complete hematologic response. Patients without events were censored at the last cytogenetic, hematologic, or laboratory assessment where progression could be assessed. OS was defined as the time from first dose to the date of death. Patients who had not died were censored at the last known alive date.

Safety data were summarized descriptively and included all patients with Ph+ CML who received at least one dose of bosutinib. The impact of dose reduction on safety was assessed by comparing the incidence of treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) before and after dose reduction.

Results

Disposition, demography, and baseline characteristics

Of 163 patients who received bosutinib, 156 had Ph+ CP CML. Efficacy in the four patients with AP CML and the three patients with Ph−/BCR::ABL1 + CML has been previously described [4]. Among the 156 patients with Ph+ CP CML, median age at study entry was 61.0 (range, 20.0–89.0) years and 51.9% were male. In total, 52.6% had demonstrated resistance to at least one prior TKI, and 47.4% had demonstrated intolerance to all prior TKIs.

The median duration of bosutinib treatment was 40.9 (range 0.2–50.1) months in patients with Ph+ CP CML (Table 1). At study completion (median follow-up, 47.8 months), 48.1% of patients were still receiving bosutinib treatment, and 68.6% completed the study. The most common (≥10%) primary reason for permanent treatment discontinuation were AEs (26.9%). The majority of discontinuations due to AEs occurred in Year 1 (17.9%). Dose escalations to 600 mg QD due to insufficient clinical response occurred in 1 (0.6%) patient. Dose interruptions and dose reductions due to AEs occurred in 76.3% and 79.5% of patients, respectively (Fig. 1). Dose reduction (without further reduction) to 400, 300, or 200 mg QD due to AEs occurred in 35 (22.4%), 46 (29.5%), and 38 (24.4%) patients, respectively.

Efficacy

Among evaluable patients, 81.1% (95% confidence interval [CI], 73.7–87.2) attained or maintained CCyR at any time on treatment (Fig. 2). Furthermore, 71.8% (95% CI, 63.9–78.9), 59.7% (95% CI, 51.4–67.7), and 48.3% (95% CI, 40.1–56.6) attained or maintained MMR, MR4, and MR4.5, respectively, at any time on treatment (Fig. 2). In patients without CCyR at baseline, 63.5% (95% CI, 49.0–76.4) achieved CCyR at any time on treatment. Among patients without the respective baseline response, 59.5% (95% CI, 47.9–70.4), 52.7% (95% CI, 43.0–62.2), and 42.7% (95% CI, 34.1–51.7) achieved MMR, MR4, and MR4.5, respectively, at any time on treatment. The majority of patients achieved a deeper MR relative to baseline while on bosutinib (Table 2).

Of patients who remained on 500 mg or higher (n = 32) for the duration of their treatment, 56.3% had MMR. Among patients who had a dose reduction to 400 mg, 71.4% had MMR > 6 months after dose reduction (40.0% maintained MMR and 31.4% achieved MMR after dose reduction; Table 3). In total, 8.6% of patients had MMR before dose reduction but discontinued treatment before the next >6-month assessment. No patients lost a previously attained MMR after dose reduction. Following a dose reduction to 300 mg, 43.5% of patients had MMR for >6 months (39.1% maintained MMR and 4.3% achieved MMR after dose reduction; Table 3); 39.1% of patients had MMR before dose reduction but discontinued treatment before the next >6-month assessment. No patients lost a previously attained MMR after dose reduction. Overall, 71.1% of patients had MMR > 6 months after having a dose reduction to 200 mg (65.8% maintained MMR and 5.3% achieved MMR after dose reduction); 10.5% of patients had MMR before dose reduction but discontinued treatment before the next >6-month assessment, and one patient (2.6%) lost a previously attained MMR after the dose reduction (Table 3). Similar trends were observed for CCyR, with CCyR rates of 62.5% in patients who remained on 500 mg or higher and 74.3%, 45.7%, and 76.3% after dose reduction to 400, 300, and 200 mg, respectively (Table 3).

Among responders, the Kaplan-Meier probabilities of maintaining CCyR, MMR, and MR4 at 36 months were 96.5% (95% CI, 89.5–98.9), 87.2% (95% CI, 78.0–92.7), and 80.7% (95% CI, 69.4–88.1), respectively. No patient progressed to AP/BP while on bosutinib treatment.

At 48 months, the cumulative incidence of on-treatment progression/death was 5.1% (95% CI, 2.4–9.4) and the OS rate was 88.3% (95% CI, 81.8–92.6). There were 17 deaths during the study; two deaths were considered CML related (off-treatment progression to AP/BP, n = 1; cardiogenic shock, n = 1), and none were considered to be treatment-related by the investigator.

Of the 112 patients with CP CML who were assessed for mutations at baseline, 106 had a post-baseline sample. Most (55/106 [51.9%]) patients were not assessed for mutations as they were in response and their BCR::ABL1 transcript level was too low (<1%) and 20/106 (18.9%) had a non-evaluable sample. Of 31 patients evaluated for new BCR::ABL1 point mutations at treatment failure, disease progression, or at the end of treatment, one patient with a baseline Y253F mutation had a newly detected T3151 mutation, and one patient with a baseline L298V mutation had both the L298V mutation and a newly detected F416S mutation on treatment.

Safety

Any grade and grade 3/4 TEAEs occurred in 99.4% and 79.5% of patients, respectively (Table 4). Results were similar to those previously reported at the 12-month analysis for the overall patient population (N = 163) [4] (Table 4). Forty-seven (30.1%) patients had AEs leading to permanent discontinuation of bosutinib; details of AEs leading to treatment discontinuation are shown in Table S1. Consistent with the primary analysis, the most frequent AEs leading to permanent discontinuation were hepatic enzyme increases which occurred early during treatment. The only AE leading to discontinuation in >1 patient since the initial report was cardiac failure (n = 2 of the total n = 3 during the study). Among the AEs of special interest [4], new AEs leading to discontinuation since the last report included diarrhea (n = 1, total n = 3), abdominal pain (n = 1, total n = 1), and pleural effusion (n = 1, total n = 3). During the study, there was 1 patient who discontinued due to vascular AEs (peripheral ischemia); no discontinuations were due to cardiovascular AEs (Supplementary Table S1). There were no COVID-19-related TEAEs or discontinuations related to COVID-19.

Discussion

Overall, this final analysis of the phase 4 BYOND study demonstrates the long-term efficacy and safety of bosutinib in previously treated patients with CML when used in the second-, third-, and fourth-line setting. After ≥3 years of follow-up, high rates of cytogenetic and MRs were observed in patients with Ph+ CP CML when treated with bosutinib. High response rates were seen across all patients as reported in the primary analysis [4]. The majority of patients achieved deeper MR relative to baseline with bosutinib. Furthermore, a low cumulative incidence of on-treatment progression/death (5.1%) and a high estimated OS rate (88.3%) were seen at 48 months, no patient had on-treatment disease progression to AP/BP CML. Long-term AEs were generally manageable with dose reductions and interruptions and were consistent with previous reports of bosutinib [2, 3], with no new safety signals identified after ≥3 years follow-up.

Although comparisons between trials are difficult due to differences in the patient populations—with variations in the proportions of TKI-resistant and TKI-intolerant patients, baseline response status, as well as the line of treatment received—the results reported here for bosutinib appear comparable to those of other second- and third-generation TKIs [5–8]. In the phase 3 ASCEMBL trial of asciminib versus bosutinib, the MMR rate at 6 months in patients with resistance or intolerance to ≥2 prior TKIs was 25.5% with asciminib and 13.2% with bosutinib [7]. In all, 16.6% and 32.9% of patients had discontinued asciminib and bosutinib, respectively, before completing 6 months of therapy, most frequently because of AEs (asciminib, 4.5%; bosutinib, 14.5%) and lack of efficacy (asciminib, 4.5%; bosutinib, 6.6%) [7]. After a median follow-up of 2.3 years, the MMR rate with bosutinib in ASCEMBL continued to be low (15.8% at 2 years), with 80.3% of patients having permenantly discontinued treatment [9]. Response rates with bosutinib in ASCEMBL were lower than those reported in the BYOND trial but similar to those from the phase 1/2 trial in resistant/intolerant patients treated with ≥2 TKIs [3, 4]. Futhermore, the discontinuation rate with bosutinib reported in ASCEMBL was higher than those reported in other bosutinib studies. These differences could potentially be attributed to the less flexible bosutinib dosage allowed by the ASCEMBL study protocol. In a post hoc analysis of the BYOND study that applied similar study criteria used in ASCEBML, the MMR rate with bosutinib was 43.5% at 6 months and 54.3% by Year 4 [10]. With ≥3 years of follow-up, discontinuation rates in this post hoc analysis were lower than that reported in ASCEBML (64.6%, with 20.8% due to AEs).

In this study, the management of TEAEs through bosutinib dose reduction to 400, 300, or 200 mg QD maintained efficacy and improved tolerability. While the majority of patients required dose reductions, response rates were similar in patients with dose reduction compared with those who remained on 500 mg QD for the duration of their treatment. The majority of patients maintained a response for >6 months after dose reduction. Most of the patients without a response after dose reduction discontinued treatment before their next >6 months efficacy assessment, rather than having a true loss of response. These results were similar to those from a phase 1/2 study of bosutinib in previously treated patients with Ph+ CML and acute lymphoblastic leukemia, which demonstrated that dose reduction did not compromise efficacy while reducing the occurrence of some common TEAEs associated with treatment, such as gastrointestinal (GI) events [11]. Dose reduction enabled patients to continue treatment, with some patients who did not have a response before dose reduction achieving a response after reduction. Notably, an analysis of the phase 3 BFORE trial demonstrated that in patients with newly diagnosed CML receiving a starting dose of 400 mg QD, management of TEAEs by bosutinib dose reductions allowed them to continue treatment, with many patients achieving MMR for the first time after dose reduction and no patients losing a previously attained response [12].

While the current analysis demonstrates that bosutinib dose reductions can be used to manage AEs while maintaining efficacy, the Bosutinib Dose Optimization (BODO) and Bosutinib in Elderly Chronic Myeloid Leukemia (BEST) studies investigated the effects of using dose optimization strategies at treatment initiation to minimize the incidence and severity of AEs [13, 14]. The single-arm, phase 2 BODO study evaluated whether a bosutinib step-in dosing regimen decreases GI toxicity while maintaining optimal efficacy in patients with CML after failure or intolerance to second-generation TKIs [14]. Bosutinib was initiated at 300 mg QD and was (in the absence of grade >1 toxicities) dose-increased by increments of 100 mg QD every 14 days if applicable up to a maximum dose of 500 mg QD. Given the limitations of a single-arm study with a limited number of patients (N = 57), the study was unable to demonstrate an advantage of the step-in dosing on the frequency of grade 2–4 GI toxicity overall. However, using this regimen, bosutinib induced optimal responses in almost two-thirds (63.3%) of patients previously resistant to second-generation TKIs while GI toxicity rarely led to treatment discontinuation. The prospective, single-arm phase 2 BEST study with bosutinib in elderly patients (aged 60–90 years) with CP CML resistant/intolerant to first-line TKIs also showed benefit when using lower doses of bosutinib. Treatment was initiated using 200 mg QD, then increasing the dose to 300 or 400 mg QD according to the molecular response, to find the minimum effective dose. This gradual dose increase based on prospective molecular monitoring allowed 57% of patients to remain on treatment with bosutinib at 36 months (94% of patients receiving 300 mg QD or less), with 78% of patients achieving or maintaining MMR by 36 months [15]. Additionally, in a real-world study of patients aged >65 years intolerant or resistant to previous TKIs, the majority (78%) of patients received bosutinib at a starting dose of <500 mg QD [16]. This study demonstrated that bosutinib was effective with a manageable safety profile in elderly patients with CML despite the lower starting doses.

Collectively, these studies show initiating treatment at a higher dose and reducing if toxicity arises—or starting on a low dose and titrating upwards—while monitoring efficacy are feasible options to achieve an optimal benefit/risk.

Studies evaluating outcomes after dose adjustments for the management of AEs have also been conducted with other TKIs [17–19]. One retrospective study involving 233 patients with newly diagnosed CP CML treated with first-line low-dose dasatinib 50 mg/day or standard-dose dasatinib 100 mg QD, showed low-dose dasatinib to be as effective as standard-dose dasatinib [18]. One-year toxicity was generally lower in the low-dose group. An analysis of adults with resistant or intolerant CP CML from the PACE and OPTIC trials with ponatinib aimed to compare the dosing dynamics between the two trials and its impact on efficacy and safety outcomes [17]. In PACE, patients received an initial dose of ponatinib 45 mg QD, and proactive dose reductions (to 30 or 15 mg QD) occurred at ~2 years as notable AEs occurred. In OPTIC, patients were randomly assigned to an initial oral dose of ponatinib 45 mg, 30 mg, or 15 mg QD, and a mandatory response-based dose reduction strategy was employed. The primary endpoint was ≤1% BCR::ABL1 at 12 months; rates were 44.1%, 29.0%, and 23.1% in the 45 mg, 30 mg, and 15 mg cohorts, respectively. Response rates were highest in the 45-mg starting dose cohort with a modest increase in toxicity [19]. The response-based dose reduction strategy in the 45-mg cohort of the OPTIC trial resulted in more rapid dose reductions, lower overall median relative dose intensity, fewer dose reductions related to AEs, and longer median time on therapy in OPTIC compared with PACE, demonstrating the benefit of a response-based dosing regimen [17].

In summary, the results from this long-term study support the use of bosutinib as a standard-of-care alternative in patients with CML that is resistant or intolerant to previous TKI therapy. High rates of both cytogenetic and molecular responses to bosutinib were observed, with MR being both deep and durable. Dose reduction and temporary treatment interruption can be used to manage the AEs associated with bosutinib treatment while achieving and maintaining cytogenetic and molecular responses.
