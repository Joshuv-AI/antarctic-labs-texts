Novel Resistance Mechanisms to Second‐Generation EGFR Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitor Afatinib and Associations With Genomic Features in NSCLC
Authors: Fengwu Lin, Miaoqing Zhao, Qiang Wu, Wenqi Wu, Xiaoying Wu, Ya Ma, Jiani C Yin, Meng Yang, Min Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAfatinib, an irreversible pan‐ERBB family inhibitor, has demonstrated promising efficacy in non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients with uncommon EGFR activating mutations. However, besides the acquisition of the secondary T790M mutation, other resistance mechanisms to afatinib remain to be explored.Materials and MethodsThis study retrospectively included 37 NSCLC patients harboring either EGFR or ERBB2 activating mutations, who had received afatinib as first‐line treatment. Targeted next‐generation sequencing data on the baseline and post‐progression samples were subjected to analysis. Comparative analyses of clinical parameters and genomic features were performed.ResultsOverall, the progression‐free survival of the EGFR‐mutated patients on first‐line afatinib was longer than that of the ERBB2‐positive patients (hazard ratio = 0.35 [95% confidence interval = 0.15–0.79], p = 0.008). Primary resistance was associated with the presence of exon 20 insertion mutation (p = 0.011), higher tumor mutational burden (p = 0.009), and higher proportion of APOBEC signature (p = 0.012), which were all enriched in the ERBB2‐mutated cohort. We further characterized acquired resistance to afatinib and discovered that baseline EGFR exon 19 deletions and higher levels of chromosomal instability and age‐related mutational signatures would contribute to the development of known resistance mechanisms such as EGFR T790M or MET amplification. We also report gaining EGFR activating mutations and losing CDKN2A, NF1, or the original ERBB2 mutations as novel resistance mechanisms to afatinib for ERBB2‐mutated patients.ConclusionThe study identified multiple genomic characteristics associated with primary and secondary resistance to first‐line afatinib treatment in EGFR‐ and ERBB2‐positive subpopulations.Keywords: afatinib, clinical outcome, EGFR, ERBB2, exon 20 insertions, resistance mechanisms

Introduction

Afatinib, recognized as the first approved ErbB family blocker, irreversibly inhibits signaling from epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR/ERBB1), epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2/ERBB2), and ERBB4. As a first‐line therapy for lung adenocarcinoma patients with EGFR mutations, the LUX‐Lung 3 study demonstrated a significantly higher objective response rate (ORR) for afatinib (56%) compared to chemotherapy (23%), along with an improvement in median progression‐free survival (mPFS) to 11.1 months versus 6.7 months for chemotherapy (p = 0.001) [1].

The FLAURA trial highlighted that osimertinib, a third‐generation tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI), provided superior survival benefits in patients with EGFR‐activating mutations relative to first‐generation TKIs. However, a subsequent multicenter study observed no significant difference in the efficacy of osimertinib and afatinib as first‐line therapies in the target patient group [2]. Notably, patients with EGFR L858R mutations and those without brain metastases showed more improved outcomes with afatinib than with osimertinib. Case studies have also supported afatinib's efficacy against several less common NSCLC EGFR mutations, such as L718X, G719X, G724S, S768I, L861Q, and certain exon 20 insertions [3, 4, 5, 6]. In contrast, dacomitinib, another second‐generation EGFR TKI, was ineffective against rare EGFR mutations, exemplified by a case report detailing failure against EGFR L792H/T790M/L718Q [7]. Furthermore, afatinib has demonstrated efficacy in patients bearing ERBB2 mutations, which is not the case with osimertinib [1, 8]. Thus, afatinib continues to display potential advantages over other EGFR‐TKIs in specific patient subgroups, underscoring the need for ongoing research to guide optimized treatment strategies for NSCLC patients with diverse molecular profiles.

Approximately 20%–30% of patients exhibit a poor response to EGFR TKIs, showing primary resistance and progressing within 3–6 months of first‐line treatment [9, 10]. Previous studies indicate that patients with concurrent EGFR activating mutations and either KRAS mutations or ALK translocations may experience a suboptimal response to EGFR TKIs [11, 12, 13]. Cases of primary resistance to afatinib are rare, with only one reported case involving EGFR S768I and V769L mutations [14]. Conversely, acquired resistance develops in nearly 70% of patients undergoing long‐term EGFR TKI therapy [15, 16]. Driven by the molecular heterogeneity of the disease, acquired resistance in EGFR‐mutant NSCLC involves a diverse spectrum of resistance mechanisms. Factors such as secondary mutations in EGFR [17, 18, 19], increased EGFR copy number [18, 20], and activation of bypass or downstream pathways through aberrant genes such as MET, ALK, and KRAS [17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23] have all been implicated in conferring acquired resistance to EGFR TKIs in NSCLC harboring EGFR mutations. In the case of afatinib, the on‐target EGFR T790M mutation is the most prevalent resistance‐conferring alteration, occurring in 40%–50% of the EGFR mutant cases [24, 25]. Amplification of MET or ERBB2 has also been reported in a few cases [24]. In ERBB2‐driven NSCLC, in vitro experiments suggest that loss of ERBB2 amplification, secondary MET amplification, and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) could contribute to afatinib resistance [26]. Beyond the prominent EGFR T790M mutation, other resistance mechanisms to afatinib in real patient cases remain to be elucidated. In this study, we aim to characterize the genetic mechanisms underlying the efficacy and resistance of first‐line afatinib in patients with EGFR‐ or ERBB2‐driven NSCLC. Genomic characteristics, such as tumor mutational burden (TMB) and mutational signatures, in relation to afatinib response will also be evaluated.

Materials and Methods

Patient Cohorts and Sample Collection

This study retrospectively included 37 NSCLC patients harboring either ERBB2 or EGFR activating mutations, who had undergone afatinib as their first‐line treatment at the PLA General Hospital, Shandong Cancer Hospital and Institute, China‐Japan Friendship Hospital, and Hainan Medical University between April 2016 and May 2020. Patient samples before and after afatinib treatment were subject to next‐generation sequencing (NGS) analysis. NGS was performed using a panel targeting 139 lung cancer‐related or 425 cancer‐related genes in a CAP, CLIA, and ISO15189‐accredited laboratory (Geneseeq Technology Inc., Nanjing, China). For patients with sufficient clinical follow‐up data, progression‐free survival (PFS) was defined as the time from the beginning of afatinib treatment to the date of progressive disease. Informed written consent was obtained from each subject or the subject's family member upon sample collection. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Nanjing Geneseeq Medical Laboratory (NSJB‐MEC‐2024‐06). An external cohort of 326 NSCLC patients with ERBB2 or EGFR mutations was sourced from the cBioPortal database, comprising data from three studies (luad_mskcc_2020, lung_msk_mind_2020, nsclc_tcga_broad_2016).

DNA Extraction and Next‐Generation Sequencing (NGS)

Genomic DNA was extracted from the patients' tumor tissues before afatinib treatment using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen). Circulating cell‐free DNA (cfDNA) was extracted from their plasma samples using the QIAamp Circulating Nucleic Acid Kit (Qiagen). Genomic DNA from white blood cells was extracted as germline control. Extracted DNA or cfDNA were processed for sequencing library preparation using the KAPA HyperPrep Kit (KAPA Biosystems). Customized xGen lockdown probes (Integrated DNA Technologies) targeting 139 or 425 cancer‐relevant genes were used for hybridization enrichment. Libraries were on‐beads PCR‐amplified, purified, sized, and quantified, and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq4000 platform.

Mutation Calling

For quality control of the FASTQ files, N and low‐quality (quality score below 20) bases were removed with Trimmomatic [27], and PCR deduplication was performed with Picard (https://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/). The filtered paired‐end reads were mapped to the human genome reference (GRCh37, hg19) using the Burrows‐Wheeler Aligner [28] and realigned around indels using GATK3 [29]. Single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and indels were identified via VarScan2 [30] and annotated by ANNOVAR [31]. The SNVs/indels were defined with a minimum variant allele frequency threshold set at 0.01 and a p‐value threshold set to 0.05. Given the differences in tumor purity between tumor tissue and plasma, we further used the following thresholds to filter SNVs/indels from different sources: (i) read depth ≥ 10; (ii) for tissue samples, unique variant supporting reads ≥ 5 and variant allele frequency ≥ 1%; (iii) for plasma samples, unique variant supporting reads ≥ 3 and variant allele frequency ≥ 0.3%; (iv) supporting reads mapped to both directions of strands; and (v) excluded if SNVs' prevalence > 1% in the 1000 Genome Project or in the ExAC database. NGS results of matched white blood cells from each patient were used to further remove germline variants and clonal hematopoiesis‐related variants. Fusion events were called by Delly [32]. Copy‐number variations (CNVs) were identified using CNVkit with default parameters [33]. Depth ratios above 2.0 for tissue and above 1.6 for cfDNA were considered as CNV gain, while ratios below 0.6 were considered as CNV loss.

Genomic Feature Analysis

The panel TMB was defined by quantifying all base substitutions and indels within the coding region of targeted genes. We included synonymous alterations to reduce sampling noise and excluded known driver mutations as they are overrepresented in the panel [34].

Chromosomal instability (CIS) was evaluated by measuring the proportion of the genome with abnormal copy numbers, defined by a segment level copy number ≤ 1 or ≥ 3 [35].

Mutant‐allele tumor heterogeneity (MATH) scores measure intratumoral heterogeneity from variant allele frequencies, and were calculated by the R package maftools [36].

Mutational Signature Analysis

Both synonymous and non‐synonymous SNVs in each sample were analyzed for the six mutation types (C > T, C > G, C > A, T > C, T > G, T > A) representing the mutational signature spectrum. The reference signatures were downloaded from the Catalog of Somatic Mutations in Cancer (COSMIC) database version 2.0 [37]. The mutational signatures in each sample were mapped to the reference signature patterns. The R package Sigminer was used to perform the analysis [38].

Survival Analysis

PFS was assessed from the initiation of afatinib treatment until disease progression. Information from the Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed to estimate PFS and OS probabilities in various genomic groups, with differences between groups analyzed by the log‐rank test.

Statistical Analysis

Fisher's exact test was used to compare the categorical variables between groups. For survival analyses, Kaplan–Meier curves were compared using the log‐rank test, and hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were calculated by Cox proportional hazards models. A two‐sided p value of less than 0.05 was considered significant for all tests unless indicated otherwise (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001). All statistical analyses were done in R V.3.5.5.

Results

Similar Clinical Characteristics in Patients With EGFR and ERBB2 Mutations

A total of 37 NSCLC patients with either EGFR or ERBB2 activating mutations who underwent first‐line afatinib treatment were included in this study. Among them, 27 patients had EGFR activating alterations, and 10 patients carried ERBB2 activating alterations at the time of diagnosis.

In the EGFR‐mutated cohort, the median age at diagnosis was 62 years (range, 33–76 years), with 51.9% females. All patients were classified as pathological stage IV according to the eighth edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) criteria. Histological examination revealed that 20 patients had adenocarcinoma (ADC), two had squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), and one had adeno‐squamous carcinoma (ADSC) (Table 1).

In the EGFR‐mutated cohort, the most frequently observed concurrent mutations were in TP53 (66.7%), followed by CDKN2A (11.1%), ROS1 (11.1%), and TGFBR2 (11.1%) (Figure 1A). None of the previously reported primary resistant alterations, such as EGFR T790M and MET amplification, were detected in the study cohort. Similarly, in the ERBB2‐mutated cohort, TP53 was the most commonly co‐mutated gene, present in 70.0% of the patients. We further classified the baseline EGFR alterations into three groups: the “common” subtype, which includes exon 19 deletion (19del) and L858R mutations (n = 11), the exon 20 insertion (“20ins”) subtype (n = 4), and the “others” subtype, which includes rarer EGFR activating mutations such as G719X and L861Q (n = 12) (Figure 1B). In the common group, six patients had 19del, with one of them also carrying concurrent EGFR amplification. Five patients had L858R, three of whom also harbored EGFR amplification. In the 20ins group, no additional EGFR alterations were observed. The specific 20ins mutations included p.A763_Y764insFQEA and p.S768_D770dup. In the others subgroup, all six patients with EGFR G719X harbored concurrent EGFR alterations, including two with EGFR amplification. Of the three patients with EGFR L861Q, two carried co‐existing EGFR alterations (V1011del and amplification). Finally, in the two patients with EGFR exon 18 insertions (18ins) and one patient with kinase domain duplication (KDD), no EGFR co‐alterations were observed.

Among the 10 patients with baseline ERBB2 mutations, 8 patients (80.0%) harbored ERBB2 20ins mutations, which included 5 patients with G776delinsVC, 2 with Y772_A775dup, and 1 with G778_P780dup (Figure 1C). Two patients with G776delinsVC also presented with concurrent ERBB2 amplification. The remaining two patients (20.0%) harbored ERBB2 missense mutations that have been reported to activate downstream signaling pathways [39, 40].

Disparities in Survival and Genomic Features Between EGFR‐Driven and ERBB2‐Driven Tumors

EGFR‐altered patients exhibited a median PFS (mPFS) value of 7.0 months, compared to 4.9 months for those with ERBB2 alterations (HR = 0.35, 95% CI = 0.15–0.79, p = 0.008; Figure 2A), despite the overall staging of the EGFR‐altered patients being more advanced. The findings were further validated by examining NSCLC cases from the TCGA database (Methods and Table S1). In the external cohort, we observed a more favorable prognosis for the EGFR‐altered cohort compared to the ERBB2‐altered cohort (median overall survival [OS] = 53.1 vs. 30.1 months, HR = 0.46, 95% CI = 0.27–0.78, p = 0.003; Figure 2B).

The difference in survival outcomes could partly be explained by pharmacologic factors, as afatinib exhibits greater inhibitory potency against EGFR than HER2 across most clinically relevant contexts [41, 42]. On the other hand, to assess additional contributors beyond target selectivity, we next examined baseline genomic features that might further account for the observed prognostic disparities. The prevalence of PIK3CA mutations was marginally higher in the ERBB2‐mutated cohort (p = 0.068), while no differences in other genes were observed (Table S1). In the ERBB2‐altered cohort, the two patients with PIK3CA mutations had significantly shorter PFS than non‐PIK3CA‐mutated patients (p < 0.001) (Table S1).

At the genomic level, we observed a significant enrichment of APOBEC‐ and POLE‐related mutational signatures in the ERBB2‐altered cohort compared to the EGFR‐altered cohort (P APOBEC = 0.005, P POLE = 0.030) (Figure 2C). In the biomarker‐evaluable cohort, there was also a trend toward higher TMB levels in the ERBB2‐altered cohort (p = 0.073). No differences in the distribution of CIS and MATH scores were observed between the two cohorts. Notably, a higher APOBEC‐related signature was associated with worse PFS outcomes, both in the overall study cohort (HR = 3.30, 95% CI = 1.11–9.78, p = 0.025; Figure 3A) and in the EGFR‐mutated subset (HR = 5.26, 95% CI = 1.26–22.03, p = 0.012; Figure S1A). Similarly, patients with high TMB exhibited poorer PFS compared to those with low TMB (overall, HR = 4.61, 95% CI = 1.48–14.39, p = 0.004, Figure 3B; EGFR+, HR = 6.04, 95% CI = 1.32–27.62, p = 0.009, Figure S1B). While the POLE‐related signature did not predict patient outcome, a higher level of tumor heterogeneity as measured by MATH was associated with worse PFS (overall, HR = 4.17, 95% CI = 1.05–16.56, p = 0.030, Figure 3C; EGFR+, HR = 11.48, 95% CI = 1.23–107.00, p = 0.008, Figure S1C). In the external cohort, we also examined the contribution of the APOBEC signature and TMB levels in survival outcome. We found that patients with high levels of the APOBEC signature (mOS = 38.3 vs. 54.5 months, HR = 2.17, 95% CI = 1.31–3.59, p = 0.002; Figure 3D) or TMB (mOS = 33.3 vs. 54.5 months, HR = 2.26, 95% CI = 1.30–3.91, p = 0.002; Figure 3E) exhibited worse prognosis. These data suggest that the enriched APOBEC mutational signature and the higher TMB levels in ERBB2‐driven tumors might partly explain the survival differences between the two cohorts.

20ins Mutation Predicts Worse Response to Afatinib

Afatinib demonstrates stronger activity against common and several uncommon sensitizing EGFR mutations but generally has poor efficacy against most EGFR exon 20 insertions [41, 42]. Given that 20ins is the predominant subtype in the ERBB2‐mutated cohort and occurs rarely in the EGFR‐mutated cohort, we hypothesized that 20ins might serve as a predictive factor for treatment outcomes. Our analysis revealed that patients with 20ins mutations experienced worse PFS compared to those without (mPFS = 7.0 vs. 4.9 months, HR = 0.38, 95% CI = 0.18–0.82, p = 0.011; Figure 4A). Among the 20ins‐mutated patients, no survival difference was observed between those with EGFR 20ins and those with ERBB2 20ins mutations (p = 0.960; Figure 4B). This finding suggests that the 20ins subtype itself, rather than the driver genes, may be the more critical predictive factor. Indeed, in the EGFR‐mutated cohort, the presence of 20ins was associated with the worst PFS compared to other EGFR driver mutations (p = 0.011; Figure 4C).

To further examine the role of genomic signatures in patient outcomes, we found that 20ins‐mutated patients exhibited significantly higher levels of TMB (overall, p = 0.006, Figure 4D; EGFR+, p = 0.043, Figure S1A) and APOBEC signature (overall, p = 0.002, Figure 4D; EGFR+, p = 0.044, Figure S1A). All patients with 20ins had either high APOBEC signature or TMB (p = 0.044; Figure S1B), suggesting significant co‐occurrence of 20ins and high TMB or APOBEC, which might be the underlying mechanism of poor response to afatinib. This was further supported by the observation that patients with either 20ins, high TMB, or high APOBEC signature had markedly shorter PFS than other patients (overall, 3.8 vs. 9.7 months, HR = 5.79, 95% CI = 1.23–27.30, p = 0.014, Figure 4E; EGFR+, 3.5 vs. 11.4 months, HR = 10.91, 95% CI = 1.27–93.89, p = 0.008, Figure S1C). A similar result was obtained in the external validation cohort with worse prognosis seen in patients with 20ins and high levels of TMB or APOBEC signature (mOS = 38.4 vs. 54.5 months, HR = 2.7, 95% CI = 1.67–4.36, p < 0.001; Figure 4F).

Acquired Resistance to Afatinib Treatment

Finally, we explored potential resistance mechanisms to afatinib treatment in our study cohort. All patients had paired baseline and post‐progression samples for the evaluation of acquired resistance alterations (Figure 5A). In the EGFR‐mutated cohort, acquired alterations were detected in 16 (59.3%) patients. The most common acquired resistance mechanism to afatinib involved on‐target alterations (25.9%), followed by MET amplification (14.8%) (Figure 5B). Among the seven patients developing EGFR on‐target resistance, five (18.5%) acquired EGFR T790M resistance, and two acquired EGFR amplification, including one gaining both EGFR T790M and amplification (Figure 5A). At baseline, three of these patients harbored EGFR 19del mutations; two carried L858R, and the other two had EGFR KDD mutations. Two of the patients who acquired the MET amplification carried EGFR L858R mutation at baseline, and the other two carried EGFR L861Q and G719A, respectively. None of them had EGFR 19del mutations at baseline. The frequency of acquired EGFR T790M resistance was significantly higher in the EGFR 19del‐mutated group compared to those with rare EGFR driver mutations (p = 0.025). By contrast, the likelihood of acquiring MET amplification was similar across all baseline driver mutation subtypes (Figure 5A). Notably, 69.4% of the acquired alterations occurred within the 10 canonical oncogenic signaling pathways [43], with the RTK‐Ras pathway (44%) being the most frequently altered following afatinib treatment (Figure 5C). In addition to MET amplification, additional bypass signaling alterations included amplifications in NTRK1 and KRAS.

Discussion

The recent introduction of intensified first‐line regimens, exemplified by FLAURA2 [44] and MARIPOSA [45], has further improved outcomes for NSCLC patients. Nevertheless, a detailed understanding of the genomic determinants of treatment response and mechanisms of acquired resistance remains essential, as they guide subsequent treatment choices and support the development of rational combination approaches. In this study, we retrospectively investigated the first‐line use of afatinib in 37 NSCLC patients, whose genomic features were associated with TKI response and resistance. We discovered significant survival differences between the EGFR‐ and ERBB2‐mutated cohorts. The 20ins alteration subtype accounts for most ERBB2 activating mutations and approximately 10% of EGFR activating mutations, with an overall prevalence of 1.5%–2% in NSCLC [46, 47]. The mutation mostly involves insertions at the αC‐β4 loop of the receptor kinases, promoting deformation which sterically hinders TKI binding [48, 49]. Afatinib has demonstrated efficacy for EGFR 20ins mutations, although poorer than that for other alteration subtypes [4]. In our study, regardless of the driver gene, 20ins‐mutated patients had significantly worse response to afatinib, which is in line with previous findings. The difference in survival outcomes could partly be explained by pharmacologic factors, as afatinib exhibits greater inhibitory potency against EGFR than HER2 and shows higher efficacy in common or uncommon sensitizing EGFR mutations than in most exon 20 insertions [41, 42]. The shorter PFS for the ERBB2‐mutated cohort compared to the EGFR‐mutated cohort might also be attributed to the enrichment for 20ins alteration subtype and for high TMB and APOBEC‐related mutational signatures, providing additional, non‐pharmacologic explanations for the poorer prognosis in this cohort. Our findings highlight that afatinib‐specific insights remain relevant in the intensified‐therapy era, as afatinib is still used in selected settings, and resistance patterns observed under afatinib pressure (on‐target EGFR changes, MET amplification) overlap with those from other EGFR‐directed regimens, informing sequencing and combinations. Moreover, risk features we identify (20ins, high TMB, APOBEC) are broadly applicable and can guide management and trial design beyond any single regimen.

Interestingly, to the best of our knowledge, we report for the first time that 20ins mutations were associated with higher levels of TMB and APOBEC‐related mutational signatures. Enrichment of these features was correlated with unfavorable survival outcomes in our study. Previous studies have also indicated that elevated TMB levels can predict poor responses to TKIs including afatinib, likely due to the increase in genomic abnormalities that facilitate the development of resistance or metastasis [50]. In addition, APOBEC‐related mutational signatures have been associated with higher TMB levels [51] and increased genomic instability, which may contribute to primary resistance to targeted therapies [52]. However, it remains uncertain whether the 20ins variant and these genomic features independently contribute to a diminished response to afatinib, or if they work synergistically to affect patient outcomes. Further studies are anticipated to elucidate the underlying mechanisms. In addition to the 20ins mutations and associated genomic features, our results indicated that high tumor heterogeneity predicted a poor response to afatinib, consistent with previous research on first‐generation TKI resistance in lung adenocarcinoma [53, 54]. However, it is worth noting that the levels of tumor heterogeneity were similar between the ERBB2‐ and EGFR‐mutated groups, as well as between the 20ins and non‐20ins‐mutated groups. Recent findings suggest that neoadjuvant afatinib treatment may promote immune cell infiltration in NSCLC patients [55]. Future studies should aim to further investigate the predictive value of TMB, APOBEC signaling, and tumor heterogeneity in larger cohorts, as well as in the neoadjuvant setting.

Following progression on afatinib treatment, the EGFR T790M mutation is a well‐established resistance mechanism [24, 25]. In our study of the EGFR‐mutated cohort, we observed a lower prevalence of acquired T790M mutations (18.5%) compared to previously reported rates of 40%–50% [24, 25]. Consistent with previous findings [24, 56], the frequency of acquiring T790M resistance was significantly higher in patients with common sensitizing EGFR mutations, particularly the 19del mutation, than in those with rare EGFR driver mutations. We also report a higher frequency of secondary MET amplification (14.8%), in contrast to the previously documented rate of 2% [24]. Our analysis also revealed that certain genomic features, such as elevated CIS levels and age‐related mutational signatures, are more likely associated with the development of known resistance mutations. While genomic instability broadly promotes acquired resistance [57], in vitro experiments by Crowley et al. [58] specifically suggest that cells with suppressed CIS levels tend to develop TKI resistance by modifying EGFR expression at the transcriptional level, rather than through the acquisition of resistance‐conferring mutations. Although age‐related mutational signatures have not been directly linked to TKI resistance, the aging process is associated with an accumulation of genomic instability [59, 60], which may similarly contribute to acquired resistance.

Furthermore, the acquired resistance mechanisms to afatinib in ERBB2‐mutated NSCLC have not been systematically examined [26]. In this study, we report that the activation of bypass or downstream pathways represents the primary mechanisms of resistance, corroborating previous in vitro findings [26]. Specifically, alterations in CDKN2A, a tumor suppressor gene involved in the cell cycle and apoptosis regulation [61], were lost in two patients, while NF1, a negative regulator of the Ras GTPase [62], was lost in one patient. In addition, we identified a case of simultaneous acquisition of EGFR L858R and T790M, while retaining the original ERBB2 activating mutations at resistance. We also observed two other acquired alterations, ERBB4 C293Y and TSC2 A894L, which have unknown clinical significance. Interestingly, we also identified a case with loss of the initial ERBB2 mutation. However, it remains uncertain whether this represents a resistance mechanism or the emergence of another dominant clone. The US Food and Drug Administration has approved the use of trastuzumab deruxtecan (T‐DXd) for use in ERBB2‐mutated NSCLC patients who progressed on a prior systemic therapy. Nevertheless, afatinib could still be a promising alternative for patients who are intolerant to the antibody–drug conjugate. Identification of patient‐specific resistant mechanisms may facilitate the tailoring of subsequent therapies, thereby improving treatment outcomes.

This study has several limitations. Due to its retrospective nature, clinical and genomic information was not complete for some patients, which limited the sample sizes for certain analyses. The small sample size of the ERBB2‐mutated cohort also restricted the ability to make meaningful comparisons of genomic features within the sub‐cohort.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we investigated the treatment response to first‐line afatinib in EGFR‐ or ERBB2‐mutated NSCLC patients. The 20ins subtype, along with higher levels of TMB and APOBEC mutational signature, predicted poorer response to afatinib treatment. We also provided new insight into the acquired resistance mechanisms to afatinib, discovering that patients with baseline EGFR 19del, higher CIS levels, or enriched age‐related mutational signatures were more likely to develop known resistance mechanisms amenable to targeted therapies. These findings contribute to a deeper understanding of afatinib‐treated NSCLC and may provide additional biomarkers that could potentially refine management strategies for such patients.
