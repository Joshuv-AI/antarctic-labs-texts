A nationwide cross-sectional study on endodontic instrument fractures and development of a comprehensive classification system
Authors: Ajay Singh Rao, M. Ashwin Surana, Nimisha Shah, Ajinkya M. Pawar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Aim:To assess the incidence and characteristics of instrument fractures in root canals and provide researchers with a systematic classification system that will facilitate diagnosis, research, and treatment planning.Materials and Methods:In this cross-sectional and observational study, we collected the data on 887 intraoral periapical (IOPA) radiographs taken from different geographical and clinical practices in India. The images containing instrument fractures were classified according to the location of fractures within the canal (coronal, middle, and apical third) and their relationship to canal curvature. The IOPA images were independently evaluated by two experienced endodontists, and the kappa statistics was applied to estimate interobserver agreement (≥80%). Descriptive statistics were analyzed using the SPSS software version 22.0.Results:The location of 54.3% of fractures was in the middle third of the canal, followed by apical thirds (24.5%) and coronal thirds (19.8%). Fractures beyond the periapex were rare (1.4%). Of the fractures, 54.3% had curvatures of the canal and of those, 33.1% or 3.1% of the fractures occurred within mild to moderate curvatures. The study found statistically significant differences in fractures in the different regions of the canal (P < 0.01). A unique classification system was created to categorize fractures by location, extent, and curvature involvement which can be used and applied in both clinical and research settings.Conclusions:The research draws the attention to the lack of a standardized classification system for instrument fractures, an underappreciated aspect of endodontics. The classification system proposed here will enhance communication, educate on diagnosis, treatment planning, and provide support for advanced education in the field.

INTRODUCTION

Root canal treatment continues to be one of the most technically challenging procedures in endodontics and requires highly advanced fine motor skills, a detailed understanding of root canal anatomy, and precision for each clinical phase. Although the principle of a root canal procedure is very simple, the process ultimately asks the practitioner to remove infected or necrotic pulp tissue, thoroughly clean, shape, and obturate the root canal system; in practice, there are often many anatomical and mechanical complexities which result in root canal treatment being clinically challenging even for experienced root canal practitioners.[1] The primary objectives of root canal therapy are to eliminate intracanal infection, relieve pain, regain form and function, and, where possible, retain the natural tooth.[2]

A fundamental part of root canal management is adequate biomechanical preparation. With the natural variation and unpredictability in root canal anatomy, such as extra canals, sudden curvatures, canal calcifications, and intricate isthmuses, it can be very difficult to ensure a complete preparation and debridement of the canal system.[3] Current endodontic instrumentation systems, especially those that use nickel–titanium (NiTi) alloys, have been designed to deal with these challenges using greater flexibility and better cutting properties. Despite these advances, instrument separation remains among the most challenging and feared intra-operative complications in endodontics.[4] A separated instrument can obstruct canal access, hinder adequate cleaning, jeopardize the tooth’s long-term prognosis, and often necessitates complex, lengthy, highly skilled retrieval procedures.

Instrument fracture can result from several factors, primarily mechanical, procedural or operator-related.[5] NiTi rotary files have unique properties of flexibility and cutting ability, but more than any other file type, they are also susceptible to fracture as they fail via two mechanisms: torsional and cyclic fatigue.[6] Torsional fracture occurs when the instrument tip binds in the canal and the shank continues to rotate to the extent of exceeding the elastic limit of the alloy, whereas cyclic fatigue develops when an instrument travels through a curved canal while subjected to repetitive tensile and compressive stress.[7] While heat-treated NiTi alloys may help with both torsional and cyclic fatigue, no instrument will be fracture resistant, especially if used multiple times, incorrectly or exceeding its clinical lifespan.[8]

An instrument separation has significant clinical implications. Retained debris can harbor residual bacteria, which may lead to treatment failure or persistent periapical infection and can obstruct both canal debridement and obturation. Instrument retrieval requires specific materials and specific clinical skills and is often not foreseeable or even possible.[9] These problems emphasize the importance of prophylactic measures, proper instrument use, and a better understanding of the basic mechanics of fractures.[10]

Although clarifying the impact of instrument fractures in endodontic treatment failure is acknowledged, there is currently no universally accepted classification to describe the location, extent, and clinical significance of the fractures. Reporting of instrument fractures is currently inconsistent, using subjective descriptors or vaguely defined classifications, making it difficult to communicate with other clinicians and researchers and limiting the development of evidence-based protocols for management and prevention. Evidence-based protocols need a standardized and comprehensive classification and would enhance spontaneous reproduction in clinical research, facilitate comparisons in clinical research, and enhance clinical decisions.

In order to establish such a system, it is imperative to comprehend the etiological elements and radiographic characteristics of the instrument fractures. The literature identifies different etiological factors that can be associated with the instrumentation of teeth, for example, curvature in a canal, amount of experience, or the initial state of the instrument when employed. For instance, severely curved canals generate greater cyclic fatigue loads and can increase the chances of separated instruments.[11] In addition, experience and/or technique can contribute to stress concentration and increased fracture risk. Also, used or overly used instruments have been associated with increased instrument fractures and demonstrate the importance of good practice guidelines.[11]

The present study was intended to fill this void in the literature, with a detailed radiographic evaluation of instrument fractures in root canal treated teeth, using a large number of available cases from the multiple clinics in India. The primary aim of the study was to determine the distribution, site and radiographic characteristics of instrument separation. Based on these findings, a new two-part classification system was developed and validated. The classification system categorizes the fractures according to where in the canal they are spatially located and their relationship to curvature and provides a detailed, reproducible, and clinically relevant classification. The secondary purpose of this study was to evaluate the effectiveness of the classification in informing treatment decision-making and clinical communication, to contribute to improved patient outcomes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This study adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the 2013 Declaration of Helsinki, ensuring the highest standards of integrity and respect for participants. Before commencing the investigation, the Institutional Ethics Committee of Sumandeep Vidyapeeth gave its ethical permission (reference number: SVIEC/ON/DentSRP/JUNE/23/107). The study’s objectives, limitations, and confidentiality measures were provided to all participants, including educators and clinicians who provided radiographic data. Participants were able to access all necessary material and consented to participate through Google Forms, a secure online application. The information contained in participants’ photographs can only be accessed by individuals with consent to see. Measures were taken to ensure anonymity and confidentiality.

Study design

This observational and cross-sectional study aimed to develop a detailed standardized classification system to facilitate improved understanding of the occurrence and type of instrument fractures sustained during root canal treatment. In addition, the study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines for reporting on the methods and outcomes of this study in its entirety and objectively.

Sample size calculation

In order to improve the statistical rigor of the study, a formal determination of sample size was made before collecting the data. The proportion estimates for a cross-sectional study were calculated using the following formula n = Z2 × p × (1 − p)/d2. The variables are: n = sample size; Z = the Z score corresponding to the specified confidence level (1.96 for 95%); p = estimate of the prevalence of instrument fracture, a value of 50% was used to ensure maximum variability as little information was available for developing the variance estimate; d = the margin of error (set 5%). After substituting ourselves into this formula, the minimum calculated sample size was 384 radiographs. To also account for the possibility of exclusions from the sample due to poor-quality images, or because of incomplete data, we therefore inflated our estimate by 20%, achieving a final target sample size of 461 radiographs. In total, our final dataset of 887 radiographs greatly exceeded this anticipated sample size, thereby ensuring there was adequate statistical power to contribute to our analysis.

Data collection

Data collection occurred across 3 months, from June 23, 2023, to September 23, 2023. 79 private or institutional practices distributed across all five Indian zones, covering 17 states/UTs (North 4, South 5, East 3, West 3, and Central 2). Recruitment took place using multiple communication methods, including telephone, E-mail, and social media (WhatsApp), in attempts to reach saturation and effectively engage participants. Participants were asked to submit high-definition intraoral periapical images in JPEG or PNG file formats that specifically displayed fractured instruments in root canals. This variety of approaches to recruitment enabled the collection of a complete data set and a manageable number of images for examination.

Assessment of curvature

Canal curvature was quantified on the long-axis view with Schneider’s method.

The angle was measured in using ImageJ 2.0; curvatures were classified as mild (<10°), moderate (10°–25°), or severe (>25°).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Strict inclusion and exclusion criteria were implemented to uphold the dataset’s integrity and quality. Radiographs were included for the analysis if there were apparatus fractures within the root canals that were visibly distinct, such as fractures of more than one instrument in different canals of the same tooth. All of the radiographs were obtained from general dental practitioners and specialist endodontists with a minimum of 5 years of independent clinical and/or academic experience in root canal treatment or specialty endodontics. This was important to ensure that the data were a representation of consistent standards of experienced clinical practice. Only radiographs with separated hand files or rotary NiTi instruments were included in the dataset. Each radiograph was checked for technical faults. Images that were at fault due to elongation, cone cuts, or blurriness… or if the image was nondigital that displayed poor development or poor fixer use were all excluded. Moreover, images that did not fully encompass the length of the tooth, as a result of improper placement of the sensor during the imaging process were excluded from the dataset. This was all done to ensure that the dataset contained high-quality, diagnostically reliable radiographs.

Evaluation of fractured instrument

Radiographs were stored in a digital repository. Images were examined with the Photos™ Microsoft Office application (Windows 11™, USA). Two independent endodontic specialists with at least 10 years of clinical experience each examined the radiographs to identify the location and extent of fractured instruments. To take into consideration the reliability and consistency of observations of the radiographs, we conducted a thorough observer calibration before analyzing our data.

To calibrate for this review, two senior endodontic specialists with ten or more years of clinical experience completed the calibration process. The calibration process required each specialist to analyze a calibration set of 50 radiographs with a variety of instrument fractures in different locations and levels of severity. Each specialist analyzed the radiographs independently, and their findings were compared with a gold standard of a senior expert panel. Where there were differences, each specialist provided a direct explanation of their observations, and a consensus agreement was established as interpretations used to standardize their analyses. This calibration process continued until we had reached an agreed upon inter-observer agreement of at least 80% with kappa statistics on the calibration dataset. This calibration process provided an assurance of interobserver reliability and was an important consideration to take into account when reviewing the radiographs for the study.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) for Windows, Version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The data were summarized using the descriptive statistics to summarize the distribution and characteristics (location and extent) of the fractured instruments. The statistical analysis provided additional understanding of patterns and trend and served as a basis for creating the classification system. We measured how often the two examiners agreed by using a weighted kappa test

Distribution by location

The middle third of the root canal had the highest incidence of fractures (482 cases, 54.3%), which shows that this region is especially at risk for instrument separation. The apical third was the next site of interest that had the next highest incidence at 217 cases (24.5%), followed by the coronal third, which was reported at 176 cases (19.8%). Crush fractures extending beyond the periapex (in the anatomical area beyond the rubber dam) were quite rare, and only happened in 12 cases, 1.4% of cases. These factors demonstrate the complexity of navigating instruments in different regions of the canal and the challenges they pose.

Extent of fractures

Fracture extent analysis indicated separate trends for each canal third, with the coronal third (176 cases). In 35 cases (19.9%), fractures were confined to the coronal third, but 42 cases (23.9%) extended to the middle third; 87 cases (49.4%) extended to the apical third; and 12 cases (6.8%) extended beyond the periapex. In the middle third, which encompassed 482 cases, 100 cases (20.7%) were confined to this region, while the majority (353 cases, 73.2%) extended to the apical third. Additionally, 29 cases (6%) extended beyond the periapex. In the apical third (217 cases), 176 cases (81.1%) were confined to this region, whereas 41 cases (18.9%) extended beyond the periapex. Among the least frequent occurrences, 12 cases involved fractures entirely beyond the periapex.

Relation with canal curvature

When the fractured instruments were analyzed concerning the curvature of the root canal, a majority (54.3%) were found inside or within the root canal curvature. The second most common location was beyond the curvature (BC) (25.8%), followed by above the curvature (AC) (19.8%).

Additional analysis based on the degree of curvature demonstrated interesting patterns. For fractures AC, there were 13.2% in mild and moderate curvatures and 6.7% in severe curvatures. Involving/inside the curvature (IC) fractures had the highest fracture rates, with 33.1% in mild and moderate curvatures and 21.2% in severe curvatures. For BC fractures, there were 14.0% of mild and moderate curvatures and 11.8% of severe curvatures. In other words, fractures occurred at a greater frequency in mild and moderate curvatures rather than severe, overall, from every location.

DISCUSSION

The present study affords new insight into the localization and characteristics of instrument fractures in root canals, with the current data indicating that the middle third is the most common location of separation. Of 887 radiographs analyzed, 54.3% of instrument fractures occurred in the middle third, approximal 24.5% of instrument fractures in the apical third 24.5%) and approximal 19.8% in the coronal third, with 12 extended beyond the periapex (1.4% of all fractures). Fractures within and at a canal curvature (54.3%), occurred considerably more often than those above (19.8%) or beyond (25.8%), further illustrating the variable contributions of anatomy and mechanical demands imposed on the instrument when considering rotary instrumentation for root canal therapy.

The division of the root canal into coronal, middle, and apical thirds is not arbitrary: this tripartite division is rooted in endodontic diagnostics and treatment planning because biomechanical and anatomical properties vary the most in these three locations. The coronal third is usually the largest in diameter and most dentin is found here, allowing optimal straight-line access to offer the highest retrieval rates, often >90% especially if ultrasonic driven. The middle third is where straight and curved canal anatomy take place. This particular morphology can increase the concentration of stress on a file and this region contains the least amount of dentin in the canal, thereby increasing the likelihood of a file separation and retrieval success is only moderate (55%–70%). Finally, the apical third is the narrowest geometrically diminutive region of the root canal system that is also the most curved. This third has the greatest limitations and operationally is the most difficult to achieve access; therefore, extraction success is low (<15%) in this region, if possible, may require surgical intervention.

In addition, this spatial classification accords with existing clinical decision pathways and serves as a standardized reference framework across the procedural guidelines. To further support this anatomical model, our work has developed an innovative two-part classification system which describes the (1) spatial extent of the fracture and (2) the relationship to canal curvature (above, inside, or beyond). In combination, this spatial and curvilinear classification system improves diagnostic clarity, guides retrieval approach and provides reproducibility of reporting for clinical and research purposes.

To ensure accuracy, two endodontists experienced in the interpretation of X-rays independently assessed a set of radiographs. The rigor of methodology for data gathering was established by using the kappa test, and the outcome confirmed inter-observer agreement (≥0.80), which confirmed the repeatability and clinical importance of data collection.

Despite the ongoing advancements in endodontic instruments and techniques, the lack of a universal classification system for instrument fractures has hindered uniform reporting, data collection, and communication in both clinical and research settings.[12] To address this gap, we provided a two-part classification system that categorizes instrument separation based on anatomic location (Part 1) and with respect to canal curvature (Part 2), as presented in Table 2, allowing for a more nuanced and clinically relevant classification and proposal for study. Part 1 identifies fractures of relatively straight canals according to location – the coronal, middle, or apical thirds and the level of development, even accounting for fractures that progress over to adjoining segments or even into periapical areas. This first part further requires classification into type (types 1–4) and then sub-type (sub-types A-D), presenting an anatomical topographical classification in terms of where the coronal head was in relation to the fracture segment’s location. Part 2 presents Schneider’s classification of canal curvature and defines the fracture location in terms of the curve as AC, IC, and BC.

Importantly, the results of the current study differ from previous studies that reported instrument separation in the apical third of the root canal as the most common location, as reported by Pedir et al.,[13] and Madarati et al.[14] This difference may be due to the differing methods of canal preparation, sample descriptions, or operator experience in each study. More middle-third fractures may, perhaps, demonstrate stresses from torsional fatigue during file engagement in narrow or irregular canal contours.

It may seem counterintuitive that fractures occur more readily in mildly to moderately curved canals than that of severely curved canals; however, this is supported by recent investigations which have reported operating variables and cyclic fatigue are more predictive of fracture than curvature alone, Instrument fatigue is thought to occur in areas which are subject to repetitive bending fatigue (especially the middle third, which is generally the transitional hollow between straight and curved) during loading.

The frequency of middle third fractures and fractures related to curvature underscored the multifactorial nature of inundation of instrument separation. Although cyclic fatigue is the main mechanism of instrument separation; this is especially true when viewed on curved canals, where alternating tensile and compressive forces are exerted, cyclic fatigue’s mechanical loading is compounded in developing countries in which practitioners do not have the luxury of inspection or replacement of instruments before re-use as reported by Li et al.,[15] and Orozco-Ocampo et al.[16] This can ultimately lead to reduced fatigue thresholds.[1718]

The field of instrument metallurgy, particularly concerning the application of heat-treated NiTi alloys, is of considerable importance. Heat-treated instruments exhibit enhanced flexibility and resistance to fatigue when compared to conventional NiTi files as rightly demonstrated by Silva et al.,[19] and Tanomaru-Filho et al.[20] Systems such as the Self-Adjusting File also preserve root-fracture resistance comparable to unprepared controls.[21] However, improper usage, prolonged clinical application, or non-compliance with recommended torque and speed parameters can lead to fractures, even in systems that have been thermally optimized.[22]

The proposed classification system has several benefits [Figure 2]. From a clinical standpoint, it can improve documentation, assist with treatment decisions, and provide guidance for retrieval versus bypass methods based on the fracture location and depth. Clinical retrieval success rates as high as 92% have been reported when a bur-platform technique is employed.[23] From an educational perspective, it enhances the systematic teaching of fracture patterns to incorporate anatomy, instrument design, and procedural intricacies. Importantly, the proposed system is able to address miscommunication that has been previously described in the literature. Madarati et al. described the confusion of terminology used to describe the separation of an instrument.[24] A standardized, organized classification, as proposed here, adds support to clinically significant reporting and helps to facilitate both meaningful data synthesis for systematic reviews and in clinical trials.[25] In addition to improving diagnostic clarity, the classification aligns with pedagogical models that promote structured knowledge acquisition among dental students and professionals. Furthermore, its practical relevance in daily practice aligns with studies emphasizing the impact of procedural standardization on the clinical outcome.

While this study introduced a clinically relevant, robust, and stratified classification system, a number of limitations must be recognized. Instrument separation is inevitably a multifactorial phenomenon, influenced by many clinical and technical factors. There are many factors that contribute including, metallurgy of the instrument alloy, number of clinical reuses, irrigant or lubricant used, adherence to the manufacturer’s shaping sequence, operator training, and experience. We recognize that all of the above variables have effects on fracture risk. However, due to the retrospective and radiographic nature of our study, we were unable to collect standardized operative details from all centers participating in the study, preventing us from including it into our statistical model and assessing its impact, limiting our capacity to make causal inferences.

Notwithstanding that limitation, the key strengths of this study come from a large and diverse dataset that is representative across the nation and therefore allows for strong generalizability and needed descriptive overview into the prevalence and patterns of instrument separation. Future prospective multicenter studies that systematically record detailed clinical metadata, including instrument type, usage, compliance to usage protocols, and clinician skill level, will be critical in objectively quantifying the overall influences of the above variables and to subsequently develop predictive models of instrument separation.

Furthermore, this research did not capture whether periapical radiolucencies were present or not, which may affect the mechanics of and visibility of fractures radiographically. There was also no information on whether the separated instruments were single-use instruments or previously used (SSA), which would prohibit conclusions from being made regarding fatigue failure. Future research may expand the characterization of fracture patterns with other three-dimensional imaging techniques such as cone-beam computed tomography. Longitudinal research into operator variability, root canal shape, instrument design, and intracanal stress distribution could provide further insight into the aspects that promote separation of instruments.

The classification system proposed in this manuscript is comprehensive as presented, but could be further refined to integrate variables such as instrument motion kinematics (i.e. rotary vs. reciprocating), alloy type, and levels of heat treatment. Extending the datasets through larger, multi-center collaborations would improve the generalizability of the classification system and more accurately represent the clinical complexities of practice in different contexts.

CONCLUSIONS

This study introduces an imposing and unified approach to classification for instrument fractures, addressing an important need in endodontic practice. The proposed classification may improve communication, aid in clinical decision-making, and accelerate research by providing a methodical approach to identification. In order to lower the frequency of fractures and enhance the patient outcomes, the results highlight the necessity of ongoing innovation in equipment design and operator training.
