Impact of molecular multimodality on neural network models for prediction tasks related to drug discovery
Authors: Marcos Martínez Galindo, Marco Luca Sbodio, Mykhaylo Zayats, Rodrigo Ordonez-Hurtado, Raúl Fernández-Díaz, Vanessa López García, Hoang Thanh Lam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe number of unimodal molecule representation constantly increases, and researchers investigate how to combine them. Intuitively, multimodal representations may provide complementary information and combining them promises better performance. In this work, we systematically explore how combining multiple molecular modalities affects the performance of downstream prediction tasks, providing a baseline for informed decision making. Our study covers 7 molecular modalities and combines them using intermediate and late fusion, and 2 neural network architectures (with or without using knowledge graphs). We conduct experiments with 3 benchmarks for drug-target binding affinity, and 22 molecule property prediction. In total, we train and evaluate over 1400 models. In summary, our results show that combining multiple modalities improve the performance provided that effective fusion strategies are chosen. Knowledge-enhanced representation learning further boosts model performance. Notably, we find that even the use of simple late-fusion approaches establishes state-of-the-art results for some tasks.Subject terms: Drug discovery and development, Computational chemistry, Computational chemistry

Introduction

Drug discovery is a complex and expensive process that, in general, heavily relies on extensive laboratory assays. Accelerating this process has become a central goal in biomedical research. Recently, research on drug discovery has increasingly adopted artificial intelligence (AI) models, including both generative models that help designing novel drug candidates1, and predictive models that estimate ligand-target binding affinities or drug properties2. At the core of AI-driven drug discovery lies small molecule representation learning, which involves building foundation models for small molecules using self-supervised learning approaches applied to publicly available databases. The number of such models has rapidly grown in recent years3. Each model is typically trained on one molecular modality among the different options available, including 2D images4, 3D structures5,6, molecular graphs7,8, and 1D strings (SMILES9–12 or SELFIES13,14). Consequently, model performance is influenced by both the data modality it observes and the data used for training the model.

Recent studies have explored multimodal fusion by introducing models that integrate information from multiple molecular modalities15. However, most of these efforts have focused on early fusion strategies, in which all modalities are combined during pretraining. Despite the rapid growth of powerful pretrained unimodal models, key questions about multimodal architectures remain unanswered. For instance, is the incorporation of multiple modalities always beneficial? When building a multimodal model, a crucial design choice is the fusion strategy, how strongly does it influence performance? As highlighted in ref. 16, performance depends not only on which modalities are used and how they are fused, but also on whether knowledge-enhanced representation learning is incorporated, particularly in intermediate fusion approaches. This raises another critical open question: what is the impact of multimodality when knowledge-enhanced representation learning is applied?

We present a comprehensive analysis to address these questions. We train and evaluate over 1400 neural network models, performing more than 6000 experiments that systematically explore combinations across three main dimensions: (1) Diverse model architectures: we train both knowledge-enhanced and simple neural network models that combine multiple molecular modalities using different fusion strategies: a simple concatenation of unimodal embeddings followed by training on downstream tasks; knowledge-enhanced fusion through integration with knowledge graph representations; and late fusion approaches, ensemble-based approaches that combine predictions from unimodal models; (2) Diverse benchmarks: we evaluate our models on 2 tasks from the widely adopted Therapeutics Data Commons (TDC) initiative17–19: prediction of drug-target interaction (DTI), and prediction of drug pharmacological properties. For the first task, we use 3 TDC DTI benchmark datasets with 7 distinct train/test splits to assess generalization performance; for the second task, we use 22 TDC ADMET (Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, Excretion, and Toxicity) benchmark datasets; (3) Diverse modalities: we test different combinations of 7 pretrained unimodal models covering a wide spectrum of small molecule representations, including fingerprints, SMILES/SELFIES, molecular graphs 2D images, and 3D molecular graphs. Each modality offers a distinct inductive bias–from symbolic topology to spatial geometry–and understanding how these biases interact is crucial for effective multimodal molecular learning.

Our analysis reveals several important insights into multimodal fusion strategies for drug discovery applications. Integrating additional modalities generally leads to improved performance of the prediction model, provided that effective fusion strategies are employed. We observe that combining the same modalities with different fusion strategies or architectures results in significant different performance in the downstream prediction tasks. Late fusion consistently outperforms intermediate fusion across most benchmarks, and knowledge-enhanced models deliver additional gains when relevant graphs are available. Specifically, we achieve state-of-the-art results on the DTI-DG benchmark dataset, improving the Pearson correlation from 0.58 to 0.64, which represents an improvement of approximately 10.34%. A simple, ensemble-based, late-fusion strategy that combines predictions from independently trained unimodal models proves to be the most effective fusion method for several TDC ADMET datasets, leading to state-of-the-art results in some benchmarks.

Overall, our findings cover a wide range of modalities, neural network architectures, fusion approaches, and downstream tasks, and provide useful insights that may accelerate the development of future molecular multimodal models for drug discovery and development.

In this work, we investigate different research questions regarding the number of modalities to use, the fusion approach, or the need of pretraining. Our experiments show that there is not a combination of modalities, a fusion approach, or a pretraining approach that is always better than the others, and optimal performance depends on the interplay of modalities, fusion strategy, and architecture, with the results varying from one benchmark to others. Although for a single dataset the best combination is usually not one with the maximum number of modalities, on average, more modalities tend to yield better results, especially in the case of late fusion approaches. Knowledge-enhanced pretraining also helps to get better results, although there are some datasets in which the Feed Forward Neural Network without pretraining is better than Otter-Knowledge (e.g., Davis Drug), on average, the results with Otter-Knowledge are better than the equivalent ones with the Feed Forward Neural Network. The fusion strategy plays an equally important role: late fusion consistently yields larger gains than intermediate fusion. With thousands of experiments across 29 different benchmarks, we provide to the research community a detailed analysis to help them decide which combination of modalities or approach to use. With our results, researchers can enhance their decision process without having to train again the same models and combinations, hopefully helping them to save time and resources.

Results

We report the results of our training and evaluation experiments performed on a variety of neural network models on two relevant tasks for drug discovery. More specifically, we conduct experiments using benchmarks from the Therapeutics Data Commons (TDC) initiative17–19: 3 drug-target interaction (DTI) benchmarks (namely, DG, DAVIS, and KIBA, with random, drug, and target train/test splits for DAVIS and KIBA)20, and 22 ADMET property prediction benchmarks21. We build multimodal models based on two architectures: a feed-forward neural network (hereafter referred to as FFNN), and with a knowledge-enhanced neural network. The latter is based on a graph neural network (GNN)22 trained on a large knowledge graph; we refer to this approach as Otter-Knowledge (hereafter referred to as OK)16. We use different combinations of 7 unimodal molecular models: ChemBERTa (CB)10,23, MolFormer (MOLF)11, SMI-TED (SMI)9, Morgan Fingerprint (MF)24,25, MolGen (MG)14, ImageMol with classification layer (IMC)4, and UniMol (UM)5 (in the rest of the text, we use the acronyms reported in parenthesis to refer to these models). We use 2 fusion strategies, namely intermediate and late fusion, to combine subsets of the 7 unimodal molecular models. In total, we train and evaluate 1467 models and provide results for 6039 different experiments (details about the unimodal models and benchmarks are reported in the Supplementary Information).

For the 3 DTI benchmarks (DG, DAVIS, KIBA), we train and evaluate both an FFNN and an OK model using both intermediate and late fusion. In these experiments, we combine the Evolutionary Scale Modeling (ESM-2 650M)26 model (a unimodal model for protein sequence representation) with subsets of the 7 unimodal molecular models CB, MOLF, SMI, MF, MG, IMC, UM. For the 22 ADMET benchmarks, we train and evaluate only a FFNN model, using both intermediate and late fusion. Since the downstream task in the ADMET datasets is drug property prediction, it would be useless to build a knowledge graph from them, as they don’t contain protein sequences, and therefore the resultant knowledge graph would be a single node of the drug. Thus, we do not use knowledge-enhanced neural network models for the ADMET datasets. In experiments with intermediate fusion, we consider combinations that contain only one model from the set of sequence-based modalities ({CB, MOLF, SMI, MG}) because they provide similar (and potentially overlapping) information, since {CB, MOLF, SMI} are pretrained on SMILES, and MG is pretrained on SELFIES. In experiments with late fusion, we do not impose that restriction because, once the results for a single modality have been computed, combining them is computationally less expensive than intermediate fusion, and the final prediction can still be beneficial (see Method for more details). To compare the results, we aggregate those that use the same metric. We split the ADMET benchmarks into 4 different subsets depending on the metric: Spearson’s Correlation Coefficient (SCC); Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Area Under the Receiver-Operating Curve (AUROC); Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (AUPRC). For the DTI benchmarks, all of them use the Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) as an evaluation metric.

The raw results presented in the Supplementary material reveal that no single combination of molecular modalities consistently surpasses all others across every benchmark; instead, the optimal combination varies depending on the specific benchmark for both OK and FFNN. Despite this variability, our findings enable us to investigate and address several important questions concerning the use of multiple modalities, various fusion techniques, and different neural network architectures.

Benefits of modality addition

One of the key questions is whether adding more modalities is always beneficial. To answer this question, we aggregated our experimental results based on the number of molecular modalities and then calculated the average.

Figure 1 shows the distributions of the average PCC, SCC, -MAE, AUROC, and AUPRC scores across benchmarks using these metrics, grouped by number of modalities. Therein, we observe two clear tendencies: (1) As the number of modalities increases, there is a noticeable decrease in both the size of the interquartile ranges and the spread of outliers. While this variance is influenced by the differing number of experiments within each group, an interesting pattern emerges when comparing groups of equivalent sizes. For instance, whether we compare one modality versus six, two versus five, or three versus four, the trend consistently shows reduced variability with increased modalities. This suggests that incorporating more modalities generally leads to more consistent results across different experimental setups; (2) The median, maximum, upper quartile, minimum, and lower quartile tend to improve, which indicates that the use of more modalities tends to produce better results.

Impact of the fusion approach

As shown in the previous section, adding more modalities is generally beneficial. However, we observe that, to some extent, this trend is not uniform across fusion strategies: the FFNN-IF does not show consistent gains and the improvements for OK-IF are less pronounced than for OK-LF. This raises a key question: to what extent does the choice of fusion strategy influence performance?

In order to answer this question, we calculate, for every experiment and architecture (OK or FFNN), the difference between the late fusion (LF) approaches and the intermediate fusion (IF) approaches. Specifically, for each combination of modalities in each architecture (OK or FFNN,), we subtract the value of the combination using IF from the value of the combination using LF. Figure 3a shows the number of times LF outperforms IF (in red), the number of times IF outperforms LF (in blue) and the draws (in green); note that for one single modality, both fusion approaches yield the same result, as there is only one modality to be fused. The results reveal that LF is better than IF in the vast majority of cases.

Analysis of modalities

As seen in the above results (details in Supplementary material), there is not a combination that is clearly better than the others. But, is there a best modality? Is there a modality that guarantees better performance when combined with others? Fig. 7 shows the distribution of the average scores of the experiments that contain each modality. From it, we can see that, generally, the distribution of scores for individual modalities is broadly similar, with no practical difference from one modality to the others, indicating most modalities provide informative representations. Several notable patterns emerge, for example, some modalities exhibit substantially wider distribution of scores — ChemBERTa (CB), SMI-TED (SMI), and MolGen (MG) under the OK-LF setting have bigger interquartile ranges — suggesting greater instability or sensitivity to the fusion and architecture choices. Conversely, modalities such as MolFormer (MOLF) and Morgan fingerprints (MF) consistently deliver strong and stable performance across approaches, indicating their robustness and broad utility. The results depended on the approach (both architecture and fusion approach) used, and modalities that performs well under one setting may not be advantageous under another. Some modalities, however, show always positive SHAP values (e.g., UM, MOLF), which means they are always beneficial. Building on prior work27,28, we performed a post-hoc meta-analysis using a surrogate model to systematically quantify feature importance. Specifically, we trained a Random Forest model to predict experimental outcomes, using an input dataset comprising a categorical variable for dataset type and binary indicators for modalities, neural network architectures, and fusion strategies. This structured approach allowed us to assess the relative influence of these factors on performance. For more details, see Modality analysis with Random Forests. Feature importance was evaluated via SHAP values computed with the TreeExplainer utility from the SHAP Python package29. To test significance, we applied Holm-adjusted Wilcoxon tests comparing SHAP values against zero; only the inclusion of UM and the use of late fusion yielded values significantly different from zero. Pairwise Conover tests (Holm-adjusted) further revealed that UM and late fusion exhibited significantly higher importance than all other features. These findings are consistent with empirical trends: UM frequently appears in top-performing configurations, likely due to its complementary geometric information.

When combining different modalities, it is not only important that each modality gives a good representation, but it is also important that each modality contributes to the final result with complementary knowledge to the other modalities. To assess complementarity, we get the predictions for each architecture using a single modality. Pairwise Pearson correlations between unimodal predictions reveal that some modalities provide highly redundant information, whereas others are more distinct. Figure 8 shows the average correlation, aggregating the correlations for each benchmark using the mean. For OK, Fig. 8a, all modalities are very correlated, with SMI and MF differing slightly from the others. With the FFNN, Fig. 8b, we observe that IMC is clearly the most uncorrelated modality. For ADMET, Fig. 8c, SMI emerges as the most divergent modality. While uncorrelated modalities could bring complementary information, in this case, SMI differs so much from the other modalities, that this indicates the distribution of scores is extremely different to the other modalities, a finding consistent with its poorer results across multiple metrics. This is aligned with the results, as IMC is clearly detrimental for FFNN (Fig. 7, AVG PCC - IMC) and SMI consistently underperforms in the ADMET benchmarks, with really low minimums (Fig. 7, AVG -MAE, AVG SCC, AVG AUROC, AVG AUPRC - SMI).

Discussion

Our experiments show that no single combination of modalities consistently outperforms the others across all benchmarks. The effectiveness of each configuration depends on the model architecture, benchmark dataset, and fusion strategy. While the optimal setup for a given dataset rarely includes all available modalities, using a larger number generally improves average performance—particularly under late fusion. Thus, multimodal fusion benefits from systematic cross-validation to identify the most complementary subset of modalities.

In our experiments, knowledge-enhanced intermediate fusion (OK-IF) proves effective when relevant external knowledge is available before fine-tuning on the downstream task. However, the feed-forward neural network with late fusion (FFNN-LF) often achieves superior performance, underscoring the importance of selecting an appropriate fusion strategy. Late fusion and multimodality pretraining yield the highest accuracy but require increased computational demand: higher input dimensionality, longer training times, and separate models per modality. As shown in Table 1, top-performing configurations typically have more parameters, especially when moving from intermediate to late fusion. The trade-off between accuracy and computational cost should guide model selection based on application requirements. Figure 2 provides a quantitative breakdown of the relative contributions of the number of modalities, the use of late fusion, and knowledge enhancement to performance. If the computational cost is critical for the user, this analysis can serve as a starting point for the decision on where to spend the available resources.

The added cost of moving from a single modality to a few additional ones under intermediate fusion is marginal, as seen in Table 1, the number of parameters of the IF combination is slightly bigger than the models of the individual modalities. In contrast, late fusion requires training a separate model for each modality, substantially increasing computational demand, but offering greater flexibility: once individual models are trained, all possible combinations can be explored efficiently, and new modalities can be integrated without retraining.

Our analysis also highlights intrinsic differences among modalities. Certain modalities, such as MF, MOLF and UM, consistently contribute to strong performance and should be included whenever possible. Others, such as SMI, provide limited benefits and can be omitted under resource constraints. Some modalities, such as IMC, show architecture-specific behavior, improving performance within Otter-Knowledge but degrading it for FFNN. These findings emphasize that modality selection depends not only on the intrinsic quality of a representation but also on its interaction with downstream architectures and fusion strategies. Overall, adding more modalities tends to be beneficial, and even multiple models of the same modality type can provide complementary information.

Ultimately, the strength of multimodal learning lies in its ability to integrate complementary views of the data, potentially leading to improved results. Notably, our experiments achieve state-of-the-art performance on several benchmarks: DTI-DG, Half Life Obach, Clearance Hepatocyte AZ, DILI, BBB Martins, CYP2C9 substrate (CarbonMangels), and CYP3A4 substrate (CarbonMangels) (see Supplementary Tables 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 and 11 in the Supplementary Note 5). These gains demonstrate that carefully designed multimodal architectures can meaningfully advance predictive modeling in drug discovery.

Methods

Modalities and experiments

In our experiments, we analyze different combinations of 7 different modalities: Morgan Fingerprint (MF)24,25, a fingerprint; ImageMol with classification layer (IMC)4, a model based on 2D images; Uni-Mol (UM)5, for 3D structures and graphs; ChemBERTa (CB)10,23, a 1D sequence representation based on SMILES; SMI-TED (SMI)9, a 1D sequence representation based on SMILES; MolFormer (Molf)11, a 1D sequence representation based on SMILES; MolGen (MG)14, a 1D sequence representation based on SELFIES.

The selection of the above modalities was guided by both diversity and representativeness. We included heterogeneous molecular modalities (including fingerprints, sequence-based (SMILES/SELFIES) or 1D, 2D spatial representations, and 3D geometric structure) to explore how distinct types of representations interact and complement one another. Sequence-based modalities (e.g., SMILES, protein sequences) primarily capture topological and symbolic relationships, reflecting the local chemical grammar and connectivity of molecules. Image-based and 3D structure modalities encode spatial and geometric information, which is crucial for capturing stereochemistry and binding-site complementarity in interaction tasks. Fingerprint-based representations (e.g., Morgan Fingerprint) incorporate handcrafted substructural patterns, acting as high-level summaries of chemical features. For the string representation, we also included multiple models to (i) assess the impact of model choice within a single modality, and (ii) quantify inter- and intra-modality similarity, thereby evaluating whether combining complementary models or modalities provides additional benefits. Due to computational and time constraints, only a representative subset of modalities was analyzed. Further details on modality and model selection are provided in Supplementary Note 1.

The experiments are designed to combine different modalities, resulting in 39 total combinations for the intermediate fusion approaches (39 for Otter-Knowledge, 39 for Simple Neural Network). Combinations containing multiple SMILES-based encoders (CB, MOLF, SMI, MG) were excluded from intermediate fusion experiments to avoid redundancy and excessive computational demand. The modality types we consider are fingerprints, 2D images, 3D structures, and string representations (SMILES/SELFIES). In contrast, for the Late Fusion approaches, we do combine modalities of the same type due to the low cost of the approach (once the individual models have been trained) and to further explore the impact of adding more modalities and combining modalities of the same type, resulting in a total of 127 combinations. Our assumption is that for Intermediate Fusion, the modalities must be complementary, as the model has to combine the embeddings and learn from them. On the other hand, the Late Fusion approach requires the final predictions to be combined, and therefore combinations of modalities of the same type can still be beneficial, even if they are not complementary. We run each combination for each dataset, resulting in 39 intermediate-fusion and 127 late-fusion combinations, yielding a total of 6039 experiments (this number doesn’t include the different seeds used for model selection, hyperparameter optimization, and ensembling).

Approaches

In order to solve the research questions stated in the Introduction, we use 4 different approaches: (i) a knowledge-enhanced neural network pretrained on a large knowledge graph with intermediate fusion (OK IF), (ii) a knowledge-enhanced neural network pretrained on a large knowledge graph with late fusion (OK LF), (iii) a simple neural network without knowledge enhancement and with intermediate fusion (FFNN IF), and (iv) a simple neural network without knowledge enhancement and with late fusion (FFNN LF). As the knowledge-enhanced neural network will be a more complex architecture than the simple neural network, and in order to compare the approaches in a more fair way, we do not use any early stopping or best model selection for the knowledge-enhanced neural network, while we do use best model selection for the simple neural network. Figure 9 shows an overview of the architectures and fusion approaches used.

Overview of the approaches.

The top part shows the Feed Forward Neural Network (FFNN) while the bottom part shows a knowledge-enhanced neural network pretrained on a large knowledge graph (OK). On the left side, with intermediate fusion (IF), on the right side, with late fusion (LF).

For the knowledge-enhanced neural network pretrained on a large knowledge graph, we adopt the Otter-Knowledge approach16, which leverages an inductive graph neural network (GNN) pretrained on a large knowledge graph to integrate multiple modalities. Otter-Knowledge enhances the representations of entities such as drugs and proteins by training inductive GNNs22 on Multimodal Knowledge Graphs (MKGs). MKGs are directed, labeled graphs where node and edge labels carry well-defined semantics, and each node is associated with a model to compute the initial embeddings. Two types of nodes are distinguished: entity nodes (representing entities) and attribute nodes (capturing properties of entities)16.

To construct our MKG, we combined data from BindingDB and UniProt, defining three types of entities: Drug, Protein, and Measurement, where Measurement links Drugs to Proteins. Additional details about the MKG construction are provided in Supplementary Note 2.1. The Otter-Knowledge architecture consists of two main components: (1) Encoder: The initial embeddings of each node are projected into a shared embedding space (embedding size of 512) using three projection layers. This is followed by a multi-relational graph convolutional network (R-GCN)30 with one hidden layer, which accounts for different edge types via relation-specific parameters; (2) Decoder: A binary classification head is trained for link prediction. Negative samples are generated synthetically by randomly pairing admissible source and target nodes for each relation type, maintaining a 1:1 ratio between positive and negative links. In addition, a regression objective minimizes the root-mean-square error (RMSE) of predicted numerical properties. Skip connections31 are incorporated in the GNN layers to improve gradient flow during training.

To efficiently handle large graphs, Otter-Knowledge employs the Graph Auto-Scaling (GAS) approach32, partitioning the MKG into smaller subgraphs using METIS33. In our experiments, we partition the MKG into 1000 parts. We train the GNN for 73 epochs using a learning rate of 0.0001, without employing early stopping or best model selection.

Following16, we augmented the base network — which integrates various modality combinations (e.g., images, SMILES, and protein sequences) — by adding a parallel branch that processes and transforms GNN embeddings. After the pretraining phase, we fine-tuned the encoders of the pretrained GNN on the benchmark training datasets. We then appended four fully-connected layers and a regression head to fuse the representations of targets and molecules for binding affinity prediction. Consistent with the findings in ref. 16, which showed that transferring pretrained encoders yields better performance than transferring representations, we adopted this strategy in our experiments.

For each experiment, we use 5 different pre-defined seed values and report an average of the 5 computed test scores.

We employ a Feed-Forward Neural Network (FFNN) as a baseline fusion model. It follows an intermediate fusion paradigm similar to Otter-Knowledge, but uses a simpler architecture without knowledge enhancement, resulting in fewer trainable parameters. Each modality embedding is processed through a dedicated projection block consisting of a single fully-connected layer that maps the input into a shared latent space. The projected drug embeddings are then averaged across modalities, while the protein features are derived from a single modality (ESM embeddings). The predicted affinity score is obtained as the inner product between the aggregated drug embedding and the protein embedding. The model is trained using Mean Squared Error (MSE) as a loss function, and we use the Pearson correlation between predicted and true affinity scores to select the best model, providing an efficient yet effective approach for capturing cross-modal relationships.

All modality embeddings are projected into a common 512-dimensional space. The network is trained for 25,000 epochs with a learning rate of 0.0001. Model selection is performed by choosing the test score corresponding to the best validation performance. Each experiment is repeated with five predefined random seeds, and the reported results correspond to the average test performance across these runs.

Multimodal fusion can be performed at different stages of the learning pipeline. Following previous literature34,35, we distinguish three principal strategies: Early fusion (input fusion): Modalities are concatenated and jointly processed by a single model; Intermediate fusion: Each modality is first encoded independently into a latent representation, which are then combined and processed by another model to generate predictions; Late fusion (output or decision-level fusion): Each modality produces its own prediction, which are subsequently aggregated using ensemble methods such as averaging or voting.

In this work, we employ intermediate and late-fusion strategies. In intermediate fusion, each modality is projected into a shared representation space before being jointly processed to produce the final prediction. In late fusion, predictions from single-modality models are averaged to obtain a combined output. For instance, in the Otter-Knowledge late fusion approach, Otter-Knowledge produces predictions for each modality (MF, IMC, UM, SMI, CB, MG, MOLF), and the mean of these predictions forms the final decision. Besides the intermediate fusion, both Otter-Knowledge and the feed-forward neural network (FFNN) perform the fusion at the prediction level rather than at the representation level, and are thus categorized as late fusion methods.

In addition, in intermediate fusion, the network must directly integrate heterogeneous embeddings; therefore, strong complementarity between modalities is required. In contrast, late fusion aggregates decisions rather than representations, allowing the combination of highly redundant or same-type modalities. Therefore, and as opposed to intermediate fusion, we decided to include combinations of multiple SMILES-based encoders (CB, MOLF, SMI, MG).

Datasets

To evaluate the performance of different modality combinations, we used three datasets from the TDC Drug-Target Interaction (DTI) benchmark suite20 (namely, DTI-DG, DAVIS, and KIBA), and 22 drug-property prediction datasets from the ADMET benchmark21.

The DTI-DG dataset includes a leaderboard with state-of-the-art results and employs a temporal split based on patent application dates. In contrast, DAVIS and KIBA use random splits, along with additional target-based and drug-based splits that assess model generalization to unseen drugs or proteins. Following16, we removed overlapping triples between the test sets and the pretrained knowledge graphs to prevent data leakage. Detailed descriptions of all benchmarks are provided in Supplementary Note 2.

Modality analysis with Random Forests

With the aim to quantify the contribution of each modality, fusion approach, and architecture, and also to assess potential relationships with dataset type, we trained Random Forest models36 using predictions from all experiments as inputs. We constructed a dataset with the following binary or categorical features: Modality: Binary indicators denoting the presence (1) or absence (0) of each modality in a given experiment. Accepted values: MF, IMC, UM, MOLF, CB, SMI, and MG; Type: Categorical variable denoting the dataset type. Accepted values: 0 for physicochemical, 1 for absorption, 2 for distribution, 3 for metabolism, 4 for excretion, 5 cor toxicity, and 6 for DTI; Architecture: Binary indicator for the used architecture. Accepted values: 1 if the experiment used the OK architecture, 0 otherwise; Fusion approach: Binary indicator for the used fusion approach. Accepted values: 1 if the experiment used the late-fusion (LF) approach, 0 otherwise.

The target variable for the Random Forrest models corresponds to the predicted value obtained from the original experiments. For each test sample across all benchmarks, we collected predictions from all models and transformed them into the described feature space to train the Random Forest models. Only samples from the test set were used to avoid biases arising from model training.

Following a leave-one-dataset-out (LODO) cross-validation scheme, we trained separate Random Forest models, each excluding one benchmark as the test set. Feature importance was quantified using SHAP values computed with the TreeExplainer method from the SHAP library29. This enabled both global and dataset-type-specific interpretations of feature relevance.

To assess statistical significance, we performed a Wilcoxon signed-rank test against zero to determine whether each feature’s SHAP values differed significantly from zero. Pairwise comparisons among features were then conducted using the Conover test to identify whether the SHAP values for one feature were significantly higher or lower than another. All p-values were adjusted using the Holm-Bonferroni correction37.

Cosine similarity

To assess the degree of alignment between modalities, we computed the cosine similarity between their corresponding embeddings produced by each unimodal model. Because the embedding spaces of each modality differ in dimensionality, we first applied principal component analysis (PCA)38 to reduce each embedding set to 128 components and mitigate redundancy. We then employed generalized canonical correlation analysis (GCCA)39 with 32 latent dimensions to project all modalities into a shared representational space, enabling direct cross-modal comparison of the resultant vectors.
