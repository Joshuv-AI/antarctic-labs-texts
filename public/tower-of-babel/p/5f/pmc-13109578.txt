Conditional reliability of chlorophyll fluorescence for inferring photosynthesis in Ginkgo biloba: evidence from fluctuating light and drought conditions
Authors: Jimei Han, Peng Ding, Lehao Li, Lin Cao, Ying Zhu, Fuliang Cao, Tingting Dai, Gaiping Wang, Fangfang Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSolar-induced chlorophyll fluorescence (SIF) is a promising photosynthetic proxy and a valuable phenotyping tool. However, under drought and fluctuating light, the mechanistic linkages between SIF and photosynthesis require further investigation due to complex light energy partitioning. In this study, we measured leaf-level SIF, gas-exchange, and active fluorescence parameters in Ginkgo biloba under drought and fluctuating light. Our results demonstrate a drought-induced decoupling of both leaf-level SIF and PSII-emitted chlorophyll fluorescence (SIFPSII, derived from active fluorescence parameters) from photosynthesis. This was driven by a slight SIFPSII reduction alongside strong photosynthetic suppression. The limited SIFPSII decrease resulted from a stable fluorescence yield (ΦSIF), maintained by the regulatory interplay between non-photochemical quenching (NPQ) and the fraction of open PSII reaction centers (qL), and the low responsiveness of ΦSIF to changes in the maximum photochemical quantum yield (ΦPSIImax). In contrast, photosynthetic suppression was largely due to decreased stomatal conductance (gs). ΦPSIImax and photorespiration are also central to balancing the light and carbon reactions, preventing energy overload. Interestingly, fluctuating light phases did not significantly alter this decoupling. However, when combining data from both high and low light, SIFPSII and photosynthesis remained tightly coupled, likely due to their shared light dependency. These insights improve the physiological interpretation of SIF and underscore the importance of SIF-based multi-trait phenotyping models for accurate field monitoring.Keywords: Drought, Fluctuating light, Maximum photochemical quantum yield, Photosynthesis, Solar-induced chlorophyll fluorescence

Introduction

The fixation of carbon dioxide through photosynthesis is essential for plant growth and biomass accumulation [1,2], and it has been established as one of the most widely measured functional traits in plant phenotyping [3,4]. However, photosynthesis is highly sensitive to drought stress, which significantly reduces carbon assimilation [5,6]. Under field conditions, drought-affected plants are also subjected to fluctuating light caused by cloud movement or canopy shading (e.g., Refs. [7,8]). These co-occurring stresses interact in complex ways to affect photosynthetic dynamics. Therefore, elucidating how photosynthesis responds to the combined effects of drought and fluctuating light is crucial, as it directly informs strategies for enhancing plant growth and biomass accumulation under variable environments. This underscores the importance of developing reliable, non-invasive tools to monitor photosynthesis in real time across scales.

Remotely sensed solar-induced chlorophyll fluorescence (SIF) has emerged as a valuable tool in plant phenotyping, enabling non-invasive, real-time monitoring of photosynthesis across spatiotemporal scales (e.g., Refs. [9,10]). It is increasingly applied to assess plant physiological responses under drought conditions [[11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16]]. At larger spatial scales, the coupling between SIF and photosynthesis has been widely explored through statistical correlations (e.g., Refs. [17,18])and constrained process-based models (e.g., Refs. [19,20]), often demonstrating either linear (e.g., Refs. [9,21]) or non-linear (e.g., Refs. [[22], [23], [24]]) trends. However, at the leaf-scale, studies have revealed that drought stress can substantially weaken this coupling [[25], [26], [27]], suggesting that environmental stress may disrupt their underlying mechanistic linkage.

This disruption is largely due to drought-induced changes in key physiological processes, including non-photochemical quenching (NPQ) during light reactions and stomatal conductance (gs) during carbon assimilation [25,26]. These alterations introduce uncertainty into SIF-based estimates of carbon exchange [28,29]. Moreover, researches have indicated that SIF alone is insufficient for reliably indicating early plant stress [29,30]. Its effective use for early stress detection therefore requires integration with complementary optical indices that are sensitive to NPQ activation. A key candidate is the photochemical reflectance index (PRI), defined as the normalized difference in reflectance between 531 nm and 570 nm [[31], [32], [33]]. To tackle the underlying physiological complexity and the associated uncertainty in SIF interpretation, Gu et al. (2019) [34] formulated a mechanistic light reaction model of photosynthesis (MLR-SIF) that integrates NPQ, the fraction of open PSII reaction centers (qL), gs, and the maximum photochemical quantum yield (ΦPSIImax) to estimate photosynthetic activity from SIF. Numerous studies have demonstrated that NPQ tends to increase under drought as a protective mechanism to dissipate excess energy (e.g., Refs. [[35], [36], [37]]). Since NPQ competes with SIF for energy dissipation, its increase usually results in reduced SIF signals, thereby altering the SIF-photosynthesis relationship [38,39]. While studies on drought-induced changes in qL remain limited, qL has been shown to respond to environmental variation [7,22,34], potentially influencing energy partitioning and, consequently the SIF signal. Simultaneously, stomatal closure under drought stress reduced CO2 availability, further suppressing photosynthetic carbon assimilation (e.g., Refs. [[40], [41]]). This decline, independent of NPQ dynamics, may further contribute to a decoupling between SIF and photosynthesis. Furthermore, a potential decrease in ΦPSIImax under drought could modulate SIF, though its highly conserved nature across species makes its specific role unclear. More directly, drought stress often enhances photorespiration, thereby diverting the essential energy carriers ATP and NADPH away from carbon fixation and further disrupting the SIF-photosynthesis relationship.

In drought-prone environments, plants are often simultaneously exposed to fluctuating light conditions, which impose additional physiological challenges. NPQ, qL, and gs all exhibit dynamic responses to rapid changes in light intensity, introducing temporal mismatches in energy dissipation and carbon assimilation. For instance, in Populus trichocarpa, qL gradually increases after a transition from low to high light, while NPQ exhibits a rapid surge before stabilizing [7]. Conversely, under declining light, NPQ relaxes slowly, while qL recovers quickly. These observations suggest that the delayed NPQ relaxation and lagged qL induction are key regulatory factors affecting the SIF-photosynthesis relationship under fluctuating light and drought conditions. Additionally, light-driven stomatal behavior occurs at a notably slower rate than photosynthetic responses [42], further complicating the interpretation of SIF as a precise indicator of photosynthetic dynamics under stress.

To investigate how the combined effects of drought and fluctuating light influence photosynthesis, SIF, and their mechanistic relationships, we conducted controlled experiments using Ginkgo biloba L. (G. biloba) as the experimental material. As a unique and valuable relict tree species, G. biloba has both ecological and medicinal significance [43]. Despite its widespread cultivation and functional importance, the physiological responses of G. biloba to these co-occurring stresses, particularly in relation to SIF, remain poorly understood. Our measurements and data analysis were grounded in theoretical frameworks of photosynthetic light and carbon reactions (Fig. 1), addressing two objectives: (1) to determine whether SIF can reliably indicate photosynthesis under concurrent drought and fluctuating light conditions, a question critical for improving non-invasive phenotyping accuracy; and (2) to identify the physiological drivers of SIF-photosynthesis (de)coupling under these environmental constraints, with the aim of informing the development of more robust multi-trait phenotyping models that integrate key photosynthetic parameters.

A simplified integrated framework for enhancing the understanding of the connection between chlorophyll fluorescence and photosynthesis. Photosynthesis consists of the light and carbon reactions. The light reactions generate reducing energy (ATP and NADPH), which is then used in the carbon reactions. A: The light reactions describe the possible fates of an absorbed photon, including photochemical quenching (PQ), non-photochemical quenching (NPQ), and chlorophyll fluorescence. Solar-induced chlorophyll fluorescence (SIF) refers to the chlorophyll fluorescence detected by a sensor, with SIFPSII specifically representing chlorophyll fluorescence from photosystem II. The fraction of open reaction centers in photosystem II (qL) and the maximum photochemical quantum yield (ΦPSIImax) are key limiting factors for the linear electron transport rate (Jflu). B: The carbon reactions of photosynthesis focus on the processes that influence the final assimilation of CO2 into sugars. The CO2 concentration available for the Calvin cycle is regulated by stomatal conductance (gs) and mesophyll conductance (gm). Note that we assume gm to be infinite in our study.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and experimental design

The G. biloba seedlings was initially grown in a shade greenhouse (providing approximately half of natural light) at Xiashu Forest Farm (119°12′E, 32°07′N), affiliated with Nanjing Forestry University, located in Jurong, Jiangsu. The seeds were collected from Tushan Village Confucian Temple, Dalun Town, Jiangyan District, Jiangsu Province, and then planted into 12.0-L pots containing a mixture of loess and organic cultivation substrate (organic matter ≥35%; fertilizer: perlite = 4:1; pH 5.5-7.0) in a 1:4 ratio. To maintain the greenhouse temperature in line with the external environment, a fan was used to accelerate air circulation. Upon development of the fifth leaf, the three-year-old seedlings were transferred to custom-built, tunnel-shaped rain-out shelter. The structure was covered with polyethylene film on top but remained open on both sides to ensure excellent ventilation. The film allowed about 90% of natural light transmittance, as measured by an optical quantum sensor (Model 3415FQF; Spectrum Technologies, Inc., USA). The test site falls within a subtropical monsoon region, characterized by plentiful precipitation and solar radiation, with an annual mean temperature between 12 °C and 22 °C.

Drought treatments were initiated after a 10-day acclimation period under the shelter. The experiment included two treatments: well-watered and drought, with each consisting of 8 plants. In the well-watered group, all plants were initially irrigated daily to achieve 30% absolute water content (AWC) until the drought treatment began. To maintain consistent AWC, plants were weighed and watered daily in the evening. The daily maintenance weight consisted of the dry nutrient soil, pot, and water. Each pot with dry soil weighed 3.5 kg, of which 3.3 kg was contributed by the dry soil alone. The seedling weight was excluded, as measuring it without destructive methods was challenging. Once the drought treatment began, progressive water withholding was applied, followed by sustained water stress at a constant level. Drought stress was applied to half of the plants by stopping irrigation, and the pots were weighed every evening at nightfall. On the 8th day of the drought treatment, soil moisture had reached the target drought level of 20% AWC. This level was chosen based on pre-experimental tests, as it reliably induces measurable physiological responses in G. biloba seedlings without causing acute lethality. The drought state was maintained by weighing the pots every day to keep the water content constant for 7 days. The sixth fully expanded leaf counted from the apex of each plant was then measured. This specific leaf position was selected to provide a developmentally consistent and comparable measurement across all plants and treatments.

We first measured leaf water potential (ψw) to verify the effectiveness of the drought treatment. Following this confirmation, to analyze the (de)coupling between photosynthesis and SIF under different water treatments, SIF was measured using the AutoSIF vegetation fluorescence observation system (Bergsun Inc., Beijing, China), which is equipped with a QE65Pro spectrometer (Ocean Optics Inc., Dunedin, FL, USA), while photosynthetically active radiation (PAR) was simultaneously recorded with an optical quantum sensor (3415FQF; Spectrum Technologies, Inc., Aurora, IL, USA). Concurrently, photosynthetic parameters were assessed using the infrared gas-exchange system (Li-6800; LI-COR, Inc., Lincoln, NE, USA), with environmental conditions configured to match those used during SIF measurements to ensure temporal synchronization. SIF is typically defined as the radiance emitted at a given wavelength, measured in watts per steradian (W sr−1). However, only the physiologically relevant fluorescence emission, expressed in quantum units and spectrally and hemispherically integrated, directly correlates with photosynthesis. Therefore, to further explore the mechanism underlying the (de)coupling of photosynthesis from SIF and to determine whether water treatment alters the physiological response of plants to light, we obtained concurrent measurements of An and active fluorescence parameters using Li-6800 gas-exchange system with an attached 6800-01A leaf multiphase flash (MPF) fluorometer chamber (LI-COR, Inc.). These primary active fluorescence parameters were then used to calculate the physiologically relevant PSII-emitted chlorophyll fluorescence (SIFPSII) in quantum units (Eqn (6)), following the established methods of Gu et al. (2019) [34] and Han et al. (2022) [22]. Simultaneously, other relevant parameters were measured or derived for each leaf under both well-watered and drought conditions. All measurements of gas exchange and fluorescence parameters were performed outdoors under natural, sunny conditions. To achieve this, plants were moved out of the rain-out shelter one day prior to measurement. This setup served two critical purposes: (1) to eliminate potential structural interference from the shelter (e.g., shading, scattering, or reflection) that could compromise the accuracy of SIF retrievals, and (2) to leverage the natural diurnal variation in sunlight, allowing the AutoSIF system to capture SIF under the full range of daily light intensities, including peak irradiance, and thus to derive comprehensive SIF light-response curves. An overview of the measurement setup is provided in Fig. 2, and detailed procedures are described below.

An overview of the measurement setup.

Leaf water potential

Leaf water potential (ψw) was determined using a water potential meter (PMS-615; PMS Instrument Company, Albany, OR, USA). A top-positioned, fully matured leaf was removed from the G. biloba plant, and its petiole was carefully inserted and sealed in a compression gasket, with the cut surface exposed. The leaf was then placed in a pressure chamber, where pressure from a nitrogen cylinder was gradually applied until xylem became visible at the excised end of the petiole.

Determination of SIF using a fluorescence observation system

SIF was measured using the AutoSIF vegetation fluorescence observation system. During measurements, leaves were kept in their natural orientation. The QE65Pro spectrometer, integrated into the AutoSIF system, was calibrated for both spectral and radiative properties to collect spectral data. The system collects spectral data within the range of 480-850 nm. It operates with a spectral resolution of 0.9 nm and a sampling interval of 0.4 nm, and achieves a signal-to-noise ratio of up to 1000:1. The observation mode is “sandwich-style,” using an upward-facing irradiance sensor equipped with cosine correction to measure downward irradiance, and a downward-looking bare optical fiber (FOV: 25°) to detect upward radiance from the leaves. During measurement, the optical fiber probe was kept vertically at a fixed distance of 2 cm above the target leaf using a calibrated height-adjustable holder, creating an interrogation spot (0.89 cm diameter) consistently smaller than the minimum leaf width. This setup ensures that the recorded SIF signal is emitted solely by the selected leaf area, while effectively minimizing interference from ambient and soil-reflected light. SIF data within the 650-800 nm spectral window was extracted using the 3FLD extraction algorithm [44] embedded in the instrument's computational module. Although a systematic comparison of SIF retrieval methods was not the focus of this study, the SIF patterns retrieved by the 3FLD method closely aligned with independent active fluorescence measurements, confirming the reliability of the SIF data. To investigate the light response of SIF, measurements were conducted at various times throughout the day to capture a wide range of natural incident PAR, spanning from approximately 50 to 1800 μmol m−2 s−1. For the specific purpose of calculating the apparent reflectance (Refapp), we utilized spectral data captured under a stable mid-morning irradiance of approximately 1450-1500 μmol m−2 s−1. Refapp was calculated as the ratio of downward irradiance to the average of two upward-facing irradiance measurements. The apparent reflectance from 750 to 800 nm was normalized to 0-1 range using the formula: (Refapp-min(Refapp))/(max(Refapp)-min(Refapp)) under both well-watered and drought conditions.

Measurements of photosynthetically active radiation with an optical quantum sensor

While recording SIF with the AutoSIF system, PAR was simultaneously measured with an optical quantum sensor. The sensor was positioned vertically, facing upward, and placed in an open area to avoid shading from surrounding objects or vegetation. These concurrent measurements allowed for the analysis of SIF data in the context of ambient light availability.

Measurements of photosynthesis to match recorded SIF

To synchronize with SIF measurements, photosynthesis was assessed immediately after recording SIF and PAR for each leaf. The Li-6800 infrared gas-exchange system was adopted to determine leaf photosynthetic parameters. During measurements, leaf chamber light levels were matched to the recorded PAR by the optical quantum sensor, ensuring consistency with SIF conditions. This matching protocol was consistently applied to ensure the direct comparability of SIF and photosynthesis measurements made under the same, simultaneously recorded ambient light conditions. Chamber settings were controlled at 60% relative humidity, 400 μmol mol−1 CO2 concentration, and a flow rate of 500 ml min−1 to minimize environmental variability. Because the Li-6800 chamber conditions closely replicated the ambient environment during SIF measurements, photosynthetic parameters stabilized within 2-4 min. While SIF is captured instantaneously and photosynthesis requires time to reach a steady state, synchronization was effectively maintained by keeping light and environmental conditions consistent and by assuming stable chlorophyll content over such short intervals. Therefore, the photosynthetic data can be considered temporally aligned with the corresponding SIF measurements.

Measurements of concurrent gas exchange and chlorophyll fluorescence

Gas exchange and chlorophyll fluorescence induction and relaxation experiments were performed on G. biloba leaves using Li-6800 gas-exchange system with an attached 6800-01A fluorometer chamber. The relative humidity inside the leaf chamber was controlled at 60%, the CO2 concentration was maintained at 400 μmol mol−1, the leaf temperature was kept at 30 °C, and the flow rate was regulated at 500 ml min−1. A 9:1 red-to-blue light ratio for PAR was set to maximize stomatal aperture. Prior to the measurement of photosynthetic gas exchange and chlorophyll fluorescence, plants were acclimated to a PAR of 300 μmol m−2 s−1 for 40 min. Data collection began at the 36th minute, recording gas exchange parameters along with steady-state (Fs) and maximum chlorophyll fluorescence (Fm’) during a multiphase light-saturating pulse flash. After 40 min, PAR was subsequently increased to 1500 μmol m−2 s−1 using the LI-6800 system and maintained for 40 min before being reduced back to 300 μmol m−2 s−1. Data were recorded every 2 min throughout the procedure. Minimum (Fo) and maximum (Fm) chlorophyll fluorescence and dark respiration (Rd) were measured in dark-adapted G. biloba leaves after they were exposed to darkness for 1 h.

Statistical analyses

We utilized the generalized additive model (GAM) in R (package ‘mgcv’) to capture the non-linear effects of time on the variables (An, SIFPSII, gs, Jflu, NPQ, qL, Jo, and Jc), while accounting for the impact of water treatments. Diagnostic checks for all GAMs were performed, including the evaluation of effective degrees of freedom (EDF) and examination of residual-vs-fitted plots; the results have been compiled as Fig. S2 to enhance the methodological rigor of the analysis. We then applied ANOVA to assess differences in the response curves of these variables under different treatments. A t-test was used to compare differences in ψw, An and SIF, ΦPSIImax, and the ratio of An to Jflu between the well-watered and drought treatments. We conducted a sensitivity analysis to examine how variations in ΦPSIImax influence SIFPSII and Jflu. Using experimentally derived mean and standard deviation values of ΦPSIImax, we generated 500 synthetic data points (permutation ΦPSIImax) through normal distribution sampling. For each permutation ΦPSIImax value, SIFPSII was calculated using Eqn. (6) with qL and NPQ values extracted at 40 min after exposure to high light conditions. Correspondingly, Jflu was determined with Eqn. (5) using the same qL and NPQ values along with the experimentally measured SIFPSII at 40 min after high light exposure, combined with permutation ΦPSIImax. Due to differing measurement scales between SIFPSII and Jflu, we employed dual y-axis visualization with appropriate scaling, using LOESS smoothing to capture response patterns. The correlations among variables were explored using linear ordinary least-square regression. SIF, along with matched PAR and photosynthesis data, were obtained from eight individual plant replicates (Fig. 4), whereas all other figures (except Fig. 7 for the sensitivity analysis) were based on measurements from five replicates (Fig. 5, Fig. 6, Fig. 7, Fig. 8, Fig. 9). All data analyses and figure plotting were executed in R (R Core Team, 2024; ggplot2).

Results

Water status of G. biloba under different water treatments

Leaf water potential (ψw) was measured in well-watered and drought-stressed G. biloba plants (Fig. 3). Ψw reflects the leaf moisture status, and as anticipated, drought-stressed G. biloba exhibited significantly lower ψw values compared to well-watered plants.

Variation in leaf water potential (ψw, MPa) under well-watered and drought treatments in G. biloba. ∗∗∗ indicates significant difference between well-watered and drought treatments at the 0.001 probability level.

Effects of drought and fluctuating light on photosynthesis and SIF in G. biloba

We first examined the impacts of drought on leaf-level An and SIF under ambient CO2, PAR, and temperature conditions (Fig. 4A and B). Our results show that drought caused a significant reduction in An, while its effect on SIF was relatively small. Additionally, the normalized Refapp across wavelengths showed no obvious differences between well-watered and drought plants (Fig. 4C). We also confirmed that PAR did not influence the response of SIF to drought (Fig. 4D).

We observed that drought reduced An regardless of light level (Fig. 5A), while SIFPSII exhibited a slight decrease under drought (Fig. 5B). An in both well-watered and drought G. biloba plants gradually increased over time following the transition from low to high light intensity, and then decreased when the light was reduced back to low levels. However, drought-stressed plants reached steady-state An more quickly than well-watered plants (Fig. 5A). In contrast to the changes in An, SIFPSII in both well-watered and drought plants exhibited a slow decline at different rates over time before stabilizing, following a sharp decrease triggered by the sudden drop in light (Fig. 5B). From Fig. 5A–B, while drought does not consistently affect An and SIFPSII, fluctuations in light levels lead to a uniform change in both. By combining the final data points from each light fluctuation step, An and SIFPSII were coupled regardless of water availability (Fig. 5C and D). In contrast, when data from well-watered and drought conditions were merged under high (Fig. 5E) and low light (Fig. 5F) scenarios, An and SIFPSII were decoupled.

Regulatory factors in the relationship of photosynthesis with chlorophyll fluorescence in G. biloba under drought and fluctuating light

We analyzed the dynamics of gs and its relationship with An under drought and fluctuating light conditions (Fig. 6). The results show that variations in gs closely mirrored those in An in response to drought and two levels of light. By pooling all the data, we found a significant correlation between An and gs, indicating that gs may play a central role in controlling photosynthetic carbon assimilation.

Jflu acts as a physiological bridge between chlorophyll fluorescence and photosynthesis. We investigated the dynamics of Jflu in G. biloba leaves subjected to high (1500 μmol m−2 s−1) and low light (300 μmol m−2 s−1) under different water treatments. Our results demonstrated that drought notably reduced Jflu in both light conditions. During the light induction phase, Jflu increased gradually. Upon transition from high to low light, it dropped sharply, then remained relatively stable throughout the light relaxation phase (Fig. 6C). We found that during the transition to high light from low, NPQ in both well-watered and drought plants increased immediately, followed by a gradual rise. Upon returning to low light conditions, NPQ exhibited a rapid decline followed by stabilization (Fig. 6D). Drought had little impact on NPQ, except during the initial minutes after exposure to high light (Fig. 6D). The response of qL to light changes was opposite to that of NPQ; It decreased (or increased) sharply with abrupt light changes, followed by a slow recovery (Fig. 6E). ΦPSIImax, a stable parameter, showed little variation with light changes, but drought caused a reduction (Fig. 6F). The sensitivity analysis further revealed that while SIFPSII showed minimal responsiveness to variations in ΦPSIImax, Jflu exhibited marked sensitivity, with its values strongly modulated by changes in ΦPSIImax across the simulated physiological range (Fig. 7). Energy partitioning analysis (Fig. 8) showed that drought had no significant effect on the quantum yields of photochemistry (ΦPSII), heat dissipation (ΦNPQ), and fluorescence (ΦSIF), whereas light had a pronounced impact on ΦNPQ and ΦPSII, but not on ΦSIF.

Further investigation revealed that the decrease in Jflu did not correspond with the reduction in An. This decoupling under drought was evident in Fig. 9A and B, where the ratio of An to Jflu decreased. This observation was further supported by a smaller decline in Jo compared to Jc under drought (Fig. 9C and D), along with elevated Jo/Jflu and reduced Jc/Jflu (Fig. 9E and F).

Discussion

Early drought-induced changes in photosynthesis are decoupled from chlorophyll fluorescence, while light intensity-driven changes are coupled in G. biloba.

We initially utilized spectral retrieval and saturation pulse techniques to evaluate the effectiveness of chlorophyll fluorescence as an indicator of photosynthesis in drought-stressed G. biloba leaves under both high and low light conditions. We observed that, regardless of light intensity, chlorophyll fluorescence (Fig. 4, Fig. 5) measured with both techniques did not correspond to the changes in An under the early drought conditions. This lack of correspondence, indicative of SIF-photosynthesis decoupling, contrasts with several studies conducted beyond the leaf scale (e.g., Refs. [13,27,52,53]), which suggest that remotely sensed SIF reliably tracks photosynthesis, capturing temporal dynamics and spatial anomalies during drought. However, our findings align with leaf-scale studies such as Marrs et al. (2020) [25], who observed a breakdown in the linear coupling between photosynthesis and chlorophyll fluorescence under artificially induced stomatal closure. Helm et al. (2020) [26] also reported SIF-photosynthesis decoupling in Populus deltoides during a one-month drought. Similarly, Pescador-Dionisio et al. (2025) [30] demonstrated that fluorescence quantum efficiency showed no change during early drought stress and therefore cannot serve as a reliable indicator of photochemical efficiency. These discrepancies likely arise from methodological differences across scales, as well as differences in the severity and duration of drought stress. Large-scale studies often emphasize factors like APAR and chlorophyll content, while also integrating the complex effects of seasonal or multi-year drought periods. This broader integration may obscure the physiological regulation of the SIF-photosynthesis relationship [22,33,34,54,55]. In contrast, leaf-scale studies typically control for APAR and can precisely define drought duration, thereby more effectively isolating its specific physiological effects.

To dissect the physiological regulation underlying the SIF-photosynthesis relationship, we therefore focus on PSII-derived component (SIFPSII), which provides a direct, mechanistically interpretable quantum yield signal. Under controlled high and low light conditions, our study revealed that early drought stress induced a decoupling between SIFPSII and photosynthesis, characterized by a slight reduction in SIFPSII emission coupled with a strong suppression of photosynthesis. The limited decrease in SIFPSII resulted from a minimally affected ΦSIF (Fig. 8). Given that SIFPSII is the product of ΦSIF and APAR (with APAR controlled in our study), its dynamics primarily reflect changes in ΦSIF. According to the mechanistic MLR-SIF model, ΦSIF is co-determined by NPQ, qL, and ΦPSIImax [22,34]. Under drought, the limited variations in NPQ and qL, coupled with the low sensitivity of ΦSIF to the pronounced decline in ΦPSIImax, contributed to an overall stable ΦSIF. The stability subsequently led to a limited reduction in SIFPSII under controlled light conditions. Instead, our data indicate that drought induced a significant decrease in gs, consistent with the majority of existing studies demonstrating that stomatal closure is one of the earliest responses to drought (e.g., Refs. [56,57]). Therefore, the SIFPSII-photosynthesis decoupling observed in G. biloba primarily stems from the asynchronous drought responses of gs and SIFPSII, aligning with Helm et al. (2020) [26]. The rapid reduction in gs under early or mild drought limits CO2 availability, thereby driving the initial decrease in An.This causal relationship is supported by their parallel dynamics across contrasting light levels. In contrast, the delayed response of SIFPSII creates a temporal mismatch that likely accounts for the observed decoupling. Furthermore, we propose that the observed decoupling may be characteristic of the early drought phase, where rapid stomatal closure drives photosynthetic decline with minimal concurrent changes in NPQ or photochemical efficiency. Under more severe or prolonged stress, increased NPQ engagement and/or sustained decline in ΦPSIImax would likely alter ΦSIF, thereby changing the SIFPSII-photosynthesis relationship. This could either restore correlation (if SIFPSII decline more sharply) or lead to further decoupling (if metabolic limitations dominate). Future studies systematically varying drought intensity and duration are needed to map the full continuum of SIF-photosynthesis coupling.

Our study further revealed that the decrease in light intensity directly reduced the SIFPSII signal, as ΦSIF remained largely stable (SIFPSII = ΦSIF × APAR). Although the relaxation of ΦNPQ up-regulated ΦPSII upon the shift from high to low light, a response consistent with Porcar-Castell et al. (2021) [54], this did not translate to an increase in An, which instead declined. This divergence demonstrates that the dominant limitation on photosynthesis shifted from light to carbon reactions, most likely mediated by a light-triggered decrease in gs that constrained carbon assimilation. Therefore, although SIF is widely observed to track photosynthesis with light at larger scales, our leaf-level mechanistic analysis of SIFPSII reveals that this apparent coupling can arise from parallel responses to APAR rather than a direct mechanistic link. This distinction points to a fundamental challenge in interpreting SIF for remote sensing. The relationship between SIF and photosynthesis is not universal but depends critically on how environmental conditions shift the balance of energy allocation. A primary task is to differentiate between stress scenarios where SIF remains informative (e.g., light-driven variations) and those where it may fail (e.g., early stomatal limitation). This challenge is particularly relevant because widely used modeling approaches at large scales, such as the light-use efficiency (LUE) framework [17,58], often directly link SIF to photosynthesis via shared drivers (i.e., APAR and chlorophyll content) while neglecting this nuanced physiological regulation. For instance, the LUE framework typically assumes SIF is a function of APAR and a constant ΦSIF:LUE ratio. However, LUE encompasses complex, poorly understood processes [29,34], and ΦSIF, though small, reflects both photochemical and non-photochemical pathways. Thus, while empirical SIF-LUE approach provides a useful foundation, a mechanistic understanding of SIF-photosynthesis relationships demands deeper exploration of energy allocation at the leaf scale across diverse environments. Addressing this may require the integration of SIF with other signals that more directly sense stomatal behavior or metabolic status to reliably detect early stress.

Reduced ΦPSIImax and enhanced photorespiratory electron flow coordinate light and carbon reactions under drought in G. biloba

Research indicates that Jflu plays a pivotal role in linking SIFPSII to photosynthesis [22,34]. Under drought conditions, plants typically downregulate Jflu to lower the production of reducing energy, which aligns with the decreased energy consumption resulting from reduced An [22,34,59]. Guided by the mechanistic MLR-SIF model [34], we identify SIFPSII, ΦPSIImax, qL and/or NPQ as the primary variables influencing Jflu within the light reactions of photosynthesis (Eqn (5)). In our study, SIFPSII remained stable or decreased only slightly, and NPQ did not increase, suggesting that neither was a key driver of the decline in Jflu in drought-stressed G. biloba. Although drought leads to an increase in qL, the magnitude of this increase was insufficient to explain the decrease in Jflu, particularly under high light. In contrast, ΦPSIImax decreased markedly. Our sensitivity analysis confirms that Jflu is highly responsive to variations in ΦPSIImax, which likely occurs because in drought-stressed G. biloba, Jflu is downregulated not through increased energy dissipation (e.g., via NPQ) but through a reduction in energy input at the source. This process is mediated by the strategic downregulation of ΦPSIImax to mitigate stress effects [60], resulting in decreased photon capture efficiency per reaction center. An alternative explanation is that the reductions in ΦPSIImax may also arise from reversible regulatory processes or reflect varying degrees of photoinhibition [[61], [62], [63], [64]]. While our experimental design does not explicitly determine which of these mechanisms predominates, the coordinated response with enhanced photorespiration suggests an integrated acclimation strategy in G. biloba, rather than solely a passive impairment. Future studies under recovery conditions would help clarify the persistence and reversibility of this response.

Although both Jflu-mediated energy production and An-mediated energy consumption are downregulated, the degree of this downregulation varies significantly between well-watered and drought conditions (Fig. 9). It is widely recognized that Jflu predominantly reflects the electron flow associate with RuBisco-driven carboxylation and oxygenation, under the assumption that alternative sinks for light-generated electrons are either negligible or remain constant [65]. Further research shows that the decrease in Jc, which is specially associated with the carboxylation reaction, is greater than that in Jflu. This is due to more electron flow being directed toward the oxygenation cycle under drought stress in G. biloba, as indicated by the increased Jo/Jflu under drought conditions (Fig. 9). Studies have shown that photorespiration can act as an energy sink, avoiding an overly reduced state of the electron transport chain and thereby protecting against photoinhibition [26,28,54,58,66]. Typically, stressed plants enhance NPQ to dissipate excess energy during light reactions, thereby minimizing the risk of damage to the photosynthetic apparatus [i.e., 35-37]. However, in our study, the drought-stressed G. biloba did not show a significant increase in NPQ under steady-state conditions, in either high or low light. These results suggest that under drought, G. biloba may rely more on photorespiration as an alternative electron sink, rather than on NPQ, to balance the light and carbon reactions.

It is important to note that our conclusions are derived from a mechanistic study on G. biloba, a phylogenetically unique deciduous gymnosperm. The specific patterns we observed, such as the stability of ΦSIF and the reliance on photorespiration over NPQ for photoprotection under early drought, may not be directly generalizable to all plant functional types. For instance, tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum), a model angiosperm, has been shown to exhibit stronger NPQ engagement or different stomatal regulation strategies under similar stress [67]. Future comparative studies across diverse species are needed to elucidate how these mechanisms vary and to establish broader principles governing SIF-photosynthesis relationships under environmental stress.

Implications for remote sensing of early drought stress

The SIF (or SIFPSII) signals analyzed in our study represent intrinsic physiological yields and are thus distinct from top-of-canopy or satellite SIF observations, which integrate canopy structure, radiative transfer, and atmospheric effects. Therefore, the implications discussed below should be viewed as providing a mechanistic physiological basis for re-evaluating the potential and limitations of SIF in plant-stress remote sensing, rather than as direct operational conclusions. Our central result, that SIF decouples from photosynthesis during early drought due to rapid stomatal closure while ΦSIF remains stable, suggests a potential blind spot. SIF might be insensitive to the primary stomatal limitation when that limitation is driven by factors other than light, such as soil water deficit or atmospheric dryness. From a remote sensing perspective, this creates a conceptual challenge: how to differentiate declines in photosynthesis that are primarily driven by reduced light availability from those primarily driven by hydrologic constraints, under comparable illumination conditions. To address this potential limitation, we propose several conceptual pathways centered on integrating SIF with complementary signals, which warrant future investigation at larger scales.

Disentangling physiology from structure using combined indices: Our analysis shows that the SIF signal is dominated by APAR, which confounds physiological interpretation. A potential strategy is to isolate the physiological component by combining SIF with indices like the near infrared reflectance of vegetation (NIRv), which shares a similar structural dependence but a different physiological basis, to derive a purer proxy for the ΦSIF (e.g., SIF divided by NIRv) [68,69]. Alternatively, the red:far-red fluorescence ratio (i.e., F687/F760) might offer a sensitive measure of plant stress and canopy structure by leveraging the inherent difference in chlorophyll reabsorption between these two wavelengths, typically offering greater sensitivity than Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) [33]. These approaches conceptually address the structural confounding issue, moving towards a more physiologically interpretable signal, but their efficacy and robustness at the canopy and satellite scale require dedicated validation.

Integrating Direct Proxies for Stomatal Limitation: Since our data pinpoint asynchronous stomatal response as the core mechanism for decoupling, a logical hypothesis for future remote sensing studies is to integrate SIF with independent proxies of stomatal behavior. Crucially, these measures must help distinguish between stomatal closure due to low light versus that due to drought stress. Canopy temperature from thermal sensors is a promising candidate for this purpose, as elevated canopy temperature under similar radiation conditions can indicate reduced transpiration due to drought. Theoretically, fusing SIF, which reports on light reactions and is influenced by both light and stress, with these signals, which report on water status and stomatal aperture, creates a diagnostic framework. This framework could be tested for its ability to detect the early stress scenario we observed: elevated canopy temperature coinciding with a lagging SIF signal under non-light-limited conditions.

Toward process-based models across scales: Our findings highlight a limitation of widely-used empirical models like the LUE framework, which may lack the mechanistic capacity to represent the SIF-photosynthesis decoupling observed in our study. Process-based models, for example, the MLR-SIF framework, which explicitly or implicitly incorporates key physiological parameters (i.e., ΦNPQ, ΦPSIImax, photorespiration, and gs), provide a structural basis capable of simulating such decoupling dynamics [34]. Nevertheless, this model has been primarily developed and validated at the leaf scale [22,34]. A critical next step toward their operational use in remote sensing is to establish robust methods for estimating these core physiological parameters directly from satellite observations. Successfully bridging this scale gap would be a significant step toward transforming mechanistic understanding into practical tools for large-scale stress monitoring.

Conclusion

In summary, this study reveals a fundamental drought-induced decoupling between SIF and photosynthesis in G. biloba, driven by asynchronous stomatal and fluorescence responses. While rapid stomatal closure strongly reduced carbon assimilation, SIF remained relatively stable owning to a drought-insensitive ΦSIF. Conversely, under varying light intensity, SIF and photosynthesis remained tightly coupled, primarily through proportional changes in APAR. Notably, in our study on G. biloba, photoprotection under drought was achieved not by enhancing NPQ, but through coordinated downregulation of ΦPSIImax and increased photorespiration. These results demonstrate that relying solely on SIF as a proxy for photosynthetic activity is considerably less reliable under drought stress. Therefore, future field-phenotyping efforts should focus on developing integrated multi-parameter models (e.g., by incorporating ΦPSIImax, photorespiration, and reflectance-based indices such as NIRv) to enhance the accuracy of non-destructive diagnostics for plant stress resilience.
