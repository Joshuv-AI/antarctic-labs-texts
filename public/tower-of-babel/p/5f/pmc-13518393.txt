Subtractive Proteomics and Immunoinformatics-Guided Design of a Multi-Epitope Vaccine Against Aspergillus fumigatus
Authors: Caixia Tan, Anhua Wu, Chunhui Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Invasive aspergillosis (IA), a life-threatening fungal infection predominantly caused by Aspergillus fumigatus, has emerged as a major global health threat, particularly in immunocompromised individuals. The rising resistance of A. fumigatus to existing antifungal therapies, coupled with the lack of an approved vaccine, highlights the urgent need for novel preventive strategies. This study aimed to design multi-epitope vaccines against IA using immunoinformatics approaches. Two highly conserved 1,3-beta-glucanosyltransferase proteins, essential for fungal cell wall remodeling, were identified as potential antigen targets via subtractive proteomics. From these, 13 optimal T-cell and 10 ideal B-cell epitopes were selected to develop two vaccine constructs, designated WLaf1 and WLaf2. Both vaccine constructs exhibited favorable physicochemical stability, antigenicity, and non-allergenicity. Molecular docking and molecular dynamics (MD) analyses suggested stable interactions between the vaccine constructs and innate immune receptors, including the TLR1–TLR2 heterodimer and TLR4, with WLaf1–TLR complexes showing relatively higher stability. Population coverage analysis indicated broad global applicability. Immune simulation analyses predicted robust humoral and cellular immune responses, with WLaf1 demonstrating predicted stronger antibody production, memory B-cell formation, and T-cell activation. Collectively, these computational findings suggest that both WLaf1 and WLaf2 represent potentially promising vaccine candidates against A. fumigatus, with WLaf1 exhibiting comparatively superior predicted performance. Further experimental and in vivo studies are required to validate the immunogenicity, safety, and protective efficacy of these vaccine candidates.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12602-026-11005-x.

Introduction

With the increasing number of immunosuppressed individuals, fungal infections are increasingly recognized as a serious global public health concern [1]. Among these, invasive aspergillosis (IA) is one of the most prevalent and lethal invasive fungal infections [2, 3]. IA primarily affects the lungs, presenting with fever refractory to conventional antibiotics, chest pain, cough, dyspnea, and hemoptysis. Angioinvasion by fungal hyphae can result in pulmonary infarction and life-threatening hemoptysis. In disseminated cases, systemic manifestations may occur, involving multiple organs such as the central nervous system (e.g., seizures, focal neurological deficits, or altered mental status), liver (hepatomegaly, jaundice), kidneys (flank pain, hematuria), heart, skin, and eyes [4].

Aspergillus fumigatus (A. fumigatus) has long been recognized as the most common causative agent of IA, responsible for approximately 90% of cases [5]. Despite a modest decline in its relative prevalence, it continues to be the leading pathogen among immunocompromised populations [6, 7]. This opportunistic fungus is ubiquitous in soil and decaying organic matter, and its microscopic spores can be readily aerosolized and inhaled by humans, serving as the primary route of infection [8]. Disease development is largely determined by the interaction between host susceptibility and environmental exposure. Conditions associated with impaired immune function—such as prolonged neutropenia, corticosteroid therapy, chemotherapy, or organ transplantation—compromise innate immune defenses and facilitate conidial germination, hyphal proliferation, and tissue invasion [9]. Environmental factors also contribute to infection risk; elevated airborne spore concentrations in construction areas, agricultural environments, or poorly ventilated healthcare settings significantly increase exposure [10]. Additionally, climatic conditions that favor fungal growth, including warmth and high humidity, may further enhance environmental exposure to Aspergillus spores [11].

In recognition of the growing global burden of fungal infections, the World Health Organization (WHO) released its first Fungal Priority Pathogen List (FPPL) in 2022, designating A. fumigatus, together with Cryptococcus neoformans, Candida auris, and Candida albicans, as critical priority fungal pathogens [12]. Globally, over 300,000 cases of IA occur annually, predominantly among immunocompromised individuals [13]. Despite timely diagnosis and treatment, IA carries a high mortality rate of approximately 50%, which may approach 100% in cases of delayed or inadequate therapy [14]. Although IA primarily affects high-risk populations, infection may occasionally occur in immunocompetent individuals following prolonged exposure to high concentrations of airborne spores [15–17].

Currently, triazoles are the only available oral antifungal agents and represent first-line therapy for A. fumigatus infections [18]. However, widespread triazole use has driven triazole-resistant strains, with resistance rates reaching up to 30% in some medical institutions [19, 20]. Additional challenges include drug toxicity, limited long-term applicability in high-risk patients (e.g., those with renal insufficiency), suboptimal diagnostic sensitivity, high treatment costs, and prolonged therapy durations [21, 22]. Moreover, the psychological burden associated with long-term antifungal therapy should not be overlooked. Therefore, the development of preventive vaccines targeting A. fumigatus has become an urgent public health priority.

To date, no licensed vaccines exist for A. fumigatus, although several promising candidates have been evaluated in preclinical studies, including live-attenuated mutants, heat-killed spores, recombinant antigens (e.g., Asp f3, Asp f9), and protein-liposome complexes [23–25]. Most of these candidates focus on single antigens and rarely incorporate comprehensive epitope profiling or systematic in silico evaluation of immune responses, limiting their potential to elicit broad and robust immunity. In the present study, we designed two multi-epitope vaccines targeting A. fumigatus using integrated proteomic and immunoinformatic approaches. Two 1,3-β-glucanosyltransferases were selected as primary antigenic targets based on their biological significance and translational potential [26]. These glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchored proteins are predominantly localized to the fungal cell wall, where they catalyze β-glucan cross-linking during hyphal growth [27, 28]. During the transition from resting conidia to germinating conidia and invasive hyphae, cell wall remodeling becomes highly dynamic, accompanied by upregulation of β-glucanosyltransferases to facilitate structural reorganization [29]. Although resting conidia are shielded by a rodlet layer and melanin that partially mask immunogenic components, these outer structures are progressively degraded during germination, exposing inner cell wall polysaccharides and associated enzymes to host immune recognition [30–32]. In the invasive hyphal stage—responsible for tissue penetration and angioinvasion—these enzymes remain surface-accessible and are indispensable for maintaining cell wall integrity [33, 34]. Such sustained surface accessibility may facilitate continuous immune surveillance in vivo, supporting their suitability as vaccine targets [34, 35]. Furthermore, their structural conservation across A. fumigatus strains, including triazole-resistant isolates, further enhances their potential as broadly protective vaccine candidates [35].

Based on an analysis of these conserved antigens, we identified thirteen optimal T-cell epitopes and ten ideal B-cell epitopes. Using these epitopes, we designed two vaccine constructs (WLaf1 and WLaf2) and thoroughly evaluated their physicochemical properties. Molecular docking was carried out to evaluate the binding affinity between the vaccine constructs and Toll-like receptors (TLRs). This was followed by molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to evaluate the stability of the vaccine-TLR complexes. Finally, in silico immune simulations were conducted to assess the immunogenicity and potential efficacy of the vaccine candidates. This integrative, epitope-based strategy offers several advantages over traditional single-antigen vaccine designs. Combining B-cell and T-cell epitopes enables the simultaneous activation of humoral and cellular immunity, thereby eliciting a broader and more robust immune response. Moreover, the application of computational methods allows for the optimization of vaccine construct structure, stability, and immunogenic potential. Collectively, these findings may provide a foundation for the future development of safe, effective, and broadly protective multi-epitope vaccines against A. fumigatus.

Methods

Identification of Target Proteins

Acquisition of A. fumigatus Protein Profiles and Subtractive Proteomic Analysis

The reference proteome of A. fumigatus (ID: GCF_000002655.1), comprising 9,630 proteins, was obtained from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database and subjected to subtractive proteomics analysis to recognize potential vaccine targets. Initially, homologous proteins shared with humans (Homo sapiens, ID: 9606) were excluded using Diamond v2.1.8 (E-value < 10− 5, bit score > 100) to reduce autoimmune risk, a strategy commonly adopted in reverse vaccinology studies [36, 37]. The remaining non-homologous proteins were screened for essentiality via the Database of Essential Genes (DEG) 20 under similar parameters, retaining proteins with > 30% identity [38]. Virulence and effector pathogen proteins were further identified by aligning essential proteins against the Pathogen-Host Interactions database (PHI-base) and the Database of Known Fungal Virulence Factors (DFVF) using Diamond v2.1.8. PHI-base provides curated data on genes influencing pathogen-host interactions [39], while DFVF includes pathogenic genes from 228 fungal strains across 85 genera, offering a comprehensive resource for fungal pathogen research [40]. Proteins with E-value < 10− 5, bit score > 100, and > 30% identity were considered putative virulence factors and prioritized as antifungal or vaccine targets.

Prioritization of Outer Membrane Antigen Proteins

Membrane proteins play crucial roles in pathogen-host interactions and are valuable targets for vaccine development due to their accessibility on the pathogen surface [41]. To prioritize potential vaccine candidates, we first used CellO v2.5 and Cell-PLoc v.2.0 tools to predict the subcellular localization (SLC) of the proteins [42, 43]. Subsequently, we refined our selection by employing the DeepTMHMM v1.0 and SignalP 6.0 servers to predict transmembrane helices and signal peptides [44, 45]. Proteins containing more than one transmembrane helix were filtered out, as transmembrane regions often correspond to membrane-bound proteins that are less likely to be exposed on the pathogen surface, making them less suitable as vaccine targets. Additionally, we assessed the antigenicity of the candidate proteins using the Vaxijen 2.0 server [46], retaining only those with an antigenicity score higher than 0.5. Finally, the physicochemical properties of the selected target proteins were evaluated.

Homology Analysis of the Antigen with Common Gut Probiotics

Based on existing literature identifying 79 common intestinal probiotic strains, the Diamond v2.1.8 (E-value < 10 − 5, bit score > 100) was used to conduct a BLASTP search to assess homology between the target protein and these gut microbiota [36]. Proteins showing homology with the gut microbiota were excluded to mitigate the potential risk of vaccine-induced intestinal dysbiosis.

Identifying Potential Immunogenic Epitopes from the Target Proteins

Cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) epitopes were predicted using NetMHCpan 4.1 based on twelve representative HLA class I supertypes covering global population diversity (Table S1) [47]. Additionally, helper T lymphocyte (HTL) epitopes were predicted using the NetMHCIIpan 4.3 web server, which covers fifteen HLA class II alleles (Table S2) [48]. Both servers apply artificial neural networks (ANNs) to estimate peptide-HLA binding affinity, with epitopes exhibiting IC50 values < 500 nM retained for further analysis [47, 48]. Linear B cell epitopes were predicted using both ABCpred and LBtope, with only overlapping candidates selected [49]. Subsequently, the Vaxijen 2.0 server was employed to assess the antigenicity of each epitope [46]. Toxicity and allergenicity were evaluated using the ToxinPred and AllerTOP v.2 tools [50, 51]. Epitopes defined by high antigenicity, non-toxic characteristics and non-allergenic profile were retained for subsequent analysis. To further evaluate the potential efficacy of the identified CTL epitopes in eliciting an immune response, the MHC I immunogenicity module of the Immune Epitope Database (IEDB) was employed to assess the immunogenicity of the selected epitopes [52]. Additionally, IFNepitope and IL4Pred servers were used to assess the potential of MHC-II epitopes to trigger IFN-γ and IL-4 production [53, 54]. Finally, to avoid potential autoimmunity, predicted epitopes were aligned against the human proteome using BLASTp (Diamond) with a stringent E-value cutoff of 10− 5. Similar immunoinformatics-based epitope screening and multi-epitope vaccine design strategies have been widely applied in previous studies [55–57]. A detailed overview of the epitope screening process was shown in Fig. S1.

Validation and Population Coverage Analysis of Selected T Cell Epitopes

To obtain a more comprehensive assessment of the HLA alleles potentially binding to the T cell epitopes incorporated into the multi-epitope vaccine, the TepiTool online tool was employed, as it provides more extensive HLA allele molecular information [58]. This additional analysis enabled a more precise determination of the binding affinity between T cell epitopes and HLA alleles across various populations, which is crucial for evaluating the broader applicability and effectiveness of the multi-epitope vaccine in diverse demographic groups. The corresponding HLA alleles with a percentile rank of ≤ 1.0 were selected and further input into IEDB to predict the population coverage for every chosen CTL and HTL epitope.

Epitope Conservation Analysis

We retrieved 291 A. fumigatus proteomes from the NCBI Genome database and extracted the sequences of the pre-screened target epitopes. The conservation of these epitopes across various A. fumigatus strains was predicted using the Epitope Conservancy Analysis tool from the IEDB [59]. Epitopes exhibiting a conservation level greater than 95% were selected for the construction of the multi-epitope vaccine constructs.

Molecular Docking of Selected T Cell Epitopes

To evaluate the structural interactions between predicted T cell epitopes and HLA molecules, molecular docking analyses were performed. For each epitope, one representative HLA allele was selected based on the availability of high-resolution structural data and its contribution to a combinatorial panel with broad global population coverage. The crystal structures of these alleles were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) (Table S3). Water molecules and heteroatoms in the structures were removed using Discovery Studio Visualizer v2021. Molecular docking was performed using the CABS-DOCK server. Interactions between epitopes and HLA molecules were analyzed with PDBsum [60], and the docking results were visualized using ChimeraX software [61]. Binding affinities between all epitopes and HLA alleles were calculated using the PRODIGY web server [62].

Construction of Vaccine Constructs

The vaccine constructs were assembled by linking CTL, HTL, and B-cell epitopes with high antigenicity, strong HLA binding affinity, and non-allergenic, non-toxic profiles using appropriate linkers. Linkers play a critical role in recombinant fusion proteins by preserving the individual immunological functions of each epitope [63]. According to other studies, the AAY, GPGPG and KK linkers were utilized to link CTL, HTL, and B-cell epitopes, respectively [64, 65]. Studies have shown that TLR2 can recognize A. fumigatus cell wall components like mannan and lipopeptides, while TLR4 can detect surface sugars and lipids, both contributing to immune activation [66, 67]. Therefore, to enhance multi-epitope vaccine efficacy, TLR2 and TLR4 agonists were incorporated at the N- and C-termini using EAAAK linkers as molecular adjuvants. Specifically, RS09 was used as the TLR4 agonist, while human β-defensin-3 (HBD-3) and Pam2Cys were used separately as TLR2 agonists in the two vaccine constructs. In our design, the incorporated TLR agonists correspond to minimal agonist motifs rather than full-length pathogen-derived proteins, which is rationally expected to preserve adjuvant activity while potentially limiting excessive TLR signaling and off-target effects. Additionally, a PADRE sequence was added to boost helper T-cell responses and antibody production [68], and a 6×His tag was appended at the C-terminus to facilitate purification. The full amino acid sequences of the TLR agonists and PADRE sequence are provided in Table S4.

Assessment of Physicochemical and Biological Characteristics

To confirm the safety and favorable physicochemical properties of vaccine constructs, we have also assessed the antigenicity, toxicity and allergenicity of the final vaccine constructs. The ProtParam tool was employed to assess the physic-chemical properties of the vaccine constructs [69]. Subsequently, a series of prediction tools was used to optimize multi-epitope vaccine design and ensure effective epitope recognition by the immune system. SignalP-6.0 predicted signal peptides to avoid mislocalization into the endoplasmic reticulum [70], and DeepTMHMM was applied to exclude transmembrane helices that might hinder epitope accessibility [44]. Additionally, SOLpro was used to assess the solubility of the vaccine constructs when overexpressed in Escherichia coli (E.coli) [71].

Structural Modeling and Stereochemical Validation of Vaccine Constructs

For three-dimensional (3D) structure modeling, the AlphaFold v3.0 server was chosen due to its outstanding performance in the CASP14 competition, where it significantly outperformed other protein structure prediction methods [72]. AlphaFold utilizes advanced deep learning algorithms trained on extensive protein databases, integrating sequence and evolutionary information to accurately predict the spatial arrangement of atoms within the protein. This precision is crucial for understanding the structural properties of the vaccine constructs and their interactions with the immune system, which ultimately aids in designing more effective multi-epitope vaccines. The top-ranked model was refined using the GalaxyRefine program to improve loop and terminus regions [73]. Structure quality of the optional refined models was further validated using PROCHECK, ERRAT and ProSA-web servers, with key indicators including Ramachandran plot distribution, ERRAT scores (> 85 indicating good quality), and ProSA Z-scores for structural reliability [74–76]. To assess the secondary structure composition of the finalized 3D vaccine constructs, ProMotif analysis was conducted using the PDBsum server [60]. This tool identifies structural features such as α-helices, β-strands, β-turns, and γ-turns based on atomic coordinates, offering a detailed evaluation of the stereochemical organization derived from the refined tertiary structure. his ensured consistency between the predicted 3D structures and secondary structure elements, facilitating accurate assessment of immunogenicity and receptor interactions.

Multi-Epitope Vaccine-TLRs Molecular Docking

As demonstrated in earlier studies, the TLR1-TLR2 heterodimer and TLR4 play key roles in fungal recognition, making them suitable for evaluating immune interactions with the vaccine constructs. TLR1-TLR2 heterodimer detects fungal components like zymosan, while TLR4 responds to molecules such as glucuronoxylomannan, underscoring their importance in antifungal immunity [66, 67]. Hence, we retrieved the crystal structures of the TLR1-TLR2 heterodimer (PDB ID: 2Z7X) and TLR4 (PDB ID: 3FXI) from the PDB. Blind docking was performed between these receptors and the vaccine constructs using the ZDOCK server, which generated 500 initial docking poses for each complex [77]. For the TLR1-TLR2 heterodimer, the docking was conducted using the full heterodimer structure without defining any active site, enabling an unbiased prediction of potential interaction regions between the vaccine constructs and the receptor complex. The ZDOCK server automatically ranks all generated docking poses using its default scoring function, and the top-ranked pose (i.e., the highest-scoring model) was selected for further refinement with the HADDOCK 2.4 server following its default protocol [78]. This refinement contributes to the docking model being closer to the real protein interaction state and enhances prediction accuracy. Subsequently, binding interfaces of the refined complexes were analyzed via PDBsum to quantify key interaction parameters (e.g., hydrogen bonds, salt bridges) [60], and complexes were further visualized using PyMOL v3.1 server [79].

MD Simulation Analysis of Multi-Epitope Vaccine-TLRs Docking Complex

To assess the structural stability, binding affinity, and interaction dynamics of multi-epitope vaccine–TLR complexes, 100-ns MD simulations were conducted using GROMACS v2023.3 [80]. The Amber14SB_parmbsc1 force field was employed to parameterize all protein molecules. Each complex was solvated in a cubic TIP3P water box with a minimum 10 Å buffer from the outermost atom to eliminate boundary effects, and 0.15 mol/L NaCl was added to mimic the ionic strength of physiological environments, with counterions included to neutralize the system charge [81]. First, energy minimization was carried out using 5000 steps of steepest descent followed by 5000 steps of conjugate gradient minimization, with a convergence criterion of a maximum force < 1000 kJ·mol⁻¹·nm⁻¹. Second, a 400-ps NVT equilibration at 310 K was performed using the V-rescale thermostat (τ_T = 0.1 ps) to stabilize temperature and achieve uniform energy distribution. Third, a 400-ps NPT equilibration at 1 atm was conducted using the Parrinello–Rahman barostat (τ_P = 2.0 ps) to stabilize system pressure and adjust the simulation box volume, thereby achieving thermodynamic equilibrium. Following equilibration, a 100-ns production MD simulation was carried out with a 2-fs time step. Bond lengths involving hydrogen atoms were constrained using the LINCS algorithm to improve computational efficiency. Trajectory coordinates were recorded every 10 ps for subsequent analysis. Structural and dynamic parameters were evaluated using GROMACS built-in tools(version X.X). Specifically, the root mean square deviation (RMSD) of backbone atoms was used to assess overall complex stability [82]; the radius of gyration (Rg) was calculated to evaluate structural compactness; hydrogen bonds—defined by a donor–acceptor distance ≤ 3.5 Å and a donor–hydrogen–acceptor angle ≥ 120°—were quantified to characterize intermolecular interaction persistence [83]; and the root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) of the vaccine chain backbone was analyzed to assess residue-level flexibility.

The gmx_MMPBSA v1.62 tool was utilized to calculate the binding free energy of vaccine-TLR complexes employing the Molecular Mechanics Poisson-Boltzmann and Surface Area (MM-PBSA) methodology [84]. Calculation of Population Coverage

To assess the global applicability of vaccine candidates, the IEDB database’s population coverage tool was used [85]. This tool generates population coverage estimates for different regions by combining data on major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules that bind to the multi-epitope vaccine epitopes with HLA genotype data from various populations [85].

Immune Simulation of the Designed Multi-Epitope Vaccines

The immunogenic potential of the designed multi-epitope vaccines was evaluated using the C-ImmSim server, which integrates epitope binding to MHC molecules, immune cell activation, and immune cell interactions to assess the antigen’s immunogenic potential [86]. Following earlier studies, the simulation step size was set to 1050 (per timestep equivalent to 8 h), with injection times at steps 1, 84, and 168 for assessing immune system changes over time [37, 64]. The rest of the simulation parameters adhered to their initial defaults. To facilitate comparative analysis, an additional immune simulation was performed under identical conditions using a previously validated multi-epitope vaccine targeting Acinetobacter baumannii (A. baumannii) as a control antigen [87].

Disulfide Engineering, Codon Optimization and Cloning

To evaluate the potential for enhancing structural stability through disulfide bond formation, the tertiary structures of the vaccine constructs were analyzed using Disulfide by Design (DbD) v2.13 server [88]. This tool identifies suitable residue pairs based on Cβ–Cβ distance (4.4–6.8 Å), χ3 dihedral angles (± 30°), and low energy scores. Residue pairs meeting stricter criteria (χ3 ≈ − 87° or + 97° ±30; energy < 2.2 kcal/mol) were selected for disulfide bond introduction.

Codon optimization was conducted utilizing the JCat server, referencing the codon usage patterns of E. coli K12 [89]. The Codon Adaptation Index (CAI), ranging from 0 to 1, was used to evaluate compatibility, with higher values indicating better expression potential. GC content was adjusted to the optimal range of 30–70%. After codon optimization, restriction enzyme sites were introduced into the sequences for cloning. After codon optimization, BamHI and XhoI restriction sites were introduced at the 5’ and 3’ ends of the optimized gene sequences, respectively, to facilitate cloning. The final constructs were cloned into the pET-28a(+) vector using the Snapgene software. The schematic flow of the multi-epitope vaccine design process was shown in Fig. 1.

Results

Identification of Target Proteins

A subtractive proteomics approach was employed to identify potential vaccine targets for A. fumigatus. From the 9,630 proteins in its reference proteome, 7,177 were identified as non-homologous to the human proteome. These were screened against DEG, PHI-base, and DFVF databases using Diamond v2.1.8, yielding 143 essential proteins, 387 virulence factors, and 70 host-interaction-related proteins. Overlapping candidates were further filtered using VaxiJen 2.0, CellO v2.5, Cell-PLoc v2.0 and DIAMOND software to identify antigenic membrane proteins. Ultimately, two 1,3-beta-glucanosyltransferases were selected for multi-epitope vaccine design (Table 1).

Screening for Immune-Responsive Epitopes

The prediction of CTL epitopes, HTL epitopes, and B cell epitopes is essential for designing multi-epitope vaccines. CTL epitopes activate specific T cells, which directly kill infected cells, providing immediate immune defense. HTL epitopes regulate immune responses by activating helper T cells, promoting B-cell proliferation, antibody production and enhancing CTL activity. B-cell epitopes induce antibody production, forming the basis of the humoral immune response. To ensure comprehensive and lasting immune protection, our study incorporates both T-cell and B-cell epitopes. 100 CTL epitopes were predicted using NetMHCpan 4.1. A total of 36 candidates met the selection criteria of IC50 < 500 nM, antigenicity score > 0.5, and were non-toxic, non-allergenic. Following immunogenicity evaluation, homology and conservation analysis, 8 epitopes were finalized for inclusion in the vaccine constructs (Table 2). Similarly, 125 HTL epitopes were initially predicted. Of these, 42 epitopes met the criteria for antigenicity, non-toxic and non-allergenic. Among them, 5 epitopes capable of inducing both IFN-γ and IL-4 responses, lacking homology with the human proteome and high conservation, were selected to ensure a balanced and effective immune response while minimizing the risk of autoimmunity (Table 3). For B-cell epitopes, 10 candidates were selected based on predictions from both the ABCpred and LBtope servers. These epitopes were antigenic, non-toxic, non-allergenic, and non-homologous to human proteins and highly conserved (Table 4).

T-Cell Epitope Interaction with MHC Molecules

The docking results of epitopes and HLA molecules were presented in Fig. 2 and Table S3. All T cell epitopes were successfully accommodated in the antigen-binding groove of the HLA molecules. PDBsum analysis revealed that hydrogen bonds were formed between all T cell epitopes and their corresponding HLA molecules, with some also forming salt bridge interactions, further stabilizing the binding (Fig. 2, Table S3). Binding affinity calculations demonstrated differences between the epitopes and HLA alleles, with all showing strong binding affinities ranging from − 9.3 to -13.7 kcal/mol. Notably, the binding affinity of the epitope DVPIFRELNTNTIRV with HLA-DRB1*01:01 was − 13.7 kcal/mol, indicating the strongest binding. Similarly, the affinity of STSGDYGAY with HLA-A01:01 was − 12.5 kcal/mol, also reflecting a strong interaction. These findings suggested that all selected T cell epitopes exhibited robust affinity and stable interactions with HLA molecules.

Vaccine Design and Properties Prediction

All final selected epitopes were linked by linkers, including EAAAK, AAY, GPGPG, and KK, to design the vaccine constructs (Fig. S2). Two distinct vaccine constructs, WLaf1 and WLaf2, were developed with different adjuvant combinations. Physicochemical properties were summarized in Table S5. WLaf2 exhibited a slightly higher antigenicity score (1.4048) than WLaf1 (1.3445), while both constructs were predicted to be non-toxic and non-allergenic. WLaf1 consisted of 454 residues, with a molecular weight (MW) of 49459.07 Da, while WLaf2 contained 428 amino acids and had a MW of 46464.34 Da. The theoretical isoelectric points (pI) of WLaf1 and WLaf2 were 9.57 and 9.41, respectively. While these values are relatively high, such pI values are not uncommon in recombinant vaccine constructs. Moreover, proteins are typically formulated in buffered solutions for multi-epitope vaccine delivery, which minimizes the risk of pH-induced unfolding or aggregation. Both vaccine candidates were predicted to have similar half-lives in mammalian reticulocytes, yeast, and mice, indicating favorable stability. The instability index of WLaf1 (20.91) was higher than that of WLaf2 (15.13), suggesting that WLaf2 may exhibit greater in vivo stability. Additionally, the GRAVY score of WLaf1 (-0.543) was more negative than that of WLaf2 (-0.475), implying better hydrophilicity for WLaf1. However, WLaf2 showed a slightly higher solubility probability (0.901) compared to WLaf1 (0.872), indicating better solubility in solution. No transmembrane helices or signal peptides were predicted for both vaccine constructs (Fig. S3–S4), supporting their suitability as vaccine candidates. Overall, WLaf1 may offer better immune recognition due to its hydrophilicity, while WLaf2 could provide greater in vivo stability and solubility.

Structural Modeling and Quality Validation

According to the optimization results of tertiary structures, model 5 (for WLaf1) and model 3 (for WLaf2) were selected as the final models and further assessed for quality (Fig. 3A). In the Ramachandran plot analysis from PROCHECK, 95.1% of WLaf1 residues were in the favored zone, 4.3% in the allowed zone, and 0.5% in the disallowed zone (Fig. 3B). Similarly, 96.4% of WLaf2 residues were in the favored zone, 2.9% in the allowed zone, and 0.8% in the disallowed zone, indicating high geometric rationality and stereochemical quality for both models (Fig. 3B). ERRAT scores were 91.59 (WLaf1) and 99.62 (WLaf2), confirming that the non-bonded interaction errors for both structures were within an acceptable range. Additionally, Z-scores from ProSA-web further validated the structural reliability, with WLaf1 scoring − 4.15 and WLaf2 scoring − 3.61, both within the expected range for protein crystal structures (Fig. 3C). In addition, the residue-wise energy plots generated by ProSA showed no significant energy spikes, suggesting that both models possess favorable local structural quality (Fig. 3C). In summary, the optimized tertiary structures of the multi-epitope vaccine models had good performance in terms of geometric rationality, stereochemical quality, and overall structure quality, making them suitable for subsequent functional studies and immunogenicity evaluation.

According to the PDBsum ProMotif analysis, the WLaf1 construct contains 13 α-helices and 2 β-strands forming a single β-sheet (Fig. 3D). Approximately 50.9% of the remaining residues adopt coil conformations. Notably, the presence of two helix-helix interactions suggests well-packed helical domains, which may contribute to the overall structural stability of the model. In comparison, WLaf2 is composed of 15 α-helices and 3 β-strands, which form one β-sheet and a β-hairpin motif. Additional structural features include a β-bulge, one helix-helix interaction, 24 β-turns, and 19 γ-turns, reflecting a more intricate folding pattern (Fig. 3D). Approximately 50.2% of WLaf2 residues are in coil conformations, which may enhance structural flexibility and promote epitope accessibility. Furthermore, the presence of three disulfide bridges in WLaf2 suggests enhanced conformational stability through covalent cross-linking.

Molecular Docking

We first performed blind docking of the multi-epitope vaccine constructs and TLRs using the ZDOCK server, and the top model from every docking was subsequently optimized using the HADDOCK 2.4 server. The refined docking results indicated that two of our designed multi-epitope vaccines, WLaf1 and WLaf2, exhibited strong binding affinity with TLR1-TLR2 heterodimer and TLR4 (Table 5). Notably, the WLaf1-TLR4 complex displayed the highest HADDOCK score (-824.4 ± 8.1), reflecting its strongest binding capacity. Additionally, the WLaf1-TLR4 complex exhibited the largest buried surface area (12305.1 ± 171.2 Å²), which suggested a stable binding interaction. PDBsum analysis revealed the formation of 6 salt bridges and 18 hydrogen bonds in the WLaf1-TLR4 complex, with these interactions playing a crucial role in stabilizing the complex (Table 5; Fig. 4A). In contrast, the WLaf1-TLR1-TLR2 complex had a slightly lower HADDOCK score (-375.4 ± 6.2) but also demonstrated a relatively small buried surface area (6407.3 ± 59.5 Å²) and formed 4 salt bridges and 18 hydrogen bonds (Table 5; Fig. 4B). The WLaf2-TLR4 complex showed a high HADDOCK score (-759.2 ± 5.3) and buried surface area (11917.9 ± 85.5 Å²), with 3 salt bridges and 14 hydrogen bonds (Table 5; Fig. 4C). The WLaf2-TLR1-TLR2 complex had a relatively low HADDOCK score (-368.3 ± 4.5), but its buried surface area (5165.3 ± 64.0 Å²), along with 2 salt bridges and 15 hydrogen bonds, still indicated a certain level of binding stability (Table 5; Fig. 4D). Overall, docking analyses indicate that WLaf1 and WLaf2, which incorporate minimal TLR-targeting agonist motifs, are predicted to engage TLR1–TLR2 and TLR4 receptors, potentially eliciting downstream immune responses. These computational predictions will require experimental validation to confirm the strength, specificity, and functional consequences of these receptor–ligand interactions.

MD Simulation

To further assess the binding affinity and interaction strength of WLaf1 and WLaf2 with TLR1-TLR2 heterodimer and TLR4, 100-ns MD simulations were performed. The resulting trajectories were analyzed to assess key structural and dynamic properties, including RMSD, Rg, Hydrogen bonds and RMSF.

RMSD indicates the degree of deviation of the complex from its initial structure, with its trend revealing the molecular complex’s stability over the simulation. As shown in Fig. 5A, both WLaf1-TLR1/2 and WLaf1-TLR4 complexes reached a plateau within the first 10 ns, indicating that the systems attained relative equilibrium after this period. The RMSD values for WLaf1-TLR1/2 and WLaf1-TLR4 complexes were 1.11 ± 0.10 nm and 0.90 ± 0.11 nm, respectively, suggesting that WLaf1 shows strong affinity for both TLR1-TLR2 heterodimer and TLR4. However, WLaf1-TLR4 complex exhibits a more stable conformation with less fluctuation compared to WLaf1-TLR1/2 complex, implying stronger binding and greater structural stability when interacting with TLR4 (Fig. 5A). Similarly, the RMSD of the WLaf2-TLR4 complex showed a gradual increase during the first 10 ns, after which it reached relative stability with an RMSD value of 1.3 nm, indicating that the WLaf2-TLR4 complex exhibits moderate flexibility and stability. In contrast, the WLaf2-TLR1/2 complex exhibited significant RMSD fluctuations early in the simulation but stabilized around 1.83 nm after approximately 35 ns. This behavior indicates substantial conformational adjustments in the initial stages of the simulation and weaker overall stability compared to the other complexes (Fig. 5A).

The Rg reflects the distribution of distances between the center of mass and all atoms, providing an important measure of structural expansion or contraction. The Rg results further support the RMSD analysis, with WLaf1-TLR1/2 and WLaf1-TLR4 complexes showing Rg values of 3.34 ± 0.16 nm and 3.20 ± 0.22 nm, respectively (Fig. 5B). These values are relatively low and exhibit narrow fluctuations, suggesting that both complexes maintain stable and compact structures throughout the simulation. In contrast, the WLaf2-TLR1/2 and WLaf2-TLR4 complexes demonstrated higher Rg values of 4.32 ± 0.11 nm and 3.54 ± 0.18 nm, respectively. This suggests that these complexes are more expanded, particularly the WLaf2-TLR1/2 complex, which may reflect weaker structural stability and greater conformational flexibility compared to WLaf1-TLR1/2 and WLaf1-TLR4 complexes (Fig. 5B).

The number of Hydrogen bonds represents the strength and stability of intermolecular interactions, which are crucial for stabilizing protein complex structures. In accordance with the RMSD and Rg results, the WLaf1-TLR1/2, WLaf1-TLR4, and WLaf2-TLR4 complexes formed a relatively higher number of hydrogen bonds, with values of 20 ± 4, 27 ± 4, and 21 ± 5, respectively, indicating stronger intermolecular interactions and greater stability (Fig. 5C). Although the WLaf2–TLR1/2 complex formed only 11 ± 3 hydrogen bonds, fewer than those observed in the WLaf1–TLR1/2 complex, the number of Hydrogen bonds remained relatively stable over time (Fig. 5C). This suggests that the binding between WLaf2 and TLR1/2 remains stable despite the reduced intermolecular interactions.

In addition, the RMSF analysis of the vaccine chain has further revealed the local fluctuation of the vaccine chain in different complexes. The WLaf1 chain exhibited minimal fluctuation, with mean RMSF values of 0.2049 ± 0.0504 nm in WLaf1-TLR1/2 complex and 0.2019 ± 0.0715 nm for WLaf1-TLR4 complex (Fig. 5D). These results indicated a relatively stable vaccine chain structure, with limited residue fluctuations. In contrast, the WLaf2 chain showed relatively greater flexibility, particularly in WLaf2-TLR1/2 complex, which had a mean RMSF of 0.3167 ± 0.0684 nm—higher than that of the other complexes—indicating higher flexibility and lower structural stability (Fig. 5D). The WLaf2 chain in the WLaf2-TLR4 complex exhibited a mean RMSF of 0.2850 ± 0.0693 nm, which was higher than that of the WLaf1 complexes but lower than that of WLaf2-TLR1/2, indicating moderate local stability (Fig. 5D). Overall, the RMSF analysis was consistent with the trends observed for RMSD, Rg, and H-bond analysis, reinforcing the superior stability and compactness of the WLaf1-TLRs complexes. Although the WLaf2-TLRs complexes initially exhibited great structural fluctuations and loose conformations, they gradually stabilized over time, particularly the WLaf2-TLR4 complex.

These findings were consistent with the RMSD, Rg, and hydrogen bond analyses, demonstrating that both WLaf1-TLR and WLaf2-TLR complexes maintain stability, with the WLaf1-TLR complexes showing greater stability.

Population Coverage

The predicted global population coverage for the vaccine candidates was 99.98%, indicating broad applicability (Fig. 6 and Table S6). Regions such as West, Central, and East Africa, along with the USA, Brazil, Mexico, and Argentina, all demonstrated 100% coverage, underscoring vaccine candidates’ potential for wide protection. In Southeast Asia and South Asia, regions known for significant air pollution and high rates of fungal infections, vaccine coverage was similarly high, with Southeast Asia reaching 99.92% and South Asia at 99.83% (Fig. 6 and Table S6). Countries like Indonesia and the Philippines, where Aspergillus infections are more prevalent among immunocompromised populations, exhibited strong coverage rates of 99.23% and 97.80%, respectively (Fig. 6 and Table S6). Although South Africa showed a lower coverage of 89.37%, it still surpassed 80%, indicating that regions with more limited healthcare infrastructure could still benefit from the vaccine. Overall, these results suggest that the vaccine has the potential to provide widespread protection, particularly in high-risk areas for A. fumigatus infections, including tropical and humid regions.

Immune Simulation

In terms of antibody production (Fig. 7A and Fig. S5A), immune simulation predicted that both WLaf1 and WLaf2 could elicit IgM, IgG1, and IgG2 responses. Antibody titers peaked approximately 25–30 days after the final dose and declined to stable levels early within the 350-day simulation period—around 20,000 for WLaf1 and WLaf2, and approximately 5,000 for the A. baumannii vaccine. These results suggest the potential for durable humoral immunity in all groups, with the designed multi-epitope vaccines maintaining notably higher predicted antibody levels. Regarding B cell and plasma cell dynamics (Fig. 7B–C and Fig. S5B–C), all multi-epitope vaccines could promote B cell differentiation and transient expansion of plasma cells. WLaf1 was predicted to induce the highest total B cell counts and to sustain memory B cell populations throughout the simulation period. WLaf2 also generated a consistent memory B cell response, albeit at a comparatively lower magnitude. The A. baumannii vaccine exhibited a similar temporal trend but with relatively reduced amplitude. For cellular immune responses (Fig. 7D–E and Fig. S5D–E), all three multi-epitope vaccines were predicted to activate both cytotoxic and helper T cell subsets. WLaf1 elicited the most pronounced predicted helper T cell response, followed by WLaf2. The A. baumannii vaccine also showed measurable T cell activation in the simulation, although the magnitude was somewhat lower than that observed for the WLaf1 and WLaf2. Cytokine profiling (Fig. 7F and Fig. S5F) revealed that all multi-epitope vaccines stimulated the secretion of key cytokines, including IFN-γ and IL-2. WLaf1 generated the highest cytokine levels, indicating potent immune stimulation. WLaf2 and the A. baumannii vaccine also produced moderate but biologically significant cytokine responses. Overall, immune simulation suggested that WLaf1 may induce the most robust and sustained immune responses across humoral, cellular, and cytokine responses, with WLaf2 also demonstrating considerable immunogenic potential. While the A. baumannii vaccine has been experimentally validated and remains an effective immunogen, these in silico findings indicate that WLaf1—and to a lesser extent WLaf2—may confer enhanced immunogenicity under similar simulated conditions. These results provide a rationale for further experimental evaluation of both vaccine candidates.

Disulfide Engineering, Codon Optimization and Cloning

Disulfide engineering of WLaf1 identified four residue pairs suitable for cysteine substitution to enhance structural stability. However, as all were located within epitope regions, no mutations were introduced to preserve antigenic integrity. For WLaf2, no suitable residue pairs were found. Thus, disulfide bond optimization was not performed for either multi-epitope vaccine to maintain epitope functionality and structural fidelity.

The results of optimized vaccine candidate codon sequences showed that both WLaf1 and WLaf2 exhibited a perfect CAI = 1.0 and GC contents of 49.19% and 48.91%, respectively. These values suggest that both vaccine candidates could be highly optimized for efficient expression in E. coli K12. The cloning vectors containing codon sequences and the pET28a (+) plasmid were shown in Fig. 8.

Discussion

In recent years, the increasing number of immunocompromised individuals has led to a notable rise in the incidence and mortality of opportunistic fungal infections, particularly aspergillosis [1]. A. fumigatus, a common environmental fungus, releases spores that can cause a range of diseases, from mild allergic reactions to severe IA, especially in individuals with weakened immune systems [8]. Although antifungal treatments are available, they are often expensive, difficult to access, and associated with side effects [21, 90]. Furthermore, the growing resistance of A. fumigatus to antifungal agents complicates patient management [19]. These challenges highlight the urgent need for novel prevention strategies to combat this escalating health threat.

Traditional vaccine development is hindered by lengthy processes, high costs, and limitations in selecting suitable target pathogens and antigenic proteins. In the current study, we used a subtractive proteomics approach to screen non-homologous, virulence-associated proteins in A. fumigatus as potential vaccine targets. Following the multi-layered screening process, two 1,3-beta-glucanosyltransferase proteins of A. fumigatus were ultimately selected as promising vaccine targets. These proteins are essential for cell wall remodeling and remain surface-accessible in both conidial and hyphal stages, enabling immune recognition [26, 34]. Moreover, 1,3-beta-glucanosyltransferase proteins are highly conserved across pathogenic fungi and absent in humans, making them ideal targets [35]. Importantly, previous studies have supported the potential of cell wall-related enzymes as vaccine targets due to their central role in fungal virulence and host-pathogen interactions, reinforcing the rationale for selecting these proteins in our study[91].

To ensure a comprehensive immune response, we screened epitopes targeting CTL, HTL, and B-cell responses. As demonstrated in previous studies, balancing cellular (CTL, HTL) and humoral (B-cell) immunity is crucial for long-term protection [92]. In our study, CTL and HTL epitopes were selected for their predicted high antigenicity and safety, aiming to induce robust cytotoxic responses and a balanced cytokine milieu (IFN-γ and IL-4), which facilitates both T-cell activation and antibody production [93, 94]. Furthermore, the inclusion of B-cell epitopes supports antibody-mediated neutralization and opsonophagocytic clearance, following a strategy that has been applied in other computational vaccine designs and emphasizes the need for balanced humoral and cellular immune responses [37, 64]. Despite these advantages, predicted epitopes may behave differently in biological systems due to the complexity of antigen processing and presentation pathways. In vivo, epitopes compete for proteasomal processing and binding to MHC molecules, which can influence epitope dominance and immunogenicity [95, 96]. Additionally, peptide degradation, differential processing efficiency, and host genetic variability may shape the final repertoire of presented epitopes [95, 97, 98]. Therefore, although computational epitope prediction provides a valuable starting point for multi-epitope vaccine design, experimental validation remains essential to confirm the immunogenicity and biological relevance of the selected epitopes.

Beyond epitope selection, the structural and physicochemical properties of WLaf1 and WLaf2 provide insights into their potential performance as vaccine candidates. Both vaccine constructs are predicted to be stable, soluble, and antigenic, supporting their suitability for immune recognition. WLaf1’s higher hydrophilicity may enhance interactions with antigen-presenting cells, potentially improving immune activation, whereas WLaf2’s greater predicted stability and solubility could favor prolonged in vivo persistence and efficient delivery. The absence of transmembrane helices and signal peptides further supports their recombinant expression and formulation potential. The results of 3D structure quality validations have confirmed the high reliability of the vaccine models, further supporting their immunogenic potential. Docking and MD simulations suggested strong and stable interactions between the vaccine constructs and the TLR1-TLR2 heterodimer or TLR4. Notably, WLaf1-TLR complexes exhibited smaller RMSD and RMSF values compared to WLaf2, indicating a potentially more rigid and stable conformation, which may facilitate more consistent epitope presentation and stronger receptor-ligand interactions [99, 100]. While these findings suggest effective receptor engagement, it is important to consider the safety of incorporating TLR agonists into the vaccine constructs. RS09, a synthetic minimal peptide, mimics lipid A–mediated TLR4 activation without including full-length lipopolysaccharide, thereby reducing the risk of excessive inflammatory signaling [101]. HBD-3 is an endogenous human peptide with antifungal activity and TLR2-stimulating properties [102, 103], and Pam2Cys has been widely used experimentally as a TLR2 agonist to enhance immune responses [104]. All agonist motifs are covalently linked to the antigenic sequence, which is expected to favor uptake by antigen-presenting cells and limit systemic exposure to free agonist molecules [105]. It is important to note that dose-dependent effects and potential immunopathology remain critical considerations. As this study is computational, experimental dose-response and toxicity data are not yet available. Future studies will include in vitro cytokine profiling and in vivo dose-escalation experiments to empirically determine safe and effective dosing ranges.

Population coverage analysis suggested that the two vaccine candidates could potentially provide broad protection across diverse global populations, highlighting their translational potential for preventive strategies against A. fumigatus. Notably, the predicted high coverage in Southeast and South Asia—regions with high prevalence of immunocompromised individuals and elevated fungal exposure—indicates that these vaccine constructs also could provide effective protection in regions with fewer resources. Nevertheless, it is important to note that these predictions rely on HLA allele frequencies from public databases, and actual coverage may vary due to unaccounted genetic diversity, regional differences, or environmental factors. Therefore, experimental validation and epidemiological studies will be essential to confirm the protective potential and practical applicability of these vaccine candidates across diverse populations.

Immune simulations performed using the C-ImmSim server predicted that both WLaf1 and WLaf2 have the potential to elicit robust adaptive immune responses, characterized by sustained IgM, IgG1, and IgG2 production and a Th1-type cytokine profile dominated by IFN-γ and IL-2. Comparative analysis indicated that WLaf1 consistently exhibited stronger predicted immunogenicity. Th1 responses are essential for activating macrophages and cytotoxic T cells, which contribute to antifungal host defense [106]. Effective defense against A. fumigatus also relies on coordinated Th17 responses, which promote neutrophil recruitment and the production of key cytokines, including IL-17 and IL-22, as well as chemokines such as IL-8. IL-23, secreted by dendritic cells and macrophages, functions as an upstream regulator to maintain and expand Th17-mediated immunity, thereby supporting these antifungal responses [107, 108]. It is important to recognize the limitations of the C-ImmSim platform. The server, which relies on position-specific scoring matrices (PSSM) and machine learning algorithms, primarily predicts Th1/Th2 responses and antibody production, but does not fully capture Th17-associated cytokine networks or neutrophil-mediated immunity [86, 109]. Furthermore, it cannot model the complex host–pathogen interactions and immune evasion mechanisms characteristic of fungal infections, which may influence the magnitude, quality, and kinetics of in vivo immune responses. Consequently, the absence of Th17-related cytokines and neutrophil-associated signals in the simulation output reflects methodological limitations rather than an inherent inability of the vaccine constructs to induce these responses. Despite these limitations, the predicted Th1-dominant responses suggest effective T-cell activation and memory formation, while moderate and sustained cytokine levels may support balanced immune activation. Vaccine efficacy in immunocompromised hosts may be reduced, as impaired T-cell or neutrophil function could limit both Th1- and Th17-mediated responses [110]. The intended application of these vaccine candidates is primarily preventive in high-risk populations rather than therapeutic. Overall, WLaf1 demonstrated stronger predicted immunogenicity in silico; however, the protective efficacy of both vaccine candidates requires experimental validation, particularly in models evaluating Th17 responses, neutrophil activity, and immunocompromised conditions.

Conclusion

In summary, our study identified highly conserved and potentially immunogenic epitopes from A. fumigatus and designed two multi-epitope vaccines based on these epitopes. Both vaccine candidates are predicted to exhibit favorable structural stability, potential interactions with TLRs, and broad population coverage. Among them, WLaf1 may elicit relatively stronger humoral and cellular responses, while WLaf2 could offer enhanced stability and solubility. These in silico findings provide a preliminary framework for further experimental validation and suggest that multi-epitope vaccines could serve as a promising prophylactic approach against A. fumigatus infections. Nevertheless, further in vitro and in vivo studies are necessary to confirm the immunogenicity, safety, and protective efficacy of these vaccine candidates, providing a foundation for future experimental and preclinical validation.

Limitation

This study employed comprehensive bioinformatics and immunoinformatics approaches to design multi-epitope vaccine constructs, providing a robust theoretical framework for preventive strategies against A. fumigatus. Nevertheless, several limitations remain. Computationally predicted epitopes, while carefully selected, may not fully reflect in vivo processing, presentation, or immunogenicity. The inclusion of TLR agonists is theoretically justified but remains untested, and potential dose-dependent effects or immunopathology are unknown. Molecular docking and MD simulations offered insights into vaccine–TLR interactions, but cannot fully account for in vivo chemical modifications or cellular context that may affect binding. Population coverage predictions rely on HLA allele databases and may not fully represent regional genetic and environmental variability. Finally, immune simulations primarily predict Th1/Th2 responses, omitting Th17-mediated immunity and neutrophil-driven effects. Collectively, these in silico predictions require experimental validation—including recombinant expression, immunization, cytokine profiling, and in vivo challenge studies—to confirm immunogenicity, safety, and protective efficacy.
