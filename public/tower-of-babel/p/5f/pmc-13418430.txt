Advances in the molecular subtyping of gastric cancer
Authors: Jiayi Chen, Xijie Zhang, Yuzhou Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Despite decades of significant progress in gastric cancer research, the prognosis remains poor because of the disease’s high aggressiveness. Historically, gastric cancers were classified based on histopathology and anatomical site—criteria that inadequately support precision therapy. Advances in molecular technologies have produced multiple molecular classification schemes, among which the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and the Asian Cancer Research Group (ACRG) classifications are the most mature and widely applied. In this review, we systematically summarize molecular subtypes of gastric cancer and explore their underlying molecular mechanisms, therapeutic implications, and prognostic associations. The evidence indicates that molecular subtyping is a key basis for improving precision therapy and enhancing patient outcomes: TCGA and ACRG provide clear clinical guidance, while emerging classifications offer new directions for individualized treatment and support the clinical translation of precision oncology in gastric cancer.

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is the fifth most common malignancy worldwide and the third leading cause of cancer-related death. Although numerous classification systems exist [1], GC is most commonly categorized by anatomical site and TNM stage [2]. Several specialized schemes are also used—for example, the Paris classification for superficial lesions and the Borrmann and Japan Gastroenterological Endoscopy Society (JGES) classifications for advanced gastric cancer [3–5].

At the histopathological level, gastric cancer is primarily classified using the WHO Classification and the Lauren classification. The WHO classification of Tumors of the Digestive System divides gastric cancer into adenocarcinoma and other rare types; adenocarcinoma subtypes include tubular, papillary, mucinous adenocarcinoma, and poorly cohesive carcinoma (including signet‑ring cell carcinoma), and this framework remains the cornerstone of clinicopathologic diagnosis [6, 7]. The classic Lauren classification categorizes gastric cancer into intestinal and diffuse types: intestinal-type tumors form glandular/tubular structures, whereas diffuse-type tumors show scattered, infiltrative growth [8]. A mixed category was subsequently added, and the Lauren scheme continues to inform clinical decision-making [9].

Although conventional morphological classifications remain widely used, they insufficiently capture the intrinsic features of gastric cancer’s pronounced heterogeneity and thus offer limited guidance for individualized therapy and prognostic assessment. With advances in molecular biology, multi-omics–based molecular classification has increasingly become key to overcoming bottlenecks in precision oncology for gastric cancer. This article aims to systematically synthesize the various molecular classification schemes proposed in recent years, elucidate the molecular basis of gastric cancer heterogeneity, compare the characteristics of different classification frameworks and their associations with clinical prognosis and treatment response, and provide a theoretical foundation for optimizing precision-management strategies in gastric cancer.

Historical development and technological advances

Literature search strategy and study selection criteria

We searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) to identify Chinese- and English-language literature addressing molecular subtyping of gastric cancer. Search terms included “gastric cancer,” “molecular subtyping” (or “molecular classification”), “TCGA,” “ACRG,” and “Lauren classification,” with the time window spanning database inception through the date of manuscript submission. We included reviews, clinical studies, and basic research specifically related to gastric cancer classification. We excluded duplicate reports, studies unrelated to the topic, and abstracts or conference papers lacking complete data. All records were screened and the eligible studies collated by investigators according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria to ensure scientific rigor and reliability.

Evolution of detection technologies

In the early 21st century, gastric cancer classification was driven largely by single-gene paradigms: investigators studied individual alterations such as HER2, VEGFR, and MET, but these approaches failed to fully capture tumor heterogeneity. In 2014, the TCGA consortium published a genomic atlas that, for the first time, classified gastric cancer into four molecular subtypes; this shifted the field from single-gene frameworks to genome-wide characterization and marked the entry of gastric cancer molecular subtyping into the whole-genome analysis era. Subsequent proteomics, transcriptomics, and other multi-omics studies have further revealed deeper, complementary molecular alterations in gastric cancer.

Concurrently, advances in molecular methodologies have given us the tools to elucidate the molecular biology of gastric cancer. First, from comparative genomic hybridization (CGH) to next‑generation sequencing (NGS), large‑scale exome sequencing has been progressively applied to gastric cancer research. Second, progress in epigenetics—particularly refined DNA methylation profiling—has uncovered the CpG island methylator phenotype (CIMP) characteristic of Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)-positive gastric cancers. Subsequently, the development of in situ hybridization (ISH) and immunohistochemistry (IHC) has enabled precise intracellular localization of nucleic acids and reliable assessment of protein expression, routinely used for EBV detection and rapid evaluation of TP53 and PD-L1 status. In addition, targeted gene sequencing and transcriptomic platforms—especially gene expression microarrays and targeted gene panels—permit multiplexed analysis of tumor‑associated genes and facilitate discovery of clinically actionable targets [10].

Molecular classification of gastric cancer

Established core classifications: TCGA and ACRG

Based on multi-omics data, the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) consortium divided gastric cancer into four molecular subtypes: EBV-positive, microsatellite instability (MSI), genomically stable (GS), and chromosomal instability (CIN) [11]. Each subtype exhibits distinct molecular and clinical features: EBV-positive tumors are driven by EBV-associated DNA hypermethylation and frequently show PD-L1 overexpression, suggesting potential sensitivity to immunotherapy [12]; MSI tumors are characterized by high tumor mutational burden and mismatch repair deficiency and demonstrate substantially higher response rates to PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors compared with other subtypes—MSI status is now an approved pan-cancer biomarker for immunotherapy [13]; the GS subtype shows low copy-number alteration burden and correlates closely with diffuse-type gastric cancer; the CIN subtype is marked by frequent TP53 mutations, is common in Lauren intestinal-type tumors at the gastroesophageal junction, and represents the primary target for conventional and certain targeted therapies [14]. A Chinese phase II clinical trial suggested that EBV-positive gastric cancer patients treated with a PD-L1 inhibitor had higher objective response and disease control rates than an unselected advanced gastric cancer population, but this observation requires prospective validation [15].

In follow-up studies, histopathologic imaging–based methods for detecting EBV positivity and MSI subtypes, as developed by Hinata et al., offer low-cost strategies that improve clinical applicability [16].

Because the TCGA classification was largely derived from Western populations, the Asian Cancer Research Group (ACRG) classification was developed to better align with East Asian patient characteristics [17]. It subdivides gastric cancer into MSI-type, MSS/EMT (mesenchymal-like) type, MSS/TP53⁺ (TP53-activated), and MSS/TP53⁻ (TP53-inactivated). While highly concordant with TCGA, ACRG’s key advancement is the definition of the MSS/EMT subtype, which is characterized by loss-of-function mutations in CDH1, CLDN18-ARHGAP26/6 fusion genes, and a strong propensity for peritoneal metastasis. Subsequent studies have found that this subtype exhibits high CLDN18.2 expression, driving clinical development of CLDN18.2-targeted monoclonal antibodies (e.g., zolbetuximab), which has been approved for gastric cancer treatment in some countries—illustrating the link between molecular subtyping and targeted drug development. Moreover, the ACRG subtype can be read with high fidelity by conventional immunohistochemistry (IHC), improving clinical applicability [18].

In sum, TCGA and ACRG classifications are not mutually exclusive; rather, they are complementary in terms of population basis, technological application, and clinical orientation: TCGA delineates the genomic defects underpinning gastric carcinogenesis, while ACRG translates molecular signals into clinically actionable subtypes. They represent, to date, the gastric cancer molecular subtyping systems with the highest levels of evidence and the widest clinical application, having been validated across multiple independent cohorts.

Genomic and transcriptomic exploratory classifications

Prior to the establishment of TCGA and ACRG, several studies attempted molecular subtyping of gastric cancer based on gene expression profiles. Notably, Tan and colleagues stratified gastric cancer into two major subtypes: G-INT (gastric intestinal-type) and G-DIF (gastric diffuse-type) [19]. G-INT is characterized by upregulation of genes related to intestinal epithelial differentiation, aligning with the Lauren intestinal type; G-DIF shows downregulation of CDH1, concordant with the diffuse type of gastric cancer. This work suggested that tumors’ biological behavior is defined early by gene expression programs, yet this classification has not been validated in independent external cohorts. Lei and colleagues further subdivided gastric cancer into the mesenchymal, proliferative, and metabolic types [20] and found that the proliferative type is sensitive to PI3K inhibitors, whereas the metabolic type is more sensitive to 5-fluorouracil (both based on retrospective analyses). Compared with the TCGA classification, Lei’s metabolic type partially maps to TCGA’s GS and CIN subtypes, but the latter has advantages in sample size and the degree of multi-omics integration. Additionally, Shah and colleagues proposed a three-way scheme based on anatomical location—proximal non-diffuse, diffuse, and distal non-diffuse types [21]—which provides molecular clues for screening hereditary diffuse gastric cancer, but this classification has not been independently validated and currently remains exploratory.

As understanding of tumor heterogeneity deepens, some studies have attempted to integrate mutational burden and genomic instability into molecular subtypes. Li et al. classified gastric cancer based on tumor mutational burden (TMB) into conventional-type and hypermutated-type [22], with the hypermutated type largely corresponding to MSI and EBV subtypes in TCGA classification. However, this binary scheme did not provide information beyond TCGA and has not been validated in independent cohorts. Wang et al. proposed S1-S4 continuous-spectrum subtypes [23], aiming to quantify the synergistic effects of chromosomal instability and TMB, but its clinical significance remains to be confirmed by prospective studies. Furukawa et al. integrated TMB with T-cell inflammation and EMT features to define four subtypes—HMT, TCI, HEMT, and EMTL [24]; among them, the HMT/TCI intersecting subtype showed higher response rates to PD-1 inhibitors in retrospective analyses, but this classification has not been systematically compared with TCGA/ACRG.

Taken together, the genome/transcriptome-based subtypes described above are derived from retrospective data, lack independent multicenter validation, and have not entered clinical practice. Currently, TCGA and ACRG remain the most frequently referenced frameworks in gastric cancer molecular classification; these exploratory subtypes may offer supplementary perspectives but require further prospective evidence to support their clinical value.

Immune-related exploratory classifications

Immuno-related typing is based on the functional state of the tumor microenvironment and aims to predict responses to immunotherapy. At present, the field remains exploratory, and no unified typing system has entered clinical practice.

The four-quadrant framework (Hot1, Hot2, Intermediate, and Cold) proposed by Sato et al. [25] is among the earlier representative works. This model uses CD8 + T cell infiltration and PD-L1 expression as indicators to stratify tumors into hot and cold phenotypes: Hot1 is rich in T cells but in a PD-L1–mediated exhausted state, suggesting potential sensitivity to PD-1 inhibitors; Hot2 may harbor other immunosuppressive mechanisms. The model has not yet been validated in independent cohorts, and its correspondence with TCGA/ACRG remains unclear.

Chen Y et al. proposed a tripartite framework of tumor-infiltrating immune cell features, defining IS1 (immune-excluded), IS2 (immune-desert), and IS3 (immune-inflamed) [26]. This classification further delineates the spatial distribution and functional states of immune cells: in IS1, T cells are hindered by cancer-associated fibroblasts and a collagen-rich stromal barrier; IS2 is characterized by a paucity of T cells and dendritic cells; IS3 is enriched for immune cells, theoretically yielding the greatest benefit from immunotherapy. The framework partially overlaps with TCGA’s MSI and EBV subtypes (primarily corresponding to IS3) but lacks validation in independent external cohorts. Subsequently, Cao et al. established a similar tripartite classification based on CD8 + T cell distribution [27], providing indirect support for the IS1-IS3 framework.

Moreover, Zhang S et al. [28] and Liu Z et al. [29] proposed continuous immunoinfiltration subtypes (Immunity-H/M/L and IM-H/L). Liu Z’s classification was retrospectively validated in the TCGA cohort; the IM-H subtype corresponds to TCGA’s MSI/EBV subtypes and is associated with better prognosis. However, continuous subtyping risks conflating exhausted with functionally active highly infiltrated phenotypes, limiting predictive accuracy.

Lin Y et al. classified patients using IMS scores into high-IMS (immune-dominant) and low-IMS (stroma-activated-dominant) subtypes [30], finding a significant negative correlation between immune infiltration and stromal activation, which helps explain why some patients with high T-cell infiltration fail to respond to immunotherapy due to microenvironmental barriers. Wu D et al. [31] and Wang G et al. [32] focused on TNF and antigen presentation pathway gene expression, partitioning gastric cancer into C1/C2 or immune-L/H clusters, emphasizing the integrity of cytotoxic T-cell function. The aforementioned classifications have not been validated in independent cohorts, and no comparative analyses with TCGA/ACRG have been reported. Notably, the spatial organization of immune cells is emerging as a crucial adjunct to immunophenotyping. Studies have demonstrated that the aggregation status of CD8⁺PD-1⁺LAG3⁻ T cells [33] and the density and spatial proximity characteristics of specific immune cells within tertiary lymphoid structures [34] can effectively predict immunotherapy response in gastric cancer patients.

Taken together, the current immune molecular classification of gastric cancer has not yet coalesced into a unified framework and remains largely based on retrospective, exploratory studies. Although immune-based subtyping can complement classical molecular classifications such as TCGA and ACRG (for example, immune-enriched subtypes often correspond to MSI or EBV-positive tumors), prospective cohort validation is required to establish its clinical utility. Looking ahead, integrative models that fuse multidimensional information hold promise for guiding immunotherapy decision-making.

TME and EMT exploratory classifications

Stratifications based on the tumor microenvironment (TME) and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) aim to elucidate mechanisms of invasion, metastasis, immune evasion, and therapy resistance. Currently, the field remains exploratory, and no unified classification independent of TCGA/ACRG has entered clinical practice.

Oh et al. classified gastric cancer into mesenchymal phenotype (MP) and epithelial phenotype (EP) based on gene expression signatures [31]. The MP subtype is enriched for EMT regulatory factors and shows substantial overlap with the MSS/EMT subtype in the ACRG classification; the EP subtype has relatively intact cell–cell adhesion structures. The study found that MP-subtype patients have shorter overall survival, lower response rates to fluoropyrimidine-based chemotherapy, and are more prone to peritoneal metastasis. This classification represents an early exploration in the EMT field but has not yet been validated in independent external cohorts.

Cheong et al. categorized gastric cancer into IM-type (immune), EP-type (epithelial), and ST-type (stromal) based on three gene-expression modules—immune, stem-like, and epithelial [35, 36]. The IM-type is characterized by T-cell infiltration and high PD-L1 expression and shows substantial overlap with TCGA MSI/EBV subtypes and the IS3 subtype described by Chen et al. The EP-type displays complete epithelial differentiation but immune inertness. The ST-type is enriched for stromal markers and accompanied by immune exclusion. The clinical utility of this classification remains to be validated by prospective studies.

Additionally, Tanaka et al. explored the correlation of EMT typing in gastric cancer with ascites-associated peritoneal dissemination [37], but this finding stems from a single cohort and lacks independent validation. Cheong et al.‘s subsequent four-subtype grouping (Groups 1–4) [38] and Zhou et al.‘s C1-C3 subtypes based on single-cell multimodal data [39] similarly lack independent validation, and comparisons with TCGA/ACRG have not yet been performed; this is not elaborated here.

In sum, Oh et al.‘s binary classification (MP/EP) and Cheong et al.‘s ternary classification (IM/EP/ST) show clear associations with the TCGA/ACRG framework and are among the most representative exploratory classifications in the current TME/EMT field, but all require prospective cohort validation of their clinical value. Other emerging classifications lack independent validation and cannot yet inform clinical decisions.

Epigenetic and metabolic exploratory classifications

Epigenetic regulation and metabolic reprogramming are important drivers of gastric cancer initiation and progression, and subtypes based on these two features remain in an early exploratory stage with limited clinical translational evidence.

Epigenetic regulation plays a pivotal role in gastric carcinogenesis, with aberrant DNA methylation driving tumor progression by silencing tumor suppressor genes [40]. Weng et al. classified gastric cancer into four subtypes, C1–C4, based on DNA methylation signatures [41]. This classification was validated in 1,521 samples from five independent multicenter cohorts. In terms of molecular and clinical characteristics, C1 exhibits upregulation of proliferative and transforming pathways and is sensitive to 5-fluorouracil and paclitaxel; C2 has an intermediate prognosis and derives greater benefit from apatinib and cisplatin; C3 presents an immunosuppressive phenotype with resistance to conventional chemotherapy and targeted therapies but sensitivity to dasatinib; C4 displays an immune-activated phenotype, potentially benefiting more from immunotherapy, but with the worst prognosis. Additionally, this classification shows significant associations with TCGA and Chen’s ternary classification [42]: C1/C2 largely correspond to MSI, C3 to MSS/GS (with prognosis similar to Chen L2), and C4 to MSS (similar to Chen L3). However, these drug-sensitivity predictions are primarily based on bioinformatic analyses and require prospective clinical validation.

Additionally, Li et al. [43] and Ye et al. [44], based on metabolic genes, proposed C1/C2 binary typing and C1–C4 four-type typing, respectively. These classifications suggest that high metabolic activity can shape an immunosuppressive microenvironment and partially overlap with EBV-positive gastric cancer, but they are all retrospective explorations and have not yet entered clinical validation; this article does not discuss them in detail.

In summary, epigenetic typing has preliminarily demonstrated an association with clinical efficacy, but the level of evidence remains exploratory; metabolism-related typing remains at the hypothesis-generating stage. Currently, TCGA and ACRG remain the mainstream frameworks for gastric cancer molecular classification, and epigenetic and metabolic features may serve as supplementary dimensions for future integrated multi-omics subtyping.

Technology-driven exploratory classifications

Advances in detection technologies have given rise to a series of gastric cancer molecular classifications anchored on emerging platforms. These classifications complement traditional omics-based stratifications such as TCGA and ACRG; some have already been applied to clinical stratification, while most remain exploratory.

Ramos et al., using immunohistochemistry and in situ hybridization, defined five subtypes: EBV-positive, MSI, p53-abnormal, E-cadherin-abnormal, and p53-normal [45]. The model reproduces TCGA molecular subtyping with routine pathological techniques, and all five markers are readily accessible, low-cost clinical assays (EBV detected by ISH, MSI inferred from MMR protein IHC, and p53 and E-cadherin assessed by standard IHC). This subtyping has been recommended by Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology (CSCO) guidelines (2023 editions) for initial screening of immunotherapy biomarkers [46], representing the most clinically translated, technology-driven model to date. However, its concordance with true molecular subtypes is limited, and prospective validation remains warranted.

In contrast to the “static snapshot” provided by a single tissue biopsy, liquid biopsies utilizing circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) and long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) offer continuous sampling via peripheral blood, thereby capturing the spatiotemporal heterogeneity and clonal evolution of gastric cancer [47]. ctDNA can simultaneously reflect genomic variant information from both primary and metastatic sites, overcoming the spatial limitations of traditional tissue biopsies [48]. During treatment, dynamic changes in ctDNA, such as the emergence of resistance-associated mutations and fluctuations in mutant allele frequency, can predict treatment response and resistance development weeks to months in advance, providing a basis for dynamic patient stratification [49]. Furthermore, the diagnostic value of exosomal lncRNA signatures (e.g., NALT1, PTENP1, HOTTIP) has been validated in multi-center cohorts [50]. Currently, liquid biopsy-based subtyping remains in the clinical exploration phase, facing challenges such as low ctDNA abundance and inconsistent detection standards, necessitating validation in large-scale prospective cohorts.

Other classifications derived from single-cell sequencing, spatial transcriptomics, and proteomics (e.g., Chen’s L1-L3 [42], Tian’s stromal subtype [51], Li’s PX1-PX3 [52], and Shi’s G-P1-G-P4 [53]) remain at the hypothesis-generating stage. These lack prospective validation and adequate comparative analysis against TCGA/ACRG datasets and thus currently do not inform clinical decision-making.

In summary, IHC-based surrogate models represent the most clinically advanced approach. Liquid biopsies offer a novel avenue for dynamic subtyping, while other emerging multi-omics technologies are still in the early stages of exploration (Fig. 1; Table 1).

Despite variations in markers and methods, these classifications are often interconnected, particularly through their correlation with the major TCGA/ACRG subtypes. For instance, the E-cadherin-aberrant subtype in the Ramos classification (based on IHC/ISH) shows high concordance with the GS phenotype. In epigenetics-based classifications, such as Weng’s, C1/C2 correspond to the MSI subtype, and the prognostic characteristics of C3/C4 align with L2/L3 in Lei’s classification.

In terms of clinical guidance value, certain subtypes have demonstrated clinical potential. For instance, the MSS/TP53⁻ subtype within the ACRG classification is frequently associated with HER2 amplification. A retrospective study indicated that patients with HER2-positive gastric cancer treated with trastuzumab achieved a median overall survival of 16 months [54]. However, most emerging classifications are still in the exploratory phase. Examples include the S1-S4 classification by Wang et al., based on mutational burden, which remains in the hypothesis-generation stage, and the Weng classification, based on epigenetics, which is currently undergoing Phase II clinical trials. However, these clinical associations described above primarily stem from retrospective analyses and require validation through prospective studies.

In summary, transitioning gastric cancer molecular classifications from theoretical discovery to clinical practice is paramount for achieving individualized precision therapy (Fig. 2).

Significance of molecular subtypes in gastric cancer

Gastric cancer is characterized by significant phenotypic and genetic heterogeneity. Traditional classifications, such as the Lauren and WHO classifications, rely on clinicopathological features and routine biomarkers. Although simple and widely used, these methods have limitations in guiding treatment and predicting prognosis [55].

Advances in technology have enabled molecular subtyping to better capture gastric cancer heterogeneity through analysis of gene mutations and transcriptional profiles. Among these, the TCGA and ACRG classifications have emerged as the most influential. However, molecular subtypes are not entirely independent of traditional classifications; rather, they serve as crucial complements and advancements [56]. For instance, the TCGA’s genomically stable (GS) subtype often correlates with Lauren diffuse-type gastric cancer, while the chromosomal instability (CIN) and some microsatellite instability (MSI) subtypes are more frequently observed in Lauren intestinal-type gastric cancer. Histologically, tubular or papillary adenocarcinomas predominantly exhibit CIN or MSI subtypes, whereas signet-ring cell or mucinous adenocarcinomas are primarily associated with the GS subtype. Undifferentiated carcinomas present more complex molecular characteristics. Tumor location also correlates with molecular subtype: gastric antrum cancers are predominantly intestinal-type and CIN subtypes, whereas gastric fundus/body cancers are more often diffuse-type, GS, or EBV-positive subtypes. Furthermore, within molecular subtypes, GS and EBV subtypes are more prone to distant metastasis and present with later TNM staging compared to CIN and MSI subtypes.

Molecular subtyping of gastric cancer can guide clinical diagnosis and treatment practices across multiple dimensions [57]. In immunotherapy, exploratory studies suggest that transforming growth factor-related genes (TSRGs) and cholesterol metabolism-related genes (CMRGs) may be associated with treatment response [58, 59]. Furthermore, host factors such as Helicobacter pylori infection can also influence the response of gastric cancer patients to immunotherapy by reshaping the intestinal and intraluminal microbiome [60]. In targeted therapy, anti-HER2 treatment has been utilized for HER2-positive gastric cancer patients (approximately 10%), and some studies indicate that the combination of targeted and immunotherapy may improve the R0 resection rate in locally advanced gastric cancer [61]. Molecular subtyping also offers more precise prognostic assessment: MSI-type gastric cancer exhibits a favorable prognosis in early stages, but its efficacy is comparable to MSS-type in metastatic disease [62]. Concurrently, findings from ongoing research are progressively being integrated into clinical practice guidelines, providing a basis for the precision diagnosis and treatment of gastric cancer.

Challenges and prospects

Despite significant advances in existing molecular subtyping systems for gastric cancer, its clinical translation faces multifaceted challenges.

Initially, the clinical translation of established and widely adopted core classifications, such as TCGA and ACRG, faces significant hurdles primarily related to technical accessibility and cost-effectiveness. While both TCGA and ACRG classifications have established a gold standard framework for molecular stratification of gastric cancer, they rely on deep multi-omics sequencing technologies. This leads to high costs, complex workflows, and stringent sample requirements, limiting their use on small biopsies or tumors with low cellularity. Specifically, TCGA classification necessitates fresh, frozen samples and whole exome/whole genome sequencing. Although ACRG classification can partially substitute key indicators through immunohistochemistry (IHC), transcriptome analysis has not yet achieved widespread clinical adoption. These limitations directly impede their dissemination in resource-constrained primary healthcare settings and routine pathological diagnostics.

Furthermore, for various exploratory classifications, the absence of standardization and clinical validation represents a primary impediment to their translation into clinical practice. As previously discussed, the majority of classifications are validated solely through single-center, small-sample cohorts, lacking prospective validation in independent, multi-center cohorts, raising concerns about their stability and reproducibility. Significant discrepancies exist among different research teams regarding feature selection criteria, technological platforms, and analytical pipelines. This heterogeneity hinders the integration of various classification systems, precluding the establishment of unified clinical diagnostic standards [63]. Specifically, certain transcriptomic classifications (e.g., Lei classification) only partially overlap with TCGA/ACRG, exhibiting limited clinical applicability. Within immunophenotyping, a universally accepted standard for “cold/hot tumors” has yet to be established. EMT classifications largely rely on mRNA expression, and single-time-point biopsies may not adequately capture dynamic processes [64]. Additionally, the epigenetic classification necessitates methylation arrays or targeted sequencing technologies, which are not yet incorporated into routine diagnostic panels.

Furthermore, while technology-driven alternative classifications offer lower costs and greater accessibility and are recommended by CSCO guidelines, their coverage of molecular markers remains less comprehensive than TCGA/ACRG classifications, limiting their stratification capability for certain atypical cases (e.g., p53 immunohistochemistry cannot fully replace the assessment of TP53 mutational status). Finally, for emerging technology-driven classifications, challenges include insufficient technological maturity and a lack of standardization in result interpretation. Although liquid biopsies overcome the spatiotemporal limitations of tissue biopsies, the abundance of ctDNA in early gastric cancer is extremely low, and lncRNA detection lacks standardized protocols. Consequently, many related classifications are still in the hypothesis generation phase and have not yet formed a stable clinical translation pathway.

In summary, future efforts should focus on several areas: First, to address the cost barrier of TCGA/ACRG subtyping, low-cost alternatives based on IHC/ISH should be further developed, or qPCR-based ctDNA assay kits should be created. Concurrently, the workflows for new technologies such as single-cell and spatial omics should be optimized for standardized validation of exploratory subtyping. Second, artificial intelligence should be leveraged to integrate multi-omics, pathological imaging, and clinical data to construct a dynamically updated, universal subtyping framework and establish an open-access, shared database. For instance, the Gastric Cancer Multi-immunohistochemical Atlas Project (GMAP) open database has mapped the spatial distribution of 65 immune cell types, providing a public platform to validate and optimize exploratory subtypes [65]. Third, relying on liquid biopsy technology, static subtyping should be upgraded to dynamic monitoring subtyping, creating a diagnosis-treatment-monitoring loop, providing tools for adaptive therapy [66]. Fourth, organoid or patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models should be utilized to validate subtyping-guided drug combinations [67], advancing prospective clinical trials and improving patient survival.

Conclusion

This review systematically examines the advancements in gastric cancer molecular subtyping. It encompasses established classifications such as TCGA and ACRG, alongside emerging, exploratory classifications based on transcriptomics, the immune microenvironment, EMT, epigenetics, and technology-driven approaches. Currently, the TCGA and ACRG classifications remain the most widely utilized molecular frameworks due to their substantial sample sizes and extensive multi-cohort validation. However, their clinical adoption is constrained by cost and technical barriers. While other exploratory subtypes illuminate gastric cancer’s heterogeneity from various perspectives and offer potential therapeutic targets for precision medicine, the vast majority lack independent, multi-center, prospective validation and are still in the hypothesis-generating stage, thus unable to directly guide clinical decision-making at present. The various subtypes are not mutually exclusive; instead, they map to and complement the TCGA/ACRG framework to varying degrees. Future research must prioritize the development of low-cost, standardized detection methods, promote validation through prospective clinical trials, and integrate technologies such as liquid biopsies for dynamic monitoring. Ultimately, this will establish a closed-loop system linking molecular subtyping to individualized therapy.
