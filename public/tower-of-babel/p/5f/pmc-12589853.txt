Efficacy and Acceptability of Licensed and Off‐Label Pharmacological Interventions for Insomnia in Patients With Severe Mental Illness: A Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis of Randomised Trials
Authors: Nina Friis Bak Fuglsang, Nanna Marker Madsen, Søren Lundorff Jacobsen, Julie Eg Frøkjær, Nicolai Ladegaard, Marc Alberg Sørensen, Christoph U. Correll, Christian Otte, Mikkel Højlund, Ole Köhler‐Forsberg
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundA wide range of drugs is used to alleviate insomnia symptoms in individuals with severe mental illness (SMI), including licensed drugs and sedating drugs prescribed off‐label. Yet, no review has gathered the evidence on illness‐specific or transdiagnostic outcomes of pharmacological interventions for insomnia. We aimed to perform a systematic review and meta‐analysis of randomised controlled trials (RCTs) studying the efficacy and acceptability of pharmacological interventions for insomnia among individuals with SMI, defined as schizophrenia, bipolar disorder (BD) or major depressive disorder (MDD).MethodsWe searched for RCTs of pharmacological interventions for insomnia that used either placebo or another medication as inactive control or active comparator. Two independent reviewers performed the literature screening, data extraction and risk of bias assessment (RoB2). We performed random effects meta‐analyses on the co‐primary outcomes total sleep time (TST), sleep quality and acceptability (all‐cause discontinuation) and the secondary outcomes safety and tolerability.ResultsThe search identified 3331 hits, of which 25 RCTs (n = 2476 individuals) were included, with 18 RCTs (n = 2199) in MDD, 4 RCTs (n = 162) in BD and 3 RCTs (n = 115) in schizophrenia. Of 25 RCTs, 22 had a high risk of bias. The most frequently studied drugs were agomelatine (RCTs = 3, n = 686), eszopiclone (RCTs = 3, n = 599) and zolpidem (RCTs = 3, n = 601). Compared to placebo, pharmacological interventions for insomnia were associated with improved sleep quality by a small effect size (RCTs = 8, g = 0.24, 95% CI = 0.05–0.43) and improved TST (RCTs = 10, MD = 30.82 min, 95% CI = 19.13–42.50), with similar acceptability (RCTs = 10, RR = 1.06, 95% CI = 0.90–1.25).DiscussionDespite their frequent use, many licensed and off‐label pharmacological interventions for insomnia have never been investigated in patients with SMI. The studies that provided sufficient data for meta‐analysis showed better efficacy with similar acceptability compared to placebo, but the generalizability of these results is limited by the high heterogeneity and low quality of the included studies. This underscores the need for high‐quality RCTs to provide a better scientific basis for the pharmacological treatment of insomnia in SMI. Trial Registration: CRD42023413787

Introduction

Insomnia symptoms encompass poor sleep quality, trouble falling asleep or maintaining sleep and affect 12%–20% of the general population [1]. In people with severe mental illness (SMI), defined as schizophrenia, bipolar disorder (BD) and major depressive disorder (MDD), insomnia occurs even more frequently, affecting approximately half of patients with schizophrenia or BD [2, 3] and > 75% of patients with MDD [4]. For chronic insomnia, cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia is the recommended first‐line treatment in the European guideline [5], but lack of resources limits the availability of this treatment. Accordingly, pharmacological interventions for insomnia, such as benzodiazepines and Z‐drugs, but also low doses of various off‐label sedative drugs, are frequently prescribed in patients with SMI [6, 7, 8]. Particularly, due to the risk of addiction and tolerance in many licensed medications [9, 10] and the intention to prescribe drugs that also target other psychiatric symptoms, off‐label prescription of sedating antidepressants, antihistamines and antipsychotics is common in clinical practice. However, especially drugs used off‐label to alleviate insomnia symptoms in SMI largely differ in their pharmacological mechanisms, efficacy, acceptability and safety, as well as in their pharmacological interactions with other psychotropic drugs.

Few systematic reviews have compared pharmacological interventions for insomnia in individuals with SMI and have either focused on single disorders or specific drug classes. In schizophrenia, melatonin and paliperidone have been found to improve sleep compared to placebo [11, 12] while the results for Z‐drugs were inconclusive [13]. However, the analyses were based on only five randomised controlled trials (RCTs) or cross‐over trials covering 240 patients in total. In BD, melatonin receptor agonists showed inconsistent effects on insomnia [14, 15]. In MDD, quetiapine showed larger improvements in sleep quality compared to placebo in post hoc analyses [16], while several antidepressants, particularly trazodone and nefazodone, have shown effects on insomnia in post hoc analyses of head‐to‐head comparisons [17].

In the general population, a recent systematic review excluding patients with mental disorders studied all pharmacological interventions for insomnia disorder, including 47,950 individuals and showed that eszopiclone and lemborexant have a favourable profile, whilst melatonin has poor efficacy [18]. Yet, despite insomnia representing an important and frequently occurring clinical challenge in SMI, there has been no similar systematic review that has comprehensively collected the entire evidence on all licensed and off‐label pharmacological interventions for insomnia studied in RCTs.

Aims of the Study

Therefore, we aimed to conduct this systematic review and meta‐analysis of RCTs on the efficacy, acceptability and safety of pharmacological interventions for insomnia compared to placebo, inactive pharmacological controls or active comparators in individuals with SMI, defined as schizophrenia, BD and MDD.

Materials and Methods

This study is a systematic literature review with a pre‐registered protocol on Prospero (CRD42023413787) covering a priori defined research question, search strategy, inclusion/exclusion criteria, risk of bias assessment and meta‐analyses including investigating causes for heterogeneity, all conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses Protocols (PRISMA‐P) statement (Table S1). This manuscript is reported in accordance with PRISMA checklists (Tables S1 and S2).

Definition of Pharmacological Interventions for Insomnia

As patients with SMI often are treated with sedative psychopharmacological agents that are not licensed for insomnia, this review will include any pharmacological treatment that by the authors of the RCT has been defined as a hypnotic/sleep‐promoting drug, with the sleep‐promoting effects being defined as the primary outcome (Table S4 lists all included drugs, divided into licensed and off‐label).

Definition of Insomnia

Throughout this article, insomnia is used synonymously with insomnia symptoms, that is, poor sleep quality, trouble falling asleep or maintaining sleep. In the included RCTs, insomnia should be diagnosed either according to standardised diagnostic criteria in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM), International Classification of Diseases (ICD) or International Classification of Sleep Disorders (ICSD) system or by the fulfilment of criteria for insomnia in validated questionnaires, such as The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index [19], Insomnia Severity Index [20], Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression [21]—sleep disturbance factor or visual analogue scales.

Literature Search

We performed a systematic literature search on Embase, Medline, PsycINFO, Cochrane Library and clinicaltrials.gov from the inception of the respective databases until August 9, 2024, combining keywords that are covered by the following four facets: (1) pharmacological interventions for insomnia, (2) insomnia, sleep parameters, (3) diagnosis and (4) RCT (Table S5a–c presents a full list of search terms by database). Each word in the facet was combined by the Boolean operator OR. Afterwards, the four facets were combined using the Boolean operator AND. Reference lists of included RCTs and previous systematic reviews were screened manually and Web of Science was used for citation tracking to identify additional relevant studies.

Data Extraction

A team of four reviewers (N.F.B.F., N.M.M., J.E.F. and M.A.S.) performed the data extraction process with each study being screened independently by two reviewers. Data extraction on study characteristics, patient characteristics, treatment characteristics and all outcomes was done using a pre‐defined template (Table S6). Conflicts were settled by O.K.‐F. and N.F.B. We contacted authors in case of missing data and sent a reminder mail in case of no response.

Quality Assessment

The risk of bias in the included RCTs was independently assessed by two reviewers (N.F.B.F., N.M.M., S.L.J., N.L. or M.A.S.) with conflicts being settled by OKF and NFBF. Based on the Revised Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB2) [24], we extracted data regarding quality for five domains, all being categorised into low risk of bias, some concerns or high risk of bias (see Supporting Information Methods in Data S1).

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were performed in Stata 18.0. Forest plots were generated in R 4.3.2 following the handbook by Harrer et al. [25].

Analyses compared those randomised to pharmacological interventions for insomnia to those randomised to placebo, inactive pharmacological control or active pharmacological comparator. Random‐effect meta‐analyses calculated risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for dichotomous outcomes, such as acceptability and mean differences (MD) or standardised mean differences (SMD) with 95% CIs for continuous outcomes, such as change in TST or sleep quality. SMDs were corrected for small‐sample bias, producing the effect size Hedge's g [26].

The degree of heterogeneity observed in the results was quantified using the I 2 statistic, which can be interpreted as the percentage of variation observed between the RCTs attributable to between‐RCT differences in effect sizes rather than sampling error (chance). The risk of publication bias was assessed visually from a funnel plot including all RCTs available for main outcomes. TST and acceptability were tested for asymmetry using Egger's test [27] when there was asymmetry at visual inspection.

Supporting Information Data S1 describes further detail regarding the statistical analyses.

Deviations From the Protocol

See Supporting Information Methods in Data S1. The most important changes were that we changed one inclusion criterion (including studies that did not refer to a standardised definition of insomnia, but used the same criteria) and added one exclusion criterion (patients experiencing a current manic episode). Meta‐regression analyses on ethnicity, study duration and duration of insomnia were not possible as these data were either infrequently reported (ethnicity and duration of insomnia) or did not vary much (study duration). The predefined analysis excluding studies with pharmaceutical industry sponsorship was not possible, as this was only clearly reported in four studies that were scattered between the three subgroups and did not all report sufficient data for meta‐analyses in the co‐primary outcomes (Table 1).

Results

From 3331 hits (Figure 1), after duplicate removal, 3101 hits were screened by title and abstract, of which 136 hits were full‐text screened and 25 RCTs with 2476 patients included. Table S7 presents the specific reasons for exclusion for each excluded RCT.

PRISMA flowchart.

Characterisation of Included RCTs

The 25 RCTs were published between 1986 and 2024 and covered 18 RCTs (N = 2199) in MDD, 4 RCTs (N = 162) in BD and 3 RCTs (N = 115 patients) in schizophrenia (Table 1). The median study sample size was 36 participants (IQR = 57) and the median treatment duration was 6 weeks (range 2–24). Mean age was 40.8 years (SD 5.6) and 1411 patients (59%) were female.

Of the 22 drugs that are approved for the treatment of insomnia, only seven have been tested in RCTs in SMIs, namely zolpidem (RCTs = 3, n = 601), eszopiclone (RCTs = 3, n = 599), melatonin (RCTs = 3, n = 97), ramelteon (RCTs = 1, n = 83), suvorexant (RCTs = 2, n = 54), trimipramine (RCTs = 1, n = 34) and flunitrazepam (RCTs = 1, n = 26) (Table S8).

We found nine trials with sedating antidepressants (all in MDD), two trials with benzodiazepines (both MDD), six trials with Z‐drugs (MDD = 5, schizophrenia = 1), four trials with melatoninergic drugs (BD = 1, MDD = 2, schizophrenia = 1), one trial with a sedating antipsychotic (BD) and one trial with gabapentin (MDD) (Table S8).

The most frequently studied pharmacological interventions for insomnia were Z‐drugs (6 RCTs, 3 on zolpidem and 3 on eszopiclone), melatonin (3 RCTs), agomelatine (3 RCTs) and nefazodone (3 RCTs) (Table 1). Only Z‐drugs, nefazodone and agomelatine were studied in RCTs with more than 100 participants and only in MDD.

Twelve RCTs were placebo‐controlled, while seven had an inactive pharmacological control and six had an active pharmacological comparator (Table 1). Of the 12 placebo‐controlled RCTs, only one study in patients with schizophrenia [52] had a complete wash‐out period before randomisation. In the other RCTs, concomitant medication was allowed, that is, participants remained on their baseline treatment or this was substituted by one open‐label drug, which was the same for all participants. Three [42, 43, 45] of the six RCTs with an active pharmacological comparator and one [49] of the seven RCTs with an inactive control allowed for concomitant medication during the intervention period.

Primary Outcomes

Of the 25 studies included in the systematic review, only 14, 18 and 20 studies provided sufficient data for meta‐analysis in the primary outcomes TST, sleep quality and acceptability, respectively.

Compared to placebo, pharmacological interventions for insomnia were associated with improved TST (RCTs = 10, MD = 30.82 min, 95% CI = 19.13–42.50, I 2 = 84%) and sleep quality (RCTs = 8, g = 0.24 [0.05–0.43], I 2 = 0%) and similar acceptability (RCTs = 10, RR of all‐cause discontinuation = 1.06, 95% CI = 0.90–1.25, I 2 = 0%) (Figure 2).

Compared to inactive pharmacological controls, we found a small effect of pharmacological insomnia treatment on sleep quality (RCTs = 5, g = 0.27, 95% CI = 0.10–0.45, I 2 = 9%) and comparable acceptability (RCTs = 6, all‐cause discontinuation: RR = 0.70, 95% CI = 0.46; 1.06, I 2 = 8%), while only two studies with high heterogeneity were available for TST (Figure 2).

Compared to active pharmacological comparators, we found no difference in sleep quality or acceptability, while only two studies with high heterogeneity were available for TST (Figure 2).

Non‐Reporting and Publication Bias

In four out of 10 RCTs registered at clinicaltrials.gov, we were unable to find the published results during the full text screening. Three of these four RCTs investigated eszopiclone and one investigated suvorexant, suggesting a high risk of non‐reporting bias for eszopiclone (Table S7).

Inspection of funnel plots for the three primary outcomes suggested potential publication bias (Figure S7). The Egger's test indicated no significant evidence of publication bias in neither TST (t = −0.16, p = 0.88) nor acceptability (t = −0.40, p = 0.69).

Sensitivity Analyses

Analyses excluding studies with significant between‐group differences at baseline and studies with imputed data are presented in Figures S8 and S9. Meta‐regression analyses of the three co‐primary outcomes TST, sleep quality and acceptability with the predictors year, sample size, age, gender distribution and baseline sleep duration are presented in Table S9. In summary, only sample size and baseline sleep duration seemed to affect the effect size in TST (sample size estimate 0.05, 95% CI 0.01; 0.10, p = 0.02 and baseline sleep duration estimate −0.24, 95% CI −0.42; −0.06) and sleep quality (sample size estimate −0.001, 95% CI −0.002; 0.0001, p = 0.04 and baseline sleep duration estimate 0.005, 95% CI 0.0002; 0.009, p = 0.04) (Figure S10).

Discussion

This systematic review examined the efficacy, acceptability and safety of any pharmacological intervention for insomnia in individuals with SMI, defined as schizophrenia, BD or MDD. The most important findings were: most licensed sleep‐promoting drugs were tested in no or few RCTs in patients with SMI, although insomnia is much more prevalent in these patients than in the general population [2, 3, 4]. Only Z‐drugs and agomelatine have been studied in several larger RCTs, primarily in MDD, while only three and four RCTs studied pharmacological interventions for insomnia in schizophrenia and BD, respectively. When combining all evidence in SMI, the main efficacy outcomes TST, sleep quality and acceptability were studied in 14, 18 and 20 RCTs, respectively. TST improved by 31 min with a slightly improved sleep quality compared to placebo and inactive pharmacological controls. Acceptability was comparable to all controls.

The total number of individuals included in RCTs in SMI (n = 2476) is almost 20 times lower than the number of healthy individuals with insomnia disorder (n = 47,950) studied in a recent systematic review [18], underscoring the evident gap in this field. The latter study indicated better efficacy compared to placebo for the Z‐drugs eszopiclone, zolpidem and zopiclone and the orexin‐receptor antagonist lemborexant, but poor efficacy for other frequently used drugs, such as melatonin and low‐quality evidence for other drugs, such as antipsychotics and antihistamines. Similarly, in our review, Z‐drugs showed improved TST with comparable acceptability in MDD, without differences versus placebo in one small RCT in schizophrenia. However, for Z‐drugs, the effect size in sleep quality was about half the size observed in mentally healthy individuals and did not differ statistically from placebo.

Orexin‐receptor antagonists have been recommended by the European guideline for treating insomnia [5]. However, this drug class has only been studied in two small RCTs [43, 47] in patients with SMI, both with suvorexant, showing no overall differences compared to either placebo in BD (n = 36) [34] or eszopiclone in MDD (n = 18) [30].

Our results indicate that pharmacological interventions for insomnia in individuals with SMI are efficacious and safe compared to placebo; however, there are only small improvements in sleep quality and TST, with a slightly higher risk for side effects. Efficacy should be interpreted with caution, as many of the included drugs act via sedative effects and cannot be considered insomnia therapy. Specific recommendations are not possible, as meta‐analyses in specific drugs or drug classes were only possible in Z‐drugs and inactive pharmacological controls/active comparators were very diverse. Furthermore, our quality assessment indicated several methodological issues in the included RCTs.

Hence, although insomnia is frequent in SMI and has a negative impact on quality of life and comorbid psychiatric symptoms, the scientific basis for pharmacological treatment is poor. The lack of high‐quality studies in this area may reflect the extra expenses associated with off‐patent drugs.

Although the evidence on head‐to‐head comparisons included very different comparison groups, making our pooled estimates difficult to interpret, the present results may still be useful for specific clinical situations guiding specific drugs.

Strengths and Limitations

Strengths include the comprehensive transdiagnostic approach to any pharmacological intervention for insomnia and the PRISMA‐conform methodology based on a pre‐defined protocol including a detailed quality assessment.

Limitations include those of the included RCTs, that is, primarily few, small RCTs with high heterogeneity, limiting robust estimates and head‐to‐head comparisons. Second, many RCTs did not report several important outcome measures (e.g., TST, insomnia scores, tolerability or absolute numbers). Accordingly, we were neither able to study potential moderators of efficacy nor calculate important aspects such as number needed to treat or number needed to harm. Third, some RCTs compared two antidepressants with insomnia as a co‐primary endpoint in addition to depressive symptoms, resulting in some medications that have insomnia as a potential side effect (e.g., venlafaxine, escitalopram, fluoxetine) being considered inactive pharmacological controls, potentially inflating effect sizes in efficacy. Fourth, only three RCTs studied the effect for > 8 weeks, which is why our results represent only acute treatment effects. Fifth, as we only included published studies, reporting/publication bias could bias our findings towards higher effect sizes.

Conclusion

This comprehensive systematic review showed that most pharmacological interventions for insomnia have not been tested or only in a few small RCTs in patients with schizophrenia, BD or MDD. Overall, both licensed and off‐label pharmacological interventions for insomnia in SMI showed small improvements in TST and sleep quality with similar acceptability, but we are not able to guide with recommendations regarding specific pharmacological interventions due to the limited evidence. The poor scientific basis warrants consideration when clinicians prescribe sleep medications. Large, high‐quality RCTs evaluating the efficacy and safety of pharmacological interventions for insomnia in patients with SMI are needed.
