Prevalence of modic changes in patients with low back pain and association with degenerative spinal findings: a retrospective MRI study
Authors: Helia Ahmadifard, Niloufar Khatibzade-Nasari, Roya Baghestani-Koozehgar, Farshid Farzaneh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundModic changes (MCs) are alterations in vertebral endplates visible on MRI and associated with various spinal pathologies, including disc degeneration, herniation, and spondylolisthesis. Despite their clinical significance, the associations between specific MC types and spinal pathologies require further investigation. This study aimed to investigate the prevalence and associations of MC types with demographic data and radiological findings in an Iranian population.MethodsThis retrospective cross-sectional study analyzed lumbar MRI scans from patients with low back pain. Patients with radiologically confirmed MCs met the inclusion criteria after excluding those with tumors, infections, fractures, or prior lumbar surgery. MCs were categorized into three types according to MRI signal characteristics. Associations between MC types and age, gender, disc degeneration, herniation, and spondylolisthesis were evaluated using statistical analysis.ResultsAmong 1350 patients evaluated, MCs were identified in 316 cases (23.4%), with Type 2 being the most prevalent (88.9%), followed by Type 1 (9.8%) and Type 3 (1.3%). MCs occurred most frequently at L4/L5 and L5/S1 levels. Type 1 MCs were significantly associated with younger age and disc herniation (p ≤ 0.05), while Type 2 MCs correlated with older age and moderate to severe disc degeneration (p ≤ 0.05). However, these associations did not remain statically significant after Bonferroni correction. Type 3 MCs were rare and predominantly observed in older patients. No significant gender differences in MC prevalence were observed, nor were there associations between any MC type and spondylolisthesis (P > 0.05).ConclusionsOur findings suggest that Modic changes, particularly Type 2, are common radiological findings in lumbar spine imaging, most frequently occurring at L4/L5 and L5/S1 levels. Although initial associations with disc degeneration and herniation were observed, these did not remain significant after correction for multiple comparisons. Nonetheless, the distribution and age-related patterns of Modic changes suggest their potential relevance in degenerative spinal conditions. Future longitudinal studies should investigate the clinical implications and progression of Modic changes to inform more targeted management strategies for degenerative spinal disorders.Keywords: Modic changes, Lumbar disc degeneration, Disc herniation, Spondylolisthesis, Low back pain

Introduction

The endplates are located between the intervertebral disc and vertebral bone and are composed of thin layers comprising cartilage and bone components. These structures play a crucial role in maintaining the health of the intervertebral discs and the spine by distributing the loads applied to the lumbar spine during daily activities to maintain posture [1]. The development of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has helped identify endplate changes, and Modic et al. were the first to describe the changes in the signal intensity of the lumbar vertebral endplates and bone marrow using MRI [2, 3]. Modic changes (MCs) are categorized into three types according to their pathological alterations and signal intensity changes in MRI. Type 1 MCs show bone marrow edema and infiltration, with low T1 and high T3 signal intensity on MRI, while Type 2 MCs exhibit fatty infiltration, with high T1 and T2 signal intensity on MRI, and Type 3 MCs display subchondral bone sclerosis, with low T1 and T2 signal intensity on MRI [4–6]. It should be noted that there are mixed types of MCs, and Modic type 1 has the potential to develop into Modic type 2 and 3 or resolve [7, 8]. Although the etiology of MCs is not entirely known, previous studies have put forward various theories concerning the etiology of MCs, including disc degeneration, abnormal gene fragments, trauma, infection, and inflammation [3, 7]. Previous studies have found a significant correlation between MCs and radiological findings such as disc degeneration, spondylolisthesis, and herniation [5, 9, 10]. Lumbar disc degeneration is classified in MRI studies based on tears in the annulus, herniated nucleus, and the height of the intervertebral disc [6]. Some studies use Pfirrmann’s method to describe disc degeneration grading and correlate the results with MCs, indicating a significant association between severe lumbar disc degeneration and MCs [2, 6, 11]. Wei and Wu observed that MCs are linked to lumbar disc herniation, spondylolisthesis, and spinal stenosis [5]. Another study by Määttä et al. found an association between MCs and disc displacement, Schmorl’s node, and disc degeneration [9]. Li et al. also identified a significant correlation between MCs and intervertebral disc height, disc angle, spondylolisthesis, and intervertebral disc degeneration on MRI [12].

While previous studies have explored the correlation between MCs and spinal abnormalities such as disc degeneration, spondylolisthesis, and herniation, there is a limited detailed analysis of how each specific type of MC (Type 1, Type 2, and Type 3) correlates with these radiological findings [2, 13]. This study aims to investigate the association between each type of MC and patient demographics (age and sex) as well as radiological features, including disc degeneration, herniation, and spondylolisthesis, within the Iranian population. This approach will enhance our understanding of the clinical and radiological implications associated with each type of MC.

Method

Study population and design

A retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted from September to November 2023, analyzing lumbar MRI scans from 1350 patients with lower back pain who visited the outpatient clinic of Khalij-Fars Hospital in Bandar Abbas, Iran. All available patients within the specified study period were included, constituting a complete census of the target population during that time. Therefore, no formal sample size calculation or power analysis was performed. The inclusion criterion was radiologically confirmed Modic changes. Patients with lumbar tumors, infections, or fractures on MRI, as well as those with previous lumbar surgery, were excluded from the study.

All Participants gave written informed consent, and the Ethics Committee of Qeshm University of Medical Sciences approved the study (IR.IAU.BA.REC.1402.015).

MRI evaluation

MRI images, retrospectively collected by one of our researchers. A single experienced radiologist, who was blinded to clinical data, evaluated the images according to standard procedures, with a focus on the L1 to S1 segments. All the images were obtained on a Philips Ingenia 1.5T scanner. The patients were placed in a supine position, and the following scans were performed: (1) Sagittal T1 Turbo Spin Echo (TSE) imaging from T12 to the sacrum (time to recovery: 600 ms; time to echo: 8 ms, slice thickness: 5 mm). (2) Sagittal T2 TSE imaging from T12 to the sacrum (time to recovery: 2700 ms; time to echo: 100 ms, slice thickness: 5 mm). (3) Axial T2 TSE imaging from L1 to S1 (time to recovery: 2800 ms; time to echo: 100 ms, slice thickness: 4 mm).

As different types of MCs could be determined at the same lumbar segment, the predominant type of MC at each level was identified as the most extended one for that specific level [14, 15]. For descriptive purposes, all observed MCs across spinal levels were reported. However, for patient-level analyses, the most clinically significant MC type was assigned per patient to ensure statistical independence.

Disc degeneration evaluation

We utilize the Pfirrmann grading system on the T2-weighted sagittal lumbar spine to evaluate disc degeneration [16]. As MCs were generally identified in the lumbar segments with degenerative disc disease grades greater than 3, this study focused on clinically significant degenerative disc disease, including grades 3 to 5 of the Pfirrmann criteria (moderate to severe stages), to maintain a comparable baseline for both groups.

Disc herniation evaluation

A disc herniation is a condition that may occur anywhere in the spine, but it is most commonly found in the lower back. It is one of the leading causes of discomfort in the lower back and can also trigger pain in the legs. The evaluation of disc herniation in this study was conducted following the standards established by the American Association of Orthopedic Surgeons, considering all forms of herniated discs, such as protruded, extruded, and sequestrated [17].

Spondylolisthesis evaluation

Degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis is the anterior displacement of a vertebra over the one beneath it, resulting from degenerative changes, and occurs without any related damage or defect in the vertebral ring structure [18]. To assess lumbar spondylolisthesis, we evaluated MRI findings that indicated facet joint effusion larger than 1.5 mm [19].

Statical analysis

The collected data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 24 software. All qualitative variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, while Continuous quantitative variables were presented as descriptive statistics (means and standard deviations). The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. For continuous variables not normally distributed, comparisons between two groups were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney test. To control for the risk of Type 1 error in multiple comparisons, the Bonferroni correction was applied where appropriate. The comparison between qualitative variables was also analyzed using the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests, as applicable. In this study, a P-value of ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For key findings, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were also reported to provide an estimate of the precision of the results.

Results

Patient demographics and prevalence of modic changes

Of the 1350 screened patients with low back pain, 316 (23.4%) met the inclusion criteria by demonstrating Modic changes (MCs). Based on Table 1, the cohort had a mean age of 52.98 years (SD = 11.51) with female predominance (61.1%). Among the types of MCs, Type 2 was the most prevalent, affecting 281 of the patients (88.9%), followed by Type 1 in 31 patients (9.8%), and Type 3 in only 4 patients (1.3%). Radiological findings revealed that disc herniation was the most common structural abnormality, present in 274 patients (86.7%), while disc degeneration was observed in 198 patients (62.7%). Spondylolisthesis was less frequent, affecting 31 patients (9.8%).

Discussion

Modic changes refer to bone marrow and end plate defects visible in MRI scans, first identified in the 1980 s with a prevalence of 22–50% in patients with degenerative involvement of intervertebral discs [20]. An evidence-based narrative review by Viswanathan et al. reported that end plate changes are closely linked to back pain. They’re common in patients reporting back pain (8-80.1%) and even present in asymptomatic individuals, with a prevalence of 0.5–47.1% [21].

Previous studies have reported inconsistent findings regarding the impact of MCs on clinical outcomes and the treatment of low back pain [22–24]. Limited and heterogeneous data are available for a definitive conclusion on the clinical significance of MCs in predicting treatment outcomes. However, a recent systematic review assessing the impact of MCs on the postoperative outcomes in lumbar spine surgery stated that their presence was negatively associated with recovery in patients undergoing discectomy, whereas a positive association was observed in those who underwent total disc replacement (TDR) [23]. In another study, MC Type 1 was linked to a slower rate of recovery following microdiscectomy, although the overall improvement remained statistically significant [25]. These findings suggest that identifying the specific type of MCs, along with their coexistence with other radiological findings, may play a valuable role in predicting the prognosis and guiding individualized treatment strategies for patients with low back pain.

Although we assessed associations between MCs and radiological findings, as well as demographic factors such as age and sex, these relationships may have been influenced by unmeasured confounding variables, including BMI, occupational history, smoking status, physical activity, or comorbid conditions, which were not controlled for in this study. Previous studies have reported varying results regarding the role of such factors. As noted earlier in the study by Sørlie et al. MC type 1 has been associated with less but still significant improvement in back pain after surgery; however, this association lost statistical significance after adjusting for smoking, suggesting a possible additive effect between smoking and MC type 1 [25]. In contrast, Saukkonen et al. found that higher BMI was significantly associated with the presence of MCs on MRI, while smoking history was not [26]. Wu et al. also reported that MCs were associated in univariate analyses with higher BMI, greater smoking, regular exercise, the absence of daily vehicle vibration, and occupational physical load; however, these associations lost statistical significance in multivariate regression analysis, suggesting that they may be confounded by other factors [27]. These discrepancies emphasize the interplay between lifestyle, metabolic, and environmental factors in the pathogenesis and clinical impact of MCs.

The current study focused on the phenotypic features of MCs, identifying their association with patients’ demographic data and MRI phenotypes, such as disc degeneration, spondylolisthesis, and herniation.

Modic changes and sex

This study aligns with previous reports indicating a higher prevalence of Modic changes in women compared to men, although no statistically significant sex difference was observed [28, 29]. On the other hand, a recent review of MCs revealed that most studies have found them to be more prevalent among male patients [21]. Lan et al. addressed this discrepancy by suggesting that men are more likely to engage in moderate to intense physical activities, which expose them to greater repetitive stress loads [3].

In contrast, in many cultures, including the Iranian population, women traditionally bear a significant portion of household responsibilities. This is particularly concerning because housework often involves biomechanically demanding tasks, such as repetitive movements, lifting heavy objects, and maintaining awkward body postures. These factors are known to contribute to an increased risk of musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) among women [30]. Supporting this, previous studies have found that more than half of Iranian housewives experience MSDs [31, 32]. This disparity in physical strain may explain the observed higher prevalence of MCs in women compared to men in our study.

Furthermore, our findings revealed distinct patterns in the types of MCs: women exhibited a higher incidence of Modic Type 2, while men showed a greater frequency of Modic Type 1. However, these differences were not statistically significant. Despite this, the observed trends align with a study by Chen et al., which reported similar gender-based differences in Modic change patterns [13].

Modic changes and age

Aging is a systemic process that profoundly impacts spinal structures, contributing to intervertebral disc degeneration and the development of MCs [33]. Age-related factors such as mechanical stress, osteoporosis, endplate thinning, and vertebral sclerosis disrupt spinal biomechanics, impair nutrient transport, and promote oxidative damage and chronic inflammation. These processes accelerate cellular senescence, reduce tissue repair, and, combined with altered load distribution, increase the risk of MCs [33, 34]. Our Assessment of the correlation between the patient’s age and MCs revealed a significant association with MC types 1 and 2, whereas no significant correlation was observed with MC type 3. This lack of significance could be attributed to the low number of participants with Modic Type 3 involvement in our study sample. Consistent with our findings, previous studies have also indicated that MCs are an age-related phenomenon, suggesting that the likelihood of MCs increases with age [13, 20, 29, 35]. However, these studies did not evaluate the association between age and each type of MC separately.

In line with our results, Wang et al. assessed the relationship between each type of MCs and age, reporting a significant association between specific type of MCs (Type 1 and Type 2) and patient age [36]. Additionally, Mostafavi et al. found that an increase in the variety of MCs was significantly associated with an increase in age [28]. Similarly, in our study, the mean age of the studied groups was the lowest in Modic Type 1 and the highest in Modic Types 3 and 2.

Modic changes and degenerative disc changes

Our study assessed the correlation between disc degeneration and different types of MCs. Although initial analysis suggested a statistically significant association (particularly with Modic Type 2), this correlation did not remain significant after applying Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. This highlights the need for cautious interpretation of such associations when multiple hypotheses are tested.

Recent studies have suggested a strong correlation between the presence of Modic changes and the severity of disc degeneration, indicating that patients with MCs may experience greater levels of disc degeneration compared to those without [6, 9, 10, 13, 35].

The assessment of disc degeneration conducted by Chen et al. relied on the same Pfirman criteria that we incorporated into our analysis. Their study reported a statistically significant correlation between all types of disc degeneration and Types 1 and 2 of Modic changes, which aligns with our unadjusted findings [13]. Teichtahl et al. also identified similar associations in the L2 to L5 regions between disc degeneration and Type 2 Modic changes [6]. However, neither study applied Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons.

Another study by Määttä et al. revealed that disc degeneration was significantly associated with the presence of any Modic changes. However, when comparing Type 1 and Type 2 of Modic changes separately with disc degeneration, no significant differences were observed between the groups. This finding is consistent with our results after applying Bonferroni correction, which also did not show a significant association between individuals’ Modic subtypes and disc degeneration [9].

Additionally, Emel Ece Ozcan-Eksi et al. revealed that severe intervertebral disc degeneration was significantly more prevalent in patients with Type 1 compared to individuals with other types of MCs [11]. This discrepancy could be attributed to a different category of disc generation considered as a baseline for comparing the correlation between disc degeneration and Modic changes in our study.

Modic changes and disc herniation

Our findings indicated that more than half of the participants with Modic changes had accompanying disc herniation, including instances of protrusion, extrusion, and sequestration. Although an initial analysis suggested a statistically significant association between Modic Type 1 and disc herniation, this significance did not persist after applying Bonferroni correction.

Previous studies have supported a potential link between Modic changes and disc herniation. Arana et al. indicated a significant correlation between the presence of MCs 1+ 1/2 and disc herniation [10]. Similarly, Wei and Wu also stated that protrusion, extrusion, and sequestration of the disc were significantly correlated with MCs, although their study did not specify the association between each specific type of MC [5]. These inconsistencies may be attributed to the differences in sample size or genetic variations among the study populations.

Our results are in Line with the findings of Chen et al., who reported no statistically significant association between MCs Types 1 and 2 and disc herniation [13].

Modic changes and spondylolisthesis

Our research revealed no significant correlation between any type of MCs and spondylolisthesis. A similar conclusion was reached by Mok et al., who indicated that the presence of spondylolisthesis did not show a significant correlation with MCs. However, their study compared all forms of MCs as a single category in relation to spondylolisthesis [37].

Arana et al. also assessed the correlation between each type of MC and spondylolisthesis [10]. They found that the occurrence of spondylolisthesis was notably Linked to the presence of MCs 1+ 1/2, while no significant association was found with MCs 2/3 + 3. This discrepancy may be due to the differences in the categorization of MCs used in their study compared to ours.

Although our results did not confirm a significant association, Lie et al. reported a notable correlation between MCs and spondylolisthesis [12]. They also noted that the occurrence of MCs is related to the type and grade of spondylolisthesis. The observed inconsistency with our findings might be explained by the fact that their study population consisted exclusively of patients with spondylolisthesis, whereas our study involved patients with MCs arising from various etiologies beyond spondylolisthesis. Their study proposed that vertebral slippage, particularly in high-grade or isthmic spondylolisthesis, can lead to abnormal stress distribution across the vertebral endplates, contributing to microdamage, endplate disruption, and subsequent Modic changes. Their findings support the concept that localized instability, loss of disc height, and altered sagittal mechanics may play a role in the development of Modic changes.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes the establishment of causal relationships. Second, the retrospective nature of the analysis may introduce biases, and a prospective study examining MRI outcomes over time would provide more robust findings. Another limitation related to the retrospective design is the inability to conduct patient follow-up to assess MRI findings longitudinally. Additionally, as all available samples within the study period were included, no formal power analysis was performed, which may affect the generalizability of the results. Lastly. While this paper did not focus on regression modeling, future research in this field could systematically evaluate various regression techniques using available data.

Conclusion

This study provides valuable insights into the prevalence, segmental distribution, and association of Modic changes with demographic factors and degenerative spinal conditions. Our findings reveal that Modic Type 2 is the most prevalent subtype, with a higher occurrence in females, although no statistically significant sex differences were observed. Age was significantly associated with Modic Types 1 and 2, with the mean age increasing across Modic subtypes. Although preliminary analyses indicated a potential correlation between Modic changes (especially Modic Type 1) and both disc degeneration and disc herniation, these associations did not remain significant after applying the Bonferroni correction. No significant association was detected between Modic changes and spondylolisthesis.

Despite the lack of statistically robust associations after correction, Modic changes represent relevant radiological features in degenerative spinal disorders. Future studies should focus on exploring the longitudinal progression of Modic changes and their predictive value in clinical outcomes. Investigating the molecular and biomechanical mechanisms underlying Modic changes may also enhance our understanding of their pathophysiology and aid in developing targeted therapeutic approaches. A more comprehensive approach integrating imaging, clinical assessment, and biochemical markers could improve the diagnosis and management of spinal degeneration.
