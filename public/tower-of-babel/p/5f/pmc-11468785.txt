Unusual Triple-State Switching of Thermally Induced Birefringence in a Two-Dimensional Perovskite Ferroelectric
Authors: Yu Ma, Beibei Wang, Wenjing Li, Yi Liu, Wuqian Guo, Haojie Xu, Liwei Tang, Qingshun Fan, Junhua Luo, Zhihua Sun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Birefringent crystals hold a significant position in optical and optoelectronic fields due to their capability to control polarized light. Despite various chemical strategies devoted to designing birefringent crystals, it remains a challenge to switch and manipulate birefringence under physical stimuli. Here we present an unusual triple-state switching of birefringence in a 2D perovskite ferroelectric, (N-methylcyclohexylammonium)2PbCl4 (1), which exhibits two reversible phase transitions at 361 and 373 K. The in-plane birefringence of 1 (Δnbc) shows three distinctive states inside the bc plane, namely, the low-, high-, and zero-Δnbc states. Strikingly, a huge augmentation of Δnbc is solidly confirmed up to ∼400% between its low and high states, far beyond other birefringent materials. The origin of this triple-state switching of birefringence involves the variation of the ferroelastic strain and domain in the vicinity of the phase transition. As an entirely new mode of switching birefringence, this work facilitates the further development of new intelligent nonlinear optics

Birefringence is an optical phenomenon that involves two different refractive indices for light with different polarizations. Birefringent crystals have a wide range of optical applications due to their ability to manipulate and control polarized light, including biomedical imaging, optical communications, and optical modulators.1−5 In the past several decades, the modulation of crystal birefringence has primarily been achieved through the incorporation of functional building blocks via chemical strategies.6−8 For instance, the commercial birefringent crystal α-BaB2O4 achieves large birefringence by the introduction of planar π-conjugated moieties (B3O6)3– with a large polarizability anisotropy.9 Birefringence can also be manipulated by introducing metal cations with stereochemically active lone pairs, such as Sn2PO4I.10 Particularly, another effective approach for switching crystal birefringence is to regulate the polarization-related properties of ferroelectric materials, in view of the close relationship between polarization and optical birefringence.11,12 This distinctive characteristic unveils an effective pathway for controlling and switching birefringence in optical applications, as exemplified by the inorganic ferroelectric BaTiO3.13 However, investigations into birefringence of ferroelectrics are currently confined to a limited selection of inorganic oxides, while the switching or even augmentation of birefringence under external stimuli remains a huge challenge. Therefore, the development of new ferroelectrics with controllable and switchable birefringence is urgent and essential for expanding new optical applications.

Conceptually, ferroelectrics have remarkable temperature-dependent characteristics that endow dramatic changes of physical properties with respect to temperature variation.14,15 Currently, two-dimensional (2D) perovskite ferroelectrics, which are capable of phase transitions triggered by external stimuli, have shown promising prospects for versatile applications in stimulus-responsive devices.16−19 Due to their distinctive physical attributes, including high absorption coefficient, long carrier mobility, and natural quantum-well structures, they have been widely used in various optoelectronic devices.20−23 Structurally, the rigid inorganic framework affords exceptional optical performance, while the flexible organic component imparts the driving force for symmetry-breaking phase transitions. The synergistic effect of organic and inorganic components induces the ferroelectricity in 2D hybrid perovskites.24−26 To emphasize, the combination of ferroelectric properties and optical characteristics would render the switching of birefringence. For instance, the birefringence in the ferroelectric (isoamylamine)2(methylamine)2Pb3Cl10 exhibits a conspicuous dependence on the spontaneous polarization, which is closely related to the symmetry breaking during phase transition;27 the birefringence of this 2D ferroelectric family can even be alternated and controlled through an external optical stimulus.28 Despite a few studies on the switching of birefringence under external stimuli (particularly thermal stimulus), the results focus on the birefringence variation from a high state to a low state.29 In this context, we endeavor to switch and manipulate birefringence of 2D perovskite ferroelectric materials, which will facilitate further advances in intelligent stimulus-responsive devices.

Herein we report an unusual triple-state switching of birefringence in a 2D perovskite ferroelectric, (NMCA)2PbCl4 (1) (NMCA = N-methylcyclohexylammonium), which possesses a spontaneous polarization (Ps) value of 2.4 μC/cm2 and exhibits two reversible phase transitions at 361 and 373 K. The in-plane birefringence (Δnbc) of 1 shows three distinctive states in the bc plane, namely, the low-, high-, and zero-Δnbc states. Notably, a huge augmentation of Δnbc is solidly confirmed up to ∼400% between its low and high states. The origin of this triple-state switching of birefringence involves the variation of the ferroelastic strain and domain in the vicinity of the phase transition. As an entirely new mode of switching birefringence, this work facilitates the further development of intelligent ferroelectric material devices.

A block single crystal of 1 was obtained through the temperature cooling crystallization method (Figure S1). The powder X-ray diffraction pattern confirmed the high purity of the as-grown crystal, in accordance with the simulated result (Figure S2). To investigate phase transition behaviors of 1, variable-temperature structures were determined by using single crystal X-ray diffraction. At 250 K, 1 crystallizes in the orthorhombic crystal system with the space group Pmn21. Its basic structure consists of infinitely extended sheets of corner-sharing PbCl6 octahedra and ordered bilayers of organic NMCA+ cations, forming a typical 2D Ruddlesden–Popper monolayer perovskite architecture (Figure 1a). Notably, the Pb–Cl bonds are distorted and interconnected within the bc plane, as verified by the distorted bond angles and uneven bond lengths (Tables S2 and S3). The ordered organic cations are arranged parallel to the crystallographic c axis. This synergistic interaction of organic and inorganic components is advantageous for the generation of electric polarization. As the temperature rises to 363 K, the crystal structure of 1 undergoes a transition to the nonpolar space group Pmmn (Tables S4 and S5). The organic cations become disordered and arrange in an antiparallel mode, thus eliminating the net polarization of 1 (Figure 1b). Upon further heating to 380 K, 1 crystallizes in the centrosymmetric space group I4/mmm (Table S6 and S7). The organic cations become highly disordered, and all of the C and N atoms locate on crystallographic mirror planes (Figure 1c). From a structural point of view, the order–disorder transition of this organic cation and the distortion of the octahedra play a huge role in inducing ferroelectricity. Therefore, symmetry breaking occurs in 1 with the Aizu notation of 4/mmmFmm2 for the emergence of ferroelectric polarization.30

Temperature-induced symmetry breaking is an essential characteristic of ferroelectric materials.31 We investigated the reversible phase transition characteristics of 1 through differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) measurements. In the DSC trace, two thermal anomalies at 361 K (T1) and 373 K (T2) are observed upon heating, providing evidence for the existence of two phase transitions (Figure 1d). The specific capacity curve of 1 further confirms its phase transitions, as verified by the drastic thermal variations at 361 and 373 K (Figure S3). Moreover, the variable-temperature dielectric measurements also display two dielectric anomalies, consistent with the DSC results (Figure 1e). As shown in Figure 1f, the ferroelectricity of 1 is confirmed by the polarization–electric field (P–E) hysteresis loops at different temperatures. The experimental values of Ps and Ec are measured as 2.4 μC/cm2 and 12.0 kV/cm at 340 K, respectively. This Ec value is comparable to those of some other molecular ferroelectrics, such as (allyammonium)2(ethylammonium)2Pb3Br10 (13 kV/cm), [(CH3)2CHCH2NH3]2PbCl4 (15 kV/cm), and (EATMP)PbBr4 (7.3 kV/cm).32−34 Similar P–E hysteresis loops can be obtained in both the b and c directions, which also coincides with our analysis of symmetry breaking (Figure S4).

Light absorption characteristics are the fundamental properties of an optical material that determine the practical range of optical applications. The ultraviolet–visible absorption spectrum of 1 reveals an absorption cutoff edge at 374 nm (Figure S5), giving a calculated bandgap value of 3.31 eV (Figure S6a). This wide bandgap suggests the low optical absorption of 1 in the visible-light range, which is important for the application of birefringent materials.35 Further density functional theory calculations demonstrate that the optical properties originate from the Pb s/p and Cl p states within the inorganic framework (Figure S6b), which is consistent with a series of other lead-based perovskites.36,37 Despite the thriving development of ferroelectricity in this 2D family, tuning of its optical birefringence has not been thoroughly investigated.

For 1, the most fascinating property is the triple-state switching of its thermally induced birefringence, as shown in Figure 2. It is known that birefringence is an inherent characteristic of optical crystals,38,39 and here we employed a polarizing microscope to investigate the birefringence switching of crystal 1 at the wavelength of 550 nm (Figure S7). Figure 2a illustrates the direct observation of birefringence color changes for 1 at different temperatures. The optical path difference and thickness of crystal 1 were determined as 0.023 and 7.66 μm (Figure S8), respectively. Consequently, the in-plane birefringence of 1 on the (100) crystal face can be calculated as Δnbc = 0.003 (i.e., the low state). This result coincides with the calculated refractive indices along the principal axes, of which the estimated Δnbc value is found to be 0.006 (Figure 2b). It is worth noting that the single-crystal image under polarized light becomes gradually darkened with the rising of temperature (at 350 K). When the temperature exceeds the first phase transition point of 361 K, the brightness of the single crystal image suddenly increases, indicating a significant birefringence augmentation at this stage. The in-plane birefringence Δnbc was measured as 0.015 (i.e., the high state), which is almost 400% augmentation compared to the low state (Figure 2c and Table S8). As the temperature increases beyond 373 K, the single-crystal image becomes completely dark, signifying a complete extinction of the crystal without any in-plane birefringence (Δnbc = 0, i.e., the zero state). During this heating process, the growth variation of cross-stripe domain patterns can be clearly observed, in agreement with the phase transition behaviors. As far as we know, this is the first study of the triple-state switching of thermally induced birefringence, along with the enhancement of birefringence in ferroelectric materials. The opposite phenomenon can also be observed during cooling, indicating that the triple-state birefringence switching of 1 is a completely reversible process. The thermogravimetric results confirm that 1 exhibits high thermal stability up to ∼520 K, which ensures the stability of the sample during the measurement process (Figure S9). This unprecedented triple-state birefringence switching might open up a new way for the rational control and manipulation of optical birefringence.

Our further studies elucidated that this triple-state switching of birefringence closely involves the successive phase transitions in 1. The in-plane birefringence Δnbc of crystal 1 stems from the different refractive indices along its b and c crystallographic directions. In the room-temperature phase, the inorganic frameworks of Pb–Cl octahedra are twisted and extended along two directions, showing extremely minor structure anisotropy. The birefringence alteration during the heating process is primarily induced by the temperature-dependent variation of Ps in the ferroelectric phase (below 361 K). There is little change in the cross-stripe domains at this stage. However, when the temperature exceeds 361 K, the cross-stripe domains grow dramatically and become more dense (Figure 3a). At this stage, the variation in the ferroelastic strain and domain results in a rapid augmentation of birefringence,40 corresponding to the high state of birefringence. As the temperature continues to approach the second phase transition, the highly symmetric configuration in the tetragonal system with space group I4/mmm leads to the entire vanishing of birefringence, namely, the complete extinction at the zero state of birefringence. It is proposed that the thermally induced variations in the conical light interference patterns corroborate this distinct triple-state switching of birefringence. In detail, crystal 1 exhibits obvious biaxial crystal patterns at low-state of birefringence; when the temperature rises to 370 K, the biaxial pattern can still be observed, but there is an obvious compaction of interference fringes in the pattern, which further confirms the sharp increase in birefringence.41 Eventually, at 380 K, crystal 1 exhibits a standard uniaxial crystal pattern (Figure 3b), corresponding to its zero state of birefringence.

In summary, we have reported an unprecedented triple-state switching of birefringence in the 2D perovskite ferroelectric (NMCA)2PbCl4, which exhibits two consecutive reversible phase transitions at 361 and 373 K. It is noteworthy that during heat stimulation, the in-plane birefringence (Δnbc) of 1 shows distinctive triple-state switching, resulting in the low-, high-, and zero-Δnbc states, respectively. Strikingly, the birefringence of 1 between its low and high states is increased by ∼400%, as solidly confirmed by polarized light microscopy. Further investigation demonstrated that the triple-state switching of birefringence in 1 results from the variation of the ferroelastic strain and domain in the vicinity of the phase transition. These results provide insights into the application of ferroelectric materials toward stimulus-responsive nonlinear optics.
