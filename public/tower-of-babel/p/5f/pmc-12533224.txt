Impact performance of rhombic honeycomb structures with non-uniform wall thickness
Authors: Du Mingchao, Yang Shibin, Zhang Kun, Wang Zixiao, Gao Zhongxiang, Ma Jian, Tong Xin, Yi Changjiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A novel non-uniform wall thickness rhombic (NTR) honeycomb structure was developed, with a particular emphasis on its energy absorption characteristics. In contrast to conventional uniform wall thickness configurations, the NTR honeycomb structure is characterized by a regular geometric (stiffness) distribution of unit cells throughout the entire panel. Nylon was employed as the primary energy-absorbing material in the structure. A combined approach of numerical simulation and experimental testing was utilized to systematically investigate the dynamic response and energy absorption mechanisms of the NTR honeycomb structure under various loading conditions. The dynamic response of the nylon-based honeycomb structure under impact loading was investigated using ABAQUS simulation software, and the numerical results were validated against experimental data. Additionally, a comparative analysis was performed between the reaction forces at the base of the nylon-based NTR honeycomb structure and those of a rigid body under varying impact velocities. The time at which the structure reached its maximum compression, as well as the evolution of kinetic energy at different impact speeds, was also investigated. With increasing velocity of the impact plate, the occurrence time of the maximum compression in the nylon-based structure was progressively advanced. The higher the velocity of the impact plate, the earlier the maximum compression of the nylon material occurs. Specifically, at a velocity of 15 m/s, the time of occurrence is 106% earlier than that at 7 m/s. In contrast to rigid bodies, the nylon material exhibits a significant delay in the occurrence time of the peak reaction force on the bottom plate, accompanied by a Substantial reduction in the peak magnitude. When the impact plate velocity is 7 m/s, the time to peak reaction force for the nylon material is 31% delayed and the peak value is 60% lower compared to the rigid body. As the impact velocity increases, the initial kinetic energy of the impact plate decreases while the internal energy increases, with both eventually converging to stable values. This trend confirms the energy absorbing capability of the structure.

Introduction

Driven by continuous technological innovation and increasing energy demand, the deployment of intelligent mining equipment in coal extraction has expanded significantly, contributing to the enhanced productivity and improved efficiency of coal resource utilization1–4. However, underground coal mining operations are characterized by complex and dynamic environments, posing multiple safety challenges such as geological instability, equipment collision risks, and rock burst hazards5,6. As a fundamental component of the underground safety system, energy-absorbing anti-collision devices reduce accident impacts through controlled energy dissipation, and have become a key research focus in the advancement of intelligent mining technologies. In recent years, progress in materials science, intelligent monitoring technologies, and structural design has facilitated the transition of energy-absorbing anti-collision devices from passive protection to active warning, adaptive response, and reusability. This evolution has substantially improved the integrated safety of personnel and equipment in underground mining environments.

Traditional anti-collision devices predominantly rely on plastic deformation energy absorption of metallic structures (e.g., anti-collision beams and energy-absorbing boxes), yet suffer from inherent limitations such as substantial space occupation and irreversibility after single use. Wang Binlin7 et al. investigated an expandable energy-absorbing device that enhances stability through ‘expansion-crushing’ coupling; however, the metallic material exhibits non-recoverability after single impact, rendering it unsuitable for frequent underground impacts. Ju Jinlong8 et al. employed the multi-material arbitrary Lagrangian-Eulerian (ALE) algorithm to simulate fluid-structure interaction in negative Poisson’s ratio honeycomb structures under impact, demonstrating lightweight design via ‘auxetic’ effects, though without addressing coal mine explosion-proof requirements. Gao Yongxin9 et al. redesigned the latch-type structure of mining hydraulic supports to a hoop-type configuration through structural analysis and similarity calculations, reducing spring latch space occupation and enhancing column impact resistance. Notwithstanding, the trade-off between ‘high load-bearing capacity’ and ‘low peak force’ remains unaddressed, prone to causing secondary damage under rock burst conditions. Against this research backdrop, this study utilizes an elastoplastic polymeric nylon material to achieve multi-impact reusability via a staged response of ‘elastic deformation-controllable plastic deformation’, while the non-uniform wall thickness design mitigates stress concentration to prevent permanent deformation from rigid impacts.

Yu Zhenglei10 et al. developed a novel Ni-Ti bionic structure, designing three-dimensional negative Poisson’s ratio load-bearing configurations (concave honeycomb [CH] and peanut-shell-inspired [PEI]) based on conventional concave cells, which improve buffering, energy absorption, and self-recovery. However, additive manufacturing incurs high costs, and nitinol corrodes readily in humid underground environments. The modified nylon herein meets underground explosion-proof/fire-resistant standards at 1/5 the cost of nitinol. The rhombic honeycomb achieves stable energy absorption through regular geometric patterning, obviating complex bionic topology, aligning with underground demands for low-cost, high-reliability solutions. Li Chengbing11 et al. proposed random cell placement and material property identification algorithms for real-structured porous aluminum, analyzing impacts of velocity, cell distribution, and wall thickness on closed-cell energy absorption, and validating mechanical properties under quasi-static/dynamic compression. Notwithstanding, porous metals (2.7 g/cm³ density) conflict with hydraulic support lightweighting, while aluminum brittleness induces low-impact fracture. Nylon (1.4 g/cm³), 48% lighter than porous aluminum, features non-uniform wall thickness enabling sequential deformation (thin beams first, thick beams later), preventing low-impact brittle failure and adapting to multi-intensity underground impacts. Hamzehei12 et al. introduced a 4D-printed corrugated metamaterial, modifying conventional concave unit cells by replacing diagonal supports with corrugations and lattice horizontal beams with corrugated connections. This metamaterial outperforms conventional designs in stability and energy dissipation, exhibiting tunable multi-stiffness, enhanced stability, internal friction, and shape memory. But high-precision manufacturing elevates costs, and poor thermal resistance limits adaptability to underground temperature fluctuations (30–60 °C). Meng13 et al. introduced a new class of multi-stage deformable metamaterials, achieving sequential deformation through optimized geometric architecture. By integrating the recoverability of elastic deformation with the high energy absorption efficiency of plastic deformation, the structure exhibited multifunctional performance suitable for impact mitigation and energy dissipation applications. However, resin-based materials do not comply with the explosion-proof specifications for underground operations. Zhang14 et al. compared the compressive behavior and energy absorption performance of a bionic ‘beetle elytron plate’ with those of a traditional honeycomb plate under identical wall thickness conditions. The bionic design, despite a mere 9% increase in weight while maintaining the same wall thickness, exhibits 2.4 times the compressive strength and 5 times the energy dissipation capacity of the honeycomb plate. Such performance advantages stem from its closed hollow column structure, which is compatible with a simplified one-piece forming process, enabling direct engineering implementation. In closed-cell structures, entrapped air during impact-induced deformation cannot be expelled rapidly, liable to generate instantaneous overpressure. Additionally, energy absorption in closed-cell hollow columns relies on single-cycle plastic deformation, rendering them unsuitable for frequent impact scenarios in coal mine roadways. Du15 et al. proposed an all-paper-polyethylene foam multicomponent non-metallic sandwich tube, and systematically explored its deformation mechanisms and energy absorption behaviors under axial drop hammer impact. By tailoring the orientation, cross-sectional geometry, and dimensional ratios, the paper honeycomb-PE foam sandwich tube enables predictable and optimizable energy absorption under axial impact, thereby offering a novel solution for green cushioning and protection applications. Paper-based materials suffer degradation in high-humidity environments, while PE foams exhibit inadequate compressive strength (< 10 MPa), unable to withstand the intense loading under coal mine rock bursts. Bozkurt16 et al. comparatively investigated, via the finite element method, deformation and energy absorption of four lightweight composite lattice cylindrical structures under quasi-static compression. They found structural configuration, cell size, etc., significantly affect peak force, mean crushing force, specific energy absorption, and crushing efficiency. The square structure shows the highest axial peak force, aluminum the maximum total energy absorption, and carbon fiber the optimal specific energy absorption, offering guidance for designing lightweight, high-energy-absorption structures.

Despite extensive progress in the development of impact energy-absorbing devices, their application in underground coal mines remains constrained by complex operating conditions. These environments impose stringent requirements for both explosion-proof and fire-resistant safety, alongside high energy absorption efficiency. Existing designs predominantly emphasize the structural optimization of individual energy-absorbing units, often overlooking in-situ operational constraints, which limits their adaptability to harsh underground conditions. To address these challenges, a novel buffer energy-absorbing device is proposed for the impact protection of advanced hydraulic supports. The design fully accounts for the geometric configuration and material properties of the energy-absorbing body. Finite element simulations using ABAQUS are employed to analyze the device’s core component—a non-uniform wall thickness rhombic (NTR) honeycomb structure—and to optimize its geometric and material parameters. The results aim to extend impact duration and attenuate peak impact forces. This device provides critical technical support for safe deep mining operations and contributes to the advancement of underground protection systems toward greater depth adaptability and intelligent functionality.

Model development

Structural design of the novel buffering and Energy-Absorbing device

In underground engineering applications-particularly under complex conditions involving high-intensity impacts and vibrational loads-the efficient absorption and dissipation of energy represent a key technical challenge. The proposed energy-absorbing device features a hierarchical top plate–absorber–base plate assembly architecture (Fig. 1) and integrates a disc spring buffering unit. The system combines a non-uniform wall thickness rhombic honeycomb structure with the disc spring element to form a novel composite buffering and energy-absorbing mechanism, mounted on the top beam of a hydraulic support to mitigate impact-induced loads. High-strength bolt arrays are used to fasten the device to the upper surface of the advanced hydraulic support’s top beam. Based on site-specific operating conditions, each top beam can be configured with four to six such devices, discretely arranged to accommodate different gradients of advance support pressure. Through coupled analysis of material yield strength and preload spring stiffness, four parallel units are capable of withstanding an initial support load of F = 4333 kN, satisfying the dynamic load specifications for advanced hydraulic Supports. A composite preload mechanism, consisting of a 30 mm thick top plate and a disc-spring-buffered column, is employed to achieve a compact axial profile while ensuring adequate shear strength. This configuration mitigates lateral tearing stress amplitudes induced by anchor bolt interference during support translation, thereby improving structural resilience under constrained underground conditions.

The preloading support is provided by a two-stage telescopic column equipped with disc-spring buffering. The primary energy absorption is achieved through disc springs-thin, conically shaped elastic elements manufactured by stamping high-strength steel sheets. These springs offer a compact form factor, high load-bearing capacity, and superior buffering and damping characteristics. By configuring them in various arrangements, such as stacking or pairing, a wide range of load–deflection responses can be tailored to match specific energy absorption requirements under different loading conditions. A key advantage of disc springs lies in their ability to sustain large load variations with minimal deformation, enabling significant reductions in the overall size and weight of the buffering system. This makes them particularly well-suited for impact protection applications where high robustness, lightweight construction, and cost-efficiency are critical.

Non-uniform wall thickness rhombic honeycomb structure

Honeycomb structures, inspired by biomimetic efficiency, utilize lightweight configurations and ordered or irregular geometric cell arrangements to deliver advantages such as low specific density, high specific strength and stiffness, excellent impact mitigation, and strong structural tunability17–19. These structures enable multifunctional performance, including in-plane deformability, out-of-plane load-bearing, and vibration isolation, making them highly promising for energy absorption in collision and impact-related applications. In this study, a non-uniform wall thickness rhombic (NTR) honeycomb structure composed of elastoplastic material is proposed as the core component of the energy-absorbing device20–24. The structure exhibits a distinct multi-stage mechanical response: the first stage involves purely elastic deformation, allowing for repeatable loading, while the second stage engages controlled plastic deformation to enhance energy absorption efficiency. This staged response overcomes the conventional trade-off between reusability and dissipation efficiency, significantly broadening the applicability of energy-absorbing systems for impact protection in advanced hydraulic supports. The NTR honeycomb represents a novel topological design optimized via differential wall thickness tailoring. Its unit cell is illustrated in Fig. 2. Building upon the intrinsic features of rhombic honeycombs with non-uniform wall thickness—such as low initial stiffness, high energy absorption efficiency, excellent crashworthiness, and superior flexural capacity—the design further integrates the typical benefits of honeycomb architectures, including low mass, high specific strength, and elevated shear modulus. These combined characteristics result in exceptional energy absorption capability and make the structure highly suitable for modular deployment in demanding engineering environments.

In ABAQUS, due to the challenge of generating a unified mesh for the area between the inclined thin and thick beams in the honeycomb structural element module, this region was first Subjected to local segmentation and meshed using tetrahedral elements. The remaining parts were meshed with hexahedral elements to enhance meshing accuracy. A global advanced algorithm was employed, with mapped meshing applied where appropriate. Size control was configured with an approximate global size of 1, and curvature control was implemented with a maximum deviation factor of 0.1. Given that both the upper and lower base plates are regular cuboids, they were meshed with hexahedral elements. Ultimately, the buffer energy-absorbing device consisted of a total of 373,639 elements and 581,898 nodes. Calculation results under various impact velocities revealed that the history variable output curves of kinetic energy and internal energy exhibited a negative correlation, with neither exceeding the total energy curve of the model. This satisfies the requirements for simulation calculations.

A unit module with a width of b = 94.5 mm was extracted from a full-scale model with a total thickness of 945 mm for detailed analysis. The module comprises four beams each measuring 17.92 mm in length and one vertical beam (AB) with a length of 16.39 mm. The inclined slender and thick beams have thicknesses of 0.95 mm and 3.78 mm, respectively, while the vertical beam thickness is 1 mm. During underground Support operations, impact on the top plate alters the slenderness ratios of the beams. The impact protection performance of the device is assessed based on the magnitude of the base reaction force and the timing of the peak impact load. The module is symmetric about the vertical beam AB, with an angle of 29° between the inclined slender and thick beams. As illustrated in Fig. 2, the structure consists of p unit cells in the horizontal direction and q unit cells in the vertical direction.

Finite element simulation model

To simulate the in-situ operating conditions of hydraulic supports in underground coal mining, a finite element model of the energy-absorbing device was developed. As shown in Fig. 3, the non-uniform wall thickness rhombic honeycomb structure is positioned between a top and bottom plate. The bottom plate is fully constrained in all degrees of freedom, while the top plate is modeled as a rigid body with an assigned initial impact velocity. The density of the impact plate is increased to emulate the inertial effects of collapsing rock masses, thereby improving the physical fidelity of the simulation. To assess the performance of the energy-absorbing device, the honeycomb structure is modeled using two material definitions-nylon and rigid-and the resulting base reaction force curves under different impact velocities are compared.

Nylon was adopted as the base material for the non-uniform wall thickness rhombic honeycomb structure. As a semi-crystalline polymer, nylon combines high rigidity with excellent toughness. Under impact loading, it absorbs energy through elastic deformation of molecular chains and dissipates energy via plastic deformation, thereby mitigating the risk of brittle fracture. These properties enable the structure to attenuate impact loads effectively through deformation modes Such as unit cell compression and torsion, resulting in a reduced peak force. Moreover, nylon exhibits favorable resilience, allowing partial shape recovery after deformation, making it well-suited for repeated energy absorption scenarios. It is important to note that during ABAQUS modeling, the rate coefficient in the viscoelastic model was set to zero, such that the dominant drivers of peak reaction force variations are geometric deformation and micro-inertia. The ‘peak reduction relative to rigid bodies’ is not exclusively attributed to material strain rate; any deformable lattice under impact accomplishes load time-history broadening and peak attenuation through configurational buckling and micro-inertial effects of element masses, an inherent distinction from rigid bodies. By contrast, the strain rate sensitivity of nylon enhances instantaneous stiffness and elevates viscoelastic energy dissipation rates with increasing velocity, primarily modulating peak timing and energy conversion dynamics.

Simulation and experimental comparison

Construction of the experimental testing platform

In experimental engineering, the accuracy and reliability of simulation results are essential for validating structural performance. To investigate the dynamic response of the energy-absorbing structure, a high-precision impact testing platform was developed. This platform is capable of replicating underground collision scenarios while capturing key impact parameters via current signals transmitted from integrated sensors, enabling accurate data acquisition and analysis. As shown in Fig. 4, the system comprises three primary components: a precisely controlled electromagnet, a mass-adjustable impact plate, and an anti-tilt support frame. The electromagnet acts as the driving mechanism, generating rapid and controllable impact energy to simulate underground dynamic events at varying velocities and intensities. This controllability is critical for evaluating the impact resistance of energy-absorbing devices under diverse conditions. Furthermore, the electromagnet is engineered for precise energy release, ensuring consistent initial testing conditions and thereby improving the repeatability and comparability of experimental results.

Within the data acquisition system, the DX-LR01 communication module is utilized for its compact form factor, long-range transmission capability, strong electromagnetic interference resistance, and broad application adaptability. Parameter configuration via AT commands enables efficient bidirectional data exchange, making it particularly suitable for compact communication systems. The Runeskee pressure sensor, based on a resistive thin-film mechanism, is installed at the base of the honeycomb structure. During testing, the electromagnetic valve is triggered at a designated height to release the impact plate, inducing deformation of the honeycomb structure. The resulting impact force is transmitted to the bottom plate, where it causes strain in the resistive membrane. This change in resistance is converted into a voltage signal through an external circuit and subsequently transmitted to the terminal for data acquisition.

Analysis of experimental data

The honeycomb structure used in the experiment was made of thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU), while the impact plate, weighing 28 kg, was fabricated from Q235 steel. It was released in free fall from a height of 5 cm above the honeycomb surface. Upon impact from the top plate, the energy-absorbing structure exhibited a staged deformation process, as shown in Fig. 5. At 500 ms, deformation was primarily characterized by the bending and fracture of the inclined slender beams, representing minor, recoverable elastic deformation. As the impact plate continued its descent, vertical beams began to bend at approximately 1000 ms. By 1200 ms, the vertical beams had undergone complete bending and entered a state of irreversible plastic deformation. A corresponding numerical simulation was subsequently conducted in ABAQUS, as illustrated in Fig. 5. The simulated deformation patterns closely aligned with those observed in the experimental setup, validating the accuracy of the finite element model and its applicability for subsequent analyses in place of physical testing.

Simulation results and analysis

Deformation behavior of the nylon honeycomb structure

Deformation Response Characteristics under Different Impact Velocities

In real-world applications, impact velocities may fluctuate due to various external conditions. To assess the structural robustness under varying operational scenarios, the dynamic response of the non-uniform wall thickness rhombic honeycomb structure was investigated across a range of impact velocities. This evaluation provides critical insights into its buffering capacity, energy absorption efficiency, damping characteristics, and structural stability—parameters essential for the design and optimization of impact mitigation systems. Owing to the strain-rate sensitivity of nylon, the structure exhibits a dynamic hardening effect under high-speed impacts, resulting in the formation of multi-stage stress plateaus. This response effectively extends the energy absorption duration and reduces peak impact forces, thereby enhancing the protection of hydraulic support systems. Figure 6 illustrates the dynamic response contours of the structure at different impact velocities. As simulation time advances, the central region of the honeycomb undergoes progressive compressive deformation. At a low impact velocity of 1 m/s, modeled in ABAQUS with an explicit dynamic step, the deformation occurs sequentially through the inclined beams, ultimately reaching the state shown in Fig. 6(a3). Vertical beams remain intact, providing structural Support, and the deformation is primarily elastic and fully recoverable. At an intermediate velocity of 5 m/s with a simulation time step of 100, the inclined beams collapse completely. This is followed by lateral deformation of the upper vertical beams and minor bending of the lower vertical elements, as shown in Fig. 6(b2). After impact (Fig. 6(b3)), the vertical beams undergo significant plastic deformation, leading to permanent structural distortion and loss of recoverability. Under higher impact velocities of 9 m/s and 13 m/s, the honeycomb structure experiences complete plastic deformation during impact. The severe loading results in structural failure, characterized by detachment from the bottom plate.

Fig. 6Dynamic response of the rhombic honeycomb structure with non-uniform wall thickness under varying impact velocities.

Dynamic response of the rhombic honeycomb structure with non-uniform wall thickness under varying impact velocities.

The simulation results reveal three distinct deformation modes of the structure under varying impact loads: (1) Elastic deformation at initial loading, characterized by recoverable bending of the inclined beams. In this stage, part of the impactor’s kinetic energy is converted into internal energy through geometric transformation of the structure. (2) Progressive plastic deformation, during which the inclined beams undergo full compression while the vertical beams begin to plastically deform. This distributed deformation pattern, enabled by the non-uniform wall thickness design, effectively dissipates the impact energy and enhances the overall energy absorption capacity. Kinetic energy is predominantly transformed into plastic strain energy while ensuring geometric compatibility. (3) Complete structural collapse under high impact loads, where vertical beams experience full plastic deformation. Significant wall thickness differences between thin and thick regions lead to localized yielding in thinner zones, initiating structural instability. As the impact energy is further absorbed through material deformation, localized stress concentrations may exceed the interfacial strength between the structure and the base plate, resulting in rebound or detachment of the honeycomb core.

This velocity-adaptive compression behavior ensures reliable protection under diverse impact conditions and supports multi-functional energy dissipation. It further exemplifies an energy conversion strategy based on structural deformation, offering a robust and efficient solution for impact mitigation in complex underground environments.

Comparative assessment of peak compression under varying impact velocities

Maximum compression serves as a key metric for evaluating the performance of honeycomb structures under dynamic loading. As impact velocity increases, the corresponding rise in kinetic energy necessitates greater compressive deformation of the nylon-based honeycomb structure to extend the energy absorption duration and attenuate peak impact forces. In the ABAQUS simulation, the central point on the top surface of the upper plate was designated as the reference point, with the initial coordinate set to zero. Downward displacement was recorded as a negative value.

As shown in Fig. 7, the nylon honeycomb structure exhibits a pronounced velocity-dependent energy absorption response. With increasing impact velocity from 7 m/s to 15 m/s, the peak compression demonstrates a clear upward trend, indicating effective kinetic energy dissipation via a progressive and controllable plastic buckling of the cell walls. Conversely, the nylon-based structure demonstrated lower peak forces, delayed response times, and smoother force profiles. These features suggest that the nylon material effectively attenuates peak impact loads, extends the energy dissipation duration, and improves impact resistance and system stability under dynamic loading conditions.

Evolution of kinetic and internal energy in the impact system under varying velocities

Kinetic energy represents the direct manifestation of the energy carried by the impactor during collision. An effective energy-absorbing structure is expected to induce a rapid and substantial reduction in kinetic energy throughout the impact event, thereby indicating high energy absorption efficiency. Simultaneously, the transformation of kinetic energy into internal energy-through mechanisms such as plastic deformation and intermolecular friction-reflects the system’s capacity to dissipate impact energy. A pronounced increase in internal energy suggests a greater degree of energy conversion and absorption by the structure.

As illustrated in Fig. 9, the kinetic energy initially rises with higher impact velocities but rapidly decreases within the first 0.01 s. This demonstrates the nylon-based honeycomb structure’s strong ability to absorb kinetic energy within an extremely short response time. Notably, even under the highest impact velocity of 15 m/s, a sharp energy decay is observed, confirming the structure’s dynamic adaptability to high-energy impact scenarios. All velocity cases exhibit a consistent trend where kinetic energy ultimately stabilizes at a low level, indicating that the majority of impact energy is effectively dissipated via progressive deformation and internal friction mechanisms. The minimal residual energy observed at equilibrium further validates the structure’s efficient energy absorption capability. These findings substantiate the structure’s suitability for advanced shock mitigation applications, particularly in forward-positioned hydraulic support systems used in deep mining environments.

Each internal energy curve exhibits a rapid increase from the vicinity of the origin, indicating that the structure quickly absorbs energy upon impact. The kinetic energy of the striker is transferred to the non-uniform wall-thickness rhombic honeycomb structure and converted into internal energy through progressive deformation mechanisms such as bending of inclined and vertical struts. In contrast, structures without energy-absorbing features tend to reflect or transmit a substantial portion of the impact energy, resulting in negligible internal energy growth.

A clear correlation is observed between impact velocity and energy absorption: higher velocities lead to faster internal energy rise and greater final values, whereas lower velocities result in slower accumulation and lower plateaus. This trend aligns with fundamental energy dissipation principles, confirming the structure’s capacity to adaptively absorb energy relative to impact severity.

All curves eventually plateau, suggesting the structure reaches an energy saturation state. At this point, the rate of energy input is balanced by the rate of dissipation via plastic deformation and internal friction. This characteristic ‘rapid absorption followed by stabilization’ trajectory underscores the efficacy of the proposed energy-absorbing system in mitigating impact loads through controlled energy dissipation.

In terms of response behavior, the nylon-based honeycomb structure exhibits velocity-dependent reaction force patterns, indicating its ability to adaptively modulate energy absorption and transmission in response to varying impact intensities. Under high-velocity impacts, although the peak reaction force is elevated, energy dissipation is effectively achieved through progressive structural deformation. In contrast, low-velocity impacts are characterized by reduced peak forces and prolonged energy absorption durations, reflecting the material’s favorable adaptability across a wide range of loading conditions.

The base reaction force, defined as the reactive load transmitted to the bottom plate, serves as a critical metric for evaluating impact transmission efficiency. As shown in Fig. 10, increasing impact velocity leads to a corresponding rise in the peak base reaction force. Under high-speed impacts (e.g., 15 m/s and 13 m/s), peak forces occur earlier, typically within ~ 0.01 s, and exhibit sharp, narrow profiles, indicative of rapid energy release. In contrast, lower-speed scenarios (e.g., 5 m/s and 7 m/s) produce broader force-time curves with smaller peak magnitudes, denoting slower and more stable energy dissipation. This distinction clearly demonstrates that the nylon honeycomb structure accommodates high-intensity impacts through rapid plastic deformation, while lower-intensity loads are mitigated via extended buffering, thereby ensuring consistent energy attenuation across diverse operational scenarios.

Conclusions

The non-uniform wall thickness rhombic (NTR) honeycomb structure, constructed with nylon as the core energy-absorbing material, leverages differential wall thickness design and multi-stage deformation mechanisms to resolve the trade-off between reusability and energy dissipation efficiency inherent in conventional energy-absorbing systems. Simulation and experimental results reveal a three-phase deformation behavior across varying impact velocities: elastic deformation under low-speed impact, partial plastic deformation of inclined and vertical struts under medium-speed impact, and full plastic collapse under high-speed impact. This adaptive response enables the structure to accommodate a wide range of dynamic loading conditions25,26.

With respect to energy absorption characteristics, the nylon-based structure outperforms rigid counterparts by significantly delaying peak reaction time and reducing peak force. For example, under a 7 m/s impact, the peak Support force is delayed from 12.45ms to 16.35ms, while the load magnitude is reduced from 134,489 N to 53,342 N-representing a 60% decrease. Kinetic energy profiles further demonstrate rapid energy dissipation within 0.01s across all tested impact velocities (5–15 m/s), with final energy levels stabilizing at minimal values. This behavior confirms the structure’s capacity for efficient energy transformation via progressive buckling and controlled deformation27,28.

The proposed NTR honeycomb design achieves an optimal combination of lightweight architecture, high specific energy absorption, and enhanced impact resistance. By extending impact response time and distributing peak loads, it establishes a robust multi-level protective strategy for advanced hydraulic supports in underground coal mining operations. These results offer essential support for the development of intelligent protective equipment in deep mining applications and provide a novel design paradigm for energy-absorbing structures under complex dynamic environments.
