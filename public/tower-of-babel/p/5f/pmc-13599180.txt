Prospective randomized controlled trial of computer simulation versus high-fidelity mannequin simulation in advanced cardiovascular life support (ACLS) training for anesthesiology novice clinicians
Authors: Jiafeng Yu, Qian Wang, Ling Yin, Fang Yuan, Yufei Kan, Zengkun Wang, Yonghao Yu, Qing Li, Yang Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTraditionally, cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) and advanced cardiovascular life support (ACLS) training programs have relied heavily on paper-based instructional materials and multimedia theoretical presentations. This study conducts the inaugural comparative analysis of the effectiveness of high-fidelity mannequin simulators versus computer simulators in ACLS training for anesthesiology novice clinicians. Additionally, it aims to identify an optimal training cycle for ACLS.MethodsThis prospective randomized controlled study included 62 participants, 30 first-year residents and 32 interns from 2020–2021, who were randomly divided into three groups: the computer simulator group (Group C), the high-fidelity mannequin group (Group M), and the combined computer-mannequin group (Group SM). Baseline (T0) and immediate post-training (T1) assessments were performed, covering technical skills, non-technical skills, and self-confidence and satisfaction. Group performance, CPR initiation time, and defibrillation time were analyzed. Follow-up assessments were conducted at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months (T2–T5) to identify the optimal ACLS training interval.ResultsGroups C, M, and SM showed significant improvements in technical skills at T1 versus T0 (P < 0.05), with higher megacode scores and reduced times to initiate CPR and defibrillation at T1 (all P < 0.05); no significant differences were observed among the three groups (all P > 0.05). Non-technical skill scores in all groups improved significantly at T1 compared to T0 (P < 0.05), and Groups M and SM had generally higher overall non-technical skill scores than Group C (P < 0.05). For self-confidence and satisfaction, Groups M and SM scored higher than Group C in "this mode can stimulate my interest" (P < 0.05). Temporal trends of technical and non-technical skills varied among groups, but all indicated a consistent decline at T5, indicating reduced memory retention.ConclusionBoth computer simulator training and high-fidelity mannequin-based scenario simulation effectively improve novice clinicians' ACLS technical and non-technical skills, though scenario simulation is more advantageous for enhancing non-technical skills. Thus, computers can complement scenario simulators in ACLS training. ACLS training effectiveness appears to decline over time, with notable reductions in knowledge retention and test scores observed at 12 months, which supports the necessity of annual retraining.Trial registrationThe study was retrospectively registered at the ClinicalTrials.gov(NCT05001659) on June 23, 2021.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12871-026-04080-8.Keywords: Computer simulation, High-fidelity mannequin simulation, Advanced cardiovascular life support (ACLS) training, Anesthesiology novice clinicians

Introduction

Cardiac arrest (CA) represents a predominant cause of sudden cardiac death and constitutes one of the most critical public health challenges worldwide. According to the American Heart Association (AHA), the survival rate for CA is positively correlated with the prompt initiation and quality of cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR). As a fundamental emergency intervention, CPR forms the cornerstone of the resuscitation chain and significantly enhances patient survival outcomes [1, 2]. Despite the crucial role of CPR and advanced cardiovascular life support (ACLS) training and assessment for clinicians across various specialties [3], students are unable to practice on actual patients due to the potential for errors during resuscitation efforts and the unpredictability of patient conditions [4, 5]. This limitation results in a superficial comprehension of clinical scenarios, thereby hindering timely and accurate decision-making in real-life situations.

Traditionally, CPR and ACLS training have primarily depended on paper-based materials and theoretical multimedia lectures [6]. However, these conventional methods do not substantially enhance skill proficiency and have limited impact on developing teamwork skills, self-confidence, and knowledge acquisition. With advancements in information technology, high-fidelity mannequin scenario simulator training has been increasingly incorporated into clinical education. This approach allows students to practice safely and repeatedly in environments that closely replicate real clinical settings [7]. Nonetheless, the high costs and resource demands associated with this method restrict its widespread implementation, particularly in resource-constrained settings[8, 9]. In response to this challenge, computer simulator training has emerged over the past decade as a viable alternative [10]. Previous studies have found that integrating computer simulation with traditional learning significantly enhances clinicians’ comprehensive emergency care abilities over an extended period, rendering computer simulation more engaging and effective than textbook learning alone [11, 12].

However, whether computer simulation training can replace high-fidelity mannequin simulation in ACLS training remains unexplored. This study seeks to address this gap by evaluating the effectiveness of computer simulators in comparison to high-fidelity mannequin simulators within ACLS training contexts. Additionally, existing research highlights a concerning trend in skill retention following CPR training, with knowledge decay occurring within 2–4 weeks and persisting up to approximately two months post-training [13]. This study also aims to investigate the extent of skill decay among anesthesiology novice clinicians over 12 months, seeking to identify a pragmatic balance between the physiological need for frequent spaced practice and the institutional constraints of clinical training.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study was a prospective, randomized controlled trial conducted within the Department of Anesthesiology at Tianjin Medical University General Hospital. The trial was duly registered with the appropriate ethics committee, and informed consent was obtained from all participants. This study was retrospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (ID: NCT05001659). The overall study design is depicted in Fig. 1. The study cohort comprised 30 first-year resident physicians who commenced their training at Tianjin Medical University in October 2020 and October 2021, along with 32 interns who began in December 2020 and December 2021. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) participants had not previously engaged in any ACLS training courses, and (2) participants voluntarily agreed to partake in the study. The exclusion criteria included: (1) withdrawal or absence during the study period, and (2) inability to ensure completion of the survey.

Flow diagram of the training and testing procedures

Comprehensive clinical and demographic data were collected from all participants, including gender, age, place of origin, and any prior experience with first aid before the training. The random allocation sequence was generated using a computer-generated randomization method by an independent statistician not involved in participant enrollment or outcome assessment. Participants were randomly assigned to the computer simulation group, high-fidelity mannequin simulation group, or combined simulation group in a 1:1:1 ratio. The allocation sequence was pre-prepared and concealed in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes to ensure allocation concealment. Upon enrollment, the attending anesthesiologist opened the envelope in sequence to assign the intervention group, with no prior knowledge of the allocation sequence. A baseline assessment conducted prior to the training was designated as T0, followed by an immediate post-training assessment labeled as T1. Subsequently, four follow-up assessments (T2, T3, T4, T5) were scheduled after T1. All evaluations were carried out by two facilitators who remained blinded to the group assignments.The facilitators responsible for outcome assessment and the researchers conducting statistical data analysis remained blinded to group assignments throughout the study. Blinding was maintained until data collection completion and database locking to minimize assessment and analysis bias.

Training protocol

Guided by the guidelines for CPR and cardiovascular life support in the “2010 American Heart Association Guidelines for Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation and Emergency Cardiovascular Care”, the ACLS theoretical lectures including Return of Spontaneous Circulation (ROSC), CPR, Basic Life Support (BLS) were conducted for the three groups [14] (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Practical sessions on CPR were also provided, including demonstrations on the use of defibrillators, covering methods, steps, and precautions. Participants were familiarized with the procedures for utilizing the simulation operating room and mannequins. Initially, all participants completed a baseline case assessment (T0), which involved a resuscitation scenario of ventricular fibrillation resulting from venous embolism induced by bone cement tamponade during orthopedic hip replacement surgery.

Training implementation

Group C participated in training utilizing computer software simulations that replicated the comprehensive progression of clinical cases. Participants were tasked with formulating diagnoses and treatment plans based on the disease progression of virtual patients. They completed all interventional procedures, including venous access establishment, drug administration, external chest compression, and defibrillation, by interacting with corresponding buttons on the computer interface (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Real-time updates on patient status and vital sign parameters were provided through computer pop-up windows. Following the simulation, facilitators conducted a debriefing session to review operational procedures.

Group M engaged in high-fidelity mannequin simulation training within a realistic simulated operating room environment, utilizing the same case content as Group C. Participants assumed the role of anesthesiologists, while two facilitators portrayed the roles of surgeon and circulating nurse, respectively. Participants performed practical operations such as venous access establishment, actual drug administration, and cardiopulmonary resuscitation (Supplementary Figs. 1 C-D). The simulation was conducted continuously without any prompts from facilitators. Following each simulation, all groups underwent a structured debriefing session conducted by experienced facilitators. These facilitators were senior anesthesiologists who were ACLS-certified and had completed a standardized instructor training program in simulation-based medical education. To ensure consistency, each debriefing session lasted approximately 15 minutes and followed the standardized ‘Plus-Delta’ debriefing model. This structure prioritized a learner-centered approach, focusing on identifying performance strengths (Plus) and areas for improvement (Delta) regarding both technical procedural skills and non-technical team dynamics. Group SM followed the same rigorous debriefing protocol, ensuring that the educational quality and standards were consistent with those applied to Groups C and M.

Evaluation and assessment standards

Assessments were conducted at six distinct time points: T0, T1, T2, T3, T4, and T5, utilizing consistent content and evaluation methodologies. At T1, a self-confidence and satisfaction questionnaire was administered, achieving a 100% response rate. Technical competence was assessed via the Megacode scale [15], encompassing dimensions such as assessment, emergency call, and airway management, with a maximum score of 25 points. The time to initiate CPR and the time to first defibrillation were recorded separately for each group at each time point (Supplementary Table 1). Non-technical skills were evaluated using the AS-NTS scale [16], which comprised three dimensions: task planning, teamwork and leadership, and team orientation. Each item was rated on a 5-point scale, culminating in a total score of 15 points, where higher scores indicated superior performance (Supplementary Table 2). Self-confidence and satisfaction were measured using a scale adapted from Levett-Jones et al. [17]., consisting of five items rated on a 5-point scale, with a total score of 25 points. Higher average scores denoted greater self-confidence and satisfaction. Differences in scores among groups for each dimension were analyzed (Supplementary Table 3).

Sample size calculation

The sample size was estimated based on the primary outcome (Megacode technical skill scores, continuous variable) using G*Power 3.1 software, with parameters set as follows: two-tailed α = 0.05, power (1-β) = 0.80, moderate effect size (Cohen’s f = 0.25), and 1:1:1 group allocation. This yielded a minimum total sample size of 59 participants.

Secondary outcomes, ordered by importance (AS-NTS non-technical skill scores, satisfaction and self-confidence scores, skill retention trends), were corrected using the Bonferroni method (corrected α’=0.0125). The 59-participant sample provided 72% statistical power for non-technical skill scores (effect size f = 0.3) and 74% for 12-month skill retention trends (effect size f = 0.25), both sufficient for analysis. For satisfaction and self-confidence scores (effect size f = 0.2), the power was 58%, but only one item showed significant differences with limited clinical significance, so no additional participants were required.

Accounting for a potential 10% loss to follow-up over the 12-month study period, the corrected target sample size was 66 participants. A total of 62 participants were actually enrolled (21, 23, and 18 in the three groups, respectively), ensuring adequate statistical power for the analysis of the primary and key secondary outcomes.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data processing and statistical analysis were performed using SPSS 26.0 and Graphpad Prism 9.0 software. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD). The Shapiro-Wilk normality test was applied to detect whether the data from each group were normally distributed. Comparisons between two independent samples were made using the two-sample t-test for normally distributed data and Welch’s correction test for non-normally distributed data. For quantitative data at a single time point, group comparisons were made using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). For group comparisons at multiple time points, two-way ANOVA was used. Categorical data were expressed as counts and percentages and analyzed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test to verify baseline homogeneity among the three groups. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participants’ baseline characteristics

A total of 62 anesthesiology novice clinicians participated in this study, consisting of 30 males and 32 females, with an average age of 22.96 ± 1.25 years (Table 1). The mean ages for the groups were as follows: Group C had a mean age of 22.15 ± 1.24 years, Group M had a mean age of 22.43 ± 1.18 years, and Group SM had a mean age of 23.09 ± 1.12 years. In terms of residential background, 26 participants were from rural areas, while 36 were from urban areas. Specifically, Group C comprised 10 rural and 11 urban novice clinicians; Group M consisted of 8 rural and 15 urban novice clinicians; and Group SM included 8 rural and 10 urban novice clinicians. The cohort included 40 resident physicians (distributed as 14 in Group C, 14 in Group M, and 12 in Group SM) and 22 interns (7 in Group C, 9 in Group M, and 6 in Group SM). Among all participants, 60 had no prior first-aid experience, whereas 2 had previous experience (1 in Group C and 1 in Group M). No statistically significant differences in baseline characteristics were identified among the groups (all P > 0.05).

Comparison of technical competence

The analysis of megacode scores at time points T0 and T1 across the three study groups yielded the following results: In Group C, the score at T1 (21.05 ± 3.39) was significantly higher than at T0 (14.24 ± 4.31) (P < 0.001) (Fig. 2A). Similarly, in Group M, the T1 score (21.33 ± 2.93) exceeded the T0 score (15.14 ± 3.66) with statistical significance (P < 0.001) (Fig. 2A). In Group SM, the T1 score (20.33 ± 3.62) was also significantly greater than the T0 score (15.06 ± 3.63) (P < 0.001) (Fig. 2A). Additionally, no statistically significant differences in megacode scores were identified between the three groups at either T0 or T1 (Supplementary Table 4, Fig. 2B).

Technical competence before and after training in the three groups. A Within-group comparisons of Megacode scale scores, CPR initiation time, and defibrillation initiation time before and after training. B Between-group comparisons of technical competence at baseline and immediately after training. Note: ***: P < 0.001, **: P < 0.01

The assessment of CPR initiation times before and after training revealed significant reductions in mean times across all groups. Specifically, in Group C, the mean initiation time at T1 (37.22 ± 13.66) was markedly lower than that at T0 (88.06 ± 48.66) (P < 0.001). Similarly, in Group M, the mean time at T1 (32.55 ± 12.42) was significantly shorter than at T0 (98.00 ± 51.26) (P < 0.001). For Group SM, the mean time at T1 (27.06 ± 11.99) was also significantly reduced compared to T0 (74.19 ± 31.66) (P < 0.001). Furthermore, no significant differences in CPR initiation times were observed among the three groups at either T0 or T1 (Supplementary Table 4, Fig. 2B).

The analysis of defibrillation initiation times at T0 and T1 revealed that, within Group C, the mean time at T1 (100.06 ± 31.79) was significantly shorter than at T0 (145.00 ± 56.04) (P < 0.001). Similarly, in Group M, the mean time at T1 (112.00 ± 44.43) was significantly reduced compared to T0 (159.07 ± 58.54) (P < 0.001). In Group SM, the mean time at T1 (94.86 ± 31.36) was also significantly shorter than at T0 (134.06 ± 46.43) (P < 0.001). Furthermore, there were no significant between-group differences in defibrillation initiation times at either T0 or T1(Supplementary Table 4, Fig. 2B).

Non‑technical skills, confidence and satisfaction evaluation

The evaluation of non-technical skills across the three groups yielded the following results: In Group C, the T0 score was 5.90 ± 2.43, which was significantly lower than the T1 score of 10.67 ± 2.33 (P < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 5, Fig. 3A). Likewise, Group M exhibited a T0 score of 6.29 ± 2.39, which was significantly lower than the T1 score of 12.38 ± 2.16 (P < 0.001). In Group SM, the T0 score was 7.11 ± 2.16, significantly lower than the T1 score of 12.20 ± 2.14 (P < 0.001). At T0, no significant differences in non-technical skill scores were detected among the three groups (Supplementary Table 5, Fig. 3B). However, a statistically significant difference was detected at T1, with scores in both Group M and Group SM significantly higher than those in Group C (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3B).

Non-technical competence before and after training in the three groups. A Within-group comparisons of non-technical competence before and after training. B Between-group comparisons of non-technical competence before and after training. C Self-confidence and satisfaction scores. Note: ***: P < 0.001, **: P < 0.01

Across the four items—“This mode is suitable for ACLS training courses,” “This mode enables rapid mastery of CPR-related knowledge,” “This mode improves analytical and problem-solving abilities,” and “This mode increased my confidence”—no significant differences were found among the three groups (all P > 0.05). For the item “This mode stimulates my interest,” the scores in Group M (4.56 ± 0.53) and Group SM (4.53 ± 0.56) were significantly higher than those in Group C (4.02 ± 0.48) (P < 0.05)(Supplementary Table 6, Fig. 3C).

Temporal changes in technical and non-technical competency retention

Over the five assessment time points, no statistically significant differences were observed in megacode scores, CPR initiation time, or defibrillation initiation time among the three groups at any single time point (all P > 0.05) (Supplementary Tables 7–9, Fig. 4A–C). A descriptive analysis of megacode scores (Fig. 4A), CPR initiation times (Fig. 4B), and defibrillation initiation times (Fig. 4C) across Groups C, M, and SM suggested two primary trends: (1) Megacode scores appeared to decline in all groups at T5, potentially indicating a reduction in the retention of technical knowledge and skills. (2) CPR and defibrillation initiation times seemed to be delayed in all groups at T5, which may suggest a decline in resuscitation proficiency and memory retention by 12 months post-training.

Regarding non-technical skills, statistically significant differences were observed among the groups throughout the five assessments, with Groups M and SM consistently achieving higher scores than Group C (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4D, Supplementary Table 10). A temporal analysis of non-technical skill levels in Groups C, M, and SM indicated that scores remained relatively stable from T1 to T4 but showed a downward trend at T5, potentially reflecting a reduction in the retention of non-technical competencies over time. Although some novice clinicians dropped out, there was no significant difference in dropout rates among the three groups (Supplementary Table 11).

Discussion

ACLS training plays a crucial role in improving the prognosis of critically ill patients [18, 19]. Presently, scenario-based simulation is the predominant method employed in ACLS training [20–22]. While this approach effectively replaces real patients for clinical instruction and practice, it is not without its drawbacks, including high costs, significant resource demands, inflexible scheduling, and heavy reliance on equipment. Many healthcare institutions are unable to bear the substantial costs associated with the necessary equipment, making the widespread adoption of scenario-based simulation in clinical education challenging. In light of the limitations inherent in conventional theoretical education and the uneven distribution of resources across regions, there is an urgent need to develop a more effective ACLS training methodology [8, 9]. Previous research has demonstrated that the use of computer simulation in ACLS training can enhance clinicians’ performance compared to traditional theoretical methods [10–12]. To enhance the accessibility of ACLS training, this study evaluates the educational effectiveness of computer-based simulation compared to scenario-based simulation. The objective is to determine whether computer simulation can effectively replace scenario simulation in clinical practice.

The findings indicate that all groups exhibited significant improvements in technical skill scores, with no statistically significant differences among them. Compared to scenario-based simulation, computer simulation offers increased privacy, enabling anesthesiology novice clinicians to consult facilitators promptly when facing challenges, thereby facilitating rapid error correction and the application of appropriate interventions. This iterative process of problem-solving and discussion further augments individual technical proficiency. Moreover, computer simulation provides realistic, pathophysiologically accurate medical environments, allowing for repeated practice opportunities. Through these simulated training sessions, novice clinicians gain clinical experience and expedite the development of procedural skills [23–25]. The findings of this study indicate that computer simulation training is comparable to scenario-based simulation in enhancing technical skills for ACLS.

In the realm of patient care, the integration of both technical and non-technical skills is imperative for upholding best practices and attaining a high standard of expertise [26]. Although technical skills are consistently emphasized in training programs, the evaluation and assessment of non-technical skills have historically received insufficient attention [27]. Our findings revealed that, in terms of non-technical skills, groups M and SM achieved significantly higher T1 scores compared to group C. Although computer simulation can enhance non-technical competencies by offering diverse scenarios, immersive experiences, and immediate feedback, it lacks the physical environment inherent in scenario-based simulation. Anesthesiology novice clinicians often concentrate excessively on the computer interface, which diminishes real-time communication and subsequently impairs team collaboration skills [28, 29]. In real-world CPR scenarios, effective communication between physicians and patients, as well as interprofessional teamwork, are essential for obtaining accurate feedback and optimizing patient outcomes [30, 31]. Although non-technical skills improve following training, the group utilizing computer simulations exhibited lower overall scores compared to those engaged in scenario-based simulations. Furthermore, previous research has indicated that up to 70% of adverse events can be attributed to human errors. To mitigate medical errors, the development of strong non-technical skills and enhanced teamwork is imperative [32]. These findings suggest that computer simulations cannot fully substitute scenario-based simulations for the enhancement of non-technical skills. The observed disparity in AS-NTS scores highlights a fundamental pedagogical distinction between computer-based and scenario-based simulation. Computer simulation platforms are primarily optimized for individual cognitive diagnostic training and algorithmic decision-making. While these platforms facilitate the mastery of technical knowledge, they lack the ‘human-in-the-loop’ social environment essential for training leadership, closed-loop communication, and interprofessional teamwork. Consequently, novice clinicians in Group C may have focused excessively on the computer interface, which limited the opportunity to practice real-time clinical negotiation and coordination. These findings suggest that computer-based training may be best utilized as a foundational prerequisite. By ensuring mastery of individual technical decision-making through computer simulation, novice clinicians can subsequently engage in scenario-based simulation with higher readiness, allowing facilitators to focus more on the critical team dynamics and NTS required for high-acuity resuscitation.

Additionally, satisfaction and self-confidence play a significant role in enhancing the effectiveness of training [33]. In a study conducted by Valente B and Melo M, high-fidelity simulation enhanced knowledge acquisition, prompted students to experience greater challenge while fostering increased self-confidence in assessing the severity of clinical cases, and improves memory retention. It has also demonstrated benefits in boosting learners’ self-confidence in identifying respiratory distress and respiratory failure in pediatric patients [34]. Conversely, a study by Hoadley and Kardong-Edgren found no statistically significant difference in student satisfaction when comparing high- and low-fidelity HPSMs for life support training [35, 36]. These contradictory findings carry substantial cost-benefit implications, thereby underscoring the need for further research, especially in light of the prevailing trend to invest in high-fidelity HPSMs despite the escalating fiscal constraints encountered by numerous universities and healthcare services. In this study, anesthesiology novice clinicians in groups M and SM reported significantly higher overall satisfaction with simulation training compared to those in group C, primarily due to increased engagement. The lower overall scores observed in group C may be attributed to the pre-training technical requirements of the computer platform used in this study, which required both novice clinicians and facilitators to dedicate substantial time to learning the software application to facilitate subsequent simulation session. This extensive technical preparation likely led to participant fatigue, gradually diminishing their patience for simulation training and reducing their motivation for further explorations [37, 38]. Conversely, scenario-based simulation, which recreates diverse rescue scenarios, surpasses the limitations of passive theoretical learning and facilitates genuine active participation. The integrated theoretical-practical learning model was highly regarded by participants, who demonstrated consistent engagement and focus throughout the training, leading to increased confidence and satisfaction [39].

However, both technical and non-technical skill retention exhibited a declining trend by the T5. These descriptive trends should be interpreted with caution due to the limited sample size at this final assessment point. The AHA suggests ACLS training should be replaced or supplemented with a spaced practice. Given multiple practical constraints—including staffing scheduling challenges, the financial burden of frequent training, and the difficulty in sustaining learner motivation over time—the AHA has not specified a definitive interval for ACLS study sessions [40]. While resuscitation skills may begin to decline as early as 3–6 months post-training, an annual interval represents a pragmatic compromise between clinical service delivery and the need for skill reinforcement. Our findings suggest that an annual ACLS training interval serves as a sustainable, institutionally feasible baseline to maintain foundational knowledge and optimal resuscitation capabilities. Future research should investigate the cost-effectiveness of shorter retraining intervals to determine the precise ‘dose’ of simulation required to maintain proficiency without compromising clinical operational efficiency.

However, this study has several limitations. First, this trial was retrospectively registered on ClinicalTrials.gov rather than prospectively. Although we strictly adhered to the pre-specified protocol approved by our Institutional Review Board throughout the execution of the study to minimize selective reporting bias, the lack of prospective registration remains a methodological limitation that readers should consider. Second, the training framework utilized in this study was based on the 2010 AHA guidelines. Because the trial was launched in October 2020, coinciding exactly with the release of the 2020 AHA updates, the original training framework was maintained throughout the research process to ensure curricular consistency. While this does not impact our primary conclusions comparing the delivery mechanisms of the different simulation modalities, future research should transition to the latest updated consensus guidelines. Third, the study population comprised a heterogeneous mix of first-year resident physicians and interns with no prior ACLS experience. Future multi-center trials with larger sample sizes should employ stratified randomization for further investigation. Fourth, due to factors such as the graduation of trainees and resident rotations, the valid sample size at T5 was limited to 11 participants. This substantial loss to follow-up constitutes a major limitation, potentially introducing attrition bias, as the participants remaining at the 12-month mark may differ systematically from those who dropped out. Consequently, our findings regarding long-term retention should be interpreted with caution as descriptive trends rather than definitive outcomes. The study did not explicitly determine the specific duration of this decline, nor did we perform a sensitivity analysis comparing T5 completers with those lost to follow-up due to the small remaining sample size. Nevertheless, the observed performance decay aligns with the general principles of spaced practice.

Conclusion

The findings of this study indicate that in ACLS training for anesthesiology novice clinicians, both computer-based simulation and scenario simulation using high-fidelity mannequins are equally effective in enhancing technical competence. However, scenario simulation and the combined simulation approach demonstrate a significant advantage over computer-based simulation alone in the development of non-technical skills. These findings indicate that while computer simulation cannot replace scenario-based training, it serves as a complementary tool for technical skill acquisition. Furthermore, our study identified a significant decline in training-related skills across all three groups at the 12-month evaluation. Therefore, we recommend the implementation of annual refresher training to reinforce knowledge and skills related to cardiopulmonary resuscitation.
