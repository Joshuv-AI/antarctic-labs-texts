Empirical comparison of iodine excretion estimated from spot urine samples with measured 24-hour urinary iodine excretion in healthy adults
Authors: Chun-Jui Huang, Yi-Wen Chen, Shun-Jie Yang, Jia-Zhen Li, Chii-Min Hwu, Harn-Shen Chen, Fan-Fen Wang, Chen-Chang Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Ideally, 24-hour urine collections are the reference standard for assessing iodine status. While median urinary iodine concentration (UIC) from spot samples has commonly replaced 24-hour collections for assessments of iodine status within populations, the validity of spot samples remains unclear. This study aimed to validate the estimated 24-hour urinary iodine excretion (e24-h UIE) derived from spot urine samples as an alternative to actual 24-hour urinary iodine excretion (24-h UIE).Methods:Healthy volunteers aged ≥18 years without thyroid or kidney diseases were recruited from National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University and Taipei Veterans General Hospital, Taiwan. Participants were recruited from a pool of university students, hospital staff, local vendors, and their social networks. Each participant provided one 24-hour urine sample, and two-spot urine samples collected immediately before and after the 24-hour period. UIC was measured using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry. The e24-h UIE was calculated from spot urine UIC and creatinine (Cr) concentrations, adjusted by anthropometry-based predicted 24-hour Cr excretion.Results:Twenty-three healthy adults (mean age: 36.4 ± 10.1 years, 60.8% men) were enrolled between September 2022 and May 2024. Median e24-h UIE (154.3 μg/d, interquartile range [IQR] 114.2-201.2) did not differ significantly from measured 24-h UIE (158.3 μg/d, IQR 106.7-213.7; Pearson’s r = 0.44, indicating a moderate correlation, p = 0.003). Passing–Bablok regression and Bland–Altman analysis indicated good agreement between methods, with a mean difference of 6.8 μg/d (regression line: Y = 0.92 × X + 9.53).Conclusion:These findings suggest that e24-h UIE from spot samples reasonably estimates actual 24-h UIE, offering a practical alternative to avoid the inconvenience of complete 24-hour urine collections.

1. INTRODUCTION

Iodine is a trace element essential for the production of thyroid hormones T4 and T3, which are critical for regulating cellular metabolism, neuromuscular development, and growth.1,2 Iodine intake is particularly important for pregnant women, and during the early developmental stages of fetuses and infants, to support normal growth and neurological development.1,3–5 Low-iodine intake is linked to iodine deficiency disorders (IDDs), such as endemic goiter and hypothyroidism.6 The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends a daily intake of 250 µg of iodine for pregnant and breastfeeding women and 150 µg for the general adult population.6 Despite the global implementation of Universal Salt Iodization (USI) as a public health measure to combat iodine deficiency, IDDs remain a global issue.7 Therefore, timely and effective iodine monitoring methods are crucial. The most commonly used population-level monitoring method involves measuring the median urinary iodine concentration (UIC) in random spot urine samples, with a median UIC of over 100 µg/L indicating sufficient iodine nutrition within a population.6,8 According to previous studies, between 100 and 500 random spot urine samples are generally required to achieve a precision range of ± 10% to ± 5% for population or subgroup estimates.9,10

In clinical settings, research often focuses on individuals or small groups. Unfortunately, a definitive standard for evaluating individual iodine status has not been established. Unlike assessing population iodine status, a single spot UIC does not reflect individual iodine status, as iodine is excreted rapidly in the urine, and the UIC value will only reflect iodine intake over the preceding 6 to 8 hours.11,12 Some experts have suggested using repeated spot urine samples collected over more than 10 days for a more accurate individual assessment.13,14 However, this method has not gained widespread utilization. The reliability of using spot urine samples to assess short-term iodine excretion remains debated.15–22 Challenges include day-to-day variability due to factors such as seasonal dietary changes, hydration levels, and differences in urinary output.19,23–26 Ideally, 24-hour (24-h) urinary collections are used as the reference standard.10,14,22,27 However, due to challenges in ensuring compliance, random spot urine samples are more practical and achieve higher success rates. Nonetheless, whether the spot urine-based method can be applied to 24-h urine samples remains a subject of debate.

The urinary iodine-to-creatinine concentration ratio (UIC/Cr) from spot urine samples has been proposed to estimate iodine excretion, as Cr is positively correlated with urinary flow rate.28 A limitation of this method is the variability in the Cr excretion rate, which is influenced by factors such as age, sex, and muscle mass. This issue could be overcome by incorporating variables such as gender, age, weight, height, and body mass index (BMI) into the UIC/Cr ratio.10,13,14,20,27,29–34 This approach involves estimating 24-h urinary Cr (e24-h Cr) excretion and multiplying it by the UIC/Cr ratio to give the estimated 24-h urinary iodine excretion (e24-h UIE). Additionally, predicted iodine intake can be roughly estimated using UIC and body weight, assuming an average 24-h urine production of 1.5 L per day in adults.11,35 This type of estimate has been used in other studies to approximate the actual excretion amount rather than trying to estimate it solely using the unit ratio adjustment in urine.10,13,14,27

Given the limitations of traditional sampling methods, there is a pressing need for low-cost, reliable indicators to monitor short-term iodine excretion in individuals.13,14 The study aimed to empirically evaluate the correlation and agreement between e24-h UIE from spot urine samples and measured 24-h UIE as the reference standard.

2. METHODS

2.1. Subjects and study design

This cross-sectional study enrolled healthy volunteers aged ≥18 years from National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University (NYCU) and Taipei Veterans General Hospital (TVGH), Taiwan, between September 2022 and May 2024. Participants were recruited from a pool of university students, hospital staff, local vendors, and their friends or relatives. All participants were confirmed to be in good overall health, with no history of thyroid disorders that might affect iodine metabolism (such as hyperthyroidism, hypothyroidism, or thyroid cancer) or any known renal dysfunction or kidney disease, as assessed through self-reported medical histories. Exclusion criteria included exposure to iodinated contrast medium within the past month, use of amiodarone and lithium within the past 5 years, or a history of neck surgery or radiotherapy. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment. The study protocol was approved by the local Institutional Review Board of TVGH (IRB No: 2021-11-008BC).

2.2. Data collection

The urine collection procedure was explained in detail to the participants. First, each participant collected a random spot urine sample (10 mL) in a 15-mL tube on the first day at any convenient time. Next, participants collected a complete 24-h urine sample in a 4-L pooled urine bag. Upon completion of the 24-h collection, the sample was mixed gently by shaking the urine bag, and a 10-mL aliquot was transferred to a 15-mL tube. Finally, participants provided a second random spot urine sample on the same day as the completion of the 24-h collection. Participants were instructed to record the exact start and end times of the 24-h collection, as well as the time of each spot urine sample. These records were reviewed by the research team to verify the completeness and reliability of urine collection. Completeness was confirmed by both recorded collection logs and total urine volume. A 24-h urine collection was considered incomplete if the total urine volume was less than 0.5 L.26 Participants were instructed to avoid exposure to iodinated contrast media and sources of excessive dietary iodine during the collection period.

An iodine-specific dietary questionnaire was administered to assess whether participants’ dietary habits reflected relatively high iodine intake. Due to the lack of information regarding iodine content in most food categories in Taiwan’s Nutrition Information Database, data from Hong Kong, where dietary culture is similar, were used.36 Iodine content per unit was obtained from Hong Kong’s dietary iodine intake risk assessment reports. In addition, the Healthy Eating Standards of the Health Promotion Administration, Ministry of Health and Welfare, Taiwan, provided the raw food weight of the edible portion for conversion into serving sizes.37 The questionnaire assessed daily intake of iodine-rich foods, including seaweed, fish, other seafood, eggs, and dairy. Participants indicated if they consumed one or more servings of these foods by checking a box and recording the number of servings. Information regarding the use of iodized salt, iodine-containing multivitamin, and eating-out habits was also collected.

2.3. Urine analysis

Samples were stored at −20°C and thawed at room temperature before analysis. UIC was measured using an Agilent 7700 Series inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) system (©Agilent Technologies Inc., Santa Clara, CA) with a detection limit of 1 μg/L. The alkali dilution method utilizing ammonia solution was applied. Iodine recovery rates were <105%, with intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation of <6% for concentrations >20 μg/L and <7.1% for concentrations ≤20 μg/L. The experimental procedures and validation steps were as previously reported.38 Quality control was ensured by including reference materials from the Ensuring the Quality of Urinary Iodine Procedures (EQUIP) program and RECIPE Co. (Munich, Germany). Urinary Cr levels were measured by the Department of Pathology and Laboratory Medicine at TVGH using a Roche Diagnostics system with a quantitation limit of 4 mg/dL.

2.5. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., NC). Continuous variables were presented as mean ± SD, median with interquartile range (IQR), and minimum–maximum values. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Spot urine each day was treated as a single data point. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of Q–Q plots and box plots. Due to the non-normal distribution of 24-h and spot urinary iodine measures, log transformation was applied. Subsequently, their relationship was assessed using Pearson’s correlation analysis. The intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC) was determined from the ratio of between-individual variance to total observed variance. The independent t test was used to determine whether there was a significant difference between the means of spot and 24-h samples and two sexes. Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical comparisons, while the Mann–Whitney U test and Kruskal–Wallis test were used for two-group comparisons. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to assess the median difference in paired datasets. Passing–Bablok regression was used to assess the relationship between estimated and actual UIE, while Bland–Altman analysis was used to evaluate their agreement. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Based on G*Power calculations, a minimum of 38 paired samples is required to achieve 80% statistical power at a 95% confidence level for preliminary validation. With 23 participants contributing two-spot urine samples each, adequate power was ensured for the planned analyses.

3. RESULTS

3.1. Characteristics of the study population

Twenty-three healthy subjects (9 women and 14 men) with a mean age of 36.4 ± 10.1 (range: 22-67 years old) were enrolled. The characteristics of the studied population are summarized in Table 1. Compared with male participants, females had significantly lower body weight (57.2 ± 13.2 vs 71.6 ± 6.2 kg; p = 0.002) and lower height (158.1 ± 5.1 vs 176.7 ± 6.5 cm; p < 0.001). On average, participants in this study ate out 5.5 days per week. The percentage of subjects that had consumed iodized salt, and a considerable amount of iodine from specific types of iodine-rich foods, were not significantly different among males and females. However, there was a significant difference between sexes in the consumption of iodine-containing multivitamins (p = 0.047).

3.3. Estimated UIE from spot urine samples (n = 44)

As shown in Table 2, the overall median UIC, UIC/Cr, and e24-h UIE from spot urine samples were 107.0 μg/L (IQR: 56.9-161.2), 126.5 μg/g (IQR: 99.0-165.2), and 154.3 μg/d (IQR: 114.2-201.2), respectively. With a mean UIC, the ICCs of two-spot UIC, UIC/Cr, and e24-h UIE were found to be 0.89, 0.57, and 0.68, respectively. The spot urine Cr concentration did not differ significantly between sexes, but males had significantly higher e24-h Cr excretion, similar to the finding in 24-h urine samples. As a result, females exhibited a higher median UIC/Cr ratio compared with males (148.5 [120.4-213.6] vs 118.7 [88.9-157.1]; p = 0.035).

3.4. Comparison of estimated and actual UIE

There was no significant median difference between e24-h UIE and 24-h UIE for all participants (Wilcoxon’s signed-rank test: Z = −0.17, p = 0.863), as well as for males (Z = 0.25, p = 0.797) and females (Z = −0.69, p = 0.487).

Table 3 presents Pearson’s correlation analysis of 24-h and spot urinary iodine measures. There was a positive correlation between e24-h UIE and 24-h UIE (r = 0.44; 95% CI, 0.16-0.65; p = 0.003) and e24-h UIE with 24-h UIC (r = 0.43, p = 0.004), whereas the correlation between spot UIC or spot UIC/Cr and 24-h UIE was weaker (r = 0.36 and 0.37, p = 0.015 and 0.014).

4. DISCUSSION

This study assessed a method for estimating e24-h UIE with 24-h UIE to assess whether the estimated value accurately reflects actual excretion. The results support this expectation, as there were no significant median differences between e24-h UIE and 24-h UIE, and sex was not a determining factor. The spot urine-based estimation using the e24-h UIE method demonstrated a modest correlation with the 24-h UIE method. Both Passing–Bablok regression and Bland–Altman analysis confirmed this agreement, suggesting that e24-h UIE from spot urine samples can serve as an alternative for estimating iodine excretion. Notably, this study does not aim to develop new predictive models, but rather to evaluate the agreement between spot urine-based and 24-h UIE values.

According to commonly used interpretive conventions, a Pearson correlation coefficient (r) between 0.40 and 0.69 is generally categorized as a moderate correlation.41 The magnitude of correlation (Pearson r = 0.44) in this study is not uncommon in nutritional epidemiology, as biomarker correlations are often attenuated by substantial within-person biological and behavioral variability.42 Therefore, the correlation coefficient should be interpreted primarily as an indicator of linear association rather than strict equivalence between methods. This interpretation is consistent with established methodological references in nutritional assessment, where correlation coefficients in the range of approximately 0.5 to 0.6 have been reported when comparing food frequency questionnaire estimates of micronutrient intake with reference dietary measures, with CIs frequently extending into the 0.4 range.43 Such values are generally regarded as acceptable evidence of relative validity rather than analytical precision. The primary evaluation of method comparability in this study was therefore grounded in agreement-based analyses, including Passing–Bablok regression and Bland–Altman plots, which assess systematic bias and limits of agreement beyond correlation metrics.

From a physiological perspective, this level of correlation reflects substantial within-person variability, likely attributable to daily fluctuations in iodine intake, hydration status, and the timing of spot urine collection.22,44 Studies have shown that hydration status and dietary habits can significantly influence UIC measurements.19,39 Additionally, the timing of spot urine collection may affect UIC due to the relatively short excretion window of dietary iodine (mostly excreted within 6-24 hours, with peak urinary excretion occurring 4-8 hours after ingestion).12,25,44 Consequently, spot samples collected at different times of day may capture varying iodine loads, which could impact the comparability and generalizability of results across populations.

While the Cr-adjusted method was developed to reduce this variability, it has inherent limitations due to the influence of sex, age, muscle mass, protein intake, and renal function on Cr excretion.17,20,45–48 According to Ji et al,48 spot urinary Cr concentration also varies significantly across populations and ethnicities. Our study confirmed significant sex differences in Cr excretion, and UIC/Cr was notably influenced by Cr concentration, suggesting that adjusting UIC by Cr alone is insufficient for accurate assessment. Our findings align with those of Knudsen et al,20 who also reported that UIC is preferred over UIC/Cr in iodine status assessments. Although age- and sex-adjusted UIC/Cr improves estimation, they cautioned that extremely low Cr excretion or low Cr concentrations in a single urine sample may invalidate the ratio.20 To address these limitations, we applied the e24-h Cr excretion equation developed by Mage et al,33 which incorporates age, sex, ethnicity, body weight, height, and BMI. This equation, previously validated in other studies,13,27,33 helped reduce bias, as similar mean differences were observed between estimated and measured 24-h UIE.

The Bland–Altman analysis was conducted to quantify the degree of statistical agreement between estimated and measured 24-h UIE, acknowledging that statistical agreement does not necessarily imply clinical equivalence. Although no universally accepted cut-offs exist for defining clinically meaningful differences in UIE, the WHO population criterion for iodine sufficiency (median UIC 100-199 µg/L) roughly corresponds to a 24-h UIE of 180 to 358 µg/d, based on the median 24-hour urine volume of 1.8 L observed in our participants.6 In our study, both the measured and estimated median 24-h UIE values (158.3 and 154.3 µg/d, respectively) were close to the lower boundary of this adequacy range. The mean difference of 6.8 µg/d observed in the Bland–Altman analysis, therefore, represents only approximately 2% to 4% of this adequacy range, which is clinically negligible and unlikely to affect the classification of iodine sufficiency status at the population level.

When compared with previous validation studies in European and Asian populations, our findings are consistent in showing moderate-to-good agreement (r = 0.5-0.7 in prior studies) between estimated and measured 24-h UIE, supporting the utility of the e24-h UIE method for population-level monitoring.14,18,20 With 23 participants contributing two-spot urine samples each, the study exceeds the minimum of 38 paired samples needed to ensure 80% statistical power and 95% CI for preliminary validation. However, the moderate ICC (0.68) between two adjacent-day e24-h UIE measurements indicates notable day-to-day variation. These findings reinforce that a single random spot sample is insufficient for assessing individual iodine status, and that repeated or time-standardized spot samples are necessary to achieve reliable individual-level estimates. This approach may have potential clinical applications, such as monitoring iodine status in patients with hyperthyroidism and in those receiving amiodarone or iodinated contrast agents, and ensuring adherence to low-iodine diets in thyroid cancer patients before radioactive iodine therapy.49–51

Taiwan is an island nation in East Asia, covering a land area of approximately 36,197 km2 with a population of around 23.5 million people.52 The country has a well-developed healthcare system, primarily supported by the National Health Insurance program, which provides universal coverage and is recognized for its efficiency, affordability, and accessibility.53 As of 2017, Taiwan had 69.83 hospital beds per 10 000 people, reflecting a high standard of medical infrastructure.54 This advanced healthcare system ensures that Cr testing is fast, convenient, and accurate, making e24-h UIE a feasible and accessible method. Compared with 24-h pooled urine collection, this approach significantly simplifies data collection and improves its practicality for epidemiological applications. As this is an initial pilot study, further rigorous validation is necessary to substantiate the findings.

Importantly, this study provides one of the first datasets from healthy Taiwanese adults to validate the e24-h UIE method for both sexes, using pooled 24-hour urine samples as the reference standard and contributing region-specific evidence to the global literature. Our findings offer preliminary evidence and a conceptual basis for future research to determine the sampling duration of urine collections needed to generate stable and representative assessments of individual iodine excretion. However, several limitations should be noted. First, the use of a Hong Kong food database, due to the limited availability of iodine composition data for Taiwanese foods, may have introduced bias and highlights the need for region-specific food iodine databases. Second, the influence of meal timing on UIC levels could not be evaluated; thus, future studies using time-standardized or repeated spot urine samples are warranted. Additionally, the relatively small sample size may have reduced the magnitude of the correlation coefficient, and the narrow age range may limit the generalizability of our findings. Future research including children, older adults, pregnant women, iodine-deficient populations, and patients with thyroid disorders is needed to confirm the broader applicability of this method.

In conclusion, this study achieved our primary aim of evaluating the relationship between estimated and actual UIE, quantifying the discrepancy between them, and validating the e24-h Cr excretion equation developed by Mage et al33 within our study population. The e24-h UIE method, which multiplies e24-h Cr excretion by the UIC/Cr ratio from spot urine samples, can serve as a feasible substitute for 24-h UIE and be reasonably applied in future research.
