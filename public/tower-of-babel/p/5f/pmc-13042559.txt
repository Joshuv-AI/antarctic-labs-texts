Chronic methanol exposure induces cognitive impairment and Alzheimer's‐like pathology in rhesus monkeys
Authors: Hongwei Li, Changhua Shi, Keya Li, Xinjing Fu, Ying Lyu, Yanfeng Xu, Yunlin Han, Wei Liang, Qin Chuan, Ling Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe absence of effective animal models for sporadic Alzheimer's disease (AD) remains a pivotal barrier to therapy development. Because methanol metabolism produces endogenous formaldehyde, a neurotoxic agent linked to cognitive decline, this study investigated whether chronic, low‐dose methanol exposure could recapitulate AD‐like pathology and cognitive deficits in rhesus monkey, thereby establishing a nonhuman primate animal model driven by this environmental‐metabolic insult.MethodsAdult rhesus monkeys received low‐concentration methanol for 9 months. Behavioral tests for cognition, locomotion, sleep, and vision were conducted. Postmortem analyses involved histopathological examination, immunohistochemistry, immunofluorescence, and Western blot to evaluate neuronal integrity, microglial activation, and the expression of key proteins associated with AD (amyloid‐β [Aβ], phosphorylated tau, TAR DNA‐binding protein 43 [TDP‐43]) and cellular stress (synaptic markers, mitochondrial fission, autophagy, and apoptosis‐related proteins).ResultsChronic methanol exposure led to progressive cognitive and memory impairment without significant motor or visual deficits. Neuropathology revealed brain atrophy, neuronal loss, synaptic damage, microglial activation, and mitochondrial structural disorganization. Critically, the exposed animals exhibited hallmark AD‐like molecular alterations, including increased Aβ deposition, tau hyperphosphorylation, and TDP‐43 dysregulation. Furthermore, neurotoxicity was associated with elevated urinary formaldehyde, enhanced mitochondrial fission, increased autophagy, and elevated apoptosis.ConclusionChronic low‐dose methanol exposure in rhesus monkeys recapitulates progressive cognitive deficits and AD‐like neuropathological features. This model, driven by endogenous formaldehyde toxicity, effectively mimics key aspects of sporadic AD. Our findings shed light on the neurotoxic mechanisms of methanol and propose a reproducible and translationally relevant nonhuman primate model for studying AD pathogenesis and evaluating potential therapeutics.

INTRODUCTION

Methanol is a structurally simple single‐carbon alcohol. High‐concentration exposure can lead to metabolic acidosis, visual impairment, nervous system dysfunction, and in severe cases death. 1 , 2 , 3 Studies have reported that the metabolism of methanol in primates mainly involves three steps. The first step is the oxidation of methanol to formaldehyde, primarily catalyzed by alcohol dehydrogenase. The second step is the oxidation of formaldehyde to formic acid, 4 which is specifically catalyzed by glutathione‐dependent formaldehyde dehydrogenase in the cytoplasm; in mitochondria, it is catalyzed by nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD)‐dependent formaldehyde dehydrogenase. The third step is the oxidation of formic acid to CO2, catalyzed by 10‐formyltetrahydrofolate dehydrogenase (a widely occurring enzyme in mammals). A research study 5 suggests that the main damaging agent in the brain is methanol's intermediate metabolite—formaldehyde. Literature reports that low‐concentration methanol exposure in rhesus monkeys leads to memory impairments and increased levels of amyloid‐β (Aβ) and phosphorylated tau proteins. 6

Cognitive impairment is one of the most challenging diseases to diagnose and treat in brain disorders. Due to the rapid aging of the global population, cognitive impairment has become a significant challenge worldwide. 7 Among the challenging diseases, Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the most common cognitive impairment disorder in the elderly. 8 , 9 Its main pathological features include the deposition of Aβ forming senile plaques in the extracellular space and the hyperphosphorylation of tau proteins forming neurofibrillary tangles (NFT) inside cells, 10 , 11 accompanied by the reduction in neurons, loss of neural synapses, failure of neurotransmitter pathways (especially the basal forebrain cholinergic system), proliferation of glial cells, and TAR DNA‐binding protein 43 (TDP‐43) proteinopathy. 12 , 13 Although research on AD has been ongoing for over a century, there is still a lack of effective treatment medications and preventive methods for AD worldwide. All AD treatment drugs and vaccines approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration can only temporarily improve cognitive functions in some patients. 14 This issue is directly related to the lack of effective sporadic animal models in the development of AD‐related drugs and vaccines. Predominantly, the commonly used animal models are transgenic animal models, which, through genetic modification, can simulate only familial AD associated with genetic mutations. These models cannot simulate the causes and mechanisms of sporadic AD, 15 which accounts for the vast majority (95%) of cases.

This article aims to systematically study the effects of long‐term exposure to low doses of methanol on the cognitive behavior, motor skills, sleep, and vision of rhesus monkeys and to explore the underlying mechanisms preliminarily. This is to further determine the damage caused by long‐term low‐dose methanol exposure to the nervous system and to verify its potential as an AD model, to provide a theoretical basis for the development of effective AD models.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Laboratory animals

Twelve male rhesus monkeys, aged 3–6 years, weighing between 3 and 6 kg, and subjected to pathogen quarantine, were used in this experiment. These animals were provided by the Non‐Rodent Research Base/Primate Research Center of the Institute of Medical Experimental Animals, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences. The use of animals in this experiment was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Institute of Medical Experimental Animals, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences, under approval number QC17005. Animals were housed in individual cages (80 × 95 × 140 cm). The room temperature was maintained at 22°C with a relative humidity of 40%–70%. Lighting was provided from 8:00 a.m. to 8:00 p.m. The diet consisted of a specialized growth feed for macaques, administered thrice daily, with apples supplemented at noon. Water was available ad libitum, either freely or via water bottles. All procedures involving the animals were strictly conducted in accordance with the Chinese National Animal Research Authority laboratory animal welfare and ethical guidelines.

Main reagents and primary antibodies

The main reagents used were as follows: methanol (Beijing Chemical Plant, China), ketamine, sodium pentobarbital (Guangzhou Blue Island Company, China), RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime Company, China), nuclear and cytoplasmic protein extraction kits (Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China), PAS staining kit (Solarbio Company, China), sodium dodecyl sulfate, Tris–HCl, ammonium persulfate (Sigma Company, USA), and DAPI (Beijing Biod Biotechnology Co., Ltd, China).

Animal experiments

Grouping and experimental arrangement of rhesus monkeys

The experimental animals were divided into two groups, namely the test group (test) and the control group (control), with six monkeys in each group (Figure 1A). Both the test and control groups underwent cognitive behavioral training simultaneously. When all the animals reached a 90% correct response rate or the prescribed number of training sessions (1000), formal intervention began. After the experimental group intervention started, drinking water in the water bottles was replaced with 3% methanol solution, which was freely available. The control group continued to receive ordinary drinking water ad libitum. Regular behavioral tests were conducted on animals from the different treatment groups, and samples including blood, cerebrospinal fluid, feces, and urine were collected; the animals were dissected at different times for further analysis (Figure 1B).

Based on the experimental testing time and content, we further grouped them. Unless otherwise specified, the groups were organized as follows: control group (control), test‐3m group (exposed to methanol for 3 months), and test‐9m (exposed to methanol for 9 months) (Figure 1A).

Variable spatial delayed response task

This experiment tests the cognitive and memory abilities of the monkeys by observing the time and accuracy of their food retrieval actions after various delay intervals. During the experiment, the monkeys were placed in a testing cage in a quiet environment. The cage had a sliding door that can move up and down, separating the monkeys from the test food boxes. When the testing door was raised (opened), the food box was pushed in front of the monkey. When the door was lowered (closed), the food box was withdrawn from the monkey's view. This experiment involved three identical food boxes, spaced 10 cm apart, and located 8 cm from the nearest point of the testing cage. When the accuracy rate reached 27/30, testing records were taken over four consecutive days, documenting 120 test scores (accuracy rate) as the initial scores.

Spatial recognition conversion task

This experiment evaluates the monkeys' cognitive ability to recognize directions by observing their actions in retrieving food from different locations. During the test, the monkeys are placed in a testing cage in a quiet environment. The testing cage, which we designed based on the literature, 6 has a sliding door that can move up and down, separating the monkeys from the test food tray. When the testing door is raised (opened), the food tray is positioned in front of the monkeys. When the door is lowered (closed), the food tray is removed from the monkeys' view. This experiment involves two food trays, easily identifiable as red and metallic, spaced 20 cm apart on either side, and located 8 cm from the nearest point of the testing cage. When 1000 tests are completed, the animals' test scores (accuracy rate) are recorded as the initial scores. Each food retrieval operation by the monkeys has a maximum allowable time of 120 s, timed from when the food tray moves in front of the test cage to the first attempt of the monkey to reach for the food.

Object recognition test

This experiment tests the monkeys' cognitive ability to recognize different objects by color/shape during food retrieval. During the test, the monkeys are placed in a testing cage in a quiet environment. The cage has a sliding door that can move up and down, separating the monkeys from the test food tray. When the testing door is raised (opened), the food tray is pushed in front of the monkeys. When the door is lowered (closed), the food tray is completely removed from the monkeys' view. This experiment involves four food trays, located 8 cm from the closest point of the testing cage. During the early training phase, only one suitable color is changed inside which reward food is placed; later, the difficulty is increased by using lids of four different shapes, with red circular ones containing food (as shown in Figure 1C). Using test phase steps for training, when the animals' accuracy rate reaches 27/30, the formal testing begins. Continuous testing occurs over 4 days, and the average accuracy rate out of 120 tests is calculated.

Object retrieval test

This experiment employs a cubic box, transparent on all sides except the bottom. The box has an opening that can be oriented differently during the experiment, and the depth at which the reward food is placed inside varies, allowing the animals to observe, discern, and then reach for the reward food. The retrieval tasks are divided into difficult (hard task) and easy (easy task) levels based on the direction of the box opening and the food placement. The experiment evaluates the animals' motor skills and cognitive abilities based on their recognition of direction and position.

Viewpoint motion activity test

This experiment uses the Viewpoint motion testing system 16 , 17 to record the animals' movement trajectories, frequency, and timing, which are analyzed to assess the animals' activity levels and monitor their sleep times. A scale is defined using the ruler tool in the toolbar above the window to mark the width of the observation area, with the actual width of the observation cage (145 × 115 × 90 cm) entered in the dialog box that appears. Top activity recording settings are adjustable: sensitivity set to 25, burst threshold set to 200, and freezing threshold set to 5. In the side motion recording settings, high detection threshold is set at 128, low detection threshold at 120, large movement threshold at 30, slight movement threshold at 10, and tension threshold at 0. The experimental parameters are configured within the menu bar. The motor test is set to last for 30 min, whereas sleep monitoring is scheduled from 9:00 p.m. to 5:00 a.m. It is essential to avoid any disturbances throughout these procedures.

Formaldehyde detection in rhesus monkey urine

Animals were fasted, and urine was collected in the morning. At least 10 min before the lights were turned on, a sterile film was spread over the base tray placed at the bottom of the breeding cage. Formaldehyde in urine was derivatized to 2,4‐dinitrophenylhydrazone with 2,4‐dinitrophenylhydrazine, and the mixture was then measured using a high‐performance liquid chromatography‐diode array detector (HPLC/DAD, Agilent1200), following the method and analysis of the national standard GBT2912.3‐2009. 18 The detailed method is as follows: 0.05 g of the derivatizing agent 2,4‐dinitrophenylhydrazine was weighed, dissolved in ~50 mL of 0.5% acetic acid in acetonitrile, and diluted to 100 mL with ultrapure water; 0.5 mL of standard solution or urine was taken in a 2‐mL vial, 1 mL of the derivatizing agent was added to it, and the mixture was vortexed and subjected to reaction at 60°C for 30 min before testing. The liquid chromatography conditions are as follows: analytical column: InerSustain C18 column (5 μm, 250 × 4.6 mm), mobile phase: acetonitrile + water (65 + 35), flow rate: 1 mL/min, column temperature: 30°C, detection wavelength: 355 nm, and injection volume: 20 μL. Formaldehyde quantification uses the external standard method, against the standard working curve, to calculate the formaldehyde content in urine.

Dissection and pathological examination of rhesus monkeys

The selected animals were fasted and sedated with ketamine (15 mg/kg) intramuscularly. Weight was recorded. After the animals were anesthetized, their chip numbers were scanned and verified. Sodium pentobarbital (0.5 mL/kg) was slowly injected intravenously. The fresh brain tissues were collected. The left brain was frozen in isopentane at −50°C in a freezing system and then stored at −80°C for immunoblotting to detect brain tissue protein expression.

The right brain was fixed in formalin and 2.5% glutaraldehyde fixative. The formalin‐fixed tissues were used for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, immunohistochemistry (IHC), and immunofluorescence staining, whereas the glutaraldehyde‐fixed tissues were used for transmission electron microscopy to examine the ultrastructure of the tissues. Fresh specimens were used for immunoblotting to analyze protein expression in brain tissues.

Statistical analysis

All data were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software, version 22.0. Results were analyzed using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA); t‐test was used to determine if the differences between groups were statistically significant, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

No abnormal sleep–wake time or significant optic nerve damage was detected in rhesus monkeys exposed to methanol

The viewpoint test assessed the impact of chronic consumption of low‐concentration methanol solution on nocturnal awakening times in rhesus monkeys, thereby reflecting its effect on sleep duration. Figure 1C,D shows that no significant differences were detected in the nocturnal awakening times between the experimental and control groups, either before or after methanol consumption at various testing intervals. The awakening times before methanol consumption and at 2, 4, 6, and 8 months after consumption were not statistically significant (p > 0.05). Although there was a trend of gradually decreasing awakening times in the control group as the number of tests increased, this trend was not observed in the experimental group. Compared to before methanol consumption, the experimental animals exhibited varying degrees of reduction in nocturnal awakening times after consuming methanol, but these differences were not statistically significant (p > 0.05). Figure 1E shows the histological morphology of the retina and optic nerve. In both the experimental and control groups, all the retinal layers exhibited clear structures, with no significant changes observed in the experimental group. However, the optic nerves of animals in the experimental group exhibit mild edema, slightly disorganized cell arrangement, and mild disruption in the neurofibrillary architecture.

The formaldehyde content in urine can reflect the endogenous formaldehyde content. In this study, the formaldehyde content in rhesus monkey urine was detected using high‐performance liquid chromatography. Figure 1F shows that in the 3 months before methanol administration, the formaldehyde content in urine was comparable to that of the control group. However, starting from the first month of methanol administration, a significant difference from the control group emerged, exhibiting a continuous increasing trend. Compared to the pre‐methanol period, the formaldehyde content in urine also significantly increased (p < 0.001).

Detected cognitive impairment in rhesus monkeys exposed to methanol

The object recognition test (ORT) revealed the impact on cognitive abilities of rhesus monkeys after they consumed a low‐concentration methanol solution. Figure 2A details the operational process during the early training stage of the ORT on the experimental animals. Figure 2B,C provides a detailed description of the operational process during the later training stage and the formal testing stage of the ORT. For detailed operations, refer to Section 2.3.2. Figure 2F shows that compared to the control group, the experimental group exhibited a decline in the accuracy rate of correctly retrieving the physical reward upon first opening the food box after methanol exposure, but no statistical difference was observed at 2 and 4 months, with p‐values of 0.4169 and 0.1161, respectively. However, statistically significant differences emerged starting from the sixth month, with p‐values of 0.0249 and 0.0338. Figure 2G shows that when the accuracy rates of the experimental group of rhesus monkeys before and after methanol consumption were compared, no significant difference was observed at 2 months, p = 0.132; however, starting from the fourth month of methanol consumption, a continuous decline in accuracy was observed, showing statistically significant differences (p < 0.05).

The effect on the memory capabilities of rhesus monkeys was assessed using the variable spatial delayed response task (VSDRT) after they consumed a low‐concentration methanol solution. Figure 2D,E shows that there was a significant decrease in the number of times the monkeys successfully opened the food box and retrieved the reward on the first attempt, which worsened with the duration of methanol exposure. Compared to the control group, there was no significant difference in the number of correct attempts before methanol administration. However, the average number of successful attempts significantly decreased (p = 0.005) 2 months after the start of methanol consumption. As the duration of methanol exposure increased, the average number of times the experimental group successfully retrieved the food reward continued to decrease, showing significant differences at 4, 6, and 8 months of methanol feeding (p < 0.05). A comparison of the experimental group's successful attempts before methanol exposure shows that a statistically significant decrease in successful attempts also started from the second month of exposure (p < 0.05).

The spatial recognition conversion task is an experiment designed to assess the spatial orientation recognition and cognitive abilities of rhesus monkeys. In this task, reward food is placed in food boxes positioned at specific spatial locations (left/right), with the position of the food boxes fixed and only the color or shape of the food box lids being changed or unchanged to test the monkeys' ability to retrieve food. Due to large individual differences in experimental results, with accuracy rates ranging from 14% to 80%, this experiment was discontinued in subsequent research. However, the extensive training duration of 1000 trials served to enhance the cognitive learning abilities of the rhesus monkeys.

The object retrieval (OR) experiment detected the effects on cognitive abilities of rhesus monkeys after chronic consumption of low‐concentration methanol. Figure 2H,I shows that compared to the control group of rhesus monkeys, the experimental group exhibited a decline in the success rate of first‐time correct retrieval of physical rewards in the difficult tasks of the OR experiment. Although there was a slight decrease in the first month, it was not statistically significant (p > 0.05). From the second month onward, statistically significant differences were observed, continuing throughout the subsequent methanol consumption periods. Comparisons between the experimental and control groups from the second to the ninth month were all significant (p < 0.05).

When the first‐time success rates of the experimental group of rhesus monkeys before and after methanol consumption was compared, no significant differences were observed in the first and second months, with p‐values of 0.800 and 0.203, respectively. However, starting from the third month of methanol use, there was a continuous decline in success rates, with significant differences compared to before methanol consumption in all months except the fifth (p < 0.05).

The viewpoint test revealed the impact on the movement distances of rhesus monkeys after chronic consumption of low‐concentration methanol. Figure 2J,K shows that there was no significant difference in the initial activity levels between the experimental and control groups (p = 0.300). After methanol consumption, the experimental group exhibited a gradual increase in movement distance; by the sixth month, a significant difference was observed compared to the control group (p = 0.034). However, no significant differences were observed in the tests conducted in the eighth and ninth months (p > 0.05). When the activity levels of the experimental group of rhesus monkeys before and after methanol consumption were compared, the distance covered in each test also gradually increased, with statistically significant differences emerging from the fourth month of methanol consumption (p < 0.05). The differences were more significant in the tests conducted in the sixth and eighth months (p < 0.05). Particularly, during the test conducted in the ninth month of methanol consumption, the movement distance of the experimental group decreased and showed no significant difference compared to that before consumption (p > 0.05). Figure 2L,M shows the movement paths of the methanol‐consuming rhesus monkeys before and up to the eighth month of methanol feeding, with significantly more activity in the eighth month compared to that before methanol consumption.

During the dissection of the experimental animals, their brain specimens were observed. Compared to the control group, the brain tissues of the experimental group were approximately symmetrical in both hemispheres. However, there was a slight deepening of the cerebral sulci visible on the sagittal plane, indicating mild atrophic changes, as shown in Figure 3A.

H&E staining utilizes the principle by which hematoxylin stains the basophilic nuclei bluish purple and eosin stains the acidophilic cytoplasm pink. This method clearly delineates the morphology of tissues and cells, allowing for the assessment of their physiological and pathological states from the structural and morphological aspects. Figure 3B,C shows that H&E staining results reveal that the brain tissues of the control group have distinct brain region structures with regular cell arrangement. In contrast, in the experimental group, the neuronal arrangement in the hippocampal region of rhesus monkeys is disordered and the shapes are irregular; there is a significant reduction in neuron numbers across different brain regions (hippocampus, frontal lobe, temporal lobe) (p < 0.05).

Applying immunohistochemical methods, the neuronal marker NeuN more clearly demonstrated the distribution and number of neurons in different brain regions of the experimental and control groups, with the experimental group showing disordered arrangement and a significant reduction in neuronal numbers (Figure 3D). Figure 3E,F shows that the Western blot analysis expression of ionized calcium‐binding adapter molecule 1 (IBA1) in the brain tissue of rhesus monkeys significantly increased with prolonged exposure to methanol, which represents an increase in microglial cell proliferation.

Toxic and erroneous proteins were detected in the brain tissue of rhesus monkeys exposed to methanol

Typical pathological features of AD include extracellular deposition of Aβ forming amyloid plaques, overexpression of abnormally phosphorylated tau protein, and formation of NFTs. Therefore, this study utilized IHC to stain and analyze the brain tissues of experimental rhesus monkeys, assessing the expression and localization of Aβ and tau proteins. Figure 4A shows that Aβ42 expression in the hippocampus and cortex was detected in the experimental group but was not detected in the control group. Figure 4B–E shows the immunohistochemical expression of 6E10, T231, S262, and TDP‐43 in the hippocampus, frontal lobe, and temporal lobe by comparing the experimental group to the control group. The expression of 6E10 was higher in the experimental group than in the control group. The expression levels of tau protein at different phosphorylation sites (T231, S262) were also higher in the experimental group. TDP‐43, a protein associated with various neurodegenerative diseases, exhibited significant differences in expression between the experimental and control groups. Figure 4H shows the immunofluorescence expression and colocalization of microtubule‐associated protein 2 (MAP2) and TDP‐43 in the brain tissues, as well as a comparison of their expression levels. Figure 4I shows the expression and colocalization of GFAP and TDP‐43 in the brain tissues, along with a comparison of their expression levels. It is evident that the expression level of TDP‐43 in the experimental group of rhesus monkeys increased.

Mitochondrial and synaptic damage was detected in the brain tissue of rhesus monkeys exposed to methanol

Using transmission electron microscopy to observe ultrastructural tissue allows for a more microscopic evaluation of tissue damage (Figure 5A). Figure 5B shows that a comparison of the number of synapses in different brain regions (hippocampus, frontal lobe, temporal lobe) reveals a statistically significant reduction in synaptic numbers in all brain regions of the experimental group compared to the control group (p < 0.05). Furthermore, the length of synapses in the brains of the experimental group of rhesus monkeys was significantly shortened. Figure 5C shows the use of Western blotting to detect the expression of postsynaptic density protein 95 (PSD95), a densin protein in the postsynaptic membrane, which represents the amount of synaptic content. This also confirms a significant reduction in synaptic numbers in the experimental group compared to the control group, with the expression of PSD95 gradually decreasing as the duration of methanol exposure increased. Compared to the control group, significant reductions in PSD95 expression were observed in the experimental monkeys after 3 months of methanol exposure (p = 0.01); after 9 months of methanol exposure, the differences in PSD95 expression in the frontal lobe of the experimental group were even more significant (p < 0.001).

Transmission electron microscopy also revealed significant mitochondrial damage in the brain tissues of the experimental group of rhesus monkeys (Figure 5D). In the control group, the mitochondria had clear double‐membrane structures with rich and regularly arranged cristae, whereas in the experimental group, the mitochondria were swollen, with sparse or even absent cristae, reduced electron density in the matrix between cristae, and vacuolization. Figure 5E shows the myelin structures in the brain tissues of the experimental and control groups. In the control group, the myelin structures were clear and regularly arranged in neat bundles; in contrast, the experimental group exhibited local disintegration of myelin, with a loose arrangement and disordered structure.

To explore the mechanism of mitochondrial damage caused by methanol/endogenous formaldehyde, we conducted Western blot experiments to detect the expression of mitochondrial fission‐related proteins MID49, MID51, and DRP1 in the frontal lobe. Figure 5F–H shows that the expression levels of MID49, MID51, and DRP1 increased to varying extents compared to the control group, with significant differences (p < 0.05).

Apoptosis and enhanced autophagy were detected in the brain tissue of rhesus monkeys exposed to methanol

DISCUSSION

In the elderly population, the most common cognitive impairment disease is AD. Currently, all drugs targeted at treating AD, although effective in the early stages in animal models, have shown unsatisfactory results in the later stages of clinical trials 15 ; which animal model can truly reflect the pathogenesis of AD and evaluate the effectiveness of drugs that can be applied in clinical practice? The most commonly used model in research is the transgenic mouse model, which, despite having various transgenic types, can simulate only familial AD related to gene mutations and cannot mimic the etiology and pathogenesis of sporadic AD. 19 Moreover, due to evolutionary differences, mice struggle to replicate all the behavioral and pathological characteristics of AD. 20 In our study, we chose nonhuman primates, 21 specifically rhesus macaques, as experimental subjects. These primates exhibit higher similarity to humans in terms of neuroanatomy, neurobiological characteristics, and physiological metabolism, and possess similar higher cognitive functions. 22 , 23 Using rhesus macaques in comparative medical research yields better simulation effects. 24 Our experimental results demonstrate that long‐term consumption of low concentrations of methanol can lead to cognitive impairment of behavioral and pathological characteristics in rhesus macaques.

Cognitive detection and its relationship with methanol itself and endogenous formaldehyde

Exogenous formaldehyde is ubiquitous, permeating all aspects of our lives, involving our diet, clothing, living quarters, and means of transportation, due to its known mutagenic and carcinogenic properties, particularly its ability to damage DNA and have adverse effects on human health. Although the negative impact of exogenous formaldehyde on human health is widely studied, current research largely overlooks the potentially harmful effects of internally produced formaldehyde on the brain. 25 Due to dysregulation of the expression and activity of enzymes involved in formaldehyde metabolism, endogenous formaldehyde gradually accumulates in the aging brain. Surprisingly, excessive formaldehyde is closely associated with the development of neurodegenerative diseases such as AD and Parkinson's disease. 26 , 27 Methanol is a toxic substance that produces endogenous formaldehyde after metabolism in the body. Studies suggest that endogenous formaldehyde may lead to cognitive impairments. 28

Currently, it is believed that the main clinical manifestation of patients with AD is cognitive impairment, primarily characterized by a decline in memory, which worsens progressively. 29 , 30 The behavioral results of this study show that the experimental group of rhesus monkeys exhibited a significant decrease in memory ability after long‐term chronic consumption of low concentrations of methanol, with a decline appearing as early as 2 months and worsening as the duration of methanol exposure increased. The degree of memory decline closely matched the results reported in the literature and the time course of memory decline. 31 , 32 ORT results also confirmed the decline in cognitive ability. The rate of successful first food retrieval gradually decreased in the experimental group of rhesus monkeys, whereas the control group maintained a level of all correct retrievals. Compared to the control group, although significant differences began to appear only after 6 months of methanol exposure, self‐comparisons before exposure showed a significant decrease in cognitive ability after 4 months of methanol exposure. Therefore, both cross‐sectional and longitudinal studies have demonstrated the formation of cognitive impairment. In the OR experiment, the rate of successful first food retrieval significantly decreased, further confirming the formation of cognitive impairment in the experimental group of rhesus monkeys. After 2 months of methanol exposure, the experimental group of rhesus monkeys exhibited a significantly lower rate of successful first food retrieval in completing difficult tasks compared to the control group. In conclusion, chronic exposure to low concentrations of methanol can lead to cognitive dysfunction and a decline in memory ability in rhesus monkeys.

We analyzed the levels of formaldehyde in the urine of rhesus monkeys using high‐performance liquid chromatography and found that compared to the control group and the pre‐methanol exposure group, the experimental group of rhesus monkeys exhibited a significant increase in formaldehyde content in urine, with a trend positively correlated with the duration of methanol exposure. The experimental results are consistent with the characteristic increase in formaldehyde levels in the urine of AD patients reported in the literature 26 compared to normal controls. The results also suggest that methanol exposure does increase the levels of formaldehyde in the body and that it accumulates with increasing exposure time. Studies 33 have shown that treatment of mouse neuroblastoma N2a cells with the same concentration of methanol and its metabolic products formaldehyde and formic acid resulted in a much higher expression of phosphorylated tau protein (T181) induced by formaldehyde compared to the other two groups and the blank control group, indicating that formaldehyde, a metabolite of methanol, is the main substance responsible for the phosphorylation of tau protein.

Possible mechanisms of cognitive impairment

Specific radiological findings in head computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging of AD patients show significant cortical atrophy, particularly in the hippocampus and medial temporal lobe. 34 Our results also support changes in brain atrophy. Pathological examination revealed mild atrophy with deepening brain sulci in the midsagittal plane of the experimental group of rhesus monkeys, corresponding to subsequent findings of neuronal loss. Immunohistochemical analysis identified Aβ42‐positive plaques in the brains of the experimental group of rhesus monkeys, a key pathological feature in AD patients. However, unlike in humans, the rhesus monkeys exposed to methanol had Aβ‐positive plaques concentrated more in the frontal and temporal lobes and relatively less in the hippocampal region compared to the first two brain regions, similar to the species differences mentioned in the literature. 35 , 36 Immunoblotting results revealed that the levels of Aβ in the brain increased with prolonged exposure to methanol. Additionally, we measured the levels of phosphorylated tau protein at different sites (T231, T181), although the result does not show a significant difference, which exhibits a positive correlation trend with the duration of methanol consumption, which consistently showed a significant increase in phosphorylated tau protein content in the brains of the experimental group of rhesus monkeys, as demonstrated using IHC. These experimental results indicate that chronic consumption of low concentrations of methanol by rhesus monkeys led to two major pathological features of AD: an increase in Aβ‐positive plaques and elevated levels of phosphorylated tau protein.

Studies have shown that in the early stages of AD, elevated phosphorylated tau levels may not be accompanied by changes in total tau, reflecting alterations in kinase/phosphatase activity rather than excessive tau production. 37 In contrast, the late stages are often characterized by decreased total tau alongside elevated p‐tau, suggesting impaired clearance or synthesis associated with aggregation. 38 The phenomenon of stable total tau with increasing p‐tau indicates that dysregulation of phosphorylation homeostasis is the primary mechanism rather than tau overexpression. A limitation of our study lies in the insufficient exploration of total tau protein expression, which is an area we plan to address in future research to further enhance the validity of these findings in studying the pathogenesis of early AD.

Neuronal loss and synaptic loss are also crucial pathological features of AD. 39 In this study, immunofluorescence analysis revealed a significant decrease in the expression of MAP2 in the experimental group compared to the control group, indicating a reduction in neuronal numbers and suggesting potential damage to microtubule structures due to methanol exposure. Transmission electron microscopy observations of the brain's ultrastructure showed a significant decrease in synaptic numbers in different brain regions (hippocampus, frontal lobe, temporal lobe) in rhesus monkeys of the methanol‐exposed group compared to the control group. Immunoblotting of PSD95 revealed a significant reduction in expression in the methanol‐exposed group, with the degree of reduction increasing with prolonged exposure, indicating a damaging effect of methanol/endogenous formaldehyde on neuronal synapses. Further observations showed that the length of synapses in the experimental group of rhesus monkeys was significantly shortened compared to the control group, potentially exacerbating functional loss and ultimately leading to a decline in learning and memory abilities, resulting in cognitive impairment.

Moreover, additional immunoblotting analysis found a significant increase in the expression of IBA1, a marker for microglial cells. Immunofluorescence results indicated a significant proliferation of microglial cells in the 9‐month experimental group of rhesus monkeys compared to the control group, suggesting that chronic methanol exposure may lead to an inflammatory response in the body. The activation of microglial cells may enhance their excessive pruning of synaptic structures, ultimately leading to neuronal damage and loss. 40 , 41

Transmission electron microscopy observations revealed significant damage to mitochondrial structures and a decrease in mitochondrial numbers in the brain tissues of rhesus monkeys from the methanol‐exposed group across various brain regions. Additionally, the experimental group of rhesus monkeys exhibited more pronounced myelin sheath damage compared to the control group. Studies 42 suggest that endogenous formaldehyde can react with many biological molecules such as amino acids, nucleotides, and proteins, forming chemically unstable compounds and irreversible DNA‐protein cross‐links, which may contribute to mitochondrial and myelin damage as biochemical factors. The reduction in mitochondrial numbers may be attributed to mitochondrial damage leading to degradation, possibly triggering increased autophagic clearance. To further validate our hypothesis, we used immunoblotting to examine the expression of mitochondrial dynamic‐related proteins MID49, MID51, and DRP1 in rhesus monkey brain tissues, revealing a significant increase in expression in the experimental group compared to the control group, indicating that endogenous formaldehyde may stimulate excessive mitochondrial fission, accelerating damage and apoptosis. Manczak et al. 43 investigated the brain tissues of AD patients and APP/PS1, 3×Tg‐AD mice cortex, and found that DRP1 interacts with Aβ and phosphorylated tau protein, leading to increased mitochondrial fragmentation, synaptic defects, neuronal damage, and cognitive decline. Examination of the expression levels of autophagy‐related proteins LAMP1 showed a significant increase in the experimental group compared to the control group, suggesting that the toxic effects of methanol/endogenous formaldehyde upregulate the body's autophagic levels, possibly as a protective feedback mechanism. Excessive autophagy may worsen mitochondrial loss and mitochondrial dysfunction, activate caspase‐3, and ultimately lead to neuronal apoptosis.

Overexpression of TDP‐43 protein but no motor function impairment

In the viewpoint motion testing system, we found that rhesus monkeys in the chronic low‐dose methanol exposure experimental group did not exhibit significant gross motor impairments. This behavioral observation presents an intriguing contrast to the significant overexpression of TDP‐43 observed in brain tissues. TDP‐43 is a multifunctional RNA‐binding protein that plays a central role in RNA transcription, splicing, and transport. 44 , 45 Its pathological cytoplasmic aggregation into inclusion bodies is a hallmark feature of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) and most cases of frontotemporal lobar degeneration (FTLD), typically closely associated with motor dysfunction. 46 , 47

However, in this study, we observed an atypical pattern of TDP‐43 pathological alteration: Western blot analysis showed significantly elevated protein levels, but immunohistochemical analysis did not reveal the characteristic dense cytoplasmic inclusions seen in ALS/FTLD. This phenotype, lacking classic inclusions, is consistent with the preservation of motor function in the experimental animals, suggesting that methanol−/formaldehyde‐induced TDP‐43 abnormalities may represent an early stage of pathological development.

This pattern of altered TDP‐43 expression is highly relevant to the pathological features of AD. A growing body of neuropathological research indicates that TDP‐43 abnormalities are prevalent in AD patients, with an incidence as high as 20%–57%, and are associated with more severe cognitive decline and faster disease progression. 48 , 49 Importantly, TDP‐43 pathology in AD often differs from that in FTLD–TDP. In AD, particularly in the early stages of the disease, TDP‐43 abnormalities may manifest as increased nuclear clearance, enhanced fine granular cytoplasmic immunoreactivity, or the formation of pre‐inclusion oligomers, rather than mature, dense inclusions. 50 This pathological pattern has now been formally defined as limbic‐predominant age‐related TDP‐43 encephalopathy, which frequently coexists with AD pathology and exacerbates cognitive impairment. 51 The phenomenon of “overexpression without inclusions” observed in methanol‐exposed monkeys may precisely model this early stage of AD‐associated TDP‐43 abnormality.

Absence of abnormal duration of wakefulness

An increasing number of studies have found a close association between sleep disorders and AD. 52 Sleep disturbances and circadian rhythm disruption may be the contributing factors to the onset of AD, whereas AD can exacerbate stress and circadian rhythm disruptions, forming a vicious cycle that perpetuates and worsens AD. 53 However, in this study, we only superficially collected data on wake time during sleep and did not observe any abnormal wake time in the experimental group of rhesus monkeys. When this data was compared to the baseline data before exposure, there was a slight decrease in wake time during sleep in the experimental group, but the difference was not statistically significant. Further in‐depth research is needed to explore the effects of chronic methanol exposure on sleep.

Absence of visual impairment

It is worth noting that previous studies on methanol toxicity have mainly focused on acute exposure, which can cause severe damage to vision. 54 In this study, the experimental group of rhesus monkeys exhibited a correct rate of over 90% in the VSDRT experiment with a delay time of 0 s, similar to pre‐exposure levels, indicating no significant impact on vision. Additionally, in the ORT, the monkeys were able to identify objects based on shape for food retrieval, demonstrating that their vision was not visibly affected. Therefore, no apparent visual impairment was observed with low‐concentration methanol exposure. Pathological histology examination revealed that the retinal layers of the experimental group of rhesus monkeys were clear and intact, with no significant differences compared to the control group. However, mild edematous changes were observed in the optic nerve, indicating slight damage. Combining these results with behavioral tests suggests that there have been no significant changes in visual acuity.

In summary, chronic low‐dose methanol exposure induces progressive cognitive impairment and behavioral abnormalities in rhesus monkeys, accompanied by typical AD‐like pathological changes, including increased Aβ deposition, tau hyperphosphorylation, TDP‐43 aggregation, neuronal and synaptic loss, glial activation, and mitochondrial structural damage. Moreover, the upregulation of autophagy‐ and apoptosis‐related proteins suggests that methanol may disrupt neuronal homeostasis through multiple degenerative signaling pathways. This study systematically demonstrates the neurotoxic effects of chronic low‐concentration methanol exposure and establishes a reproducible and reliable large‐animal model for AD and related cognitive disorders. More important, it provides essential in vivo evidence in nonhuman primates for elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying methanol‐induced cognitive impairment.
