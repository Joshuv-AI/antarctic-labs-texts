Ultrasound-powered bovine bone protein hydrolysate-flaxseed gum emulsions: modulation of stability, interfacial properties, and curcumin bioaccessibility
Authors: Niancheng Hong, Shengnan Liu, Yueyu Bai, Yaodi Zhu, Miaoyun Li, Lijun Zhao, Gaiming Zhao, Dong Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Stabilized emulsion systems play a crucial role in the food industry, and their quality directly influences the texture, mouthfeel, and shelf life of food products. However, most traditional emulsifiers are synthetic compounds that may pose potential health risks and are increasingly inconsistent with consumer demand for natural, clean-label foods [1,2]. Proteins and polysaccharides, owing to their safety, biocompatibility, and functional properties, show great potential as natural emulsifiers [3]. BBPH, rich in various functional amino acids, demonstrates strong emulsifying ability and nutritional value, making it suitable for constructing emulsion systems with functional properties. However, BBPH-stabilized emulsions become less stable as the pH approaches the protein's isoelectric point, leading to delamination [4,5]. FG is a natural polysaccharide widely utilized in emulsion systems for its thickening and stabilizing properties [6]. Emulsion systems stabilized by natural emulsifiers also show tremendous promise for loading and releasing bioactive compounds, particularly hydrophobic functional compounds. Curcumin is a well-known bioactive substance with a range of health benefits, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer properties. However, its poor water solubility and susceptibility to degradation by external factors, such as light and oxygen, limit its application in the food and pharmaceutical industries [7]. Studies have shown that encapsulating curcumin in emulsions not only significantly enhances its water solubility but also improves its bioavailability [8]. Protein-polysaccharide composite emulsions, with their multilayered interfacial structure, facilitate the encapsulation and slow release of curcumin, thereby enhancing its stability in food systems [9]. Despite the potential of protein-polysaccharide systems as natural emulsifiers, achieving stable and functional emulsions remains a challenge. Previous studies have predominantly focused on individual emulsifiers or conventional homogenization methods, which often fail to provide satisfactory results in terms of stability and bioactive compound delivery. Therefore, alternative methods are necessary to improve the arrangement of composite emulsifiers at the oil–water interface and further enhance the stability of emulsion systems.

In recent years, ultrasound has garnered widespread attention as a green processing technology for further enhancing the stability and loading capacity of composite emulsions. Through its cavitation effect, ultrasound creates a localized high-temperature, high-pressure environment in the liquid, denaturing the protein structure, thereby increasing the interfacial activity of proteins, promoting interactions between protein and polysaccharide molecules, and ultimately enhancing the stability of emulsions [10]. Ultrasonication can also effectively reduce the emulsion particle size, enhance droplet homogeneity, and regulate the rheological properties, further improving the stability of the emulsion system [11]. It has been shown that variations in ultrasound power significantly affect the particle size distribution and interfacial behavior of emulsion systems [12]. However, the effects of ultrasound, particularly the role of varying ultrasonic power, on the physicochemical properties and interfacial behavior of protein–polysaccharide emulsions have not been systematically explored. Moreover, most research has not fully addressed the influence of processing conditions on the molecular interactions between proteins and polysaccharides at the oil–water interface.

Based on these questions, we hypothesize that moderate ultrasonic power will facilitate optimal molecular rearrangement, thereby improving protein-polysaccharide interactions, curcumin encapsulation efficiency, and bioaccessibility by reducing droplet size and enhancing emulsion stability. This study, therefore, systematically investigates the mechanisms underlying changes in the stability and interfacial behavior of the BBPH-FG composite emulsion system under various ultrasonic power treatments and examines the effect of ultrasonic processing on curcumin loading in the emulsion. The aim is to provide theoretical foundations for the development of highly efficient and stable natural emulsifier systems, promote the use of ultrasonics in the food industry, and offer a scientific rationale for the application of emulsifiers in functional food development.

Materials and methods

Materials

BBPH was prepared in the laboratory. Flaxseed gum (FG) (purity ≥ 98 %, Mn = 2.77 × 106Da, composed of acidic polysaccharide and neutral polysaccharide, and the molar ratio of acidic polysaccharide to neutral polysaccharide is 2:1) purchased from Henan Wanbang Chemical Science and Technology Co., Ltd. (Henan, China). Soybean oil was purchased from Yihai Jiali Golden Dragon Fish Grain and Oil Food Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Curcumin (purity: HPLC ≥ 98 %) was purchased from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Nile Red, Nile Blue, and 8-Aniline-1-naphthalene sulfonic acid (ANS) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich (Shanghai) Trading Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All other chemical reagents were analytically pure and purchased from National Medicine Group Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Preparation of BBPH-FG complexes

A 4 % BBPH solution and a 0.8 % FG solution were prepared by dissolving BBPH and FG in deionized water, respectively, and stirring continuously for 2 h at 120 rpm and room temperature to ensure complete solubilization of the proteins and polysaccharides. Subsequently, the two solutions were mixed in a 1:1 vol ratio and stirred for 1 h at room temperature to ensure homogeneous dispersion, resulting in a final blended solution with a BBPH concentration of 2 % and a polysaccharide concentration of 0.4 %. The complex was then freeze-dried for subsequent experiments.

Emulsion preparation and ultrasonication

The aqueous phase was prepared by dissolving 2 g of the BBPH-FG complex in 100 mL of deionized water. The aqueous phase was then combined with the oil phase at a volume ratio of 4:1, and the resulting emulsion was prepared by homogenizing at 10,000 rpm for 3 min using a high-speed disperser (T10 basic, IKA, Staufen, Germany). The emulsions were subsequently sonicated using an ultrasonic breaker (JY92-IIN, Xinzhi, Ningbo, China) under ice-water bath conditions for 10 min at power levels of 0, 250, 500, 750, and 1,000 W. The processed emulsions were designated as UE0, UE250, UE500, UE750, and UE1000, respectively. Additionally, curcumin-loaded emulsions were prepared following the same method as above, except that 0.2 % (w/v) of curcumin was first completely dissolved in soybean oil before emulsion preparation. To inhibit microbial growth, 0.01 % (w/v) sodium azide was added to the emulsion [13].

Particle size, PDI and zeta potential of emulsions

The emulsions were diluted 100-fold using 0.01 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.2) to minimize multiple scattering effects. The diluted samples were analyzed using a laser particle size analyzer (Mastersizer-3000, Malvern PANalytical, Malvern, England) to determine the mean particle size, zeta potential, and polydispersity index (PDI) of the droplets. For the measurements, the refractive indices of the oil and aqueous phases were set to 1.470 and 1.330, respectively [14]. Three measurements were performed for each protocol, with 13 scans per measurement, and each sample was analyzed in triplicate.

Droplet morphology of emulsions

Optical microscope observation

An optical microscope (BX53, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) was utilized to observe the morphology of the emulsion droplets. To prevent excessive emulsion concentration from affecting the observations, the emulsion was diluted 20-fold with deionized water before analysis. The diluted sample was added dropwise onto a slide, and a coverslip was carefully placed to prevent bubble formation [15]. The emulsion was then observed under a 20 × eyepiece.

Confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM) observation

The microstructures of the prepared emulsions were analyzed using a laser confocal scanning microscope (A1R HD25, Nikon, Japan) using the previous method and slight modifications [16]. The oil phase and protein were stained by adding 50 μL of Nile Red solution (1 mg/mL, dissolved in ethanol) and 50 μL of Nile Blue solution (1 mg/mL, dissolved in ultrapure water) to 1 mL of emulsion samples, respectively. The excitation wavelengths of Nile red and Nile blue were 633 nm and 488 nm, respectively.

Interfacial properties of emulsions

Interfacial tension of emulsions

An optical contact angle meter (OCA20, Shanghai, China) was employed to measure the oil–water interfacial tension (mN/m) following the previously established method [17]. The aqueous phase consisted of a 20 mg/mL BBPH-FG solution, while the oil phase was soybean oil. A 5 μL sample was loaded into a syringe and injected into an optical glass dish containing the oil phase. The interfacial tension at the oil–water interface was then calculated using the Young-Laplace equation.

Interfacial protein adsorption properties of emulsions

Using the previous method and slight modifications [18]. A 5 mL sample was drawn from the bottom of the emulsion in a 10 mL centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 10,000 g for 30 min to induce oil–water phase stratification. The clarified liquid at the bottom of the sample was collected using a syringe and filtered through a 0.45 μm filter membrane. The protein concentration in the supernatant was determined after the filtered protein solution was centrifuged again at 10,000 g for 30 min. The protein adsorption rate (AP, %) was calculated according to Eq. (1), and the interfacial protein concentration (Γ, mg/m2) was calculated according to Eq. (2).(1)AP=(CS-CF)×100/C0(2)Γ=(CS-CF)×d/6φwhere C0 is the protein concentration in the original emulsion (mg/mL), CF is the protein concentration in the aqueous phase after centrifugation (mg/mL), CS is the protein concentration in the emulsion after centrifugation (mg/mL); d is the average particle size of the emulsion (μm), φ is the volume fraction of the oil phase (0.2).

Interfacial structure of emulsions

Raman spectra of the emulsion samples were collected in the range of 1000–4000 cm−1 using a Raman spectrometer (LabRAM HR Evolution, Horiba Electronic Technology Co., Ltd., Paris, France). During the test, a drop of the emulsion sample was placed onto the slide, which had been wrapped in aluminum foil, and observed using a 20 × objective lens. The spectra were acquired using a laser wavelength of 532 nm, three scans, an exposure time of 60 s, a resolution of 2 cm−1, and a sampling rate of 120 cm−1/min [19]. The spectral data were smoothed and baseline-corrected using Labspec 6.0 software.

Rheological properties of emulsions

According to the method of the literature with minor modifications[20], at 25 °C, the apparent viscosity profiles of the emulsion samples were determined over a range of shear rates from 0.1 to 100 s using a rotational rheometer (HAAKE Mars60, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Shanghai, China) with a measurement gap width of 1000 μm. The storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G′′) were expressed by determining the angular frequency (ω) as a function in the range of 0.1 to 10 rad/s.

Stability of emulsions

Storage stability

Nine milliliters of freshly prepared emulsion were placed in a 10 mL glass vial, and 0.02 % sodium azide was added to inhibit microbial growth. The glass vials were stored at room temperature (25 °C). After 25 days of storage, the appearance was documented using a digital camera, and the creaming index (CI) of the emulsions was calculated.(3)CI(%)=HSHT×100where HT is the total height of the emulsion (mm), Hs is the height of the emulsion layer (mm).

Freeze-thaw stability

Eight milliliters of freshly prepared emulsion were placed in a sealed glass vial and stored in a − 18 °C freezer for 22 h. After freezing, the emulsion was thawed in a constant-temperature water bath set at 40 °C for 2 h[21]. This entire process constituted one freeze–thaw cycle. After completing three freeze–thaw cycles, the stability of the emulsions was evaluated to assess freeze–thaw resistance.

Oxidative stability

The freshly prepared emulsions were placed in a thermostatic oven at 50 °C to accelerate the oxidation process, and samples were taken every 5 days to determine the peroxide value (POV value) and TBARS value of the emulsions as an indicator to assess the oxidative stability of the emulsions during storage[22]. The POV values were determined as follows: 1 mL of the emulsion was mixed thoroughly with 5 mL of iso-octane and isopropanol (3:1, v/v), vortexed for 30 s, and then centrifuged for 2 min at 4000 rpm. 4000 rpm centrifugation for 2 min. Take 200 μL of supernatant and mix with 2.8 mL of methanol and 1-butanol (2:1, v/v), then add 20 μL of potassium thiocyanate and 20 μL of ferrous solution and mix thoroughly. After the reaction at room temperature for 20 min, protected from light, the absorbance value was measured at 510 nm. In the blank control group, the emulsion samples were replaced by iso-octane and isopropanol solutions. The TBARS values were determined as follows: 1 mL of the emulsion was mixed with 2 mL of thiobarbituric acid (TBA) reagent and then cooled rapidly after 15 min in a boiling water bath, followed by centrifugation at 1500 rpm for 10 min, and then the supernatant was taken after 10 min of resting, and the absorbance values were measured at 532 nm.

Encapsulation effect of emulsion on curcumin

Encapsulation efficiency of curcumin

The encapsulation efficiency of curcumin was determined using a UV spectrophotometer (UV-2600, Shimadzu, Tokyo, Japan) following the method outlined by previous method [23]. Initially, curcumin-methanol solutions at varying concentrations were prepared, and their absorbance values at 425 nm were measured to generate a standard curve for curcumin. A specific amount of emulsion was transferred to a centrifuge tube, and an appropriate volume of methanol was added to extract curcumin. After thorough vortex mixing, the sample was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 20 min. The resulting supernatant was appropriately diluted with methanol, and the absorbance was measured at 425 nm. The encapsulation efficiency (EE) was calculated using the following formula:(4)EE(%)=(Ct/C0)×100where Ct is the amount of curcumin in the emulsion, C0 is the initial addition of curcumin.

Stabilization of curcumin in emulsions

The stability of curcumin in emulsions was evaluated in terms of storage stability, irradiation stability, and thermal stability [24]. Regarding storage stability, the samples were stored at 25 °C for 10 days protected from light, and then determined. For the irradiation stability test, the emulsions were placed in clear glass bottles and irradiated with ultraviolet (UV) light at a wavelength of 365 nm for 1 to 5 h. Thermal treatment, on the other hand, consisted of placing the emulsions in clear glass sample bottles and exposing them to a temperature of 50 °C for 1 to 5 h. Subsequently, the retention of curcumin in the treated emulsions was determined.

Bioaccessibility of curcumin

The bioaccessibility of curcumin was determined according to the method of Yang, Zhenyu [25]. After complete digestion, 10 mL of the original digest was collected and centrifuged at 10,000 r/min for 60 min. After centrifugation, the sample was divided into an upper oil phase, an intermediate micellar phase, and a bottom insoluble precipitate, and the micellar phase was subsequently collected. Curcumin was extracted from the digest and micellar phase using methanol as an organic solvent. 1 mL of the sample was mixed with 4 mL of the organic phase and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 20 min to collect the clear organic phase and quantify the concentration of curcumin. Bioaccessibility and digestive stability were calculated according to the following equations:(5)Bioaccessibility(%)=CM/CD∗100(6)Digestivestability(%)=CD/CI∗100where CM is the concentration of curcumin in micelles, CD is the concentration of curcumin in the crude digest, and CI is the concentration of raw curcumin.

Statistical analysis

Each experimental measurement was performed in triplicate, with results expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. Data analysis was conducted using analysis of variance (ANOVA) with SPSS 22.0 software. Post hoc comparisons were conducted using Duncan’s test and the least significant difference (LSD) method, with the significance threshold set at p < 0.05. All graphical data representations were created using Origin 2021 software.

Results and discussion

Droplet characterization of emulsions

The effect of ultrasonic power on the droplet size, PDI, and zeta potential of the BBPH-FG emulsion was systematically evaluated by varying the ultrasonic power, with the results shown in Fig. 1, Fig. 1. The droplet size of the emulsions decreased significantly with increasing ultrasonic power, reaching a minimum of 3.7 μm at 750 W, then increased slightly to 3.9 μm at 1000 W. This suggests that ultrasonic treatment can effectively improve droplet homogenization within an optimal power range, attributed to the intense micro-jetting and microturbulence induced by ultrasonic cavitation, which significantly enhances droplet fragmentation [26]. However, when the power was increased further to 1000 W, the droplet size rebounded. This can be attributed to the excessive shear generated by ultrasonic cavitation at high power, which not only promotes droplet fragmentation but may also disrupt the interfacial membrane required for droplet stabilization, leading to droplets regrouping in the emulsion [27]. The PDI is a key indicator of the homogeneity of the emulsion size distribution. The results in Fig. 1A show that the PDI decreased with ultrasonic power in the range of 500 W to 750 W. However, when the power was further increased to 1000 W, the PDI increased slightly (0.2508), likely due to the excessive cavitation effect, which disrupts the interfacial layer structure and leads to a more diverse droplet size distribution [13]. Zeta potential is an important parameter for measuring the surface charge density of particles in emulsions and their electrostatic stability. The absolute value of the zeta potential increased with ultrasonic power, reaching a minimum of −19.66 mV at 750 W, then slightly increased to −21.76 mV at 1000 W. This suggests that ultrasonic treatment can significantly alter the adsorption behavior and charge distribution of BBPH-FG complexes at the oil–water interface. The protein unfolding effect triggered by ultrasonic treatment exposes more hydrophobic groups, leading to denser adsorption at the interface [28]. Emulsion droplet morphology

Observations using optical microscopy (Fig. 1C) and CLSM (Fig. 1D) revealed that ultrasonic power significantly affected as ultrasonic power increased from 0 W to 750 W, the particle size of the emulsion droplets gradually decreased, the distribution became more uniform, and protein adsorption at the oil–water interface became denser. In UE0, a large and inhomogeneous distribution of oil droplets was observed, with protein adsorption (red fluorescent markers) on the surface of the droplets (green fluorescent markers) being uneven. The interfacial layer was loose and lacked density, leading to droplet aggregation and instability. In UE250 the particle size of the droplets decreased slightly, but the distribution of adsorbed proteins at the interface remained non-uniform, and the CLSM image revealed that a complete interfacial membrane structure had not formed. When ultrasonic power was increased further to 500 W, the droplet size was significantly reduced, and the proteins were redistributed at the interface to form a uniform and compact interfacial layer. After ultrasonic treatment at 750 W, the droplets were further refined to reach the minimum particle size, and the interfacial proteins were more uniformly and densely coated. This indicates that ultrasonic power at this level maximized the adsorption efficiency of interfacial proteins and the stability of the emulsion. However, in UE1000 the particle size of the emulsion slightly increased, and protein adsorption on the surface of the oil droplets became inhomogeneous. This is because high ultrasonic energy leads to partial deconstruction or denaturation of the protein molecular structure, weakening its stabilizing effect at the interface, which promotes emulsion droplet aggregation and decreases stability [13]. Based on droplet micromorphology, it was found that appropriate ultrasonic power could significantly reduce emulsion droplet size and enhance dispersion uniformity through effective cavitation and shear effects.

Interfacial tension of emulsions

Fig. 2A shows that interfacial tension decreased significantly as ultrasonic power increased from 0 W to 750 W, indicating that ultrasonic treatment can effectively promote the adsorption and distribution of proteins and polysaccharides at the oil–water interface, thereby enhancing interfacial activity. However, when ultrasonic power was further increased to 1000 W, interfacial tension increased slightly. As ultrasonic power increased, the cavitation effect was gradually enhanced, generating intense microscale turbulence and shear force within the emulsion system, which effectively dispersed the oil droplets. In addition, ultrasonication can induce partial deconstruction of interfacial protein molecules, exposing more hydrophobic regions, which facilitates their adsorption on the oil–water interface, thereby improving the stability of the interfacial membrane [29]. This effect was optimal at 750 W when interfacial tension was minimized. However, when ultrasonic power was increased to 1000 W, interfacial tension increased slightly, likely due to the destruction of protein structure caused by excessive sonication. Excessive ultrasonic power can subject protein molecules to intense mechanical shear and cavitation effects, leading to disruption of their secondary and tertiary structures, thereby weakening their adsorption at the interface [30]. Interfacial protein adsorption of emulsions

Fig. 2B illustrates the effects of varying ultrasonic power on the adsorption rate of interfacial proteins and protein concentration. The adsorption rate of proteins and interfacial protein concentration in UE0 is relatively low-54.01 % and 21.61 mg/m2, respectively-indicating that the hydrophobic region inside the protein molecule is not fully exposed in its natural state, limiting its effective adsorption at the oil–water interface. After 250 W ultrasonication, partial deconstruction of the protein molecules due to the acoustic cavitation effect and localized shear force resulted in a more flexible and interfacially active structure, significantly increasing the adsorption rate and interfacial protein concentration [31]. As power was further increased to 500 W and 750 W, the protein adsorption rate at the interface peaked (nearly 80 %), and the interfacial protein concentration stabilized at about 36 mg/m2, indicating that the proteins could be rapidly adsorbed and form a dense protective film at the interface under appropriate ultrasonic intensities, contributing to the long-term stability of the emulsion. However, when ultrasonic power was increased to 1000 W, the adsorption rate slightly decreased to 71 %, although the interfacial protein concentration remained largely unchanged. Ultrasound enhances emulsion stability by altering the molecular conformation of proteins and improving their adsorption efficiency at the interface. However, when ultrasonic intensity is too high, it may cause excessive deconstruction and aggregation of proteins, weakening their adsorption capacity on the surface of oil droplets and, consequently, reducing the stability of emulsions [32].

Structural properties of emulsion interface proteins

The response signals in Raman spectroscopy primarily arise from the vibrations of functional groups, allowing it to detect changes in the structural characteristics of lipid molecules and the interactions between proteins and polysaccharides during the emulsion formation process [33,34]. Fig. 2, Fig. 2 illustrate the effects of ultrasonic power on the conformation of proteins in the emulsion system and its impact on interfacial behavior. The spectra reveal absorption peaks corresponding to the protein amide I band (1600 cm−1–1700 cm−1), double peaks of tyrosine vibration (834 cm−1, 857 cm−1), as well as 2853 cm−1 (CH2 symmetric stretching), 2892 cm−1 (CH2 asymmetric stretching), and 2930 cm−1 (CH3 symmetric stretching) [35,36].

Secondary structures

Analysis of the secondary structures of emulsion interface proteins under varying ultrasonic powers revealed that ultrasound treatment significantly modulated the molecular conformation of the proteins, thereby affecting their adsorption properties at the oil–water interface and the stability of the emulsions. Fig. 2F shows that the interface proteins of the UE0 emulsion primarily consist of α-helix and β-turn as their secondary structures. As the ultrasonic power increased from 250 W to 500 W, the α-helix content decreased significantly, while the β-sheet and random coil content increased. This shift in secondary structure suggests that ultrasound induces partial unfolding of the protein, exposing more hydrophobic groups for enhanced adsorption at the oil–water interface [34]. As the ultrasonic power increased to 750 W, the β-sheet content reached its maximum, while the α-helix content remained relatively stable. This suggests that the conformational adjustment of protein molecules at the interface equilibrates under moderate ultrasound power, promoting stronger interfacial adsorption and enhanced stability. However, when the ultrasonic power was further increased to 1000 W, the proportion of α-helices and random coils increased again, while the β-sheet content decreased. This suggests that excessive ultrasonication may lead to further unfolding and partial aggregation of protein secondary structures, reducing interfacial adsorption capacity and, consequently, emulsion stability.

Emulsion interactions

To further analyze the changes in interactions between the components in the emulsions, the intensities in the range of 2800–3100 cm−1 in the Raman spectra were normalized. The normalized Raman spectra of the interfacial layer of the emulsions after treatment with different ultrasonic powers are shown in Fig. 2E. The changes in the I857/I834 ratio reflect the exposure of tryptophan residues in the emulsions to their microenvironment and hydrophobic groups. The ratio decreased significantly from 1.11 to 0.654 as ultrasonic power increased from 0 W to 750 W, indicating that ultrasonication reduced the solvent accessibility of tryptophan residues and significantly increased surface hydrophobicity, thereby facilitating intermolecular hydrophobic interactions. When ultrasonic power was increased to 1000 W, the I857/I834 ratio increased slightly, likely due to the enhanced aggregation of protein molecules caused by excessive sonication, forming a new hydrophobic microenvironment and reducing further exposure of hydrophobic groups [37,38]. Previous studies have shown that the I2853/I2892 ratio is closely linked to the interaction between oil droplets and surface coating materials in emulsions, while the I2930/I2892 ratio reflects the effect of ultrasonic treatment on isomerization within the protein chain and protein-polysaccharide interactions in the emulsion system [34]. As the I2853/I2892 ratio gradually decreased with increasing ultrasonic power, the cavitation effect promoted the rearrangement and tight binding of protein and polysaccharide molecules, enhancing the interaction between the surface coating layer and the oil droplet phase. This interaction helped inhibit oil droplet aggregation and phase separation in the emulsion. However, when ultrasonic power was further increased to 1000 W, the I2853/I2892 ratio increased, likely due to excessive shear generated by high ultrasonic power, which weakened the structural integrity of the interfacial film on the oil droplets, thereby decreasing emulsion stability [39]. The I2930/I2892 ratio followed a similar trend, indicating that the cavitation effect induced by ultrasound promotes the synergistic interaction between proteins and polysaccharides at the interface. After further increasing ultrasonic power to 1000 W, the I2930/I2892 ratio decreased slightly, likely due to excessive protein denaturation, which weakened its interaction with the polysaccharide. Therefore, moderate sonication (e.g., 750 W) can enhance emulsion stability by promoting hydrophobic interactions between proteins and their synergistic interfacial adsorption with polysaccharides. In contrast, excessive sonication power may trigger protein aggregation and denaturation, negatively affecting emulsion stability.

Rheological properties of emulsions

Analyzing the rheological behavior of emulsions is essential for predicting their stability. Fig. 3A illustrates the effect of different ultrasonic power treatments on the apparent viscosity of the emulsions. The figure shows that the apparent viscosities of all samples decreased significantly with increasing shear rate, demonstrating typical shear-thinning behavior, indicating that all emulsions are non-Newtonian fluids [40]. At low shear rates, the initial apparent viscosities of the emulsions increased with increasing ultrasonic power, reaching a peak at 750 W. The apparent viscosities slightly decreased after the ultrasonic power was increased to 1000 W. This suggests that within the ultrasonic power range of 0 W to 750 W, the cavitation effect promotes a more uniform dispersion of the droplets, forming a compact particle network structure. This enhances droplet interaction and ultimately improves system viscosity. When ultrasonic power is too high (1000 W), strong spatial shear disrupts droplet particle interactions, leading to a decrease in viscosity [41].Fig. 3Effect of ultrasonic power on (a) apparent viscosity and (b) energy storage modulus G′ and loss modulus G″ of emulsions.

Fig. 3B illustrates the effect of different ultrasonic power treatments on the emulsion storage modulus (G') and loss modulus (G“). Across the frequency range, G' for all emulsions is greater than G”, increasing with ultrasonic power and reaching its maximum value at 750 W. This trend mirrors the variation pattern observed in apparent viscosity. Peak values for G' and G“ were observed at 750 W, indicating that the cavitation effect at this power induced aggregation and bridging flocculation between particles, increasing both the energy storage and loss moduli. The enhanced bridging effect increases inter-particle interactions, thereby improving the system's viscoelasticity [42]. However, when power was increased to 1000 W, both G' and G” slightly decreased, suggesting that high ultrasonic power may have disrupted the particle network structure, weakening the rheological properties of the system [43]. This may be due to excessive cavitation, leading to disintegration of the inter-particle bridging structure or weakening of network reorganization.

Emulsion stability analysis

Storage stability

The stability of the emulsions during storage can be assessed by observing changes in their appearance and measuring the creaming index. Fig. 4A shows that the CI of the emulsions decreased significantly with increasing ultrasonic power, then slightly increased after reaching a critical value. When ultrasonic power was increased from 0 W to 500 W, the creaming index decreased significantly from 71.42 % to 3.28 %, indicating that ultrasonic treatment has a substantial effect on emulsion stability within this range. When ultrasonic power was increased to 750 W, the creaming index further decreased to 1.86 %, achieving the optimum stabilization effect. Cavitation at this power not only promotes the formation of a denser structure at the interface between protein and polysaccharide molecules but also enhances their synergistic interactions, possibly by increasing the flexibility of the molecular chains. This coincides with the increasing trend of the I2930/I2892 ratio, suggesting that ultrasound treatment promotes the effective binding of proteins and polysaccharides at the interface, further inhibiting emulsion precipitation. Notably, the creaming index slightly increased to 2.27 % when ultrasonic power was raised to 1000 W. The results showed that the creaming index was higher than the I2930/I2892 ratio. This phenomenon may be attributed to excessive shear and cavitation effects induced by high ultrasonic power, leading to partial denaturation and aggregation of protein molecules. Freeze-thaw stability

The effect of ultrasound on the freeze–thaw stability of emulsions was evaluated by measuring changes in particle size and PDI after three freeze–thaw cycles, with the results shown in Fig. 4B. As ultrasonic power increased, the particle size of emulsions decreased from 28.68 μm at UE0 to a minimum of 11.74 μm at UE750, then slightly increased to 14.14 μm at UE1000 after three freeze–thaw cycles. The PDI of the emulsions showed a similar trend, reaching a minimum at 750 W before slightly increasing. These changes indicate that moderate sonication significantly enhances emulsion stability, helping maintain smaller oil droplet sizes and better uniformity after freeze–thaw cycles. The enhancement of freeze–thaw stability may be due to the formation of a denser protein-polysaccharide interfacial layer on the surface of oil droplets through ultrasonic treatment, which enhances electrostatic repulsion and spatial barriers between the droplets, effectively inhibiting aggregation during freeze–thaw cycles [44]. However, when ultrasonic power was further increased to 1000 W, both particle size and PDI rebounded, indicating that excessively high ultrasonic intensity may damage the structural integrity of the interfacial membrane, decreasing the freeze–thaw stability of the emulsion.

Oxidation stability

POV and TBARS values are two key indicators of lipid oxidation stability of emulsions during storage [45]. Fig. 4, Fig. 4 show the POV and the changing pattern of oil droplets in emulsions stored under the same conditions for 25 days compared with control soybean oil. The emulsions after moderate ultrasound treatment exhibited lower POV and TBARS values, especially at 750 W ultrasound power; the POV and TBARS values decreased to 9.24 mg/L and 1.25 mg/L, respectively, a phenomenon indicating that the emulsion system significantly improved the oxidative stability of the lipids through the construction of the interfacial membrane and the synergistic effect of ultrasound treatment. The ultrasonic treatment promoted the rapid adsorption of proteins and polysaccharides at the oil–water interface through the cavitation effect and shear force, resulting in the formation of an interfacial layer with dense properties. This interfacial layer can effectively block the penetration of oxygen into the interior of the oil droplet, reduce the chance of contact between the oil droplet and oxidizing substances, and thus delay the occurrence of lipid oxidation reactions [46]. In addition, the reduction of oil droplet size caused by the cavitation effect further enhances the dispersion stability of the emulsion and increases the apparent viscosity and viscoelastic modulus of the emulsion, resulting in a decrease in the oxidative sensitivity of the system. However, excessive ultrasonication (1000 w) can damage the integrity of the interfacial film of the oil droplets due to excessive shear and induce the denaturation of interfacial proteins to reduce the interfacial stability, which ultimately weakens its antioxidant barrier function.

CI showed a positive correlation with the interfacial protein β-S, which indicated that higher β-sheet content could help to improve emulsion stability. This is due to the ability of the β-sheet structure to form a dense protein network at the oil–water interface, thereby enhancing the mechanical strength of the interfacial film. On the contrary, the negative correlation between the irregular curl (RC) and PS indicates that more irregular curl structures lead to an increase in droplet size, which may be due to the unstable protein adsorption at the interface, making it difficult to form a dense membrane layer, thus leading to a decrease in the stability of the emulsion. Environmental stability of curcumin in emulsions

To better assess the delivery capacity of the sonicated emulsions for curcumin, we further investigated the impact of various environmental factors (storage time, light, and heat treatment) on the stability of curcumin encapsulated in the emulsions. Fig. 6A shows the change in curcumin retention in the emulsions after 15 days of storage. Compared to curcumin dissolved in soybean oil (which retained only 33.95 % after 15 days), the emulsion system treated with ultrasound significantly improved curcumin retention. Specifically, curcumin retention in the emulsion reached 55.67 % with 750 W ultrasonic treatment, which was significantly higher than that in the non-sonicated group. This trend suggests that ultrasonic treatment effectively encapsulates curcumin by enhancing emulsion stability and reducing oil droplet aggregation, thereby slowing curcumin degradation. Fig. 6B demonstrates that ultrasonication also significantly improves curcumin stability under UV irradiation. In the untreated emulsion group, the retention rate of curcumin was 43.67 % after 5 h of UV irradiation, whereas the retention rate increased to 53.67 % with 750 W ultrasonication. This improvement is attributed to the fact that proper ultrasonication forms a denser protective film on the droplet surface, effectively preventing UV transmission and curcumin degradation [47]. Ultrasonication also improved the thermal stability of curcumin in the emulsions (Fig. 6C), with a retention rate of 50.67 % after 750 W treatment, significantly higher than that of the other groups. This is due to ultrasound enhancing the interaction between proteins and polysaccharides on the oil droplet surface, forming a strong protective film that improves the thermal stability of curcumin in the oil phase. Therefore, based on these results, 750 W ultrasonication was selected as the optimal treatment for subsequent experiments.

Bioaccessibility of curcumin in emulsions

Bioaccessibility refers to the fraction of curcumin absorbed by small intestinal epithelial cells after digestion. Fig. 6E shows the bioaccessibility and digestive stability of curcumin following simulated in vitro digestion. Both emulsion loading and sonication significantly enhanced the bioaccessibility and digestive stability of curcumin during in vitro digestion compared to curcumin dissolved in soybean oil. The bioaccessibility of curcumin dissolved in soybean oil was only 30.38 %, while the bioaccessibility of the curcumin emulsion (0 W) without sonication increased to 60.74 %. This suggests that the emulsion structure enhances the dissolution and dispersion of curcumin, thereby improving its solubility in the digestive solution. The bioaccessibility of curcumin in the emulsion after 750 W ultrasonic treatment increased further to 71.37 %. This decrease is because ultrasonic treatment reduces emulsion particle size through cavitation and turbulence, improves dispersion homogeneity, and reduces oil droplet aggregation during digestion. This increases the contact area of oil droplets with lipase, promotes lipolysis of fat, and ultimately releases more curcumin for absorption in the small intestine [48]. Studies have shown that appropriate ultrasonication significantly improves the structure of the emulsion interfacial membrane, inhibits unfavorable factors such as enzymatic degradation and oxidation in the gastrointestinal environment, and reduces curcumin loss during digestion [49]. This conclusion is consistent with the present results, where curcumin loaded in UE750 exhibited excellent stabilization (77.47 %) after simulated in vitro digestion, significantly higher than that of the control group.

These improvements demonstrate that ultrasonically prepared BBPH-FG emulsions can serve as effective delivery systems for bioactive compounds in functional foods and health supplements. This offers the potential for creating clean-label natural emulsifiers while reducing reliance on high-pressure homogenization methods in emulsion production, thereby enabling a more efficient and sustainable manufacturing process.

Conclusion

In this study, we systematically investigated the effects of ultrasonic power modulation on BBPH-FG emulsions, emphasizing the critical role of ultrasonic treatment in enhancing emulsion stability and the encapsulation of functional ingredients. Ultrasonication significantly reduced droplet particle size and improved dispersion uniformity. Additionally, it increased the β-sheet content by altering protein conformation within the droplets, thereby enhancing the adsorption rate of interfacial proteins. At 750 W ultrasonic power, the cavitation effect substantially enhanced the synergistic adsorption of proteins and polysaccharides, leading to the formation of a denser interfacial film. This improvement resulted in optimal rheological properties while simultaneously reducing droplet aggregation, effectively inhibiting oxygen penetration, and improving both physical and oxidative stability during storage. Furthermore, emulsions treated with 750 W ultrasound exhibited a significant reduction in curcumin decomposition under various environmental conditions and enhanced its digestive stability and bioaccessibility during simulated in vitro digestion. These results provide both theoretical and experimental support for developing highly stable and functional food emulsions using ultrasound technology, promoting the application of sustainable processing methods in the food industry. However, the study was conducted under controlled laboratory conditions, and environmental factors such as pH and temperature were not fully explored, which constitutes a limitation. Future research should investigate the impact of these factors and explore the encapsulation of other bioactive compounds within the ultrasound-assisted emulsion system. Additionally, in vivo studies are needed to evaluate the bioavailability and health benefits of these bioactive compounds in real-world applications.
