RNA modifications in cancer: regulators of tumor evolution and therapeutic response
Authors: Xinyu Gu, Yuting He, Ziyi Xu, Shujun Zhang, Yafeng Liu, Zihan Yang, Hongyan Liu, Wenjuan Chen, Lifan Zhang, Yingda Xie, Jiaxiang Hu, Huiwu Xing, Jing Yan, Qingmiao Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
RNA modifications, such as N6-methyladenosine (m6A), N1-methyladenosine (m1A), 5-methylcytosine (m5C), 7-methylguanosine (m7G), pseudouridine (Ψ), and adenosine-to-inosine (A-to-I) editing, constitute a dynamic epitranscriptomic network that profoundly regulates RNA metabolism and gene expression. Their dysregulation is increasingly recognized as a hallmark of cancer. This review critically synthesizes the multifaceted roles of RNA modifications to bridge the gap between descriptive epitranscriptomic mapping and functional tumor biology. We systematically evaluate how writers, readers, and erasers dictate transcript stability and translation efficiency, driving tissue-specific tumor evolution across diverse malignancies. Crucially, we explore the intersection of RNA modifications and the tumor immune microenvironment, detailing their mechanisms in orchestrating immune evasion, altering antigen presentation, and regulating immune checkpoints. Furthermore, we examine how epitranscriptomic reprogramming dictates cellular responses to chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted treatments, and immunotherapy. By comprehensively analyzing these mechanisms, this review aims to facilitate the translation of epitranscriptomic findings into clinical applications, laying a theoretical foundation for targeted anti-tumor strategies.

Introduction

Epigenetic modifications are widespread across biological molecules, including DNA, histones, and RNA [1]. These chemical marks have central roles in determining the intensity and spatiotemporal specificity of gene expression by regulating chromatin accessibility, transcriptional activity, and molecular interactions. Consequently, they can disrupt the normal function of biological molecules and contribute to the onset and progression of diseases such as cancer [2]. Among these modifications, RNA modifications have received increasing attention in cancer research. Extensive studies have highlighted the significance of RNA modifications, such as N6-methyladenosine (m6A), N1-methyladenosine (m1A), 5-methylcytosine (m5C), 7-methylguanosine (m7G), pseudouridine (Ψ), and adenosine-to-inosine (A-to-I) editing, in both mammalian physiology and disease processes [3]. These modifications modulate RNA molecules via three types of proteins: methyltransferases (“writers”), demethylases (“erasers”), and RNA-binding proteins (“readers”). “Writers” and “Erasers” are proteins responsible for adding and removing modifications at specific RNA sites, while “Readers” recognize and bind to these modified sites [4–6].

Emerging research suggests that the programmable epitranscriptome is reshaping our understanding of immune evasion and response in ways previously unrecognized [7, 8]. Since the initial discovery of m6A, RNA modifications were viewed primarily as fine-tuning mechanisms at the molecular level [9, 10]. However, the advent of high-throughput sequencing and single-cell multi-omics has unveiled their broader impact. These dynamic and reversible chemical marks can swiftly reprogram immune cell differentiation, antigen presentation, and cellular metabolism, contributing to cancer resistance and immune evasion. They accelerate processes such as angiogenesis, upregulate programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression, promote major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I) degradation, reduce antigen peptide availability, and reshape the tumor microenvironment (TME) [11–13]. In addition to m6A, other RNA modifications like N4-acetylcytidine (ac4C) and m5C have been implicated in stabilizing immune suppression-related transcription processes, such as those involving PD-L1 and FASN, thereby enhancing the TME’s capacity to suppress immune responses [14]. Moreover, RNA modifications not only affect tumor cells but also immune cells, including T cells and macrophages, inhibiting their immune activity and promoting cancer progression [15].

At present, therapeutic strategies targeting RNA modification molecules, such as methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3), fat mass and obesity-associated protein (FTO), alkB homolog 5 (ALKBH5), N-acetyltransferase 10 (NAT10), YTH domain family 1 (YTHDF1), insulin-like growth factor 2 mRNA binding protein (IGF2BP), and Y-box binding protein 1 (YBX1), are emerging as promising approaches to reversing malignant immune evasion, blocking metastasis, and overcoming therapeutic resistance [16]. Together, RNA modifications that influence cancer immunity have central roles in tumor progression. However, a comprehensive understanding of how RNA modifications relate to tumor development and immunity remains lacking [17].

Concurrently, the landscape of cancer immunotherapy is being revolutionized by the advent of personalized cancer vaccines, particularly those utilizing mRNA technology [18]. These vaccines represent a major clinical breakthrough by eliciting robust, patient-specific anti-tumor T-cell responses against unique tumor neoantigens [19]. Crucially, the clinical success of these personalized vaccines is fundamentally dependent on RNA modification technologies. For instance, the incorporation of modified nucleosides, such as Ψ or N1-methylpseudouridine (m1Ψ), significantly dampens the innate immune sensing of exogenous RNA and dramatically enhances translation efficiency [20]. This precise epitranscriptomic engineering not only prevents the premature immune clearance of the vaccine but also maximizes sustained antigen presentation, thereby underscoring the indispensable role of RNA modifications in translating personalized immunotherapy from experimental concepts into clinical realities.

While extensive research has mapped the diverse landscape of RNA modifications, comprehensively understanding their systematic impact on tumor-immune interactions remains an evolving challenge. The primary objective of this review is to provide a cohesive synthesis of how the epitranscriptome functions as an interconnected regulatory network. Distinct from existing literature that frequently focuses on individual modification types, our central message emphasizes the dynamic interplay among various RNA modifications, tumor evolution, and immune evasion. By systematically integrating recent mechanistic findings, this review delineates the critical transition from basic epitranscriptomic characterization to the identification of viable therapeutic targets, offering a refined perspective for precision oncology.

Overview of key RNA modifications

RNA modifications refer to the chemical alterations that occur in RNA molecules, critical for regulating RNA stability, translation efficiency, and interactions with proteins or other molecules. Over 170 distinct RNA modifications have been identified to date, including m6A, m5C, m1A, ac4C, and adenosine-to-inosine (A-to-I) RNA editing (Fig. 1). These modifications are not only fundamental to basic biological processes but are also closely linked to the development of various diseases [21, 22].

N6-Methyladenosine (m6A)

m6A is one of the most prevalent internal RNA modifications in eukaryotes, found extensively in mRNA, transfer RNA (tRNA), and non-coding RNA (ncRNA). The reversible m6A modification is dynamically regulated by specific enzyme complexes: methyltransferases, primarily METTL3 and methyltransferase-like 14 (METTL14), catalyze methylation at the N6 position of adenosine, whereas demethylases FTO and ALKBH5 mediate oxidative demethylation [23–27]. The functional outcomes of m6A are subsequently mediated by specific binding proteins, predominantly the YTHDF family [28]. Studies show that m6A methylation impacts RNA splicing, nuclear export, and translation efficiency, contributing to processes like neurodevelopment and tumor formation [29]. Moreover, dynamic changes in m6A are closely associated with various diseases, including cancer, cardiovascular diseases, and autoimmune disorders [30]. Thus, understanding the mechanisms of m6A methylation and its role in disease is critical for developing new therapeutic strategies.

5-Methylcytosine (m5C)

The m5C modification is widely distributed across all RNA types, including cytoplasmic and mitochondrial ribosomal RNA (rRNA), tRNA, mRNA, enhancer RNA (eRNA), and various long non-coding RNA (lncRNA). The m5C modification is catalyzed by members of the NOP2/Sun RNA Methyltransferase (NSUN) family and DNMT2, dynamically removed by the TET family and alkB homolog 1 (ALKBH1), and functionally interpreted by proteins such as ALYREF and YBX1 [31–33]. These regulatory factors interact and reciprocally regulate each other, making m5C modifications dynamically reversible [34]. The m5C modification is crucial not only for RNA translation efficiency but also for cellular metabolism, proliferation, and tumorigenesis. In cancer, m5C methylation plays an important role in regulating RNA metabolism-related signaling pathways, impacting both tumor suppression and promotion [35]. m5C methylation is significant in cancer initiation, metastasis, progression, as well as drug resistance and recurrence, positioning m5C as a potential target for precision therapeutic strategies.

Other types of RNA modification

In addition to m6A and m5C, several other RNA modifications exist, including m1A, m7G, ac4C, A-to-I RNA editing, and Ψ. m1A methylation involves the addition of a methyl group at the N1 position of adenosine, influencing RNA stability and translation. It is found in tRNA, rRNA, mRNA, and lncRNA. m7G methylation primarily occurs at the 5’ cap structure of mRNA, playing a key role in RNA nuclear export and translational regulation [36]. Ac4C refers to the acetylation of cytidine at the N4 position within RNA molecules, catalyzed by NAT10, affecting mRNA stability and translation efficiency [37, 38]. A-to-I RNA editing involves the conversion of adenosine to inosine, catalyzed by RNA-specific adenosine deaminase (ADAR), which acts on double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) [39]. This modification diverges from traditional nucleotide incorporation mechanisms, leading to A-to-I editors being characterized as both writers and editors of RNA [40]. Ψ is the most abundant modified nucleoside in RNA, often referred to as the “fifth nucleoside”. Ψ is formed by linking the fifth position of uridine to ribose, and its synthesis is primarily catalyzed by pseudouridine synthases (PUSs), which convert uridine to Ψ, thereby modulating RNA stability and translation efficiency [41]. These various RNA modifications influence RNA fate and cellular functions through complex regulatory networks, highlighting the significance of RNA modifications in cell biology and diseases, and offering novel avenues for therapeutic interventions in related diseases.

Beyond their endogenous biological functions, RNA modifications have become indispensable engineering tools in clinical therapeutics [42]. The strategic incorporation of modifications, particularly Ψ and its derivatives (such as, N1-methylpseudouridine), fundamentally alters RNA pharmacokinetics by drastically reducing the innate immunogenicity of exogenous RNA while enhancing its in vivo stability and translation efficiency [43]. This precise epitranscriptomic engineering, which underpinned the monumental success of FDA-approved mRNA vaccines for infectious diseases, is now aggressively driving the next generation of cancer therapeutics. While fully FDA-approved modified RNA drugs exclusively for oncology are currently limited, this strategy has recently achieved major clinical milestones. For instance, personalized modified mRNA cancer vaccines, such as mRNA-4157 (V940), have been granted FDA Breakthrough Therapy Designation for the treatment of high-risk melanoma when combined with pembrolizumab [44]. In these advanced therapeutic applications, RNA modifications are not merely biological markers, but critical determinants of therapeutic efficacy and structural stability.

The impact of RNA modification on RNA transcription

Notably, RNA modifications, particularly m6A methylation modification, is important for transcriptional regulation. Studies have shown that m6A modification governs gene transcriptional activity by influencing RNA structure and stability. The presence of m6A enables RNA to bind to specific “reader” proteins, thus influencing its post-transcriptional fate. m6A modification facilitates mRNA splicing, nuclear export, and translation, which are essential for dynamic gene expression regulation [45]. Moreover, the demethylation of m6A is linked to the inhibition of gene expression, suggesting that m6A may play a role in bidirectional transcriptional regulation [46]. Thus, RNA modifications serve as a pivotal mechanism in gene expression regulation, offering novel insights into the complexity of gene regulation.

Regulation of RNA stability by RNA modification

Specific epitranscriptomic marks directly govern mRNA half-life. For instance, m6A dynamically alters mRNA stability by modulating its affinity for translation initiation factors [47]. Conversely, the increased activity of demethylases can lead to mRNA instability, influencing the persistence and intensity of gene expression [48]. Another key RNA modification, m5C, is closely associated with RNA stability, impacting degradation rates by regulating RNA structure and interactions [49]. The ac4C modification also enhances the stability and functionality of mRNA and lncRNA by modulating the binding of RNA-binding proteins [50, 51]. In addition, m1A modification affects RNA stability by influencing RNA-protein interactions and altering RNA secondary structures. A-to-I RNA editing can impact RNA stability by altering RNA sequences, affecting splice site recognition, microRNA (miRNA) binding sites, and RNA structure [52, 53].

Crucially, the intersection of RNA modification biology with cancer genetics broadens the field beyond the conventional writer-reader-eraser framework. Emerging evidence demonstrates that cancer-associated synonymous mutations, traditionally overlooked as “silent”, can actively promote tumorigenesis by disrupting m6A-dependent mRNA metabolism [54, 55]. By destroying or creating m6A consensus motifs without altering the encoded amino acids, these mutations act entirely through RNA-level mechanisms to modulate transcript stability and expression. These findings highlight the important role of RNA modifications in regulating gene expression, particularly in maintaining RNA stability and mediating the oncogenic effects of genomic mutations.

The role of RNA modification in the translation process

RNA modifications directly alter ribosomal engagement and decoding efficiency. m6A dictates translation rates by modifying mRNA-ribosome interactions [56, 57], whereas m5C and Ψ maintain tRNA secondary structures, ensuring aminoacylation accuracy [58, 59]. Research has shown that m6A modification can regulate the translation of tumor-related genes, influencing tumorigenesis. For example, WTAP regulates the glycolysis and progression of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma by modulating the stability and translation efficiency of lncRNA PDIA3P1 [60]. The m6A writer METTL14 increases the m6A modification of circORC5, inhibiting the growth of gastric cancer (GC) cells [61]. In addition, Ψ modification may be a key mechanism by which cancer cells evade immune surveillance; by regulating RNA translation, Ψ may promote tumor growth and metastasis [62, 63]. Moreover, A-to-I RNA editing can influence protein translation and function by altering protein codons. For example, A-to-I editing alters the specific codon in the RHOQ transcript, leading to an asparagine-to-serine substitution at the 136th amino acid of the resulting RHOQ protein, enhancing its activity and invasiveness, which in turn promotes the progression and recurrence of colorectal cancer (CRC).

Despite these established roles, the precise mechanistic depth of m6A-mediated translation remains a subject of intense and unresolved debate. Current models of how m6A and its associated machinery promote translation are highly context-dependent. While some studies provide evidence that m6A promotes cap-independent translation (often under cellular stress), others argue it facilitates cap-associated translation through eIF3/METTL3-mediated mRNA looping. Complicating this further, recent reports have also uncovered m6A-independent translation functions of METTL3 [64–66].

Adding to this mechanistic complexity, m6A also intersects with noncanonical translation and alternative splicing to drive tumor evolution. Recent data show that altered RNA metabolism can generate specific oncogenic isoforms whose subsequent translation is highly m6A-dependent [67]. Depending on transcript position and reader recruitment, this mechanism may generate or favor oncogenic protein isoforms in selected contexts, but the evidence remains heterogeneous across models. Some studies provide direct ribosome-profiling or reporter-based support, whereas others infer translational control from expression changes. Future work should therefore integrate m6A mapping, ribosome occupancy, proteomics, and functional rescue experiments to define which m6A-dependent translation events and specific mechanistic pathways are clinically meaningful. Thus, RNA modification is not only a vital regulatory mechanism in translation control but also provides new insight and potential therapeutic targets for diseases, particularly cancer.

Gastrointestinal and hepatic malignancies

RNA modifications regulate cancer progression in tissue-specific contexts across the gastrointestinal tract and hepatobiliary system. In GC, the m6A modification enzyme METTL3 accelerates malignant progression and peritoneal metastasis by stabilizing PBX Homeobox 1 (PBX1) mRNA [68]. In addition, METTL4 promotes cancer cell migration and invasion by inducing epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [69]. The m5C modification contributes to GC development by regulating selective splicing of phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) pre-mRNA through the NONO/NSUN2/PTEN axis [70].

In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), multiple RNA modifications work together to regulate tumor progression. In the case of m6A modification, METTL3 facilitates HCC metastasis and chemotherapy resistance by modifying β-catenin and c-Jun mRNA [71]. FTO contributes to immune escape by stabilizing GPNMB mRNA and suppressing CD8+ T cell activity [72]. Regarding m5C modification, NSUN6 inhibits tumor growth via BMP-binding endothelial regulator (BMPER). Liu et al. demonstrated that overexpression of NSUN6 suppresses tumor growth, confirming its role in liver cancer development. They identified BMPER as a key target, showing that its knockout reverses the inhibitory effects of NSUN6 overexpression on HCC proliferation and migration, suggesting the NSUN6/BMPER axis as a potential therapeutic target for HCC [73]. In addition, NSUN2 stabilizes pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2) mRNA via m5C modification, enhancing the Warburg effect, glycolysis, and liver cancer progression. He et al. showed that glucose metabolism regulates NSUN2 expression [74]. Furthermore, Qin et al. demonstrated that NSUN2 stabilizes PKM2 mRNA, which increases glycolysis activity in advanced HCC, driving glucose uptake, lactate production, and promoting the proliferation and migration of liver cancer cells [75]. The positive regulation of NSUN2 expression by glucose metabolism creates a feedback loop that synergistically accelerates liver cancer progression. In the case of ac4C modification, NAT10 upregulation, commonly associated with poor prognosis in HCC, enhances ac4C modification of high mobility group box 2 (HMGB2) mRNA, activating eukaryotic elongation factor 2 (EEF2) to bind and increase translation efficiency, thereby promoting cancer progression [76]. In addition, Tao et al. revealed that NAT10 stabilizes DNA damage-induced apoptosis suppressor (DDIAS) mRNA, activating the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase-protein kinase B (PI3K-Akt) pathway to drive HCC progression [77]. In m1A bile acid metabolism, omics analysis found downregulation of the primary bile acid biosynthesis pathway in high-risk patients with HCC [78], with Zhang et al. revealing that bile acid-regulated gene expression patterns resemble those of certain antitumor compounds, suggesting bile acid metabolism plays a key role in HCC progression, with related metabolites as potential therapeutic targets. In cholesterol metabolism, the tRNA methyltransferase 6/61A complex (TRMT6/TRMT61A) complex enhances peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor delta (PPARδ) translation by increasing tRNA m1A methylation [79], which activates cholesterol synthesis and the Hedgehog signaling pathway, ultimately driving liver cancer stem cell self-renewal and tumorigenesis. This provides a theoretical basis for developing inhibitors targeting TRMT6/TRMT61A for HCC treatment. However, evaluating these targets requires distinguishing correlative omics from mechanistic evidence, as bulk sequencing often masks spatial heterogeneity. Furthermore, because RNA readers exhibit promiscuous binding, such as YTHDF1 recognizing both m6A and m1A, validating direct oncogenic mechanisms demands rigorous approaches like CLIP-seq and conditional in vivo knockout models.

Regarding Ψ modification, upregulation of PUS1 promotes tumor cell proliferation, migration, and invasion by activating the mitochondrial respiratory chain and the hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha (HIF-1α) pathway through oxidative phosphorylation [80]. Inhibiting PUS1 expression significantly reduces tumor cell proliferation and growth. Overexpression of dyskerin pseudouridine synthase 1 (DKC1) is associated with elevated expression of MYC proto-oncogene and marker of proliferation Ki-67 (MKI67), indicating its role in tumorigenesis and its potential as a poor prognostic factor in patients with HCC [81]. For m7G modification, methyltransferase-like 1 (METTL1)-mediated m7G modification regulates circIPP2A2 expression and stability. Guo et al. found that circIPP2A2 is upregulated in HCC and linked to poor prognosis. Functional experiments revealed that silencing circIPP2A2 reduces HCC cell invasion and migration. Mechanistically, circIPP2A2 functions as a molecular scaffold, facilitating the interaction between Hornerin and PI3K, thereby regulating the AKT/GSK3β signaling pathway [82].

In CRC, regarding m6A modification, FTO regulates ferroptosis by modulating the expression of solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11) and glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) [83]. In addition, ALKBH5 inhibits CRC malignant progression by regulating the stability of Jumonji domain containing 8 (JMJD8), PKM2 enzyme activity, and glycolysis. In vitro and in vivo experiments confirmed that ALKBH5 knockdown promotes CRC cell growth [84]. For m5C modification, Cui et al. discovered that NSUN6 may act as an upstream regulator of METTL3, influencing the progression of colon adenocarcinoma [85]. Luan et al. found that, under hypoxic conditions, NSUN2 upregulation promotes m5C modification, enhancing methylation at the tRNA-Arg-C34 site. This increase in methylation facilitates the generation of the pro-cancer tRNA-derived fragment (tRF)-Arg-CCG, which binds to Tribbles pseudokinase 1 (TRIB1) mRNA, inhibiting its expression and promoting EMT, thus enhancing cancer cell migration and invasion [86]. In ac4C modification, Zheng et al. found that NAT10 knockout impairs the proliferation, metastasis, and invasion abilities of HT-29 and LoVo colon cancer cell lines. Further experiments revealed that NAT10 promotes tumor progression by enhancing ferroptosis suppressor protein 1 (FSP1) mRNA stability [87]. For m1A modification, ALKBH1 is significantly overexpressed in CRC tissues and negatively correlates with overall survival in patients with CRC [88]. In Ψ modification, Zhang et al. reported that PUS is upregulated in CRC, and PUS7 promotes CRC cell proliferation by stabilizing sirtuin 1 (SIRT1), thereby activating the Wingless-related integration site/β-catenin (Wnt/β-catenin) signaling pathway [89].

Pulmonary malignancies

In lung cancers, the m6A reader YTH domain containing 1 (YTHDC1) regulates the stability and expression of FSP1 and PTEN mRNAs, thereby inhibiting cell proliferation, migration, and inducing apoptosis [90]. In m7G modification, Zhao et al. demonstrated that TRMT10C mediates m7G modification of circFAM126A, leading to reduced stability of circFAM126A. CircFAM126A is downregulated in lung cancer and significantly suppresses tumor growth both in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistically, circFAM126A promotes the ubiquitination of heat shock protein 90 (HSP90) and inhibits AKT1 expression, thereby regulating cellular glycolysis and suppressing lung cancer progression [91]. Regarding ac4C modification, NAT10 stabilizes SGK2 mRNA [92], promoting cancer development. In lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), NAT10 activates the CDK2/DNMT1 pathway [93], which weakens antitumor immunity.

In m7G modification, LUAD, the primary subtype of lung cancer, is particularly influenced by METTL1. Peng et al. found that METTL1 is upregulated in LUAD, enhancing the stability of Forkhead Box M1 (FOXM1) transcripts through m7G modification, leading to increased FOXM1 expression. Elevated FOXM1 transcriptionally suppresses the expression of PTPN13, promoting LUAD proliferation, invasion, and gefitinib resistance [94].

In non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), Sun et al. discovered that NAT10-mediated ac4C modification enhances the stability of TRIM44 mRNA, activating the PI3K/AKT pathway in NSCLC cells, conferring cisplatin resistance, and promoting cancer progression [95]. Yuan et al. further explored the relationship between ENO1 in A549 and PC9 cell lines, revealing that NAT10 promotes NSCLC development by mediating ac4C modification of ENO1 mRNA [96]. Ψ, a widely occurring RNA modification, is abnormally expressed in various cancers and can serve as a biomarker for cancer diagnosis and prognosis. PUS enzymes, the writers of Ψ modification, have central roles in this process. The expression of PUSs, such as the core enzyme DKC1, is significantly elevated in tumor cells, particularly in lung cancer, and its high expression is closely associated with tumor malignancy and poor patient prognosis [97, 98].

Genitourinary cancers

In bladder cancer (BCa), m6A modification plays multifaceted roles through various regulators. IGF2BP2 contributes to monocyte-macrophage recruitment and M2 macrophage polarization in the TME, fostering an immunosuppressive environment that promotes BCa progression [99, 100]. WTAP regulates the stability of TNFAIP3 mRNA, influencing BCa cell apoptosis [101], and enhances cellular antioxidant capacity through m6A-dependent mechanisms to suppress ferroptosis, thereby promoting cancer cell survival [102]. Recent studies have identified HSP90AB1 mRNA as a direct target of IGF2BP3, which activates the PI3K/AKT pathway, driving BCa progression [103]. IGF2BP3 further promotes cell cycle progression and chemotherapy resistance by binding and stabilizing CDK6 mRNA, suggesting that IGF2BP3 intervention could enhance chemosensitivity [104]. In contrast, METTL16 exhibits tumor-suppressive properties, with high expression correlating with improved survival of patients with BCa through m6A-mediated regulation of PMEPA1’s 3’-UTR and involvement in autophagy pathways that inhibit proliferation [105]. The m6A readers YTHDC1 and YTHDC2 show reduced expression in multiple solid tumors. In non-muscle invasive BCa, YTHDC1 functions as a tumor suppressor by binding the 5’UTR of SMAD6 mRNA to modulate SMAD6 expression [106]. In m5C modification, Wang et al. demonstrated the consistent upregulation of m5C regulators, including ALYREF, in BCa. ALYREF stabilizes PKM2 mRNA to enhance glycolysis, promoting proliferation, migration, and invasion. This research established the ALYREF/RABL6/TK1 axis as a contributing factor to BCa development [107]. Further studies confirmed that ALYREF promotes PKM2 stability through m5C-dependent mechanisms, driving BCa progression via PKM2-mediated glycolysis [108]. In ac4C modification, NF-κB p65 enhances AHNAK acetylation to potentiate ac4C-related DNA repair, promoting bladder carcinogenesis. Similarly, NAT10 facilitates ac4C modification of BCL9L, SOX4, and AKT1 mRNAs, enhancing their stability and contributing to BCa progression [109]. For m7G modification, METTL1 upregulation correlates with BCa occurrence and poor prognosis. Mechanistically, METTL1 promotes miR-760 maturation in an m7G-dependent manner, with increased miR-760 downregulating ATF3 mRNA levels [110]. Additional studies confirm that METTL1 silencing suppresses BCa proliferation, migration, and invasion through m7G tRNA modifications that regulate EGFR/EFEMP1 translation [111]. Moreover, m7G-3′-tiRNA LysTTT (mtiRL), a novel METTL1-catalyzed tsRNA, promotes BCa proliferation and migration by enhancing the interaction between Yes1 and ANXA2 and facilitating ANXA2 phosphorylation at Tyr24 [112]. These findings position m7G-modified tsRNA and METTL1 as promising therapeutic targets.

In prostate cancer (PCa), m6A modification plays diverse roles. YTHDF2 regulates p27 protein stability through m6A-dependent mechanisms, influencing PCa proliferation and migration [113]. YTHDC2 functions as an oncogene, promoting growth and invasion [114], while FTO-IT1 acts as an m6A writer complex inhibitor, suppressing p53 tumor suppressor activity by regulating epitranscriptional modifications of p53 downstream genes [115]. For ac4C modification, NAT10 promotes cancer development through two pathways: enhancing HMGA1 stability to facilitate cell cycle progression and mediating KRT8 acetylation to promote EMT [116]. DKC1 expression is elevated in high-grade and recurrent PCa, correlating with TR and MKI67 levels. DKC1 knockdown inhibits tumor proliferation without affecting apoptosis or senescence [117]. In A-to-I editing, miR-379 undergoes editing that reduces its growth-inhibitory effects in androgen-sensitive PCa cells. In addition, PCa antigen 3 (PCA3) regulates PRUNE2 levels through ADAR-mediated editing, influencing PCa proliferation [118].

In epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC), METTL16 exhibits tumor-suppressive activity by promoting metastasis-associated lung adenocarcinoma transcript 1 (MALAT1) degradation and upregulating β-catenin, thereby inhibiting EOC growth [119]. In m5C modification, YBX1 binds CHD3 mRNA to enhance its stability, activating a YBX1-E2F1 positive feedback loop that promotes homologous recombination and DNA damage repair pathways, sustaining oncogene transcription [120]. Regarding ac4C modification, NFE2L3 is highly expressed in ovarian cancer, correlating with progression and chemotherapy resistance [121]. In Ψ modification, PUS7 is highly expressed in ovarian cancer, promoting proliferation and self-renewal [122].

In cervical cancer (CC), IGF2BP1 exerts pro-oncogenic effects by stabilizing specific mRNAs, with its role being dependent on the functions of its target genes [123]. In ac4C modification, CircMAST1 acts as a tumor suppressor by inhibiting ac4C modification of YAP mRNA and promoting its degradation [124]. Abnormal NAT10 expression correlates with poor prognosis in CC, with HOXC8 activating NAT10 to enhance FOXP1 mRNA ac4C modification, increasing its stability and translation efficiency. This cascade induces GLUT4 and KHK expression, promoting cancer development [125]. Concurrent studies show that NAT10 promotes CC progression through ac4C-mediated enhancement of HNRNPUL1 and regulation of SLC7A5 expression [126, 127]. In m1A modification, TRMT10C catalyzes m1A modification at position 9 of mitochondrial ND5 mRNA. Due to the distinct GC content of mitochondrial tRNAs, TRMT10C is essential for maintaining their functional folding and stability. TRMT10C silencing suppresses proliferation, colony formation, and migration in gynecological cancer cells [128], positioning TRMT10C as a potential prognostic biomarker.

Head and neck cancers

Elevated NAT10 expression is strongly correlated with poor prognosis in nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC). Xie et al. found that NAT10 enhances ac4C modification of CEBPG, DDX5, and HLTF mRNAs, thereby increasing their stability and translation efficiency. Notably, HLTF forms a positive feedback loop with NAT10, and NAT10-mediated ac4C modification of DDX5 promotes HMGB1 expression, collectively driving cancer progression [129]. Gong et al. further demonstrated that NAT10 stabilizes the lncRNA SIMALR, which binds to EEF1A2, enhancing its GTPase activity and promoting protein synthesis. Additionally, SIMALR-induced phosphorylation of EEF1A2 accelerates the translation of ITGB4/ITGA6, ultimately facilitating NPC progression [130]. Xue et al. revealed that NAT10 mediates ac4C modification of SLC7A11 mRNA, increasing its protein expression and thereby reducing the efficacy of sorafenib treatment [131]. In m7G modification, Chen et al. identified upregulation of the METTL1/WD repeat-containing protein 4 (WDR4) complex in NPC, with both components significantly elevated and linked to poor patient prognosis. This complex activates the WNT/β-catenin signaling pathway and induces EMT, promoting NPC cell proliferation, invasion, and metastasis. METTL1 also enhances resistance to cisplatin and docetaxel by modulating the WNT pathway, suggesting the METTL1/WDR4 complex as a potential therapeutic target [132]. Regarding A-to-I editing, Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)-encoded miR-BART3 undergoes A-to-I editing in EBV-infected epithelial cells, disrupting its biogenesis and targeting processes, ultimately affecting its function and expression [133].

In laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma (LSCC), ATXN7 was identified as an indirect downstream target of YTHDC1, implicating YTHDC1 in LSCC pathogenesis. For ac4C modification, NAT10 promotes FOXM1 mRNA translation, exerting its oncogenic effects [134].

Hematologic malignancies

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML), a prevalent and highly heterogeneous hematological malignancy, is characterized by high relapse rates due to diverse triggers and complex microenvironmental influences [135]. The METTL1/WDR4 complex is significantly upregulated in patients with AML and is closely associated with poor prognosis. Knocking down METTL1 reduces tRNA m7G modification levels, effectively inhibiting AML cell proliferation and inducing apoptosis, highlighting METTL1’s role as an oncogenic driver in leukemia [136].

Promyelocytic leukemia protein (PML), a tumor suppressor that forms nuclear aggregates to maintain genomic stability, has its functional aggregates attenuated by histone lactylation [137]. This modification upregulates alkB homolog 3 (ALKBH3) expression, and the ALKBH3-demethylated tRNA significantly enhances protein translation efficiency, promoting malignant transformation.

Central nervous system malignancies

Glioma, a common and aggressive brain tumor, often carries a poor prognosis. In m6A modification, high YTHDF2 expression in patients with glioma correlates with worse survival, potentially due to YTHDF2-mediated degradation of specific mRNAs that activate proliferation and survival pathways, such as NF-κB signaling [138]. For m7G modification, both METTL1 and WBSCR22 are highly expressed in glioma compared to adjacent normal tissues, with METTL1 levels increasing with tumor grade. Fan et al. confirmed that METTL1 upregulation promotes m7G modification of phosphoglycerate kinase 1 (PGK1) mRNA, enhancing its stability and expression. Elevated PGK1 levels stimulate tumor cell proliferation and glycolysis, driving glioma progression. Silencing METTL1 suppresses these processes and inhibits tumor growth [139]. METTL1 also promotes tumor proliferation by activating the MAPK signaling pathway [140], while WBSCR22 enhances cell growth and metastasis via the PI3K/AKT/GSK3β pathway. WBSCR22 deletion inhibits AKT and GSK3β phosphorylation, downregulates CCND1 and β-catenin expression, and suppresses tumor progression [141]. Both METTL1 and WBSCR22 represent promising therapeutic targets for glioma. In Ψ modification, PUS7 is highly expressed in glioma and ovarian cancer, promoting tumor cell proliferation and self-renewal [142]. In glioblastoma stem cells (GSCs), both ADAR1 and global RNA editing levels are elevated. Inactivating ADAR1 or inhibiting the upstream JAK/STAT pathway via TYK2 suppression reduces GSC self-renewal and stemness [143]. ADAR2-mediated A-to-I editing redirects miR-589-3p from the tumor suppressor PCDH9 to ADAM12, promoting GSC migration and invasion [144].

Bone tumors

Osteosarcoma, the most common malignant bone tumor in adolescents and children, is associated with a high mortality rate [145]. Chemotherapy with doxorubicin and platinum-based drugs is widely used, but resistance often limits its efficacy. The METTL1/WDR4 complex is overexpressed in osteosarcoma and linked to poor prognosis [146]. Decreasing METTL1 or WDR4 expression reduces tRNA m7G modification levels and suppresses osteosarcoma progression in vitro and in vivo, while overexpression promotes cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. Mechanistically, the complex enhances tRNA m7G modification, improving the translation efficiency of specific mRNAs and driving tumor progression and chemoresistance [147]. Another study revealed that METTL1-mediated m7G methylation is downregulated in osteosarcoma tissues, influencing chemosensitivity by regulating FTH1 and pri-miR-26a. Specifically, METTL1 stabilizes FTH1 mRNA and pri-miR-26a through m7G modification, while mature miR-26a-5p inhibits FTH1 protein translation and promotes ferroptosis, thus increasing osteosarcoma cell sensitivity to chemotherapy [148]. These findings indicate that METTL1 plays dual roles in osteosarcoma through distinct RNA modification mechanisms, suggesting that modulating METTL1 function and m7G methylation levels may offer potential therapeutic strategies. In m6A methylation, METTL14 regulates mRNA stability and translation rates, mediating chemoresistance to all-trans retinoic acid [149]. Recent studies also identified the METTL5-YTHDF1-UBE3C-AHNAK signaling axis as regulating ferroptosis and driving osteosarcoma progression [150]. In ac4C modification, ac4C regulates the stability of the m6A reader YTHDC1 mRNA, enhancing m6A modification of PFKM and LDHA mRNAs, which promotes osteosarcoma progression [151]. In A-to-I RNA editing, studies have shown that A-to-I editing is significantly upregulated in osteosarcoma, increasing oncogene EMP2 expression by altering miRNA target regions [152].

RNA modification and tumor immunity

The immune system fundamentally dictates tumor recognition and clearance. However, tumors frequently exploit RNA modifications to orchestrate an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Dysregulated epitranscriptomic networks profoundly alter both innate and adaptive immune cell functions, ultimately enabling robust tumor immune evasion. In this section, we outline how diverse RNA modifications govern tumor immunity and drive complex mechanisms of immune escape (Table 3).

The regulatory role of RNA modification in tumor immunity

The relationship between RNA modification and tumor immune cell function

Immune cells within the TME are dynamically regulated by RNA modifications, dictating the critical balance between tumor suppression and progression. These epitranscriptomic mechanisms intricately modulate the recruitment, polarization, and effector functions of diverse immune populations. In this section, we review how RNA modifications govern both innate and adaptive immune cell phenotypes to reshape the tumor immune landscape (Fig. 4).

Innate immune cells

The immune system plays an important role in recognizing and eliminating infected or cancerous cells [153–155]. Immune cells, a key component of the immune system, are primarily classified into innate and adaptive immune cells [156]. Innate immune cells serve as the first line of defense, providing the foundation for antigen presentation and creating a microenvironment essential for subsequent adaptive immunity [157]. RNA modifications are involved in the response of innate immune cells to the TME, influencing their migration and activation status, which in turn affects the tumor’s immune surveillance and clearance capacity [158–160]. This section focuses on RNA modification and its close association with key innate immune cells, such as macrophages and NK cells, in tumor innate immunity.

Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) are pivotal in the TME, where RNA modifications regulate their functions through various mechanisms, thereby influencing tumor initiation, development, invasion, metastasis, and drug resistance [161, 162]. For instance, m6A RNA methylation can impact macrophage polarization and function, thereby modulating the immune response to tumors [163]. m5C RNA methylation modifies the phagocytic ability and cytokine secretion of macrophages in tumor cells, contributing to the establishment of an immunosuppressive TME [164]. The role of m6A modification in TAM polarization is particularly well-studied. YTHDF2, which is highly expressed in TAMs, significantly promotes their polarization towards a pro-tumor phenotype, facilitating the secretion of various cytokines and chemokines that drive tumor growth, invasion, and metastasis [165]. YTHDF3 regulates macrophage polarization via m1A RNA methylation, which has been linked to the pathogenesis of abdominal aortic aneurysms [166]. NSUN6 inhibits M2 macrophage recruitment through m5C methylation, negatively regulating immunosuppression [167]. Collectively, RNA modifications that regulate macrophage function not only influence tumor growth and metastasis but may also offer new targets for cancer immunotherapy.

The activity of natural killer (NK) cells is also modulated by RNA modification. m6A methylation influences NK cell antitumor activity by affecting their cytokine secretion and cytotoxicity. NK cells lacking FTO exhibit enhanced antitumor responses in vitro [168]. Moreover, the hypoxic conditions in the TME impair NK cell function, leading to reduced cytotoxicity, which is closely linked to RNA modification status [169]. The expression of METTL5 has been shown to have oncogenic effects in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), potentially related to the infiltration of immune regulatory genes, such as those involved in NK cell and macrophage infiltration [170]. Therefore, modulating the RNA modification status of NK cells may provide new strategies to enhance their antitumor activity, particularly in cancer immunotherapy.

Dendritic cells (DCs) are key antigen-presenting cells that activate T cells and induce antitumor immune responses [171]. Recent studies suggest that RNA modifications can influence the status and function of DCs in the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME), making these modifications a valuable tool for predicting the prognosis of patients with various cancers. For instance, a prognostic model for CRC based on m6A-related lncRNAs indicates that patients in the low-risk group have longer overall survival, and this group is associated with the status of DCs. This suggests that m6A modification may indirectly affect DC function in the TME by regulating lncRNAs [172]. In addition, in patients with CRC, GABRD expression is significantly positively correlated with DC infiltration levels, indicating that GABRD may influence the recruitment or function of DCs by modulating the immune microenvironment, thereby indirectly affecting tumor immune responses [173]. The RNA m5C methyltransferase NSUN1 is upregulated in various cancers, with single-cell sequencing showing high expression in DCs, and its elevated expression correlates with poor overall survival [174]. In low-grade glioma, m6A and m5C-related lncRNAs have prognostic significance, with a significant increase in various immune cells, including myeloid DCs, observed in the TIME of high-risk patients [175].

Neutrophils, the most abundant white blood cells in the human body, play dual roles in tumor progression, either inhibiting or promoting tumor growth depending on their functional state and phenotype in specific cancer types and stages [176, 177]. Tumor-promoting neutrophils, often referred to as the N2 phenotype, are more prevalent in the TME. Tumor cells secrete factors like G-CSF, GM-CSF, and IL-8, which drive neutrophil recruitment, contributing to tumor growth and metastasis [178, 179]. For example, in breast cancer, senescent neutrophils secrete exosomes containing piRNA-17,560, which can promote m6A demethylation of ZEB1 transcripts by upregulating FTO expression, enhancing chemotherapy resistance and EMT in breast cancer cells [180]. In papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC), downregulation of METTL3, an m6A methyltransferase, leads to reduced m6A modification, promoting the secretion of IL-8, attracting tumor-associated neutrophil infiltration, and accelerating tumor growth and metastasis [181]. In HNSCC, prognosis analysis based on m1A-related lncRNAs shows that high-risk patient groups have significantly higher levels of neutrophil infiltration compared to low-risk groups [182].

Adaptive immune cells

Adaptive immune cells include the primary lineages of T cells and B cells, along with a small subset of non-classical T cell subgroups, such as NKT cells, and antibody-secreting terminal cells, namely plasma cells [183]. T cell lineage members differentiate into mature subgroups, including CD8+ cells, CD4+ cells, and Tregs, in the thymus. Various methylation regulatory factors influence the adaptive immune response, primarily targeting T cells and their subgroups, thus affecting tumor cell proliferation, activation, and apoptosis.

m6A methylation regulates T cell differentiation, proliferation, and effector functions, while also modulating T cell activation and memory formation through the expression of transcription factors and signaling pathways [184, 185]. In mouse tumor models, YTHDF2 affects the tumor-killing ability of CD8+ T cells, and YTHDF1 is highly expressed in CD8+ T cells, regulating their multifunctionality through multiple mechanisms, ultimately influencing the antitumor immune response [186]. YTHDF1 enhances the translation of lysosomal cathepsin, inhibits type I interferon responses and antigen cross-presentation, impairs CD8+ T cell activation, and enables tumors to evade radiation-induced killing [187]. YTHDF2 also modulates the infiltration of CD8+ T lymphocytes into urothelial cells [188].

In addition, CD8+ T cell infiltration is influenced by other RNA modifications. m1A methylation negatively regulates the proliferation of CD8+ T effector cells in colon cancer [189]. In HCC, high m7G risk correlates with reduced CD8+ T cell infiltration and increased infiltration of regulatory T cells and macrophages [190–192]. METTL1 stabilizes CXCL8/Cxcl through m7G methylation, inhibiting CD8+ T cells and promoting intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC) progression [193]. NSUN5-dependent m5C RNA methylation inhibits CD8+ T cell infiltration in the immune microenvironment of GC, contributing to immune evasion [194]. Knockdown of RPUSD3, a key gene modified by pseudouridine, increases CD8+ T cell infiltration and inhibits HCC growth in vivo [195]. Deletion of PUS1 can suppress tumor progression and enhance T cell infiltration and function. Targeting PUS1 activates the dsRNA sensing pathway, boosting the antitumor immune response and improving immunotherapy efficacy [196].

Malignant tumors derived from B cells, including various types of B-cell lymphomas and B-cell leukemia, arise from abnormal B-cell proliferation and dysfunction [197]. m6A modification influences B cell development and function by regulating gene expression and cell signaling pathways within B cells [198]. In B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL), METTL3/METTL14 regulate m6A modification of key genes (such as PES1, ITGA4), enhancing their expression and promoting leukemia cell proliferation and survival [199]. Downregulation of WTAP expression inhibits the proliferation of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) cell lines [200]. METTL14 deficiency impairs the development of large pre-B cells into small pre-B cells by reducing m6A methylation levels, while YTHDF2 absence significantly disrupts pre-B cell proliferation [201]. In addition, ADAR1 in DLBCL promotes malignant transformation of B cells by editing the key base MAVS. The expression of ADAR1 in DLBCL is linked to T cell exhaustion, aiding tumor cells in evading immune surveillance [202].

The role of RNA modification in tumor immune evasion

Immune evasion is a critical hallmark of cancer, serving as a significant barrier to the effectiveness of anti-cancer therapies. Recently, the role of RNA modification in tumor immune evasion has become an emerging area of research. Tumor immune evasion is a multifaceted process involving complex interactions between tumor cells and the immune system [203]. Immune checkpoints are among the most extensively studied mechanisms and are widely applied in clinical settings [204]. In addition, cytokine-driven immunosuppression represents another key mechanism that facilitates immune escape. For example, key cytokines such as TGF-β and IL-10 are utilized by tumor cells within the TME to directly suppress the activation and function of effector T cells, while promoting the differentiation and immunosuppressive activity of Tregs, collectively weakening the antitumor immune response [205]. Tumor cells can also evade immune surveillance by altering the presentation of subclonal antigens, including dysregulation of MHC molecule expression or function, which ultimately promotes immune escape [206]. In this section, we review how RNA modifications facilitate these evasion strategies, specifically focusing on their impact on immune checkpoint regulation, the inactivation of effector T cells alongside Treg enhancement, and the disruption of antigen presentation (Fig. 5).

Immune checkpoint regulation

Immune checkpoints are regulatory mechanisms within the immune system that tumor cells exploit to evade immune surveillance, thereby promoting tumor growth and metastasis [207]. Various RNA modifications, such as m6A, m7G, m5C, Ψ, and A-to-I, primarily regulate PD-L1, jointly determining both the initial sensitivity and acquired resistance to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy. These modifications provide a theoretical foundation for combinatorial targeting of epitranscriptomic regulatory factors.

The m6A methyltransferase METTL3 promotes the m6A methylation of PD-L1, enhancing its stability and expression, which allows tumor cells to evade T cell-mediated attacks [208]. In a pan-cancer analysis, high expression of METTL1 is positively correlated with microsatellite instability, tumor mutation burden, and PD-L1, suggesting that METTL1 can serve as a predictive biomarker for ICB response [209]. In BCa, m6A-related lncRNA expression patterns correlate with upregulation of PD-L1 and alterations in the TIME [210]. The methionine-m6A-YTHDF1 axis promotes RIG-I degradation, inhibits the RIG-I/MAVS-IFN-I pathway, and diminishes the effectiveness of ICB in BCa [211]. In CRC, m5C modification enhances PD-L1 expression via NSUN5-mediated post-translational modifications, systematically increasing the immunosuppressive score and reducing the ICB response rate [212]. Despite these promising associations, the clinical meaningfulness of targeting RNA modifications for ICB sensitization remains to be fully established. A critical limitation is that most current mechanistic insights are derived from syngeneic murine models or in vitro co-culture systems, which often fail to recapitulate the profound metabolic and spatial heterogeneity of human tumors. Moving forward, large-scale prospective clinical trials incorporating epitranscriptomic biomarker stratification are urgently required to determine whether these preclinically robust findings translate into meaningful clinical efficacy.

Inactivation of effector T cells and enhancement of Treg

The inactivation of effector T cells is a central mechanism in tumor immune evasion. This process results from tumors exploiting RNA modification and other epitranscriptional mechanisms to synchronize antigen signaling with innate sensing capabilities, metabolic toxicity, and remote suppression through small extracellular vesicles. Ultimately, these factors lead to a reduction in CD8+ CTL numbers, infiltration, and cytotoxic function, completing immune evasion. YTHDF2 directly reduces the proportion of CD8+ T cells in tumors and inhibits CD4+ T cell activation, thereby weakening immune recognition in B cell malignancies [213]. tRNA m1A methylation modification mediated by the protein TRMT6 enhances ATP citrate lyase translation, supporting CD8+ T cell cholesterol metabolism and the antitumor activity of CRC cells [214]. High METTL1 expression correlates with increased Treg infiltration in clear cell renal cell carcinoma, promoting immune escape [215]. IGF2BP3 and YTHDF1 drive lactic acid accumulation through the m6A-dependent lactic acid metabolism axis, enhancing Treg differentiation and suppressive function, which promotes CC progression [216, 217].

In summary, these mechanisms constitute the core epitranscriptional switch that drives the imbalance between effector and inhibitory T cell populations, leading to tumor immune evasion.

Antigen presentation disorder

RNA modifications can also hinder antigen presentation, affecting tumor immunity. m6A methylation modulates antigen presentation by regulating the expression of MHC molecules. For example, alterations in m6A methylation can lead to MHC-I molecule degradation, reducing the tumor cell recognition ability of T cells and promoting immune evasion [218]. Lin et al. found that YTHDF1 enhances MHC-I and antigen degradation by promoting lysosomal protein translation, thus weakening antigen presentation and facilitating tumor immune evasion [219].

In GC, YTHDF1 accelerates the translation of lysosomal protease mRNA in specific DCs by binding to its m6A sites, leading to excessive tumor antigen degradation. Inhibiting YTHDF1 significantly upregulates MHC-II and IL-12 expression, enhances DC cross-presentation, and improves tumor elimination by blocking PD-L1 [220]. Recent research also shows that YTHDF2 is highly expressed in B-cell malignancies, where it recognizes m6A-modified MHC-II class molecule mRNA and promotes its degradation [221, 222]. This reduces MHC-II levels on tumor cell surfaces, helping the tumor evade immune detection. Targeting YTHDF2 can restore MHC-II expression, allowing tumor cells to re-engage with the immune system.

Emerging evidence underscores the profound impact of RNA modification regulators on the efficacy of cancer treatments. By dynamically remodeling the epitranscriptome, these regulators govern cellular adaptation to stress, thereby modulating responses to chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy (Table 4).

Impact of RNA modification regulators on cancer therapeutic responses in preclinical models

Chemotherapy response

In the context of chemotherapy, prolonged exposure to cytotoxic drugs acts as a powerful evolutionary pressure, triggering adaptive epitranscriptomic reprogramming that frequently drives acquired resistance. Consequently, this dynamic RNA remodeling enhances cellular survival and metabolic plasticity. For instance, under the selective pressure of oxaliplatin and 5-fluorouracil in CRC, the m6A reader YTHDF1 is actively enriched and promotes resistance by binding to m6A-modified NOTCH1, enhancing its translation [223]. The direct cellular consequence of this YTHDF1-NOTCH1 axis is the sustained maintenance of cancer stemness, which fundamentally desensitizes tumor cells to drug-induced apoptosis and drives ultimate clinical relapse. Similarly, in LUAD, oxidative stress induced by cisplatin upregulates the m5C methyltransferase NSUN5. NSUN5, alongside the reader YBX1, stabilizes GLUT1 mRNA, accelerating glycolysis and promoting homologous recombination repair to efficiently blunt the initial genotoxic insult [224]. Conversely, these epigenetic vulnerabilities can be therapeutically exploited. A novel carrier-free nano-epidrug dually targeting METTL1, an m7G tRNA modifier, and HDAC1 successfully disrupted the m7G-mediated translation of DNA repair proteins, drastically sensitizing osteosarcoma to doxorubicin [225].

Radiotherapy response

RNA regulators also profoundly influence radiotherapy outcomes by coordinating both tumor-intrinsic DNA damage responses and TME remodeling. Severe genotoxic stress and massive double-strand breaks generated by ionizing irradiation serve as the primary triggers for activating these epitranscriptomic survival networks. Upon irradiation, YTHDF1 accumulates in the nucleus to facilitate the m6A-dependent splicing of key DNA repair transcripts, including BRCA1 and TP53BP1. As a consequence, irradiated cancer cells acquire an anomalously robust DNA repair capacity, allowing them to evade radiation-induced apoptosis and continue proliferating [226]. Furthermore, radiotherapy itself induces another m6A reader, YTHDF2, which activates NF-κB signaling to promote the expansion of immunosuppressive myeloid-derived suppressor cells, thereby remodeling the TME into a highly radioresistant and immunosuppressive niche [227]. Consequently, targeted inhibition or deletion of YTHDF1/2 robustly potentiates the efficacy of both radiotherapy and radioimmunotherapy combinations.

Targeted therapy response

Resistance to targeted therapies, particularly tyrosine kinase inhibitors like lenvatinib, remains a major clinical hurdle in HCC. Recent profiling reveals that RNA methylation acts as a crucial bypass mechanism when primary oncogenic signaling is pharmacologically blocked. Faced with severe metabolic and hypoxic stress induced by lenvatinib’s continuous anti-angiogenic effects, the m7G tRNA methyltransferase complex METTL1/WDR4 is significantly upregulated as an adaptive survival response, thereby selectively enhancing the translation of EGFR pathway genes to drive acquired resistance [228]. Concurrently, as a compensatory reaction to continuous drug exposure, an elevation in m5C modification, driven by the DNMT2-YBX1-ACLY axis, hyperactivates the Notch signaling pathway via increased histone acetylation [229]. Targeting these specific RNA methylation axes effectively overrides lenvatinib resistance, highlighting them as promising intervention targets.

Immunotherapy response

Finally, RNA modifiers are critical regulators of ICB efficacy, frequently orchestrating the intrinsic and acquired mechanisms that lead to immunotherapy failure. In highly immunogenic tumors, the constant pressure of immune surveillance drives tumor cells to exploit epitranscriptomic machineries to create an immunosuppressive shield. For instance, the m6A methyltransferase complex METTL3/14 plays a distinct oncogenic role in melanoma, and its targeted degradation via specific peptide inhibitors alters immune-related gene expression, reversing T-cell exclusion and significantly enhancing the in vivo response to anti-PD-1 therapy [230]. In parallel, RNA acetylation machineries dictate immune evasion; driven by the necessity to evade cytotoxic T-cell recognition, the acetyltransferase NAT10 facilitates NPM1 acetylation, which directly stimulates PD-L1 transcription [231]. Pharmacological inhibition of NAT10 downregulates PD-L1 expression and synergizes robustly with anti-CTLA4 therapy across various tumor models.

Collectively, RNA modification machineries act as master rheostats of therapeutic sensitivity. Pharmacological intervention against specific writers, readers, and erasers holds immense translational potential to dismantle therapy resistance and broaden the clinical utility of existing cancer regimens.

Challenges and opportunities

While the literature highlights the profound impact of RNA modifications in oncology, a critical evaluation reveals significant variations in the strength of evidence across studies. Historically, many investigations have relied heavily on correlative observations derived from bulk tumor sequencing databases, which lack spatial resolution and fail to differentiate tumor-intrinsic modifications from those in the complex TME [232–234]. Consequently, findings that merely link high expression of RNA regulators to poor prognosis must be interpreted with caution until validated by robust functional assays.

Furthermore, conflicting results frequently emerge due to inherent limitations in current experimental systems. For example, the dual roles reported for certain m6A regulators, acting as either tumor promoters or suppressors, often stem from differences in cell line models, varying efficiencies of targeted gene silencing, and the absence of robust physiological contexts [235]. Conventional 2D in vitro systems fail to replicate the hypoxic and metabolic gradients of solid tumors, which are known to drastically influence the activity of epitranscriptomic enzymes like ALKBH5 and FTO [236].

To advance the field and ensure clinical meaningfulness, future research must shift from correlative profiling to rigorous mechanistic validation. The integration of high-dimensional multi-omics, such as single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics, with patient-derived organoids or humanized in vivo models is essential [237, 238]. Only by rigorously evaluating the spatiotemporal dynamics, off-target effects, and functional redundancies of these modifications can the scientific community identify robust, clinically viable therapeutic targets.

Additionally, targeting RNA modification regulators offers a promising but technically demanding therapeutic strategy. Writers such as METTL3, METTL1, NSUN2, and NAT10 may sustain malignant RNA programs, whereas erasers such as FTO and ALKBH5 can remodel immune evasion and drug resistance pathways. Readers such as YTHDF proteins and IGF2BP family members provide additional intervention points because they determine the fate of modified transcripts. Nevertheless, the same regulator can have different roles across cancer types, which makes patient selection essential.

Several barriers limit clinical translation. First, biomarker validation remains incomplete because expression of an RNA-modification regulator does not always predict its catalytic activity or transcript-specific function. Second, systemic inhibition may affect normal hematopoietic, immune, and stem cell compartments because RNA modifications regulate essential physiology. Third, delivery to solid tumors is challenging, particularly for RNA therapeutics and nucleic-acid-based inhibitors. Finally, specificity is a major concern because many enzymes act on multiple RNA classes and participate in broader protein interaction networks.

A feasible translational path may therefore require combination strategies rather than single-agent targeting. Examples include pairing epitranscriptomic inhibitors with immune checkpoint blockade, using RNA-modification signatures to stratify patients, and combining therapeutic RNA vaccines with agents that enhance antigen presentation. Such strategies should be evaluated with pharmacodynamic markers, spatial immune profiling, and models that better reflect tumor heterogeneity and prior treatment exposure.

Conclusion

RNA modifications provide a dynamic regulatory layer that connects RNA metabolism with tumor progression, immune escape, and therapeutic resistance. The evidence summarized in this review supports a central model in which m6A, m5C, m1A, m7G, ac4C, Ψ, and A-to-I editing influence cancer through transcript stability, translation, antigen presentation, checkpoint regulation, and immune-cell remodeling. This framework moves beyond cataloguing individual regulators and highlights how epitranscriptomic control can integrate genetic, metabolic, and immune signals.

At the same time, the field faces important limitations. Many reported associations are based on bulk expression profiles or prognostic models and require direct mechanistic validation. Findings from cell lines and mouse models may not fully capture tumor heterogeneity, spatial immune architecture, metastatic niche biology, or treatment history in patients. In addition, the same writer, reader, or eraser may act as an oncogene in one context and a tumor suppressor in another. These boundaries should be considered when interpreting therapeutic potential.

Future studies should integrate mutation-aware m6A mapping, single-cell and spatial multi-omics, ribosome profiling, proteomics, and functional perturbation. This will help identify which RNA modification events are drivers rather than passengers. Clinically, progress will depend on validated biomarkers, selective delivery systems, toxicity assessment, and rational combinations with immunotherapy, targeted therapy, and personalized RNA vaccines. With these advances, RNA modification biology may become a practical basis for precision oncology rather than only a descriptive molecular catalogue.
