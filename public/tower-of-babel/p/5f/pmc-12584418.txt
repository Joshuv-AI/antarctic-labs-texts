Conserved sphingolipid metabolism under transcriptomic diversity: a prognostic and therapeutic target in triple-negative breast cancer

Background

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is an aggressive subtype, characterized by the absence of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) expression. This heterogeneous molecular profile restricts treatment options for TNBC, rendering hormonal therapies and HER2-targeted treatments ineffective [1]. Chemotherapy remains the primary treatment for TNBC, but its efficacy is limited, and patients often develop resistance. The combination of immune checkpoint inhibitors and chemotherapy has become the standard therapy. In neoadjuvant treatment, anti-PD-1 antibodies such as pembrolizumab combined with chemotherapy significantly improve both pathological complete response rates and event-free survival [2, 3]. However, the predominant “cold tumor” immune microenvironment in TNBC limits immunotherapy responses [4]. Consequently, there is an urgent need to identify novel molecular targets and prognostic biomarkers to guide personalized treatment strategies for TNBC.

Transcriptional diversity, the degree of variation in gene expression profiles among different tumors or within individual tumors, has emerged as a critical factor influencing tumor behavior, treatment response, and clinical outcomes [5]. Previous key research [6, 7] has analyzed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from thousands of tumor samples, revealing recurring gene expression patterns. These meta-programs are expressed in malignant cell subsets across diverse tumors, explaining the transcriptional diversity in cancers such as breast cancer. Despite substantial heterogeneity, some transcriptional programs may remain consistently active, suggesting their fundamental role in tumor survival and progression. These conserved pathways could serve as reliable therapeutic targets [8]. For instance, conserved glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation pathways have been found to support the high-energy demands of rapidly proliferating TNBC cells [9, 10]. Similarly, immune-related transcriptional programs, while variable across tumors, often exhibit core conserved elements that influence the tumor microenvironment and immunotherapy response [11]. Furthermore, given their stability across diverse tumor area, conserved transcriptional patterns may offer more reliable prognostic information than those derived from single-region biomarkers.

This study aims to characterize the transcriptional diversity and identify conserved pathways in TNBC. By integrating bulk RNA sequencing, scRNA-seq, and spatial transcriptomics, we identified stable and functionally relevant gene expression programs. Combining computational and experimental approaches, we evaluated the prognostic value and therapeutic potential of key conserved pathways. Despite the marked heterogeneity of TNBC, essential transcriptional modules crucial for tumor survival are preserved, making them viable targets for intervention.

Methods

Genomic diversity and tumor immune microenvironment

Mutant-Allele Tumor Heterogeneity (MATH) was used to assess genomic diversity [12]. TCGA-BRCA samples were categorized as TNBC or non-TNBC status. Differences in MATH values between groups were visually compared using boxplots generated with ggplot2. Next, to characterize immune microenvironment features, we calculated z-score-normalized immune signature scores using the IOBR 2.0 package [13]. Boxplots were then used to visualize immune signatures that showed significant differences (p < 0.05) among MATH-based subgroups.

Processing of single-cell RNA sequencing data

Single-cell RNA sequencing data for TNBC samples was acquired from the GSE176078 dataset. The expression matrix was used to construct a Seurat object followed by removal of genes detected in fewer than five cells and cells containing fewer than 200 genes, with validated clinical metadata incorporated via the AddMetaData function. Cells annotated as “TNBC” were subsequently extracted and subjected to additional quality filtering (UMI counts >250, detected genes >200, mitochondrial gene percentage < 20%). Data normalization was conducted using the LogNormalize method (scale factor = 10,000), followed by identification of the top 2,000 highly variable genes through variance-stabilizing transformation (VST) and global z-score normalization. Batch correction was performed using Harmony with sample origin as the batch factor. Principal component selection incorporated JackStraw analysis (100 permutations) and elbow plot evaluation. Cellular distributions were visualized using UMAP dimensionality reduction based on original study annotations [14].

Non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) analysis

The Seurat object was partitioned by patient origin using SplitObject to generate individual tumor sample subsets. Multi-sample non-negative matrix factorization was then performed with a rank range of k = 4:9 using 2,000 feature genes. Meta-programs were subsequently extracted through getMetaPrograms with the following parameters: five core programs containing < = 50 genes each, clustered via WARD2 method and filtered for high-confidence genes (confidence threshold = 0.5). Results were visualized using plotMetaPrograms (Jaccard similarity ranging 0–0.8). After saving the meta-program gene lists, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was conducted against the Hallmark gene sets for all expressed genes.

Single-cell transcriptomic diversity analysis

Principal component analysis (PCA) [5] was performed on all TNBC malignant cells: after selecting highly variable genes and scaling the data, we generated 50 principal components and selected the top 30 principal components (determined via elbow plot inflection point) for downstream analysis. For each sample, we extracted cellular coordinates in PCA space, computed pairwise Euclidean distances, and used the average intra-sample cell-to-cell distance as a diversity metric. These diversity values were mapped to source sample information and stored as metadata. Samples were then stratified into high (Div-High) and low (Div-Low) diversity groups based on median diversity thresholds.

Cross-group cell communication analysis

The CellChat [15] object was initialized using Seurat subsets annotated by ‘celltype_minor’ along with the human ligand-receptor database. Preprocessing involved filtering low-expression genes and identifying overexpressed genes, followed by calculating ligand-receptor interaction probabilities and aggregating them to the pathway level. The mergeCellChat function was used to integrate data from different diversity groups, enabling cross-group comparison of cell-cell communication patterns. The analysis included generating bar plots to compare interaction numbers, performing differential network analysis to identify altered interaction patterns between groups, and assessing pathway similarity through ranking. This approach allowed characterization of communication differences between high- and low-diversity populations.

Identification of conserved functional pathways

Functional conservation analysis was performed on multi-region TNBC dataset [16] through the following workflow: First, we calculated ITH scores for each gene, defined as intratumor variance / (interpatient variance + intratumor variance) [17]. We then screened the top 30% (high ITH) and bottom 30% (low ITH) genes for KEGG pathway enrichment analysis, identifying the top 10 most significant pathways in each group. Furthermore, single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) combined with the IOBR metabolic database was employed to compute pathway activity scores, enabling quantitative characterization of interpatient heterogeneity (IPH) and ITH at the pathway level.

Spatial omics analysis

Representative spatial transcriptomic samples of breast cancer were obtained from GSE203612. Spatial transcriptomic analysis was performed following the pipeline proposed by He et al. [18]. To explore the relative expression of gene sets across different microregions, we employed the AUCell package [19] for analysis. The spatial expression patterns of AUC scores were visualized using the SpatialFeaturePlot function from the Seurat package. Additionally, the linkET package was applied to assess and visualize correlations between microenvironment components.

Cell culture

Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator. MCF-10 A cells were cultured in MEGM™ (Lonza, Switzerland). For MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-436 cells, DMEM medium (Gibco, USA) containing 10% heat-inactivated FBS (Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, USA) was used. All cell lines were passaged at 70–80% confluency by dissociation with 0.05% trypsin-EDTA for 1–2 min, followed by centrifugation (1000 rpm, 5 min) and resuspension in fresh medium at a 1:3 splitting ratio. Media were refreshed twice weekly to maintain cell health and proliferation. All procedures were performed under sterile conditions in a biosafety cabinet, and all consumables/reagents were autoclaved to prevent contamination.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Using TRIzol reagent (TIANGEN, China), total RNA was extracted from the cells. RNA was then reverse transcribed into cDNA. qRT-PCR reactions were conducted using Advanced qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (YEASEN, China). β-Actin served as the internal reference control for normalization. Relative mRNA expression levels of target genes were quantified using the 2 − ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences used are as follows: SPHK1: F: 5’-AACTACTTCTGGATGGTCAG-3’, R: 5’-TCCTGCAAGTAGACACTAAG-3’. β-Actin: F: 5’-TTGGCAATGAGCGGTTCC-3’, R: 5’-AGACAGCACTGTGTTGGC-3’.

CCK-8 assay

MDA-MB-231 cells were seeded in a 96-well cell culture plate, with 100 µL of culture medium and 3000 cells added to each well. The plate was then incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 24 h to allow cell adherence. The experimental group wells were treated with the sphingolipid metabolism inhibitor PF-543 (Cayman Chemical Company, USA), while the negative control group (0.1% DMSO solvent) and blank control group (with medium only) were set up. All wells were further incubated under the same conditions for 24 h to evaluate inhibitor effects. Subsequently, 10 µL of CCK8 solution was added to each well and incubated for 4 h for color development. Finally, absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader, with background values subtracted using readings from blank wells.

Live/dead cell staining

MDA-MB-231 cells were seeded at 5 × 10⁵ cells/well in 6-well plates and incubated for 24 h. Cells were treated with a SPHK1 inhibitor, with blank control and 0.1% DMSO solvent control groups included. After 24-hour treatment, the medium was centrifuged and washed to collect cells. Subsequently, cells adherent to the plates were digested with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA, followed by centrifugation and washing to collect the cells. All collected cells were resuspended in a light-protected staining solution containing Calcein-AM and propidium iodide (PI), then incubated at 37 °C for 20 min in the dark. The stained cell suspension was loaded onto confocal dishes and observed under a confocal microscope: live cells were detected via the green fluorescence channel, and dead cells via the red fluorescence channel. ImageJ software was used to separately count green-fluorescent live cells and red-fluorescent dead cells.

In vivo tumor model

Female BALB/c nude mice (4-week-old) were purchased from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology (Beijing, China). All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of The Third Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University (RY202508291). A total of 5 × 10⁷ MDA-MB-231 cells were injected subcutaneously into the right armpit of each mouse. Tumor volume was measured every two days.

Statistical analysis

For continuous variables, comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (two groups) or Kruskal-Wallis test (for three or more groups). Differences in categorical variables were assessed via chi-square test. Univariate Cox proportional hazards regression was used for initial screening of significant prognostic variables, followed by multivariate Cox regression to independently evaluate key predictors, with hazard ratios (HRs) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) reported. Kaplan-Meier survival curves visualized group differences in survival rates, and the log-rank test determined statistical significance. Predictive performance was quantified using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the concordance index (C-index). Additionally, Spearman’s rank correlation was applied to assess non-linear relationships. Unless otherwise specified, a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Genomic diversity may mediate an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment in TNBC

Genomic diversity, reflecting the degree of genetic variation, generates expression differences in immune-related genes that may underlie interpatient variations in response to cancer immunotherapy. Comparative analysis of samples from TNBC and non-TNBC patients revealed that TNBC samples had significantly higher MATH scores (Fig. 1A; p = 0.00018), a key metric for assessing tumor mutational allelic heterogeneity. This finding indicates that TNBC exhibits more pronounced genomic diversity, which may drive its greater heterogeneity in treatment response. Notably, tumors with low MATH scores showed significantly elevated immune cell infiltration (Fig. 1B-H), including B cells, dendritic cells (DCs), macrophages, neutrophils, natural killer cells, and T cells, suggesting that less heterogeneous tumors recruit immune cells more effectively for antitumor responses. This phenomenon was further corroborated at the pathway level (Fig. 1I-N): Low-MATH tumors exhibited enhanced activity in immune-related pathways such as immune co-stimulation, interferon receptor signaling, and TCR signaling pathways, implying more active T cell functionality and potentially stronger tumor-killing capacity. Conversely, high-MATH tumors demonstrated increased cell cycle regulation indices (Fig. 1O; p = 0.03), reflecting more dysregulated proliferation patterns in genomically diverse tumors that may directly compromise therapeutic efficacy. Collectively, tumor genomic diversity emerges as a pivotal factor influencing breast cancer treatment responses through its dual regulation of the immunosuppressive microenvironment and abnormal proliferative characteristics.

Identification of tumor cell diversity

This study analyzed 9 TNBC single-cell samples from the GSE176078 dataset. Original annotation identified 9 major and 29 minor cell clusters (Fig. 2A and D). Major populations included B cells, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), malignant epithelial cells, endothelial cells, myeloid cells, normal epithelial cells, plasmablasts, perivascular-like (PVL) cells, and T cells. Differential analysis of average epithelial marker gene expression [20] revealed distinct enrichment patterns between malignant and non-malignant cells (Fig. 2B and C), confirming that epithelial signatures effectively distinguish cancer cells from stromal components in the tumor microenvironment, providing critical basis for cell subtyping. Further analysis uncovered significant heterogeneity within malignant epithelial cells, which were subdivided into five functional subtypes (Fig. 2E): Cancer Basal SC (stem cell), Cancer Cycling, Cancer Her2 SC, Cancer LumA SC, and Cancer LumB SC. To characterize malignant stemness properties (Fig. 2F), we calculated average expression levels of 10 cancer stemness-associated genes (EPCAM, KRT19, PROM1, ALDH1A1, CD24, ANPEP, CD44, ICAM1, CD47, SOX9). These genes exhibited diverse expression patterns across tumor subclusters, demonstrating substantial transcriptomic heterogeneity at single-cell resolution.

Analysis of transcriptomic heterogeneity metaprograms in TNBC malignant cells

To deeply investigate transcriptomic diversity in TNBC malignant cells, we introduced the “ITH expression program” concept from single-cell RNA sequencing, defined as functionally coordinated gene sets. Previous glioblastoma studies confirmed four expression programs as core sources of transcriptional heterogeneity [21], while head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) research identified EMT-like and epithelial senescence (EpiSen) programs whose expression levels influence metastatic potential and drug response [20, 22]. We applied non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) to deconvolve ITH programs within malignant cells of each tumor sample, with each program defined by its top 50 signature genes. Iterative NMF across parameters (K = 4 to 9) identified stably recurring programs as robust program sets. To detect recurrent ITH patterns, we clustered these robust NMF programs based on shared highly-expressed genes, ultimately deriving 5 biologically significant metaprograms (MPs) (Fig. 3A). Functional enrichment annotated MPs and highlighted the top 5 core pathways per MP (Fig. 3B). Critically, detecting an MP in a tumor does not imply its uniform high expression across all malignant cells, but rather indicates coexisting subpopulations with divergent expression levels within that tumor. UMAP visualization projected MPs onto malignant subclusters (Fig. 3C and D), revealing MP1’s most pronounced enrichment in Cancer Cycling cells. We quantified MP expression differences across cell subtypes (Fig. 3E): While MPs represent conserved biological patterns across tumors, their differential utilization by malignant subclusters generates intricate intratumoral transcriptomic heterogeneity landscapes.

Transcriptomic diversity and cellular communication in TNBC

To accurately assess tumor heterogeneity, we used a previous reported computational method [5] for tumor cell-specific transcriptomic diversity scoring, circumventing influences from biopsy sampling variability. Principal components rather than raw malignant cell gene expression profiles were leveraged to quantify diversity, capturing key biological signals while reducing noise. The top 30 principal components were selected based on eigenvalues and permutation testing of significant components (Fig. 4A). Using the median diversity score, TNBC samples were stratified into high-diversity (Div-High) and low-diversity (Div-Low) groups. Div-High tumors showed significantly higher proportions of patients over 65 (Fig. 4B). We posit that age-accumulated genetic and epigenetic alterations may increase transcriptomic diversity in elderly patients. This group also had more metastatic breast cancer (MBC) cases (Fig. 4B), suggesting that metastatic adaptation to diverse microenvironments elevates transcriptomic diversity.

Subsequent cellular communication analysis revealed that although there was no obvious difference in communication intensity between the two groups, a notable increase in cellular interactions was observed in the Div-High group (Fig. 4C). Given that TNBC is predominantly of the basal-like molecular subtype, we focused our cellular communication investigation on basal-like cancer cells (Fig. 4D). In the Div-High group, Cancer Basal SCs exhibited significantly more active interactions with CAFs and circulating tumor cells. This pattern can be explained by the tendency of Cancer Basal SCs to establish connectivity with survival-supporting cells in highly heterogeneous microenvironments. CAFs interact with basal-like cancer cells by secreting specific factors such as PDGF-CC, FGF2, and SFRP1, promoting both self-renewal and drug resistance in Cancer Basal SCs [23, 24]. Concurrently, Cancer Basal SCs activate circulating tumor cells through paracrine signals, facilitating collaborative invasion and metastasis [25]. These findings suggest that Cancer Basal SC subpopulations engage in dynamic competition within high-transcriptomic-diversity environments, ultimately selecting for cell clones with invasive advantages.

The VEGF pathway in high-transcriptomic-diversity TNBC

Given the limited therapeutic options for TNBC, bevacizumab is being explored as a treatment [26]. We therefore investigate the VEGF pathway within the context of transcriptomic diversity (Fig. 5A-D). Our analysis reveals an increase in VEGF sender cells, leading to heightened concentrations of this pro-angiogenic factor in the tumor microenvironment, indicating intensified angiogenesis. Concurrently, we observe elevated mediator cells, particularly endothelial cells expressing RGS5 which correlate with abnormal, immature vasculature that shows increased VEGF sensitivity but also greater resistance to anti-angiogenic therapies, with RGS5 overexpression itself recognized as a biomarker for treatment resistance [27]. Moreover, expanding influencer populations suggest tumors establish more adaptable vascular signaling networks. This comprehensive remodeling (increased sender cells, optimized mediators, and amplified influencers) subjects unaltered receiver cells to stronger, more persistent VEGF signaling, capable of driving robust angiogenesis. These findings provide mechanistic insights into clinical limitations: the transient efficacy of single-agent bevacizumab (anti-VEGF-A) in TNBC requires combination therapies [26], as abundant sender cells sustain VEGF production despite partial blockade while resistant mediator cells maintain functionality. Crucially, PGF (Placental Growth Factor)-VEGFR1 interactions (Fig. 5E and F) emerge as the dominant pathway contributor, suggesting that high-diversity TNBC may pivot toward PGF-VEGFR1-driven vascular maturation to form stable, durable tumor-supporting vascular networks.

Identification of transcriptionally conserved sphingolipid metabolism

In exploring transcriptionally conserved targets in TNBC, we began with KEGG enrichment analysis to identify conserved pathway categories. Using multi-regional TNBC datasets, we calculated intratumor and interpatient variances for each gene and defined the heterogeneity index as intratumor variance / (intratumor variance + interpatient variance). Genes were classified accordingly: the low heterogeneity group heterogeneity index < 0.3) represented intratumorally conserved yet interpatient-distinguishable targets, while the high heterogeneity group (heterogeneity index > 0.7) reflected genes with high intratumoral heterogeneity (Fig. 6A). KEGG enrichment revealed that low-ITH genes predominantly clustered in metabolic pathways (Fig. 6B), whereas high-ITH genes were enriched in immune-related pathways (Fig. 6C). This suggests metabolic reprogramming serves as a core conserved mechanism for TNBC survival and proliferation, while diverse immune pathways reflect an evolutionary strategy where tumors maintain heterogeneous clone coexistence by establishing immunosuppressive microenvironments.

To pinpoint specific transcriptionally conserved metabolic targets, we computed activity scores for IOBR metabolic pathways via ssGSEA and evaluated their heterogeneity indices across multi-regional TNBC tumors. The sphingolipid metabolism pathway exhibited the highest interpatient heterogeneity and lowest intratumoral heterogeneity (Fig. 6D), marking it as an ideal biomarker for molecular subtyping and therapeutic targeting.

In breast cancer spatial transcriptomic samples, spatial enrichment analysis of sphingolipid metabolism genes [28] demonstrated significantly elevated activity in malignant and mixed regions (Fig. 6E-H), showing strong positive correlation with tumor cells. This spatial pattern indicates sphingolipid metabolic reprogramming is an inherent, conserved feature of tumor cells rather than being stromal-driven. Concurrently, its negative correlation with immune cells implies this pathway may promote tumor progression by suppressing antitumor immune microenvironments.

Sphingolipid metabolism-related genes predict TNBC prognosis

To investigate the prognostic value of sphingolipid metabolism in TNBC, we performed univariable Cox proportional hazards regression on sphingolipid metabolism-related genes, identifying three genes with significant prognostic relevance: SPTLC2, SGMS1, and SGPP2. A prognostic risk scoring model was subsequently developed through multivariable Cox regression. Patients were stratified into high-risk and low-risk groups based on median risk scores. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis (Fig. 7A) revealed statistically significant differences in overall survival between groups (log-rank test, p = 0.013). Further validation via time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis (Fig. 7B) demonstrated robust and consistent prognostic predictive capability, with area under the curve (AUC) values for 1-, 3-, 5-, and 7-year survival predictions confirming the model’s reliability. The discrimination capability of the model for high- and low-risk patients was validated in the TNBC dataset from METABRIC (Fig. S1A, S1B).

After univariable and multivariable Cox regression analyses (Fig. 7C), both the calculated risk score and pathological tumor stage were identified as independent prognostic factors influencing TNBC patients’ overall survival. Incorporating these factors, we developed a comprehensive prognostic nomogram (Fig. 7D) combining risk scores and tumor staging to provide personalized survival probability predictions for individual TNBC patients. This integrated model maintained discrimination accuracy (C-index > 0.7) for survival predictions spanning up to 10 years (Fig. 7E). Calibration curves (Fig. 7F) confirmed strong alignment between nomogram-predicted survival probabilities and actual observations, validating the clinical utility and accuracy of our sphingolipid metabolism-based model for long-term TNBC prognosis prediction.

Targeting the sphingolipid pathway effectively induces TNBC cell death

This study further validated the efficacy of targeting the sphingolipid pathway in inducing cell death in TNBC cells. Analysis of TNBC samples from TCGA (Fig. 8A) revealed that SPHK, a key target in canonical sphingolipid metabolism, exhibited significantly elevated expression in TNBC tumor tissues compared to normal breast tissues (p < 0.001), suggesting its potential oncogenic role in disease pathogenesis. This finding was corroborated at the cellular level: RT-qPCR analysis of representative TNBC cell lines (including MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-468) consistently showed significant upregulation of SPHK1 at the mRNA level (Fig. 8B), strongly supporting its viability as a therapeutic target.

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of targeting SPHK1, a critical node in the sphingolipid pathway, we employed PF-543, a highly specific small-molecule inhibitor. In vitro functional assays using MDA-MB-231 TNBC cells treated with varying concentrations of PF-543 demonstrated that PF-543 induced cell death in a significant dose-dependent manner (Fig. 8C). To visually confirm and quantify this cytotoxic effect, we performed dual-fluorescence live/dead cell staining assays. Compared to untreated control cells and the vehicle control group (0.1% DMSO, the solvent for PF-543), cells treated with an effective concentration of PF-543 exhibited extensive red fluorescence-positive signals alongside a dramatic reduction in green fluorescent cells (Fig. 8D). Quantitative analysis confirmed a significant increase in the proportion of dead cells (Fig. 8E), demonstrating the potent cytotoxic effect of PF-543 on TNBC cells. These findings highlight the crucial role of the SPHK1-mediated sphingolipid signaling pathway in sustaining TNBC cell survival. They further establish targeting this pathway, specifically SPHK1, as a highly promising therapeutic strategy, providing novel experimental evidence to address the current challenge of limited effective targeted therapies for TNBC.

To assess the role of targeting sphingolipid metabolism in in vivo tumor growth, we established a subcutaneous tumor model by injecting MDA-MB-231 cells. Compared with the control group, tumor growth in the SPHK1-targeted group was suppressed, with significantly lower volume and weight (Fig. 9A-D).

Discussion

In the era of personalized medicine, comprehensive molecular-level assessment has become crucial, particularly manifested by the search for biomarkers that can guide accurate diagnosis and targeted therapy [29–31]. Our study integrates TNBC genomic, transcriptomic, and spatial profiling data to reveal sphingolipid metabolism as a core conserved pathway transcending transcriptional diversity, offering a selection for addressing TNBC therapeutic resistance. Sphingolipid metabolism serves not only as a robust prognostic biomarker but also as a promising therapeutic target.

We demonstrate that genomic diversity is a core driver of immune suppression in TNBC. Elevated MATH scores in TNBC correlate significantly with reduced immune cell infiltration and suppressed immune pathway activity. This mechanistic link explains why TNBC often presents as an immunosuppressive “cold tumor” microenvironment resistant to checkpoint inhibitors, while suggesting that genomic diversity assessment could identify patient subgroups most likely to benefit from immunotherapy. Conversely, low-MATH tumors exhibit enhanced immune activity and may be more responsive to immunomodulatory therapies.

At single-cell resolution, we identified five recurrent metaprograms (MPs) in malignthelial cells. These MPs represent ITH transcriptional modules whose differential deployment across malignant subpopulations generates functional diversity. Tumor cell-specific diversity scoring revealed higher transcriptomic diversity in metastatic and elderly TNBC patients, suggesting migration adaptation and age-related genomic instability fuel transcriptional plasticity.

Cellular communication analysis in high-transcriptional-diversity tumors revealed adaptive network remodeling: cancer basal SCs preferentially interact with CAFs and circulating tumor cells via PDGF-CC/FGF2 pathways, forming a cooperative microenvironment promoting stemness and metastasis. Div-High tumors not only increase VEGF secretion but also expand RGS5 + intermediate endothelial cells, mediators of resistance to anti-angiogenic therapies. The dominance of PGF-VEGFR1 signaling in high-diversity contexts explains the transient efficacy of VEGF monotherapy in TNBC, highlighting the need for combination strategies targeting resistance-mediating cells [32].

A key finding is the coexistence of transcriptional conservation and interpatient heterogeneity. While immune pathways show high intratumoral variability, metabolic pathways, particularly sphingolipid metabolism exhibit striking conservation across tumor regions. This pathway displays the high interpatient heterogeneity and low intratumoral heterogeneity, positioning it as an evolutionarily constrained core dependency. Spatial transcriptomics confirmed that sphingolipid metabolism genes are intrinsically highly expressed in malignant regions and inversely correlated with immune cells, unveiling a mechanism through which this pathway modulates the tumor microenvironment by promoting immunosuppression. Previous studies have shown that SPHK1 can catalyze the production of S1P, which impairs T cell function by upregulating PD-L1 expression and inducing T cell exhaustion [33, 34]. Targeting SPHK1 may reverse the immunosuppressive environment through modulation of the S1P signaling network, holding therapeutic potential in TNBC.

Clinically, our integrated prognostic model incorporating sphingolipid pathway genes (SPTLC2, SGMS1, and SGPP2) reliably predicts TNBC survival independent of traditional staging. A nomogram combining genetic signatures and tumor stage provides clinically deployable risk assessment for up to 10 years. Crucially, inhibiting the rate-limiting enzyme SPHK1 demonstrates dose-dependent cytotoxicity in TNBC models. Overexpression of SPHK1 in TNBC tissues and cell lines confirms its pathogenic role, while PF-543-induced cell death validates pathway dependence. Unlike VEGF inhibition, the evolutionary conservation of sphingolipid metabolism makes it less prone to therapeutic escape under pressure.

Our study has several limitations. First, the quantification of transcriptome diversity was based on distances after single-cell PCA dimensionality reduction, which may miss non-linear heterogeneity. Additionally, we did not incorporate data such as epigenetics or proteomics to assess overall heterogeneity. Finally, our single-cell data were derived from only nine patients.

Conclusion

Within the context of transcriptomic diversity, we identify sphingolipid metabolism as a conserved functional pathway in TNBC, which may represent a potential prognostic and therapeutic target.
