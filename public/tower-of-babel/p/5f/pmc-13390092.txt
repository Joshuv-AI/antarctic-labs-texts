Evaluation of transvenous lead extraction in young adults: Insights from the University of California San Diego Lead Extraction Registry
Authors: Andrew Y. Lin, Anne-Sophie Lacharite-Roberge, Gordon Ho, Omar Aldaas, Sandeep Toomu, Travis Pollema, Ulrika Birgersdotter-Green
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTransvenous lead extraction (TLE) is an important aspect of lead management. The impact of younger age on procedural complexity is not well characterized.ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the procedural outcomes of TLE in young adults using the University of California San Diego Lead Extraction Registry.MethodsPatients who underwent TLE between January 2012 and December 2024 were included and stratified by age into 2 groups: ≤50 years (group 1) or >50 years (group 2). We compared procedural characteristics and outcomes between the 2 groups.ResultsOf 1025 patients in this study, 173 patients (17%) were ≤50 years old (group 1) and 852 patients (83%) were >50 years old (group 2). Group 1 was younger at the time of initial device implantation (28.2 ± 11.0 vs 62.0 ± 11.2 years; P < .001). Lead malfunction was the most common indication for extraction in both groups, followed by infection and system upgrade. With multivariable adjustment, younger age was associated with the need for upsizing the laser sheath or switching to a rotating mechanical sheath (odds ratio 0.977; 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.966–0.988; P < .001), lower chance of complete procedural success (odds ratio 1.024; 95% CI 1.003–1.046; P = .025), and increased fluoroscopy time (beta coefficient −0.183; 95% CI −0.207 to −0.061; P < .001). There were no differences in major (3.5% vs 1.8%; P = .079) and minor complications (1.7% vs 2.3%; P = .619).ConclusionTLE in young adults is associated with increased procedural complexity but without higher rates of complications. The findings highlight the importance of risk stratification and preprocedural planning.

Introduction

There have been significant advancements in cardiac implantable electronic device (CIED) therapy for cardiac pacing and prevention of sudden cardiac death over the past two decades. As CIED options have become more diverse, transvenous lead extraction (TLE) has become an integral aspect of comprehensive device management for indications such as device infection, lead failure, or device upgrade. A unique population worth special consideration are young adults with CIEDs. Given that pacing and defibrillation indications have encompassed younger patients, especially those with congenital heart disease or inherited arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathies, the lifetime burden of transvenous CIEDs has grown substantially. These patients often have devices implanted at a younger age, a known risk factor for lead complications, and are faced with an increased cumulative risk of infection owing to repeat procedures and the need for device upgrade or revision.1,2

Lead extraction has been demonstrated to be safe in young adults and can be performed with high rates of success.3,4 However, previous studies have hinted at increased procedural complexity in this patient population, possibly related to longer lead dwell time and younger age at device implantation resulting in robust lead fibrosis and calcification.5, 6, 7 Given the importance of appropriate lead management in young adults, we chose to evaluate the impact of age on procedural characteristics and outcomes in patients referred for TLE.

Methods

Patient selection and study design

The University of California San Diego Lead Extraction Registry is a prospectively collected database of clinical and procedural characteristics of patients who undergo TLE at our medical center. We included 1025 patients who underwent TLE between January 2012 and December 2024 and stratified them into groups 1 (age ≤50 years) and 2 (age >50 years). The exclusion criteria include patients who had planned open surgical extraction and those with implanted leads for less than 1 year. Patient characteristics and procedural outcomes were defined in accordance with the Heart Rhythm Society consensus statement and analyzed.8 Indicators of procedural complexity were compared between the 2 groups, including upsizing of the laser sheath or switching to a rotating mechanical sheath, the need for a femoral snare extraction tool, complete procedural success, procedural complications, fluoroscopy time, and procedure duration. Our study adhered to the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the University of California San Diego Institutional Review Board. A written patient consent was not required owing to the retrospective nature of the study.

Extraction procedure

Preprocedural computed tomography imaging with lead extraction protocol was performed to identify high-risk features such as vascular stenosis, lead adhesion, or endothelization.9 All extraction procedures were done with general anesthesia in the hybrid operating room with an electrophysiologist and a cardiac surgeon, monitored by a cardiac anesthesiologist with transesophageal echocardiogram and invasive arterial pressure monitoring. The extraction procedure has been described previously.10 Briefly, after the pectoral device pocket is exposed and the pulse generator explanted, a stylet is introduced into the inner lumen of each lead to retract the helix and attempt manual traction. If unsuccessful, we generally proceed with a laser sheath first (CV-300 Eximer Laser and SLS II, Spectranetics Corporation, Colorado Springs, CO), followed by upsizing of the laser sheath or switching to a rotating mechanical sheath (Evolution, Cook Medical Inc, Bloomington, IN, or TightRail Mechanical rotating dilator sheath, Phillips, Andover, MA) if needed. A 12F femoral sheath is used for insertion of an occlusive superior vena cava balloon, and femoral extraction tools were used when necessary, based on operator discretion. An additional 5F femoral arterial sheath is placed in patients with previous sternotomy.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are presented as numbers with proportions and compared using the χ2 test. Continuous variables are presented as mean with standard deviation and compared using the independent sample t test. To adjust for confounders in assessing age as a predictor of procedural complexity, multivariable logistic regression was used for categorical variables and multivariable linear regression was used for continuous variables. Covariates for adjustment were selected based on clinical judgment and a previous study4 and included sex, body mass index, bacteremia, CIED type, number of leads extracted, and lead dwell time. Comparisons were performed with the younger adults (group 1) as reference. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS for Windows version 30 (SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL). For all analyses, P < .05 (2 tailed) was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

Of 1025 patients included in this study, 173 patients (17%) were 50 years or younger (group 1) and 852 patients (83%) were older than 50 years (group 2). Patients in group 2 had higher rates of comorbidities, with the most prevalent being hypertension (23% vs 68%; P < .001), heart failure (35% vs 61%; P < .001), and atrial fibrillation (13% vs 47%; P < .001). The most common CIED type in both groups was pacemakers (41% vs 44%), followed by defibrillators (47% vs 31%) and biventricular devices (12% vs 25%). Procedural characteristics

Procedural characteristics are presented in Table 2. A total of 1878 leads were extracted from 1025 patients using traction (n = 214), laser sheath (n = 677), and a rotating mechanical sheath (n = 134). Patients in group 1 had longer lead dwell time (108.5 ± 72.3 months vs 90.5 ± 61.1 months; P = .003) and lower complete procedural success (93% vs 97%; P = .031) with no difference in laser or mechanical sheath needed (80% vs 75%; P = .136) and the use of femoral snaring tool (1% vs 1%; P = .667). There were no statistically significant differences in rates of major and minor complications between the 2 groups. The overall major complication rate was 2.0% in the entire cohort, including cardiac avulsion (n = 6), vascular laceration (n = 11), cardiac arrest (n = 2), tricuspid valve injury (n = 1), and procedural death (n = 1). Younger age as a predictor of procedural complexity

On crude analysis, more patients in group 1 required upsizing of the laser sheath or switching to a rotating mechanical sheath (38% vs 24%; P < .001), had longer fluoroscopy time (13.3 ± 11.9 minutes vs 10.6 ± 10.5 minutes; P = .007), and had lower complete procedural success (93% vs 97%; P = .031) (Table 2). Discussion

The broad spectrum of indications for cardiac pacing and prevention of sudden cardiac death in heart failure and arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy has led to a significant increase in CIED implantation over the past decades. Consequently, the number of patients who experience device failures has also increased, thrusting the management of device complications, especially lead extraction, to the forefront of device management considerations. Given the necessity for lifelong device therapy in the younger adult population who experience device complications, a comprehensive understanding of the challenges encountered by TLE is essential. In a large, single-center retrospective observational study, we report no difference in procedural complication and mortality rates between the younger and older cohorts. However, younger patients had increased procedural complexity with findings of longer fluoroscopy time, higher likelihood of needing to upsize the laser sheath or switch to a mechanical rotating sheath, and lower rates of complete procedural success.

Several studies have investigated the impact of age on TLE outcomes. A large, single-center study of 5090 patients who underwent extraction was stratified into 4 age groups: younger than 65 years, 65–74 years, 75–84 years, and 85 years or older.4 Complete procedural success exceeded 95% in all groups, and there was no significant association between age and procedural complications. Similarly, a separate large, single-center study of 774 extraction patients using the age of 40 years as a cutoff did not report any difference in procedural success or complication rate.5 However, younger patients did require femoral extraction tools more frequently, suggesting the challenging nature of TLE in this group. In addition, Tan et al11 reviewed the Nationwide Readmissions Database of patients who underwent TLE owing to CIED infection between 2017 and 2020 and noted higher rates of conversion to open heart surgery in patients younger than 50 years. The message from these findings supports the results of our study that TLE in young patients can be more complex and prudence should be given to risk stratification and preprocedural planning. Importantly, despite increased procedural complexity, we did not observe higher rates of complication or mortality in the younger adult cohort.

As observed in our study, young patients who were referred for TLE were also younger at the time of initial CIED implant, a known risk factor for lead failure owing to their life expectancy exceeding that of conventional devices.2 In an early study evaluating the longevity of implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) leads, the estimated lead survival rate at 5 and 8 years was only 85% and 60%, respectively, with a progressively higher rate of failure after 10 years.1 Another study estimated a 6.5% failure rate at 5 years and 16.4% at 10 years.12 To that point, it is notable that lead malfunction was the leading indication for TLE in both groups (proportionally higher in the younger group), followed by CIED infections and system upgrade. Furthermore, the younger cohort in our study also had significantly longer lead dwell time, a known risk factor for difficult extraction.13,14 It has been postulated that younger patients have a more robust inflammatory response to surgical stress, which may result in more fibrosis and calcification around the leads creating dense vascular adhesions and lead binding sites.7,15

Appropriate risk stratification and preprocedural planning are imperative in young patients with indications for TLE. Our group had previously demonstrated the utility of preprocedural computed tomography to identify patients with severe lead adhesions who may be at risk of complex extraction.9 Other risk prognostication tools involving patient characteristics and lead age have also been proposed to guide the extraction strategy.16,17 Enhanced understanding of the increased procedural complexity may also guide informed consent and shared decision making when discussing with patients and their families regarding lead extraction. Referral of these patients to experienced, high-volume centers should be the standard of care, and patients should be evaluated by a multidisciplinary team.18 Finally, there has been substantial progress in the implantation and management of subcutaneous ICDs, extravascular ICDs, and leadless pacemakers. In the appropriate patients, reimplantation of these nontransvenous-lead CIEDs should be considered to preserve vascular patency and decrease the number of indwelling leads over time.

We acknowledge several limitations to our study. First, this was a single-center study, so our results may not generalize to other centers with different extraction experiences. In addition, what is defined as “young” can be subjective and the decision to use the age of 50 years as a cutoff is somewhat arbitrary given that there is no consensus in the literature (previous studies have used a range between 40 and 65 years).4,5,11 However, in a survey of 34 centers from the European Heart Rhythm Association on the management of malfunction leads, 63% of responders considered younger than 50 years of age to be considered “young” in lead management.19

Conclusion

TLE can be performed safely in young adults but can be associated with increased procedural complexity, as evidenced by longer fluoroscopy time, higher likelihood of upsizing the laser sheath or switching to a mechanical rotating sheath, and lower rates of complete procedural success. The findings highlight the importance of performing extractions at experienced medical centers and the value of risk stratification and appropriate preprocedural planning.
