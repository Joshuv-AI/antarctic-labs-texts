Does expansion of college education benefit urban entrepreneurship and innovation in China?
Authors: Han Zhang, Dandan Zhang, Yanming Jin
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractIt is important to determine whether China's unprecedented expansion of college education (ECE) since 1999 has made a significant impact on the urban entrepreneurship and innovation (E&I). Using the data of 284 cities at the prefecture level and above from 2000 to 2020, this study empirically identifies the average treatment effect (ATE) of China's ECE on the urban E&I with its spatial spillover and explores two mediating channels (i.e., talent accumulation, and labor misallocation) to reveal how China's ECE impacts urban E&I. The results verify that with strong significance and robustness, the ECE for either undergraduates or master's students wholly matter for the E&I of cities, especially cities located in eastern or northeast economic zone，cities with a population less than 5 million or the ones without “Mass Innovation and Entrepreneurship Demonstration Bases”. Meanwhile, both the “Matthew effect” and the spatially “beggar-thy-neighbor” of ATE induced by the ECE are also be confirmed. Arguably, the China's ECE contributes either more talents accumulation or less labor misallocation, thereby furtherly boosting urban E&I. Above results were beneficial, especially at the decision-making for the developing countries to promote urban innovation and entrepreneurship under the scenario of college enrollment expansion.Keywords: Expansion of college education, Urban entrepreneurship and innovation, China, Talent accumulation, Labor misallocation

Introduction

Ever since China introduced the “Century-Oriented Education Revitalization Action”1 in 1999, which aimed to expand college education, the number of students enrolled in regular colleges and universities across the country has increased almost four times. In 2000, there were 1.16 million undergraduates, whereas in 2020, the number rose to 4.43 million.

The gross enrollment rate of China's higher education has risen rapidly from 59 % to 90 %, and the number of colleges and universities have also increased from 603 to 1270. The ECE has led to a rapid growth in the scale of higher education in China within a short period of time. This has resulted in profound changes in China's human capital structure and labor force supply, both of which are important driving forces for innovation and entrepreneurship.

Plus, during the same period, China's innovative performance has also improved notably, with the number of China's total R&D investment and R&D intensity growing by more than 24.7 times, from 89.57 billion yuan to 2212 billion yuan and 2.23 times, from 1.0 % to 2.23 %, respectively. Meanwhile，the number of newly registered businesses has upsurged from 3.2 million to more than 29 million.

Thus, we are inclined to ask the question: Does China's unprecedented ECE proposed in 1999 play a supporting or opposing role in its innovation and entrepreneurship? Here, we examine whether unprecedented ECE proposed in 1999 contributes to urban E&I in China, especially the influence of ECE at three educational levels (i.e., expansion of undergraduate enrollment, master's enrollment and doctoral enrollment) with their respective regional heterogeneity and spatial spillovers. Moreover, we try to examine the mechanisms regarding how China's ECE impacts urban E&I through its effect on the either talent accumulation or labor misallocation.

There is a large body of the literature related to the effects of ECE on innovative or entrepreneurial activities; yet, most of them have not reached a unified conclusion. Some scholars have reported positive conclusions because ECE would increase the stock of high-end human capital and quality of the teaching staff, thereby promoting innovation or aggregate productivity [1,2]. Nonetheless, some scholars have pointed out that ECE could lead to excessive investment in talent training or education, thus crowding out R&D investment and intensifying competition among graduate job seekers in the labor market, which possibly hinder productivity or innovation [3]. Also, a strand of literature has documented some cynicism and concerns for the ECE in developing countries, with poor quality of education, a lack of textbooks, low level of teacher's engagement, and weak labor markets [[4], [5], [6]].

Regarding on the socioeconomic effects of ECE or higher education, some early studies have paid attention to its impacts on labor market [7], housing price [8], earnings inequality [9], urbanization [10], firm performance [11], productivity [12], and so on. Some studies have confirmed that due to ECE, earnings are lessened [13], employment thresholds or pressures are heightened [14], and reducing existing individual inequality are limited [15]. Recently, some studies have explored the effects of China's ECE on innovation or entrepreneurship in micro-corporate level [16,17], lacking of discussion at the mesoscopic city level with typically the channels through which ECE operate.

Relatively, most of researches on China's E&I only qualitatively describe or interpret implication, inclusion or principle of E&I policy [[18], [19], [20]]. Part of quantitative studies only focus on establishing a simple connection between ECE and E&I. For instance, Wang and Tan (2020) examine China's mass innovation/mass entrepreneurship, with particular attention to the community of maker-entrepreneurs in the new techno-political ordering of society and their social territories. They argue that the cultural frame of mass entrepreneurship is an attempt to establish a new value in the politics of production in a manner to unleash bodily actions of existing labor to transform themselves [21].

Therefore, this study makes three major contributions to the existing literature. First, our study contributes to the literature by providing evidence at the mesoscopic city level that higher education serves as an important tool to promote urban innovation and entrepreneurship. Specifically, using a panel data of 284 cities at the prefecture level and above from 2000 to 2020, we investigate the policy-induced exogenous shock of China's ECE issued in 1999 on urban E&I. Specifically, addressing the potentially counterfactual issues or endogeneity concerns and taking China's ECE as a quasi-experiment, we adopt various DID (difference-in-differences) methods to evaluate the average treatment effect (ATE) of China's ECE for undergraduate, master's and doctoral students on urban E&I. Not only we effectively confirm that China's ECE for undergraduates or master's students is mostly beneficial to urban E&I through diversified robustness test, such as placebo test, changing estimated samples, IV estimation, PSM-DID and so forth, but also duly conduct regional heterogeneity tests from three respects (i.e., economic zones, population size, entrepreneurship and innovation base).

Furthermore, considering that previous studies mostly provide micro evidence of China's ECE on innovation and entrepreneurship; however, its spatial effects are unclear. Therefore, we build several spatial DID model to capture the heterogeneous spatial effects of China's ECE on urban E&I. Our evidence also contributes to the debates regarding the spatial spillover of ATE (i.e., spatial ATE) derived from ECE, identifying that on different extent, local implementing the China's ECE for students with different degrees and doctoral students might harm the urban E&I of neighboring cities with different geographically distance.

Finally, we contribute to the literature by identifying the role of channels, namely, human capital and labor misallocation, through which ECE affects urban innovation and entrepreneurship. Estimated by mediating effect models, our results add to the growing literature concerning the determinants of the effectiveness of ECE, it is crucial to improve human capital stock and labor allocation efficiency. Specifically, on the one side, growing pool of highly-educated graduates resulted from China's ECE lowers R&D or entrepreneurial learning costs and then promotes urban E&I that intensively needs knowledge and talents. On the other side, with the sharply rising numbers of college-educated job seekers, the ECE allows China to transfer high-skilled labor to high-skilled or skill-intensive industries, thereby reducing labor misallocation the diminishing and benefit urban E&I. Also, policy implication based on empirical results can be generally applied to many other developing countries that have implemented or are preparing to implement policy of enrollment expansion.

The remainder of this study is organized as follows. Section 2 puts forward the theoretical analysis and research hypothesis. Section 3 gives preliminary analysis of urban E&I in China. Section 4 is composed of model specification, variable selection and data description. Section 5 presents the empirically validation including benchmark tests, robustness test, regional heterogeneity tests and spatial DID test. In Section 6 empirically testifies driving mechanisms. Section7 concludes and puts forward policy implication as well as the limitations.

Theoretical analysis and research hypotheses

Talent accumulation hypothesis

In the endogenous growth model of human capital proposed by Lucas (1988) [22], capital is categorized into two forms: physical and human. Based on long-term production supplies, with diminishing marginal returns, physical capital, such as machinery, equipment, and buildings, dominates in the classic growth model. Differentially, the latter, that is, human capital, refers to the productive force form in which economic subjects acquire specialized knowledge and skills through investing time to receive education and training to obtain future returns; therefore, human capital has increasing marginal returns [23,24]. Particularly, with the arrival of the knowledge economy era, human capital significantly exceeds physical capital in terms of both quantity and returns.

Although ECE may delay the initial employment time of graduates, it improves the quality of human capital in the long term [25]. Thus, getting a higher education or ECE is conducive toward enhancing individual ability, which implies that innovation in different regions can be influenced by heterogeneous human capital accumulation [26,27]. Generally, highly educated talents who have undergone long-term education or training have a more significant role in promoting innovation or entrepreneurship, which is reflected by the fact that highly educated talents have a more prominent ability to learn new knowledge and use new technologies. This is a dynamic process of driving innovation via continuous knowledge creation and transfer. Inevitably, highly educated talents are more inclined to undergo various training activities, which further promotes the accumulation of knowledge and experience, decreases the decline of knowledge and skills with age, and enhances resistance to the impact of technological change [28,29].

In Addition, accompanied with the increase of human capital brought about by the ECE, the input of physical capital, increase of teaching staffs and the scientific research funding, also producing a driving force for urban entrepreneurship or innovation [21,30]. Meanwhile, central or local governments provide corresponding policy support to ECE in terms of land prices, infrastructure support, and faculty recruitment, in order to attract public, private, or third-party entities to participate in the construction of new projects or campuses, effectively driving the development of knowledge-based industries led by universities and creating a favorable atmosphere for urban innovation and entrepreneurship. Labor misallocation hypothesis

Referring to Murphy et al. (1991) [31] and Baumol (1996) [32], the contribution of education to aggregate productivity depends on how talent and skills are allocated across different sectors. The ECE has significantly increased the number of students receiving higher education and the potential pool of research and development personnel in the short term, which might do good to urban E&I. However, when the population of higher education graduates reaches a certain scale, college graduates with more education will be unable to find jobs that match their professional skills. Thus, they have to lower their job expectations and compete for occupations with lower professional and technical requirements against labors with low-levels of education [33,34]. In that situation, both high-educated and low-educated workers will face the issue of labor misallocation or skill mismatch, intensifying labor market competition, raising the threshold of employment or unemployment rate, and lowering marginal return of graduates than the corresponding resource investment, which phenomenon is referred to as “overeducation” or “overskilling” [[35], [36], [37], [38], [39]].

Unless the labor market is able to effectively mop up the newly added highly educated labor force, the probability of labor misallocation among workers will not increase with ECE. For instance, the late 1980s information technology revolution rapidly increased the demand for information technology professionals in the US labor market, leading to a shortage of graduates in related fields. Timely ECE helped fill the market gap, ultimately solidifying the US's leadership position in the information technology revolution and creating significant economic benefits [[40], [41], [42]] (see Fig. 1), we proposed:

The China's ECE might be detrimental to urban E&I, if it exacerbates the labor misallocation between employment demands of graduates and supplies of actual jobs.

Preliminary analysis of urban E&I in China

To represent the level of urban E&I in China We use the “Index of Regional Innovation and Entrepreneurship in China”2 at prefecture (municipality) and above -year level released by the Peking University Open Research Data Platform, which mainly focus on three core elements, i.e., entrepreneurship, capital, and technology.

Fig. 2, Fig. 3 shows the value of overall E&I and the top 15 cities of overall E&I in China between 2000 and 2020, respectively. On the one hand, generally, the urban E&I in China has an upward trend and the average value of national E&I is 61.32 from 2000 to 2020; the urban E&I of both eastern China (76.30) and central China (63.99) are higher than this value. Furthermore, in 2000, the overall E&I gap between central, western, and northeastern and eastern China are 18.48, 30.01, and 19.25, respectively; in 2020, these gaps narrow to 5.04, 17.47, and 18.47, respectively. Besides, the annual average growth rate of the four major economic zones is 2.69 % for eastern China, 4.50 % for central China, 5.67 % for western China, and 3.77 % for northeast China, wholly reflecting that there is a narrowing regional disequilibrium or gap among economic zones from 2000 to 2020. In terms of ranking, the east and central regions remain the top two, yet the northeast China have dropped since 2017 from the third one to the last one while the western China behaves the opposite.

The possible explanations for above results are that: On the one side, increasing sufficient E&I awareness and market-oriented economy, cities in the eastern China have endowment advantages derived from abundant E&I resource comparatively, with the prominent scale effects of innovation clusters, the relatively open entrepreneurial environment and continuous policies on talents attraction or tax breaks for supporting E&I. Since China joined the WTO in 2000, the eastern region has benefited the most from international trade and economic globalization, especially cities in the Pearl River Delta and the Yangtze River Delta have become China's highly competitive E&I clustering zone.

On the other side, since strategy called “the Rise of Central China” began, the cities in Central China such as Wuhan, Changsha, Hefei, Zhengzhou and the like, have further clarified urban industrial positioning, taken colleges and universities, high-tech parks, and technological-industries complexes as carriers, to accelerate the agglomeration of talents and entrepreneurs, and optimize the layout of high-tech industries, actively undertake high-end industries, and fully release the potential of E&I. Analogously, both the “Great Western Development Strategy” issued in 1999 and the “Belt and Road Initiative” put forward in 2013, give the western China strong impetus and macro-policy guidance. Even though the foundation of western China is weak with the relatively scarce high-tech industries and newly-established firms, however, the latecomer advantage or potential vitality of E&I in western China, especially provincial capital or first-tier cities, such as Xian, Chengdu, Chongqing, get a full release. Conversely, dominated by state-owned enterprises and the old heavy industries, the northeast China is filled with heavy bounding of planning economy and solidified bureaucratic consciousness, plus backward production capacity and brain drain, resulting in the gradual deterioration of urban entrepreneurial environment and innovation market.

Model specification, variable selection, and data description

Model specification and variable selection

Based on the abovementioned analysis and hypothesis, to estimate the ATE of China's ECE on urban E&I, this study considers China's ECE as an exogenous quasi-experimental shock, and then uses the DID model, where of the specific equation is given as follows:

where i = 1, …, N, and t = 1, …, T. N is the cross section and T is the time dimensions of the panel. EI is the dependent variable, reflecting the level of urban E&I in China measured by the IRIEC. Treat is a city dummy variable. If there is at least one college or university for undergraduates set up in city i between 2000 and 2020, then Treat = 1; otherwise, Treat = 0. Post is a time dummy variable. Since China's ECE policy was implemented in 1999, the first batch of graduates got a bachelor's degree in 2003, master's degree in 2006, and doctoral degree in 2009. We assumed that the ECE began to have an impact on urban E&I in 2003 (Post_2003), 2006 (Post_2006), or 2009 (Post_2009); when the range of variable t was between 2000 and 2003 (or between 2000 and 2006 or between 2000 and 2009), Post_2003 = 0 (or Post_2006 = 0 or Post_2009 = 0). Otherwise, Post_2003 = 1 (or Post_2006 = 1 or Post_2009 = 1). Thus, the regression coefficient β is used to measure the ATE of China's ECE for undergraduates (Treat × Post_2003)/master's students (Treat × Post_2006)/doctoral students (Treat × Post_2009) on urban E&I. That is, the coefficient β of interaction Treat × Post_2003/2006/2009 is used to explore whether the gaps in the E&I values exist between cities with colleges or universities (i.e., treatment group cities) and cities without such institutions (i.e., control group cities) after issuing policy of the ECE for undergraduates/master's students/doctoral students.

X refers to be control variables that affect the urban E&I in China, including: ①government support (gov), which is expressed by the proportion of government education expenditure in GDP; ②financial support (fin), which is expressed by proportion of sum of year-end both deposits and loans of all financial institutions in GDP; ③urbanization rate (urb), expressed as the proportion of the urban population in total population; ④capital stock (cap), which is calculated by method called perpetual inventory, and the specific formula is: capi,t=Ei,t-1+(1-δ)*capi,t-1, capi,2000=Ei,2000/(g+δ), where capi,t is the capital stock of city i in year t, and Ei,2000 is the gross investment in fixed assets on the city i in 2000, δ is the depreciation rate (10 % set in here); g is the geometric average growth rate of investment in fixed assets in city i from 2000 to 2020. μi is city-fixed effects. τt is time-fixed effects. εit is a random error.

Data description and variable statistics

After deleting sample of some cities with too much data missing, the final sample of this study covers 284 prefecture-level and above cities in China from 2000 to 2020. Some missing values were filled by linear interpolation. Avoiding potential heteroscedasticity and the dependence on the regression model setting, we took all variables as their natural logarithm. To avoid the influence of extreme values, all control variables are winsorized by 1 %. The data of urban E&I (i.e., IRIEC) is from issued by the Peking University Open Research Data Platform, and other data involved is from the China Urban Statistical Yearbook. Robustness tests

To check the robustness of above basic results estimated by model (1), we use several methods including placebo test, substituting and separating the dependent variable, excluding specific samples, PSM-DID estimation, and instrumental variable (IV) estimation.

Placebo test

In the placebo test, first, the full sample is grouped according to whether there is a college or university set up in city i, and then a certain year is randomly selected between 2000 and 2020 as the setting time of China's ECE, and the aforementioned process is repeated 500 times. As shown in Fig. 6, the normal part of the coefficients β are located on both sides of the zero line, that is, the estimated coefficients β is not statistically different from 0 and most of them are less than the real coefficient β of interaction Treat × Post_2003 and Treat × Post_2006, while only A little more than half of them are less that of Treat × Post_2009 in Table 2, indicating that China's expansion of either undergraduate or master's degree enrollment has significantly boosted urban E&I, rather than expanding doctoral enrollment. Thus, the benchmark results are robust.

IV estimation

Considering that the endogeneity of the China's ECE in model estimation, interfering with the benchmark results, we take the cities located in the provinces3 where the Christian church established junior high schools in 1919 offer by China Continuation Committee as the instrumental variable for cities implementing policy of the ECE (i.e., the treatment group), indicated as the treatment group variable, Treat_IV, which, on the one side, positively correlate with the probability to have a college or university, because the more established junior high schools in a city, the richer the potential educational culture and resources that is an important basis for establishing a college, satisfying the condition for relevance of effective instrumental variables; on the other side, hardly correlate with the urban E&I in China, meeting the condition for exclusivity in the selection of instrumental variables. Therefore, the coefficient of interaction term Treat_IV × Post measures ATE of China's ECE on urban E&I. Seen from Columns (4)−(6) of Table 5, the coefficients of Treat_IV × Post_2003, Treat_IV × Post_2006, and Treat_IV × Post_2009, are all significantly positive, indicating that after eliminating the endogeneity, the ECE is still effective in improving the urban E&I in China.

PSM-DID estimation

To minimize the sample bias caused by the systematic differences between the treatment group and the control group, we use “Propensity Score Matching” (PSM) method to estimate the ATE of the ECE, by selecting the samples with the closest scores as the control group, based on four control variables, that is, financial support (fin), governmental support (gov), urbanization (urb) and capital stock (cap) for DID estimation robustly after excluding the samples with obvious differences. Seen from Columns (7)−(9) of Tables 5 and via PSM-DID estimation, the coefficients β presented in Column (7) and (8) are both positive, while the coefficient β of the interaction Treat × Post_2009 in Columns (9) is positive with no statistical significance, which is basically as same as benchmark results in Table 2. The above-stated results again verify that the expansions of undergraduates and master's students are indeed helpful to urban E&I in China.

Quantile DID estimation

The ordinary DID model regression requires an important condition to be met: the Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption (SUTVA), which assumes that treatment effects are constant across groups and over time. However, this assumption is difficult to satisfy in the real economy. In order to capture the estimation of heterogeneous treatment effects under the policy of ECE, we follow Athey and Imbens (2006) to use Quantile DID (QDID) method to comprehensively analyze the differential ATE of China's ECE on urban E&I at different percentile [43]. The estimation of QDID model is less affected by outliers, making the regression results more robust.

Table 6 presents the regression results of the QDID for different percentiles, indicating that: firstly, with respect to the China's ECE for undergraduates, the regression coefficient β of the interaction Treat × Post_2003 is significantly negative at the 10th percentile, which suggests that the expansion of undergraduate enrollment is not favorable for cities with originally low levels of E&I. Moreover, at the 90th percentile, the coefficient β is significantly positive, indicating that the expansion of undergraduate enrollment is beneficial for cities with originally high levels of E&I, while for cities with E&I at the intermediate level is not significant. That is to say, China's ECE for undergraduates exhibits significant heterogeneous ATE on urban E&I, also reflecting the possibility that the expansion of undergraduate enrollment may lead to polarization, i.e., the “Matthew effect”.

It is possible that unlike the ones with E&I base(s), although the cities without E&I base have less suitable industrial carriers or platforms for E&I, the expansion of higher education makes it easier for them to explore newer technology fields, more entrepreneurial opportunities, or even leap-forward.

Possibly because in the past 20 years, China's economy has maintained rapid growth and continuous upgrading of its industrial structure, substantially causing a drastic change in labor supply, creating a lot of new jobs and expanding demand for high-skilled labor. That is to say, China's ECE not only leads to a situation of employment tension, but instead meets the surging market demand, thereby unleashing the potential of urban E&I.

Conclusions, policy implications and the limitations

Conclusions

The potential or actual impacts of ECE on the urban development have been the academic focus, but few studies examine the ATE of China's ECE on urban E&I quantitively and meticulously. Accordingly, with the panel data of prefecture-level and above cities from 2000 to 2020 in China, this study explores how the conduct of China's ECE policy in 1999 affects urban E&I and highlight two important channels (i.e., talent accumulation and labor misallocation) through which China's ECE make influences, using various quasi-natural experimental methods (such as DID, DSD-DID, SSD-DID, PSM-DID, QDID and so on) to empirically confirm the both ATE and SATE of the ECE on urban E&I with heterogeneity, also furtherly built mediating effects model to test the mediating roles of talent accumulation and labor misallocation.

In conclusion, generally China's ECE for either undergraduates or master's students benefits urban E&I with strong significance and robustness, while performance of the ECE for doctoral students is relatively weak. Possibly, the China's ECE do harm to cities with low levels of E&I while do good to the cities with high levels of E&I, which is called the “Matthew effect”. Furthermore, regional heterogeneity tests illustrates that the ECE for undergraduates or master's students, benefits urban E&I in all four economic zones in China, while the ECE for doctoral students is favorable to E&I of cities in the northeast or eastern economic zone. Additionally, compared with large cities (i.e., the cities with a population above 5 million) or cities with the E&I base(s). The positive ATE of China's ECE for undergraduates and master's students could be relied on urban E&I of small cities or cities without the E&I base. Undeniably, with a “N-shaped” trend, the spatially “beggar-thy-neighbor” or negative SATE of China's ECE on urban E&I is also verified by static and dynamic spatial DID testing, especially the ECE for doctoral students. Arguably, with the help of the serial mediating effect models, the mechanisms testing of the China's ECE on urban E&I proves that through two mediating channels, i.e., either increasing talent accumulation or decreasing labor misallocation, can the ECE for favorably promotes urban E&I in China.

Policy implications

Our findings lend support to previous studies concerning the economic effects of China's higher education or its enrollment expansion of colleges [[48], [49], [50]], especially its innovative or entrepreneurship effect on meso-level. Moreover, our study adds to the growing literature concerning the determinants of high-skilled human capital [51,52], and effective labor reallocation or labor market or unemployment of college graduates [[53], [54], [55]], by identifying the role of China's higher education or enrollment expansion.

Thus, based on the above conclusions, we propose two policy implications as followings:

First, given the positive ATE of the ECE on urban E&I in China with potentially the “Matthew effect,” the importance of progressive expansion of higher education should be highlighted. Therefore, universities should fully play their role as a basic support in the E&I, and China's education department should rationally allocate educational resources, and continuously improve educational support policies for underdeveloped cities of central zone and western zone. On the one hand, related department in China should furtherly improve the E&I environment in small cities or cities without E&I base, and balance the distribution of educational resources between developed and underdeveloped zones; on the other hand, colleges and universities should strengthen E&I education for students to enhance their enthusiasm and ability for E&I, and actively guide and encourage graduates to work and start businesses in central zone and western zone. The central government should increase support and investment in higher education in the central and western zones the local governments should issue a series of policies to support the inflow of talents.

Second, based on the mechanism channel of the China's ECE, the importance should be attached to the accumulation of human capital and the cumulative circulation of talents, coordinate and adjust the employment expectations of graduates, widely carry out vocational cognition education to promote the rational employment concept of graduates, and improve their self-adjustment and adaptability to careers. Also, it is necessary to continuously break down the administrative household registration system barriers and establish a matching industrial policy, to promote reasonable flow of graduates between urban and rural areas, to consolidate the input of relevant physical capital elements, in order to achieve the coordination and unification of human capital and physical capital.

The limitations

In this study, considering China's entrepreneurship and innovation (E&I) as a whole, we conducted theoretical analysis and empirical testing the causal effects and potential mechanisms of China's ECE on it. Apart from the robustness tests, the other parts of this studies did not distinguish the independent impact of ECE on either urban innovation or urban entrepreneurship. Meanwhile, the “China Innovation and Entrepreneurship Index” used to measure E&I, includes multiple dimensions such as ‘New companies entering’, ‘Attract foreign investment’, ‘Attract venture capital’, and so on. However, due to the limitation of research dimension, quantitative methods and data availability, we do not explore how the China's ECE actually affects in through these aspects at the prefecture level and above the city dimension. Thus, the fruitful areas for future studies could be carried out in more detail. In addition, in different periods, the driving mechanisms through which the China's ECE affects urban E&I are different. We only take the talent accumulation and labor misallocation as the mediating variables to discuss, but other important mediating channels, such as factor mobility, professional skill of workers, quality of education, should never be overlooked. Therefore, the follow-up research should be further explored.
