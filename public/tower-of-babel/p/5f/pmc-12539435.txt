Double-Edged Signaling
Authors: Neha E.H. Dinesh, Dieter P. Reinhardt
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Thoracic aortic disease (TAD) remains a devastating cardiovascular condition with high mortality and limited pharmacological prevention. Pathogenic variants in genes associated with TAD, including FBN1, LOX, TGFB2, TGFBR1, ACTA2, MYH11, and others, cause defects in the aortic wall. These genetic alterations primarily affect vascular smooth muscle cells (vSMC) in the tunica media, which play a critical role in maintaining aortic integrity by sensing and responding to mechanical stress and changes in extracellular matrix (ECM). Recent evidence suggests that dysfunction of the ECM–vSMC contractile unit constitutes a central mechanism in TAD pathogenesis. This unit is a biomechanically integrated system that includes extracellular components such as fibrillin-1–rich microfibrils and elastic fibers connecting to the intracellular actin–myosin contractile machinery via integrin receptors at focal adhesions (FA).1 FA act as mechanosensors, transmitting mechanical cues from the ECM to the cytoskeleton, enabling vSMC to adapt to hemodynamic forces.1

Disruption of either the ECM structure or vSMC contractility through genetic alterations predisposes individuals to thoracic aortic aneurysms (TAA) and dissections. In Marfan syndrome, for instance, disease-causing variants in FBN1 impair the integrity of microfibrils and elastic fibers, leading to progressive aortic dilation and multisystemic complications. Recent work using Marfan syndrome patient-derived induced pluripotent stem cells revealed elevated integrin αv–focal adhesion kinase (FAK)–Akt signaling in vSMC, emphasizing the importance of FA in ECM–cell communication.2 Given the central role of FAK in mechanotransduction, its dysregulation is likely to contribute to disease progression. In this context, Zhou et al3 provide a timely and important contribution in this issue of JACC: Basic to Translational Science by identifying FAK-dependent signaling pathways that differentially regulate dissection susceptibility in the ascending vs descending aorta, revealing spatially distinct mechanisms in TAD.

Differential FAK Signaling in Aortic Protection and Disease

Zhou et al3 treated wild-type mice starting from postnatal day (P) 21 with the established lysyl oxidase inhibitor β-aminopropionitrile (BAPN) to induce TAD.4 BAPN-treated mice exhibited classical pathological features in the aorta, including disrupted elastic fiber architecture, necrosis, infiltration of blood cells into the medial layer, and increased deposition of proteoglycans and collagen. By P49, 61% of the mice had died, highlighting the significant effects of BAPN treatment and the critical role of lysyl oxidase in elastin and ECM cross-linking. Most analyses in this study were performed on aortic tissues at P35, before the earliest dissection-related deaths occurred. Using single-cell transcriptomics, the authors showed that vSMC were the most affected cell population in the treated mice, with 5 distinct subclusters identified. Correlation analysis revealed increased Fn1 gene expression and elevated fibronectin (FN) protein levels in the vSMC of BAPN-treated mice, associated with fragmented FN. The authors also observed increased gene expression and protein levels of α5β1 integrin in the dysregulated vSMC subsets of BAPN-treated aortas. Bioinformatic interaction analysis of single-cell data further suggested enhanced FN–α5 integrin binding. Although this interaction was not experimentally validated, these findings align well with the authors’ observation of increased FAK phosphorylation, which subsequently activated downstream Rho/ROCK and mTOR pathways in the aortic vSMC of BAPN-treated mice.

The authors continued with pharmacologic inhibition targeting these pathways. Although FAK inhibition effectively reduced elevated p-FAK levels, it failed to improve mouse survival or alleviate aortopathy. In contrast, inhibition of Rho/ROCK signaling increased the incidence of ascending aortic dissections, without effects on descending dissections. These findings led the authors to propose a novel region-specific, protective role for Rho/ROCK signaling in the ascending aorta. This hypothesis gains significance given the distinct embryonic origins of vSMC within the aortic media. The ascending aorta is populated by vSMC derived from the neural crest and the secondary heart field, whereas those in the descending aorta arise from somites.5 Rho/ROCK signaling is known to regulate vSMC differentiation and function through pathways involving serum response factor and actin dynamics.6 Therefore, the findings by Zhou et al3 suggest that Rho/ROCK signaling may exert differential roles in vSMC depending on their embryonic origin, warranting further investigation of the underlying mechanisms.

Furthermore, inhibition of mTOR signaling in BAPN-treated mice using rapamycin significantly improved survival and reduced aortic dissections primarily in the descending aorta. However, combining rapamycin with a Rho/ROCK inhibitor provided no additional benefit. These findings suggest that mTOR signaling drives a pathological cascade in the vSMC of the descending aorta, leading to aortic dissections. Notably, increased mTOR activity was observed at a later stage, around P32 to P35, whereas alterations in Rho/ROCK signaling and α5 integrin expression were evident as early as P24 and P28, respectively, only a few days after BAPN administration. This temporal sequence suggests that, although mTOR signaling may represent a distinct pathway downstream of FAK, its activation is likely a progressive consequence of earlier disruptions in ECM–integrin interactions, ultimately contributing to the gradual development of descending aortic dissection. It would be interesting to assess whether mTOR inhibition reduces FN fragmentation, α5 integrin levels, or the proposed FN–α5 interaction in the aorta. Nevertheless, the improvement in aortic pathology upon mTOR inhibition in this model aligns with findings from other studies using ECM perturbations that cause aortic disease. For instance, in elastin-deficient mice, mTOR inhibition reduced aberrant mechanosignaling, medial fibrosis, and aortic stiffening.7 Similarly, in Fbn1mgR/mgR mice, short-term rapamycin treatment significantly decreased aortic aneurysm formation and improved survival.8 Although rapamycin reduces the risk of dissections, its long-term effects on vascular remodeling and stiffness remain uncertain and warrant further studies.

Open Questions

An intriguing finding by Zhou et al3 is the accumulation of fragmented FN in BAPN-treated aortas, despite unchanged matrix metalloproteinase expression. Although other ECM-degrading enzymes, such as members of the ADAMTS or cathepsin families, cannot be ruled out, inactivation of lysyl oxidase may play a role in this context. Beyond its function in collagen and elastin crosslinking, lysyl oxidase is also crucial for FN fibrillogenesis and matrix assembly.9 It is possible that its inactivity upon BAPN treatment may impair FN organization into fibrils, prompting cells to perceive an "FN-deficient" matrix, leading to compensatory FN up-regulation, disordered accumulation, and increased FN fragmentation. Moreover, FN conformation in the matrix dictates how it interacts with cells and interacting proteins. Zhou et al3 report increased FN and α5 integrin expression, along with down-regulation of α8β1 integrin, which is typically enriched in contractile vSMC and mediates, among other integrins, interactions with fibrillin-1. This suggests a shift in integrin–ECM engagement, favoring α5β1–FN interactions that activate FAK signaling. Because FN fibrils are critical scaffolds for fibrillin-1 and elastic fiber assembly, a defective FN matrix caused by lysyl oxidase inhibition may disrupt microfibril organization and compromise the elastic fiber architecture. Although not directly addressed in this study, the impact of FN fragmentation on fibrillin-1 expression and matrix organization warrants further investigation. This line of inquiry would be particularly relevant, considering findings from studies using the fibrillin-1 hypomorph Fbn1mgR/mgR mice, which rapidly develop aggressive TAA. These mice show increased FN accumulation in the tunica media, which contributes to TAA pathogenesis through α5β1 integrin signaling.10 These findings, along with the data reported by Zhou et al,3 highlight a possible compensatory and interdependent relationship between fibrillin-1 and FN in integrin-mediated FAK signaling in TAD.

Conclusions

Zhou et al3 provide compelling evidence that altered vSMC mechanosensing via FAK signaling plays a key role in regional aortic dissection susceptibility. These findings explain the anatomical heterogeneity of TAD and support the development of spatially targeted therapies. The study also links FAK pathway disruption to human TAD, thereby reinforcing the clinical relevance and opening new translational and therapeutic directions.
