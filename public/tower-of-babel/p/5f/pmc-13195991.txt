Quality of tuberculosis diagnostic laboratories in Ethiopia assessed through External Quality Assessment components: a systematic review
Authors: Bewket Mesganaw, Ketema Misganaw, Mekuriaw Belayneh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundReliable tuberculosis (TB) diagnosis is central to TB control programs, particularly in high-burden countries such as Ethiopia. External Quality Assessment (EQA) is a core component of laboratory quality assurance, encompassing blinded rechecking, panel testing, and on-site evaluation. This systematic review aimed to evaluate the quality of tuberculosis diagnostic laboratories in Ethiopia through EQA components and to synthesize evidence on laboratory performance, error patterns, and associated factors.MethodsA systematic review of published studies conducted in Ethiopia was performed. Seventeen original articles published between 2005 and 2025 were included. All studies assessed TB diagnostic laboratory quality using one or more EQA components. Data were extracted on study characteristics, EQA methods, performance indicators (sensitivity, specificity, agreement), error rates, and determinants of quality. A narrative synthesis approach was employed.ResultsSeventeen studies (n = 17) conducted between 2005 and 2025 met the inclusion criteria. EQA methods included blinded rechecking (n = 15), panel testing (n = 13), and on-site evaluation (n = 12). Reported sensitivity ranged from 83.5% to 96.6%, and specificity from 92.9% to 99.7%. Agreement between peripheral and reference laboratories was consistently high (κ = 0.60–0.97). Major errors, particularly high false negatives, were reported in several regions and were associated with poor smear quality, lack of internal quality control, inadequate training, reagent shortages, and absence of regular EQA participation. Decentralized and sustained EQA implementation demonstrated measurable improvements in laboratory performance over time.ConclusionExternal Quality Assessment has substantially improved the quality of TB diagnostic laboratories in Ethiopia; however, persistent technical, infrastructural, and human resource gaps remain. Strengthening EQA coverage, integrating internal quality control, and continuous capacity building are essential to ensure reliable TB diagnosis and support national TB control efforts.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13309-3.

Introduction

Tuberculosis remains a major public health challenge globally, with Ethiopia ranked among high TB burden countries [1]. Accurate and timely diagnosis is fundamental to TB control, interrupting transmission and ensuring appropriate treatment [2]. In resource-limited settings, sputum smear microscopy continues to be the backbone of TB diagnosis despite the expansion of molecular technologies [3].

However, sputum smear microscopy is highly operator-dependent and vulnerable to technical and interpretative errors. To address these challenges, the World Health Organization (WHO) recommends comprehensive quality assurance systems, of which External Quality Assessment (EQA) is a critical pillar. EQA typically includes blinded rechecking of routine slides, proficiency (panel) testing, and on-site laboratory evaluation [4].

Ethiopia has progressively expanded EQA services from centralized reference laboratories to regional, zonal, and peripheral health facilities [5]. However, despite multiple primary studies evaluating TB laboratory quality through EQA components in Ethiopia, the evidence has not been systematically synthesized to inform national policy and laboratory strengthening strategies. This systematic review therefore aimed to synthesize available evidence on EQA-based evaluations of TB diagnostic laboratories in Ethiopia, focusing on performance outcomes, error patterns, and determinants of laboratory quality.

Methods

Study design

This review was conducted following the PRISMA 2020 guidelines for systematic reviews [6]. This systematic review was conducted and reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines (Supplementary File 1).

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted using PubMed, Google Scholar, African Journals Online (AJOL), and institutional repositories. The search strategy was developed using controlled vocabulary and free-text terms. The PubMed search string was as follows: (“tuberculosis” OR “TB”) AND (“sputum smear microscopy” OR “AFB microscopy” OR “acid fast bacilli”) AND (“external quality assessment” OR “EQA” OR “proficiency testing” OR “panel testing” OR “blinded rechecking” OR “quality assurance”) AND (“Ethiopia”). Similar Boolean-adapted searches were applied to Google Scholar, African Journals Online (AJOL), and institutional repositories (Supplementary File 2).

Data sources and study selection

All retrieved records were imported into reference management software, where duplicate citations were identified and removed. Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts for relevance, followed by full-text assessment of potentially eligible articles. Any disagreements during the selection process were resolved through discussion and consensus. The database search yielded 445 records, of which 17 studies fulfilled the inclusion criteria after duplicate removal and screening. The study selection process is summarized in the PRISMA 2020 flow diagram (Fig. 1). The included studies were published between 2005 and 2025 and covered diverse geographic regions of Ethiopia, including Addis Ababa, Amhara, Oromia, Southern Ethiopia, Tigray, Hararge, and national reference laboratories.

Data extraction

Data extraction was conducted using a standardized form to ensure consistency across included studies. Information collected included the author(s) and year of publication, the study setting and geographic region, and the study design employed. In addition, details on the external quality assessment (EQA) components evaluated were extracted. Performance outcomes such as sensitivity, specificity, and agreement rates were recorded where reported. The extraction process also captured the types of errors identified, including false-positive, false-negative, and major or minor errors. Furthermore, factors associated with poor laboratory performance were documented to facilitate qualitative synthesis and interpretation of findings.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of included studies was assessed using an adapted version of the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklist for cross-sectional and observational studies. Studies were evaluated for clarity of objectives, appropriateness of methodology, completeness of outcome reporting, and relevance to the review objectives. All studies clearly defined objectives and laboratory performance outcomes. However, common limitations included incomplete reporting of sampling procedures, and inconsistent reporting of confidence intervals for diagnostic accuracy measures. Despite these limitations, all studies were considered sufficiently robust to contribute to the narrative synthesis. No study was excluded based on quality appraisal.

Data synthesis

Due to heterogeneity in study design, varying in EQA components assessed, and different outcome reporting, a narrative synthesis was conducted rather than meta-analysis.

Results

Characteristics of included studies

Seventeen studies conducted in Ethiopia were included in this systematic review. The majority employed a cross-sectional design (n = 12), while four studies used retrospective program reviews (n = 4). One study was a longitudinal national review spanning ten years at the National TB Reference laboratory. Study settings ranged from public peripheral and district laboratories to private diagnostic facilities, regional laboratories and nationwide EQA networks involving up to 956 health facilities. Sample sizes varied considerably, from fewer than ten laboratories in district-based assessments to large-scale national evaluations covering hundreds of facilities (Table 1).

External Quality Assessment components

Blinded rechecking was the most commonly implemented EQA component, applied either as a standalone approach [7–13] or in combination with other EQA methods, including panel testing and onsite evaluation [14–16]. Panel testing was commonly employed in cross-sectional studies assessing laboratory diagnostic accuracy [14–21], while on-site evaluation was less frequently reported and usually integrated with other EQA components to make assessment of laboratory practices, infrastructure, and diagnostic performance [14–16, 19].

Laboratory performance indicators

Reported laboratory performance indicators showed generally high but variable results across studies. Sensitivity ranged from 83.5% in eastern Ethiopia [14] to 96.6% in Addis Ababa laboratories [7], while specificity ranged from 92.9% in southern Ethiopia [19] to 99.7% in nationwide and urban assessments [7, 10]. Agreement between peripheral and reference laboratories was consistently strong, with kappa values ranging from 0.72 (good agreement) [18] to 0.99 (almost perfect agreement) [7]. Proficiency testing outcomes demonstrated acceptable performance in most laboratories, with PT scores ≥ 80% reported in regional and national EQA programs [20, 22, 23].

Studies comparing EQA participation over time showed that laboratories with regular engagement in EQA activities achieved higher agreement rates and improved diagnostic accuracy [11, 12].

Error patterns

Major diagnostic errors were frequently reported across studies, with false-negative results being the most prevalent and concerning [17, 18, 22]. High false-negative rates were particularly noted in panel testing and blinded rechecking assessments, posing a substantial public health risk by allowing infectious tuberculosis cases to remain undetected [17, 22]. False-positive errors were also documented, though generally at lower frequencies, particularly in blinded rechecking studies [10–12]. Minor errors, including low false positives, low false negatives, and reading or reporting discrepancies, were commonly observed and often attributed to poor smear quality, staining errors, and inadequate microscopy practices [9, 13, 14].

Factors associated with poor quality

Poor laboratory performance was consistently associated with a range of interrelated factors. In particular, deficiencies in smear preparation such as inappropriate thickness, uneven size, and poor staining quality were frequently reported [9]. These technical issues were often compounded by poor quality of reagents or the use of substandard materials, as well as inadequate maintenance of microscopes [16, 21].Furthermore, the absence of robust internal quality control practices, coupled with limited training opportunities and insufficient supervisory support, negatively affected overall performance [9, 12]. Irregular or absent participation in external quality assessment programs also emerged as a key contributing factor to poor laboratory quality [11, 13].

Impact of EQA implementation

Studies evaluating decentralization and long-term EQA participation demonstrated a reduction in error rates, improved sensitivity, and increased proportions of laboratories reporting error-free results. This highlights the effectiveness of sustained EQA programs when adequately supported [10].

Discussion

This systematic review provides comprehensive evidence that External Quality Assessment (EQA) has substantially contributed to improving the quality of tuberculosis (TB) diagnostic laboratories in Ethiopia. Overall laboratory performance indicators, including sensitivity, specificity, and agreement rates, were generally high across regions and laboratory levels, particularly in facilities that consistently participated in EQA programs. These findings align with World Health Organization (WHO) guidance, which emphasizes EQA as a cornerstone of laboratory quality assurance for sputum smear microscopy in high TB burden settings [4].

The reported sensitivity (83.5%–96.6%) and specificity (92.9%–99.7%) observed in Ethiopian laboratories are comparable to findings from other low- and middle-income countries implementing structured EQA systems [24], indicating that Ethiopia’s EQA framework performs comparably within resource-limited contexts.

The high kappa values (0.72–0.99) reported across the included studies indicate good to almost perfect agreement, suggesting that Ethiopia’s EQA framework has effectively standardized smear microscopy interpretation across peripheral and reference laboratories.

Despite these encouraging outcomes, this review identified persistent diagnostic errors, with false-negative results being the most frequent and concerning. Similar patterns have been reported in studies from Tanzania, where false-negative smear results were predominantly attributed to poor smear preparation, inadequate staining techniques, and suboptimal microscopy practices [25] In Ethiopia, the predominance of false-negative errors poses a significant public health concern, as undetected infectious cases may continue to transmit TB within the community.

Consistent with previous Ethiopian studies, poor smear quality emerged as a major determinant of suboptimal laboratory performance. Inadequate smear thickness, uneven distribution, and staining errors were repeatedly linked to diagnostic inaccuracies [9]. Similar associations have been reported in quality assessments conducted in Indonesia [26], where deficiencies in smear preparation accounted for a substantial proportion of major errors. These findings highlight that technical competency at the pre-analytical and analytical stages remains a critical bottleneck, even in settings with established EQA systems.

Human resource capacity also played a central role in laboratory performance. Limited training opportunities, insufficient supportive supervision, and high staff turnover were repeatedly associated with poor diagnostic outcomes [27]. Similar findings have been reported in multi-country evaluations of TB microscopy services, where continuous professional development and mentorship were shown to significantly reduce error rates [24].

Importantly, evidence from longitudinal and decentralization-focused studies included in this review demonstrated measurable improvements in laboratory performance over time [10]. These findings are consistent with experiences from Pakistan, where decentralized EQA models enhanced accessibility, timeliness of feedback, and corrective action implementation [28].

Overall these findings have important implications for national TB programs, particularly in high-burden settings where sputum smear microscopy remains widely used and laboratory quality directly influences case detection and treatment outcomes [29]. However, the persistence of technical, infrastructural, and human resource challenges indicates that EQA alone is insufficient to eliminate diagnostic errors. A holistic approach that integrates EQA with internal quality control, continuous training, infrastructure investment, and supply chain strengthening is essential to sustain and further improve TB diagnostic services in Ethiopia [30].

Conclusion

External Quality Assessment has substantially strengthened tuberculosis diagnostic laboratory performance in Ethiopia. However, persistent technical, infrastructural, and human resource gaps remain. Strengthening EQA coverage, integrating internal quality control, and sustaining capacity-building initiatives are essential to ensure reliable TB diagnosis and support national TB control efforts.

Recommendations

To sustain and further improve the quality of tuberculosis diagnostic laboratory services in Ethiopia, External Quality Assessment (EQA) should be expanded and institutionalized nationwide, with particular focus on peripheral and resource-limited facilities. EQA activities must be systematically integrated with robust internal quality control practices to ensure timely identification and correction of diagnostic errors, especially false-negative results. Continuous professional development, mentorship, and supportive supervision should be prioritized to strengthen technical competency in smear preparation and microscopy interpretation. In parallel, strengthening laboratory infrastructure, ensuring uninterrupted supply of quality reagents, and implementing routine equipment maintenance are essential. Regular review and use of EQA performance data at facility and program levels are recommended to guide targeted interventions, promote accountability, and support evidence-based decision-making within the national TB control program.

Strengths and limitations

This review synthesized nationwide evidence across multiple laboratory levels and EQA approaches, providing a comprehensive picture of TB microscopy quality in Ethiopia. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, heterogeneity in study design, outcome definitions, and reporting formats limited quantitative meta-analysis. Second, some regions were underrepresented, potentially affecting generalizability. Third, selective reporting and observational study designs may introduce bias.
