Beyond the Adversarial Rivalry: A Developmental Rights‐Based Model for Minor‐on‐Minor Crime, Part 1
Authors: Tali Gal, Ruthy Lowenstein Lazar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWhen children harm children, the conventional victim–perpetrator framework is ill‐equipped to address the developmental, relational, and institutional complexities involved. While juvenile justice scholarship increasingly emphasizes rehabilitation, and victims' rights literature has advanced child‐sensitive protection, minor‐on‐minor offending remains underexplored across legal and social science disciplines. This Review Article draws on legal and criminological literature in juvenile justice, child development, and therapeutic jurisprudence, to examine tensions between the rights and interests of minor defendants and victims, and to identify the absence of an integrated framework within the juvenile justice system. To address this gap, we outline a four‐pillar model integrating therapeutic justice, developmental principles, relational procedural justice, and children's rights norms. In this first of a two‐parts project, we develop the theoretical framework for such cases and consider its novelty. Part II applies it to two case studies and offers practical implications.

Introduction

The intersection of juvenile justice and childhood victimization presents one of criminal law's challenging, yet under‐researched phenomena: cases where both defendant and victim are minors. Despite the prevalence of such scenarios (Finkelhor et al. 2009), scholarly discourse has largely overlooked the unique challenges they present. This Review article analyses the criminal justice process in cases where both the defendant and victim are minors, offering a novel framework that addresses the distinct complexities embedded in these cases.

While juvenile justice scholarship has made significant strides in understanding youth offending and developing rehabilitation‐focused approaches (Levick 2019), and victims' rights literature has extensively explored childhood victimization (Beijer and Liefaard 2011), the distinctive dynamics of minor‐on‐minor offenses remain largely unexamined in socio‐legal scholarship. This gap in juvenile justice theoretical and practical discourse becomes particularly significant as it reveals the limitations of the conventional victim‐perpetrator binary so prevalent in adult‐perpetrated offenses, when applied in juvenile cases.

Accordingly, we propose a distinctive developmental rights‐based model that, while operating within the criminal justice system, embodies its own theoretical and procedural foundations. This interdisciplinary model is based on and combines four key pillars: therapeutic justice, child development theory, relational procedural justice, and children's rights. The model considers the unique vulnerabilities of minors and recognizes that cases involving children and youth from both sides of the adversarial rivalry require specialized legal mechanisms that account for their rights, alongside their specific developmental needs. This framework offers a theoretically novel approach to juvenile justice that better serves the complex characteristics of young participants while maintaining fundamental principles of due process and fairness. Although cases vary considerably with respect to the characteristics of the child who caused harm and the child who was harmed, including their respective ages and developmental stages, the severity of the offense, and the surrounding familial and social contexts, the framework is intended to support both principled consistency and context‐sensitive application.

The article begins by identifying and analyzing four epistemic tensions that, taken together, reveal the limitations of existing legal frameworks: The clashes between therapeutic needs and adversarial rules; between victim and defendant interests and rights; between rehabilitation and due process approaches in delinquency cases; and between minor victims' participation versus protection. Against these intersecting tensions, we introduce a four‐pillar framework drawing on therapeutic jurisprudence, relational procedural justice, children's rights norms, and child development theory. After outlining each perspective, we consider how their integration and implementation in minor‐on‐minor cases offers a novel approach, and address concerns and limitations.

The Adversarial Trap: How Criminal Processes Fail Minors

The contemporary criminal justice system operates within an adversarial framework that emphasizes procedural formalism over comprehensive resolution of participants' long‐term needs. While originally designed to safeguard constitutional protections, this jurisprudential architecture frequently operates to exclude non‐legal, therapeutic considerations that may be essential to achieving equitable outcomes. The system's emphasis on evidentiary and procedural rules creates an environment in which the lived experiences of defendants and victims are systematically marginalized (Casey and Rottman 2000). The proliferation of technical jargon functions to demarcate boundaries of professional competence, effectively precluding meaningful participation by “outsiders” such as defendants, victims and family members, despite the direct impact of the proceedings on their interests (Bibas 2012). Even repeat players, such as probation officers and psychiatrists acting as expert witnesses or consultants, are often marginalized and silenced in a hyper‐legalized justice process (Cohen et al. 2023).

The reliance of contemporary criminal justice systems on plea agreements as the predominant mode of case disposition exemplifies the dysfunctional consequences of adversarial structures. The prevalence of negotiated resolutions accounting for approximately 95% of criminal convictions, belies the system's commitment to truth‐seeking through trial (Paolini et al. 2025). Instead, the process has evolved into a framework of positional bargaining characterized by strategic posturing and tactical leveraging of procedural advantages. In this context, prosecutorial and defense positions are calibrated not primarily as accurate assessments of culpability or appropriate sanctions based on truth and science, but rather as strategically exaggerated opening positions designed to establish favorable bargaining parameters. This dynamic creates a negotiation environment predicated on mutual threat rather than collaborative and interdisciplinary problem‐solving.

The adversarial nature of the criminal process is in stark contradiction with the intersection of social, developmental, and relational concerns in cases involving minor victims and defendants. Juvenile‐on‐juvenile cases depart from the adversarial construct and regularly require the inclusion of non‐legal considerations in decision‐making. As we explain below, this need for exo‐legal capacities results from the salience of developmental needs of the young people in both sides of the adversarial rivalry; the dominance of non‐legal professionals in the justice process; and the active involvement of family members, particularly parents, in such proceedings.

A first defining feature of such cases is the need to consider the long‐term and developmental needs of both the victimized and victimizing minors. In minor‐on‐minor cases, a purely legal approach is inappropriate, and, accordingly, courts typically adopt interventions informed by child development research and psychological knowledge. Indeed, despite periodic shifts toward retribution in recent decades, the juvenile justice system has generally retained its original rehabilitative mandate (Pelletier 2024). This ethos acknowledges that both minor defendants and minor victims benefit from evidence‐based, future‐oriented interventions that require individualized assessments. The rehabilitative approach, however, has been traditionally pitted against the due process perspective, and the integration of rehabilitative and developmental considerations in juvenile justice remains a challenge within the adversarial context of criminal law (Pelletier 2024).

A second characteristic of minor‐on‐minor cases which transcends a purely‐legal framework is the involvement of professionals from multiple disciplines. Whereas cases with adult defendants and victims primarily occupy legal actors, that is prosecutors, defense attorneys, and judges (and, at times, victims' advocates), juvenile delinquency cases involve child investigators, youth probation officers, school officials, and social workers. This multi‐layered involvement can create conflicting priorities, from purely legal interests to rehabilitative, educative, and welfare‐focused concerns, while also offering more potential avenues for collaboration and tailored solutions. Clearly, the centrality of non‐legal players challenges the adversarial nature of juvenile justice, and clashes between the legal and therapeutic ethos may appear regularly in such cases.

Third, family dynamics play an outsized role because of the special legal status of the parents or guardians of the minors involved. Parents may be powerful supporters of the minor victim or the minor defendant or, in some instances, obstacles, given their own emotional reactions, biases, or lack of resources. This dual role of families underscores the importance of addressing family engagement and intergenerational relationships during the justice process, but doing so extends beyond the scope of the traditional adversarial framework.

With such unique characteristics, minor‐on‐minor cases require an emotionally intelligent justice (Sherman 2003), that is open to knowledge from social sciences, and that involves the use of effective communication and non‐legal jargon by judges and lawyers with professionals, young people, and their family members. However, the adversarial nature of the criminal process places restrictions on such practices (King et al. 2014). Furthermore, the legal training of judges and lawyers generally does not include non‐legal elements drawn from social and behavioral sciences. With such rigid structures, it is no wonder that the legal players often fail to address the broader, rehabilitative, and long‐term interests of both young delinquents and young victims.

Justice's Dilemma: The Victim‐Defendant Rights Clash

A second layer of complexity reflects a structural tension between victims' and defendants' rights and interests. While the victim‐defendant dichotomy has long informed legal reform debates, cases involving two minors expose its limits, as both parties hold concurrent claims to protection, participation, and developmentally appropriate treatment. In this context, the challenge is not merely one of procedural balancing, but of reconciling competing normative commitments within a single legal encounter. Precisely because heightened weight is accorded to children's developmental needs, vulnerabilities, and capacity for rehabilitation, the underlying tension is not mitigated but intensified in minor‐on‐minor cases. At the same time, a historical perspective on the development of victims' and defendants' rights suggests that some of these tensions are shaped by contingent social and political constructions. Recognizing this may help to clarify areas of convergence and the longer‐term interests shared by both parties.

In the mid‐20th century, the Warren Court era marked a significant expansion of defendants' rights, emphasizing due process and reinforcing protections necessary for fair trials and prevention of wrongful convictions. In subsequent years, the United States Supreme Court further developed these rights, particularly in relation to confrontation, cross‐examination, and self‐representation (Dressler 2020). These developments reflect the adversarial structure of Anglo‐American justice systems and their foundational commitment to due process.

Beginning in the 1970s, a parallel shift reconceptualized victims' roles beyond passive participation, influenced by procedural justice theories emphasizing voice and recognition (Wemmers 2010). In the United States, the victims' rights movement led to extensive legislative reforms, culminating in the Crime Victims’ Rights Act (2004), which established a comprehensive set of federal rights. At the same time, states adopted constitutional amendments and statutory victims' bills of rights (Levine 2021). As a result, the criminal justice system has evolved from an exclusive focus on defendants' rights toward a more inclusive consideration of victims' rights (Cassell and Garvin 2020).

This evolution has produced a complex legal landscape marked by ongoing tension and periodic recalibration between these two stakeholders in the criminal process (Erez 1994). The tension has become a focal point of scholarly and policy debates, raising fundamental questions about how to balance due process protections and victims' rights and needs (Cassell and Morris 2024).

This enduring tension reflects fundamental questions about the values and priorities that should guide the criminal justice system. Should the system emphasize retribution, rehabilitation, deterrence, or some synthesis of these objectives? To what extent should punishment be informed by victims' accounts of harm? How should courts balance demands for public safety and victims' redress against principles of equality, due process, and offender rehabilitation?

At the same time, scholarship increasingly suggests that these interests are not invariably in conflict, and that aspects of victims' claims have at times been shaped by broader political agendas. Instead, evidence‐based rehabilitative and restorative approaches may both reduce recidivism and address victims' material and non‐material needs (Krinsky and Komar 2021).

Where both parties are minors, these considerations point toward the need to move beyond binary victim‐defendant frameworks and to adopt approaches tailored to the developmental characteristics, rehabilitative needs, and rights of both children within the juvenile justice system.

When the Defendant Is a Minor: The Rehabilitative‐Procedural Justice Pendulum

The treatment of juvenile delinquents in Anglo‐American criminal justice systems presents a unique paradigm that attempts to balance the seemingly conflicting goals of ensuring due process rights and prioritizing rehabilitation (Lambie and Randell 2013). While the due process approach prioritizes equality in punishment and strict attainment of procedural rules, the rehabilitative ideology encourages broad judicial discretion to attend to the individual, future‐oriented needs of the defendant. The rehabilitative approach reflects a recognition of the developmental differences between minors and adults, as well as among minors of different ages, which are associated with reduced culpability and greater malleability. These characteristics support a preference for rehabilitative and non‐punitive responses to juvenile offending, with an increasing emphasis on rehabilitation at younger ages.

The establishment of separate juvenile courts in the late 19th and early 20th centuries marked a significant shift in the approach to juvenile justice. These courts were founded on the principle of “parens patriae”, whereby the state assumes a parental role in protecting wayward youth (Farrell 1994). The juvenile courts were created to be different from adult courts and to address the distinct needs of children, acknowledging their age, dependency, and rehabilitative potential. This model initially emphasized informality, discretion, and rehabilitation, focusing on the best interests of the child rather than on just deserts.

The diversion of children away from the adult justice system and the effect of rehabilitative principles on the juvenile system resulted in the absence of fundamental constitutional protections for defendants (Kaye 2011). Because the ideology was the well‐being of the child, the absence of constitutional safeguards in the juvenile justice system was not addressed by the Supreme Court until the Due Process revolution of the 1960s and 1970s. The mid‐20th century saw a pivotal change in juvenile justice philosophy, exemplified by some key cases that modernized the juvenile justice system to a system that prioritizes constitutional rights as well as the psychological and developmental needs of minors. The case of Kent v. The US (1966) established that juveniles are entitled to the same level of due process as provided in adult courts and the landmark U.S. Supreme Court case In re Gault (1967) extended constitutional protections to juvenile defendants. These cases reflected a shift in the nature of the system from being a “welfarist” system, focusing on rehabilitation and children's needs, to a legal one that prioritizes the due process rights of juvenile defendants. Overall, these decisions also reflected the recognition that juvenile courts, while well‐intentioned, were often failing to provide fundamental fairness to defendants.

The insistence on due process has since become an inherent element of the juvenile justice system in Anglo‐American legal systems, and courts have consistently held that juvenile proceedings must adhere to constitutional standards of due process. The expanded constitutional protections and due process rights to juvenile defendants blurred the original distinction between juvenile courts and the adult justice system. While these protections were intended to safeguard juveniles' rights, they ironically moved juvenile proceedings closer to the adult criminal court model that juvenile courts were created to avoid. During the 1980s and 1990s, many states implemented delinquency laws that focused on punitive tools rather than on rehabilitation, resulting in more frequent transfers of young defendants to adult courts and harsher sentencing policies (Zimring 2010).

However, the beginning of the 21st century marked the return movement of the pendulum to rehabilitation as a primary goal of juvenile justice. The Roper v. Simmons Supreme Court case (2005) that banned the death penalty for those convicted under the age of 18, recognized the effect of developmental and psychological theories on adolescents. That decision, as well as additional landmark Supreme Court decisions such as Graham (2010) and Miller v. Alabama (2012), recognized the reduced culpability of juvenile offenders and their capacity for change, leading to significant reforms in juvenile sentencing practices which represent a developmental approach. Within the last 2 decades, most US states have reduced their youth incarceration rates by approximately 50%, in favor of approaches like probation supervision and community‐based rehabilitation programs (Puzzanchera et al. 2022).

The rehabilitative model is reflected in various other aspects of juvenile justice systems, spanning across the various stages of the criminal process, from pre‐arrest programs such as school‐based rehabilitative interventions, through court‐ordered diversions such as juvenile problem‐solving courts, to post‐adjudication mechanisms such as developmentally‐adjusted probation programs (Heilbrun et al. 2017; Wilson et al. 2019). Within juvenile facilities, educational and vocational training programs are implemented to promote skill development and enhance future opportunities, acknowledging that education and employability are crucial factors in reducing recidivism. Mental health and substance abuse treatment services tailored specifically to adolescent needs, form another component of the rehabilitative approach, addressing underlying issues that may contribute to delinquent behavior. Additionally, many juvenile justice systems have incorporated restorative justice practices, which focus on repairing harm and reintegrating offenders into their communities, fostering a sense of responsibility and community connection among young offenders. These approaches collectively embody the rehabilitative ideal, aiming to address the root causes of juvenile delinquency and provide young people with the tools necessary for positive development and successful reintegration into society.

Despite these rehabilitative trends, controversy prevails regarding the current dominant orientation of juvenile justice. Unlike other nations, the U.S. lacks a unified and binding federal juvenile justice code. In 2018, the Juvenile Justice Reform Act (“JJRA”) was enacted (Juvenile Justice Reform Act of 2018 2018). It required state juvenile justice codes to consider theoretical and empirical knowledge regarding adolescent development and behavior, representing the notion that minors develop and behave differently from adults (D'Agostino 2024). As required by the Federal Constitution, however, states are free to construct their criminal law regimes, and the JJRA only uses funding incentives to encourage adherence to its requirements. The US is the only country that has not ratified the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child, leading to inconsistent policies and practices. The system faces significant challenges, including racial disparities and punitive practices in some jurisdictions (Egan 2024).

The current landscape of juvenile justice thus represents a complex interplay between due process protections and rehabilitative goals, with these tensions most pronounced at the adjudication stage, where diverse sentencing objectives and public interests intersect. Key questions persist about how to balance accountability with rehabilitation and ensure due process protections without impeding effective interventions; and how to offer equality, fairness, and consistency in the application of law while maintaining the individuality of the disposition so that the diverse needs of young defendants are met (Egan 2024).

The introduction of victims' rights into the juvenile justice equation adds an additional layer of complexity, particularly when the victim is a minor, as the next section discusses.

Minor Victims in the Criminal Process: Between Protection and Participation

The inherent complexities and potential traumatization associated with criminal justice proceedings, challenging for victims of all ages, present particular difficulties for child and adolescent victims. First, minor victims are considered vulnerable, and indeed, in many cases, their involvement in the criminal process requires special measures to protect their physical and emotional safety. Experiences of alienation and lack of understanding of the legal procedures are some of the contributors to secondary victimization of children and adolescents who have experienced crime (Piamenta and Gal 2025). An additional contributor to secondary victimization is the repeated requirement to answer sensitive questions to multiple people, as it forces young victims of crime to relive their experiences repeatedly. Testifying in court and especially being cross‐examined are considered brutal and hostile parts of the legal process. The need to confront the defendant in an intimidating and unfriendly environment can heighten feelings of insecurity and distress (Crenshaw et al. 2019). Research shows that, for victimized children, giving testimony in court and being cross‐examined are associated with long‐term negative emotional effects (Quas et al. 2009). When the defendant is self‐represented, being cross‐examined presents a particularly stressful situation for young victims. These cumulative experiences are particularly harmful for younger victims and in cases involving more severe offenses. However, as features of the adversarial process, they arise across offense types and age groups and may substantially hinder children's emotional and psychological recovery, underscoring the impact of secondary victimization.

At the same time, the vulnerability of minor victims does not negate their need to have an active role in the process, receive information, express their opinions, and be able to make choices, for example regarding the manner of testifying in court (Cashmore 2008, Hayes and Bunting 2013).

Despite the compelling evidence about the importance of opportunities to assert control in stressful situations for children, law enforcement professionals often tend to make decisions that reflect protective and patronizing views, thus unintendedly lessening minor victims' sense of independence and power. For example, when possible, children will not be invited to testify in court about their victimization, and information about the progress of the case will normally be delivered to the child's guardian (Piamenta and Gal 2025).

Given the risk of secondary victimization in the criminal process, a complex tension often arises between prioritizing the victimized child's perceived “best interests” and respecting the child's right to participate in the legal process. This tension is evident in situations where a child's desire to receive information and be involved in the legal process may conflict with the perceived need to protect them from further harm (Cashmore 2008). For instance, young victims may express a desire to testify against their perpetrator and present their story with no external filters. However, law enforcement may advise against this, fearing that the formal and intimidating environment of the court could exacerbate the child's psychological distress and hinder their recovery (Hayes and Bunting 2013).

The defendant‐victim adversarial contest adds an additional layer of complexity, especially when the defendant is a minor as well. In some cases, alleged concerns about the victimized child's safety and well‐being might be rooted in the young defendant's interest in not having the victim testify in court. In others, opposite arguments of the victimized child's autonomy and self‐determination might stem from the defense's interest to cross‐examine them, or from the prosecution's interest to argue for a punitive sentence. This conflict highlights the need to consider the rights, wishes, and interests of the victimized minor as separate from prosecutorial or defense interests, and underscores the delicate internal balance required in such cases, as protecting the mental health of victimized children must be weighed against honoring their autonomy (Cashmore 2008).

Striving to address these challenges, many jurisdictions established separate legal paradigms, granting protections to young victims above and beyond those granted to adult crime victims in the criminal process. For example, the Federal Rules of Evidence (FRE) and many state rules recognize that minors who have been victimized may be uniquely vulnerable, leading to situations where their testimony is “unavailable” (Fla. 2024; Cal. 2024). Naturally, deeming the young victim unavailable contradicts the defendant's right to confront witnesses, and courts have highlighted the need to balance the two interests (State v. Robinson 2002). Other examples of the special protections granted to minor victims are the use of close circuit television (CCTV) testimony and the ban on self‐represented defendants' cross‐examination of child victims. Most of these reforms, however, are designed to alleviate the suffering of the young victim in the criminal process. Much less attention is paid to rules enabling victimized children and youth to exert some level of control, for example through receiving age‐appropriate updates about the case, being present in court hearings, and submitting a victim impact statement with age‐appropriate assistance (Hayes and Bunting 2013). When the defendant is a minor as well, the interests of minor victims are the least considered, and defendants' due process and rehabilitative interests take precedence.

It seems, then, that cases involving minor victims and defendants raise cumulative layers of tensions in the implementation of the justice process: First, tensions related to the needed balance between defendants' due process rights and victims' rights; second, dilemmas regarding the young defendant's rehabilitative and legal interests; and third, concerns about the young victims' own safety and wellbeing against their wishes, rights, and needs for a sense of control in the process through active participation in it. Ignoring either one of these tensions risks unduly violating the minor parties' needs, interests, and rights.

Against this multifaceted system of complexities and tensions in the background, and recognizing the distinct nature of these cases as involving minors on both sides of the adversarial equation, the proposed framework offers new balances in adjudicating minor‐on‐minor criminal cases in the juvenile justice system.

Reimagining Juvenile Justice: A Four‐Pillar Model in Minor‐on‐Minor Cases

While child‐against‐child offending is common, very little has been written about the distinct challenges and conflicting interests during the criminal case following such offending. The traditional adversarial system, with its binary framework of victim versus perpetrator, fails to address the fact that both parties are developmentally immature individuals who require support, protection, and opportunities for growth. It also fails to acknowledge the unique characteristics of minors, such as their dependence on adults, the need for peer approval, the importance of group membership, their sensitivity to authorities' treatment, and their strong need to be heard and seen. While separate child‐appropriate pre‐trial services are more readily available for young defendants and young survivors of crime, it is at the trial stage that their competing interests become most pronounced. Accordingly, the proposed model focuses primarily on the trial stage, where these tensions are most directly engaged.

In contrast with the prevalent approach, which positions juvenile defendants and minor victims on either side of the adversarial rivalry, a developmental, needs‐based rights approach would lead to solutions that respect the needs, rights, and interests of both parties. We propose a developmental rights‐based model that operates within, but is distinct from, the traditional criminal justice system. This model is based on four elements: therapeutic justice, child development, relational procedural justice, and children's rights. By integrating these four elements, the framework advances the rights and recovery of child victims while promoting rehabilitative outcomes for young defendants, thereby serving the best interests of children in both groups. Moreover, this integrated approach helps bridge the often‐perceived tension between victim protection and participation and offender rehabilitation and due process, demonstrating that these objectives can be pursued concurrently through carefully designed legal and therapeutic interventions.

Therapeutic Justice

Therapeutic jurisprudence (TJ) examines how legal rules, procedures, and behaviors of legal actors can produce therapeutic or anti‐therapeutic outcomes (Wexler 2000). The main argument is that the adversarial approach often neglects the psychological well‐being of individuals involved in legal processes. Instead, TJ presents a paradigm that integrates insights from psychology, criminology, and social sciences to craft legal frameworks that leverage legal encounters to act as rehabilitative opportunities (Wexler 2000; Winick 1997). TJ seeks outcomes that foster positive change without sacrificing legal principles (Wexler 2000). Philosophically, TJ embraces a normative framework that acknowledges the subjective experiences of legal participants and prioritizes human flourishing. TJ aligns with restorative justice principles and procedural justice theory, emphasizing the importance of voice, respect, and repair in the legal process. TJ is particularly applicable to juvenile justice, where courts can balance accountability with interventions aimed at addressing the root causes of delinquent behavior (Winick 1997). The TJ lens does not offer concrete solutions to specific scenarios. Rather, it offers the insight that “law reform can sometimes resolve or ameliorate apparent conflicts among values by revising legal rules… in a manner that renders the law consistent with all of the relevant values.” The way to achieving this goal is through the integration of behavioral science research and legal analysis (Schopp 1999).

Inspired by the TJ lens, jurisdictions in the United States and elsewhere have established specialized courts aimed at addressing the root causes of crime, such as addiction, mental health issues, poverty, and trauma (Hora and Stalcup 2008). Specialized youth courts have also proliferated under the influence of therapeutic justice, aiming to reduce recidivism by integrating rehabilitative services tailored to young offenders' needs. For instance, juvenile drug courts employ therapeutic approaches to address substance abuse issues, while youth mental health courts provide specialized care for juveniles with psychiatric conditions. While these models are strongly therapeutic with respect to defendants, they have generally evolved with more limited and uneven attention to victims' needs (Ben‐Dror and Dancig‐Rosenberg 2025).

TJ has also influenced practices in mainstream courts, where judges may employ techniques such as active listening, empathetic communication, and supportive messaging (Winick 1997). Such practices, however, were often implemented toward either defendants or victims, without appropriate attention paid to the possibility of treating both parties in TJ‐informed ways (Kaylora et al. 2022). Against this backdrop, the proposed model advances a normative, conceptual integration of TJ principles, calling for their application with comparable weight to both young defendants and young victims.

Child Development Theory

Child development theory has introduced important insights into both juvenile justice and childhood victimization; however, similar to TJ scholarship, it has largely evolved along separate tracks for defendants and victims. Within juvenile justice, Emily Buss advances a developmental jurisprudence approach focused on young defendants (Buss 2016). This approach recognizes both the diminished culpability of minors and the potentially harmful developmental effects of punitive interventions, particularly incarceration. It supports a shift toward less punitive, more development‐oriented responses that promote desistance and reintegration, alongside age‐appropriate procedures that emphasize understanding, transparency, and meaningful participation (Buss 2016). It also calls on legal actors to adopt a more supportive role, facilitating children's ability to engage with legal processes. In this vein, the law is understood not as restoring a prior state, but as supporting ongoing development (Peleg 2019).

A related dimension concerns children's legal socialization. Drawing on the work of Tyler and Trinkner (2017), this perspective highlights how children's respect for legal authority is shaped by their interactions with legal actors. Encounters characterized by respect, fairness, and constructive engagement may foster trust and legitimacy, whereas distant or overly formalistic interactions may undermine them.

A separate strand of the scholarship applies developmental insights to young victims and witnesses. This literature emphasizes the need for trauma‐sensitive procedures that account for the effects of trauma on minors' ability to participate effectively. Such approaches include the use of developmentally appropriate language and the creation of less intimidating environments, and they often critique adversarial practices as being counterproductive for child witnesses (Crenshaw et al. 2019).

The proposed model advances a normative, conceptual integration of these strands by applying a developmental lens to both young defendants and young victims at trial. It calls for responses that account for the specific needs, capacities, and interests of both parties, and for procedures that are attuned to their age, maturity, and developmental context.

Relational Procedural Justice

Procedural justice theory emphasizes the significance of process fairness in decision‐making mechanisms in shaping perceptions of institutional legitimacy, suggesting that the structural and interpersonal components of decision‐making processes carry greater weight than the substantive outcomes they produce (Tyler 1990; MacCoun and Tyler 1988). Central to procedural justice theory is the idea that individuals' experiences of the process, rather than case outcomes, shape people's perceptions of the system's legitimacy and affect their willingness to comply with the law (Tyler 1990). Tyler's model, which has been examined in the context of defendants as well as crime victims, delineates four elements that constitute procedural justice: respect, neutrality, voice, and trustworthiness (Wemmers 2010). Respectful treatment involves treating people with respect and dignity. Neutrality refers to authorities' demonstrated commitment to unbiased decision‐making, transparent decision‐making, and consistent implementation of established procedures. Voice relates to the opportunity for the participants to present their narrative and have an input in the process; and trustworthiness refers to the way authorities demonstrate that they act out of honest intentions, are concerned about stakeholders' well‐being, and act transparently.

The relational procedural justice model examines how relationships influence perceptions of justice and institutional legitimacy and focuses on the interaction between individuals and organizations (such as the criminal justice system). The relational model is shaped by two elements: the identification of the individual with the group—the significance of belonging to a “human circle” that shares values, traits, and beliefs; and “group procedures,” which are the relations that govern the group's activity (Wemmers 2010).

When minors encounter the justice system, their perceptions of fairness and justice are profoundly shaped by whether they experience respectful treatment, inclusion in the process, and opportunities to present “their side of the story.” This emphasis on procedural justice recognizes that young people, whether victims or defendants, are especially attuned to how authority figures and institutions treat them and their level of participation. These experiences can significantly influence their trust in the system, psychological well‐being, and willingness to engage constructively with legal processes (Tyler and Trinkner 2017).

Relational procedural justice can be implemented to both young defendants and young victims at the same time. A model based on relational procedural justice principles may foster defendants' greater cooperation with legal processes, improved compliance with legal rules, and enhanced potential for rehabilitation and societal reintegration. For minor victims, implementing relational procedural justice practices that prioritize respectful treatment and meaningful participation can significantly contribute to emotional recovery and psychological well‐being. Perhaps most importantly, such an approach can help mitigate secondary victimization often associated with traditional criminal proceedings by creating a more supportive legal environment that addresses the unique vulnerabilities of young victims.

Children's Rights

A children's rights framework offers a valuable analytical tool for examining competing rights and interests between child victims and child defendants in the criminal justice system. Drawing on the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child (CRC 1989), this framework recognizes children as rights‐holders entitled to a range of Protection, Participation, and Provision rights (Qvortrup 1996), while acknowledging their evolving capacities and need for developmentally‐tailored protections (Todres and Kilkelly 2025). The strength of this framework, which departs from formalist and atomist rights approaches, lies in its ability to move beyond the traditional justice‐welfare dichotomy and instead focus on the universal interests of minors, as manifested within their social and relational contexts (Todres 2025). Specifically, the CRC eases the tension between the rights of child victims and juvenile defendants. The CRC adopts a holistic rights discourse that extends beyond formal implementation to address children's long‐term, multifaceted needs. At the same time, a children's rights approach creates a legal obligation on courts to consider the needs and interests of young defendants and young victims when dealing with minor‐on‐minor cases.

Several key principles emerge from this framework. First, the fact that both victims and offenders are entitled to rights under the CRC reminds us that child victims' rights are procedural in nature, not tied to any particular outcome, certainly not punitive, severe, or “populist” outcomes as sometimes portrayed in public discourse. The CRC rights granted to child victims are to information, participation, and protection, not entitlements to specifically harsh sentences for their offenders. Indeed, empirical research shows that victims' justice needs, including those of children, often do not focus on severe punishment but rather on procedural rights, primarily visibility and recognition (Krinsky and Komar 2021).

Simultaneously, a CRC‐based understanding of the procedural rights of juvenile defendants helps move away from a populist retributive approach toward them. Such a framework highlights the importance of due process in promoting deeper interests such as rehabilitation and development. Second, a children's rights perspective recognizes the significant overlap between child offenders and child victims: not only are both entitled to protection from harm, participation in decision‐making, and provision of rehabilitation services, but they often face similar social problems of exclusion and disempowerment (Gal 2021). Additionally, for both populations, group membership and a strong need for social belonging are significant. Both want to be heard and seen and receive respectful treatment from authority figures. Third, the children's rights framework articulated in the CRC is relational in character. Rather than framing children's rights as atomized entitlements held by autonomous individuals, the relational perspective locates children's rights within networks of care, interdependence, and mutual responsibility that structure their lived experience (Laufer‐Ukeles 2016). The relational character of children's rights draws strength not only from legal scholarship but also from moral and developmental theory. In particular, the work of Carol Gilligan offers a foundational lens through which to understand the embedded nature of children's rights claims. Rejecting the dominant paradigm of moral agency as rational, and detached, Gilligan has shown that moral development, especially in children, is fundamentally relational. Voice, agency, and ethical awareness emerge not in isolation, but through dialogue and connection. The self, in her account, is formed in and through relationships of care (Gilligan 1982, 2023). This relational orientation has particular relevance in cases of minor victims and defendants. It prompts a rethinking of the traditional adversarial posture in which one child's rights must be vindicated at the expense of another's. Instead, it urges legal actors to ask how the realization of one child's rights might be pursued in a manner that acknowledges, sustains, or even repairs the broader network of relationships in which both children are embedded. Responses to this challenge may vary depending on factors such as the severity of the offense, the ages of the children, and the nature of their relationship. However, the underlying objective remains constant: to identify approaches that give effect to the relational, developmental, and procedural rights of both victim and defendant.

A fourth characteristic of the CRC is its ecological perception of children's rights, reflected in its emphasis on the responsibilities of families, communities, and states toward children. This ecological dimension recognizes that children's rights are fulfilled or violated within a set of interconnected systems: the family, the community, and the institutions that structure children's daily lives (Gal 2020). This perspective directs attention beyond the immediate dynamics between victim and offender to the broader social, institutional, and cultural contexts that shape those dynamics. By situating rights within these systemic contexts, the ecological approach opens space for interventions at different levels such as family, school, and the justice system itself that can advance the rights of both victims and offenders while reinforcing the systems that sustain them.

Integration

This part considers how the four perspectives outlined above may be integrated into a unified framework offering theoretically‐robust guiding principles to address criminal cases involving minor‐against‐minor offenses. To make the case for integration, we first consider the distinctive contribution of each perspective and then highlight how they coincide. Only by appreciating both their differences and their points of convergence can the integration be seen as holistic and compelling.

The Distinctive Contribution of Each Perspective

Therapeutic Justice (TJ) and Procedural Justice share an orientation toward the process, yet their emphases differ in important ways. Procedural justice focuses narrowly on the fairness of decision‐making procedures, stressing neutrality, transparency, respect, and trustworthy treatment. TJ, while attentive to process, reaches further: it insists that law and legal practice should affirmatively promote the well‐being and empowerment of those subject to them, through future‐oriented solutions that are based on behavioral science knowledge. Accordingly, TJ values rehabilitative outcomes and problem‐solving collaboration among justice professionals. Unlike procedural justice, TJ explicitly recognizes the role of empathy and the expression of emotions within the process, provided that due process rights are respected. Together, these two perspectives promote meaningful participation of defendants and victims in the process and can significantly contribute to emotional recovery and psychological well‐being. By balancing fairness with well‐being for both minors, they yield a process that is not only procedurally just but also rehabilitative, emotionally intelligent, and interdisciplinary in character.

The child development perspective adds a different lens. It emphasizes that the justice process must be procedurally fair, respectful and therapeutic not only because of the importance of the present moment, but also because the criminal justice process is a developmental opportunity in itself. A fair and empowering process promotes victimized children's legal socialization and trust in others. A process which promotes accountability and responsibility without stigmatizing shame may be a life‐changing event for a young defendant. The developmental perspective also emphasizes that the process outcomes should contribute to healthy development, whether for the child as defendant or as victim. For victims, this means processes that foster trust, resilience, and compassion rather than being retraumatizing. For defendants, it requires outcomes that support rehabilitation and growth rather than merely imposing punishment. A developmental perspective also underscores the diversity among children, emphasizing the need for flexible responses tailored to each child's evolving capacities and developmental stage (Kambam and Thompson 2009). The participation of a victimized adolescent, for example, cannot be equated with that of a very young child; similarly, the ability of a twelve‐year‐old defendant to articulate remorse differs significantly from that of a seventeen‐year‐old. Developmental sensitivity requires therefore a tailored approach that is also attentive to trauma and its effects on children's needs and capacities. Accordingly, a developmental lens highlights the importance of age‐appropriate procedures that promote transparency and participation, elements that align with procedural justice theory, so that children can understand the process and express themselves in developmentally appropriate ways.

The children's rights framework introduces yet another dimension. Informed by the CRC's relational and ecological conception of rights, it emphasizes the justice system's duty to consider the needs, interests, and views of both young victims and defendants at every stage, and to treat them not as isolated individuals but as embedded in networks of care, dependency, and responsibility. This perspective supports processes that are child‐inclusive, safeguarding each child's distinct interests and capacities while also engaging their families, communities, and support systems. Where a child's immediate environment cannot provide adequate support, this gap should be recognized and addressed through appropriate adult assistance, including preparation, information, and support. At the same time, children should be enabled to participate to the extent they wish and are able.

Finally, circling back to procedural justice reminds us that however flexible, therapeutic, or relational the process becomes, the principles of neutrality, equality, transparency and trustworthiness must remain intact. These principles align with children's rights to equality before the law and ensure that efforts to adapt the system do not compromise its legitimacy.

Toward a Unified Framework

Taken together, these four perspectives form a coherent framework. Procedural justice anchors the model in fairness and legitimacy. Therapeutic justice ensures that outcomes promote well‐being and constructive problem‐solving, while also legitimating the expression of empathy and emotions as integral to a humane justice process. Child development theory keeps the model flexible, individualized, and sensitive to growth, trauma, and capacity. The children's rights approach situates children within their relational and ecological contexts, ensuring that both defendant and victim are treated as rights‐bearing subjects embedded in networks of support. Integrated, these perspectives yield a justice process that is procedurally fair, substantively rehabilitative, developmentally attuned, and ecologically grounded in children's interests and rights. It promises not merely to adjudicate disputes but to promote the flourishing of all children involved, while safeguarding equality and due process. In such a framework, minor‐against‐minor cases can be handled in a manner that respects the dignity and rights of both children, prioritizes their developmental and therapeutic needs, and recognizes their embeddedness in families and communities.

Conclusions

The current paradigm, where minors appear on opposite sides of the aisle and where legal rules and procedures are not well adjusted to the unique nature of minor‐on‐minor cases, creates a set of reductive categories: the young defendant may be cast either as a mere “holder of strict procedural rights” or as a “broken child” in need of fixing, while the minor victim is labeled “fragile” or, paradoxically, a “threat” to the defendant's interests and rights. These binary constructions fundamentally overlook the fact that both participants are in crucial developmental stages, each with vulnerabilities and a range of unique needs. These characteristics will be better addressed through more holistic, supportive interventions.

Rather than forcing these cases into ill‐fitting adversarial templates, this article proposes a novel four‐pillar model, situated within the juvenile justice framework but beyond the reductive victim‐perpetrator dichotomy. Each pillar constitutes a theoretical aspect developed separately in the literature focusing on either childhood victimization or juvenile justice. Through their theoretical and practical integration, our model reconceptualizes the legal process in cases involving minors as both victims and defendants and considers their unique needs and characteristics, while leveraging the distinct nature of juvenile justice.

Cases may differ significantly in terms of the severity of the offense and resulting harm, the ages of the children involved, the availability of support systems, and the motivations and dispositions of both minors. The proposed model does not offer concrete solutions; rather, it provides guiding principles for approaching individual cases. For practitioners tasked with balancing the interests of a minor defendant and a minor victim, the model invites consideration of the following questions, corresponding to its four pillars:What are the likely implications of each possible course of action for the psychological well‐being of both the child harmed and the child responsible for the harm?How does each option align with the developmental needs, capacities, and interests of both children?To what extent does each option respect—or limit—the fundamental rights of both parties, including their rights to protection, participation, developmentally appropriate treatment, and consideration of their best interests?How does each option uphold procedural fairness and relational integrity for both parties, including the provision of clear information, opportunities for input, and respectful, trustworthy engagement by decision‐makers?

These questions should be considered both in relation to the decision‐making process and to its outcomes.

The adversarial criminal justice system's persistent tensions between victims' and defendants' rights, alongside the conflict between rigid legal proceduralism and stakeholders' emotional needs have catalyzed a reconceptualization of justice processes. Rather than framing justice as a zero‐sum contest between competing rights, contemporary scholars have reimagined it as an integrated system encompassing multiple goals, values, and problem‐solving mechanisms. However, this theoretical evolution has largely overlooked the specific complexities in the juvenile justice system emerging in cases where both parties are minors. The four‐pillar model addresses this gap by acknowledging the distinctive nature of minor‐on‐minor cases while incorporating insights from broader scholarship on the evolving landscape of criminal law. The integration of therapeutic justice, child development principles, relational procedural fairness, and children's rights offers a transformative reconceptualization of the juvenile justice legal process, especially in cases where both the defendant and victim are minors.

As presented here, the model remains at a conceptual level; Part II of this project will examine its implementation through an analysis of two case studies.
