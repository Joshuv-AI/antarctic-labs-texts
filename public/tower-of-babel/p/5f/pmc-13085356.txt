Identifying depression in middle-aged and older adults stratified by chewing ability using machine learning models: a nationwide cross-sectional study in China
Authors: Huan Song, Hui Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveDepression and chewing difficulty are prevalent health concerns among middle-aged and older adults. This study aimed to develop group-specific machine learning models to identify depression in middle-aged and older adults with preserved versus impaired chewing ability, enabling tailored prevention and management strategies.MethodsThe research data was derived from the 2018 China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). Five machine learning algorithms were employed to develop group-specific models stratified by chewing ability, which were compared against a unified model for validation. The evaluation metrics for the models included accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score, and the area under the receiver operating curve (AUC). The SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) method was utilized to analyze feature importance and to interpret the individualized identification process within the optimal model.ResultsThe analyzed sample consisted of 15,459 middle-aged and older adults, with 82.84% of them aged between 45 and 69. Of the participants, 32.06% were identified as having depressive symptoms, while 38.63% exhibited impaired chewing ability. Surpassing the unified model in performance, the Deep Neural Network (DNN) and Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) models emerged as the optimal algorithms for identifying depression in middle-aged and older adults with preserved versus impaired chewing ability, respectively. Feature importance analysis revealed that self-rated health was the most significant feature in the DNN model, while life satisfaction was the most important factor in the MLP model.ConclusionsThis study developed group-specific DNN and MLP models to identify depression in middle-aged and older adults with preserved versus impaired chewing ability. These findings provide valuable references for developing targeted screening tools to support the prevention and early intervention of depression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-26920-5.Keywords: Machine learning, SHAP value, Depression, Chewing ability, Middle-aged and older adults, CHARLS

Introduction

Depression among middle-aged and older adults has emerged as a significant public health issue worldwide [1]. The typical symptoms of depression include persistent low mood, fatigue, loss of interest, sleep disturbances, and decreased appetite. Depression not only diminishes patients’ physical and social functioning but also increases their risk of suicide [2]. Multiple factors contribute to the susceptibility of middle-aged and older adults to depression, including aging, chronic health conditions, social isolation, and major life changes [3, 4]. Over the past thirty years, China has experienced rapid transformations in its demographic structure, socioeconomic conditions, nutrition, and health. China has long been a unique and important subject of research, and these significant changes have made the study of China even more crucial. Research has indicated that depression affects over 30% of middle-aged and older adults in China, significantly reducing the quality of life related to health in their later years [4]. It is projected that by 2040, 28% of China’s population will be over the age of 60 [5]. The growing elderly population in China poses substantial challenges to the mental health service system, which is already limited in public resources.

Existing research has established a significant association between diminished chewing ability and depression among middle-aged and older adults [6–9]. This correlation is likely mediated by multiple pathways. Physiologically, poor chewing ability—often resulting from tooth loss, periodontal disease, or dental pain—is linked to nutritional deficiencies and physical frailty, both of which are recognized risk factors for depression [10, 11]. Socially, chewing function is pivotal for interaction; difficulties in eating or swallowing can restrict food choices and prolong mealtimes, thereby limiting participation in communal dining and social gatherings [12–14]. According to the 4th National Oral Health Epidemiological Survey of China, the oral health status of adults aged 55–64 and 65–74 is concerning [15]. In these respective groups, root caries prevalence reaches 51.0% and 61.9%, and the average number of teeth with attachment loss (≥ 4 mm) exceeds five [15]. These conditions contribute significantly to tooth loss: edentulism rates are 1.1% and 4.5%, respectively, with mean retained teeth dropping to 26.27 and 22.50 [15]. Despite a high dental disease prevalence of 97.6%, which results in widespread chewing difficulties, consultation rates remain below 5% [15, 16]. As the elderly population grows in China, investigating depression identification patterns specific to chewing ability is crucial for early detection and targeted intervention.

To facilitate such targeted identification, employing advanced computational measures is essential to assist researchers in assessing the factors associated with depression among middle-aged and older adults with varying chewing abilities. Recent studies have highlighted the promising potential of machine learning in the field of depression, ranging from risk screening to diagnosis [17–19]. Machine learning techniques are particularly adept at conducting iterative analyses of non-linear and high-dimensional correlations among variables, demonstrating impressive capabilities in constructing data-driven models. Particularly in complex multi-dimensional variable contexts, models based on machine learning methods can distinguish the most influential variables impacting outcomes and provide individual-level identification. While numerous studies have utilized algorithms such as random forest [20], neural networks [21], and gradient boosting [22] to identify depression in middle-aged and older adults, a critical gap remains. Most existing studies have not utilized chewing ability as a stratification variable, thereby overlooking the potential differences in depression identification patterns between middle-aged and older adults with preserved versus impaired chewing ability.

This study employed machine learning techniques to construct differentiated depression identification models stratified by chewing ability for middle-aged and older adults at the population level, while also providing detailed insights into the identification process at the individual level. The research utilized SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values to rank the importance of input variables associated with depression, thereby identifying significant features. Specifically, the SHAP method quantifies the contribution of each input feature to the model’s output, allowing for a visual representation of the decision-making process of the machine learning models. The application of this method enhances the interpretability of the identification models developed in this research. Furthermore, recognizing that machine learning algorithms require extensive training data to ensure robustness, this study employed a large-scale nationwide sample. Concurrently, the analysis centered on characterizing specific depression identification patterns for the respective groups defined by chewing ability among middle-aged and older adults in China. Given the widespread prevalence of chewing difficulties and the high incidence of depression in the elderly population, the results aimed to provide a technical reference for the accurate screening and effective management of depression among middle-aged and older adults with varying chewing abilities.

Methods

Data source

The data in this study came from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). It is a nationally representative longitudinal survey, designed to collect aging information of middle-aged and older adults aged 45 and above, and promote interdisciplinary research in related fields. Its baseline survey was launched in 2011, covering 450 communities and villages across 150 counties nationwide, with follow-up surveys conducted every two years. The CHARLS adopted a multistage stratified probability-proportionate-to-size design and used computer-assisted personal interviewing systems. Its data has a high response rate and credibility, and has been applied in many international papers that adopt machine learning methods [17, 23, 24]. The CHARLS was approved by the Peking University Biomedical Ethics Committee (Approval No.: IRB00001052-11015) and obtained the written informed consent of all participants. More details about the CHARLS can be found in the study of Zhao, et al. [25].

This study was an analysis of a publicly available cross-sectional dataset. It selected and utilized the CHARLS survey conducted in 2018 that was related to the core characteristics of the research design. The 2018 CHARLS data contains extensive information on dental health status, physical and mental health, and various general indicators concerning demographics, behavioral activities, lifestyle and economic conditions. A critical factor dictating the choice of the 2018 survey was the exclusive inclusion of the chewing ability variable, which served as the basis for stratification in this analysis. The absence of this item in other waves precluded the possibility of constructing a longitudinal cohort. A total of 19,816 participants were interviewed in the 2018 CHARLS. According to the purpose of this study, the inclusion criteria for the subjects were as follows: (a) aged 45 years and above; (b) completed all the items of the depression scale; (c) answered the chewing ability questions. After excluding cases with missing values for the selected input features, the final sample included in the analysis consisted of 15,459 cases.

Assessment of depression

The outcome variable of this study was self-reported depressive symptoms. This status was measured using the 10-item Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression Scale (CESD-10), a widely used self-reported screening tool that has demonstrated sufficient reliability and validity for middle-aged and older adults in China [26]. For each item of the CESD-10 scale, participants in the 2018 CHARLS were asked to rate “how you have felt and behaved during the last week”. Responses were recorded on a four-point scale: 0 = Rarely or none of the time (less than 1 day); 1 = Some or a little of the time (1–2 days); 2 = Occasionally or a moderate amount of the time (3–4 days); 3 = Most or all of the time (5–7 days). Two items with positive wording were reverse-coded. The total score, ranging from 0 to 30, reflected the level of depressive symptoms, with higher scores indicating greater severity. Following existing studies [27, 28], a score of 10 or higher was utilized as the cut-off point for identifying significant depressive symptoms in this study. The Cronbach’s alpha for the CESD-10 in this study was 0.82.

Assessment of chewing ability

The stratification variable of this study was self-reported chewing ability. It was assessed using a standardized binary question in the 2018 CHARLS: “Have you experienced difficulty in chewing solid foods (for example, apples)?”, specifying that denture wearers should evaluate their ability while wearing dentures. The response format was binary: 0 = No; 1 = Yes. Based on the respondents’ answers, chewing ability was divided into two categories: preserved chewing ability (PRCA) and impaired chewing ability (IMCA). Participants selecting the response option “Yes” were classified into the IMCA group, while those selecting “No” were classified into the PRCA group. This self-reported measure has been shown to be a valid proxy for clinically assessed masticatory function in large population studies [29–31].

Input features

Based on previous research, the models employed in this study integrated a range of input features covering demographic characteristics, behavior and lifestyle, physiological functions, psychological perceptions, and chronic health conditions. Demographic characteristics encompassed age [32], gender [33], and place of residence [34]. Behavior and lifestyle included smoking status [20], adequate sleep [35], participation in social activities [36], and the type of cooking fuel used [37]. Physiological functions were assessed through the identification of disabilities in basic activities of daily living (ADL) and instrumental activities of daily living (IADL) [38, 39]. Psychological perceptions were characterized by self-rated health status [40] and life satisfaction [41]. Chronic health conditions were identified based on the presence of 14 long-term diseases, such as hypertension, dyslipidemia, heart disease, stroke, and arthritis [21, 42]. Statistical analysis

Frequency and percentage statistics were employed to delineate the fundamental characteristics of the research sample. A chi-square (χ²) test was conducted to test the differences between the PRCA group and the IMCA group. The identification model consists of five algorithms: logistic regression (LR), multilayer perceptron (MLP), deep neural network (DNN), K-nearest neighbor (KNN), support vector machine (SVM). LR is a classic statistical method used for binary classification problems [43]. MLP is a type of feedforward neural network that demonstrates a robust capacity for addressing complex nonlinear relationships [44]. DNN is a neural network containing multiple hidden layers and can achieve multi-layer nonlinear transformation [44]. KNN is one of the most fundamental and simplest machine learning algorithms and does not have an explicit learning process [45]. SVM is a supervised learning algorithm widely used in classification and regression analysis in high-dimensional data scenarios [46]. Furthermore, to validate the stratification strategy, a set of unified models was also developed for the total sample, incorporating chewing ability as a direct input feature for comparative analysis.

The samples of the study were randomly divided into the training set and the test set in a ratio of 8:2 in each algorithm. The performance of the model under different algorithms was compared and evaluated using the following indicators: accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score, and the area under the receiver operating curve (ROC) (AUC). The SHAP method was adopted to conduct a comprehensive analysis of the optimal model at both the global and local levels. This approach not only elucidates the model’s underlying decision-making principles and quantifies the impact of each input feature, but also visualizes the decision path for individual cases. Dropout and L2 regularization techniques were applied to minimize the potential overfitting of the model. The StratifiedKFold method was utilized to implement 5-fold cross-validation, facilitating multiple divisions of the training and test sets to enhance the stability of the results. Data analysis was carried out using Stata version 17 and Python version 2.9.

Results

Sample characteristics

The total sample for this study consisted of 15,459 participants, of whom 9486 were assigned to the PRCA group and 5973 to the IMCA group. The majority of the participants were female (52.16%), aged between 45 and 69 years (82.84%), and resided in rural regions (71.14%). In the PRCA group and the IMCA group, 2646 individuals (27.89%) and 2311 individuals (38.69%) were identified as experiencing depression respectively. The PRCA group and the IMCA group exhibited significant differences across various factors related to demographic characteristics, behavior and lifestyle, physiological functions, psychological perceptions, as well as chronic health conditions. Detailed information can be found in Table 1.

Model performance comparison

Tables 2 and 3 shows the performance of machine learning models using five different algorithms in the PRCA group and the IMCA group, respectively. Accuracy measures the proportion of correctly identified samples to the total number of samples. Precision represents the proportion of the number of actual positive samples to the total number of samples predicted as positive. Recall refers to the proportion of the number of predicted positive samples to the total number of actual positive samples. F1 score is defined as the harmonic mean of Precision and Recall. The AUC serves as an indicator of the classification performance of a model across various thresholds. It specifically measures the area under the ROC curve. As shown in Table 2, in the PRCA group, the DNN model had the highest accuracy, recall, F1 score and AUC (0.778, 0.412, 0.509, 0.783, respectively). The SVM model had the highest precision (0.717). The KNN model had the lowest accuracy, precision and AUC (0.724, 0.508, 0.661, respectively). The SVM model had the lowest recall (0.240) and F1 score (0.360). The LR model and the MLP model had the same F1 score (0.489). As shown in Table 3, in the IMCA group, the MLP model had the highest accuracy, recall, F1 score and AUC (0.733, 0.523, 0.582, 0.770, respectively). The LR model had the highest precision (0.695). The KNN model had the lowest accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score and AUC (0.661, 0.579, 0.432, 0.495, 0.668, respectively). The LR model and the SVM model had the same recall (0.484).

An increase in the values of these indicators was associated with an enhancement in the performance of the model. The utilization of these indicators facilitated a thorough comparison and quantitative evaluation of the models. As a result, among the five models in the PRCA group, the DNN model was recognized as the top performer, achieving the highest scores in four out of five evaluation metrics. The SVM model only exceled in one metric, while the remaining models performed worse than both the DNN and SVM. In the IMCA group, the MLP model was the leading performer, attaining the highest scores in four out of five evaluation metrics, whereas the LR model only performed well in one metric. The other models did not match the performance levels of either the MLP or LR models. As a comparative benchmark, unified models incorporating chewing ability as an input feature were evaluated using the total sample (see Supplementary Table S1 for details). The results demonstrated that constructing separate models based on chewing ability yielded superior identification accuracy compared to a single unified model.

Feature importance analysis

This study used SHAP values to illustrate the relationship between input features and outcome variables in the optimal model. The DNN model has been identified as the best-performing model in the PRCA group, while the MLP model has been acknowledged as the most effective model in the IMCA group. A higher SHAP value for a feature indicates a more important influence on the ultimate result. In this study, 15 key features were selected from an initial pool of 25 using SHAP values to generate Fig. 2. As shown in Fig. 2D, the SHAP values for life satisfaction, self-rated health, adequate sleep, age, and digestive disease were negative with relatively high feature values, indicating that these factors significantly reduced the probability of depression being identified. Conversely, the SHAP values for IADL disability, female gender, solid fuel use, current smoking status, kidney disease, ADL disability, and rural residence were positive with relatively high feature values, indicating that these factors significantly increased the probability of depression being identified.

Discussion

Depression is one of the most common and prevalent psychological disorders among middle-aged and older adults. In the present study, 32.06% of the participants were identified as having depression symptoms, with a prevalence of 27.89% in the PRCA group and 38.69% in the IMCA group. This finding is consistent with previous epidemiological studies utilizing the CHARLS dataset, which have reported prevalence rates of depressive symptoms ranging from approximately 30% to 40% among middle-aged and older adults in China [4, 27, 47]. Such a high burden highlights a critical public health challenge, as depressive symptoms are often overlooked or misdiagnosed. Delays or a lack of appropriate intervention may lead to a significant decline in the quality of life [48]. Although effective treatments exist, a substantial proportion of these individuals do not receive timely and adequate care [48]. Compounding this mental health burden is the critical state of oral health. As indicated by national epidemiological data, dental diseases and subsequent chewing difficulties are near-universal among middle-aged and older adults in China, yet clinical intervention remains exceptionally rare [15, 16]. While machine learning offers a promising avenue for rapid depression screening, most existing studies [18, 19, 49] have overlooked this widespread functional impairment. This oversight leaves the potential heterogeneity introduced by chewing ability unaccounted for, resulting in a lack of tailored identification models for subgroups with varying chewing abilities.

This study utilized the 2018 CHARLS data to develop machine learning models for identifying depression in middle-aged and older adults, stratified into preserved and impaired chewing ability groups. The identification models incorporated multidimensional features associated with demographic characteristics, behavior and lifestyle, physiological functions, psychological perceptions, and chronic health conditions. Five machine learning algorithms—LR, MLP, DNN, KNN, and SVM—were implemented in this research. Comprehensive performance comparisons revealed a divergence in the optimal algorithms: the DNN model proved superior for the PRCA group, whereas the MLP model was most effective for the IMCA group. This selection was driven by empirical validation rather than a one-size-fits-all approach. To further validate the necessity of this group-specific strategy, a unified model including chewing ability as an input feature was constructed (results provided in Supplementary Table S1). The results indicated that the unified approach failed to surpass the performance of the stratified models. The superiority of the group-specific models over the unified approach likely reflects distinct interaction patterns and non-linear relationships within the data of each group, creating a heterogeneity that a single unified model cannot fully capture. Consequently, the group-specific modeling strategy was adopted not only to accommodate these distinct data characteristics but also to maximize the identification accuracy for each specific population.

Furthermore, this study output the SHAP values for the best-performing models of both groups to identify the importance of input features. It is conducive to researchers and practitioners recognizing the factors related to depression in middle-aged and older adults at both the population level and the individual level. Based on the global analysis of the SHAP values, the study screened the top 15 features in terms of importance. Self-rated health emerged as the most significant feature in the PRCA group. Numerous studies have shown that older adults tend to be more prone to depressive moods if they feel their health conditions are not good [50, 51]. Life satisfaction was the most important feature in the IMCA group. Life satisfaction is regarded as an essential protective factor for mental health [52]. Existing studies have shown that the life satisfaction of older adults is often closely related to family support and social participation [53, 54]. A positive relationship network can enhance life satisfaction and, consequently, mitigate the risk of depression [55, 56]. The order of the importance of other features demonstrated considerable variation between the PRCA group and the IMCA group. However, adequate sleep ranked fourth in terms of importance in both groups. Extensive research has confirmed that high-quality sleep is crucial to mental health [57–59]. Overall, the findings from a global perspective were largely consistent with those of other studies employing binary classification machine learning models, confirming most of the features for identifying depression in middle-aged and older adults, including life satisfaction, self-rated health, sleep conditions, physical disabilities, gender, and age [3, 21, 22].

From the local and individual perspectives, the SHAP values provides an important basis for data-driven depression identification. It is worth noting that the force plots of individual identifications derived from SHAP values necessitate cautious interpretation. In this study, although force plots distinctly illustrated the complex relationships between input features and outcome variables, these relationships were not possessed by every individual. Some features may be powerful indicators of depression in certain cases, but may have no impact on depression or even have the opposite effect in other cases. It underscored that the SHAP method can provide tailored identifications for individual cases, offering personalized insights. At the same time, it emphasized the importance of developing a low-dimensional identification model to better serve end-users in practical applications. Research has shown that low-dimensional models concentrating on key features not only enable healthcare professionals to accurately identify high-risk individuals and formulate personalized monitoring and intervention strategies, but also empower older adults and their families to understand the factors associated with depression, thereby assisting them in making informed decisions about prevention [38, 60].

In contrast to previous studies that depended solely on a single algorithm for constructing depression identification models [61, 62], this study adopted five different machine learning methods and employed the optimal model through a comprehensive evaluation using multiple performance indicators. The accuracy and AUC metrics of the optimal model were equal to or greater than those of the depression identification models developed using machine learning methods in related studies, which reported a range of 0.60 to 0.78 for their indicators [20, 22, 38]. This study addressed the prevalent “black box” concerns associated with machine learning models through the application of SHAP methods, thereby enhancing the transparency and interpretability of the findings. By ranking the significance of input features based on SHAP values, this research revealed 15 key factors influencing the identification of depression in middle-aged and older adults with both preserved and impaired chewing ability. The differences in the ranking of features between the PRCA and IMCA groups emphasized the intergroup variations arising from the disparities in chewing ability. However, it is also important to acknowledge that these variances may also be due to the use of different optimal models for each group. In conclusion, this research considered the critical influence of varying chewing abilities on the identification of depression, thereby providing targeted and finely differentiated identification models for depression in middle-aged and older adults. Furthermore, the visualization presentation of the DNN and MLP models through SHAP methods further illustrated the application potential of machine learning models in the identification of depression.

Several limitations of this secondary data analysis have to be mentioned. Firstly, the cross-sectional nature of this study precluded causal inferences. Although key features associated with depression were identified across groups stratified by chewing ability, the temporal directionality cannot be determined. Specifically, features such as life satisfaction—which represents a global cognitive assessment distinct from the affective symptoms measured by the CESD-10—likely exhibit a bidirectional relationship with depression. While a degree of conceptual overlap may exist between these constructs, life satisfaction serves as a highly significant marker for identifying high-risk individuals in screening contexts. These identified features should be interpreted as proximal markers for risk screening rather than confirmed etiological causes. Future longitudinal research is warranted to clarify these causal pathways. Secondly, this study relied on self-reported measures rather than clinical diagnoses or objective examinations. The results reflected depressive symptoms (measured by CESD-10) rather than Major Depressive Disorder (MDD), and subjective chewing difficulty rather than objective masticatory function. Thirdly, due to the unavailability of external datasets with comparable variables, the generalizability of the models requires independent validation in future cohorts. Subsequent studies with expanded samples could further refine these algorithms, enhancing their utility in the identification of depression among middle-aged and older adults. Fourthly, the unavailability of data covering the COVID-19 pandemic limited the assessment of its impact on depression dynamics. The generalizability of the findings to the current post-pandemic context may be restricted. Future research should incorporate updated datasets to evaluate these temporal changes.

Conclusions

This study developed a DNN model and an MLP model to identify depression in middle-aged and older adults with preserved and impaired chewing ability, respectively. Both models demonstrated superior identification performance compared to the LR, KNN, and SVM models. Moreover, the effectiveness of this group-specific strategy was validated by its superior performance over a unified model approach. Based on the SHAP values, key factors associated with depression under the DNN and MLP models were identified, prominently including self-rated health, life satisfaction, and IADL disabilities. These findings provide an effective and accessible tool for the early screening and intervention of depression among middle-aged and older adults with varying chewing abilities.
