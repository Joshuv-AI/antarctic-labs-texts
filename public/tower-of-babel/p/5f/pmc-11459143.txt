The physicist Robert H. Goddard is considered one of the founding fathers of rocketry and space flight. Among his early work was an article published in 1920 in PNAS titled, “The possibilities of the rocket in weather forecasting” (1). In this now-classic article, Goddard outlined the potential use of rockets to collect high-altitude data on atmospheric conditions to improve weather forecasting. Goddard estimated that rockets carrying recording instruments could reach a height of 10 kilometers, remain at altitude for 1 to 5 minutes, and deploy a parachute to safely return to the ground.

Scott E. Heatwole. Image credit: Scott E. Heatwole (NASA’s Wallops Flight Facility, Wallops Island, VA).

Rockets carrying scientific instruments, of the kind Goddard imagined, are now called sounding rockets. While radar and satellites have largely replaced the use of rockets for weather forecasting, sounding rockets have been used extensively over the past century to study Earth’s upper atmosphere, as well as for applications in solar physics, plasma physics, and astronomy. Scott E. Heatwole of NASA’s Wallops Flight Facility in Virginia offers his views on the 1920 PNAS Classic. PNAS spoke with Heatwole as well as Robert F. Pfaff, a project scientist for NASA’s Sounding Rocket Program, about the article’s legacy.

Robert F. Pfaff. Image credit: Robert F. Pfaff (NASA’s Wallops Flight Facility, Wallops Island, VA).

How accurate was Goddard’s idea for sounding rockets as described in the 1920 article?

He was sort of doing back-of-the-envelope calculations, “You'll need this sort of velocity coming out of the nozzle,” and estimating weight based on the current fuels. He was only looking at 10 kilometers. It wasn't very high that he was saying was feasible to reach. Later on, in the 1950s, instead of 10 kilometers, sounding rockets were easily achieving altitudes of 100 kilometers or higher.

It shows that Goddard was a forward-thinking pioneer. He was able to come up with an idea of what could be done long before it was actually done. Making measurements in the upper atmosphere really wasn't done until the late 1950s. Before that, there really wasn't the ability to make these measurements because the sensors weren't there yet. Radar wasn't developed until World War II. So the idea of using rockets to forecast the weather was forward thinking by decades.

His motivation, as stated in his paper, to do weather forecasting is excellent. Nowadays, of course, we don't use rockets for most meteorological applications. Conceptually, it's not a bad idea. It's just not as practical, and there are other techniques used today. In his 1920 article, to retrieve the data, it was recorded on board and came down on a parachute. Nowadays, we have radio wave telemetry, so you get the data essentially instantly from rockets. Although there are still a few experiments carried out on rockets that acquire such large quantities of data, the data are still recorded onboard the payload and recovered with a parachute.

Goddard talks about having the instruments remain at 10 kilometers altitude. When you have a parabolic, “suborbital” flight, the payload spends most of its time at apogee. So having the measurements be right at apogee, as detailed in this paper, makes a lot of sense. Weather models back in 1920 were still in their infancy. Today, we use satellite images of weather systems and hurricanes and track their motions, etc. Back then, there was a lot of work done with different weather models, and the most important thing was obtaining data to input to the models. Goddard had the foresight to know that that was extremely important.

What are the advantages of using sounding rockets to record atmospheric measurements compared with other methods?

The unique ability of sounding rockets is the fact that they can reach particular layers of the atmosphere that other methods can't. Scientific balloons can get up to 170,000 feet, roughly. They can get to the bitter edge of the atmosphere, but they can't get into the mesosphere very far at all. Maybe the very lower mesosphere. Then you get the thermosphere above. Those two layers, nothing but sounding rockets really can reach that in situ.

The beauty of satellites is that they keep going, gathering continuous measurements along horizontal trajectories that often stay in orbit for many years. Satellites, however, are not able to routinely sample the lower altitudes of space, below 200 kilometers, or else they would re-enter. They also travel at very fast speeds, about 8 kilometer/second. The sounding rocket, on the other hand, provides what we call a vertical profile on its upleg and downleg, traveling at much lower speeds so the scientific instruments remain in one region of interest or space for longer times.

Importantly, we still need rockets to gather measurements in the lower ionosphere and below, between 50 kilometers and 150 kilometers altitude. Remote measurements from the ground, and also in space, are very important, including radars, imagers, and scanning photometers, but to gather direct measurements, you need to have the actual probe in the medium.

Beyond weather forecasting, what applications have sounding rockets had over the past century?

When NASA got started in 1958, sounding rockets were a big deal, as they still are today. There was a large sounding rocket division here at the Goddard Space Flight Center in Greenbelt, Maryland, as well as at the Wallops Flight Facility in Virginia, launching rockets from Wallops Island as well as White Sands and other places. The program continues to the present and is enormously popular with the scientific community. The date of Goddard’s paper is 1920. Most of the scientific research that's been carried out since that time with rockets started in the late 1940s and continues to this day.

There have been countless scientific questions that research with sounding rockets have answered. An example is, “What causes the aurora?” We all love to look at the beautiful aurora or northern lights. Instruments on a rocket provided fundamental knowledge regarding what causes these optical emissions which occur at high latitudes in space. We learned from instruments on rockets that auroral emissions are caused by energetic electrons directed down from much higher altitudes in the magnetosphere (2).

We use sounding rockets to carry telescopes to high altitudes to observe light at wavelengths that are absorbed in the atmosphere and hence never reach optical telescopes on the ground. We mount telescopes on the end of a payload and use an attitude control system to point the payload at various astronomical “targets.”

For example, we point at the Sun and do measurements of flares in UV, X ray, and visible spectrums trying to understand solar dynamics (3). There are also astrophysics payloads which have telescopes. They look at anything from binary stars to X-ray sources, nebula, gamma ray sources. In fact, we’ve used telescopes on sounding rockets to study planets such as Mercury and Venus (4), which are too close to the sun to be observed safely with larger space-borne telescopes.
