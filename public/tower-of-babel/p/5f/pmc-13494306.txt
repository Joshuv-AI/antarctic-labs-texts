Modeling multistage transition probability in blood pressure progression stages among hypertensive patients, southern Ethiopia: A retrospective cohort study
Authors: Dereje Danbe Debeko, Tayesew Shegaw
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionHypertension is the primary cause of cardiovascular death and a major contributor to heart attack, stroke, chronic heart disease, and kidney failure. The aim of this study was to estimate multistage transition probability and identify associated risk factors among hypertensive patients.MethodsA total of 210 patients who underwent hypertension treatment follow-up at Hawassa University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (HU-CSH) were included in the study. The multistate Markov model is used to estimate transition probability, transition intensity between disease stages, and mean time spent in each BP progression stage. We used the proportional hazards regression model to estimate effects of different covariates on transition intensity within and between BP progression stages.ResultsAmong the total study participants, 56.7% were female and 43.3% were male; of these, 66.2% were urban and 33.8% were rural residents. The transition probability from stage 1 to stage 2 was 0.151, from stage 2 to stage 3 was 0.128, and from stage 3 to stage 4 was 0.205. Stage 3 had the shortest estimated mean sojourn time. Old-aged (30+) patients had 3.19 times (19%) more risk of transitioning from stage 2 (grade 1 hypertension) to stage 3 (grade 2 hypertension) compared to patients below 30 years of age.ConclusionPatients who were in stage 3 (grade 2 hypertension) had a higher risk of progressing to stage 4 (grade 3 hypertension). A family history of hypertension, older age (30+), male sex, urban residence, history of previous hypertension and diabetes were major risk factors of hypertension and increased BP.

Introduction

Despite substantial improvements made in hypertension treatment and management since 1990 worldwide, 1 the number of individuals with hypertension doubled between 1990 and 2019, while the majority of sub-Saharan African and Oceania countries have low rate (below 10%) of disease control. These resulted in tens of millions of deaths attributed to uncontrolled blood pressure worldwide, 2 where 88% of these deaths occur in low- and middle-income nations. 3 Hypertension is also a root cause of cardiovascular disease and other severe disorders such as heart attack, chronic heart failure, stroke, and kidney failure. 4

Uncontrolled blood pressure and related deaths continue to be the most important public health concerns in Ethiopia, 5 where dietary changes, socioeconomic status, demographic shifts, and disease epidemiology are the main causes of the increasing prevalence of hypertension in the country. 6 Despite inconsistent and fragmented data on the incidence of non-communicable diseases in Ethiopia, some studies have reported that there is a relatively high rate of non-communicable disease burden in the country. 7 Most of these studies attempted to evaluate the relationships between hypertension stages and identify determinant factors via various analytical approaches.8,9 The majority of these studies focused on estimating the transition probability between disease stages, the rate of transition, or the duration of an individual’s stay in the same stage by estimating the transition probability for each BP progression stage to add clinical knowledge on the gaps in preventing and controlling hypertension.10–12 On the other hand, some of the previous studies attempted to estimate the transition probability using three hypertension stages, 8 merging pre-hypertensive stages (normal and elevated BP) into a single stage. This study was aimed to estimate transition probability between four BP progression stages and identify risk factors among hypertensive patients under treatment follow-up using a multistate Markov model.

Method

Study area, study design and data sources

This study was conducted in Hawassa University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, the only public university hospital at the tertiary level. It is the largest referral hospital in southern Ethiopia, serving more than 15 million people annually. The hospital is located in Hawassa city, capital of the Sidama regional state, which is 275 kilometers away from Addis Ababa. The hospital has six major departments: Internal Medicine OPD, Surgical OPD, Pediatrics OPD, Obstetrics and Gynecology OPD, Special Clinics, and Emergency OPD. 13

A retrospective cohort study design was employed, and longitudinally measured BP progression data of hypertensive patients under treatment follow-up for 24 months (registered between 2017 and 2022) was extracted from the departments of internal medicine OPD. The subsequent records were collected from patients’ registration logbooks and registration cards.

BP progression stages

The European Society of Cardiology (ESC) and the European Society of Hypertension (ESH) have developed new recommendations in the treatment of arterial hypertension. 5 A person whose systolic blood pressure (SBP) is 140 mmHg or higher or diastolic blood pressure (DPB) is 90 mm Hg or higher is considered to be hypertensive. The ESC/ESH guidelines categorize blood pressure (BP) into optimal (BP < 120/80 mmHg), normal (BP = 120–129/80–84 mmHg), high normal (BP = 130–139/85–89 mmHg), grade 1 hypertension (BP =140–159/90–99 mmHg), grade 2 hypertension (BP = 160–179/100–109 mmHg), and grade 3 hypertension (BP ≥ 180/≥ 110 mmHg). Patients whose systolic blood pressure (SBP) > 140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) > 90 mmHg at the first visit to the hospital were considered to be hypertensive.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Patients who had a minimum of two checkups (one initial checkup and a minimum of one follow-up) regardless of age and a maximum of 24 months of follow-ups were included in the study. Patients having no record on sex, area of residence, or age and patients who had more than 24 months of follow-up and those who had no baseline checkup and first treatment follow-up were excluded from the study.

We used a list of patients with a unique identification number as a sampling frame. The simple random sampling technique formula was used to determine the sample size with the following details14. n0=(Zα/2)2(pqd2) If n0÷N≤5%,then n0=n,andn=(Zα/2)2(pqd2)1+1N[(Zα/2)2(pqd2)−1]=n01+1N(n0−1)

Since the proportion of the population with hypertension is not known, we assumed that p = 0.5.

Then, n0=(1.96)2(0.5)2(0.05)2=384.16

Since n0N=0.83, is much greater than 5%, the final sample size was adjusted to the size of the population as follows: n=n01+(n0−1)N≅210 where n0 = the initial sample size and n is the size of the sample adjusted for the size of the population. Thus, a total of 210 patients were selected and included in the study. Patients whose systolic blood pressure (SBP) was 140 mmHg or higher or whose diastolic blood pressure (DBP) was 90 mmHg were considered hypertensive.

Variables in the study

The response variable of this study was the change in systolic and diastolic blood pressure (BP) of patients measured in mmHg during each visit to the hospital (Stage 1: BP<140/90 mmHg, Stage 2: BP = 140-159/90-99 mmHg, Stage 3: BP = 160-179/100-109 mmHg, Stage 4: BP ≥180/110 mmHg). Socioeconomic, demographic, and clinical characteristics extracted from patients' registration logbooks and history cards were explanatory variables included in the study.

Data analysis

During the statistical evaluation, cross-tabulated values were reported for descriptive analysis via percentages, and the multistate Markov model was used to estimate the transition probability, transition intensity, and the mean length of time. An effect of each covariate on transition rate between BP stages was estimated using proportional hazard regression analysis.

Multistate Markov model

The multistate Markov model is a stochastic process with a finite space. The process begins in one of the states and progresses systematically through each state. If the process is currently in state i, e.g., si, , then it will move to the next state j, e.g., sj , with a transition probability of pij . In this study, we classify states using four BP progression stages denoted by S(t) = {1,2,3,4}. Stage 1 refers to the pre-hypertension stage, stage 2 refers to grade 1 hypertension, stage 3 refers to grade 2 hypertension, and stage 4 refers to grade 3 hypertension (Figure 1). An individual in a transient state remains in that stage or moves to the next stage after passing through the stage right next to it. This refers to a two-step movement, where a transition can only be made between one stage at a time (t).Figure 1.Multistage paths between BP progression stages (stage 1 → stage 2, stage 2 → stage 1, stage 2 → stage 3, stage 3 → stage 2, stage 3 → stage 4, and stage 4 → stage 3).

Multistage paths between BP progression stages (stage 1 → stage 2, stage 2 → stage 1, stage 2 → stage 3, stage 3 → stage 2, stage 3 → stage 4, and stage 4 → stage 3).

The transition probability was obtained assuming that the future stage depends only solely on the current stage and that the transition intensities remain constant over time.

Transition probability

In this study, the transition probability matrix ( P(t) ), whose entry into row i and column j is denoted by: 1 pij(t)=[p11⋯p14⋮⋱⋮p41⋯p44] where pij (t) denote the transition probabilities of an individual in stage i will transit to stage j at time t. Diagonal probabilities ( p11,…,p44 )represent the probabilities of remaining in the same stage, while off-diagonal matrices ( pij,for i≠j) denotes the transition probabilities of moving from one progression stage to another at time t. In the transition matrix, rows represent the current stage ( Xt ), and the columns represent the next stage ( Xt+1 ).

The likelihood of moving from stage i to stage j can be denoted by: 2 pij=P (Xt+1=j| Xt=i)

Transition intensity

The intensity matrix represents the instantaneous risk of moving from stage i to stage j15,16 . The intensity between two disease stages denotes the rate of change made in a very small-time interval, e.g., δt. A patient can generally be in one of the four stages at any given time t and can make a possible transition to another stage after a certain period of time. We used the intensity matrix to determine the transition intensity that an individual encountered and to calculate the duration of time spent in the same disease stage. 3 qij(t)=limδt→0p(X(t+δt)=j|X(t)=i)δt (for i,j=1,2,3,4) where qij(t) is the instantaneous rate (risk) of moving from stage i to stage j at time t, X(t) = i represents stage i at time t and the transition intensity of moving to stage j during time interval (t, t + δt). The transition intensity matrix, denoted as Q, can be obtained as: 4 Q=[q11⋯q14⋮⋱⋮q41⋯q44]

The instantaneous rates a patient remains in the same stage are represented by the diagonal matrix, whereas the off-diagonal elements represent the rates at which a patient transits into the next stages. We used the Kolmogorov equation to measure the strength of the relationship between the transition intensity and the transition probability 17 using the following equation: ∂∂tp(t)=p(t)Q , where P(0) = the identity matrix (I) referring to the probability at the baseline.

Distribution of the mean time length

One of the important features of the Markov model is its ability to estimate the mean sojourn times that a patient spends in the same disease stage. This modeling framework assumes that the waiting time in each stage follows a homogeneous continuous Markov model and that the amount of time an individual spends in a stage is assumed to be independent and exponentially distributed random variables with a rate denoted by −qii and a mean value represented by −1qii .

Effects of covariates on the transition rate

When various covariates are included in a model, the focus of the study shifts from estimating the transition matrix between various disease stages to calculating the effect size of each covariate. We estimated the effect size of each covariate on transition intensities within and between the progression stages via the proportional hazards regression model obtained as follows: 5 qij(zij)=qij(0)exp⁡(βijTZij) where, qij(0) represents the baseline intensity; exp(βijTZij) refers to the hazard ratio for the ith subject at the jth stage; βij is the vector of coefficients corresponding to each covariate; and zij represents the vector of covariates for the ith subject at the jth stage.

Variable selection

We used bivariate analysis techniques to select predictors to be included in the model and check their significance. We include predictors having a p-value < 0.25 in the final model (multivariable) to prevent the accidental exclusion of important variables before they are tested together. 18

Model assessment

Since the true transition times are unknown and our data consist of observations taken at arbitrary times, assessing goodness of fit is very important. We verified the constant transition rates assumption between the progression stages through visual checking.19,20 This technique compares observed values against the predicted number of subjects in each stage over time. When the assumption holds, i.e., the proportions of observed and predicted stages remain similar over time, we would keep the time-homogeneous model.

Handling missing data

Despite exclusion criteria used in the study, there were subjects having missing outcomes in the data set. The effects of missing data can be accounted for using techniques. In this study we used the complete case analysis technique, which is one of the widely applied methods for handling missing observations in the data. 19 We used this technique, believing that our dataset is large, missing data is minimal, and the missingness is completely random.

Methods of parameter estimation

All the model parameters were estimated via the maximum likelihood estimation method. The transition probability matrix was obtained by dividing the number of subjects transitioning from stage i to stage j to the total number of transitions from the current stage to other stages. All the data analyses were performed via R software (version 4.2.3), and the statistical tests were performed at the 5% level of significance.

Patients with a family history of hypertension had a roughly 70% lower rate of recovering back to stage 3 from stage 4 (grade 3 hypertension) compared to those who had no family history. Patients who had a history of previous hypertension treatment and diabetes were more likely to transition into a worse disease stage compared to those who had a history of hypertension and diabetes. Discussion

With this study we estimated the transition probability, transition intensity, and mean length of time an a patient spends at a particular stage between BP progression stages and identified contributing factors. After 24 months of follow-up, the probability of transiting from stage 1 (prehypertensive stage) to the next adjacent state was greater than that of transiting to any nonadjacent disease stages. There was a greater chance of moving from the pre-hypertensive stage to stage 2 (Grade 1 hypertension). It is a fact that unless there is an uncontrollable shock such as an accident or severe comorbidity, it is quite common for a patient to transition into the adjacent disease stage rather than suddenly jump into a nonadjacent progression stage. A study conducted in Ghana revealed consistent outcomes. 10 However, once a patient transit into Grade 1, Grade 2, or Grade 3 hypertension, the probability of remaining in the same stage is much greater than that of progressing to a better stage. This result might have occurred because of the low level of societal adherence to hypertension in the study area. 7 Patients spend 5 and 2 times higher time in stage 1 (prehypertension stage) compared to patients in stage 3 (grade 2 hypertension) and/or stage 4 (grade 3 hypertension). Patients in stage 3 (grade 2 hypertension) spend the shortest mean stay time, on average. This could be aggressive clinical intervention that could rapidly force change either by stabilizing back into better status or rapidly deteriorating into end-organ damage 21 due to complications of comorbidities that act as synergistic multipliers and because of the isolated hypertension. 22 However, there are contradictory findings reported from a study conducted in Ethiopia. 12 The predicted transition probability revealed that the probability of maintaining prehypertension status sharply decreased in the first 12 months of follow-up time and then became stable thereafter. A study conducted in Gambia 23 reported a consistent finding. This finding is in line with findings from China and South Korea.24,25

Compared with female, male patients are more likely to transit into grade 1, grade 2, and grade 3 hypertension stages. Several studies confirmed that males have a greater incidence of hypertension than females do.26,27 One possible explanation for the gender disparities in hypertension is that women are likely to seek medical attention, which of course may not be directly linked to hypertension status checks. 28 Older patients (30+) have an increased risk of transitioning into worse disease stages than patients who are below 30 years of age. Studies conducted in China and other countries reported similar findings.29–33 Compared with rural residents, urban residents had an increased risk of transitioning from prehypertension stages to hypertensive stages (grades 1, 2, and 3). This may be because people living in cities engage in less physical activity and are more likely to consume foods and beverages that increase the risk of hypertension. A study conducted in Ghana, India, and other places has also reported consistent findings.27,31 However, a study conducted in Gambia reported no significant difference in the prevalence of hypertension between rural and urban dwellers. 23 Patients who had a family history of hypertension and diabetes had more risk of transitioning from prehypertension stages to hypertension stages than those who had no family history of hypertension and diabetes. Consistent findings from a study in South Korea reported that a history of diabetes mellitus and a family history of hypertension are major risk factors for the development of hypertension.18,24

Study limitation

This study was aimed to estimate transition probability and transition intensity and identifying associated risks between stages of blood pressure progression using a time homogeneous Markov model. However, there are some limitations this study does not take into account in the data analysis. This study used data extracted from a single center that limits wider generalization. Thus, for wider generalization and to provide a complete picture of disease burden in the country a suitable sample size must be determined, considering the other socioeconomic and demographic diversity of the patients. In this study we handle the effects of missing observations using “missing completely at random” technique because we assume a large sample and missingness occur in a completely random manner. This might lead to biased results that lead to invalid inferences. Another limitation of this study is we used a time homogeneous Markov model, assuming a constant transition rate between disease stages over time. Even though the constant rate of change assumption holds, we recommend future study comparing different Markov process frameworks, including semi-Markov models, since factors such as treatment changes, adherence, and clinical management might affect the model assumptions. Finally, some significant covariates, such as diet, smoking, exercise, BMI, and alcohol consumption, were not covered in the current study. Therefore, in future research we recommend a study incorporating those covariates and accounting for effects of individual-level rate of change between disease stages over time and using different imputation techniques to account for effects of missing observations.

Conclusions and recommendations

At the initial check-up time (baseline), majority of study participants were in grade 1 hypertension. Patients in grade 2 hypertension had a higher risk of transitioning to grade 3 hypertension. The likelihood of remaining in the pre-hypertensive stage was much greater than that of remaining in other disease stages. As age increases, the risk of progressing to worse disease stages increases, having less chance of remaining in the better disease stage. The probability of remaining in the same disease stage for a longer duration is lower. Male sex, older age (30+), urban residence, and family history of hypertension were potential risk factors of increased BP and hypertension. Thus, more clinical care and treatments are needed for the elderly and for patients with grade 2 hypertension. For improved hypertension treatment and control, the health actors should create awareness-rising campaigns and provide free diagnostic tests to both urban and rural residents.
