Organ Donor Potential After Extracorporeal Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation for Out‐of‐Hospital Cardiac Arrest: A Post‐Hoc Analysis of a Randomized Controlled Trial

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundExtracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR) is a potentially life‐saving intervention in refractory out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA). ECPR enables, in most of the treated cases, ICU admission to patients who otherwise would have died at the emergency department (ED). Still, many of them die, frequently after withdrawal of life‐sustaining therapies due to irreversible critical conditions and related complications. The additional time provided by ECPR, however, might allow for the assessment of organ donor suitability. The aim of this study was to evaluate the impact of ECPR on the number of potential organ donors after refractory OHCA.MethodsWe performed a post‐hoc analysis of the multicenter INCEPTION trial, which randomized 134 OHCA patients between conventional CPR (CCPR) and ECPR. Detailed patient reports were presented to transplant physicians to determine the acceptability of the liver and kidneys for organ donation. In addition to the intention to treat analysis, we performed an “as‐treated” analysis, limited to patients arriving without return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) at the ED.ResultsOut of 70 patients randomized to ECPR and 64 to CCPR, potential organ donors were identified in 14 (20%) and 4 (6%) patients, respectively (χ 2 test, p = 0.038). In the as‐treated analysis, 15 out of 55 (27%) treated with ECPR were potential donors, compared to 0 out of 59 treated with CCPR (p < 0.001). This included five (9%) potential kidney donors and 15 (27%) potential liver donors.ConclusionAlthough ECPR is currently used with life‐saving intentions, it may simultaneously increase the number of potential organ donors following cardiac arrest in selected patients.Trial RegistrationClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03101787Keywords: extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation, organ donation, out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest

Introduction

The shortage of donor organs is a long‐lasting global challenge. Although in the European Union the number of patients registered on waiting lists for organ transplantation is over 143.000, there are only approximately 34.000 organ transplants each year [1]. Annually, 5% of patients waiting for a donor organ die [1]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to expand the pool of organ donors. One of the strategies that is advocated to achieve this goal is the application of organ donation after cardiac death (DCD). DCD donors are classified according to the Maastricht criteria [2]. Most organ procurements of DCD donors are performed in a controlled manner after withdrawal of life‐sustaining treatment at an Intensive Care Unit (DCD‐III). Another option is to procure organs of a potential donor immediately after termination of attempted cardiopulmonary resuscitation. The logistics of organ donation after uncontrolled death (DCD‐II) are complex and time as well as resource‐sensitive, which hampers widespread clinical implementation. Moreover, grafts from DCD‐II donors show worse outcomes than grafts from DCD‐III donors or donors after brain death (DBD) [3], although this aspect warrants further investigations.

Extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR) is the application of extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) during cardiac arrest. ECPR is increasingly used as a rescue therapy in refractory out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) [4]. Due to ECPR, more OHCA patients, who otherwise would have died at the emergency department (ED), are admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) compared to patients treated with conventional cardio‐pulmonary resuscitation (CCPR). Still, many of them die in hospital, frequently after withdrawal of life‐sustaining treatment with a dismal neurological prognosis as the leading cause of death [5, 6]. Potentially, these patients could be evaluated as organ donors. They may be considered as DCD‐III or even DBD donors, whereas without ECMO, they could, at best, have been considered as DCD‐II donors had they died in the ED [7].

The actual clinical effectiveness of ECPR for OHCA is still subject to debate, with only a limited amount of evidence from randomized trials at hand. Several retrospective studies report high rates of organ donations after ECPR [8, 9, 10, 11]. Just as for survival, the data on organ donation potential after ECPR, derived from prospective, randomized data are scarce. Thus far, only one randomized trial on ECPR for OHCA, performed in a single center of excellence, has reported the number of organ donations after ECPR versus CCPR [12].

We recently published the results of the INCEPTION trial, a pragmatic multicenter, randomized, controlled trial performed in cardiosurgical centers in the Netherlands, comparing the clinical effectiveness of ECPR versus CCPR for OHCA with respect to survival with a favorable neurological outcome [9]. In the current post‐hoc analysis, we aimed to evaluate the impact of ECPR on the number of potential organ donors after refractory OHCA.

Methods

Study Design

This study is a post‐hoc analysis of the pragmatic, multicenter INCEPTION trial performed in the Netherlands to study the clinical effectiveness of ECPR for OHCA [9]. The original study protocol, as well as the collection and utilization of data for additional research questions, was approved by the medical ethical committee (METC) of Maastricht University (NL58067.068.16/METC162039). This approval also contained the application of deferred consent. Details about the original trial and protocol are published separately [9, 13].

Study Population

The study population consisted of patients between 18 and 70 years old who had a witnessed refractory OHCA (defined as the absence of ROSC after 15 min of advanced life support) with an initial ventricular arrhythmia where bystander basic life support was initiated. Exclusion criteria were based upon co‐morbidities or contra‐indications for ECPR (Table S1) or when the interval between arrest and initiation of cannulation was expected to exceed 60 min. If this time was exceeded in patients who were already included, they were retained in the study.

Study Procedures

Study procedures are extensively reported in the study protocol [13] and the original publication of the main study [9]. All patients were transported to the hospital during ongoing CCPR. Randomization was performed by the receiving physician and could occur before hospital arrival. The emergency medical teams were unaware of the allocation. Patients who were randomized to ECPR and did not regain ROSC during transport underwent ECPR; the remainder of the patients underwent CCPR until ROSC or until cessation of resuscitation efforts. Crossover from CCPR to ECPR was discouraged but allowed. Post‐resuscitation care, including end‐of‐life decisions were left to the discretion of the treating physicians. Neuro‐prognostication was performed in adherence with European and Dutch guidelines.

Data Collection

A donor report according to Eurotransplant format was made retrospectively by one of the authors (J.L.) and checked by a second (H.V.) for all participants who died after ICU admission and had invasive ventilation. This report contains information on age, sex, body mass index (BMI), laboratory results, and information on the reasons of death or withdrawal of life‐sustaining treatment. For a complete example report, see Supplemental 2.

The Netherlands is organized in three regions that coordinate organ retrieval and donation. Three transplant coordinators from all three different organ donation regions in the Netherlands (J.W., H.H., M.D.) with more than 10 years of experience performed the initial screening of these reports. If two out of three transplant coordinators would consider the patient as a potential donor, the donor report was presented to a possible accepting specialist [three transplantation nephrologists from three different transplantation centers in the three different regions (M.H.L.C., J.S.F.S, A.D.Z.) and three transplantation liver surgeons from the three‐liver transplantation centra in the Netherlands (J.J., W.N.N., V.E.M.)]. If the transplant coordinators found the patient exclusively a potential liver donor, only the liver data were offered for further assessment. Similarly, only the kidney data were presented for further evaluation if the liver was deemed not acceptable for transplantation. An organ was classified as potential organ to donate if two or more specialists deemed the organ potentially acceptable. Acceptance for normothermic regional perfusion and/or normothermic machine perfusion in the remarks section was scored as acceptance. Patients who died before ICU admission could be regarded as potential DCD‐II donors but were rejected as potential donors in the current analysis, as there is currently no active program for the preservation of uncontrolled DCD donors (DCD‐II donors) in the Netherlands.

As an additional analysis, donation forms were analyzed for one participating hospital, the hospital with the most inclusions. This was done to document the reasoning behind treating physicians not reporting a patient as a potential donor.

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome of this analysis was the proportion of potential organ donors. The proportion of potential liver and/or kidney donors was reported as a secondary outcome. The primary analysis followed intention‐to‐treat principles. An “as‐treated” analysis was performed in a predefined subgroup of patients arriving without ROSC at the hospital [13]. As secondary outcomes, we looked at patient characteristics, ICU admission and reason to stop treatment of potential donors versus no donors and deceased.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were calculated as means (±standard deviation, SD) for normally distributed data and otherwise as median and interquartile range (IQR, 25th–75th percentile) and categorical data as number (percentage). Continuous variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test, while the Chi‐squared test (with continuity correction) or Fisher's exact test was used for categorical variables. Two‐sided p value from Fisher's exact test was obtained by multiplying the one‐sided p value by two.

To assess factors that were associated with potential donorship after ECPR, we performed an univariable analysis in deceased patients from the intention to treat population [13], comparing potential organ donors with patients who were not considered potential organ donors.

In line with the original INCEPTION study (9), odds ratios (OR) are reported as effect estimates with 95% confidence intervals. p values ≤ 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Missing values are shown as number [n = ], if not mentioned there were no missing data. Analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows version 27.0.1.0 (Armonk, NY: IBM Corp).

Results

Patients

The clinical results of the original INCEPTION study [9] are reported extensively elsewhere. Here we focus on the organ donor potential in the study population. A total of 134 patients were included of which 70 were allocated to the ECPR arm and 64 to the CCPR arm. Baseline characteristics of the study population are presented in Table 1. ECPR was initiated in 52 out of 70 patients in the ECPR arm; while in 13 patients allocated to ECPR, the intended procedure was aborted because of stable ROSC. The number of patients admitted to the ICU was 57 out of 70 (81%) in the ECPR arm versus 23 out of 64 (36%) in the CCPR arm (OR 0.13 [0.06–0.28], p < 0.001). The other patients died at the ED and were discarded as potential organ donors (Table 2).

As Treated Analysis

In the intention‐to‐treat analysis 11 out of 70 (15.7%) patients allocated to the ECPR group had stable ROSC on hospital admission versus nine out of 64 patients allocated to the CCPR group (14.1%). In addition, four patients allocated to the CCPR group had exclusion criteria present before the intervention (1× age >70 years, 1× no bystander BLS, 1× ROSC within 15 min of ACLS, 1× terminal heart failure). These patients were excluded from the as‐treated analysis as specified in the study protocol [13]. Three patients allocated to the CCPR group received ECPR, whereas seven patients allocated to ECPR, who reached the hospital without stable ROSC, did not receive ECPR due to various reasons. These patients were crossed over between study groups for the as‐treated analysis. A consort flowchart for the as‐treated analysis, including reasons for crossover, is presented (Figure S3). The number of patients surviving to ICU admission in the as‐treated analysis was 46 out of 55 (83.6%) in the ECPR‐as‐treated group versus 14 out of 59 (23.7% in the CCPR‐as‐treated group) (OR 16.43 [6.25–50.0], p < 0.001). ICU mortality was 41 out of 46 (89.1%) versus 8 out of 14 (57.1%) in the ECPR‐as‐treated and CCPR‐as‐treated group, respectively (OR 6.15 [1.50–25.0], p = 0.028). The initial assessment by the transplant coordinators yielded a total of 22 potential liver donors (21 [38.2%] in ECPR‐as treated and 1 [1.7%] in CCPR‐as treated group, p < 0.001), 15 potential kidney donors (14 [25.5%] in ECPR‐as treated and 1 [1.7%] in CCPR‐as treated group, p < 0.001). Further evaluation by the specialists identified 15 as potential liver donors (15 [27.3%] in ECPR and 0 in CCPR, p < 0.001), five potential kidney donors (5 [9.1%] in ECPR and 0 in CCPR group, p = 0.048), and five potential multi‐organ donors (kidneys and liver) (5 [9.1%] in ECPR and 0 in CCPR group, p = 0.048) in the whole population (Table S4).

When the consensus rate is compared between accepting specialists there was 26.7% full consensus (three out of three accepted) and 73.3% partial consensus (two out of three accepted) to accept the liver. For the kidney, there was 60% full consensus and 40% partial consensus.

Factors Associated With Potential Donorship

The percentage of patients in whom life‐sustaining treatment was withdrawn due to an unfavorable neurological prognosis was significantly higher in potential organ donors. In addition, the use of ECMO was significantly higher in potential donors just as the percentage of ROSC before ED or during CPR (Table 4, Table S5). The total duration of CPR and serum lactate on admission were not significantly associated with potential donorship.

Reasons Why the Treating Physician Did Not List Potential Donors as Donors

In the sub‐analysis of one hospital, the reason why a patient did not become a donor was analyzed in the as‐treated population. In this sub‐analysis, there were four potential donors and 31 no donors and deceased. In the potential donor group, one was missing because of documented multi‐organ failure, one was not accepted after consulting the transplant coordinator, another one had already passed away when they checked the register (result: approval), and the last one was not recognized for unknown reasons (Table S6).

Discussion

This post‐hoc analysis of the multicenter randomized INCEPTION trial shows that the use of ECPR leads to a significant increase in the number of potential donors after OHCA compared to CCPR. The opportunity to evaluate ECPR patients with an unfavorable prognosis for organ donation may contribute to increasing the size and quality of the organ donor pool, which is desirable given the mismatch between the number of available donor organs and the size of the waiting list.

In our study, we found that over 27% of the ECPR patients would potentially be suitable as organ donors. In a post‐hoc analysis of the Prague OHCA study, 19 out of 92 patients (21%) who actually received ECPR donated one or more organs [12]. The third RCT performed to compare CCPR to ECPR did not report on organ donation or the potential to donate [10]. A retrospective study in France compared the 1‐year kidney transplant results from DBD donors after CPR with and without ECPR. There was no statistically significant difference in kidney function in the recipient between the groups [14].

ECPR is increasingly being used in OHCA. Two single‐center randomized trials, the ARREST trial and the Prague OHCA study, showed survival rates from 30% to 40% after ECPR for OHCA [8, 10]. The INCEPTION trial recently demonstrated that such excellent results reported by these centers of excellence cannot be reproduced easily during nationwide widespread and pragmatic implementation [9]. This is also substantiated by a recent individual pooled patient data meta‐analysis of randomized and propensity‐matched data showing an absolute risk reduction of approximately 6% [15]. Whereas the life‐saving potential of ECPR is clear, there is still discussion about its place in care for OHCA patients given its large impact on healthcare systems and healthcare professionals; in addition, its cost effectiveness is unclear [16].

It has been advocated to take the secondary yield of donor organs into account when evaluating the health economic benefit in cardiac arrest research [17]. Love et al. estimated the incremental cost‐effectiveness ratio of CCPR for traumatic cardiopulmonary arrest, considering the secondary yield of donor organs. According to this estimation, which measured each transplanted organ as an additional life saved, the costs per life saved decreased from $1.8 million to less than $77.000, despite only 2% of the study population actually becoming an organ donor [18].

In a cost‐effectiveness analysis of the PARAMEDIC2 trial [19], evaluating the clinical benefit of adrenaline in cardiac arrest, it was estimated that the incremental cost‐effectiveness with a lifetime horizon decreased from £81.000 to £16.000 per QALY when the health economic impact of organ donation was taken into account, even while the total number of organ donors was less than 1% in both study arms [19]. Therefore, the accessory gain of organ donation has an important impact on the cost‐effectiveness of therapies in cardiac arrest, even when the clinical benefit of the treatment or the yield of organ donors may seem modest. Given the high percentage of potential organ donors after ECPR, it may be conceived that organ donation has a substantial positive effect on the cost‐effectiveness of ECPR and indirect saved lives count. It must be underlined that such theoretical exercises still need to be verified by (prospective) real‐world data. Moreover, from an ethical perspective, it is important to apply ECPR only with the primary aim of survival of the patient and not with the sole objective of organ procurement. However, ECPR progressing into an organ‐preserving procedure may be a gray area [20]. Interestingly, ECMO techniques are increasingly being applied in organ procurement particularly in DCD‐III donors. However, early experiences are also emerging using in‐situ normothermic regional perfusion (NRP) in potential DCD‐II donors where resuscitation is deemed futile. In such cases, in situ NRP is performed by means of peripheral veno‐arterial ECMO in combination with a supradiaphragmatic aortic balloon. In most instances, such procedures occur in centers were ECPR is readily available. Still, it may be an ethical challenge to decide when to proceed to ECPR or to convert to in situ NRP, applying ECMO without further life‐saving intent. Moreover, the results of in situ NRP in DCD‐II donors are unclear.

In this study, we found a potential donor group of 15 donors out of the 114 patients who passed away in our as‐treated INCEPTION cohort consisting of patients with refractory OHCA. All potential donors received ECPR. There were no statistically significant differences in patient characteristics (age, BMI, sex), time to CCPR, AED use, lab values of pH and lactate upon admission, and days of ICU stay. The only remarkable factors for the potential donor group are the reason to stop treatment (mainly neurological but also (cardiac) shock) and the use of ECMO. The reason to stop treatment was an expected difference as mainly patients who become organ donor are neurologically untreatable.

The observed difference in organ acceptance rates in the as‐treated cohort between potential liver (27%) and kidney (9%) donors warrants a closer look (see Supplemental 4). We believe it is related to a few key factors related to pre‐transplant organ assessment. A primary reason for the disparity is the difference in how organ viability is evaluated. Liver viability can be assessed relatively easily on the pump before transplantation, providing a direct measure of the organ's function. In contrast, while kidneys are also transported on a pump, there is currently no comparable method for real‐time organ viability testing. This lack of a direct assessment tool for kidneys forces a more cautious approach. The anuria and creatinine trends observed after OHCA are related to this issue. OHCA often leads to acute kidney injury (AKI), and without pre‐existing creatinine values, it's difficult to predict the long‐term expected kidney function. The high cardiovascular risk profile and prevalence of conditions like diabetes in OHCA patients further complicate this, as these conditions can already compromise kidney function. Additionally, a lack of data on proteinuria—a key parameter used by nephrologists to assess kidney function—may have also contributed to the lower acceptance rate for kidneys. The research was only focused on kidneys and livers. We excluded pancreases because, it is very rare for a pancreas to be accepted for donation after OHCA. This is due to ischemia and the trauma from resuscitation compressions. The heart is also rarely accepted when the cardiac arrest has a cardiac cause. Lungs were not included in our study because donation potential could not be reliably assessed retrospectively. Although this could have been a valuable area to investigate, it was not part of our initial survey.

Another limitation of the present post hoc analysis is the retrospective and theoretical character. No organ donor was effectuated from the INCEPTION study population [21]. At the time of the study, an opt‐in system for organ donation was in place in the Netherlands, which has generally restrained the number of effectuated potential donors. In addition, the intended transition to an opt‐out system has sparked a fierce societal debate, which in our perception has led to an initial transient increase of family refusals. Therefore, the number of effectuated donors in this trial provides no reliable representation of the true donor potential. However, the organ donor conversion rate may vary greatly amongst countries and should be taken into consideration when evaluating the organ donation potential of ECPR as a secondary benefit. In addition, this secondary benefit may be further mitigated in countries that do not accept donors after cardiac death.

Since the assessment as a potential organ donor was not prospectively recorded, we designed the current post‐hoc study to mimic the donor selection process. This limits our analysis by the inability to distinguish between DCD and DBD donors, as in the Netherlands, brain death is never assessed in patients who are not already recognized as potential organ donors. The first screening was performed by transplant coordinators using a standardized donor report, and thereafter potential donors were offered for acceptance to transplant specialists. If a donor was rejected this could be augmented in an open‐text field, the responses to this field were not consistently completed, making it difficult to analyze detailed reasons for why certain patients were not considered donors. In the donor report, there was only one lab value mentioned to evaluate organ functions. No trends in creatinine or transaminases were shown that potentially could have altered the decision to accept or reject an organ. We aimed to make the reports as complete as possible and therefore transplant physicians were not blinded to whether a patient had received ECPR. Although this is a potential limitation, none of the evaluating physicians were involved in the original INCEPTION trial or are in involved in ECPR practice or research, assuring their objective assessment. Difficulty to blind caregivers and researchers is a general limitation of ECPR research.

Application of normothermic regional perfusion or machine perfusion techniques has increased the number of livers that are procured but discarded for transplantation after functional testing. Although both NRP and Normothermic Machine Perfusion (NMP) are used in the Netherlands, NMP is the more common method, with NRP currently in the initial stages of a nationwide rollout. Based on previous research [22], we know that approximately 2/3 of livers tested on the pump are successfully transplanted. These livers are scored as potential donors in our study, which may result in a slight overestimation of the true liver donor potential since viability testing may lead to post‐procurement rejection.

Conclusion

ECPR for OHCA results in a potential large percentage of potential organ donors. Therefore, ECPR may contribute to reducing the still‐existing organ shortage. Although ECPR should exclusively be used with life‐saving intentions, the attributed yield of potential organ donors may benefit the health economic impact of ECPR and contribute to further societal acceptability. The additional benefit, however, is dependent on the actual conversion rate of potential organ donors.
