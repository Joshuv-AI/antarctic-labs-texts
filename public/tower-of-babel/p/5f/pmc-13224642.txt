Introduction

Hydatid disease is a zoonotic parasitic infection caused by the larval stage of Echinococcus, most commonly Echinococcus granulosus [1]. It remains endemic in regions where livestock rearing is common, including the Mediterranean basin, the Middle East, Africa, South America, Australia, and parts of Asia [2]. Humans become accidental intermediate hosts through ingestion of parasite eggs, usually via contaminated food or close contact with infected dogs, which serve as the definitive hosts. After ingestion, the embryos penetrate the intestinal wall and disseminate through the portal and systemic circulation, subsequently developing into hydatid cysts in various organs [3, 4].

The liver is the most frequently involved organ, accounting for approximately 70–75% of cases, followed by the lungs in 10–30%. Other organs such as the spleen, kidneys, brain, bones, and peritoneum are affected less commonly and usually as secondary sites [1]. Peritoneal hydatidosis typically results from rupture of a primary hepatic or splenic cyst, either spontaneously or during surgical intervention, leading to intraperitoneal dissemination [5, 6]. In contrast, primary peritoneal hydatidosis without evidence of hepatic or pulmonary involvement is exceedingly rare, representing less than 2% of cases of intra-abdominal hydatid disease [7].

Clinical manifestations are often nonspecific, and many patients remain asymptomatic until the cysts enlarge sufficiently to cause pressure-related symptoms such as abdominal pain, distension, or features of obstruction. Although imaging modalities and serological tests are helpful, they may lack specificity, particularly in atypical locations, making preoperative diagnosis challenging [1]. Owing to its rarity and diagnostic difficulty, primary peritoneal hydatidosis remains an uncommon clinical entity. We report a rare case of primary intra-abdominal hydatid disease and discuss the associated diagnostic and therapeutic considerations.

Case presentation

A 28-year-old male patient, with no known history of chronic illness or previous hepatic or pulmonary hydatid disease, presented with progressive abdominal distension and abdominal pain of four months’ duration. The pain was more pronounced in the left lower abdomen, insidious in onset, continuous, non-radiating, exacerbated by physical activity, and relieved by rest. It was not related to food intake and was associated with occasional nausea without vomiting. The patient denied fever, shortness of breath, bowel or urinary symptoms, or weight loss. There was no history suggestive of bowel obstruction, including constipation or absolute constipation, and no urinary complaints. Family history was negative for hydatid disease or similar conditions. Past surgical history included an appendectomy 10 years earlier.

Physical examination

Physical examination

On physical examination, the patient was clinically stable with normal vital signs. There was no pallor, jaundice, cyanosis, or pedal edema. Abdominal examination revealed generalized distension with an inverted umbilicus. No visible peristalsis or dilated veins were noted. The abdomen was soft with mild diffuse tenderness. Multiple cystic, non-pulsatile masses were palpable (Fig. 1), with no guarding or rigidity. The liver and spleen were not palpably enlarged. Bowel sounds were present and normal.

Laboratory investigations

Laboratory investigations

Laboratory investigations showed hemoglobin of 16.0 g/dL (reference range: 13.0–18.0 g/dL), total leukocyte count of 8.3 × 10⁹/L (4–10 × 10⁹/L), neutrophils 62%, lymphocytes 26%, and eosinophils 4%. Liver function tests, renal function tests, coagulation profile, and serum electrolytes were all within normal limits.

Imaging findings

Imaging findings

Abdominal ultrasonography revealed a well-defined cystic lesion in the left lower abdomen measuring approximately 10 × 7 cm. The lesion was anechoic with clear contents, a thick wall, no internal vascularity on color Doppler imaging, and no significant solid components. A second similar cystic lesion measuring approximately 8 × 7 cm was identified in the right lower abdomen. The presence of multiple cystic intra-abdominal lesions raised suspicion for hydatid disease, particularly in the setting of an endemic region, and further evaluation with contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) was recommended.

CECT of the abdomen and pelvis demonstrated multiple well-defined cystic lesions with double-wall appearance, many containing internal daughter cysts, without significant soft-tissue components or post-contrast enhancement. The largest cyst was located in the left lower abdomen, extending into the pelvic cavity, measuring approximately 10.6 × 8.0 cm. Additional cysts were identified in the right upper abdomen (8.8 × 6.6 cm), right paracolic gutter (4.3 × 3.9 cm), and anterior right pelvic cavity (6.9 × 5.8 cm). Two smaller partially calcified cysts were also noted: one in the right paracolic gutter measuring 2.5 × 1.9 cm and another at the level of the pelvic inlet anteriorly measuring 2.9 × 1.6 cm. All cysts were confined to the peritoneal cavity and were inseparable from adjacent bowel loops and the anterior abdominal wall, with minimal associated ascites (Fig. 2). The coexistence of daughter cysts, double-wall morphology, and partial calcification strongly favored hydatid disease over other cystic intra-abdominal lesions. These findings were consistent with peritoneal hydatid cysts, including Gharbi type II cysts representing active lesions with daughter cysts and type IV cysts reflecting degenerative transitional lesions. No hepatic or pulmonary involvement was identified, supporting the diagnosis of primary peritoneal hydatidosis.

Therapeutic intervention

Based on the clinical and radiological findings, a diagnosis of disseminated peritoneal hydatid disease was established. The patient was started on preoperative albendazole therapy at a dose of 400 mg orally twice daily for three weeks, followed by surgical intervention.

A midline laparotomy was performed. Intraoperatively, six well-encapsulated cysts were identified within the peritoneal cavity. The largest cyst occupied the left lower abdomen and pelvis, causing displacement of adjacent bowel loops posteriorly and adherence to the parietal peritoneum. Additional cysts were identified in the right upper abdomen, right paracolic gutter, pelvic cavity, attached to the dome of the urinary bladder, and adherent to the inferior pole of the right kidney. Dense but separable adhesions to surrounding structures were noted. Careful adhesiolysis and meticulous dissection allowed complete en bloc excision of all cysts without rupture or intraperitoneal contamination. Strict anti-spillage precautions were implemented using hypertonic saline-soaked pads placed around the operative field as scolicidal protection, with careful isolation of adjacent viscera during cyst manipulation and excision (Figs. 3 and 4). Histopathological examination demonstrated characteristic laminated acellular membranes and germinal layers consistent with hydatid cyst disease.

The postoperative period was uneventful, and the patient demonstrated a satisfactory clinical recovery. Oral intake was resumed gradually, and no postoperative complications were observed. The patient was discharged in stable condition on the 5th postoperative day. Postoperatively, adjuvant antihelminthic therapy with albendazole was initiated at a dose of 400 mg twice daily to reduce the risk of recurrence. The treatment was continued for three months, with a two-week drug-free interval after each month of therapy. Liver function tests and complete blood counts were regularly monitored during treatment and remained within normal limits.

Follow-up

Follow-up

The patient was followed for 12 months through regular outpatient clinical assessments and serial abdominal ultrasonography every three months. Liver function tests and complete blood counts were monitored during albendazole therapy and remained within normal limits. No evidence of recurrence was detected during follow-up.

Discussion

Primary peritoneal hydatidosis is an exceptionally uncommon manifestation of echinococcal disease and poses significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges [7]. In contrast to the more typical secondary peritoneal involvement resulting from rupture of hepatic or splenic cysts, primary peritoneal disease occurs without detectable cysts in solid organs [5]. The absence of hepatic or pulmonary involvement in our patient, together with intraoperative findings, supports the diagnosis of primary peritoneal hydatidosis.

The clinical presentation of peritoneal hydatid disease is often nonspecific, leading to delayed diagnosis. Symptoms usually arise from the gradual enlargement of cysts and their mass effect on surrounding structures rather than from systemic illness. In pelvic and lower abdominal involvement, patients may present with abdominal distension, pain, or pressure-related symptoms involving the bowel or urinary tract. In the present case, progressive abdominal distension and localized pain were the predominant complaints, consistent with previously reported cases [8, 9]. The long asymptomatic course underscores the indolent nature of the disease. Some cases of abdominal hydatidosis may remain completely asymptomatic and are discovered incidentally during imaging or surgery for unrelated conditions [10].

Primary peritoneal hydatidosis is an exceptionally rare manifestation of echinococcosis, accounting for less than 2% of abdominal hydatid disease cases [7]. Several mechanisms have been proposed for primary peritoneal involvement, including hematogenous dissemination, lymphatic spread, or direct transmural migration of embryos through the intestinal wall into the peritoneal cavity without hepatic filtration. The differential diagnosis of multiple intra-abdominal cystic lesions includes mesenteric cysts, duplication cysts, cystic lymphangioma, pancreatic pseudocysts, and cystic neoplasms [11]. Imaging modalities play a pivotal role in differentiating these entities. Ultrasonography is useful as an initial diagnostic tool, whereas computed tomography provides superior assessment of cyst number, anatomical relationships, calcifications, and operative planning.

Imaging plays a crucial role in establishing the diagnosis, particularly in atypical presentations. Ultrasonography serves as an effective initial modality, while contrast-enhanced computed tomography provides superior delineation of cyst number, size, distribution, and internal architecture. The presence of daughter cysts, double-wall configuration, partial calcification, and characteristic signs such as the water-lily appearance strongly support the diagnosis. In our patient, CT imaging demonstrated multiple peritoneal cysts of varying stages, consistent with active and transitional disease, without evidence of primary organ involvement. These findings were instrumental in preoperative planning. Additionaly, The absence of hepatic or pulmonary cysts on imaging was particularly important in supporting the diagnosis of primary peritoneal hydatidosis rather than secondary intraperitoneal dissemination.

Serological tests are useful adjuncts but should be interpreted in conjunction with imaging and clinical findings, as false-negative results may occur, particularly in isolated peritoneal disease. In our case, serological testing was not performed because the diagnosis was strongly supported by characteristic imaging findings and due to limited local availability of serological assays. Although eosinophilia may be observed, laboratory investigations are frequently unremarkable, as was noted in this case.

Surgical management remains the cornerstone of treatment for disseminated peritoneal hydatidosis, particularly in symptomatic patients and those with large or multiple cysts. The primary surgical objective is complete excision of cysts while preventing spillage, which could result in secondary dissemination or anaphylaxis [12]. In the present case, meticulous dissection and en bloc removal of all cysts were achieved, with strict intraoperative precautions to avoid contamination. The use of scolicidal agents and protective isolation of surrounding viscera is essential in minimizing recurrence.

Adjunctive antiparasitic therapy with albendazole is widely recommended in disseminated disease to reduce the risk of residual microscopic disease and recurrence [13]. Postoperative albendazole therapy was administered in this case with close monitoring of liver function and hematological parameters. The patient demonstrated an excellent response, with no evidence of recurrence during follow-up. Long-term surveillance is mandatory in patients with peritoneal hydatidosis, as recurrence may occur years after treatment. Serial imaging and clinical assessment remain the mainstay of follow-up. The favorable outcome observed in our patient highlights the importance of early recognition, thorough surgical clearance, and appropriate adjunctive medical therapy in achieving optimal results.

Conclusion

This case highlights the rarity of primary peritoneal hydatidosis and underscores the importance of early recognition, comprehensive imaging, meticulous surgical technique, and postoperative antiparasitic therapy in achieving favorable outcomes.
