Feasibility of longitudinal relaxation rate mapping with non-Cartesian sampling and compressed sensing on a 1.5 T magnetic resonance linear accelerator
Authors: Lucas McCullum, Michael J. van Rijssel, Ken-Pin Hwang, Yao Ding, Chad Tang, Comron Hassanzadeh, Jinzhong Yang, Peter A. Balter, Jihong Wang, Clifton D. Fuller, Ergys D. Subashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

The integrated Elekta Unity magnetic resonance (MR)-linear accelerator (Linac) (Elekta AB; Stockholm, Sweden) provides a novel platform for precision radiotherapy, combining a 1.5 T MR imaging (MRI) scanner with a 7 MV Linac [1]. This system enhances accuracy in the delineation of targets and organs-of-interest due to its high potential for imaging-based biomarkers and superior soft tissue contrast compared to conventional computed tomography (CT)-based methods. As a subset of adaptive radiation therapy, MR-guided adaptive radiotherapy (MRgART) further supports several strategies optimizing radiation dose delivery to the target while minimizing risks to adjacent normal tissues [2].

The versatility of MRI pulse sequences and their corresponding contrast mechanisms have the potential to improve radiotherapy outcomes by enabling noninvasive mapping of functional [3], physiologic [4], and metabolic biomarkers [5]. Multiparametric MRI protocols complement and extend current diagnostic tests for detecting and characterizing malignancies with strong correlations to histological validation [6]. Furthermore, recent research emphasizes the importance of biologically informed adaptive planning, integrating spatiotemporal variations in tumor biology into MRgART workflows. Specifically, changes in the longitudinal relaxation rate (R1 = 1/T1) have been used to predict outcomes in glioblastoma patients [7] and evaluate radiation response in the heart [8], prostate [9], and head and neck [10]. Additional work is also beginning to focus on how changes in T1 affect normal tissue [11].

The use of T1 mapping is a major building block for several multiparametric MRI protocols [12], [13], [14]. However, the implementation of these protocols is challenging in anatomical sites that experience large physiological motion, leading to significant challenges in radiotherapy, particularly for targets in the thorax and abdomen [15]. The use of MRI pulse sequences relying on non-Cartesian sampling, specifically radial (projection-encoding) methods, offers intrinsic advantages in clinical imaging for moving targets. For example, 3-dimensional (3D) radial sampling provides inherently volumetric data, reduces sensitivity to flow artifacts, and enables shorter echo times compared to Cartesian techniques. When the Nyquist criterion is satisfied across the entire domain in k-space, these sampling functions enhance image quality for regions with high amounts of motion with minimal streaking artifacts [16]. Additionally, radial k-space trajectories inherently encode the respiratory motion signal, eliminating the need for external tracking devices, a method known as self-gating [17], [18], [19]. The inherent redundancy in projection encoding facilitates the implementation of a range of advanced reconstruction methods derived from view-sharing [20], compressed sensing [21], and machine learning algorithms [22], enabling reduced acquisition times at higher spatial and temporal resolution [23], [24].

One of the main challenges for implementing non-Cartesian T1 mapping on MR-Linacs is the limited acquisition time and the lack of studies systematically evaluating optimal methodologies under these constraints. Therefore, in this study, four different non-Cartesian k-space trajectories and eight different offline compressed sensing algorithms were investigated to determine the optimal combinations that provide the highest quantitative T1 mapping accuracy at the highest level of undersampling with minimal imaging artifacts.

Material and methods

Validation of clinical methods

The initial feasibility of T1 mapping was evaluated using clinical non-Cartesian pulse sequences and reconstruction at two institutions, The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center and UMC Utrecht. All experiments were performed on a 1.5 T MR-Linac running Philips software version R5.7.1.2. Quantitative T1 mapping was implemented using the variable flip angle (VFA) method [25], [26], [27] with a radiofrequency (RF) spoiled gradient echo sequence (SPGR). The two optimal flip angles were determined using the formalism in Driven Equilibrium Single Pulse Observation of T1 (DESPOT1) [28] by averaging across T1 values in the range of 250–2000 ms, additionally restricting T2 relaxation times to less than 200 ms, in line with typical clinical values in the abdomen [29]. The flip angle used during the preparation phases was determined by calculating the respective mean Ernst angle [30] across the desired range of T1 values. Two different 3D non-Cartesian k-space sampling strategies were investigated: radial kooshball and stack-of-stars. The total acquisition time was maintained within 5 min to accommodate workflow constraints for MRgART. A Cartesian VFA sequence was used for benchmarking [27]. Relevant imaging parameters are summarized in Table S1 in Supplementary Materials.

Gradient delay optimization

In this study, we estimated ghosting artifacts due to gradient delays [31] by generating 360 radially equal-spaced line profiles at the edge of the CaliberMRI National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) / International Society for Magnetic Resonance in Medicine (ISMRM) Premium System Phantom (Model 130; CaliberMRI; Boulder, CO) due to its use as a reference for NIST-traceable T1 values [32]. The edge ghosting artifact was quantified by comparing the mean signal intensity inside and outside the phantom edge in ROIs located within 2 cm of the edge. The artifact was evaluated for gradient trajectory delays in the range 0–5 μs. The optimal delay was determined based on the minimum ratio of signal intensities outside and inside the phantom.

Phantom studies

At The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center, repeat scans using the proposed Cartesian and non-Cartesian sequences with the optimized gradient delay were acquired once per day over five days to evaluate longitudinal bias and reproducibility. Additionally, UMC Utrecht validated our proposed sequences in a single scanning session using the MagIQ Eurospin II TO5 Phantom [33] (Leeds Test Objects; Boroughbridge, UK). Phantoms were placed on a 4 cm solid water slab to position closer to the isocenter of the MR-Linac. The room temperature was monitored using an analog thermometer placed outside the bore and found to be in the range 19-22°C. The nominal T1 values in the phantom were referenced to the values specified for the respective serial number.

Offline compressed sensing reconstruction

In this work, we adopted the formalism of Parallel Imaging and Compressed Sensing (PICS) as provided in the implementation of the Berkley Advanced Reconstruction Toolbox [34], [35], [36], [37] (BART, version 0.9.00):(1)x=argminx‖Ax-y‖22+∑kλkRkxwhere x= unknown image, y= measured k-space data, A= imaging acquisition model, λ= regularization parameter, R= regularization operator, and k= number of regularization operators [38]. This formalism allowed for the combination of several regularization operators along the spatial, temporal, or spatiotemporal domain. In our study, the undersampling factor was varied by a factor of 5, 10, 15, and 20, and the optimal regularization parameter was identified based on an L-curve search [39]. The search was initially focused on maximizing resolution in the spatial domain, followed by a search for minimizing bias and coefficient-of-variation (CoV) in the parameter (T1) domain. Volunteer and patient studies

All experiments in volunteers and patients were performed at The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center and research subjects provided informed consent under an internal imaging protocol (PA15-0418) as approved by the institutional review board. The feasibility of the sampling and reconstruction methods were demonstrated in one healthy volunteer and one patient undergoing radiation therapy for a tumor in the left kidney. The optimal non-Cartesian sampling and reconstruction method was compared to the Cartesian sequence during free-breathing imaging. To accommodate patient size, the acquired field-of-view (FOV) and sampling matrix were changed to 35 × 35 × 35 cm3 and 116 × 166 × 116 with 1 mm3 isotropic reconstructed resolution without major changes in scan time.

In human subjects, the respiratory signal was identified by principal component analysis (PCA) of the signal extracted from the center of k-space from all eight receive RF-channels. The PCA methodology was performed along the channel direction, identifying the receive signal with the strongest representation of the respiratory signal. Thereafter, the respiratory signal was phase or amplitude-sorted into a user-selected number of equally spaced breathing phases. The re-gridding algorithm with sum-of-squares reconstruction [41] was used as implemented by default in the MR-Linac and used as a reference reconstruction. The offline parallel imaging with compressed sensing (PICS) was implemented with the optimized regularization operator and parameters identified in the search process in the phantom experiments.

Image analysis

All computations unless otherwise noted were performed using Python 3.7.9. For the phantom analysis, a circular region-of-interest (ROI) with a 1 cm diameter was created at the center of each vial using 3D Slicer [42]. The T1 maps were reconstructed offline by following the DESPOT1 formalism [28]. Bias and reproducibility were assessed using both the NIST/ISMRM and Eurospin phantom and quantified using the percent difference from Cartesian benchmark values and CoV, respectively. Longitudinal repeatability was assessed in the NIST/ISMRM phantom by acquiring repeated scans over five days and quantified using the median bias and CoV across repeat scans. The Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient [43] was used to evaluate the correlation of median bias across five days with measured T1 within each respective phantom vial. The Lin’s Concordance Correlation Coefficient [44] (LCCC) was calculated as a measure of agreement between the measured values and the phantom reference values. Bias was assessed with respect to the T1 values measured with the benchmark method, while reproducibility was assessed using the longitudinal CoV for the repeat scans. A Spearman’s test was used to evaluate the correlation of bias with T1 as a measure of R1-dependence on accuracy of acquisition method. To evaluate the effect of reconstruction method on image quality, for each regularization operator and parameter combination, the median full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) of the NIST/ISMRM phantom edge were sorted as a function of undersampling and regularization. This metric was used as a surrogate for line spread function, hence spatial resolution. When two methods achieved equal FWHM values, they were sorted based on their T1 bias and CoV.

All sampling methods had negligible longitudinal bias with a maximum CoV ∼ 3.3%. Additionally, the correlation between bias and measured T1 was not statistically significant for the half-spoke measurements while significant for the full-spoke. The full-spoke acquisitions demonstrated a negative correlation with bias as the T1 values increased. A similar trend was noted in the full-spoke kooshball sequence, though less severe which was investigated further in Figs. S2 and S3 in the Supplementary Materials. Therefore, the 3D kooshball half-spoke sequence was evaluated in further experiments in volunteers and patients.

Discussion

This study demonstrated the feasibility of implementing robust and clinically compatible T1 mapping sequences on a 1.5 T MR-Linac using non-Cartesian sampling strategies and advanced offline PICS reconstruction. Our results highlighted the potential of combining 3D radial acquisitions with tailored regularization schemes to overcome traditional challenges in MRgART, particularly in anatomical regions subject to large respiratory motion such as the abdomen. The implementation of T1 mapping on the MR-Linac has traditionally been limited by long scan times, motion artifacts, and a lack of optimized reconstruction techniques. In this work, we addressed these limitations through: (1) the use of the variable flip angle method; (2) the use of radial k-space trajectories that support volumetric and motion-resilient acquisitions; (3) the application of compressed sensing reconstruction with regularization in the spatial/temporal and contrast domain; (4) the incorporation of gradient delay correction for minimizing trajectory-mismatch artifacts; and (5) respiratory phase-resolved sorting for retrospective correction of motion-induced artifacts.

Our phantom results confirmed that all tested non-Cartesian methods maintained high reproducibility with minimal longitudinal bias. Among the trajectories evaluated, the half-spoke kooshball sampling exhibited the best combination of spatial resolution, minimal bias, and shortest acquisition time. The slightly larger bias observed in the stack-of-stars and full-spoke trajectories at high T1/T2 values is likely due to incomplete spoiling and signal recovery, a known limitation of VFA methods using RF-spoiled gradient echo sequences [45]. These observations are consistent with prior reports [11], [46], [47], [48], [49], [50] emphasizing the importance of sequence spoiling in high dynamic range applications [45]. We also noticed that the stack-of-stars reconstructions have considerable undersampling artifacts leading to streaks of underestimated T1 which appear to contribute to the individual vial values and their apparent underestimation of phantom reference values.

Compressed sensing reconstruction further improved the accuracy and robustness of T1 estimates in undersampled datasets. Notably, regularization strategies that exploit spatiotemporal redundancies, such as L1WST and LLRST, were superior in preserving spatial resolution and minimizing parameter-domain uncertainty across all tested undersampling factors. These results support prior work demonstrating the effectiveness of sparsifying transforms and rank-constrained models in enabling dynamic and quantitative MRI with aggressive undersampling [51].

When compared to the Cartesian acquisition, 3D kooshball with PICS reconstruction provided more accurate T1 estimates in mobile tissues such as the kidney cortex and medulla. The clinical feasibility of implementing this framework was demonstrated in a patient undergoing radiation therapy, where phase-resolved T1 mapping revealed physiologically relevant contrast and acceptable agreement with reference values. While the current implementation requires substantial offline reconstruction time, further optimization and hardware acceleration (e.g., artificial intelligence-enabled pipelines) are anticipated to improve integration into MRgART workflows. However, reconstruction of images could be done between fractions for downstream assessment. For same-day online assessment, access to supercomputing resources through the Philips Research Imaging Development Environment (PRIDE) 2.0 tool would be a logical next step for this study.

A few limitations should be noted. First, although two phantoms were used to validate the methods, the sample size for in vivo evaluation was limited. Larger studies will be necessary to evaluate inter-subject variability, assess reproducibility across different anatomical sites, and validate longitudinal changes in response to therapy. Second, the accuracy of VFA-based T1 mapping remains sensitive to B1 inhomogeneity (which leads to proportional errors in the resulting T1 maps), slab selection profile, and imperfect spoiling, which may necessitate corrections or alternative techniques such as B1 mapping or Look-Locker-based methods [52] in future work. Third, while this study focused on static and respiratory-sorted reconstructions, additional temporal dimensions (e.g., contrast-enhanced or perfusion imaging) could be incorporated using the same framework to enable true multiparametric imaging on the MR-Linac. Under clinical operation, the Elekta Unity 1.5 T MR-Linac has a maximum gradient strength of 15 mT/m and slew rate of 65 T/m/s which is substantially lower than diagnostic systems and may affect the sampling efficiency and potential distortions under inhomogeneous conditions. One of the potential artifacts is ghosting due to the differences between the intended k-space location and the actual k-space location which was partially addressed in this paper through gradient delay optimization. Future work could also investigate gradient waveform analyzers to increase correction capabilities [53], [54]. Other effects such as eddy currents are not expected to be high for this sequence due to the rapid readout.

In summary, the proposed methods enable volumetric, motion-resolved T1 mapping on the MR-Linac in anatomies prone to respiratory motion and were validated across two phantoms, a healthy volunteer, and a clinical case. These capabilities establish a pathway for integrating quantitative MRI into MRgART workflows. Future studies will focus on expanding clinical evaluation, optimizing reconstruction speed, and extending the framework to additional quantitative biomarkers and multiparametric imaging protocols.
