A bayesian dynamic borrowing framework for improving the efficiency of clinical trials
Authors: Zengyue Zheng, Keer Chen, Pengfei Zhu, Haiyan Wu, Shuping Jiang, Dingheng Zhang, Shein-Chung Chow, Ying Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo develop a Bayesian method for dynamic borrowing of information from historical clinical trials and real-world data that has the potential to improve the efficiency of clinical trials.MethodsWe propose a novel statistical metric to quantify heterogeneity among data sources. Based on this metric, a Multi-Source Dynamic Borrowing (MSDB) Bayesian prior framework was proposed, which can address baseline imbalance and dynamically discount information from each data source separately without assuming exchangeability. In a simulation study, we compared the proposed framework and metrics with historical methods.ResultsWe evaluated performance by comparing power, type I error, bias, and mean squared error (MSE) across methods. Our approach outperformed existing methods by enhancing power and reducing MSE, while effectively controlling type I error and bias in the presence of heterogeneity and baseline imbalances. Moreover, an application involving isatuximab in relapsed and refractory multiple myeloma further validated the performance of the MSDB prior.ConclusionThe MSDB prior method dynamically adjusts borrowing information, allowing for a more robust and accurate incorporation of historical data. Its flexible framework allows it to play a crucial role in various applications, thereby accelerating drug development and supporting regulatory decision making.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12874-025-02691-2.

Introduction

Advances in computing and health information technology have created a rich source of biomedical real-world data (RWD), the potential value of which has become increasingly significant [1]. Additionally, the inherent shortcomings of randomized controlled trials (RCT) are driving a growing interest in obtaining confirmatory real-world evidence [2, 3]. Nevertheless, it must be acknowledged that it is challenging to achieve the strength of RCTs evidence using RWD alone [4–7]. A possible solution is to incorporate real-world data into a new trial, in which the most intuitive method is to use an informative prior constructed by RWD. However, this method results in bias and inflated type I errors if the current trial data conflict with the prior [8].

Pocock [9] was the first to propose a basic model for the inclusion of external trials, given the different patient populations and study designs. Ibrahim and Chen proposed power priors based on the weighted log-likelihood. Subsequently, modified power prior and commensurate power prior were developed as improvements [10–13]. Another well-known method for incorporating external data is the hierarchical modeling approach [14, 15]. The meta-analytic-predictive (MAP) prior accounts for heterogeneity by assuming exchangeability between different data sources based on a random-effect meta-analysis framework [16]. Schmidli et al. proposed a robust MAP prior by incorporating a weakly informative component into the MAP prior, which allows for more effective discounting of external data [17]. Zhang et al. proposed the empirical Bayes robust MAP prior (EB-rMAP) method, which utilizes Box’s prior predictive p-value and adaptively calculates robust weights for information borrowing [18]. Yang et al. advanced a novel approach, the self-adapting mixture prior (SAM), which is based on the likelihood ratio test and offers a fresh perspective on the specification of robust weights [8].

This paper introduces a novel statistical measure of heterogeneity, the Prior-Posterior Consistency Measure (PPCM), which enables a more precise assessment of consistency across different data sources. PPCM serves as a foundation for accurate information borrowing and the dynamic adjustment of borrowing strength. Based on the PPCM metric, this paper proposes the multi-source dynamic borrowing Bayesian (MSDB) prior framework, which allows for the incorporation of multiple historical datasets with varying degrees of heterogeneity while simultaneously addressing baseline imbalances and effect heterogeneity. Furthermore, we extended the methodology to time-to-event endpoints to address the relatively underexplored challenge of borrowing information for such outcomes.

Materials and methods

Piecewise exponential model

Consider a bridging study in which original area RCT data and real-world data in a new area are available for both the treatment and control arms. In the following, the original area RCT data and RWD are collectively referred to as the historical data. The objective was to incorporate historical data into the analysis of the new trial and obtain a posterior inference for the parameter representing the treatment effect.

Within this scenario, the event times of the patients, such as death or progression-free survival (PFS), were assumed to follow the piecewise exponential (PWE) model, with the treatment and control groups modeled separately [19, 20]. Prior-Posterior consistency measure

In this part, we propose a new metric to measure the consistency between data, which is the basis of the new information borrowing method proposed in this paper. Accurately measuring the consistency between data, the amount of borrowed information can be rationally adjusted when information is borrowed, so as to achieve the expected statistical performance.

In our informative borrowing framework, the target is to reduce conflicts between historical prior and observed data. This is achieved by mixing the historical prior with the non-informative prior, and assigning greater weights to the latter when the prior-posterior heterogeneity is large. As well, when the heterogeneity is excessive it is completely transformed to non-informative prior. Therefore, a consistency measure is needed to choose the mixture weights such that the consistency measure increases along with the mixture weights. However, the consistency expressed by traditional measures such as the Kullback-Leibler Divergence and the Hellinger distance decreases as mixture weights of non-informative priors increase. This contradicts the target of reducing borrowing in the presence of high heterogeneity. Therefore, this study proposes a PPCM metric.

The main idea of the prior-posterior consistency measure (PPCM) is as follows. First, the posterior predictive probability function, updated using the prior information, is used to calculate the consistency between the prior information and the data by measuring the predictive probability that is lower than the current data. The detailed calculation process is as follows:

Assume there is prior information about the parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:f\left(\theta\:\right)$$\end{document}, and the sample data \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:y=\left({y}_{1},\dots\:,{y}_{n}\right)\sim\;f\left(Y\right|\theta\:)$$\end{document}. When researchers use PPCM to assess the consistency between two datasets, one dataset can be treated as historical data to construct the prior, while the other is regarded as the observed data (sample data) for the calculation.

Multi-source dynamic borrowing bayesian prior

In this section, we introduce the proposed Multi-Source Dynamic Borrowing Bayesian (MSDB) prior. Since both real-world data (RWD) and external RCT may introduce baseline imbalances relative to the current RCT, the first step of the MSDB prior constructs a propensity score model and performing stratification and matching based on the estimated scores. Subsequently, information from external sources is incorporated into the prior distribution through a modified Meta-Analytic-Predictive (MAP) framework. To address potential conflicts between prior and current data, and to enhance robustness, Schmidli et al. proposed the robust MAP (rMAP) method. The MSDB prior adopts a similar idea and mixes a vague component into the prior distribution. Since our method applies the same borrowing procedure separately to the treat arm and control arm, the following theoretical derivations are presented using the control arm as an example. Although the theoretical presentation focuses on two external sources, the MSDB prior is applicable to multiple historical data sources. See the Supplementary Materials (Part D Theoretical details of extending the MSDB prior to studies with more than three sources of external data) for the general formulation.

Where\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:n\:$$\end{document}denote the sample size and\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:\epsilon\:$$\end{document}the degree of consistency between historical and current data. Specifically, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{n}_{ETD,s,k}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{n}_{RWD,s,k}$$\end{document} represent the sample sizes from the external trial data and real-world data, respectively, in stratum \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\text{s}$$\end{document} and time interval \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:k$$\end{document}. The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\epsilon_{RWD,s,k}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\epsilon_{ETD,s,k}\:$$\end{document}are defined analogously. The weights \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\omega\:}_{RWD,s,k}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\omega\:}_{ETD,s,k}$$\end{document} consider both the sample size and the consistency between the data, allowing more information to be borrowed from historical data with a larger sample size and greater consistency. Once the stratum-specific MAP priors are obtained, the overall prior distribution for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:K$$\end{document} time intervals can be defined using the weights based on the sample sizes as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\lambda\:}_{k}={\sum\:}_{s=1}^{S}\frac{{n}_{CTD,s,k}}{{n}_{CTD,k}}{\lambda\:}_{s,k}\:.$$\end{document}

Step 4: the parametric density estimate of the MSDB prior

After Steps 2 and 3, the modified MAP prior \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{f}_{mMAP}\left({\lambda\:}_{k}\right)$$\end{document} for the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\lambda\:}_{k}$$\end{document} in the new trial can be derived. However, the challenge is that the prior \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{f}_{mMAP}\left({\lambda\:}_{k}\right)$$\end{document} is usually from an unknown distribution, which means that it is difficult to directly sample from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{f}_{mMAP}\left({\lambda\:}_{k}\right)$$\end{document} in Bayesian computation [17]. Using an MCMC sample from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{f}_{mMAP}\left({\lambda\:}_{k}\right)$$\end{document}, the authors replaced \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{f}_{mMAP}\left({\lambda\:}_{k}\right)$$\end{document} with its approximation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\widehat{f}}_{mMAP}\left({\lambda\:}_{k}\right)$$\end{document} which is based on the results of Dalal and Hall [21]. It was stated that any parametric prior can be satisfactorily approximated by a mixture of conjugate priors. With time-to-event data \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{E}_{Z,s,k}\sim\;Gam\left({r}_{Z,s,k},{\lambda\:}_{Z,s,k}\right)$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{f}_{mMAP}\left({\lambda\:}_{k}\right)$$\end{document} can be approximated using a weighted mixture of gamma distributions as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\widehat{f\:}}_{mMAP}\left({\lambda\:}_{k}\right)=\widehat{f}\left({\lambda\:}_{k}|{D}_{HD}\right)=\sum\:_{j=1}^{J}{\omega\:}_{j}Gamma\left({a}_{j},{b}_{j}\right)\:,$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sum\:}_{j=1}^{J}{\omega\:}_{j}=1$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{HD}=\{{D}_{ETD},{D}_{RWD}\}$$\end{document} denote the historical data included external RCT data and real-world data. The number of components \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:J$$\end{document} is up to the number of data sets, and the other parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\omega\:}_{j},\:{a}_{j},\:{b}_{j}$$\end{document} are estimated by the EM algorithm. However, sometimes the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\widehat{f}}_{mMAP}\left({\lambda\:}_{k}\right)$$\end{document} may still introduce bias when heterogeneity is excessive. To enhance the robustness of the informative prior, we add a vague prior component to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\widehat{f}}_{mMAP}\left({\lambda\:}_{k}\right)$$\end{document}:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned}\:\widehat{f}\left({\lambda\:}_{k}|{D}_{HD}\right)=&\left(1-{\omega\:}_{R}\right)\ast\:\sum\:_{j=1}^{J}{\omega\:}_{j}Gamma\left({a}_{j},{b}_{j}\right)\\&+{\omega\:}_{R}\ast\:Gamma\left({a}_{0},{b}_{0}\right)\:,\end{aligned}$$\end{document}

where robust weight \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\omega\:}_{R}\in\:\left[\text{0,1}\right]$$\end{document}. The general principle is to set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\omega\:}_{R}$$\end{document} close to 0 if the historical data is believed to be “similar with” current data, or vice versa.

The setting of\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:{\omega\:}_{R}\:$$\end{document}is the emphasis to address prior-data conflicts. The most traditional method is to set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\omega\:}_{R}$$\end{document} as a fixed value based on previous research [17], but it often leads to less reliable results. Consequently, data-driven \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\omega\:}_{R}$$\end{document} setting methods have become a popular research topic, and the Box’s method [22, 23], EB-rMAP method [18] and SAM method [8] have been developed sequentially. In this study, we propose a prior-posterior consistency measure (PPCM) based weight that can more accurately scale the consistency between data. The posterior distribution can be obtained according to Bayesian theory as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned}\:\widehat{f}\left({\lambda\:}_{k}|{d}_{CTD},{d}_{HD}\right)=&\left(1-{\stackrel{\sim}{\omega\:}}_{R}\right)\ast\:\sum_{j=1}^{J}{\stackrel{\sim}{\omega\:}}_{j}Gamma\left({a}_{j}+{r}_{CTD,k},{b}_{j}+{E}_{CTD,k}\right)\\&+{\stackrel{\sim}{\omega\:}}_{R}\ast\:Gamma\left({a}_{0}+{r}_{CTD,k},{b}_{0}+{E}_{CTD,k}\right)\end{aligned}$$\end{document}

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\text{w}\text{h}\text{e}\text{r}\text{e}\:{\stackrel{\sim}{\omega\:}}_{R}={\omega\:}_{R}{f}_{0}/\{{\omega\:}_{R}{f}_{0}+(1-{\omega\:}_{R}\left){\sum\:}_{j}{\omega\:}_{j}{f}_{j}\right\},\:{\stackrel{\sim}{\omega\:}}_{j}={\omega\:}_{j}{f}_{j}/\left({\sum\:}_{j}{\omega\:}_{j}{f}_{j}\right),$$\end{document}\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned}&\:{f}_{0}=\frac{\varGamma\:({a}_{0}+{r}_{CTD,k})/{({b}_{0}+{E}_{CTD,k})}^{{a}_{0}+{r}_{CTD,k}}}{\varGamma\:\left({a}_{0}\right)/{b}_{0}^{{a}_{0}}},\\&{f}_{j}=\frac{\varGamma\:({a}_{j}+{r}_{CTD,k})/{({b}_{j}+{E}_{CTD,k})}^{{a}_{j}+{r}_{CTD,k}}}{\varGamma\:\left({a}_{j}\right)/{b}_{j}^{{a}_{j}}}.\end{aligned}$$\end{document}

And \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{d}_{CTD},{d}_{HD}$$\end{document} was the observation of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{CTD},{D}_{HD}$$\end{document}. Thus, the posterior predictive distribution can also be obtained as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned}\:\widehat{f}\left(D|{d}_{CTD},{d}_{HD}\right)&=\int\:f\left(D|{\lambda\:}_{k}\right)\widehat{f}\left({\lambda\:}_{k}|{d}_{CTD},{d}_{HD}\right)d{\lambda\:}_{k}\\&\:=\int\:f\left({E}_{k}|{r}_{k},{\lambda\:}_{k}\right)\widehat{f}\left({\lambda\:}_{k}|{d}_{CTD},{d}_{HD}\right)d{\lambda\:}_{k}\:.\end{aligned}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:f\left({E}_{k}|{r}_{k},{\lambda\:}_{k}\right)=Gamma({r}_{k},{\lambda\:}_{k})$$\end{document}. The proposed \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\epsilon$$\end{document} value to evaluate prior-data consistency is defined as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\epsilon=2\ast\text{min}\left\{F_{D\vert d_{CTD},d_{HD}}\left(d_{CTD}\right),1-F_{D\vert d_{CTD},d_{HD}}\left(d_{CTD}\right)\right\}\:,$$\end{document}

Where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{F}_{D|{d}_{CTD},{d}_{HD}}$$\end{document} was the cumulative distribution function of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{f}\left(D\right|{d}_{CTD},{d}_{HD})$$\end{document},i.e.\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned}&\:{F}_{D|{d}_{CTD},{d}_{HD}}\left(d\right)={\int\:}_{0}^{{E}_{k}}\widehat{f}\left(D|{d}_{CTD},{d}_{HD}\right)d{e}_{k}=\\&{\int\:}_{0}^{{E}_{k}}\int\:f\left({e}_{k}|{r}_{k},{\lambda\:}_{k}\right)\widehat{f}\left({\lambda\:}_{k}|{d}_{CTD},{d}_{HD}\right)d{\lambda\:}_{k}d{e}_{k}\:.\end{aligned}$$\end{document}

However, there is no analytical solution for this cumulative distribution function, so it is approximated in this paper by Monte Carlo simulations. The optimal \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\omega\:}_{R}$$\end{document} is then defined as the smallest weight that satisfies the minimum required consistency value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\varGamma\:$$\end{document}, while ensuring effective information borrowing. Determination of the minimum required consistency is generally achieved through discussions between statisticians and clinical investigators at the trial design stage. If no weight satisfies this requirement, then \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\omega\:}_{R}$$\end{document}=1 is set, indicating no information borrowing.\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\widehat{\omega\:}}_{R}=\left\{\begin{array}{c}argmi{n}_{{\omega\:}_{R}}\left\{{\omega\:}_{R}:\epsilon\ge\:\Gamma\:,ESS\ge\:0\right\}\:,if\exists\:{\omega\:}_{v}\:s.t.\:\epsilon\ge\:\Gamma\:\\\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:1\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:,if\:otherwise\end{array}\:,\right.$$\end{document}

where the threshold parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\varGamma\:\in\:\left(\text{0,1}\right)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:ESS\:$$\end{document}represents the effective sample size of the prior distribution [24, 25]. Because a small \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\epsilon$$\end{document} value indicates a strong prior-data conflict, we recommend setting \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\varGamma\:$$\end{document} to a relatively high value. A simple grid search algorithm is used to solve for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\omega\:}_{R}$$\end{document}.

Step 5: Decision-making based on posterior distribution

When using Bayesian inference to make decisions, probability statements are often constructed to express the likelihood of achieving a clinically meaningful effect size (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:CM$$\end{document})[26]. If \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{CM}$$\end{document} is the desired minimum probability or threshold of that effect size, then a decision rule can be expressed as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:P({\theta\:}_{effect}>CM)>{P}_{CM}$$\end{document}, where the probability of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\theta\:}_{effect}$$\end{document} is derived from the Bayesian posterior probability distribution. The selection of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:CM$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{CM}\:$$\end{document}depends on factors such as the disease state, unmet medical needs, and patient population; however, this is beyond the scope of this study.

Once \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\widehat{\lambda\:}}_{k}$$\end{document}, is obtained, the cumulative hazard function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:H\left(t\right)$$\end{document} and the survival function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:S\left(t\right)$$\end{document} can be derived. In the PWE model, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:H\left(t\right)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:S\left(t\right)$$\end{document} are expressed as\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned}\:H\left(t\right)&={\int\:}_{0}^{t}\lambda\:\left(u\right)du\\&={\lambda\:}_{{K}_{t}}\left(t-{d}_{{K}_{t}-1}\right){\sum\:}_{k=1}^{{K}_{t}-1}{\lambda\:}_{k}\left({d}_{k}-{d}_{k-1}\right);\end{aligned}$$\end{document}\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned}\:S\left(t\right)=\text{exp}\left\{-H\left(t\right)\right\}\:.\end{aligned}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{K}_{t}={argmin}_{k}({d}_{k}>t)$$\end{document} denotes the interval in which the events occur. Finally, the median survival time\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:M\:$$\end{document}can be obtained by solving \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{M}={S}^{-1}\left(0.5\right)$$\end{document}. Owing to the complexity of deriving \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:M$$\end{document} from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\lambda\:}_{k}$$\end{document}, obtaining an analytical solution for the posterior distribution of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:M$$\end{document} directly from the posterior distribution of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\lambda\:}_{k}$$\end{document} is highly challenging. Therefore, it was approximated using a Monte Carlo simulation.

Effective sample size

When incorporating external information to augment the current study by a prior, it is essential to understand the amount of information contained in the prior. Effective sample size (ESS) can quantify the amount of information provided by the prior distribution. ESS are well understood for conjugate one-parameter exponential families. Morita et al. proposed a new algorithm to measure the ESS of one parameter prior, so as to extend to the general nonconjugate case [24]. However, it has been shown that Morita’s method fails a basic predictive consistency criterion. Thus, Neuenschwander et al. introduced the expected local-information-ratio (ELIR) [25] method to compute the ESS, which has been as well used in this study.

ELIR ESS is defined as the mean of the prior information to the Fisher information ratio:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned}\:ES{S}_{ELIR}&={E}_{\theta\:}\left\{r\left(\theta\:\right)\right\}={E}_{\theta\:}\left\{\frac{i\left(\text{p}\right({\uptheta\:}\left)\right)}{{i}_{F}\left(\theta\:\right)}\right\}\\&={E}_{\theta\:}\left\{\frac{i\left(p\right(\theta\:\left)\right)}{{E}_{{Y}_{1}|\theta\:}\left\{{i}_{F}\left({Y}_{1};\theta\:\right)\right\}}\right\}\:,\end{aligned}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:i(p\left(\theta\:\right)=-\frac{{d}^{2}logp\left(\theta\:\right)}{d{\theta\:}^{2}}$$\end{document} is the information of the prior distribution and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{i}_{F}\left({Y}_{i};\theta\:\right)=-\frac{{d}^{2}logf\left({Y}_{1}\:\right|\theta\:)}{d{\theta\:}^{2}}$$\end{document} is the observed Fisher information for one information unit.

Simulation study

Simulation study setting

Simulation data generation

We considered a two-arm study with a time-to-event endpoint, aiming to borrow external RCT data and real-world data to augment both treatment and control arms. For simplicity, two covariates are considered: one continuous variable following a normal distribution, and one dichotomous variable following a binomial distribution. The covariates were defined as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned}&\:{X}_{1,CTD}\sim\;B\left(N,{p}_{CTD}=0.3\right),\\&\:{X}_{1,RWD}\sim\;B\left(N,{p}_{CTD}+{\delta\:}_{RWD}\right),\\&\:{X}_{1,ETD}\sim\;B\left(N,{p}_{CTD}+{\delta\:}_{ETD}\right),\end{aligned}$$\end{document}\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned}&\:{X}_{2,CTD}\sim\;N\left({\mu\:}_{CTD}=1,\sigma\:=0.2\right),\\&{X}_{2,RWD}\sim\;N\left({\mu\:}_{CTD}+{\delta\:}_{RWD},\sigma\:\right),\\&{X}_{2,ETD}\sim\;N\left({\mu\:}_{ETD}+{\delta\:}_{ETD},\sigma\:\right)\:,\end{aligned}$$\end{document}

where, the parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\delta\:}_{RWD}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\delta\:}_{ETD}$$\end{document} represent the baseline imbalance between real-world data (RWD) and external trial data (ETD) relative to the current trial data (CTD). Without loss of generality, the survival time was simulated using the Weibull distribution and the hazard rate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\lambda\:$$\end{document} in\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{T}_{0}\sim\;Weibull\left(shape=2,scale=\frac{{t}_{median}}{{\left(\text{log}\left(2\right)\right)}^{0.5}}\right)\:.$$\end{document}

To simulate the survival time with different distributions, the median survival time \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{t}_{median}$$\end{document} was modeled as\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned}\:{t}_{median}=&{X}^{T}{\alpha\:}_{0}+{\beta\:}_{CTD}*group\\&+{\gamma\:}_{ETD}*{Z}_{1}*group\\&+{\gamma\:}_{RWD}*{Z}_{2}*group\:.\end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Again, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Z=({Z}_{1},{Z}_{2})$$\end{document} indicates different data sources, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:group\:=\:1$$\end{document} denotes the treatment group and the other denotes the control group. Therefore, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\beta\:}_{CTD}$$\end{document} represents the treatment effect between groups in the current trial, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\gamma\:}_{ETD}$$\end{document} represents the difference in treatment effects between the external trial and the current trial, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\gamma\:}_{RWD}$$\end{document} represents the difference in treatment effects between real-world data and the current trial.

Thus, the survival time can be obtained as\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:{T}_{0}={\left(-log\left(U\right)\right)}^{\frac{1}{2}}*\frac{{t}_{median}}{{\left(log\left(2\right)\right)}^{0.5}}\:(U\in\:U(\text{0,1}\left)\right)$$\end{document}, whereas the censoring time \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:C=U(0,c)$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:c$$\end{document} is a parameter obtained by the Monte Carlo simulation with a specified censoring rate. The final event time is given as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:T=\text{m}\text{i}\text{n}({T}_{0},C)$$\end{document} and the censoring status as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\delta\:=I({T}_{0}<C)$$\end{document}.

Simulation scenario setting

In this section, four simulation studies were conducted to demonstrate the performance of the proposed method. First, the impact of baseline imbalance on information borrowing was explored by adjusting the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\delta\:}_{ETD}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\delta\:}_{RWD}$$\end{document} parameters, with two scenarios: “low baseline difference” and “high baseline difference.” Second, the effect of different levels of heterogeneity on the method was investigated by adjusting the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\gamma\:}_{ETD}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\gamma\:}_{RWD}$$\end{document} parameters. Different values of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\gamma\:}_{ETD}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\gamma\:}_{RWD}$$\end{document} were set to examine how information borrowing from historical data and current data is affected under varying degrees of heterogeneity. Third, the influence of different consistency thresholds \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\varGamma\:$$\end{document} on the method was explored. To evaluate the performance of the proposed method in handling effect heterogeneity and baseline imbalance, we conduct comparisons with the EB-rMAP and PS-MAP methods across various simulation scenarios. Application

Relapsed and refractory multiple myeloma (RRMM) primarily remains an incurable disease, and almost all patients relapse either on or after these treatments [27]. Monoclonal antibodies targeting CD38 have emerged as a novel treatment, such as daratumumab and isatuximab (Isa), which was investigated in combination with pomalidomide and dexamethasone (Pd) in a phase III, randomized, multicenter, open-label trial (ICARIA-MM) [28] and received approval from the National Medical Products Administration through a real-world study in Hainan, China. Discussion

We propose a flexible MSDB prior framework for combining multiple historical data, including not only RCT data but also real-world data, thus increasing the effective sample size of RCTs and the strength of RWD evidence. The theoretical derivations of time-to-event data were also overcome, which makes it easy for other readers to extend the theory to normal and dichotomous data. In addition, historical rMAP-based methods are either too aggressive or too conservative for selecting vague weights, leading to inappropriate borrowing from the historical data. We propose PPCM weights that enable a more precise assessment of consistency across different data sources.

Simulation results demonstrated that the PS-MAP method tended to inflate Type I error under high historical effect scenarios and reduced power under low historical effect scenarios. This is likely because PS-MAP is a covariate-adjusted extension of the MAP method, which provides limited flexibility in addressing effect heterogeneity, thus leading to the borrowing of biased information. The EB-rMAP method did not adjust for baseline differences across datasets, which may have led to an overestimation of heterogeneity and inaccurate borrowing. As a result, its performance was compromised. When EB-rMAP was embedded within the MSDB framework, its operating characteristics performed better. However, it still showed a risk of power loss under low historical effect scenarios. To borrow information more appropriately, we explored other consistency metrics within the MSDB framework, including SAM method and Box’s method. However, neither of these approaches performed better on operational characteristics. But this doesn’t mean the SAM method and Box’s method are absolute unsuitable approaches. Each approach has its own strengths and unique features. In particular, the performance of SAM relies on the correct setting of the tuning parameter, clinically significant difference (CSD). In our simulation study, a relatively large value of CSD was chosen, which may be the reason for the poor performance of the SAM method within the MSDB framework. Yet this precisely reflects a practical challenge: at the design stage, it is often difficult to predict heterogeneity between data sources. As a result, it is hard to choose a suitable tuning parameter. If the choice is not appropriate, it may lead to over-borrowing (which can inflate type I error or reduce power) or under-borrowing (which fails to improve trial efficiency). The tuning parameters of the SAM method and the proposed PPCM method serve entirely different purposes, making it difficult to establish comparability under a specific setting. Consequently, the comparison in this study primarily demonstrates the robustness of the proposed PPCM method across various heterogeneous scenarios and the advantages of its tuning parameter setting. On the other hand, the different performances of various consistency metrics under the MSDB framework also indicate that, although the proposed MSDB prior framework has theoretical advantages in adjusting for baseline imbalance and incorporating information from multiple sources, it still needs to combine with more appropriate consistency metrics to achieve optimal performance. Thus, we proposed a novel consistency metric—PPCM, which was integrated into the MSDB framework that allows for both baseline-adjusted and multi-source information borrowing. This combination demonstrated improved statistical performance in simulation studies: it effectively controlled type I error, borrowed an appropriate amount of effective sample size to enhance power, and overcame power loss under low historical effect scenarios. Furthermore, it achieved lower bias and MSE. Simulations conducted under small sample size settings further illustrate the practical feasibility of the proposed MSDB prior and PPCM method in actual application.

The flexibility of the MSDB prior framework makes it applicable to a wide range of scenarios, such as bridging trials for new regional drug applications, multi-regional clinical trials in the context of global collaborative research and development, and pediatric trials involving extrapolation from adults to pediatric populations [5, 7, 31, 32]. It could be useful in exploratory Phase II trials where efficacy or safety has not yet been established. In such cases, the MSDB prior allows effective borrowing of efficacy or toxicity data from previous studies or real-world evidence to optimise dose exploration strategies. In pediatric or rare disease trials with highly limited patient enrolment, historical or adult trial data often serves as an important source of evidence. MSDB prior measures consistency and borrows information through the PPCM, which allows for integration of evidence as well as enhanced statistical power, thus significantly improving trial efficiency. In the context of multiregional or multicentre trials, a key challenge is to assess the consistency of treatment effects across regions. The MSDB framework is particularly helpful in this context as it considers baseline heterogeneity across regions and uses the PPCM to accurately quantify the consistency of effects[31].. Additionally, with regulatory agencies such as the FDA and NMPA placing greater emphasis on the use of real-world data for pharmaceutical research [33], the MSDB prior framework holds significant potential in applications. In particular, the special drug and device policies implemented in Hainan Lecheng, China, provide a promising environment for our methodologies. In this study, we validated the reliability of the MSDB prior approach using an application study. Moreover, the consistency measures proposed in this study can be extended for broader applications. Furthermore, the theoretical structure of MSDB for time-to-event outcomes is built on modeling the hazard rate, which naturally allows generalization beyond median survival time to other endpoints such as hazard ratios and restricted mean survival time (RMST), broadening its applicability in survival analysis.

Conclusion

The MSDB prior with PPCM measure provides a dynamic approach to borrowing information from historical data, ensuring a more robust and precise integration of external evidence. By addressing key challenges, such as effect heterogeneity and baseline imbalances, it enhances statistical power while maintaining control for type I error and bias. Its flexible framework allows for broad applicability, including bridging trials, multi-regional clinical studies, and pediatric extrapolation, making it a valuable tool in pharmaceutical research. With regulatory agencies increasingly emphasizing the use of real-world data, the MSDB prior with PPCM measure has significant potential to accelerate drug development and support evidence-based regulatory decision-making.
