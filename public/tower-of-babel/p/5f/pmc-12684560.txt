Associations of Immune Cell Subsets With Coronary Artery Calcium Incidence and Progression in the Multi‐Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis
Authors: Riddhi D Patel, Petra Buzkova, Sally Huber, Alan Landay, Matthew Budoff, Joshua C Bis, Nels Olson, Stephen Rich, Susan R Heckbert, Ani Manichaikul, James Floyd, Russell P Tracy, Bruce M Psaty, Margaret F Doyle, Colleen M Sitlani, Joseph AC Delaney, Alison E Fohner, Matthew J Feinstein
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLimited data exist on associations of immune cell subsets with longitudinal changes in subclinical coronary artery disease.MethodsIn the MESA (Multi‐Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis) study, we used a case‐cohort approach to explore associations of 28 immune cell subsets measured at baseline (2000–2002) with longitudinal changes in coronary artery calcium (CAC). We examined incident CAC from examination 2 (2002–2004) through examination 5 (2010–2012) in participants with 0 CAC at baseline using multivariable‐adjusted Cox regression. In participants with CAC >0 at baseline, we analyzed changes in CAC through examination 5 using multivariable‐adjusted linear mixed models. Because no studies have investigated immune cells and longitudinal CAC changes, analyses were considered exploratory, with P<0.05 as the threshold for possible significance.ResultsOf 975 participants with immune cells subsets and CAC measurements at baseline, 378 had CAC 0 at baseline (mean age, 58.4; 37.0% men) and 597 had CAC >0 at baseline (mean age, 65.7; 57.6% men). Natural killer cells were associated with higher incident CAC (hazard ratio [HR], 1.26 per SD higher natural killer cell proportion; P=0.03), whereas T helper type cells were associated with lower incident CAC (HR, 0.81; P=0.04). B cells were associated with CAC progression (β=53.1 Agatston units per SD higher B‐cell proportion, P=0.04), whereas CD14+CD16+ monocytes (β=−71.6; P=0.03) and T regulatory cells (β=−61.9; P=0.03) were associated with lower CAC progression.ConclusionsNatural killer cells may be associated with incident CAC and T regulatory cells may be associated with attenuated CAC progression, among other findings. These warrant replication and experimental investigation.Keywords: coronary artery calcium, immune cells, inflammation, subclinical atherosclerosisSubject Categories: Biomarkers

What Question Should Be Addressed Next?

While these data provide important initial observational data related to potential relationships between systemic immune phenotypes and calcific coronary artery plaque, important next steps include causal experimental validation as well as determination in large‐scale human cohorts of how these blood immune cell imprints relate to plaque immune phenotypes.

Coronary artery calcium (CAC) is a measure of subclinical arteriosclerosis and strongly associated with coronary events. 1 , 2 However, despite decades of data linking inflammation to coronary artery disease, both subclinical and clinical, few large‐scale human data exist on relationships between immune cell effectors of inflammation and coronary plaque. We previously observed that nonclassical monocytes were positively associated (and classical monocytes negatively associated) with common carotid intima‐media thickness progression in the MESA (Multi‐Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis) study and that intermediate monocytes were associated with incident stroke in MESA. 3 , 4 Whether similar associations of monocyte subsets, along with other immune cell subsets, exist with CAC incidence and progression is largely unknown. We therefore explored associations of immune cell subsets, measured at the MESA examination 1 (years 2000–2002) with incident CAC and CAC progression over 11 years. A lack of other cohorts with parallel multiparameter flow cytometry and serial CAC measurement data precluded replication of our exploratory analyses in other cohorts.

METHODS

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. Our 2 coprimary end points were (1) progression to incident non‐0 CAC among those with CAC=0 and immune cell subset measurements at baseline (N=378) and (2) CAC progression over time for individuals with non‐0 CAC and immune cell subsets measurements at baseline (N=597). Details regarding the design and objectives of MESA, measurement of immune cells from baseline in MESA (examination 1, years 2000–2002), and measurement of CAC in MESA have been described in detail previously. 5 , 6 , 7 Our analysis used data from a study of immune cells and cardiovascular risk in MESA, for which the flow cytometry methods have been described in detail. 8 Briefly, immune cells were measured using cryopreserved peripheral blood mononuclear cells isolated during examination 1 in MESA. Cryopreserved blood samples were assayed at the University of Vermont in 2016 to phenotype lymphocyte and monocyte subsets. Myeloid and lymphoid lineage cells were measured from cryopreserved (−135 °C) samples from MESA at baseline after thawing using polychromatic flow cytometry and intracellular cytokine activation staining. 3 Flow cytometry gating strategies and immune phenotyping data from this study derive from the same analyses performed in 2016 for which the methods, including antibodies and gating strategy, have been described in detail. 8 Cell phenotype data are presented as proportions, rather than absolute numbers, because cryopreservation can lead to different absolute levels of certain subsets compared with whole blood despite expected consistency of relative levels of cell proportions within an overall lineage. 9 , 10 In addition, in MESA, distributions of cryopreserved cells from baseline (measured in 2016 following thawing) paralleled those of whole blood samples analyzed freshly in 2005 through 2007 (MESA examination 4). 11 , 12 Overall, a total of 28 different immune cell subsets were analyzed. Because these are exploratory analyses, we used an α of 0.05.

A case‐cohort design including all myocardial infarctions and cases of angina with 2 available vials of cryopreserved blood necessitated use of sampling weights in our statistical models, with weighting done to ensure representativeness of the full MESA cohort. CAC was measured at examination 1 and again at examinations 2 (2002–2004), 3 (2004–2005), 4 (2005–2007), and 5 (2010–2012). All participants gave informed consent, and the study protocol for MESA was approved by the institutional review board at each site.

Given the lack of preexisting normative data on immune cell subsets and longitudinal changes in CAC, as well as our limited sample size, we considered all analyses exploratory, with P<0.05 indicating possible statistical significance. We also applied a Bonferroni correction at P<0.0017 (0.05/28), with findings P<0.0017 then being considered significant at a higher threshold of confidence. The minimally adjusted Model (Model 0) was adjusted for age, gender, race and ethnicity, and site. Our primary model, model 1, was additionally adjusted for baseline education (≥12th grade, dichotomous), smoking (current, never, former), systolic blood pressure (continuous), body mass index (continuous), diagnosis of diabetes (binary), total cholesterol (continuous), high‐density lipoprotein (continuous), statin use (dichotomous), and hypertension medication use (dichotomous), measured at examination 1. We used weighted Cox regression with robust standard errors to model hazard of incident CAC in the population without prevalent CAC at examination 1. As above, weights were constructed to ensure the sample was representative of the full MESA cohort. The proportional hazards assumption was not violated in any analysis, using our primary model (model 1) and a 0.05 significance level. Participants were followed for time to first non‐0 CAC at examinations 2 through 5, with censoring for loss to follow‐up or end of follow‐up at examination 5. In those with non‐0 CAC at examination 1, we modeled CAC with weighted linear mixed effects models with random intercept. Separate models were created for each immune cell subset because we aimed to study the associations with outcome for each immune cell subset without controlling for other subsets, as there is a complex relationship among them and some subsets are inherently related to one another. For instance, in some cases, cell measurements represent varying levels of subsetting of the same overall groups of cells (eg, CD4+CD28−CD57+ T cells as a proportion of CD4+ T cells is a subset of CD4+CD57+ T cells as a proportion of CD4+ T cells). In addition, the 3 monocyte subsets are mutually exclusive and thus percents (as a proportion of total monocytes) typically add up to 100%, thus a lower proportion of 1 subset would mean a higher proportion of the other subsets. Given prior findings of interactions by sex with respect to monocyte associations with carotid atherosclerosis, 4 we also performed interaction analyses of by sex by immune cell populations and subsequently performed additional sex‐stratified secondary analyses when significant interactions were observed.

For incident CAC analyses in those with CAC=0 at baseline, maximum follow‐up time from examination 1 to examination 5 was 11.1 years, and median time to incident CAC (which was possible anytime from examination 2 to examination 5) was 4.8 (interquartile range, 2.99–9.35) years. In incident CAC analyses (Table 2), none of the monocyte subsets were associated with incident CAC. Natural killer (NK) cells (CD3–CD16+CD56+) were associated with incident CAC (multivariable‐adjusted odds ratio [OR], 1.26 [95% CI, 1.03–1.56]), whereas T helper type cells (OR, 0.80 [95% CI, 0.64–0.99]) were associated negatively with incident CAC; these associations were of borderline statistical significance with P<0.05 for exploratory analyses but not meeting Bonferroni‐corrected P<0.0017. A significant interaction by sex was present (Table S2), prompting a gender‐stratified secondary analysis (Table 3, Figure 1). This demonstrated an association of NK cells with incident CAC in men (OR, 1.56 [95% CI, 1.14–2.13]) but not women (OR, 1.21 [95% CI, 0.86–1.70]). In addition, CD4+CD25+ T cells were associated with incident CAC in men (OR, 2.00 [95% CI, 1.25–3.19]) but not in women (OR, 0.89 [95% CI, 0.69–1.15]).

For analyses of baseline immune cells and longitudinal CAC progression (Table 4, Figure 2), several findings were borderline significant at the exploratory P<0.05 but did not meet the Bonferroni‐corrected threshold significance of P<0.0017. Intermediate monocytes were associated negatively with CAC progression (β=−71.58 Agatston units per higher SD of intermediate monocyte proportion [95% CI, −134.9 to −8.3]; P=0.027). DISCUSSION

We newly investigated associations of several defined immune cell subsets with longitudinal changes in CAC over >10 years and 5 repeat examinations in a large‐scale, multicenter longitudinal cohort study. We observed that NK cells (CD3–CD16+CD56+) may be associated positively with incident CAC, whereas T helper type cells may be associated negatively with incident CAC. Significant interactions by sex were present, whereby NK cells associated with incident CAC for men but not women, and CD4+CD25+ T cells were associated with incident CAC in men but not women. The hazard ratio of 1.26 for incident CAC per standard deviation of NK cells corresponds to a 26% relative increase in risk for incident CAC per standard deviation higher NK cell proportion; this effect size of this association per SD of predictor is higher than that of C‐reactive protein with respect to CAC and comparable with that of C‐reactive protein with respect to cardiovascular events and death. 13 , 14 In longitudinal CAC progression analyses of individuals with non‐0 CAC at examination 1, intermediate monocytes were negatively associated with CAC progression over time among those with preexisting CAC, whereas nonclassical and classical monocytes were not associated with CAC incidence or progression. Tregs were negatively associated with CAC progression, whereas B cells were positively associated with CAC progression. Importantly, all analyses were considered exploratory, with a P<0.05 indicating possible significance. This was due to a lack of preexisting data on peripheral immune phenotypes and longitudinal changes in CAC to inform more cell subset‐specific prespecified hypotheses, as well as our sample size and naturally occurring variation in immune phenotypes between individuals. Together, these may have led to relatively wide CIs and less stable results than if our sample size was larger; however, this cohort was unique in having cryopreserved cells that were rigorously phenotyped via high‐parameter flow cytometry and serial CAC data from the same individuals over a decade. Although our primary findings are biologically plausible, as discussed next, they should be interpreted with caution given significance at an exploratory P<0.05 but not at a Bonferroni‐corrected significance threshold of P<0.0017.

Despite the exploratory nature of the analyses, 2 main findings were notable for their consistency with previous literature and possible biologic plausibility. These were the association of NK cells with incident CAC and the negative association of Tregs with CAC progression. Although the preponderance of data implicating NK cell activation and signaling in proatherogenesis derives from experimental (primarily mouse) models, small studies of human blood 15 and plaque 16 , 17 samples have been corroborative, demonstrating an association of circulating NK cells with atherosclerosis severity and of intraplaque NK cell–macrophage interactions potentially contributing to plaque destabilization. Our observed sex differences in the association of NK cells with incident CAC are interesting and hypothesis generating given recent experimental findings on sex differences in NK cell number and function. 18 The negative association of CD4+CD25+CD127− Tregs with CAC progression is likewise plausible: Tregs have been implicated in inflammatory modulation and self‐tolerance in atherosclerosis, whereas recent data suggest their conversion to inflammatory ex‐Tregs may disrupt self‐tolerance, thereby increasing inflammation and plaque destabilization. 19 , 20 Our finding thus provides a large‐scale human data‐related corollary for this hypothesized role of Tregs in atheroprotection, raising the possibility of circulating Tregs in human atheroprotection before the onset of overt clinical disease. Interestingly, the broader CD4+CD25+ subset, representing a combination of activated and regulatory CD4+ T cells, exhibited start divergence by sex in association with incident CAC, whereby these cells were strongly associated with incident CAC in men, whereas no positive association was observed in women. This highlights potentially interesting gender‐specific differences relevant to T‐cell function and atherosclerosis worth further exploration, particularly analyses investigating more precise and functionally distinct clusters within this broader CD4+CD25+ T‐cell subset.

Meanwhile, our monocyte findings with respect to presence and progression of CAC—in particular, the negative association of intermediate monocytes with CAC progression—do not parallel prior findings in MESA with respect to carotid intima‐media thickness. Specifically, prior findings observed intermediate monocytes to be associated with increased risk for stroke 3 and a recent cross‐sectional study using single‐cell transcriptomics and protein expression to analyze 61 individuals undergoing coronary angiography observed intermediate monocytes to be associated with increased coronary artery disease severity, as measured by Gensini score. 21 These distinctions warrant further evaluation; it is possible that they are explained by differences in atherosclerotic beds (eg, stroke‐relevant carotid atherosclerosis versus coronary atherosclerosis) 22 , 23 , 24 or distinctions in the phenotype of atherosclerosis measured (in this investigation, only calcific coronary atherosclerosis measured as CAC, rather than overall coronary plaque measured on angiography). Further study on systemic and vascular bed‐specific immune responses in humans is warranted, as this has the potential to inform on milieu‐specific pathophysiology of inflammatory plaque progression.

Other notable exploratory findings include T helper type cells associating negatively with incident CAC and B‐cell proportions associating with CAC progression. The finding that B‐cell proportions overall were associated with CAC progression is interesting given data on both potentially pathogenic and atheroprotective features of B cells in atherosclerosis, 25 underscored by discovery of several functionally unique B‐cell subsets and their diverse effects on vascular disease. 26 In this investigation, B cells were defined broadly by an overall lineage marker; thus, we lacked granularity to define specific associations of distinct B‐cell subsets with longitudinal changes in CAC.

There are additional limitations worth noting. These include the reliance on CAC, which represents an incomplete portion of overall coronary plaque; this was unavoidable, as serial data on noncalcified plaque were not available. We also had limited power due to sample size and naturally occurring interindividual variation in immune cell subsets, which precluded us from using a more stringent multiple comparisons correction in primary analyses. This also led us to present this investigation—to our knowledge, the first investigating associations of high‐parameter immune cell phenotypes with longitudinal changes in CAC—as exploratory, with the goal being to inform future hypotheses related to mechanistic and observational investigation. It is therefore important to interpret our findings cautiously given that they did not meet a more rigorous Bonferroni‐corrected P<0.0017. Given relatively wide confidence intervals from a limited sample size, we elected to report findings that met the exploratory P<0.05 rather than rejecting these as null, as we judged this to be scientifically informative for hypothesis generation given that, to our knowledge, no other cohorts with similar data on high‐parameter flow cytometry and longitudinal CAC changes over several years in nearly 1000 individuals are available. Importantly, given the exploratory and associative nature of this study and sparse prior data on immune cell subsets and changes over time in CAC, we were not able to rigorously incorporate causal frameworks predicting complex interrelationships between immune cell subsets, covariates, and the CAC‐related outcomes. We anticipate that the data presented here will help inform future studies more able to incorporate causal inference or causal experimental models. Overall, these limitations were unavoidable on the basis of the nature of the cohort and available data.

In conclusion, we observed that NK cells may be associated with incident CAC, whereas intermediate monocytes and Tregs may be associated negatively with CAC progression. These hypothesis‐generating findings, particularly the NK cell and Treg findings, provide rationale for experimental approaches and warrant replication in longitudinal human cohorts as data become available.
