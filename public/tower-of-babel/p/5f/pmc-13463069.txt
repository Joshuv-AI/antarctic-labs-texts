Programmable one-pot polymerase-mediated DNA synthesis via temperature control
Authors: Jinho Kim, Jangho Choi, Woojin Kim, Namjin Cho, Yushin Jung, Seongjun Park, Eunjin Choi, Chaerim Lee, Youngeun Kim, Taehoon Ryu, Hansol Choi, Yeongjae Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPolymerase-mediated DNA synthesis is fundamental to numerous biotechnology applications, but existing programmable synthesis methods depend on exchanging DNA building blocks, thereby increasing reagent use and complicating multistep workflows. Here, we introduce the TEmperature Mediated Primer Exchange Reaction (TEMPER), a programmable platform for arbitrary DNA synthesis that operates solely through temperature control without solution exchange. TEMPER uses hairpin DNA as temperature-responsive building blocks that define specific temperature range for DNA synthesis. The temperature range is determined by the length design of the hairpin, which regulates thermodynamic interactions between DNA molecules and allows selective and sequential DNA synthesis in one-pot. We validate its versatility by developing a DNA data storage writer, a colorimetric temperature indicator, and a temperature data logger, highlighting its broad potential in nanotechnology and biotechnology applications.Subject terms: DNA, DNA and RNA, Nanobiotechnology, Enzymes

Introduction

Polymerase-mediated DNA synthesis is essential across numerous areas of biotechnology research, including diagnosis1–8, synthetic biology9–13, biosensing14–16, and DNA nanotechnology17–26. Conventional methods such as polymerase chain reaction (PCR)27 and strand displacement amplification28 replicate existing DNA sequences using DNA polymerases, but lack the ability to synthesize programmable DNA. Recently, several programmable polymerase-mediated DNA synthesis methods have been developed. These include repeated replication of a DNA fragment consisting of several nucleotides using circular DNA building blocks1,29 and sequential concatenation of DNA fragments using hairpin DNA building blocks30. Additionally, a single type of nucleotide serves as a building block, such as in methods employing terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase17,18,31.

Despite these advances, current programmable DNA synthesis methods can generate only a single predetermined DNA sequence or nucleotide in a one-pot reaction. The synthesis of arbitrary sequences still depends on concatenating predetermined fragments through successive building block exchanges. This limitation arises because DNA polymerases cannot distinguish the identities of building blocks, making one-pot programmability unfeasible. Consequently, even with improvements in building block design10,11,30 and enzyme engineering31, programmability remains constrained by the need for building block exchange. This dependence increases reagent consumption and complicates reaction mechanisms as synthesis complexity grows, underscoring the need for alternative approaches.

Here, we present the TEmperature Mediated Primer Exchange Reaction (TEMPER), a programmable one-pot DNA synthesis platform that enables synthesis of arbitrary DNA sequences through temperature control. Temperature, which directly influences thermodynamic interactions between DNA molecules and DNA polymerase activity, has been conventionally used to turn DNA synthesis mechanisms on and off, but we leverage the temperature to enhance programmability in polymerase-mediated DNA synthesis. TEMPER regulates the optimal reaction temperature range for DNA synthesis based on a primer exchange reaction30, in which a hairpin DNA serves as a reusable building block for primer elongation. In TEMPER, we design hairpin DNA as a temperature-responsive building block that functions only within specific temperature ranges. These ranges are controlled by tuning hairpin length and sequence, which regulates thermodynamic interactions between DNA molecules. When each hairpin functions within a distinct temperature range, the primers can selectively synthesize DNA with hairpins whose optimal reaction temperature range matches the incubation temperature, even in a solution containing multiple hairpins. Moreover, TEMPER is designed such that the elongated products serve as primers for subsequent synthesis steps, enabling sequential synthesis and thereby achieving programmable DNA synthesis through temperature changes.

To demonstrate the versatility of TEMPER, we experimentally implement it across several applications. First, we develop a DNA data storage writer that synthesizes binary data-encoded DNA through a controlled temperature stage. Second, we implement a colorimetric temperature indicator that allows naked-eye detection of temperature changes by visualizing DNA synthesis using TEMPER. Moreover, we develop a temperature data logger capable of recording the temperature, time, and order of temperature changes. To the best of our knowledge, TEMPER is a unique approach for achieving programmable one-pot DNA synthesis through temperature control.

Results

Selective DNA synthesis through temperature control in TEMPER

TEMPER enables diverse DNA sequence synthesis in one-pot through temperature control, eliminating the need to exchange DNA building blocks (Fig. 1a). It uses hairpin DNA that functions only with its optimal reaction temperature range. The hairpin is designed to operate within programmed temperature range, where synthesis is inhibited by either a failure of primer hybridization at high temperatures or a failure of product dissociation at low temperatures. This temperature-dependent selectivity is governed by the thermodynamics of the hairpin structure. The range is determined by the length and sequence of the primer hybridization site and the product dissociation site within the hairpin. The primer hybridization site corresponds to the site complementary to the primer within the hairpin, and the product dissociation site corresponds to the overhang of the hairpin complementary to the product. Assuming similar GC contents, shorter primer hybridization and product dissociation sites require less energy for base pairing, resulting in DNA synthesis at lower temperatures. Conversely, longer sites raise the optimal reaction temperature, preventing synthesis at lower temperatures. Thus, hairpins with distinct optimal reaction temperature ranges can be designed, and each hairpin can operate independently even in the presence of multiple hairpins within a one-pot.

Previously, primer exchange reactions30 have used hairpin DNA to programmably synthesize DNA by concatenating DNA fragments in one-pot. However, no prior studies have demonstrated arbitrary DNA synthesis without building-block exchange. TEMPER achieves arbitrary DNA synthesis in one-pot by utilizing temperature as a regulatory factor. The TEMPER mechanism begins with the hybridization of the primer and hairpin at the primer hybridization site, which is designed to be fully complementary to the primer along its entire length (Fig. 1b). A longer primer hybridization site allows for stable hybridization at higher temperatures, raising the upper bound of the optimal reaction temperature range when the GC contents are similar. Once hybridized, strand-displacing DNA polymerase elongates the primer along the stem of the hairpin until it reaches the stop sequence, concatenating the hairpin stem sequence with the primer. The stop sequence is a DNA sequence intended to terminate DNA polymerase-driven synthesis30 (Supplementary Note 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1). The synthesized product then undergoes random walk via three-way branch migration, competing with the complementary site of the hairpin. After the hairpin stem is fully closed, the shorter product dissociation site allows for facile dissociation at lower temperatures, thereby lowering the lower bound of the optimal reaction temperature range. If the hairpin fails to dissociate from the product, the subsequent DNA synthesis cannot be initiated. In our design, we maintained the primer concentration at least tenfold higher than that of the hairpins to ensure that the reaction state is strictly determined by temperature. Within the optimal temperature range, a single hairpin can undergo multiple reaction cycles until the excess primers are depleted. Conversely, at temperatures below the optimal range, the hairpins remain locked with the synthesized products and cannot access new primers, effectively halting further synthesis. Based on this mechanism, the DNA synthesis yield of TEMPER is influenced by the primer hybridization yield, product dissociation yield, polymerase activity, strand displacement yield during branch migration, and competitive hybridization of primers and products to the hairpin (Fig. 1c). Primer hybridization and product dissociation yields are calculated from the free energies determined by their lengths and sequences. Temperature-dependent DNA polymerase activity is fitted based on previously reported DNA polymerase activity data32. By integrating these variables, the DNA synthesis yield of hairpins as a function of temperature can be estimated, enabling the prediction of an optimal reaction temperature range based on the hairpin design.

Even with multiple hairpins coexisting in one-pot, TEMPER enables selective DNA synthesis by utilizing only the hairpin whose optimal reaction temperature matches the incubation temperature (Fig. 1d). To demonstrate this, primers were incubated with three distinct hairpins of varying lengths of primer hybridization and product dissociation sites. Each hairpin was designed with primer hybridization sites of 7, 9, and 16 nt paired with product dissociation sites of 7, 10, and 24 nt. These designs resulted in optimal reaction temperatures of 18, 46, and 64 °C. After incubation at different temperatures, the products were analyzed using complementary fluorescent probes labeled with FAM (18 °C), Cy5 (46 °C), and IRDye 800CWN (64 °C), respectively (Fig. 1e). A single fluorescent band corresponding to each hairpin was observed at the optimal temperature. Although all product DNA strands and probes were designed with identical sequence lengths of 34 and 10 nt, respectively, the bands exhibited slight variations in migration height. These mobility shifts were attributed to the distinct physicochemical properties of the fluorophores labeled on the probes (Supplementary Fig. 2). These results demonstrated that temperature-dependent selective DNA synthesis was successfully achieved in a one-pot reaction.

Experimental and modeling-based validation of the TEMPER design mechanism

To validate the TEMPER design mechanism, we investigated temperature-dependent DNA synthesis yield by varying the lengths of the primer hybridization and product dissociation sites. For both experiments and modeling, hairpins were denoted as “H X/Y” according to the lengths of the primer hybridization site (X nt) and product dissociation site (Y nt), respectively, while the primer (P1) and elongation site lengths were fixed at 20 and 10 nt, respectively (Fig. 2a).

TEMPER was performed using hairpins with hybridization site lengths ranging from 7 to 16 nt in 1 nt increments and dissociation site lengths ranging from 1 to 23 nt in 2 nt increments, and the DNA synthesis yield was compared with respect to the lengths of both sites (Supplementary Fig. 3). The yield was quantified via PAGE analysis based on the band intensity ratio of the synthesized product to the input primer and visualized as a heatmap across various temperatures for each hairpin. When the length of the product dissociation site was 5 nt or shorter, the primer hybridization site was embedded within the hairpin stem, thereby preventing the initiation of DNA synthesis. Among the cases with product dissociation sites 7 nt or longer, we selected two representative cases for comparison. First, the primer hybridization site length was varied while the product dissociation site was fixed at 7 nt, and second, the product dissociation site length was varied while the primer hybridization site was fixed at 11 nt (Fig. 2b). When the product dissociation site was fixed, longer primer hybridization sites enabled DNA synthesis within a higher temperature range, whereas shorter sites lowered the upper bound. Conversely, when the primer hybridization site was fixed, shorter product dissociation sites enabled synthesis within a lower temperature range, whereas longer sites raised the lower bound. By simultaneously adjusting the lengths of both sites, we identified five hairpins H 7/7, H 7/9, H 8/11, H 9/15, and H 16/23 that exhibited optimal reaction temperature ranges from 20 to 60 °C in 10 °C increments (Fig. 2c, d). These results demonstrate that the optimal reaction temperature range for DNA synthesis can be tuned by modifying the lengths of primer hybridization and product dissociation sites.

The temperature-dependent DNA synthesis yield of the hairpins was modeled by multiplying the primer hybridization yield, product dissociation yield, and DNA polymerase activity, which varied with temperature. The nearest-neighbor model predicted the hybridization and dissociation yields as equilibrium binding probabilities across different temperatures (Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5). The temperature-dependent activity of DNA polymerase32 was fitted using the Sharpe-Schoolfield model33–36 across a temperature range from 15 to 85 °C (Supplementary Note 2 and Supplementary Fig. 6). The DNA synthesis yield was modeled across primer hybridization sites ranging from 7 to 16 nt and product dissociation sites ranging from 7 to 25 nt, revealing that an increase in either site length led to a higher optimal reaction temperature range (Supplementary Fig. 7). For hairpin H 9/15, the modeled optimal reaction temperature was 52 °C, which closely matched the experimentally observed value of 50 °C (Fig. 2e). Furthermore, the same model was applied to hairpins H 7/7, H 7/9, H 8/11, and H 16/23, and the modeled DNA synthesis yields were compared with the experimental data as a function of temperature (Fig. 2f). The modeled optimal reaction temperatures for each hairpin were 15, 18, 35, and 59 °C, respectively. The modeled DNA synthesis yield also showed an optimal reaction temperature range within a similar temperature range to the experimental data, measured in 10 °C increments. The slight difference in the optimal reaction temperature between the experimental and modeling data may arise from the omission of strand displacement efficiency and competitive hybridization of the synthesized products and primers with the hairpin. Furthermore, we demonstrated the robustness of DNA polymerase activity fitting by comparing it with simpler models (Supplementary Note 2 and Supplementary Figs. 8 and 9). The modeling and experimental results validated the underlying thermodynamic principles of TEMPER based on DNA hybridization, dissociation, and polymerase activity profiles. The ability to adjust the lower and upper bounds of the optimal reaction temperature range by tuning the length and sequence of hairpins demonstrates the programmable and modular nature of the TEMPER system.

Programmable one-pot DNA data storage writer using TEMPER

TEMPER enables DNA synthesis by concatenating multiple DNA fragments in a sequence defined by successive temperature stages, which can be applied to binary data encoding (Fig. 3a). To demonstrate this concept, we used three hairpins with distinct optimal reaction temperature ranges. The “low hairpin” and “high hairpin,” with optimal reaction temperatures of 18 and 64 °C, respectively, synthesized the “0” and “1” sequences. The “mid hairpin,” with optimal reaction temperature range at 43 °C, synthesizes the connector sequence, enabling linking of each character and preventing repeated synthesis of the same character during a prolonged incubation at either 18 or 64 °C. The mid hairpin should be hybridized with both the “0” and “1” sequences to concatenate the connector sequence, but two sequences need to be distinguishable while maintaining sequence similarity. To achieve this, the “1” sequence was designed by adding an additional 2 nt to the 5′ end of the “0” sequence. One temperature stage consists of 18 °C followed by 43 or 64 °C followed by 43 °C, and the stepwise synthesis of arbitrary n-bit data is enabled through combinatorial repetition of these stages.

To demonstrate this, a 10-bit data-encoded DNA was synthesized and assessed. The binary data “1010101010”-encoded DNA was synthesized through 10 temperature stages (Fig. 3b). DNA synthesis at each temperature step was analyzed by PAGE (Fig. 3c). Distinct major bands progressively shifted upward with each step, suggesting the successful stepwise synthesis of binary data-encoded DNA using temperature changes. The ratios of the target binary data were analyzed at each stage using next-generation sequencing (NGS) (Fig. 3d). In the first stage, 99.92% of the reads matched the target binary data, whereas 73.25 and 61.70% of the reads matched the target data after 8 and 10 stages, respectively. The overall stepwise efficiency was calculated to be 96.45% through exponential fitting in MATLAB, demonstrating the successful construction of the 10-bit data-encoded DNA.

To evaluate the feasibility of TEMPER as a DNA data storage writer, we encoded and synthesized the 8-character text “NDS_GIST” into DNA (Fig. 3e). Unlike conventional DNA synthesis methods, TEMPER-based DNA data storage operates in a one-pot reaction without building block exchange. As it relies solely on temperature control, DNA can be synthesized using standard thermal cyclers without specialized equipment. This simplicity not only improves experimental accessibility and automation potential but also enables the simple and scalable writing of data into DNA. The text “NDS_GIST” was encoded into 8-bit data for each character using a predefined encoding table (Supplementary Table 1). The 8-bit data comprised two segments, with the first three bits encoding the position data of the character and the remaining five bits encoding the type of character. These binary data were then translated into a combinatorial repetition of the temperature stage for TEMPER. Data stored in the form of DNA can be preserved over long periods without additional energy consumption, owing to its inherent molecular stability37–40.

“NDS_GIST”-encoded DNA was synthesized using a TEMPER-based DNA data storage writer, and its decoding was analyzed using NGS (Fig. 3f). The decoded texts were determined from the decoded binary data that matched in more than half of the reads, successfully reconstructing the original text “NDS_GIST.” For all characters, the average ratio of the target binary data was 74.13%.

For a detailed analysis, the reads were divided into eight segments corresponding to each bit, and the decoding accuracy of each segment was compared across all characters (Fig. 3g). The decoding accuracy in the first stage was 98.97% with a standard deviation of 1.78%. Although accuracy slightly decreased in subsequent stages, it remained consistent at an average of 91.10% with no significant differences among them. Even as a one-pot reaction, TEMPER preserved the per-stage decoding accuracy across multiple stages, maintaining reliable temperature-dependent reaction. The decoding accuracy was further assessed to verify whether the binary data of the previous stage affected the synthesis in the next stage (Fig. 3h). When synthesizing the same binary data as the previous stage, such as “0 to 0” or “1 to 1”, the decoding accuracy remained high at 97.34 and 94.66%. However, when synthesizing a different binary data from the previous stage, such as “0 to 1” or “1 to 0,” decoding accuracy decreased to 87.12 and 86.92%. Although accuracy differed between repeat and transition states, no significant variation existed regarding specific bit types or sequence orders. This confirms that there is no directional bias in miscalling 0 as 1 or 1 as 0. This decrease was attributed to unintended mid hairpin activity outside the optimal reaction temperature range, measured at 6.58% at 18 °C and 3.69% at 64 °C (Supplementary Fig. 10). The mid hairpin was likely involved in DNA synthesis simultaneously with the low hairpin at 18 °C and with the high hairpin at 64 °C, which could have led to duplication of the same character within a single temperature incubation. Through these error analyses, we confirmed that while synthesis accuracy is influenced by the binary data of the previous stage, there is no inherent bias toward the number of stages or the specific character being recorded.

While we have selected the target only if its abundance was higher than 50% for DNA data storage writer application, the decodable length limit could be extended by selecting the most abundant sequences even when the target ratio falls below 50%. Based on the fitted stepwise efficiency of 96.45%, the theoretical ratio of the target binary data is 52.17% at 18-bit. Rather than requiring an absolute majority, the results suggest that decoding may remain feasible as long as the target binary data is the highest ratio. The stepwise error increase rate was fitted to 0.78% based on NGS data (Supplementary Fig. 11). These results suggest that the target binary data could maintain a higher ratio than individual error sequences even beyond 20-bit, suggesting the possibility of a decoding limit exceeding 20-bit.

Through further characterization, we demonstrated the potential to maximize the synthesis yield contrast between target and off-target temperatures by optimizing the hairpin concentration (Supplementary Note 3). Since the rate constant for DNA synthesis varies significantly depending on whether the temperature is within or outside the optimal reaction range, the yield differences among the three temperatures could be regulated by adjusting the hairpin concentration. By conducting TEMPER across various concentrations and temperatures, we fitted the rate constants to be 2.00 × 103, 1.04 × 105, and 2.10 × 103 M−1 s−1 for the mid hairpin at 18, 43, and 64 °C (Supplementary Fig. 12a, b). While the high rate constant at 43 °C causes the synthesis yield to saturate rapidly, the yields at 18 and 64 °C continue to rise steadily as the hairpin concentration increases. It allows the yield contrast between the target and off-target temperatures to be tuned by varying the hairpin concentration (Supplementary Fig. 12c). These results suggest that optimizing the concentration could enhance data integrity by sharpening the temperature range and mitigating duplication issues. Furthermore, we explored the temporal efficiency of TEMPER by reducing the incubation time per stage from 60 min to 30 and 15 min. For an 8-bit “10101010” sequence, the ratio of the target binary data remained 71.04% at 30 min, but dropped to 3.96% at 15 min (Supplementary Fig. 13). It suggested that the synthesis time per each temperature stage can be reduced to 30 min without compromising fidelity, but 15 min of incubation does not yield a sufficient amount of DNA for data storage. Despite the fast catalytic speed of the DNA polymerase, it indicates that synthesis speed is governed by the time required for hybridization, strand displacement, and dissociation.

To characterize the decoding performance of the TEMPER-based DNA data storage, we investigated the minimum NGS depth required for data decoding (Fig. 3i). After sequencing the DNA corresponding to the 8-character text with coverage higher than 500×, we performed downsampling in silico to simulate reduced depth and compared the average read counts and standard deviation between the target sequence and the predominant error. The target sequence remained dominant across all characters with a sequencing depth as low as 100×. The successful synthesis of arbitrary 8-bit data-encoded DNA demonstrates the potential of TEMPER as a programmable, one-pot, temperature-controlled DNA synthesis platform.

TEMPER-based temperature monitoring device with colorimetric temperature indicator and temperature data logger

To explore the feasibility of TEMPER in temperature monitoring applications, we used TEMPER to indicate exposure above a defined temperature and record the temperature histories of the samples for potential use in cold chain logistics (Fig. 4a). Cold chain logistics refers to the temperature-controlled storage and delivery of temperature-sensitive samples. For example, the Moderna COVID-19 vaccines lose efficacy after 6 to 12 h of cumulative room temperature exposure, highlighting the unmet need for the technologies that can track accumulated long durations41.

The colorimetric temperature indicator is a system in which DNA synthesis induces a color change, allowing the direct detection of out-of-range temperature exposure by the naked eye for a direct and intuitive visual readout (Fig. 4b). The indicator contains primers, hairpin DNA with a distinct optimal reaction temperature range, and colorimetric dyes42–45 that are responsive to the byproducts of DNA synthesis. The lengths of the primer hybridization sites in the hairpins were fixed at 14 nt, thereby setting a constant upper bound for the optimal reaction temperature range. In contrast, the lengths of the product dissociation site of the hairpins are 10, 15, and 17 nt, allowing the lower bound of the optimal reaction temperature range for each hairpin to be tuned at 30, 40, and 50 °C, respectively. When the indicator is incubated above the defined temperature, tandem repeat DNA is continuously synthesized, accompanied by the generation of magnesium pyrophosphate (Mg₂P₂O₇)42–44 and hydrogen ions (H⁺)45. These changes trigger a color transition of the reaction solution from brown to green, which is mediated by dyes such as hydroxynaphthol blue (HNB) and cresol red.

To validate the performance of the indicators, three distinct indicators, each designed to detect temperatures at or above 30, 40, and 50 °C, respectively, were incubated under different temperature conditions ranging from 20 to 50 °C in 10 °C increments. To achieve a perceptible color change within a short time, a higher concentration of DNA was used in the indicator than in previous experiments (see “Methods” for details). Color changes were observed within 2 h only above the defined temperature, whereas the initial color remained after 6 h of incubation below the defined temperature (Fig. 4c). The progression of color change was further quantified by analyzing the color difference using CIEDE200046–48 at 1 h intervals during the 6 h incubation (Fig. 4d). The color change was defined by thresholds ΔE₀₀ values ranging from 12 to 14, with slight differences depending on hairpin type. These threshold differences are likely attributable to variations in the DNA synthesis yield arising from differences in the dissociation yield depending on the length of the product dissociation site. For all the indicators, the color difference rapidly increased and exceeded the threshold within 2 h when the incubation temperature exceeded the defined temperature. The results show that the indicators can detect even short-term temperature exposure lasting only 2 h during cold-chain logistics, while ensuring stability under room temperature conditions. Furthermore, because the optimal reaction temperature range can be tuned through a hairpin design, this strategy suggests the feasibility of developing low-cost temperature indicators capable of detecting diverse and tunable temperature ranges.

In addition to the indicator that detects a single temperature change, we developed a TEMPER-based temperature data logger that continuously records multiple temperature changes as a repetitive DNA sequence using multiple hairpins. The repetitive DNA sequence was then decoded using NGS to infer the temperature, time, and order of the temperature changes. Repetitive DNA consists of a series of sequentially connected “recording units.” Each recording unit is composed of either a low temperature sequence and connector sequence, or high temperature sequence and connector sequence. At 20 °C, recording units containing the low temperature sequence were synthesized, whereas at 60 °C, those with the high temperature sequence were synthesized (Fig. 4e). Contrastingly, at intermediate temperatures, either low or high temperature sequences can be randomly synthesized. When the incubation was prolonged, the same type of recording unit was repeatedly concatenated to synthesize repetitive DNA. By comparing the ratios of low and high temperature sequences in repetitive DNA at corresponding recording units, temperatures ranging from 20 to 60 °C can be distinguished. To enable a clear discrimination of temperature differences, the concentrations of hairpins were optimized to successfully infer the ambient temperature (see “Methods” for details). Therefore, the timing of temperature changes can be inferred by analyzing the sequential variation in the ratio of recording units from the 5′ end of DNA.

Five hairpins were used to construct a TEMPER-based temperature data logger, enabling continuous concatenation of high or low temperature sequences via the connector sequence. Two hairpins function optimally at 20 °C, with “low hairpin,” which concatenates a 10 nt low temperature sequence after a 10 nt connector sequence, and the “low connector hairpin,” which concatenates the connector sequence after the low temperature sequence. Three hairpins function optimally at 60 °C, with “low-to-high hairpin” and “high-to-high hairpin” that concatenate a 10 nt high temperature sequence after the connector sequence, and “high connector hairpin” that concatenates the connector sequence after the high temperature sequence. Unlike the low hairpins, which have a 7 nt primer hybridization site, the high hairpins have a 16 nt primer hybridization site that is longer than the 10 nt connector sequence. Therefore, low-to-high and high-to-high hairpins were designed to include a 6 nt portion of the low or high temperature sequences, respectively, within their primer hybridization sites.

Accordingly, we incubated reactions from 20 to 60 °C in 10 °C increments for 12 h to verify whether the logger could discriminate temperature differences at 10 °C intervals (Fig. 4f). After DNA synthesis, repetitive DNA containing eight recording units was extracted via PAGE purification and analyzed by NGS. The ratios of high temperature sequences at 20, 30, 40, 50, and 60 °C were 2.97, 8.00, 47.74, 87.65, and 94.21%, respectively. These results indicate that the relative contributions of low and high hairpins vary with temperature, enabling discrimination of temperature ranging from 20 to 60 °C.

Next, we evaluated whether the logger could resolve differences in incubation time when the temperature was changed from 20 °C to either 60 or 40 °C. Within a total reaction time of 12 h, the incubation time at 20 °C was varied in 2 h intervals from 0 to 12 h (Fig. 4g). NGS results showed that seven distinct incubation times could be distinguished under both temperature conditions. Additionally, the ratios of high temperature sequence exceeded 93.02% at 60 °C and ranged from 40.14 to 84.74% at 40 °C, with an average of 68.70, confirming that the logger can reliably differentiate temperature differences.

Finally, three case studies were conducted to validate whether the logger could detect diverse temperature changes and record their histories in DNA (Fig. 4h). In each case, the synthesized DNA was analyzed based on the sequence ratio at the corresponding positions of the recording units. The change in sequence ratio across recording units from the 5′ end reflected temperature changes in each case. The analysis revealed that the ratio of the high temperature sequence exceeded 80.09% after exposure to 60 °C, remained between 37.26% and 48.56% after exposure to 40 °C, and decreased to below 24.21% after exposure to 20 °C. This highlights the significance of TEMPER as a low-cost, compact, and convenient temperature data logger that can record multiple temperature changes without requiring additional energy input.

Discussion

In this study, we propose TEMPER, a programmable one-pot polymerase-based DNA synthesis method that operates solely through temperature control. This approach established for the first time that temperature is a key factor governing the programmability of DNA synthesis without requiring DNA building block exchange. Even when multiple hairpins with distinct optimal reaction temperature ranges were mixed, the desired DNA products could be selectively synthesized. TEMPER enables superior programmability by solely modulating temperature within a fixed set of hairpins, whereas most conventional polymerase-mediated methods are constrained by the necessity of constant template redesign for every arbitrary target sequence30. Furthermore, the reliance on a standard thermal cycler enhances both the scalability and accessibility of the platform, in contrast to alternative de novo synthesis approaches that necessitate specialized and custom-built instruments17,18.

By controlling the temperature, we successfully encoded 8-bit data into DNA. The primary value of TEMPER-based data storage writer lies in its one-pot operational simplicity, as the entire synthesis process is performed using only a standard thermal cycler without any solution changes or washing steps (Supplementary Table 2)17,18,49–52. Compared to conventional writing methods, TEMPER utilizes a smaller number of reagents and eliminates the need for specialized modifications. While its writing speed, bit density, and bit error rate are currently lower than those of some established methods, the streamlined reaction system enhances accessibility and scalability. This allows for immediate implementation in unique applications such as temperature indicator and logger that extend beyond data storage writer.

TEMPER-based colorimetric temperature indicators and temperature data loggers demonstrated the ability to distinguish temperature change ranging from 20 to 60 °C. By integrating the visual threshold detection of chemical indicators and temperature change logging of electronic devices, both of which represent the current standards in cold chain logistics, TEMPER provides a power-free, scalable, and economical platform for less than $1 in a compact size (Supplementary Table 3). Furthermore, TEMPER offers advantages over previous nucleic acid-based temperature indicators by enabling broad range temperature sensing and immediate visual readout without specialized equipment (Supplementary Table 4)53–55. As the first DNA-based platform to achieve practical temperature logging, TEMPER delivers a versatile and scalable solution for modern cold chain logistics.

More complex and scalable programmable DNA synthesis can be achieved on a microchip by enabling parallel multiplex DNA synthesis at each step, by coupling TEMPER with spatial temperature control. To enhance the programmability and encoding capacity of TEMPER, expanding the number of orthogonal temperature channels is essential by narrowing the temperature range of each hairpin. Optimizing experimental parameters, including hairpin concentration and sequence compositions, such as GC content and strategic mismatches56, could potentially allow for narrowing of the temperature range. Furthermore, refining molecular modeling with accurate enzyme activity data and accounting for competition between primers and products will enhance predictability. Integrating these with DNA thermodynamics could then enable more reliable optimization of reaction conditions for each hairpin design. These enhancements will enable advanced applications, including ternary and higher-order DNA data storage and temperature monitoring systems with diverse operating ranges. Beyond molecular level optimizations, the integration of robust error correction codes commonly utilized in DNA data storage11,20,57 would ensure high data integrity and effectively address yield reduction and errors to ensure accurate reconstruction. These advancements represent the technical scalability and the strategic research direction of the TEMPER.

Methods

Oligonucleotide preparation

The oligonucleotide sequences used in all experiments are listed in Supplementary Data 1. Hairpins, primers, and FAM- or Cy5-modified oligonucleotides were synthesized and purified by Macrogen using MOPC or PAGE purification. IRDye 800CWN-modified oligonucleotides were synthesized by Integrated DNA Technologies and purified using high-performance liquid chromatography. All oligonucleotides were pre-suspended in nuclease-free water at a concentration of 100 µM and further diluted to working concentrations. Stock solutions were stored at −20 °C.

Universal protocol for TEMPER experiment

The protocol for TEMPER was established by referencing concentration guidelines from previous studies on primer exchange reactions30. In addition, initial concentrations were increased while maintaining the established molar ratios to ensure clear PAGE visualization and offset low PAGE extraction recovery for the products synthesized through multiple synthesis steps. The concentration ratios of the hairpins were adjusted according to Supplementary Note 3. Depending on the experimental design, one or more types of hairpin DNA with final concentration ranging from 10 to 100 nM were mixed with 1 µL of 10× ThermoPol buffer (200 mM Tris-HCl, 100 mM (NH₄)₂SO₄, 100 mM KCl, 20 mM MgSO₄, 1% Triton X-100, pH 8.8 at 25 °C; New England Biolabs, Cat. No. B9004S), 1 µL of 100 mM MgSO₄ (New England Biolabs, Cat. No. B1003S), and nuclease-free water to a final volume of 6 µL. The mixture was heated to 95 °C for 3 min, and gradually cooled to 4 °C at a rate of 1 °C per 30 s for hairpin structure formation. Subsequently, 1 µL of 1 mM dATP/dTTP/dCTP mixture, 1 µL of 1 µM Cleanup G hairpin DNA (Supplementary Note 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1), 1 µL of 8000 units/mL Bst DNA polymerase large fragment (New England Biolabs, Cat. No. M0275S), and 1 µL of 10 µM primer was added to the mixture to reach a total reaction volume of 10 µL. The primer sequence varies depending on the experiment. The mixture was incubated in a thermal cycler (Biorad, Cat. No. C1000) at the temperature conditions defined in the experimental design. After incubation, polymerase was heat-inactivated at 80 °C for 20 min.

Selective one-pot DNA synthesis and fluorescent probe labeling

The universal TEMPER protocol was performed using 1 µL of 1 µM Low Hairpin-1 (final concentration: 100 nM), 0.2 µL of 1 µM Mid Hairpin-1 (final concentration: 20 nM), and 1 µL 1 µM of High Hairpin-1 (final concentration: 100 nM) with 1 µL of 10 µM Primer-1 (final concentration: 1 µM). Reactions were incubated at 18, 46, and 64 °C for 2 h. Subsequently, 2 µL each of 10 µM Low FAM Probe, 10 µM Mid Cy5 Probe, and 10 µM High IRDye 800CWN Probe were added. The mixture was then heated to 95 °C for 3 min and gradually cooled to 4 °C at rate of 1 °C per 30 s to allow probe hybridization.

Experimental validation of hairpin design mechanism in TEMPER

The universal TEMPER protocol was performed using 1 µL of each 1 µM hairpins (final concentration: 100 nM), with primer hybridization site lengths ranging from 7 to 16 nt and product dissociation sites ranging from 1 to 23 nt, combined with 1 µL of 10 µM Primer-2 (final concentration: 1 µM). Reactions were incubated from 20 to 60 °C for 1 h.

Synthesis of binary data-encoded DNA

The universal TEMPER protocol was performed using 1 µL of 1 µM Low Hairpin-3 (final concentration: 100 nM), 0.2 µL of 1 µM Mid Hairpin-3 (final concentration: 20 nM), and 1 µL of 1 µM High Hairpin-3 (final concentration: 100 nM) with 1 µL of 10 µM Cy5 Primer-3 or Primer-3 (final concentration: 1 µM). Incubation followed the temperature stage protocol corresponding to the target binary data.

Colorimetric detection of DNA synthesis

The reaction mixture, containing 3.5 μL of 200 μM indicator hairpins (final concentration: 25.5 μM), 2.75 µL of 10× homemade ThermoPol buffer (100 mM (NH₄)₂SO₄, 100 mM KCl, 20 mM MgSO₄, 1% Triton® X-100, and pH 8.8 adjusted with 8 M KOH), and 2.75 µL of 100 mM MgSO₄, was heated to 95 °C for 3 min and gradually cooled to 4 °C at a rate of 1 °C per 30 s. Depending on the indicator type, either the 30 °C indicator hairpins-1 and -2, the 40 °C indicator hairpins-1 and -2, or the 50 °C indicator hairpins-1 and -2 were selected. The hairpin mixture was combined with 1 µL of 10 mM dATP/dTTP/dCTP mixture, 5.5 µL of 8000 units/mL Bst DNA polymerase large fragment, 3.5 µL of 200 μM each of primer-4-1 and primer-4-2 (final concentration: 25.5 μM), 0.5 µL of 2.418 mM HNB (DOJINDO, Cat. No. H007-10), and 1 µL of 3.71 mM cresol red sodium salt (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No.114480) were added to the reaction mixture. The reactions were incubated at 20, 30, 40, and 50 °C, and color changes were observed every hour for 6 h.

Synthesis of repetitive DNA in temperature data logger

The universal TEMPER protocol was performed using 0.1 µL of 10 µM Low Hairpin-5 (final concentration: 100 nM), 0.1 µL of 10 µM Low Connector Hairpin-5 (final concentration: 100 nM), 1 µL of 100 nM Low-to-High Hairpin-5 (final concentration: 10 nM), 1 µL of 100 nM High-to-High Hairpin-5 (final concentration: 10 nM), and 1 µL of 100 nM High Connector Hairpin-5 (final concentration: 10 nM) with 1 µL of 10 µM Primer-5 (final concentration: 1 µM). Reactions were incubated following the temperature change protocol defined by the experimental design.

PAGE analysis and extraction

Double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) reaction products were analyzed on 8% native polyacrylamide gel containing 1× TAE at 4 °C and 150 V for 90 min with SE 260 Mighty Small II Electrophoresis Unit (Hoefer, Cat. No. SE260-10A-.75) and a 300 V power supply (Hoefer, Cat. No. PS300B). The single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) reaction products were analyzed by electrophoresis on 8% denaturing polyacrylamide gel containing 1× TAE and 7 M urea, run at 50 °C and 200 V for 30 min. Gels were stained with SYBR Gold (Thermo Scientific, Cat. No. S11494) for 10 min and subsequently imaged using an Invitrogen iBright FL1500 Imaging System (Thermo Scientific, Cat. No. A44115). The FAM-, Cy5-, and IRDye 800CWN-modified products were imaged using the Cy3 (Ex/Em = 554/566 nm), Cy5 (Ex/Em = 647/665 nm), and IRDye 800CWN (Ex/Em = 767/791 nm) channels. Band intensities were quantified using a GelAnalyzer 23.1 to evaluate the TEMPER yield.

The DNA band of interest was excised from the gel by using an LED transilluminator (Korea Ace Scientific, KA.33-61). The excised gel slice was crushed and incubated in the TE buffer at 45 °C overnight with gentle agitation to promote DNA diffusion. The eluted DNA was precipitated with ethanol in the presence of 0.3 M sodium acetate, washed with 70% ethanol, air-dried, and resuspended in nuclease-free water.

NGS library preparation and sequencing

The reaction mixture contained 5 µL of extracted DNA product, 1 µL of 10 µM TruSeq R2 adapter primer, 1 µL of 10× ThermoPol buffer, and 1 µL of 100 mM MgSO₄. The mixture was heated to 95 °C for 3 min and then gradually cooled to 4 °C at a rate of 1 °C per 30 s. Subsequently, 1 µL of 1 mM dNTP and 1 µL of 8000 units/mL Bst DNA polymerase large fragment were added to the reaction mixture. The reaction was incubated at 60 °C for 1 h, followed by enzyme inactivation at 80 °C for 20 min. The product was purified using AMPure XP Beads for DNA Cleanup (Beckman Coulter, Inc., Cat. No. A63881), according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed to determine the cycle threshold (Ct) value prior to amplification. The reaction mixture contained 10 µL of Luna Universal qPCR Master Mix (New England Biolabs, Cat. No. M3003S), 0.5 µL each of TruSeq R1 and R2 primers, 1 µL of DNA sample, and 8 µL of nuclease-free water. qPCR was carried out on a CFX Connect Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, Cat. No. 1855200) under the following thermal cycling conditions: (1) initial denaturation at 95 °C for 60 s, followed by 40 cycles of (2) denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s and (3) extension at 60 °C for 30 s.

Samples were amplified using AccuPrime Taq DNA Polymerase High Fidelity (Thermo Scientific, Cat. No. 12339016) at the saturation cycle threshold as determined by qPCR. The reaction mixture included 2 µL of 10× AccuPrime™ PCR Buffer I, 0.08 µL of AccuPrime™ Taq DNA Polymerase, 0.5 µL each of TruSeq R1 and R2 primers, 1 µL of DNA sample, and 15.92 µL of nuclease-free water. PCR was carried out under the following thermal profile: (1) initial denaturation at 94 °C for 15 s, followed by repeated cycles of (2) denaturation at 94 °C for 15 s, (3) annealing at 55 °C for 15 s, and (4) extension at 68 °C for 30 s, according to the saturation cycle number. The final extension step was performed at 68 °C for 5 min.

To isolate DNA fragments of the desired size and remove nonspecific products and residual PCR components, 2% agarose gel electrophoresis was performed at 150 V for 1.5 h using a Horizontal Electrophoresis Kit (Biofact, Cat. No. GSA-FT) and a 300 V power supply. The gel was stained with the SYBR™ Safe DNA Gel Stain (Thermo Scientific, Cat. No. S33102), and DNA bands of the expected size were excised and purified using a Monarch Spin DNA Gel Extraction Kit (New England Biolabs, Cat. No. T1120). Indexing PCR was performed at ATG Lifetech Inc. (Seoul, Republic of Korea) using an overlap PCR approach with Illumina index primers to attach adapters to the amplified products. The library was purified via PCR cleanup and sequenced on an iSeq 100 system (Illumina, Cat. No. 20021535).

NGS data analysis

Raw FASTQ files were obtained from NGS using the Illumina iSeq system, following the manufacturer’s recommendations, with the iSeq 100 i1 Reagent v2 under a paired-end 150 bp (2 × 150 bp) run mode. Target DNA sequences with adapter-excluded lengths of 150 bp or shorter, including adapter and downstream sites, were removed using Cutadapt. For sequences longer than 150 bp, reads containing adapter sequences were discarded using Cutadapt. The processed reads were subsequently quality filtered using FASTP58 to exclude sequences with a quality score lower than 30.

For 1–10 bit binary data-encoded DNA samples, paired-end reads were merged using FLASH59 to cover the specific length corresponding to the number of the temperature stages. These merged reads were then aligned to a reference database (Supplementary Data 2) containing all possible binary sequence combinations using BWA60. The resulting SAM files were converted to BAM format using SAMtools (http://www.htslib.org/doc/samtools.html), and only high-quality reads with a mapping quality (MAPQ) exceeding 10 were retained. The final decoded binary data was determined by identifying the most frequent binary data and read counts were normalized by the total number of filtered reads in each BAM file.

In the case of DNA data storage samples synthesized via “NDS_GIST” writing, raw reads were first downsampled to depths of 800, 500, and 100×. A two-stage decoding process was implemented to address length variations resulting from the varying counts of “0” and “1” sequences within each binary data. In the first stage, paired-end reads were merged using FLASH to cover all potential overlap from 117 to 133 bp, and then aligned to a reference database comprising all possible binary data reference (Supplementary Data 2) using BWA. The resulting SAM files were converted into the BAM format and reads with a MAPQ > 10 were retained using SAMtools. The most frequent bit was identified at each of the eight bit positions and combined into a single binary data to determine the specific merge length. In the second stage, this fixed length was applied to re-execute the merging and alignment process to generate a refined BAM file. The final decoded binary data was determined by identifying the most frequent binary data and read counts were normalized by the total number of filtered reads in each BAM file.

For DNA temperature logger samples, a single-stage decoding was performed because the merge length remains constant. The reads were merged using FLASH with a fixed length of 122 bp. The repetitive DNA were divided into eight recording units, and the number of low-temperature or high-temperature sequences in each unit was normalized by the total sequence count.

Color quantification using CIELAB and CIEDE2000

Color changes were quantified in the CIELAB color space using the CIEDE2000 color-difference formula. Images of the TEMPER indicators were captured with a digital camera (NIKON D7500) under consistent lighting. In Adobe Photoshop, the region of interest corresponding to the solution in each tube was selected using the Quick Selection Tool. The average color of the selected region was obtained using an average blur filter, yielding a single representative color for each sample. The resulting color was converted to a CIELAB value. For each indicator, the CIELAB value at 0 h served as the reference, and the color difference (ΔE₀₀) at subsequent time point was calculated relative to this reference using the CIEDE2000 metric implemented in the Python color-science package.
