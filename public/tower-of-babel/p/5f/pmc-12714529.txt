Diagnostic Value of Stress Right Heart Catheterization for Dynamic Mitral Regurgitation in Atrial Fibrillation
Authors: Shodai Kawanami, Yasuyuki Egami, Ayako Sugino, Masaru Abe, Masami Nishino
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAccurate evaluation of dynamic mitral regurgitation (MR) during stress echocardiography is challenging due to atrial fibrillation (AF), time constraints, and poor reproducibility.Case PresentationAn 85-year-old man with exertional dyspnea had moderate atrial functional MR on transthoracic echocardiography. Quantitative assessment during handgrip stress echocardiography was difficult due to AF. Stress right heart catheterization (RHC) with handgrip revealed increased mean pulmonary capillary wedge pressure and V-wave. The mitral transcatheter edge-to-edge repair markedly reduced MR. Postprocedural stress RHC demonstrated only slight increase in the mean pulmonary capillary wedge pressure and the V-wave. He remained free from heart failure hospitalization.DiscussionAF increases the risk of atrial functional MR, making it common in clinical practice. Stress RHC with handgrip is a simple and reproducible invasive test even with AF.Take-Home MassageStress RHC with handgrip provides valuable diagnostic information when noninvasive results are inconclusive, especially in dynamic MR with AF.

History of Presentation

An 85-year-old man complained of shortness of breath on exertion. A few months ago, he began noticing shortness of breath even while walking on level ground. He was alert with a heart rate of 73 beats/min, blood pressure of 130/82 mm Hg, and oxygen saturation of 99% on room air. On auscultation, heart sounds were clear and breath sounds were normal. Additionally, no lower leg edema was observed.Take-Home Messages•Accurate evaluation of dynamic MR during stress echocardiography is challenging due to AF, time constraints, and poor reproducibility.•Stress RHC with handgrip exercise is a simple and widely used method that can provide valuable diagnostic information in cases where noninvasive assessments yield inconclusive results.

Past Medical History

He had a history of hypertension, ischemia-positive coronary artery disease involving the midleft anterior descending artery, and persistent atrial fibrillation (AF). Percutaneous coronary intervention and catheter ablation were not performed because he experienced repeated gastrointestinal bleeding due to antithrombotic drugs. Therefore, transcatheter left atrial appendage closure was performed 1 year ago.

Differential Diagnosis

We considered the possibility of shortness of breath due to AF and ischemic cardiomyopathy. In addition, dynamic mitral regurgitation (MR) was also considered as a cause. Other differential diagnoses were anemia and pulmonary disease.

Investigation

His electrocardiogram showed AF rhythm. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated left ventricular ejection fraction of 69% and no wall motion abnormalities. His left ventricular diastolic diameter was 53 mm, his left atrial diameter was 52 mm, and his estimated systolic pulmonary pressure was 39 mm Hg. Moderate MR was observed without left ventricular dilatation, wall motion abnormalities, and leaflet tethering. Left atrial enlargement, presence of AF, and preserved left ventricular ejection fraction supported the diagnosis of atrial functional MR rather than ischemic MR. Management

Although treatment with a sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor and an angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitor were initiated, symptoms remained unimproved, and N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide level increased from 4,597 to 8,330 pg/mL. After discussion by the heart team, mitral transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (M-TEER) was planned. Preprocedural transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) revealed that the main MR jet originated from the center-medial portion of the A2-P2 scallops. A MitraClip G4 XTW device (Abbott Vascular) was implanted at the center-medial portion of the A2-P2 scallops, resulting in significant improvement of the MR jet (Figure 3, Video 3). To evaluate the efficacy of M-TEER, stress RHC was performed again. At rest, mean PCWP was 8 mm Hg. After 2 minutes of handgrip stress, mean PCWP slightly increased to 11 mm Hg, and V-wave amplitude also showed only a slight increase, both markedly improved compared with preprocedural values (Figure 4). After M-TEER, shortness of breath improved, N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide decreased from 8,330 to 2,368 pg/mL, and left atrial diameter on TTE decreased from 52 to 48 mm. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 4. Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first reported case where stress RHC contributed to the diagnosis and evaluation of MitraClip efficacy for dynamic MR.

Challenges in Diagnosing Dynamic MR

It has been reported that there is no single optimal parameter for quantifying the severity of MR assessed by TTE and TEE.1 Although RV and EROA contribute to the assessment of MR severity, each method has inherent limitations. The proximal isovelocity surface area method is commonly used to calculate the EROA and RV; however, its accuracy is limited in the presence of multiple or eccentric jets. The continuity equation can also be used to calculate EROA and RV; however, it is not applicable in cases of concomitant aortic regurgitation or when the mitral annulus is heavily calcified. Furthermore, AF confounds the assessment of MR because it interferes with most standard quantification methods.2 In particular, the volumetric approach is less reliable in the presence of AF due to beat-to-beat stroke volume variability, which has been reported to increase with higher heart rates.3 This variability frequently leads to measurement errors when MR severity is evaluated using volumetric techniques. On the other hand, AF has been reported to be associated with a 3-fold increased risk of developing atrial functional MR compared with sinus rhythm.4 Consequently, MR occurring in patients with AF is commonly encountered in clinical practice. In particular, dynamic MR is especially challenging to evaluate accurately during stress echocardiography, due to the limited time and difficulties in confirming reproducibility. Additional testing including cardiac catheterization can be useful when there is a discrepancy between physical examination findings and TTE or TEE in the assessment of MR severity.1 Therefore, stress RHC with handgrip exercise can provide valuable diagnostic information in cases where noninvasive findings are inconclusive, particularly in patients with dynamic MR with AF.

Efficacy of Handgrip Exercise

Treadmills and ergometers are commonly used modalities for stress loading.5 However, their availability may be limited in certain facilities, and they are frequently not feasible for patients with impaired exercise capacity due to heart failure. Handgrip exercise represents a simple and widely accessible method of stress testing.6 Given that catheterization is performed via the internal jugular vein, handgrip stress testing is generally well tolerated and applicable to a wide range of patients.

Protocol of Stress RHC With Handgrip Exercise

Each patient was positioned supine and underwent RHC via the internal jugular vein using a Swan-Ganz catheter. After baseline measurements at rest, patients performed a sustained, maximum-effort isometric handgrip exercise, avoiding the Valsalva maneuver as much as possible. The exercise was maintained for at least 1 minute. Once sufficient hemodynamic stress was confirmed, systemic arterial pressure, heart rate, PCWP, and pulmonary artery pressure were promptly recorded. PCWP and pulmonary artery pressure were measured at end-expiration. The duration of handgrip exercise was individualized based on hemodynamic responses and patient tolerance, typically lasting 2 to 3 minutes.6 Previous studies have reported that PCWP ≥25 mm Hg during handgrip exercise is associated with exertional dyspnea in patients with MR.6 In the present case, PCWP during handgrip exercise was 28 mm Hg, consistent with this threshold. Accordingly, these findings suggest that PCWP ≥25 mm Hg may represent a clinically relevant cutoff value for considering therapeutic intervention during stress RHC. However, further studies and validation are warranted.

Therapeutic Effect of MitraClip on Dynamic MR

It has been reported that the MitraClip is associated with a reduced risk of the composite endpoint of all-cause mortality and hospitalization for heart failure in patients with dynamic MR.7 Additionally, the RESHAPE-HF2 (Randomized Investigation of the MitraClip Device in Heart Failure: Second Trial in Patients with Clinically Significant Functional Mitral Regurgitation) trial demonstrated that the addition of the MitraClip not only resulted in improved health status at 12 months but also reduced the risk of clinical events.8 This case highlighted a significant decrease in PCWP during handgrip stress after MitraClip treatment. It suggests that reducing left atrial pressure elevation during exertion may not only improve patients’ health status but also decrease the incidence of clinical events.

Conclusions

We report a case where the severity of MR was difficult to assess using echocardiography partially due to AF. However, dynamic MR was identified through RHC with handgrip exercise. After MitraClip treatment, an improvement in exercise-induced elevation of PCWP was also observed. Stress RHC with handgrip exercise is a simple and widely used method that can provide valuable diagnostic information in cases where noninvasive assessments yield inconclusive results.
