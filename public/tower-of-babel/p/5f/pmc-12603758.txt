A case of central sleep apnea in an adult with agenesis of the corpus callosum
Authors: Valentin Cabriada-Nuño, Sonia Castro-Quintas, Amaia Urrutia-Gajate, Beatriz Tijero-Merino
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCentral sleep apnea (CSA) is an important diagnostic and therapeutic challenge, which presents in a heterogeneous group of pathologies and comorbidities. Sometimes no cause is found to justify the presence of CSA, and it is classified as idiopathic. In this context, the performance of more specific complementary tests may reveal the presence of less frequent pathologies. We present a case of severe CSA in which brain MRI showed the presence of agenesis of the corpus callosum and dilatation of the third ventricle and lateral ventricles, without other associated malformations. It required treatment with adaptive servoventilation for its resolution. This association has only been described on one other occasion, so we believe that our case may contribute to focus attention on this infrequent cause of CSA.Keywords: Central sleep apnea, Agenesis of corpus callosum, Adaptative servoventilation, Sleep-disordered breathing, Congenital neurologic disorders

Introduction

Central sleep apnea (CSA) encompasses a group of sleep related breathing disorders (SRBD) that often occur as a consequence of other medical conditions, such as chronic heart failure (CHF), stroke, medication or substance use, as opioids [1], or even after treatment for obstructive sleep apnea (OSA). According to the third International Classification of Sleep Disorders (ICSD-3) CSA is defined by the presence of 5 or more central apneas or hypopneas per hour of sleep, with central being more than 50 % of the total episodes [2]. Its diagnosis and treatment is a major challenge due to the complexity of comorbid diseases and the prognostic impact of therapeutic options.

Case report

A 65-year-old male, ex-smoker, with a history of dyslipidemia and a recognized disability of 35 % since childhood due to a language and learning disability, without a specific diagnosis was referred from primary care to rule out OSA. No other relevant comorbidities or chronic treatment. The patient reported nocturnal asphyxitic crises, snoring, and unrestful sleep, with a score on Epworth scale of 9. The oro-pharyngeal and cardiopulmonary examination was normal, with BMI 28 kg/m2.

A home sleep apnea test (HSAT) was performed with the following result: AHI 42/h, AI 13/h, CAI: 11/h, ODI3 41/h, mean SpO2 92 % and minimum SpO2 82 %, CT90 10.3 %. Cheyne-Stokes respiration was not observed (Fig. 1).

– Diagnostic HSAT. The image shows the following channels, in order from top to bottom: audio, body position, thermistor, nasal cannula, RIPc flow, thorax, abdomen, SpO2, heart rate.

With this result, it was decided to initiate treatment with PAP and telemonitoring, with good levels of adherence to treatment (close to 6 hours per night on average) and no relevant leaks. However, the treatment was ineffective in both AutoCPAP and CPAP modes at various pressures tested, with residual AHI greater than 15/h and a predominance of central events in all modes and pressures (telemonitoring data). The best values were obtained with CPAP 8 cm H2O, so it was decided to perform a HSAT with this therapy, confirming the absence of response: AHI 31/h, AI 9/h, CAI 7/h, ODI3 30/h, mean SpO2 93 %, minimum SpO2 87 %, CT90 1.3 %.

Due to the predominantly central nature of respiratory events, an echocardiogram was performed and showed the presence of mild dilatation of right atrium and ventricle, with normal right ventricular systolic function, minimal mitral and tricuspid regurgitation, with a normal left ventricle size and function (LVEF Teich 60.2 %).

With these results, treatment was started with adaptive servoventilation (ASV), ASV-Auto mode, EPAP 5–12; PS 3–15, with practical disappearance of symptoms. A new HSAT was performed with ASV and the resolution of the SRBD was verified, with a residual AHI of 3/h (Fig. 2).

– HSAT on ASV (ASV-Auto mode, EPAP 5–12 cm H2O; PS 3–15 cm H2O). The image shows the following channels, in order from top to bottom: audio, body position, mask pressure, pneumoflow sensor, RIPc flow, thorax, abdomen, SpO2, heart rate.

In the absence of an evident cause to justify the CSA, a magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) had been previously requested, which showed an agenesis of the corpus callosum (ACC) with marked dilatation of the lateral ventricles, more prominent in the trigonal region and occipital horns, as well as a significant dilatation of the third ventricle causing a morphological distortion of the midbrain and some hypoplasia of both hippocampi (Fig. 3, Fig. 4).

– MRI image: agenesis of the corpus callosum (ACC) with marked dilatation of the lateral ventricles, more prominent in the trigonal region and occipital horns, and dilatation of the third ventricle.

– MRI image: midbrain distortion.

The patient was evaluated by neurology, which only found a decrease in verbal fluency without alterations in verbal comprehension or nomination. The rest of the examination was normal. There were no clinical or examination findings compatible with intracranial hypertension.

Discussion

ACC is a congenital brain malformation characterized by the total or partial absence of this structure. The corpus callosum (CC) is the most important interhemispheric commissure, and its fundamental function is the exchange of information for both sides of the brain to work in a coordinated manner; it derives from the lamina terminalis in the anterior neuropore region of the cephalic neural tube and its formation occurs from 11 to 15 weeks of gestation. The fetal development of this structure may be disrupted by various genetic factors. It may occur in isolation or in association with other systemic or central nervous system (CNS) disorders. It constitutes 14 % of congenital CNS malformations and is estimated to be present in 1/4000 to 1/5000 newborns, although may be underestimated due to the frequent asymptomatic course. Prevalences between 0.2 and 0.7 % of the general population have been described [3].

The clinical manifestations of this anomaly can be very varied, depending on the severity of the symptoms, the grade of agenesia (partial or complete), and if it is an isolated malformation or associated with other alterations [3]. We can find asymptomatic patients or with slight alterations in social behavior or intellectual development, while others present severe forms of neurodevelopmental delay, motor disorders including hypotonia, alterations in coordination and spasticity, alterations in visuospatial tasks or epileptic seizures of very variable typology (predominantly infantile spasms). The diagnosis is made by neuroimaging, being MRI the technique with which the diagnosis is most frequently reached, and it's especially relevant for pauci or asymptomatic patients.

A high prevalence of SRBD has been described in pediatric patients with ACC. Kwon et al. [4] in a cohort of 18 children referred for polysomnography (due to symptoms or risk factors for SRBD) found the presence of OSA in 11 (61 %), all mild or moderate, and an CAI ≥5 in 6 (30 %). Only 1 of them was an isolated CSA, while the other 5 also had OSA. We do not have similar data in the adult population, although the relationship between OSA and changes in the morphology of the CC has been evaluated, finding that only severe OSA, but not mild-moderate, present structural changes, compared to a group of simple snorers [5]. These changes are preferentially located in the genu and in the rostrum of the CC.

SRBD in these patients is usually related to the presence of other CNS alterations associated with ACC. In this situation, central apneas related to medical conditions without a Cheyne-Stokes pattern may occur, as in the case presented here. However, our patient does not present any other associated neurological process that could explain the presence of CSA. MRI images do not show brainstem involvement, except for a midbrain morphological distortion in relation to the dilatation of the third ventricle. The participation of the midbrain in ventilatory control is secondary, acting only as a modulator in specific contexts such as stress response, locomotion and integration with higher functions [6], so we do not believe that the appearance of CSA can be attributed to this morphological distortion. We have found only another case described in the literature of CSA associated with ACC as an isolated pathologic finding. Unlike the patient presented here, who required treatment with ASV, in this patient the CSA was correctly resolved with CPAP [7].

The presence of CSA in the context of other congenital neurological disorders has also been described. Joubert's syndrome, characterized by hypoplasia of the cerebellar vermis and brainstem, presents central apneas in up to 71 % of cases, as well as other cerebellar malformations that do not meet the strict criteria of this syndrome [8]. The diagnosis is usually made in childhood, but, as in our case, the initial diagnosis has been described in adulthood [9]. In contrast, treatment with ASV is ineffective in this syndrome.

In Wolfram syndrome the presence of CSA is not so frequent, but it appears in up to 11 % of cases [10]. In this pathology, morphological lesions are usually detected in the MRI (brainstem atrophy). Recently, a case has been reported of a 14-year-old girl with CSA associated with Wolfram syndrome, but without anatomical abnormalities detectable on MRI, as in the present case [11]. The authors hypothesize that the underlying mechanism is the presence of neurodegenerative alterations affecting the respiratory control centers of the brainstem.

It is noteworthy the fact that our patient did not present striking neurological symptomatology beyond a language and learning disorder detected in childhood, without further diagnosis. Most probably these alterations may be related to the ACC and colpocephaly identified later in adulthood. Kosky et al. have reported the case of a 55-year-old male with ACC and colpocephaly presenting low average intellectual capacity with deficits in processing speed, executive functions and social cognition that was diagnosed in adulthood as a result of symptoms of cognitive impairment. This patient's history also includes an OSA under CPAP treatment, but no further details were provided [12].

In the presence of CSA without a clear etiology, the performance of more specific complementary tests may provide relevant diagnostic information on less frequent entities that present with alterations in ventilatory control, and may benefit from available treatments such as ASV.
