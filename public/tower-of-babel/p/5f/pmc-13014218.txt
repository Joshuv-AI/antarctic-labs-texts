Connectivity of the Superior Temporal Polysensory Area With the Presubiculum, Parasubiculum, Entorhinal Cortex, and Claustrum–Endopiriform Complex in the Common Marmoset (Callithrix jacchus)
Authors: Yoshiko Honda, Keiko Moriya‐Ito, Nanako Hashimoto, Yasushi Kobayashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe rodent hippocampal–presubicular–entorhinal circuit is thought to be a key part of the circuitry involved in memory formation. In primates, additional connections are believed to enable more complex and higher‐order memory information processing. We investigated whether such additional connections exist in the cortical regions adjacent to the hippocampal formation using a tracer injection method. We discovered that the marmoset parasubiculum (ParS), presubiculum (PreS) (distal portion), and entorhinal cortex (EC) (proximal or medial portion) have strong reciprocal connections with the superior temporal polysensory area (STP), which is known as a well‐developed brain region in primates and where projections from diverse sensory areas converge. When the cholera toxin B subunit (CTB), a bidirectional tracer, was injected into various portions of ParS, PreS, and EC in one hemisphere, anterograde and retrograde labeling were observed in STP in both hemispheres. When CTB was injected into STP on one side, many retrogradely labeled cells were observed in layers III–V of ParS on the same side as the injection, and anterogradely labeled axons and terminal boutons were observed in layers I–III and V of ParS on both sides. In the distal portion of PreS and proximal portion of EC that were adjacent to ParS, retrogradely labeled cells were mostly observed in layer V, and substantial anterograde labeling was observed in all layers. Both in cases of STP injection and PreS (distal)‐ParS‐EC (proximal) injection, substantial anterograde and retrograde labeling were found in the claustrum–endopiriform complex (Cl–En). These results suggest the existence of a neural circuit that reciprocally connects STP, Cl–En, and PreS‐ParS‐EC, and may support functions such as higher‐order multisensory memory consolidation.Keywords: cholera toxin B subunit, hippocampus, memory formation, postsubiculum

Introduction

As a region receiving input from multiple sensory areas, the temporal‐parietal‐occipital association area (TPO) in the superior temporal gyrus has been reported in macaques using autoradiography (Seltzer and Pandya 1978). Subsequently, the superior temporal polysensory area (STP), which responds to auditory, somatosensory, and visual stimulation, was functionally defined in the dorsal (upper) bank of the superior temporal gyrus (Desimone and Gross 1979; Bruce et al. 1981). Based on their similar location and cytoarchitectonic features, STP was defined as corresponding to TPO, and its position was depicted on two‐dimensional (2D) unfolded maps of the primate cerebral cortex (Felleman and Van Essen 1991; Burman et al. 2011). In this way, STP/TPO has been identified as a brain region where projections from various sensory areas converge; it is also known to be particularly expanded in primates, including humans (Bruce et al. 1981; Petrides 2023). As for marmosets, Cappe and Barone (2005) first reported the existence of polysensory areas in the superior temporal gyrus using multiple tracer techniques. Marmosets are known to have a cooperative breeding system similar to that of human families, exhibit social behavior, and be able to recognize the face or voice of specific individuals (Miller et al. 2016; Schiel and Souto 2017; Tyree et al. 2023). Therefore, it is inferred that marmosets form higher‐order, complex memories by integrating multiple sensory and interpersonal information with the accompanying context of the event. In macaques, detailed reports have indicated that STP/TPO has numerous connections with key cortical areas in the limbic system, such as the EC and retrosplenial cortex (Insausti and Amaral 2008; Muñoz and Insausti 2005; Morris et al. 1999; Kobayashi and Amaral 2007). These previous findings suggest that STP is deeply functionally involved in the mechanisms underlying memory formation.

The presubiculum (PreS) and parasubiculum (ParS) are located between the hippocampal formation and EC. They serve as relay areas within the hippocampal–entorhinal pathways (the fundamental system for memory consolidation) and are known to form indirect feedback routes from the hippocampus to EC (Cappaert et al. 2015). Given the numerous discoveries of grid and head‐direction cells primarily in rodents, their functional relevance to navigation and spatial memory is of particular interest (Boccara et al. 2010; Tang et al. 2016; Simmonet and Fricker 2018). On the other hand, previous reports in humans suggest that PreS/ParS preferentially engages in both the imagination and recall of scenes involving visual information and spatial representations, thereby playing a crucial role in the cognitive mechanism for the internal representation of the world as spatially coherent scenes (Zeidman et al. 2015; Zeidman and Maguire 2016; Dalton and Maguire 2017). This implies that PreS and ParS function as essential regions for higher‐order memory formation in primates by directly connecting various association cortices with the fundamental memory formation system.

We have previously elucidated the connectional organization among the hippocampal formation, PreS, and EC in the rat, rabbit, and marmoset using tracer injection methods to clarify the basic memory circuit structure common across animal species and the neuronal connectivity characteristics of primates (Honda and Ishizuka 2004; Honda and Shibata 2017; Honda et al. 2022). Our findings revealed that while the hippocampal–entorhinal pathways are conserved across animal species, projections from CA1 (where CA stands for cornu ammonis) to PreS and bidirectional connections between PreS and EC, which are scarcely observed in rats, are abundantly present in rabbits and marmosets. Such additional connectivity was thought to enable higher‐order memory information processing in rabbits and marmosets compared with rats. We have also reported that PreS and ParS are strongly connected with the claustrum (Cl)–endopiriform (En) complex (Cl–En) in the marmoset (Honda et al. 2024). Specifically, the medial–ventral portion of the rostral Cl and adjacent dorsal portion of the endopiriform nucleus (En) have reciprocal connections with PreS (distal portion), ParS, and proximal (medial) EC, and unidirectional connections to hippocampal CA1 and/or subiculum (Sub). Cl–En is known to be bidirectionally connected to most cortical areas, and has been reported to be associated with various whole‐brain network mechanisms such as consciousness and attention (Goll et al. 2015). Furthermore, the dorsal part of En has been reported to be connected to the limbic system (Smith et al. 2019). At this point, two questions arise. The first is whether the marmoset STP has strong connectivity with PreS and ParS, and the second is whether the portions of Cl–En that connect with PreS, ParS, and EC also connect to STP. To address these questions, the present study investigated the connectivity of STP with PreS, ParS, and EC using the cholera toxin B subunit (CTB), retrograde and anterograde tracer injection methods. During this process, we also investigated the connectivity between STP and Cl–En.

Materials and Methods

We conducted all experiments under protocols approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Tokyo Metropolitan Institute of Medical Science (TMIMS), in accordance with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Nonhuman Primates in Neuroscience Research of the Japanese Society for Neuroscience and the US National Institutes of Health Guidelines for the Use of Laboratory Animals. Six adult common marmosets (Callithrix jacchus) (males, age range: 2–9 years, weight range: 316–402 g) from the TMIMS breeding colony were used in this study. Throughout all experiments, efforts were made to minimize the number of animals used and their pain and distress. CTB was used as a tracer for both retrograde and anterograde labeling. Surgery and Tracer Injections

For the surgical procedure, each monkey was anesthetized with ketamine hydrochloride (15–30 mg/kg body weight, intramuscular) and xylazine (1.5–3.0 mg/kg body weight, intramuscular). For sedation, sodium thiopental (30 mg/kg body weight, intraperitoneal) or secobarbital (30 mg/kg body weight, intraperitoneal) was administered. Each animal was placed in a stereotaxic instrument, and a hole was drilled in the skull at coordinates according to the atlas of Paxinos et al. (2012) (Table 1). For the tracer injection, a glass micropipette (tip inner diameter: 30–70 µm) filled with 2% low‐salt CTB (List Biological Laboratories, Campbell, CA, USA) dissolved in distilled water was manipulator‐guided into the hole, and CTB solution (0.2–0.5 µL) was injected under pressure using a Hamilton syringe (1 µL or 10 µL) inserted into each glass capillary. In all cases, CTB was injected into the left hemisphere. Five of the six cases presented in this study (except case M34) were identical to our previous studies (Honda et al. 2022, 2024); in two cases (10F and 13H), 10% biotinylated dextran amine (BDA) solution was also injected into the left (case 10F) or right (case 13H) hemisphere; in three cases (M29, M34, and M36), only CTB was injected into the left hemisphere; and in one case (M32), the tracer could not be injected because of a clogged pipette tip. In case 13H, we discriminated BDA from CTB labeling by enhancing BDA with 3,3′‐diaminobenzidine (DAB)‐nickel, and BDA labeling was ineffective in case 10F. We focused only on CTB labeling for these two cases.

Fixation and Cutting

After a survival period of 7 days following CTB injection, the marmosets were deeply anesthetized with ketamine hydrochloride (15–30 mg/kg body weight, intramuscularly) and xylazine (1.5–3.0 mg/kg body weight, intramuscularly), followed by intraperitoneal administration of pentobarbital sodium or thiopental sodium (80 mg/kg body weight). The brain was then perfused transcardially with physiological saline followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (pH 7.4) in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (PB). After the brains were removed and postfixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, they were blocked and cryoprotected in 20% glycerol in PB (4°C) and serial frozen sections (50 µm in thickness) were made on the coronal plane. Four parallel series of serial sections were collected in PB, and two series were used in this study. In both series, visualization of CTB was performed using DAB‐4HCl as a chromogen.

CTB Immunohistochemistry

To visualize CTB‐labeled cells by immunohistochemistry, the sections were first incubated overnight at 4°C in Tris‐buffered saline (TBS) containing 2% normal rabbit serum and 0.5% Triton X‐100, and then incubated for 48 h at 4°C with goat anti‐CTB (1:30,000 or 1:60,000; List Biological Laboratories; Table 2), 1% normal rabbit serum, and 0.5% Triton X‐100 for 48 h at 4°C. After washing in TBS, the sections were incubated in biotinylated rabbit anti‐goat IgG (H + L) solution (1:400; Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA) in TBS containing 1% normal rabbit serum and 0.5% Triton X‐100 for 1 h at room temperature. After washing several times with TBS, the sections were immersed in an avidin–biotin–horseradish peroxidase complex solution (Vectastain Elite ABC Kit; Vector Laboratories) for 1 h, and then treated with nickel‐enhanced DAB solution containing 0.02% DAB, 0.6% nickel (II) sulfate hexahydrate, and 0.003% H2O2 in TBS for 30 min. After washing several times with TBS, all sections were mounted on gelatin‐coated glass slides and dried, and then dehydrated in a graded series of alcohols, cleared in xylene, and covered with a cover slip. Some of the serial sections were counterstained with thionine.

Imaging and Data Analysis

Retrograde and anterograde labels were mapped using a computer‐assisted microscope system and data analysis program (Neurolucida; MicroBrightField, Williston, VT, USA). In trace diagrams, a black‐filled area represented the center of the tracer IS, magenta dots indicated individual labeled cells, and small blue dots indicated individual labeled terminal boutons. Light blue‐filled areas surrounding the injection center represented both the halo of tracer diffusion and the high‐density distribution of the anterogradely labeled axons and terminals, which were difficult to distinguish. On the other hand, the light blue‐shaded areas distant from the IS represented the high‐density distribution of labeled terminals (all labeled terminals that could be plotted were represented by blue dots, and areas too dense to be depicted were shaded). To indicate the location and extent of the IS, 2D unfolded maps, including PreS, ParS, EC, and temporal cortical regions, were created by measuring the length of each area along the proximal–distal axis, with Sub/PreS boundaries or fundus of the lateral sulcus (ls) as the zero points, as explained in our previous report (Honda et al. 2022) and in the Section 3 of the present study. In the unfolded maps, black‐filled and gray‐filled areas indicated the center of the IS, and each magenta dot indicated single labeled cells. The light blue shading around the injection center represented the tracer diffusion and high‐density distribution of labeled cells, which were difficult to distinguish. Based on our experience, we have confirmed that effective tracer uptake occurred only in the black‐filled injection center, and the diffusion areas were not significantly involved in tracer uptake. Digital microscopic photographs were taken in extended focus mode on a digital slide scanner (LINCE; Path Imaging, Tokyo, Japan). The number of focus levels was 25, and the focus step size was 5 µm. Low‐magnification microscope photographs were taken with a digital microscope (BZ‐X810; Keyence, Osaka, Japan). The digital images taken were adjusted to the optimal resolution and brightness using graphic editing software (Adobe Photoshop ver. 26.5.0; Adobe Systems, San Jose, CA, USA). All photographs, figures, and unfolded maps were modified and combined using Adobe Illustrator software (ver. 29.4; Adobe Systems).

Gallyas Staining

We used the silver impregnation method of Gallyas (1979) to stain the myelinated axon fiber for one of four series of serial frontal sections in case M32. The details are as described in Honda et al. (2024).

Results

Nomenclature and Cytoarchitectonic Description

In this study, the hippocampal formation was defined as the region including the dentate gyrus, CA, and Sub. The term “PreS‐ParS‐EC areas” was defined as the cortical areas adjacent to the hippocampal formation, including PreS, ParS, and EC. The boundaries of these regions were defined according to the Marmoset Brain Atlas (Paxinos et al. 2012), as described in our previous report (Honda et al. 2022). In rodents, some reports have defined the dorsal half of PreS as the postsubiculum, but from the perspective of connectivity with other regions, we defined this as PreS.

In primates, the elongated region that runs along the upper bank of the superior temporal sulcus (sts) is generally referred to as STP and is functionally defined as a region where inputs from the somatosensory, auditory, and visual areas converge (Bruce et al. 1981; Felleman and Van Essen 1991). Anatomically, it corresponds to TPO defined in the macaque genus (Seltzer and Pandya 1978; Cusick et al. 1995). The Marmoset Brain Atlas referenced in this study (Paxinos et al. 2012) defines STP according to Burman et al. (2011), and we have followed that definition. Specifically, the marmoset STP is located in the transition zone between the superior and inferior temporal cortices, ventral to the auditory parabelt, with broadly indistinct upper and lower boundaries, a gradually merging layer VI, and a poorly developed Baillarger's striation. To define the boundaries of STP in the present study, cyto‐ and myelo‐architectures were investigated by applying Nissl staining to one of four series of frontal sections and Gallyas staining to an adjacent series of sections in one case (M32) in which the tracer injection failed (Figure 1). According to the Marmoset Brain Atlas (Paxinos et al. 2012), STP was observed through all levels where the anterior commissure (ac) and Cl–En could be seen (i.e., +10.00 and +9.00 mm from interaural zero point; 61b and 69b in the atlas). At more caudal levels, where the lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN) was observed (+5.00 mm from interaural zero point; 101b in the atlas), STP became just dorsal (superior) to the fundus of sts area of the cortex, which was located dorso‐ventrally across sts. Area TE (TE) was located adjacent to the ventral side of parietal areas PGa and IPa (PGa/IPa; fundus of the superior temporal ventral area), and further proximally (closer to the rhinal fissure [rf]), while areas 35 and 36 (A35/36; the perirhinal cortex) were located rostrally and area TF (TF) caudally. Figure 1 shows sections of three representative levels on the rostral–caudal axis: rostral (A: level of ac), mid (C), and caudal (D: level of LGN), where STP could be clearly identified. Compared with the auditory cortex (including the core, belt, and parabelt regions) (Au; according to the Marmoset Brain Atlas [Paxinos et al. 2012]) (Figure 1A‐1), the widths of layers II–III and V–VI were clearly thicker in STP (Figure 1A‐2,C‐1,D‐1). Layer IV of STP was also thick, but the cell density was not as high as Au. The myelin density of STP was slightly high in layers IV and VI, but clearly lower than that of distally adjacent Au (Figure 1B,B‐1,B‐2). In our experimental cases (n = 6), the marmoset STP was extended by an average of approximately 7 mm in the rostral–caudal direction and approximately 3 mm in the dorsal–ventral (proximal–distal) direction. Due to variations in the depth and location of sts, it was difficult to indicate clearly the overall range of STP based solely on the location of sts. Therefore, we created 2D unfolded maps that included not only STP, but also the cortical areas surrounding STP (e.g., auditory association cortices, areas TE, PGa/IPa, 35/36, and TF) for each experimental case, ensuring that the entire STP was included in the unfolded maps.

Distribution of Retrogradely Labeled Cells and Anterogradely Labeled Terminals in a Case of CTB Injection Into STP

In a case in which CTB injection was confined to all layers of STP (Figure 2A and case M34 in Table 3), many labeled cells and terminals were found in ParS as well as the distal portion of PreS and the proximal (medial) portion of EC on the same side as the IS (Figure 2B,C). Labeled cells were mostly found in layer V of these regions (Figure 2B‐1,C‐1), and many of the labeled cells in ParS were pyramidal neurons with apical dendrites extending in the direction of the pial surface (Figure 2B‐2,C‐2). In the same location as the labeled cell distribution, there was a high density of anterogradely labeled axons and terminals in the deep part of layer I to the superficial part of layer II (Figure 2B‐3,C‐3). In Cl–En on the same side as the injection (Figure 3A), both labeled cells and terminals were densely distributed in the dorsal–medial and ventral portions of En and the caudal–lateral Cl (clCl). Most of the labeled cells were polymorphic neurons and distributed mixed with multiple unlabeled cells (Figure 3A‐1,A‐2). In the injection side of STP at the caudal level (Figure 3B,C), many labeled cells were found in layers II–III and V–VI (Figure 3B‐1,C‐1), and the majority were pyramidal neurons (Figure 3B‐2,C‐2, B‐3,C‐3). Labeled terminals were mainly distributed in the superficial layers, including layer I (Figure 3B‐2,C‐2). On the contralateral side (Figure 3D), many labeled cells were observed at a high density in layer III of STP (Figure 3D‐1), most of which were pyramidal neurons (Figure 3D‐2). Labeled terminals were observed in all layers of STP, with the highest density observed in layer III (Figure 3D‐1,D‐2). When labeled cells and terminals were plotted on section trace diagrams of the hemisphere of the injection side (Figure 4), many labeled cells were found in layer V of ParS, the distal part of PreS, and the proximal (medial) part of EC (Figure 4D–H), as well as in layers V–VI (Figure 4G–I) of area TH (TH). Labeled terminals were densely distributed in the superficial layers (layers I–III) of the same regions where labeled cells were densely observed. Labeled cells and terminals were both distributed across a wide range of PreS‐ParS‐EC areas along the rostral–caudal axis. Within the ipsilateral STP, numerous labeled cells were widely distributed in all layers, except for layer IV, in the rostral–caudal direction, and a moderate number of labeled cells were also distributed in the adjacent Au and PG/IPa (Figure 4A–G). In TE (distal portion) near sts, the number and distribution range of labeled cells seemed to be more prominent in the deep (infragranular) than in the superficial (supragranular) layers (Figure 4E,F). Labeled cells and terminals were also distributed in Cl–En and surrounding insular and parainsular cortices (Figure 4A–C). In addition, small volumes of labeled cells and terminals were observed in A35/36, TF (Figure 4E–H), layers III–V of area 30 (Figure 4G–I), and several amygdaloid nuclei (data not shown).

Discussion

The results of the present study indicate that the marmoset STP has strong bidirectional connections with PreS‐ParS‐EC areas (especially ParS, the distal part of PreS, and the proximal part of EC). In addition, connections were identified between STP and Cl–En. Moreover, our results suggest the existence of intrinsic connections that connect rostrocaudally within STP and between the ipsilateral and contralateral STP. Based on the present results, multiple circuit structures connecting STP, PreS‐ParS‐EC areas, and Cl–En were predicted to exist, and these structures in the left and right hemispheres appear to have complex interactions with each other via STP and PreS‐ParS‐EC areas (Figure 15).

The portions of Cl–En connecting to PreS‐ParS‐EC areas also connected reciprocally with rSTP. rSTP had moderate reciprocal connections with ipsilateral A30, Au, Ins/PaI, and PGa/IPa, but the number of connections between A35/36 or TF and rSTP was much lower.

Connections Between STP and PreS‐ParS‐EC Areas

Previously, reciprocal connections between STP and EC have been reported in macaques. Insausti and Amaral (2008) found that the dorsal (upper) bank of sts (corresponding to STP) projects mainly to layer I of EC (especially mid‐caudal levels) and partially (ELc) to all layers, while Muñoz and Insausti (2005) reported strong outputs from layer V of EC to the rostral part of STP. In the present study, we confirmed the presence of projections mainly from layer V of the caudo–medial (proximal) EC to the rostral STP, consistent with previous reports. Saleen et al. (2000) reported that the lateral part of EC primarily projects to STP based on a peroxidase‐conjugated wheat germ agglutinin injection study. In our STP injection case (M34), labeled cells were observed in layers V–VI of the rostral levels of EC at its lateral (near rf) portion (Figure 8B); however, it remains unclear why labeled cell populations were separated into two groups along the rostral–caudal axis in our injection cases (with cells located in the lateral portion rostrally and the medial portion caudally). The influence of the IS containing a small amount of TE cannot be ruled out. Generally, the lateral EC is involved in the ventral cortical “What” stream (processing information about objects and persons), entering the hippocampus via the lateral EC through the perirhinal cortex (A35/36) from areas TG and TE. The medial EC is involved in the dorsal cortical “Where” stream (representation of spatial location), entering the hippocampus via the medial EC from the parietal cortex through areas TF and TH. However, in macaques and humans, numerous cortico‐cortical and cortico‐hippocampal connections exist that do not fit this dual‐stream model (Huang et al. 2021). In the present study, we did not distinguish medial EC and lateral EC, but our results revealed that the marmoset STP constructs a pathway connecting to the hippocampus via distal PreS‐ParS‐medial EC areas. The development of higher‐order memory formation mechanisms in primates may give rise to this diversity of information pathways. Among our three cases of injection into PreS‐ParS‐EC areas, case 10F showed a larger number of labeled cells in STP compared with the other two cases (M29 and 13H). If the number of EC projection neurons in STP, and/or the number of terminal arborizations of afferent fibers from STP within EC, are significantly greater than the connections between STP and other cortical areas, this may explain why a small degree of CTB uptake in EC caused a large amount of retrograde labeling in case 10F.

Several studies have reported connections between the temporopolar cortex and ParS in the rhesus monkey (Van Hoesen et al. 1979; Morán et al. 1987); however, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study demonstrating direct connections between STP and ParS in nonhuman primates. ParS is present in many mammals, from rodents to primates, and its cortical connections and molecular characterization have mainly been found to receive inputs from Sub and form reciprocal connections with layer II of EC in addition to PreS, postrhinal cortex, and retrosplenial cortex in rodents (Köhler 1985; van Groen and Wyss 1990; Caballero‐Bleda and Witter 1993; Agster and Burwell 2013; Sammons et al. 2019). Grid, border, head‐direction, and speed cells have been found in ParS of rats, and their involvement in spatial cognitive functions has been investigated (Taube 1995; Boccara et al. 2010; Tang et al. 2016; Spalla et al. 2022). Witter and Amaral (2020) reported strong projections from ParS to layers II–III of EC in macaque monkeys; however, the structure of fiber connections in the primate ParS remains largely unclear. Our discovery that the marmoset ParS is strongly connected to STP may provide clues to elucidating the unknown function of ParS in primates.

Mainly in rodents, PreS has been known to have strong cortical connections with Sub, EC, and retrosplenial cortex, and because it contains many grid and head‐direction cells, it has been noted for its involvement in spatial cognitive functions (Boccara et al. 2010; Simmonet and Fricker 2018). Connectivity among PreS, hippocampal formation, and EC has not yet been fully analyzed in primates, except for our first report on the detailed connections of PreS in marmosets (Honda et al. 2022). Interestingly, the distal portion of PreS, which was found to be strongly connected with STP in the present study, was almost consistent with the region that was strongly and bidirectionally connected with Cl–En in marmosets (Honda et al. 2024).

Connectivity and Functional Characteristics Corresponding to the Location Within STP

In macaques, TPO (area corresponding to STP) has been subdivided into three or four regions along the rostral–caudal axis, that is, rostral (anterior), intermediate, and caudal (posterior) TPO, or TPO‐1 to 4, according to different cytoarchitectonic, connectional, and functional viewpoints (Seltzer and Pandya 1989, 1991, 1994; Cusick et al. 1995; Seltzer et al. 1996; Cusick 1997; Padberg et al. 2003). The rostral STP is known to contain numerous neurons that respond to moving stimuli in the visual field (Desimone and Gross 1979; Bruce et al. 1981), and consensus has also been reached that the rostral STP is motion‐sensitive (Andersen and Siegel 2005; Nelissen et al. 2006). Our research has revealed that the rostral STP is strongly connected bidirectionally with ParS and distal PreS, suggesting that these areas may exert feedback influences on higher‐order visual information processing pathways via the rostral STP. On the other hand, Wang et al. (2025) reported that the posterior (caudal) STP plays a role in identifying the orientation and depth of three‐dimensional surfaces, including during movement. This requires the integration of visual and vestibular information, suggesting that such conversion occurs within the caudal STP. Although the present study examined only one case of rostral STP injection, open‐access data on marmoset cortico‐cortical connections (The Marmoset Brain Connectivity Atlas in Majka et al. 2020) include TPO injections into the caudal part (cases CJ122‐FB and FE). According to this resource, retrogradely labeled cells were observed in large numbers in STP/TPO, the deep and superficial layers of ParS, and layer V of the distal portion of PreS and proximal portion of EC. These findings were consistent with our results. In the cases of tracer injection into PreS‐ParS‐EC areas presented in this study, labels were widely distributed in the rostral–caudal direction of STP, suggesting that there are probably no significant differences in the connection patterns between the rostral and caudal STP (at least in terms of connections with PreS‐ParS‐EC areas or Cl–En).

In our cases M29 (Figure 10B), 13H (Figure 11B), 10F (Figure 12B), and M36 (revised Figure 14C), the distribution of labeled cells in layers V–VI of STP continued caudally or distally beyond the STP border to Au, which we presented on the 2D unfolded maps. Majka et al. (2019) investigated direct cortical afferents to the primary visual area (V1) in marmosets, demonstrating the existence of a region between the caudal auditory areas and dorsal MST that sends substantial direct projections to V1. They suggested that this region could be homologous to TPOc, the most caudal subdivision of TPO in macaques (Padberg et al. 2003). If, as they suggested, the marmoset STP extends caudo‐dorsally (distally) beyond the range presented in Paxinos et al.’s (2012) Marmoset Brain Atlas, this could be considered one factor explaining our findings.

In the present study, some of the PreS‐ParS‐EC area injection cases (such as cases M29 and 10F) showed patchy or curved strip distribution patterns of labeled cells in STP and surrounding cortical regions on unfolded maps. In macaques, similar patchy patterns of chemoarchitecture and cortical connectivity in TPO have been reported using serial sections cut tangentially to the cortical surface of the upper bank of STS (Padberg et al. 2003). Although it remains unclear how the neurochemical and connection modules observed in the higher‐order association areas, including TPO, are related to functional organization, our results suggest that the patchy pattern of cortical connections in STP of marmosets may play a role in not only environmental perception but also memory formation via PreS‐ParS‐EC areas.

Associational and Commissural Intrinsic Connections Within STP

The present results demonstrated that the marmoset STP has reciprocal intrinsic connections within the ipsilateral STP and between the ipsi‐ and contralateral STP that extend widely in the rostral–caudal direction. In several reports on TPO in macaque monkeys, bidirectional projections have been demonstrated among TPO‐1 to TPO‐4, that is, the rostral–caudal subdivisions (Seltzer and Pandya 1989, 1994; Seltzer et al. 1996). All these reports showed that, as a result of restricted tracer injection into TPO‐1 to TPO‐4, labeled cells and terminals were distributed over a wide range of TPO in the rostral–caudal direction in the hemisphere on the same side as the injection. Our findings are basically consistent with these reports, and thus, it is conceivable that there are bidirectional connections in the rostral–caudal direction within the marmoset STP, as in the macaque. Regarding the associational intrinsic connections of TPO in macaques, Seltzer and Pandya (1989) reported that the projections from the rostral to caudal direction (for instance, from TPO‐2 to TPO‐3) originate mainly from the supragranular layers (layers II–III) and terminate in layer IV, whereas the projections from the caudal to rostral direction originate mainly from the infragranular layers (layers V–VI); they suggested that these connections could be a case of cytoarchitectonic progression of the neocortex. Our results regarding injection into the rostral STP of marmosets indicate that the rostral STP projects strongly to almost all layers of the middle and caudal STP, especially the superficial layers (layers I–III), and that the projections from the middle and caudal STP to the rostral STP originate from all layers, including not only layers II–III but also layers V–VI. On the other hand, there have been few reports on the commissural connections between the left and right STP. Jacobson and Trojanowski (1977) reported commissural connections of the prefrontal granular cortex in the macaque by tracer injection into various areas of the prefrontal cortex, and demonstrated that the distribution of labeled cells in the contralateral hemisphere was concentrated in the same location as IS (i.e., homotopical distribution) and that the cells of origin were mainly distributed in layer III. They also reported that the most common distribution pattern of retrogradely labeled cells in the homotopical cortical regions was a horizontal band‐like distribution with variable (waxed and waned) cell density, especially in the ipsilateral cortex, and that labeled cells were observed as patches and clusters in the heterotopic cortical regions. In our STP injection case (case M34), the distribution of labeled cells in the contralateral STP was also localized in layer III and spread over a wide range in the rostral–caudal and proximal–distal directions. However, the distribution pattern did not appear to be patchy or cluster‐like, as in the cases of injection into PreS‐ParS‐EC areas (cases M29 and 10F in Figures 10 and 12). Compared with Old World monkeys such as macaques, the brains of New World monkeys, including marmosets, are significantly smaller, with a smoother surface and fewer cerebral sulci. Such changes in brain size and morphology are suggested to involve a rearrangement of neural resources, which may play a crucial role in higher‐order brain functions such as visual perception and spatial cognition in marmosets (Lui and Rosa 2015). Therefore, interspecies differences may be one of the reasons for the different distribution patterns of neurons involved in intrinsic connections in STP reported in the present study (e.g., uniform pattern) and those reported in macaque monkeys (e.g., patchy pattern).

Connections Between STP and Cl–En

In the present study, we have demonstrated that the marmoset STP (rostral‐mid STP) is reciprocally connected to Cl–En. Furthermore, the parts of Cl–En that connect to STP have been found to overlap almost completely with the regions that strongly connect to PreS (Honda et al. 2024). Cl–En is known to be bidirectionally connected to many cortical areas, including the prefrontal cortex, and is suggested to play a central hub‐like role in whole‐brain functional networks such as the default mode network, salience network, and sleep network (Smith et al. 2019). The present results suggest that STP might be involved in such basic whole‐brain networks through Cl–En, and that in both STP injection cases (M34) and PreS/ParS/EC injection cases (M29, 13H, and 10F), little if any labeling was observed in the contralateral Cl–En. Moreover, in the Cl–En injection case (case M36), almost no labeling was observed in the contralateral Cl–En (data not shown), suggesting that the Cl–En‐mediated connection network is mainly limited to the ipsilateral hemisphere (Figure 15).

Connections of STP With Other Cortical Regions

In macaques, strong reciprocal connections between the rostral TPO and the dorso‐anterior TE have been reported (Saleen et al. 2000), suggesting that similar connectivity might exist in marmosets. In the case of retrograde tracer injection into the caudal TPO described in The Marmoset Brain Connectivity Atlas (Majka et al. 2020), a wide distribution of retrogradely labeled cells was observed in the rostral–caudal direction within TE1–3 (particularly TE3: the distal portion of the caudal TE). The numerous labeled cell populations observed in the distal portion of the rostral TE (near STP) in our rostral STP injection case (M34) are also likely STP projection neurons. This suggests that the entire rostral–caudal extent of STP/TPO projects to TE in marmosets. Our IS slightly included the distal portion of the rostral TE (corresponding to the dorso‐anterior subdivision of TE in macaques, as described by Saleen et al. 2000, i.e., TEad). Therefore, the labeled TE cell populations may include intrinsic connection neurons within the ipsilateral TE. PGa/IPa, adjacent to the caudal‐distal (dorsal) portion of TE, has been reported to have reciprocal connections with TPO in macaques (Seltzer and Pandya 1989); moderate reciprocal connectivity was also observed in our rostral STP (rSTP) injection case.

In macaques, projections from the rostral (TPO‐1,2) and middle (TPO‐2,3) sectors of TPO to TF, TH, and A35/36 (perirhinal cortex) have been reported (Seltzer and Pandya 1991; Barnes and Pandya 1992; Blatt et al. 2003). Furthermore, as for the region corresponding to TPO, the dorsal bank of the superior temporal sulcus (STSd), strong reciprocal connections with TF and TH, as well as projections from A36 to the rostral STSd, have been reported (Lavenex et al. 2002; Suzuki and Amaral 1994). In our case of rostral STP injection (M34), many labeled cells were observed in the ipsilateral TH, primarily in layers V–VI, with labeled terminals mainly in layers I–III; however, labeling in A35/36 and TF was very sparse (Figure 15). Moreover, in a caudal TPO injection case of the Marmoset Brain Connectome Atlas (Majka et al. 2020), numerous retrograde labels were observed in the deep layers of A35/36 and TH, while labeling in TF was scarcely detected. Our finding that the marmoset STP lacks strong connectivity with TF may reflect species differences compared with macaques.

Area 30 of the retrosplenial cortex has been reported to connect with TPO in macaques (Morris et al. 1999; Kobayashi and Amaral 2007). In our STP injection case (M34), anterograde and retrograde labeling were observed in area 30, suggesting that bidirectional connections between the retrosplenial cortex and STP/TPO also exist in marmosets. By contrast, few labeled cells or terminals were detected in area 29. Similarly, in a case of retrograde tracer injection into the caudal TPO from The Marmoset Brain Connectivity Atlas (Majka et al. 2020), area 29 showed almost no labeled cells, with only a few observed in the caudal part of area 30. These findings are consistent with reports by Kobayashi and Amaral (2003, 2007) in macaques. Thus, it is plausible that the connection between STP/TPO and the retrosplenial cortex, one of the common connectivity features between marmosets and macaques, is primarily mediated by area 30, but not area 29.

Regarding connections with auditory areas, our STP injection case (M34) showed numerous labeled cells in Au adjacent to STP (particularly the auditory parabelt), similar to reports in macaques (Smiley et al. 2007; Hacket et al. 2014), indicating direct connections between the caudal belt and parabelt regions and TPO. On the other hand, in our STP injection case, the IS might have extended into part of the Au, and the population of labeled cells may have included intrinsic connection neurons within Au.

Projections from the rostral sectors of TPO (TPO‐1,2) to the insula have been reported in macaques (Seltzer and Pandya 1991). The caudal TPO injection case described in The Marmoset Brain Connectivity Atlas (Majka et al. 2020) showed numerous labeled cells in insular cortices. Therefore, the numerous labeled terminals and labeled cells observed in the parainsular and insular cortices in our STP injection case (M34) suggest reciprocal connections between STP/TPO and insula. Furthermore, in our case M34, numerous labeled cells were observed in the area prostriata (data not shown), which is adjacent to the primary visual cortex and connects to various multisensory and higher‐order association areas (Morecraft et al. 2000; Yu et al. 2012). This will be reported in a separate study.

Functional Considerations

In humans, the multisensory integration of auditory, visual, and spatial information in the language‐dominant hemisphere gives rise to higher‐order conceptual processing, that is, semantic processing. Petrides (2023) argued that in the cortical evolution of primates, multisensory areas that were mainly restricted to sts in macaques expanded ventrally in the human brain to include a part of the middle temporal gyrus, and that this expansion of multisensory areas played a decisive role in the emergence of semantic processing functions in the language‐dominant hemisphere.

Marmosets are known to live in family units and engage in social behavior using vocal communication. Therefore, it would not be surprising if there were a semantic processing area corresponding to the auditory language center in the superior temporal gyrus in the vicinity of the auditory cortex (Takahashi et al. 2015; Jafari et al. 2023). A recent neuroimaging study in humans suggested that PreS/ParS contributes to the mechanism of modeling the world during perception through access to the overall representation of the environment and supports the ability to construct internal scenes mentally during episodic memory recall and imagination (Dalton and Maguire 2017). As an overview of the memory circuits, it is known that in both rats and monkeys, various areas centered around the hippocampal formation, such as PreS‐ParS‐EC areas, cingulate cortex, and diencephalic regions (i.e., the anterior thalamic nucleus and mammillary bodies) are connected (all reciprocally excluding the mammillary bodies, which receive input from the hippocampus and project to the anterior thalamus) and topographically organized to form subsystems, thereby creating complex networks of connections (Bubb et al. 2017).

Rolls (2023, 2024) and Rolls et al. (2023) analyzed the effective connectivity of cortical regions in the Human Connectome Project multimodal parcellation (HCP‐MMP) atlas and presented five distinct visual cortical processing streams that lead to the hippocampal episodic memory system via cortical areas of the parahippocampal gyrus. Among them, the “Where” streams (i.e., the ventromedial visual and dorsal visual streams) connect to PreS and EC via PHA1‐3 (parahippocampal areas: located in the posterior and medial parts of the parahippocampal gyrus, corresponding to area TH of macaques) (Rolls et al. 2023). These pathways are crucial for constituting the “Where” element of episodic memory by activating the hippocampal spatial view cells, providing information about where the visual object is located within a scene, and further supplying updates on the subject's location within the scene based on self‐motion (Rolls 2023). ParS was not depicted between PreS and EC or TH in the HCP‐MMP atlas; instead, PHA1, 2, and 3 were presented as regions adjacent to PreS, arranged in a proximal–distal direction. Considering that PHA1 and PHA2 directly connect to PreS and EC (Rolls et al. 2023), and that in macaques, ParS is located between area TH and PreS (Kobayashi and Amaral 2003, 2007), as we demonstrated in marmosets, PHA1 and PHA2 (at least PHA1) could correspond to or include ParS in humans.

In macaques, it has been reported that neurons selectively responsive to faces exist in TPO (Baylis et al. 1987); a similar facial (with vocal) recognition system has been reported in marmosets (Hung et al. 2015; Dureux et al. 2024). The rostral TPO (TPOJ1) is involved in the sts visual/semantic “What” stream (Rolls et al. 2023), which might represent the self and its social relations to others by implicating dynamically changing stimuli, including those involving vocalization and the face (Rolls et al. 2022). The findings of the present study, showing that the marmoset STP/TPO directly connects with ParS and adjacent distal PreS and proximal EC, suggest that various data processed at higher levels via the “What” and “Where” streams could be integrated in PreS‐ParS‐EC areas immediately before entering the hippocampal formation. Furthermore, our finding that Cl–En connects to STP via portions nearly identical to its PreS‐connecting portions suggests that, in addition to the route where information flows through the sts, the visual/semantic “What” stream reaches STP/TPO followed by the hippocampal formation (the fundamental structure for memory formation) via PreS‐ParS‐EC areas, and thus, there may be pathways connecting STP and Cl–En, and Cl–En and PreS. This suggests that visual cortical streams and the memory system could connect to whole‐brain networks, such as the salience and attention networks (Jackson et al. 2020) and the default mode network (Buckner and Margulies 2019). Moreover, the present study revealed the presence of numerous commissural projections between the STP of the left and right hemispheres in marmosets. This suggests that information from the circuit among STP, Cl–En, and PreS‐ParS‐EC areas in each hemisphere is exchanged via cortical regions, including STP. Confirming whether pathways connecting STP, PreS‐ParS‐EC areas, and Cl–En exist universally in both human and nonhuman primates is crucial for elucidating the underlying mechanisms of episodic memory consolidation and retrieval. These mechanisms involve multisensory information for constructing the internal scenes and information related to social behavior.
