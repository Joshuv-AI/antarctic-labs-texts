Impact of Magnification Endoscopy on Diagnostic Accuracy and Confidence in Real‐Time Histological Prediction of Colorectal Lesions: A Prospective Multicenter Study
Authors: Kotaro Shibagaki, Yoichi Miyaoka, Takafumi Yuki, Kazuo Yashima, Hisao Tanaka, Hirofumi Fujishiro, Yasumasa Tada, Koichiro Kawaguchi, Yuichiro Ikebuchi, Yusuke Takahashi, Tomotaka Yazaki, Satoshi Kotani, Norihisa Ishimura, Hajime Isomoto, Shunji Ishihara
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAccurate endoscopic diagnosis of colorectal lesions is essential for clinical management. This prospective multicenter study assessed the impact of magnification endoscopy on real‐time decision‐making for colorectal lesions.MethodsWe included 419 patients with 876 colorectal polyps and assessed using three approaches: single (white light endoscopy, WLE), dual (WLE + narrow‐band imaging with magnification endoscopy, NBIME), and triple (WLE + NBIME + pit pattern analysis using magnification chromoendoscopy or acetic acid–enhanced NBIME). Expert endoscopists provided histological predictions and confidence. Predictions were further classified into three management categories: surveillance (hyperplastic polyps), endoscopic resection (conventional adenomas, sessile serrated lesions [SSLs], traditional serrated adenomas [TSAs], T1a carcinoma), and surgical resection (T1b carcinoma).ResultsLesions included 24 hyperplastic polyps, 654 conventional adenomas, 85 SSL/TSAs, 90 T1a carcinomas, and 23 T1b carcinomas. Histological prediction accuracy was 84.9%, 85.3%, and 87.4% (p = 0.35), and clinical diagnosis accuracy was 94.6%, 95.5%, and 96.5% (p = 0.17) for single, dual, and triple modalities, respectively, showing numerically higher values with additional modalities. High‐confidence rates significantly increased with additional modalities (73.4%, 80.0%, 83.6%; p < 0.05), correlating with improved accuracy. In high‐confidence cases, histological prediction accuracy was 93.3%, 92.7%, and 93.2%, compared to 66.5%, 60.6%, and 67.4% in low‐confidence cases (p < 0.05). A similar trend was observed for clinical diagnosis, with accuracy of 98.3%, 97.9%, and 97.8% versus 85.0%, 85.7%, and 89.6% (p < 0.05). Confidence gain was associated with larger size in both single–dual and dual–triple transitions (p < 0.05), particularly ≥ 10 mm lesions.ConclusionMagnification endoscopy has minimal impact on diagnostic accuracy but significantly enhances diagnostic confidence, aiding clinical decisions.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer is a major global health concern and was the third leading cause of cancer‐related deaths in 2022 [1]. Early detection and removal of colorectal polyps significantly reduce mortality [2], with screening programs and advances in endoscopic imaging improving detection rates. Despite advances in artificial intelligence [3], detailed histologic prediction remains challenging, so treatment decisions still rely on endoscopic assessment by trained endoscopists.

White light endoscopy (WLE) is the primary diagnostic tool. Narrow‐band imaging with magnification endoscopy (NBIME) improves vascular pattern visualization [4, 5, 6], while magnification chromoendoscopy (MCE) with crystal violet staining or acetic acid‐enhanced NBIME (A‐NBIME) highlights glandular pit patterns [7, 8, 9, 10]. These techniques aid histologic prediction by visualizing glandular structural abnormalities on the polyp surface, with NBIME preferred for its simplicity. However, magnification endoscopy is not widely adopted due to technical complexity.

Although magnification endoscopy has shown superior diagnostic accuracy over WLE, its main benefit is distinguishing neoplastic from non‐neoplastic polyps [11, 12]. Its role in the qualitative diagnosis of neoplastic lesions has been evaluated mainly in studies using still images rather than real‐time assessments. In our previous study, A‐NBIME showed higher diagnostic accuracy than WLE and NBIME [13], but clinical practice often combines multiple modalities to improve accuracy. This prospective multicenter study was conducted in accordance with STARD 2015 [14] to evaluate the real‐world diagnostic performance of these approaches and their additional value in routine practice.

Patients and Methods

Participants

This study enrolled 428 patients with 885 colorectal polyps between July 2018 and Jan 2022. All patients undergoing colonoscopy during the study period were included, regardless of whether it was their first or a follow‐up examination. The study was approved by the ethics committees of five institutions, and all patients provided written informed consent. It was registered in the UMIN Clinical Trials Registry (UMIN000034549). Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) suspected precancerous lesions or early‐stage colorectal cancer and (2) difficult differentiation between hyperplastic and neoplastic polyps. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) prior histological diagnosis, (2) inadequate histological evaluation due to tissue damage or loss, and (3) familial adenomatous polyposis.

Colon Preparation

All patients received 150 mg of sodium picosulfate hydrate or another stimulant laxative the night before colonoscopy, followed by 2–3 L of polyethylene glycol electrolyte solution on the morning of the procedure. To inhibit bowel peristalsis, scopolamine butylbromide or glucagon was administered unless contraindicated.

Endoscopic Procedure

Upon detection, colorectal polyps were registered with patient consent. Macroscopic evaluation was conducted with WLE, followed by NBIME to assess vascular and surface patterns using the JNET classification [15] and sessile serrated adenoma/polyp reports [16, 17]. Pit pattern analysis followed, using MCE with 0.05% crystal violet or A‐NBIME with 1.5% acetic acid [7, 18, 19], and these two modalities were treated as equivalent for pit pattern diagnosis based on prior prospective evidence demonstrating comparable accuracy and intra‐ and interobserver agreement between them [10]. Expert endoscopists determined diagnoses using JNET and pit pattern classifications as references, not definitive criteria. Lesions were resected endoscopically or surgically as needed. Board‐certified endoscopists, each with > 1000 magnification colonoscopies, performed all endoscopic procedures. Equipment included magnification endoscopes (CF‐H260AZI, PCF‐H290AZI) and a standard endoscopic system (EVIS LUCERA ELITE) from Olympus (Tokyo, Japan).

Endoscopic Diagnosis and Diagnostic Confidence

Endoscopic diagnosis in this study was assessed using three sequential approaches (Figure 1): single modality (WLE), dual modality (WLE + NBIME), and triple modality (WLE + NBIME + MCE or A‐NBIME). For each approach, endoscopists provided a real‐time histologic prediction, defined as classification into one of five histologic categories: hyperplastic polyp, conventional adenoma, sessile serrated lesion (SSL) or traditional serrated adenoma (TSA), T1a carcinoma, and T1b carcinoma. In addition, a clinical diagnosis was assigned based on expected treatment strategy—surveillance (for hyperplastic polyps), endoscopic resection (for conventional adenomas, SSL/TSA, and T1a carcinomas), or surgical resection (for T1b carcinomas).

Sequential diagnostic approach using single, dual, and triple modalities. Expert endoscopists performed endoscopic diagnoses using three approaches: single (white light endoscopy [WLE]), dual (WLE + narrow‐band imaging with magnification endoscopy [NBIME]), and triple (WLE + NBIME + magnification chromoendoscopy [MCE] or acetic acid‐enhanced NBIME [A‐NBIME]). For each, histological predictions and diagnostic confidence (high or low) were recorded. Predictions were classified into five categories: hyperplastic polyp, conventional adenoma, sessile serrated lesion (SSL) or traditional serrated adenoma (TSA), T1a carcinoma, and T1b carcinoma. They were further grouped into three clinical categories based on treatment strategy: surveillance (hyperplastic polyp), endoscopic resection (conventional adenoma, SSL/TSA, T1a carcinoma), and surgical resection (T1b carcinoma).

Diagnostic confidence was recorded for each prediction and rated as high or low. High confidence, defined as a subjective estimate of ≥ 80% probability that the diagnosis was correct, indicated strong diagnostic certainty, whereas low confidence reflected diagnostic uncertainty or comparable likelihoods of alternatives. This threshold was agreed upon and applied by all participating endoscopists.

Histological Evaluation

Histological diagnosis, serving as the gold standard, was classified into five categories corresponding to endoscopic diagnosis: hyperplastic polyp, conventional adenoma, SSL/TSA, T1a carcinoma, and T1b carcinoma. Resected specimens were examined by board‐certified pathologists at each participating institution, following the criteria of the Japanese Research Society for Cancer of the Colon and Rectum [20].

Diagnostic Accuracy

Overall diagnostic accuracy was defined as the proportion of cases where histological and endoscopic diagnoses matched. Sensitivity represented correctly diagnosed cases, specificity indicated correct exclusion of other categories, PPV reflected the proportion of matching endoscopic and histological diagnoses, and NPV represented correct exclusion of non‐target categories. Diagnostic accuracy and confidence were compared across the three approaches for both histological prediction and clinical diagnoses. Accuracy was also analyzed by confidence level within each approach.

Sample Size Calculation

Based on our previous study [13], expected diagnostic accuracy was 81% for the single, 80% for the dual, and 86% for the triple modality. Given the small accuracy differences, the sample size was calculated to assess whether the triple modality had a significant advantage. A total of 678 lesions were needed for 80% statistical power at a 5% significance level. With a 20% anticipated dropout rate, the final target was set at 847.

Statistical Analysis

The primary and secondary endpoints were diagnostic accuracy and confidence, respectively. Values were reported with 95% confidence intervals and compared across the three approaches using the chi‐squared test (p < 0.05). Post hoc pairwise comparisons used the Bonferroni correction (p < 0.016) for multiple comparisons. Diagnostic accuracy between confidence levels was compared at p < 0.05. In addition, subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate associations between confidence gain and lesion characteristics. The chi‐squared test or Fisher's exact test was used for categorical variables, and the unpaired t‐test for continuous variables. For lesion size, both overall comparisons across three categories (≤ 5, 6–9, ≥ 10 mm) and 2 × 2 comparisons between ≥ 10 and < 10 mm were performed using the chi‐squared test.

Results

A total of 885 colorectal lesions from 428 patients were assessed using three diagnostic modalities. All polyps were resected endoscopically or surgically. Four lesions were excluded due to tissue damage (n = 2) or sample loss (n = 2), and five were nontarget polyps (three inflammatory, two hamartomatous), leaving 876 polyps for analysis. Lesions were distributed as follows: right hemicolon (n = 501), left hemicolon (n = 291), and rectum (n = 84), with a mean size of 9.7 ± 9.2 mm. Pit pattern diagnosis was performed on 569 lesions using MCE and 307 using A‐NBIME. Histological diagnoses included 24 hyperplastic polyps, 654 conventional adenomas, 85 SSL/TSAs, 90 T1a carcinomas, and 23 T1b carcinomas. Representative cases are shown in Figures 2, 3, 4. No adverse events occurred during endoscopic examinations or treatments.

Comparison of Endoscopic Histological Prediction and Histological Diagnoses

Hyperplastic Polyp

Sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV were 50.0% (29.0–71.0), 96.9% (95.4–97.9), 31.6% (17.5–48.7), and 98.6% (97.3–99.3) for the single modality; 37.5% (18.8–59.4), 98.2% (96.9–99.0), 37.5% (18.8–59.4), and 98.2% (96.9–99.0) for the dual modality; and 50.0% (29.0–71.0), 98.4% (97.2–99.2), 48.0% (27.8–68.7), and 98.6% (97.4–99.3) for the triple modality. Sensitivity (p = 0.60) and PPV (p = 0.42) remained low across all approaches due to frequent misclassification as SSLs, while specificity (p = 0.06) and NPV (p = 0.75) remained high. No significant differences were observed among the three approaches for any of these measures.

Conventional Adenoma

Sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV were 90.3% (87.8–92.5), 83.8% (78.2–88.3), 94.3% (92.0–96.1), and 74.6% (68.4–80.0) for the single modality; 89.6% (87.0–91.9), 85.1% (79.7–89.6), 94.7% (92.5–96.4), and 73.5% (67.7–78.6) for the dual modality; and 90.5% (88.0–92.8), 88.2% (83.3–92.0), 95.8% (93.7–97.4), and 76.0% (70.3–81.2) for the triple modality. Sensitivity (p = 0.83) and PPV (p = 0.44) remained high across all approaches, whereas specificity (p = 0.37) and NPV (p = 0.81) were relatively low. No significant differences were observed among the three approaches for any of these measures.

SSL/TSA

Sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV were 74.1% (63.0–83.0), 98.3% (96.9–99.2), 82.2% (71.5–89.9), and 97.4% (96.0–98.5) for the single modality; 90.1% (81.5–95.6), 97.6% (96.0–98.7), 79.3% (69.4–86.6), and 99.0% (97.8–99.6) for the dual modality; and 86.4% (77.2–92.9), 97.9% (96.4–98.9), 80.5% (70.6–87.7), and 98.6% (97.4–99.3) for the triple modality. Sensitivity was significantly higher with the dual modality than the single modality (p < 0.016), while the triple modality showed a trend toward higher sensitivity (p = 0.048). NPV remained high, with significant differences among the three approaches (p < 0.05), though post hoc pairwise comparisons did not reach statistical significance. In contrast, specificity (p = 0.56) and PPV (p = 0.90) showed no significant differences among the three approaches.

T1a Carcinoma

Sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV were 66.6% (56.2–75.5), 93.3% (91.2–94.9), 53.1% (44.0–61.9), and 96.1% (94.4–97.3) for the single modality; 65.6% (55.1–74.9), 92.5% (90.4–94.2), 50.0% (41.0–59.0), and 95.9% (94.1–97.1) for the dual modality; and 76.7% (66.8–84.6), 93.5% (91.4–95.1), 57.5% (48.3–66.3), and 97.2% (95.6–98.3) for the triple modality. Sensitivity (p = 0.20) and PPV (p = 0.50) remained low across all approaches due to frequent misclassification as conventional adenomas, whereas specificity (p = 0.70) and NPV (p = 0.32) remained high. No significant differences were observed among the three approaches for any of these measures.

T1b Carcinoma

Sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV were 78.3% (56.3–92.5), 99.5% (98.8–99.8), 81.8% (59.7–94.8), and 99.4% (98.7–99.8) for the single modality; 73.9% (51.6–89.8), 99.6% (99.0–99.9), 85.0% (62.1–96.8), and 99.3% (98.6–99.7) for the dual modality; and 87.0% (66.4–97.2), 99.6% (99.0–99.9), 87.0% (66.4–97.2), and 99.6% (99.0–99.9) for the triple modality. Sensitivity (p = 0.20) remained relatively low across all approaches due to frequent misclassification as T1a carcinoma, whereas specificity (p = 0.70) and NPV (p = 0.32) remained consistently high. PPV also showed no significant differences among the three approaches (p = 0.50).

Histologic prediction accuracy was 84.9% (82.5–87.0), 85.3% (82.9–87.4), and 87.4% (85.2–89.3) for the single, dual, and triple modalities, respectively, showing slight but nonsignificant improvement with additional modalities (p = 0.35). Meanwhile, confidence in histological prediction varied. High‐confidence rates were 73.4% (70.4–76.2), 80.0% (77.3–82.5), and 83.6% (81.1–85.9) for the single, dual, and triple modalities, respectively. Confidence was significantly higher with dual and triple modalities than with the single (p < 0.016), while the triple showed a nonsignificant trend over the dual (p = 0.054).

Association Between Confidence Gain and Lesion Characteristics

Subgroup analyses were performed to identify factors associated with diagnostic confidence gain during modality transitions (Table S4). Lesions with confidence gain had a significantly larger mean size than those without gain in both the transition from single to dual modality (12.0 ± 10.5 mm vs. 9.3 ± 8.9 mm, p < 0.05) and from dual to triple modality (12.1 ± 13.0 mm vs. 9.5 ± 8.8 mm, p < 0.05). When stratified by lesion size, confidence gain was most frequently observed in lesions ≥ 10 mm. Chi‐squared testing demonstrated a significant difference in the single‐to‐dual transition (p < 0.05) and a nonsignificant trend in the dual‐to‐triple transition (p = 0.084). Macroscopic type and histology were significantly associated with diagnostic confidence in the single‐to‐dual transition (both p < 0.05), whereas no significant associations were observed in the dual‐to‐triple transition (p = 0.060 and p = 0.055). This distribution reflects the predominance of Is‐type adenomas—typically diagnosed with high confidence under WLE—in the without‐gain group.

Discussion

Accurate endoscopic diagnosis of colorectal polyps is essential for determining surveillance, endoscopic resection, or surgery. WLE depends on subjective parameters such as lesion size, morphology, color, and consistency, whereas NBIME emphasizes vascular patterns. MCE and A‐NBIME improve objectivity and reproducibility through standardized pit pattern criteria [12]. Most magnification endoscopy studies have compared still images between modalities using JNET or pit pattern classifications [21, 22]. In practice, histological prediction integrates multiple modalities, and clinical diagnoses may not always match classification‐based predictions [23, 24]. In this study, classifications served as references rather than absolute criteria, with final predictions based on expert judgment and multiple diagnostic methods. This prospective multicenter study is the first to assess the real‐time clinical utility of magnification endoscopy for treatment decision‐making in routine practice.

Sensitivity and PPV for hyperplastic polyps were low across all approaches due to selection bias, as resected lesions were often misidentified as SSLs. Histological differentiation between hyperplastic polyps and SSLs is challenging [25, 26], reducing endoscopic diagnostic accuracy. However, specificity remained high (96.9%–98.4%), indicating WLE alone can identify lesions requiring treatment. NBIME significantly improved SSL sensitivity, making it the only histologic category in which magnification endoscopy‐enhanced performance and may be particularly useful for distinguishing SSLs from hyperplastic polyps when WLE is insufficient.

Sensitivity for conventional adenomas was high across all approaches, with a high PPV reflecting their predominance among colorectal polyps. T1a carcinoma is difficult to distinguish from high‐grade adenomas both endoscopically and histologically [27, 28], lowering adenoma specificity and NPV as well as T1a carcinoma sensitivity and PPV. However, since both require endoscopic resection, the clinical impact is likely minimal. T1b carcinoma specificity and NPV exceeded 99% across all approaches, confirming WLE's reliability in excluding cases not requiring surgery.

Magnification endoscopy has minimal impact on diagnostic accuracy but markedly enhances confidence, increasing from 73.4% with WLE to 80.0% with NBIME and 83.6% with A‐NBIME. Higher confidence correlated with notable accuracy gains—about 30% for histologic prediction and 10% for clinical diagnosis. Globally, image‐enhanced endoscopy is often performed without magnification, and meta‐analyses report no significant sensitivity or specificity differences between NBI and NBIME [29]. However, NBIME improves confidence over non‐magnifying NBI [30], with studies showing substantial accuracy gains in high‐confidence cases [31, 32]. Confidence gain was most common in lesions ≥ 10 mm, likely reflecting greater histologic heterogeneity in larger lesions. In this study, adding magnification endoscopy to real‐world workflows improved confidence and accuracy in high‐confidence cases, aiding precise treatment decisions. We recommend a stepwise strategy in which WLE is followed by NBIME when confidence is low, and pit pattern analysis is added if it remains low, optimizing diagnostic yield while limiting procedural burden (Figure 5). In the future, integrating such multimodal information into AI‐based systems may further enhance accuracy and confidence.

Proposed stepwise endoscopic diagnostic strategy based on confidence level.

In this study, CV‐MCE and A‐NBIME were grouped under the triple modality category, reflecting institutional variability and operator preference during data collection. Traditionally, pit pattern diagnosis used CV‐MCE with crystal violet staining, but concerns about its carcinogenicity led to regulatory actions, including a 2019 Health Canada warning [33] and similar warnings in Japan [34]. A‐NBIME, by contrast, uses 1.5% acetic acid—a safer alternative with a lower concentration than household vinegar. Its small molecular weight and hydrophilic nature allow rapid penetration into mucus‐lined crypts, producing faster and clearer pit visualization than CV‐MCE [10, 35]. Thus, A‐NBIME enables rapid, high‐quality pit pattern assessment. Although differing in method and aim, Kinugasa et al. recently showed that acetic acid–indigo carmine chromoendoscopy significantly improved proximal SSL detection compared with WLE or indigo carmine alone [36], suggesting acetic acid–enhancement may aid both histologic assessment and lesion detectability.

This study has several limitations. First, pathological diagnoses were made by expert pathologists at each institution without centralized review, potentially introducing inter‐institutional variability, though this reflects the real‐world clinical setting in which treatment decisions are based on institutional pathology. Second, all procedures were performed by expert endoscopists, which may limit generalizability; studies involving non‐experts are needed. Third, while additional modalities increased diagnostic confidence and improved accuracy in high‐confidence cases, overall accuracy improved only modestly, suggesting limited benefit in routine practice and underscoring the need for multicenter validation with broader populations, centralized pathology review, and objective confidence metrics. Fourth, including multiple lesions from the same patient without adjusting for within‐patient correlation may have introduced clustering bias. Fifth, the ≥ 80% high‐confidence threshold was based on subjective estimation, which, although standardized among experts, may limit reproducibility. Lastly, grouping CV‐MCE and A‐NBIME in the same modality may have obscured subtle performance differences.

In conclusion, although magnification endoscopy has minimal impact on overall diagnostic accuracy for colorectal lesions, it significantly improves diagnostic confidence, yielding meaningful accuracy gains. Effective use of magnification endoscopy in routine practice enhances feasibility, diagnostic performance, and clinical decision‐making.
