Integrated single-cell, spatial, and bulk transcriptomics reveal a chromatin regulator-TME prognostic framework guiding precision therapy in cervical cancer
Authors: Xiong Tian, Rong Lin, Jiaqian Bao, Yixiao Pan, Jianbo Liu, Hanxi Zhou, Hangshuai Qu, Dali Hu, Na Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChromatin regulators (CRs) play a critical role in tumorigenesis, drug response, and prognosis, with dysregulation of chromatin regulator genes (CRGs) potentially disrupting the tumor immune microenvironment (TME) and influencing immune responses in cervical cancer. However, the prognostic and therapeutic implications of integrating CRGs and TME parameters in cervical cancer remain poorly understood.MethodsThis study used prognostic CRGs and TME cell signatures identified through Cox regression and Kaplan–Meier survival analyses to construct the CRG and TME score. The CRG score for each cell was calculated by integrating single-cell and spatial transcriptome, which were subsequently integrated to develop a CRG-TME classifier for prognostic prediction. The prognosis, somatic mutations, immune characteristics, therapeutic benefits, and drug sensitivity were then analyzed across CRG-TME defined subgroups.ResultsBoth CRG score and TME score demonstrated prognostic value, and the high CRG score group showed distinct alterations in TME signaling dynamics and increased intensity of intercellular communication. Patients in CRGlow/TMEhigh subgroup exhibited enhanced prognosis and therapeutic responses compared to other subgroups, attributable to variations in tumor somatic mutations, immune-related molecules, cancer signaling pathways and drug sensitivity. Additionally, the clinical sample verification and in vitro experiments demonstrated that the key prognostic gene TSPYL2 was downregulated in cervical cancer tissues and overexpression of TSPYL2 inhibited the malignant progression of cervical cancer cells via regulating MAPK pathway and immune escape.ConclusionsThis study develops a novel CRG-TME classifier to predict prognosis and therapy response in cervical cancer and unveils the inhibitory effect and underlying mechanism of TSPYL2 on cervical cancer progression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07085-y.Keywords: Cervical cancer, Chromatin regulators, Tumor immune microenvironment, Prognosis, Immunotherapy response, TSPYL2

Background

Cervical cancer, a malignant neoplasm arising from the cervix, ranks as the fourth most common cancer among women globally and is the fourth leading cause of cancer-related mortality in females, accounting for an estimated 604,000 new cases and 342,000 deaths reported worldwide in 2020 [1, 2]. In recent years, the incidence of cervical cancer has shown a concerning trend toward younger populations, with an annual increase exceeding 3% among young female adults and adolescents in China [3]. Despite advances in human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination, early screening and treatment of early stage diseases, a substantial proportion of patients are still diagnosed with advanced stages, recurrent or persistent cervical cancer [4–6]. For women with metastatic or recurrent cervical cancer, therapeutic options remain limited, and the prognosis is extremely poor, with a 5-year overall survival (OS) less than 20% [7], underscoring the urgent need to identify accurate molecular predictors and effective therapeutic targets of cervical cancer.

Epigenetic alterations driven by chromatin regulators (CRs) are a well-established hallmark of cancer [8]. CRs are a class of enzymes with specialized functional domains that recognize, establish, and maintain epigenetic states in a context-dependent manner. Functioning as essential upstream regulators in epigenetics, CRs are generally classified into three categories based on their roles: DNA methylators, histone-modifying factors, and chromatin remodelers [9–11]. Accumulating evidence indicates that aberrant expression of chromatin regulator genes (CRGs) is closely associated with diverse biological processes, including inflammation, immunity, cell proliferation, and apoptosis [12–14]. In addition, functional perturbations of CRGs have also been shown to play critical roles in the invasion, and metastasis of various cancers, including cervical cancer [15, 16]. A recent study demonstrated that abnormal expression of CRGs is preferentially involved in tumor progression rather than the initiation of cervical cancer, and shorten the survival of patients in collaboration with co-expressed genes [17]. Therefore, systematically evaluating the prognostic impact significance of CRGs may provide new insights into the treatment of cervical cancer.

The tumor microenvironment (TME) plays a pivotal role in the tumorigenesis, metastasis, and treatment of cervical cancer. It comprises tumor cells, immune cells, stromal cells, extracellular matrix (ECM), endothelial cells, and inflammatory mediators [18]. These cellular and non-cellular components within the TME undergo dynamic interactions and modifications, influencing key processes, such as tumor cell growth, metastasis, immune escape, and immune cell differentiation [19, 20]. A recent study has demonstrated that tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) progressively accumulate in the cervical TME with the increase of high-risk HPV integrated infection [21]. TAMs can activate the STAT3 signaling pathway and upregulate the expression of chemokines in lymphatic endothelial cells by secreting IL-10, thereby recruiting more macrophages and tumor cells and promoting efficient lymph node metastasis in cervical cancer [22]. Besides, tumor-infiltrating dendritic cells (DCs) expressing PD-1 are linked to advanced stages and CD8+ T cell inactivation [23], highlighting their critical role in tumor immune escape and disease progression in cervical cancer.

Strikingly, dysregulated expression of CRGs can disrupt the TME and affect immune responses. For instance, patients with uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma in the low CRG risk group exhibited elevated levels of immune infiltration and mutation burden, rendering them more sensitive to immunotherapy and chemotherapeutic agents [24]. Deletion of the chromatin regulator CHD1 has been shown to remodel the immunosuppressive TME in prostate cancer, exerting anti-tumor activity and suppressing tumor progression in PTEN-deficient models [25]. Combination of histone deacetylase and proteasome inhibitor was found to activate CD8+ T cells and enhance subsequent immune responses of cervical cancer in a murine model [26]. A comprehensive understanding of the relationship between TME cells and CRG may thus help identify new molecular biomarkers and therapeutic targets for cervical cancer.

To our knowledge, no studies have investigated the combined impact of CRGs and the TME landscape on the prognostic prediction and therapeutic benefits in cervical cancer. In this study, we constructed CRG and TME scores based on the identified CRGs and TME cells with prognostic value, and developed a novel CRG-TME classifier by integrating CRG and TME scores, which effectively stratifies cervical cancer patients into subgroups with distinct prognostic outcomes and immunotherapy responses. Differences in tumor somatic mutations, immune-related genes, cancer-related signaling pathways and drug sensitivities across CRG/TME subgroups also unveiled the value of the classifier. Further clinical sample verification and in vitro experiments demonstrated that the low expression of TSPYL2 in cervical cancer tissues, and high expression of TSPYL2 inhibited the malignant progression of cervical cancer through modulating MAPK pathway and immune escape.

Methods

Data source

The expression profiles and survival data of cervical cancer samples were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database, and the expression profile data were transformed by log2 (TPM + 1) for further analysis. The TCGA-CESC dataset with 309 cervical cancer samples was served as the training set for constructing the CRG score and TME score, while the GSE52903 dataset was served as an independent validation cohort with 55 cervical cancer and 17 normal samples, GSE39001 dataset as also served as an expression validation cohort with included 43 cervical cancer and 12 normal samples. In addition, we also retrieved single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data of 3 CESC tumors and 3 tumor-adjacent tissues from the ArrayExpress database (E-MTAB-11948) to visualize the CRG score of each cell.

Screening prognostic CRGs and TME cells

A total of 870 CRGs extracted from a published literature [8] underwent differential expression analysis using “limma” R package, and a set of 15 prognostic CRGs were finally identified to construct a CRG score through univariate Cox regression and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (lasso) regression analyses (Supplementary Table 1). Besides, the infiltration levels of 22 types of immune cells in both training and validation cohorts were evaluated by the CIBERSORT deconvolution algorithm. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed to determine the prognostic value of immune cells, and immune cell types with p-value < 0.05, including B cells naïve, CD8 T cells and T cells CD4 memory activated were selected to establish a TME score.

Construction of CRG score, TME score and the CRG-TME classifier

The establishment of CRG score and TME score was conducted based on the multivariate Cox regression coefficients (Coef) of the 15 CRGs and 3 TME cells in the TCGA dataset. To improve the accuracy of CRG and TME models, we randomly sampled all cervical cancer samples 1000 times, and conducted multivariate Cox analysis each time to further obtain the standard deviation (SD) values of Coef of each gene and cell, whose weight in the corresponding model depended on the ratio of SD value to Coef. Thereafter, tumor samples were divided into CRGlow/TMEhigh group, Mix group (CRGlow/TMElow and CRGhigh/TMEhigh) and the CRGhigh/TMElow group. The prognostic prediction capability of the classifier was assessed by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis.

Visualization of CRG score at the single-cell level

For clustering analysis, annotation, and visualization of the single-cell data, Seurat objects were generated for the scRNA-seq gene expression matrix. Cells expressing 200–2,500 genes with mitochondrial RNA content < 15% and hemoglobin gene expression < 3% were retained for analysis. Principal component (PC) analysis was then carried out to select the top 3000 highly variable genes for dimensionality reduction, and UMAP was used to visualize the data in two dimensions. Subsequently, we use FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions to identify different cell clusters, and annotated cell types on the basis of typical cell type markers reported by Bischoff et al. [27]. The immune cells, epithelial cells, and stromal cells were classified, and the CRG score for each cell was calculated according to the above formula of CRG score. Meanwhile, we also conducted cell communication analysis using “CellChat” R package.

Spatial transcriptome analysis

The spatial transcriptomic data of cervical cancer was acquired from the 10X Genomics Visium platform (https://www.10xgenomics.com/). The Raw data transformation was performed using the SCT algorithm, followed by dimensionality reduction, clustering, and visualization. Spatial transcriptomic deconvolution analysis employed the RCTD algorithm to predict cell type compositions at each spatial coordinate through integration with single-cell reference annotations. CRG score were systematically evaluated in each spatially resolved cell using the Seurat-package implemented AddModuleScore computational framework.

Gene set functional annotation and enrichment analysis

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed to explore the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways and Hallmark pathways potentially associated with CRG score and TME score. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was employed to identify gene modules affecting the CRG-TME classifier, and the top 5000 genes with absolute median deviation in the TCGA profile were included for WGCNA analysis. The biological functions and related pathways were discovered through Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG analysis, followed by the generation of protein maps by an online tool (https://proteomaps.net/).

Tumor immune cycle analysis

The cancer immunity cycle consists of the following seven steps: release of cancer cell antigens (Step 1), cancer antigen presentation (Step 2), priming and activation (Step 3), trafficking of immune cells to tumors (Step 4), infiltration of immune cells into tumors (Step 5), recognition of cancer cells by T cells (Step6), and killing of cancer cells (Step 7). Tracking tumor immunophenotype (TIP, http://biocc.hrbmu.edu.cn/TIP/), a web server for resolving tumor immunophenotype profiling, was used to analyze and visualize the cancer immunity cycle. The heatmap plot and boxplot display the differences in tumor immune cycle between different subgroups.

Tumor Somatic mutation, immune microenvironment and immune response

The mutation annotation format (MAF) data of TCGA-CESC was obtained from TCGA database, and the waterfall maps of top 20 mutant genes across CRG-TME subgroups were created through “maftools” R package. The tumor mutation burden (TMB) of each tumor sample was obtained by calculating the total number of somatic mutation per million bases in the tumor genome after removing germline mutations. Next, the expression levels of immunoregulatory genes were compared between CRG-TME subgroups, including immune checkpoint genes, immune activators, major histocompatibility complex (MHC), and chemokine receptors. Tumor immune dysfunction and exclusion (TIDE) is a database used to predict immune responses. We used TIDE to predict immune responses of patients in TCGA training cohort and GSE52903 validation cohort, then examined the relationship between immune response and the CRG-TME classifier. Furthermore, the differences in biological pathways among these subgroups were compared. IMvigor210 (urothelial cancer, n = 298) and GSE78220 (melanoma, n = 37) were used to confirm the feasibility and reliability of the CRG score to predict the ICI therapy response. Patients were categorized into two groups based on treatment outcomes: responders defined as those achieving complete response (CR) and partial response (PR), and non-responders defined as those experiencing progressive disease (PD) or stable disease (SD).

Drug sensitivity analysis

The Cancer Drug Sensitivity Genomics (GDSC) database comprised of two datasets about tumor cell sensitivity and response to drugs: GDSC1 and GDSC2. We used the above two datasets to predict the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of various chemotherapy drugs for patients in both the training and validation cohorts.

Cell culture and transfection

The human cervical cancer cell lines Hela and SiHa were obtained from the Cell Bank of Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China), All cells were cultured in R1640 medium with 10% FBS and 1% PS. The TSPYL2 overexpression plasmid and negative control was purchased from the GeneCopoeia (Guangzhou, China). The 2 μg plasmid was transfected into Hela and SiHa cells by Lipofectamine™ 3000 (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA) and cell function experiments were then performed 48 h after transfection.

RNA extraction and qPCR assasy

Total RNA was extracted using the Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Japan). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was conducted using SYBR (Takara, Japan), and the relative expression levels of TSPYL2 were normalized to GAPDH levels. The primer sequences were as follows: TSPYL2-F:5'-ACAGGTGCTGGCCGATATG-3', TSPYL2-R:5'-CCGACTCGATGGTAGAATCCC-3'; GAPDH-F:5′-GGAGCGAGAT CCCTCCAAAAT-3′, GAPDH-R:5′- GGCTGTTGTCATACTTCTCATGG-3′.

Cell proliferation assay

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Beyotime, China) was used to detect the proliferation of cervical cancer cells. 2 × 103 cells were inoculated into 96-well plates, and 10μL CCK-8 reagent was added into the plates at 24 h, 48 h, 72 h and 96 h, respectively, and incubated for 1 h at 37℃. The absorbance value was measured by 450 nm using a microplate reader (Spectramax ID3, USA).

Cell cloning assays

In total, 1 × 103 TSPYL2-transfected cells were inoculated to 6 cm dish and cultured for 8 days. The cells were immobilized with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with crystal violet, and photographed and counted.

Wound healing

The overexpression of TSPYL2 cells was cultured in 6-well plates until 90% confluence. A sterilized 100 μl pipette tip was applied to make a horizontal scratch in each well, Then, the shed cells were cleaned with a PBS buffer. The scratch width was recorded by microscope at 0 h and 24 h, respectively.

Transwell assay

Firstly, the upper chamber was coated with extracellular matrix gel (Corning, USA) and 1 × 104 TSPYL2-transfected cells were seeded into the upper chamber with 100 μl of serum-free medium. Then, 500 μl of RPMI-1640 medium with 20% FBS was added to the bottom chamber and incubated with the cells for 24 h. The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and counted by crystal violet staining.

Cervical cancer samples and immunohistochemical staining

Twenty paraffin-embedded cervical cancer samples and tumor-adjacent samples was obtained from the Department of Pathology, Taizhou Hospital Affiliated to Wenzhou Medical University. Informed consent was secured from all patients, and the study received approval from the Ethics Committee of Taizhou Hospital of Zhejiang Province (No. KL20240913). Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining was performed as follows: the tissue sections were dewaxed and hydrated, followed by 10% goat serum blocking. The samples were then incubated overnight with anti-TSPYL2 antibodies at a dilution of 1:800. The secondary antibody was applied on the next day, and the tissues were stained using diaminobenzidine.

Western blot

Western blotting was conducted by extracting proteins from cell samples using RIPA buffer and quantifying with a BCA assay. Proteins were separated via SDS-PAGE and transferred to a PVDF membrane. The membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk, and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with a diluted primary antibody, including anti-TSPYL2 (1:3000; 12,087–2-AP, proteintech), anti-ERK (1:1000; CY5487, abways), anti-p-ERK (1:1000; CY5277, abways), anti-P38 (1:2000; CY5262, abways), anti-p-P38 (1:500; BY0109, abways), anti-JNK (1:2000; YM8450, immunoway), anti-p-JNK (1:2000; YM8683, immunoway), and anti-GAPDH (1:10,000; 60,004–1-Ig, proteintech), followed by washes. After incubation with secondary antibodies, detection was performed using Image Quant LAS 500 system (GE, USA) and the Image J software was then utilized for gray value analysis.

Multiplex fluorescent immunohistochemistry (mIHC)

Tissue microassay slides from cervical cancer patients were purchased from Outdo Biotech Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China) and ethical approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Outdo Biotech Company (No. SHYJS-BC-2310001). mIHC staining of anti-TSPYL2 (12,087–2-AP, 1:100, proteintech), anti-PANCK (ZM-0069, 1:2000, ZSGB-BIO), anti-PD-L1 (13684S, 1:200, ZSGB-BIO), anti-CD3 (ab16669, 1:200, Abcam), anti-CD8 (66,868–1-ig, 1:4000, proteintech) and anti-PD1 (ZM-0381, 1:400, ZSGB-BIO) were conducted. Slides were counterstained with spectral DAPI (Akoya Biosciences) and mounted using anti-fade fluorescence mounting medium (ab104135, Abcam). Imaging was performed using a PANNORAMIC SCAN II System (3Dhistech, Hungary) at × 200 magnification.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analysis was completed on R 4.1.2 or GraphPad Prism (Version 8.0, CA, USA). Correlations between variables were analyzed using Pearson and Spearman methods. Comparisons between different subgroups were performed using nonparametric tests, including the Wilcoxon and Kruskal–Wallis rank sum tests. A p-value of < 0.05 was valuable.

Results

Identification of prognostic CRGs and TME cells

To establish a method evaluating the status of chromatin organization and immune cells in patients with cervical cancer, a TCGA training dataset (n = 309) and a validation cohort (n = 55) were enrolled in this study. The schematic diagram of the whole study is shown in Fig. 1. Differential expression analysis and univariate Cox analysis of 870 CRGs were performed in the training cohort and 18 CRGs were identified to be strongly relevant to the overall survival of cervical cancer patients (Fig. 2A–C). Lasso regression analysis ultimately screened 15 prognostic CRGs for establishing CRG score (Fig. 2D). In addition, we applied the CIBERSORT algorithm to assess the abundance of 22 types of immune cells in cervical cancer samples, and Kaplan–Meier survival curve analysis was then conducted to identify prognostic immune cells. After abandoning immune cells negatively correlated with prognosis, 3 immune cells positively associated with prognosis were involved in TME score construction, including naive B cell, T cell CD4 memory activated and CD8 T cell (Fig. 2E). We also analyzed the relationships between CRGs and TME cells, and results showed that most of CRGs was negatively correlated with TME cells (Figure S1).

Prognostic value and function assessment of CRG score and TME score

Subsequently, we constructed a CRG score and TME score based on the prognostic CRG and TME signatures. Cervical cancer patients were then divided into two subgroups based on the mean value of the CRG score and TME score, respectively. Survival analysis suggested that cervical cancer patients with high CRG scores had worse prognosis than those with low CRG scores, while the TME score was opposite (Fig. 2F, G). We also observed that CRG scores exhibited negative correlations with the majority of immune cells, such as CD8+ T cells, activated CD4+ memory T cells, and activated NK cells (Fig. 2H). GSEA analysis was then conducted to reveal the enriched KEGG pathways between low and high CRG and TME score groups, and the top 5 pathways were displayed in Fig. 2I, J. We found that pathways enriched in the CRG score group included ECM receptor interaction, focal adhesion, TGF-β signaling pathway and Pathway in cancer, which showed a positive relationship with CRG score. Conversely, TME scores were strongly inversely associated with these pathways. Besides, TME score was closely correlated with immune-related pathways, such as antigen processing, presentation pathways, allograft rejection and cell adhesion molecules CAM.

Evaluation of the functions of CRG score at single-cell landscape

To further investigate the role of CRG score within a single-cell landscape in cervical cancer and reveal the association between CRG score and different cell types, UMAP analysis of single-cell data of 3 tumor biospecimens and 3 tumor-adjacent samples was conducted, with each cell annotated based on known specific marker genes, such as endothelial cells, immune cells and stromal cells (Fig. 3A, B). The CRG score for each cell was calculated using the AddModuleScore function in Seurat, based on the average expression of 15 prognostic genes (Fig. 3C). Cells were then classified into low and high CRG score groups using a threshold determined by the median CRG score across all cells (Fig. 3D). The distribution of CRG scores at each cell level was also presented in order from highest to lowest (Fig. 3E). It is also easily observed that the CRG score was diminished in immune cells, such as CD8 T cells and NK cells, while the stromal cells had the highest CRG score. Immune cells exhibited significantly lower CRG scores than stromal or epithelial cells (Fig. 3F). Furthermore, we presented the analysis results of spatial transcriptomics samples, highlighting the differential distribution of immune, epithelial, and stromal cells, and calculated the CRG score for the spatial samples. Consistent with the single-cell analysis results, regions with high CRG scores were primarily concentrated in the stromal and epithelial areas (Fig. 3G).

Cell–cell communication analysis between high‑ and low‑CRG groups

Moreover, the CellChat algorithm was applied to construct intercellular communication networks between cancer samples with high and low CRG scores to elucidate the mechanisms underlying cell–cell interactions between two groups. Our analysis revealed an overall increase in the strength of cell–cell interactions in the high CRG score group (Fig. 4A, B). Specifically, interactions between fibroblasts and stromal cells with CD8⁺ T cells, monocytes, NK cells, and macrophages were markedly enhanced in the high CRG score group (Fig. 4C, D). Furthermore, comparative pathway analysis revealed significant differences in signaling activity between the high and low CRG score groups. Key signaling pathways were significantly upregulated in the high-score group, including MHC-I, MIF, SPP1, FN1, VEGF, and PTN (Fig. 4E, F). We further investigated intercellular communication patterns among epithelial cells, stromal cells, fibroblasts, and key immune cell subsets (CD8⁺ T cells, B cells, NK cells and monocytes). As shown in Fig. 4G, in the high-score group, epithelial cell interactions with CD8⁺ T cells were strengthened through critical tumor progression-related signaling pathways, such as THBS1-CD47, PTN-NCL, MIF (CD74 + CXCR4), COL1A1 (ITGA1-ITGB1) and COL1A2 (ITGA1-ITGB1). In addition, stromal cells exhibited enhanced communication with CD8⁺ T cells via MIF (CD74-CD44), LAMB1-CD44 and LAMB1 (ITGA1 + ITGB1). Similarly, fibroblast-to-CD8⁺ T cell signaling was enhanced through MIF (CD74-CXCR4), while fibroblasts and monocytes displayed elevated FN1-CD44 interactions. Conversely, immune cell communication pathways, including ANXA1-FPR1, ANXA1-FPR2, and MDK-NCL were significantly attenuated in the high CRG score group, suggesting distinct alterations in TME signaling dynamics.

CRG-TME classifier was an independent prognostic indicator for cervical cancer

Based on the above findings, we attempted to integrate CRG score with TME score to contemporaneously characterize the CRG and immune status of cervical cancer, and developed a CRG-TME classifier based on the median value of CRG score and TME score for each cohort, which classified cervical cancer patients into four subgroups: CRGhigh/TMElow, CRGlow/TMEhigh, CRGhigh/TMEhigh and CRGlow/TMElow. Kaplan–Meier curve analysis was used to evaluate the prognostic value of the CRG-TME classifier, and results indicated that both the CRG score and TME score contribute significantly to the prognosis. Patients in the CRGhigh/TMElow subgroup had the worst prognosis in comparison to patients in the other three subgroups, while the CRGlow/TMEhigh subgroup demonstrated the best prognostic value (Fig. 5A). Through further prognostic analysis, we found that the prognosis of CRGhigh/TMEhigh patients lied intermediate between CRGhigh/TMElow and CRGlow/TMEhigh (Figure S2A). Hallmark gene enrichment analysis revealed that CRGlow/TMEhigh group displayed enriched immune-related pathways, such as interferon gamma response, and inflammatory response (Figure S2B) and CRGhigh/TMElow group displayed enriched pathways associated with tumor progression, such as epithelial-mesenchymal transition, angiogenesis, TGF-β signaling and glycolysis (Figure S2C), while CRGhigh/TMEhigh exhibited enrichment in both tumor progression pathways and immune-regulatory pathways (Figure S2D). These findings suggested that CRGhigh/TMEhigh represented an intermediate phenotype in terms of both prognosis and biological regulatory pathways.

Next, we combined the CRGhigh/TMEhigh and CRGlow/TMElow subgroups as a mixed subgroup, and the prognostic value of the CRG-TME classifier was further confirmed in both the training and validation cohorts (Fig. 5B, C). FGSEA analysis revealed distinct pathway enrichments among the subgroups. The CRGhigh/TMElow subgroup was enriched in wound healing regulation, collagen metabolism, and cell–cell junction assembly. The CRGlow/TMEhigh subgroup showed enrichment in leukocyte adhesion, leukocyte-mediated immunity, and NK cell proliferation. The mixed subgroups were associated with hormone transport and epithelial cell proliferation. These findings reflect the unique biological processes of each subgroup (Fig. 5G). Besides, we analyzed gene modules strongly associated with the classifier through WGCNA analysis, and revealed that the yellow and blue module genes were highly correlated with the classifier (Fig. 5D–F), which were further selected for functional enrichment analysis. As illustrated in 5H and 5I, the yellow module genes were showed enriched biological processes, including cell adhesion, migration, angiogenesis and pathways in cancer, while the blue module genes showed a close correlation with immune response, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction and chemokine signaling pathway.

Then, ROC analysis was performed to estimate the predictive capacity of the CRG-TME classifier, and the data illustrated that the classifier could reasonably predict the 3-, 5- and 7-year overall survival with AUCs of 0.693, 0.727 and 0.757, respectively (Fig. 6A). The multivariate Cox regression analysis conducted in the training and validation cohorts suggested that the CRG-TME classifier was an adverse prognostic factor for cervical cancer (Fig. 6B, C). Of note, the CRG-TME classifier also demonstrated a good performance in predicting prognosis in patients with distinct clinical subtypes, including age, TMN, and grade (Fig. 6D–L). Additionally, its effectiveness was validated in both HPV-positive and negative cohorts, showing worse outcomes for patients in the CRGhigh/TMElow subgroup (Figure S2E, F). Similarly, the classifier demonstrated strong stratification in patients with squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma (Figure S2G, H).

Distinct patterns of tumor somatic mutations among CRG-TME subgroups

We next explored the somatic mutation landscape among CRG-TME subgroups, and the top 20 variant mutations in CRGhigh/TMElow and CRGlow/TMEhigh groups were identified and represented in Fig. 7A. The CRGlow/TMEhigh subgroup showed a higher gene mutation rate than the CRGhigh/TMElow subgroup (89.98% vs.78.41%), and 19 out of 20 mutations exhibited a higher mutation frequency in the CRGlow/TMEhigh subgroup. The mutation frequency of titin (TTN) and histone lysine (K)-specific methyltransferase 2C (KMT2C) was significantly comparable between two subgroups. Patients in the CRGlow/TMEhigh subgroup with TTN mutation might have the best survival outcomes (Fig. 7B, C). Besides, we calculated the TMB score of each cancer sample, and found that the CRGlow/TMEhigh subgroup showed higher TMB than the CRGhigh/TMElow and mixed subgroups (Fig. 7D), indicating that patients in the CRGlow/TMEhigh subgroup may be more sensitivity to immunotherapy. As expected, survival analysis demonstrated that patients in the CRGlow/TMEhigh + TMBhigh subgroup showed best survival outcomes than other subgroups (Fig. 7E).

Different immune landscapes in tumors among CRG-TME subgroups

By analyzing the immune cell cycle, we observed that the CRGlow/TMEhigh group had higher scores for recruiting T cells, CD4 T cells, CD8 T cells and Th1 cells, and these key immune cell recruitment processes showed significant differences when compared to the CRGhigh/TMElow group (Fig. 8A, B). Then, the expression profiles of genes related to immune response among different subgroups were further explored from five aspects, including immune checkpoint genes, immune activation genes, major histocompatibility complex (MHC), chemokines and chemokine receptors (Fig. 8C–G). It was observed that the expression of 15 immune checkpoint genes, such as BTLA, CD160, IDO1, CD274 and PDCD1, was higher in the CRGlow/TMEhigh subgroup than that in the mixed group and the CRGhigh/TMElow subgroups. Besides, the CRGlow/TMEhigh subgroup exhibited significantly elevated levels of most immune-activating genes, chemokines, chemokine receptors and all MHC compared to the other two subgroups. These findings suggested that the CRG-TME classifier was closely associated with tumor-related immune processes and immunoregulatory genes.

CRG-TME classifier-guided treatment strategies

To determine whether the CRG-TME classifier could predict clinical responses in cervical cancer patients undergoing immunotherapy, TIDE was applied to evaluate the predictive ability of the CRG-TME classifier. Compared to the non-responder group, the CRG scores of patients in the responder group decreased, while the TME scores were upregulated (Fig. 9A, B). The CRGlow/TMEhigh had highest percentage (79%) of patients who responded to immune checkpoint blockade, while the percentage of patients from mixed and CRGhigh/TMElow subgroups were 31% and 55%, respectively (Fig. 9C). Similar results were observed in the GSE52903 validation cohorts (Fig. 9D–F), suggesting that CRGlow/TMEhigh subgroup patients were more likely to benefit from immunotherapy. Furthermore, we validated the significance of the CRG score in two real-world immunotherapy cohorts, including the IMvigor210 cohort (urothelial cancer, n = 298) and the GSE78220 cohort (melanoma, n = 37). Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed that high-risk patients receiving immunotherapy exhibited poorer OS (Figure S3A). In addition, CRG score were markedly reduced in responders (CR/PR) compared to non-responders (PD/SD) (p < 0.001; Figure S3B). High-risk individuals showed markedly lower clinical response rates to PD-L1 blockade therapy compared to low-risk individuals (Figure S3C). A similar trend was observed in the GSE78220 validation cohort (Figure S3D, E). These results revealed the potential significance of stratifying cervical cancer patients who may benefit from immunotherapy. Moreover, the Proteomaps were utilized to visually reveal the underlying mechanisms of CRG-TME classifier among subgroups. Intriguingly, the proteomes of CRGlow/TMEhigh were quite similar to that of the responder group, and high similarity was observed between the CRGhigh/TMElow and non-responder groups (Fig. 9G). Moreover, the CRGhigh/TMElow subgroup was more sensitive to chemotherapeutic agents, such as ERK 2440, SCH772984 and Ulixertinib, while the CRGlow/TMEhigh subgroup was more sensitive to Cisplatin, Gemcitabine and Cyclophosphamide (Fig. 9H). These results indicated that the CRG-TME classifier could effectively reflect the TME and could be used to stratify cervical cancer patients before immunotherapy.

Validation of the function of the prognostic gene TSPYL2 in cervical cancer

Through Friends analysis, we analyzed the associative significance of 15 genes using their functional information, and identified TSPYL2 as one of the 15 most important genes in prognostic models (Fig. 10A). Therefore, we analyzed the expression of TSPYL2 in normal tissues and cervical cancer tissues using multiple databases, and the results showed that the expression of TSPYL2 is downregulated in cervical cancer (Fig. 10B). KM survival analysis indicated that patients with low expression of TSPYL2 had worse prognosis (Fig. 10C). Immunohistochemical results validated the significantly declined expression of TSPYL2 in cervical cancer samples compared to adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 10D, E). The TSPYL2 overexpression plasmid was then constructed and transfected into Hela and SiHa cells, and the TSPYL2 overexpression efficiency was further verified by western blot (Fig. 10F). CCK-8 assays revealed that overexpression of TSPYL2 significantly inhibited the proliferation of cervical cancer cells (Fig. 10G). Colony formation assays demonstrated that high expression of TSPYL2 suppressed the colony-forming ability of cervical cancer cells (Fig. 10H). Furthermore, scratch and transwell assays indicated that overexpression of TSPYL2 markedly inhibited the migration and invasion capabilities of cervical cancer cells (Fig. 10I, J).

Further analysis of TSPYL2-related genes through enrichment analysis revealed that they were enriched in the MAPK signaling pathway (Fig. 11A). Western blot analysis confirmed that overexpression of TSPYL2 significantly inhibits the phosphorylation of proteins associated with the MAPK and JNK signaling pathways, including p-ERK, p-P38, and p-JNK (Fig. 11B, C). Moreover, mIHC analysis indicated that the infiltrating levels of CD8 T cells were significantly enhanced in samples with high expression of TSPYL2, while the levels of PD-L1-positive cells were markedly reduced. In contrast, samples with low TSPYL2 expression showed the opposite pattern (Fig. 11F, G). These findings revealed that TSPYL2 might function as an important tumor suppressor in cervical cancer and its high expression exhibited a significant inhibitory effect on MAPK pathway and immune escape.

Discussion

CRs are key epigenetic regulators and play crucial roles in the progression of various cancers. The abnormal expression of CRs can affect the malignant behaviors of cancer cells and contribute to poor outcomes of malignancies, including cervical cancer [28], highlighting the potential of CRs as reliable predictors of prognosis. In addition, by regulating the genes mediating tumor immunogenicity and influencing tumor-infiltrating immune cells, CRG-related machinery can modulate the TME and ultimately affect antitumor-immune responses [29, 30], making them potential targets for combination therapy with immune interventions. In fact, a strong response to epigenetic drugs combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors has been observed in mouse models [31]. These observations motivate us to systematically explore the clinical implications of the integrated CRG and TME signatures in cervical cancer, which has not yet been reported.

In this study, we first constructed the CRG scores and TME scores for cervical cancer prognostic prediction based on screened prognostic CRGs and TME cells using bulk RNA-seq data, and found that the decreased CRG score implies favorable prognosis and enhanced immunity, whereas the TME score was the opposite. GSEA analysis revealed that pathways affecting the development and progression of cervical cancer, such as ECM receptor interaction, focal adhesion, antigen processing and presentation, and pathways in cancer were significantly associated with both the CRG scores and TME scores. Crucially, through scRNA-seq and spatial transcriptomics integration, we achieved the localization of CRG score at the cellular level and observed that the CRG score was lower in immune cells compared to stromal and epithelial cells, Besides, our results showed that single-cell cell-communication analysis bridged bulk RNA-seq findings with cellular behaviors, and revealed enhanced cellular communications among epithelial, stromal, and immune cells driven by regulatory signals, such as THBS1-CD47, MIF and LAMB1 signals, in high-CRG score groups, probably explaining the immune-suppressed microenvironment identified by bulk RNA-seq. Macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF) is a cytokine that has been shown to promote tumor progression and tumor immune evasion, and the overexpression of MIF was found to be involved in the tumorigenesis and progression of cervical cancer [32, 33], and specific inhibition of the THBS1/CD47 interaction with antagonist peptides reduced the invasion of cancer cell invasion [34]. It was found that the expression of LAMB1 was up-regulated in the high-risk group of cervical cancer and negatively correlated with immune cell infiltration in the tumor microenvironment [35]. Our cell communication results also found that these receptor-ligand signals were significantly enhanced in high CRG score group, which means that the stromal cells, epithelial cells, and fibroblast cells in the CRG high score group directly or indirectly affect immune cells through these pathways, thus influencing the immune microenvironment of cervical cancer.

Given the close association of the CRG score and TME cells, we further developed a CRG-TME classifier by integrating CRG scores and TME scores, and divided cervical cancer patients into CRGhigh/TMElow, CRGlow/TMEhigh, CRGhigh/TMEhigh and CRGlow/TMElow subgroups. Patients in the CRGhigh/TMElow subgroup showed the worst prognosis and treatment responses and the CRGlow/TMEhigh subgroup had the best prognosis, while the CRGhigh/TMEhigh group showed an intermediate survival outcomes between the other two subtypes. Similar findings were observed in Hallmark pathway analysis, revealing an intermediate phenotype of CRGhigh/TMEhigh subgroup. Importantly, we combined the CRGhigh/TMEhigh and CRGlow/TMElow subgroups as a mixed subgroup, and further confirmed the classifier’s prognostic value in both training and validation cohorts.

To elucidate the underlying mechanisms of the CRG-TME classifier in cervical cancer, Go analysis of the module genes strong relevant to the classifier displayed enriched biological processes involving tumor metastasis, such as cell adhesion, cell migration and angiogenesis, and KEGG analysis indicated that pathways in cancer, focal adhesion, ECM-receptor interaction and TNF-ɑ signaling pathway were enriched. ECM receptor interaction pathways are critical for tumor shedding, adhesion, differentiation and proliferation, and the dysregulation of ECM genes can promote cancer invasion, metastasis and progression in a variety of cancer, including cervical cancer [36–39]. The high expression of desmoglein-2 maintaining cell–cell adhesion was found to promote the migration and invasion of cervical cancer cells by regulating mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPK) pathway [40]. TNF-ɑ could promote tumorigenesis and lymphatic metastasis in cervical cancer by activating AKT and ERK pathways [41]. These findings revealed the importance of cellular pathways or genes associated with the CRG-TME classifier in the tumorigenesis and malignant behaviors of cervical cancer.

Through further survival analysis, we attempted to evaluate the capacity of the CRG-TME classifier in predicting prognosis, and revealed that the classifier served as an adverse prognostic factor for cervical cancer and could reasonably predict the long-term survivals of patients. Our findings also demonstrated that the CRG-TME classifier effectively distinguished different outcomes among patients with distinct clinical subtypes, such as age, TMN, and grade. HPV infection is recognized as the primary cause of cervical cancer, resulting in over 90% of cervical cancer cases [42, 43]. The predictive value of the classifier was then assessed in both HPV-positive and negative cohorts, and we found that patients in CRGhigh/TMElow subgroup showed worse outcomes. Although HPV is an important factor in the occurrence and progression of cervical cancer, our classifier seems not to be affected by HPV infection, as a good separating effect displayed in both HPV-positive and HPV-negative cases. Meanwhile, it also exhibited a strong performance in stratifying patients with squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma. Our findings may provide reference for clinical decision making of cervical cancer.

Besides, the investigation of the somatic mutation characteristics among different CRG-TME groups demonstrated that the CRG-TME classifier could also reflected the heterogeneity of DNA. A combination of TTN mutation status and the CRG-TME classifier showed a better prognostic prediction in cervical cancer patients, which further supported the previous findings that mutant TTN was correlated with favorable overall survival and efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors [44, 45]. It is widely known that the immune cells within TME play vital roles in tumorigenesis, tumor progression, recurrence and metastasis, and will affect the immunotherapy responses and clinical outcomes of cancer patients [46–48]. Patients in the CRGlow/TMEhigh subgroup exhibited higher recruitment levels of CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, Th1 cells, and NK cells into the tumor microenvironment (TME), indicating enhanced immune cell trafficking at Step4 of the tumor immunity cycle and suggesting an immunologically activated state [49]. Immune checkpoint plays a key role in immune escape of tumor cells and blockade of immune checkpoint, such as PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors, can benefit cervical cancer patients [50, 51]. Our results revealed the distinct expression patterns of immune checkpoint genes, activating markers, MHC molecules and receptors in different CRG-TME subgroups, suggesting that antitumor immune responses might differ among subgroups. Similar results observed in the training and validation cohorts indicated that more percentages of patients in the CRGlow/TMEhigh subgroup responded to immunotherapy, verified by the high similarity of the proteomaps between CRGlow/TMEhigh and responder groups. Besides, we verified the significance of the classifier in two real-world immunotherapy cohorts undergoing anti-PD-L1 therapy, and revealed its effective capacity in distinguishing different prognosis among patients received immunotherapy, and observed that patients classified as low-risk experienced substantial benefits compared to high-risk patients. These findings underscored the potential value of CRG-TME classifier in predicting the benefits of cervical cancer patients undergoing immunotherapy.

The sensitivity of chemotherapeutic agents was then estimated among three subgroups and a high sensitivity to ERK 2440, SCH772984 and Ulixertinib was found in CRGhigh/TMElow subroup. Interestingly, these drugs are inhibitors of the MAPK signaling pathway. MAPK cascades are critical in regulating tumor cell proliferation, survival, angiogenesis and drug resistance, and affected cancer development and progression [52]. Several drugs targeting the inhibition of MAPK pathway have been shown promising anticancer activity in cancer treatment and potential synergistic effects when combined with immunotherapy in several types of cancers [53, 54]. Thus, our results may provide new perspectives for cervical cancer treatment.

In this study, our findings also identify TSPYL2 as the most significant contributing factor of cervical cancer survival through Friends analysis, which plays a crucial role in the regulation of cell cycle, cell proliferation, DNA damage repair and gene transcription and translation [55–58]. It acts as a tumor suppressor in lung cancer, colorectal cancer, and thyroid cancer, and its downregulation or loss of function has been linked to tumor progression and drug resistance [59, 60]. To elucidate the role of TSPYL2 in cervical cancer, a series of validation experiments were conducted, and our results confirmed that the markedly decreased expression of TSPYL2 in cervical cancer tissues predicted poor prognosis, and overexpression of TSPYL2 inhibited the proliferation, migration and invasion of cervical cancer cells. To further explore the underlying mechanisms of TSPYL2’s function, we conducted GESA analysis of TSPYL2-related genes and observed the enrichment of MAPK signaling pathway. Overexpression of TSPYL2 in Hela and SiHa cell lines significantly suppressed the MAPK and JNK signaling pathways. Moreover, as observed in both immune cell infiltration analysis and mIHC staining, TSPYL2 was positively associated with CD8+ T cell infiltration and negatively correlated with PD-L1-positive cell, highlighting the important role of TSPYL2 in regulating immune escape within the tumor microenvironment. These results suggested that TSPYL2 could be a suppressor of cervical cancer progression by regulating the MAPK pathway and might serve as a key regulatory gene of immune infiltration, thereby affecting the prognosis for immunotherapy of cervical cancer patients. However, there are certain limitations in our study. First, the prognostic prediction ability of CRG-TME classifier was verified only based on the public datasets, an in-house cohort is needed for further evaluation of the classifier's capacity. Second, the biological function of key CRG-TME signature genes in the pathogenesis of cervical cancer still needs to be further explored through in vitro or in vivo experiments. Taken together, we developed a novel CRG-TME classifier with good predictive value for prognosis and therapeutic responses, offering a potential tool for personalized treatment strategies of cervical cancer. Our study aids in understanding the interplay between CRGs and TME in cervical cancer and exploring novel prognostic predictor for patients with cervical cancer.

Conclusions

This study systematically elucidates the prognostic and therapeutic implications of chromatin regulator genes (CRGs) and their interplay with the tumor microenvironment (TME) in cervical cancer. By constructing a CRG-TME classifier based on integrated CRG and TME scores, we identified distinct subgroups with divergent clinical outcomes, immune landscapes, and therapeutic responses. Mechanistically, the CRG-TME classifier was linked to key pathways driving tumor progression, including ECM-receptor interaction, focal adhesion, and TNF-α signaling, and reflected somatic mutation heterogeneity. Further, TSPYL2 emerged as a critical tumor suppressor in cervical cancer and a marker of immune infiltration, validated through functional experiments demonstrating its role in inhibiting the malignant progression of cervical cancer cells via modulating the MAPK pathway and immune escape. our findings highlight the classifier’s potential as a robust tool for prognostic stratification and personalized treatment guidance. This work advances the understanding of epigenetic-TME crosstalk in cervical cancer and provides a framework for integrating multi-dimensional molecular features to optimize clinical decision-making and therapeutic strategies.
