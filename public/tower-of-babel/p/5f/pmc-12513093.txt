Clinical application value of nanopore-targeted sequencing technology for primary spine infection: a mulicenter, retrospective observational study
Authors: Zengshuai Han, Qiang Zhang, Zhaohui Li, Chuqiang Yin, Jinlong Ma, Yuelei Wang, Feng Shen, Tengbo Yu, Ting Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEtiological diagnosis of primary spinal infections (PSI) is critical for ensuring precise treatment. Currently, the positive rate of microbial culture in clinical practice remains low. Recently, nanopore-targeted sequencing (NTS) has demonstrated significant advantages in the etiological diagnosis of infections at other sites.PurposeTo evaluate the application value of NTS in detecting pathogens from PSI.MethodsIn this multicenter retrospective study, the clinical data of 169 patients with suspected spinal infection who were admitted to Affiliated Hospital of Qingdao University, Qingdao Municipal Hospital, Jinan Public Health Medical Center from January 2019 to January 2024. According to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, all patients underwent blood and tissue samples collection after admission, samples were subjected to traditional laboratory testing protocols and NTS test. Paired χ2 test (Mcnemar test) was used to compare the positive rate of microbial culture and NTS. The kappa value was used to compare the consistency between the results of NTS and culture.ResultsAmong the 169 cases, 147 were diagnosed as spinal infections, while 22 were classified as noninfectious according to the histopathological results. Microbiological evidence was found in 98 cases, while 49 cases were clinically diagnosed as spinal infections without definitive microbiological evidence. the detection rate of the pathogen is 66.7% (98/147). The positive rate of NTS was significantly higher at 80.3% (118/147) compared with microbiological culture. (38.1%, 56/147) (X^2=60.02, P < 0.001). At the genus level, the positive percentage (PPA) consistency between NTS and microbial culture was 98.2%(55/56), the negative percentage consistency (NPA) was 31.9% (29/91), and the overall percentage (OPA) consistency was 57.1% (84/147). The consistency of the results of all the two assays was low (kappa = 0.263).ConclusionsThis study highlights the diagnostic value of NTS testing in PSI. However, microbial culture remains indispensable for clinical testing. NTS serves as a complementary approach to traditional culture testing, providing higher positive rate and shorter detection time, rendering it of immense diagnostic in the realm of spinal infection diseases.Study designA retrospective, observational study.Keywords: Primary spine infection, Nanopore-targeted sequencing, Traditional laboratory testing protocols, Microbial culture, Pathogen detection

Introduction

Primary spinal infections (PSI) are destructive diseases of the intervertebral space and paravertebral tissues caused by a variety of microorganisms, including bacteria, tuberculosis, brucella, and fungi. The incidence of PSI has been estimated to be 2.4/100,000 per year, and the incidence is increasing every year due to the advancements in diagnostic technology [1, 2]. However, the insidious onset of the disease, the lack of specific clinical symptoms pose a huge diagnostic challenge, and improper treatment can easily lead to catastrophic consequence.

Currently, the diagnosis of PSI mainly includes clinical manifestations and signs, blood test results (such as C-reactive protein [CRP], procalcitonin [PCT], and erythrocyte sedimentation rate [ESR]), imaging examination (X-ray, computed tomography[CT], magnetic resonance imaging [MRI]), histopathology, and microbial culture [3, 4]. Although MRI can be effective in diagnosing PSI, it is widely believed that the most critical diagnosis to assist clinicians identify the pathogenic microorganism early is microbial culture [5, 6]. However, traditional microorganism culture technology has several drawbacks, including a lengthy process, low sensitivity and specificity, potential antibiotic interference, insensitivity to rare microorganisms, in addition, traditional media did not identify mycobacterium tuberculosis, which is common in spinal infections [7, 8]. In recent years, gene sequencing technology has been widely used in the diagnosis of infectious diseases, theoretically allowing for the rapid and unbiased detection of all known pathogens from clinical samples. The advent of metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) technology has revolutionized the field of clinical mi-crobiology, enabling rapid and accurate identification of pathogens directly from clinical samples [9, 10]. However, the mNGS has a short read, a long sequencing time, and the uncertain accuracy, which requires further innovation. Nanopore-targeted sequencing (NTS), a type of third-generation sequencing technology, has gained attention due to its portability, long read lengths, and real-time data output. This technology harness-es the electrical signal alterations that occur as DNA or RNA molecules transit through a nanopore for genomic sequencing, effectively merging the advantages of long read lengths from first-generation sequencing with the high throughput of mNGS [11, 12]. It has shown great potential in the diagnosis of PSI. However, the application of NTS in the diagnosis of PSI has been reported far less often than mNGS.

Therefore, we retrospectively analyzed the multi-center database of patients with suspected spinal infection from the Affiliated Hospital of Qingdao University, Qingdao Municipal Hospital, Jinan Public Health Center, to evaluate the diagnostic value of NTS in PSI.

Materials and methods

Study population

This study retrospectively analyzed 169 patients with suspected spinal infection based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria from the Affiliated Hospital of Qingdao University, Qingdao Municipal Hospital, and Jinan Public Health Center from January 2019 to January 2024.

Patients were required to meet the following inclusion criteria: (1) Patients with suspected spinal infection based on clinical manifestations, blood test indicators, and imaging examination; (2) Specimens can be obtained by C-arm fluoroscopy or CT-guided puncture, or open surgery; (3) NTS detection and a package of laboratory tests were performed (Fig. 1);

(4) A suspected spinal infection is cdefined as new or aggravated pain accompanied by at least one of the following criteria:①clinical manifestations such as fever or neurological deficits; ② abnormal increase in CRP, ESR, PCT, or other indicators of infection; ③ imaging findings on CT or MRI suggestive of infection, including vertebral endplate destruction, intervertebral disc inflammation, necrosis or pus in the intervertebral disc space, paravertebral soft tissue and epidural space;④ a favorable response to antibiotic therapy [13].

Patients who met the following conditions were excluded from this study: (1) specimens were obviously contaminated during sampling, transport, and processing, or cold chain transport failed. At the same time, a spine surgeon must be present and supervise the sampling process. Samples for spinal infections must be collected from within a sealed body cavity, without any contact with the skin. After collection, the sample should be placed directly into a sterile specimen bottle, sealed, and preserved on dry ice. It should be transported via cold chain immediately and subjected to NTS testing within 24 h. (2) The following situations are considered definitive indicators of contamination: sampling from a conduit or cavity that is connected to the external environment; contact with skin, gauze, or any other container during sampling; failure to preserve the sample under dry ice conditions, transport taking longer than 24 h, or premature depletion of dry ice; damage to the sterile specimen bottle or breakage of its seal. (3) all routine inspections and NTS tests were not completed. (4) Incomplete medical information of patients clinical records.

Research design

Blood and tissue samples were collected from all enrolled patients. Blood specimens were obtained upon admission, while tissue samples were harvested by experienced spinal surgeons at the central region of the lesion tissue through either open surgery or CT- and C-arm-guided puncture. All samples were placed in sterile culture containers, sealed, and stored in a refrigerator at −80 °C. To ensure sample integrity, samples were transported to the testing facility within 2 h under sterile and low-temperature conditions. Paired samples from each patient were used for both traditional laboratory testing protocols [14] (Fig. 1) and NTS test.

Diagnostic criteria

Clinical diagnostic criteria for PSI

The final clinical diagnosis of primary spinal infection is based on histopathologic testing. Histopathology positivity is defined as follows.①the demonstration of acute, chronic, or granulomatous inflammation, suppurative inflammation, including inflammatory infiltration, congested or thrombosed blood vessels, bone necrosis, and/or infectious organisms;②In tuberculous PSI, histological findings include the presence of granulomatous inflammatory, caseating necrosis and giant cell formation with or without a positive stain for acid-fast bacilli; ③brucellar PSI is characterized by noncaseating granulomas;④fungal infections cause granulomatous changes without caseation, fungal spores may or may not be found [13, 14].

Positive criteria of diseased microorganisms

(1) Bacteria: positive for bacterial culture or blood culture. (2) Brucella: the initial screening of the Tiger red plate agglutination Test was positive, and the titer of the SAT was more than 1: 160, or Brucella culture was positive. (3) Tuberculosis: positive for culture, acid-fast smear, pathology, or PCR. (4) Fungi: any positive smear, G or GM test, culture, or pathology [15–17].

Positive criteria for NTS detection

(1) It was necessary to establish specific criteria for pathogens identification. Pathogens were classified at the species level based on the percentage of coverage and abundance score. Generally, the sequence coverage is ≥ 80% and the relative abundance score is ≥ 0.5% were classified as pathogens. Mycobacterium tuberculosis was considered positive when at least one read was mapped to either the species or genus level [18, 19].

Comparison of the diagnostic performance of NTS and microbial culture for PSI

Our analysis revealed that the primary spinal infection rate is 87.0%(147/169), the detection rate of the pathogen is 66.7%(98/147), the positive rate of NTS was remarkably high at 80.3% (118/147) compared with the modest 38.1% (56/147) for microbial cultures. These findings indicated that the positive rate of NTS was significantly higher than that of microbial culture (X^2=60.02, P < 0.001). 56 samples were positive on both the microbial culture results and NTS tests, while 1 patient was inconsistent between the NTS and culture test and 29 samples were negative on both microbial culture results and NTS tests. At the genus level, the positive percentage (PPA) consistency between NTS and microbial culture was 98.2%(55/56), the negative percentage consistency (NPA) was 31.9% (29/91), and the overall percentage (OPA) consistency was 57.1% (84/147). Because of the low positive rate of culture, the consistency of the results of all the two assays was low (kappa = 0.263). (Table 3) (Fig. 3). Among 56 positive culture samples, the culture results of 55 samples are completely consistent with NTS. 1 samples was inconsistent between NTS and culture results. (Fig. 4).

Disscussion

Identifying the pathogen is crucial for the treatment of primary spinal infections. Currently, pathological examination is still the gold standard for.

diagnosing spinal infection, and microbial culture is the direct evidence of spinal infection, which has a positive detection rate ranging only from 20–60% [22, 23]. In addition, the culture of tuberculosis typically requires two months, while the culture of brucella generally takes four weeks [24, 25]. Furthermore, some rare microorganisms are not cultivable under current laboratory conditions. Consequently, the time-consuming nature of these processes contributes to a lower positivity rate in culture-based diagnostics. Given that conventional detection methods cannot meet the needs of clinical diagnosis, so NTS is an unbiased approach for pathogen detection that allows for universal pathogen detection regardless of the type of pathogen (tuberculosis, bacteria, fungi, and brucella) [26]. At present, the application of NTS in infection mainly includes diagnosis of bacterial meningitis, bacterial lower respiratory tract infection, infective endocarditis, pneumonia and infection in prosthetic joints, it has not been confirmed in the diagnosis of primary spinal infection [27]. In our study, the data from various medical centers were retrospectively analyzed to assess the suitability of NTS in diagnosing PSI. Given the large patients cohort and the involvement of multiple medical centers, this study offers robust support for the initial application of NTS in PSI.

The results of this study showed that the positive rate of microbial culture in patients with PSI was 38.1%, which was significantly lower than the positive rate of NTS detection, which was 80.3%. We analyzed that the lower culture positive rate can be attributed to several factors. Firstly, tuberculosis, brucellosis, and fungal infections constituted a proportion of cases, which are inherently challenging to culture. Secondly, intracellular and anaerobic bacteria require extended culture times, further contributing to the lower positive rates. Additionally, prior antibiotic usage has been shown to inhibit the growth of pathogenic bacteria, thereby reducing the likelihood of successful cultures. We have 1 case of NTS result are not consistent with the result to cultivate, which NTS results show that for the Enterobacter cloacae, culture result as Salmonella enteroides, but the result of the second bacterial culture was still Salmonella enteroides, and we subsequent use anti-gram-negative-bacillus drug therapy, the prognosis is good. The Brucella Serum Agglutination Test results of another patient showed brucella, and the NTS result was Streptococcus pseudopneumoniae. Considering that we used doxycycline combined with rifampicin anti-infection treatment, and the patient had a good prognosis, so we believed that this patient have a Brucella infection. Another patient’s G, GM test and pathology results were fungus, the NTS result was Microonas microbiota, Considering the low positive rate of fungi in culture, we used amphotericin B anti-infection treatment, and the patient had a good prognosis. The pathological findings, in conjunction with the treatment efficacy, suggest a strong inclination towards the diagnosis of a fungal infection in the patient. Among the 98 cases of pathogens clearly defined in the traditional laboratory testing protocols, the consistency between culture and traditional laboratory testing protocols was 57.1% (56/98), and the consistency between NTS and traditional laboratory testing protocols was 94.9% (93/98). The consistency between NTS and traditional laboratory testing protocols detection is significantly better than that of culture. Among the 49 cases of pathogens with unclear clinical diagnoses, NTS can effectively provide diagnostic basis. NTS can detect certain tuberculosis, brucella or fungi that are difficult to culture. NTS detection does not rely on culture, and its results only depend on the DNA content of pathogenic microorganisms. A recent study with 218 cases of pulmonary infection, NTS outperformed conventional microbial testing, identifying more pathogens with a sensitivity increase of 47.9% (77.2% vs. 29.3%) [28], In the diagnosis of intracranial tuberculosis, the sensitivity and specificity of NTS were 60.0% and 95.5% [29]. The various results of NTS may result from significant differences among laboratories, sequencing platforms, analysis strategies.

Our experience indicates that the integration of traditional laboratory testing protocols with NTS can offer an effective treatment approach for patients with PSI. Open surgery for debridement is only necessary in cases with compression symptoms, most patients have undergone transpedicular or transforaminal C-arm guided puncture biopsy. To enhance the detection rate, it is advisable to obtain tissue samples under the guidance of professional spinal surgeons using C-arm fluoroscopy or open surgery, rather than CT guided puncture biopsy. During the progression from admission through treatment to recovery, the empirical medication timeframe typically spans 3 to 7 days [14]. This duration can be particularly intolerable for patients whose infection symptoms worsen or progressive neurological symptoms. Therefore, for pathogen with prolonged culture times or those that are challenging to culture, NTS detection enables clinicians to initiate targeted medication within 24 to 48 h.

The NTS method we have researched integrates nanopore technology with targeted sequence detection. Firstly, it overcomes the limitation of short read lengths inherent to traditional targeted sequencing platforms, which often fail to obtain complete marker genes necessary for distinguishing pathogens. This issue arises because marker genes of varying sizes cannot be effectively incorporated into a single gene database, thereby precluding simultaneous detection of bacteria, fungi, and tuberculosis. In contrast, DNA molecules of different lengths can traverse nanopores, enabling the inclusion of diverse marker genes within the same gene library regardless of their size. Consequently, due to its reliance on targeted amplification of DNA with long read lengths that are less prone to interference, NTS achieves highly accurate detection results [26]. Secondly, the nanopore sequencer employed in our study features a mobile cell equipped with 512 channels, each containing 4 nanopores, thus providing a total of 2048 nanopores for DNA sequencing. This multiplexing capability accelerates turnaround times and markedly reduces per-sample sequencing durations. Additionally, NTS operates by monitoring changes in the current as DNA molecules pass through the nanopore, enabling real-time determination of nucleotide sequences and modifications. This mechanism facilitates the detection of rare microorganisms or those that become inactive post-sequencing. The time for NTS is 24–48 h, which is considerably shorter than the cultural period. If the NTS testing process exceeds this timeframe, retesting of the specimen is essential to avoid contamination.

The application of NTS in clinical practice faces several limitations that need to be addressed. Firstly, due to the diversity of sequencing platforms and protocols, variations in samples, and the presence of background microbial profiles, it is essential to establish standardized norms and interpretive guidelines for NTS sequencing results. Secondly, regarding antimicrobial resistance, NTS provides significantly less information compared to traditional microbial cultures. Samples from spinal infections typically consist of lesion tissues, where human DNA accounts for more than 99%, leaving only a minimal proportion of microbial DNA. This scarcity makes it challenging to obtain sufficient data on drug resistance【30】. Therefore, further advancements in host DNA depletion techniques and transcriptome sequencing are necessary to enhance the detection of resistance-related information. Additionally, NTS detection relies heavily on both the “quantity” and “quality” of pathogen nucleic acids, which imposes higher demands on nucleic acid extraction methods. Achieving unbiased and efficient extraction of pathogen nucleic acids remains a significant technical challenge. Moreover, the high cost associated with NTS currently hinders its widespread adoption in clinical settings. Finally, the current pathogen reference databases lack comprehensiveness, particularly the lack of bioinformatics tools specifically designed for diagnostic purposes.

Conclusions

The key to the clinical treatment of PSI is to accurately identify the pathogens and to treat it early. Microbial culture remains an essential tool in clinical practice. As a complementary approach to culture detection, NTS can facilitate the rapid identification of pathogens, thereby enhancing the detection rate of pathogens, particularly rare or atypical pathogens. Our results show that NTS has a high positive rate in the detection of spinal infections and has good application prospects in the diagnosis of spinal infections.
