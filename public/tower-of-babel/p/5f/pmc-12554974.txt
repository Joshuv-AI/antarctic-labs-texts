Effects of different packaging on dried sword prawn (Parapenaeopsis hardwickii) quality, flavor, microbial communities, and lipid profiles during chilled storage: An integrated analysis
Authors: Wenxiong Zheng, Feili Zhan, Shanshan Shui, Ronglin Yang, Yi Hu, Zhengxuan Qin, Soottawat Benjakul, Bin Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study examined the impact of common packaging (CP), vacuum packaging (VP), and nitrogen packaging (NP) on muscle quality, volatile compounds, microbial communities, and lipid profiles in dried Parapenaeopsis hardwickii during 120 d of storage at 4 °C, using physicochemical analysis, HS-SPME-GC–MS, high-throughput sequencing, and lipidomics. As storage time increased, the L*, a*, W, hardness, springiness, chewiness, and gumminess of dried shrimp muscle gradually decreased, while thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS), total volatile basic nitrogen (TVB-N), and total viable count (TVC) gradually increased, accompanied by widening and disorganization of the gaps between the muscle fibre bundles, indicating that the dried shrimp muscle underwent varying levels of deterioration during chilled storage. However, the rate of quality deterioration in the VP and NP groups was lower than that of the CP group. Additionally, 10 key volatile flavor compounds (ROAV > 1) were identified during chilled storage. Six dominant bacteria and 25 key differential lipids played a crucial role in the quality changes and flavor formation of dried shrimp. The results showed that VP and NP effectively extended the shelf life of dried shrimp, with lipid oxidation, protein degradation, and microbial activity likely contributing to muscle quality decline, color deterioration, and key flavor formation. This study adopted a multi-omics approach, particularly the combined application of volatile metabolomics and lipidomics, to investigate dried P. hardwickii with different packaging during chilled storage, providing a theoretical basis for future research on flavor control, packaging, and preservation of dried shrimp.

Introduction

The sword prawn (Parapenaeopsis hardwickii) is the most economically valuable species within its genus, distributed along the Indo-Western Pacific coast (Tzeng, 2007). P. hardwickii is highly regarded by consumers for its rich protein and fatty acid content as well as its tender meat. However, fresh sword prawns are highly susceptible to spoilage shortly after capture due to the activity of endogenous enzymes and microbial growth, significantly impacting their marketability. Therefore, processing is crucial to the development of the sword prawn industry. Currently, sword prawns are mainly processed into products such as shrimp cakes, drunk shrimp, and dried shrimp (Zheng et al., 2025). Nevertheless, there are relatively few processed products derived from P. hardwickii, and research on the drying of this species is limited.

Dried shrimp has become one of the most exported seafood products globally. However, it is also prone to quality deterioration during storage. To extend the shelf life of aquatic products, common preservation methods include freezing, refrigeration, and packaging technologies. Although freezing and refrigeration delay spoilage in aquatic products by inhibiting microbial growth and enzyme activity, microbial activity may still lead to quality deterioration over time. (Jitpasutham et al., 2024). Among protective strategies, vacuum packaging (VP) effectively blocked oxidative reactions (Chu et al., 2024), nitrogen packaging (NP) offered a safe and economical modified-atmosphere alternative (Kitabayashi et al., 2019), and coating fresh-keeping packaging (CFKP) delivered potent antioxidant and antibacterial effects (Chen et al., 2021). Despite these advantages, research on packaging for dried shrimp, particularly the synergistic effects of packaging and chilled storage, is limited.

Volatile organic compounds (VOCs) served as indirect index of quality, headspace solid-phase microextraction coupled with gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (HS-SPME-GC–MS) had proven essential for monitoring these markers and refining storage protocols. During processing and storage, lipid oxidation, enzymatic activity, and microbial catabolism generated distinctive VOCs, with microorganisms recognized as a principal driver of flavor change (Broekaert et al., 2013). High-throughput sequencing (HTS) has emerged as a rapid, precise tool for profiling microbial communities. Huang and Xie (2020) used HTS to isolate 78 bacterial strains from hybrid grouper stored at 4 °C, mostly Pseudomonas, whose microbial activity produced off-odors from nitrogenous bases, aldehydes, ketones, alcohols, and esters. (Parlapani et al., 2014). Previous studies had linked Pseudomonas proliferation to the accumulation of 3-methyl-1-butanol and 2-ethyl-1-hexanol (Gram, 2009). Clarifying the interplay between VOCs and microbial consortia in chilled, differently packaged dried shrimp therefore promised to illuminate the mechanisms underlying flavor evolution.

Lipids constituted critical flavor precursors. Zhang et al. (2011) demonstrated that hydrolysis of muscle lipids released free fatty acids, which subsequently oxidized to hydroperoxides and then fragmented into aldehydes, ketones, and alcohols. Ultra-high performance liquid chromatography - tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS)-based lipidomics has become the method of choice for tracking these transformations. Wang et al. (2024) observed a marked decline in most phospholipids in stored Penaeus vannamei, with triglycerides (TGs) and diacylglycerols (DGs) rich in 18:1 and 18:2 fatty acids decreasing sharply while lysophospholipids accumulated. Given the established link between lipid profiles and flavor (Zheng et al., 2025), mapping the relationships between lipids and VOCs in chilled, packaged dried shrimp was expected to reveal how lipid dynamics shaped sensory attributes.

In summary, this study investigated the muscle quality, microbial community, and lipid profile of dried P. hardwickii under common packaging (CP), VP, and NP during chilled storage, and clarified the mechanisms underlying flavor evolution. Correlations among physicochemical indicators, main microorganisms, and key volatile flavor compounds, as well as between those volatiles and critical lipids, were examined. The findings provided a reference and data foundation for optimizing packaging and refrigeration strategies to prolong shelf life and control flavor quality in dried shrimp.

Materials and methods

Chemical reagents

Sodium chloride (NaCl), hydrochloric acid (HCl), paraformaldehyde solution, methanol-chloroform mixture, and chloroform were procured from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, China). The malondialdehyde (MDA) assay kit was obtained from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China).

Shrimp samples and treatments

Fresh specimens of P. hardwickii (weight: 13 ± 1 g, width: 0.7 ± 0.2 cm, length: 8 ± 1 cm) were purchased in December 2024 from Zhoushan International Aquatic City (Zhoushan, China). The shrimp were promptly transported to the laboratory within 30 min. Upon arrival, the shrimp were rinsed with cold water at 4 °C. They were then immersed in a 3 % (m/v) NaCl solution and boiled for 3 min before being placed on a metal mesh to remove excess surface moisture. Subsequently, the shrimp were transferred to a drying oven (BPG-9106 A, Shanghai Yiheng Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd., China) set to 57 °C for 14 h (Zheng et al., 2025).

The dried shrimp samples were then subjected to three different packaging treatments: CP (ordinary sealed bag), VP (vacuuming), and NP (nitrogen-filled with 99.999 % purity). The packaged samples were stored in a refrigerator (RSA2SQSW, Suzhou Samsung Electronics, China) at 4 °C for 0, 30, 60, 90, and 120 d. During the storage period, samples for determining basic physicochemical indicators were collected every 30 d. Additionally, samples for determining microstructure, VOCs, microbial community composition, and lipid profiles were collected every 60 d.

Appearance and sensory evaluation analyses

The appearance of dried shrimp samples was documented via photography under uniform illumination. Sensory evaluation was performed in accordance with the Chinese industry standard SC/T 3220–2016 (2016). A panel of 10 graduate students, all professionally trained and thoroughly screened in accordance with the ISO 11132:2021, evaluated the samples. The specific criteria for sensory evaluation were outlined in Table S1. Other requirements can be found in the ethical statement.

Color analysis

The color of the dried shrimp samples was detected following the methodology described by Zheng et al. (2024). The L*, a*, and b* values were recorded using a colorimeter (MODEL, Shenzhen Weifu Optoelectronic Technology Co., Ltd., China), with the second abdominal segment of P. hardwickii serving as the test point. The whiteness value (W) was calculated in accordance with Formula (1).(1)W=100−100−L∗2+a∗2+b∗2

Textural properties analysis

The textural of the dried shrimp samples was measured using the method reported by Wu et al. (2023). The samples were examined with a texture analyzer (TMS-PRO, FTC, USA) fitted with a TA/0.5S spherical probe. The operational parameters were configured as follows: pre-test, test, and post-test speeds set at 1 mm/s, a deformation of 55 %, a trigger force of 5 g, and a test distance of 5 mm.

pH analysis

The pH of the dried shrimp samples was determined in accordance with GB 5009.237–2016 (2016). Briefly, the dried shrimp samples were crushed, and 1 g of the sample was weighed into a beaker and mixed with 10 mL of pure water. The mixture was homogenized at 5000 r/min for 2 min using a high-speed homogenizer (FJ200-S, Hunan Lichen Instrument Technology Co., Ltd., China), allowed to stand for 0.5 h, and then filtered and measured.

Thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) analysis

The TBARS of dried shrimp muscle was assessed using a MDA assay kit. Dried shrimp samples were processed according to the kit's instructions, then heated in a 95 °C water bath for 40 min, cooled with cold water, and centrifuged at 4000 r/min for 10 min (HC-1016, Anhui Zhongkejia Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd., China), and the supernatant was collected. The absorbance of the final reaction mixture was detected at 532 nm using a UV–vis spectrophotometer (TU-1810, Beijing Puxi General Instrument Co., Ltd., China), and the TBARS value was calculated as per the manufacturer's protocol (Wu et al., 2023).

Total volatile basic nitrogen (TVB-N) analysis

The TVB-N content was measured following the method reported by Wu et al. (2023). Dried shrimp samples (10 g) were accurately weighed into a distillation tube, mixed with pure water (75 mL), shaken, and immersed (0.5 h). Subsequently, the magnesium oxide (1 g) was added to the distillation tube, and boric acid was used as the receiving liquid. The determination was performed using an automatic kjeldahl nitrogen analyzer (K9840, Shandong Haineng Instrument Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd., China). Methyl red-bromocresol green was used as the indicator, and a standard titration solution of 0.1 mol/L HCl was employed for titration. The experimental data were then recorded.

Total viable count (TVC) analysis

TVC was measured following GB 4789.2–2022 (2022). Dried shrimp samples (25 g) were accurately weighed, placed in a container with sterile saline (225 mL), and then homogenized. Subsequently, one to three appropriately diluted sample homogenateswere selected, and 1 mL of each was transferred to a sterile petri dish. The cooled plate count medium (46–50 °C) was added to the petri dish, which was then inverted and incubated at 30 °C for 72 ± 2 h.

Histological analysis

The microstructure of dried shrimp muscle with different packaging treatments during chilled storage was evaluated using hematoxylin-eosin (HE), masson staining, and scanning electron microscope (SEM). HE- and Masson-stained samples were observed at 200× magnification, while SEM samples were observed at a 500× magnification.

VOCs analysis

The experimental operation was conducted according to the method described by Luo et al. (2025). VOCs were detected using HS-SPME-GC–MS to characterize changes in the flavor of dried shrimp samples with different packaging treatments during chilled storage. The analysis was performed using an Agilent 8890 gas chromatography with an 7697 A headspace sampler coupled to an Agilent 5977B mass selective detector (Agilent, USA).

High-throughput sequence analysis

The microbial community composition of dried shrimp was measured in accordance with Dong et al. (2024). The quality of the extracted genomic DNA was assessed by 1 % agarose gel electrophoresis, and the DNA concentration and purity were determined using a NanoDrop® ND-2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific Inc., USA). The purified PCR product was sequenced using the NEXTFLEX Rapid DNA-Seq Kit. The library was constructed and sequenced using the Illumina Nextseq2000 platform (Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology CO. Ltd., Shanghai, China). All data analysis was performed on the Majorbio cloud platform.

LC-MS/MS-based lipidomics analysis

Lipidomics analysis was performed on samples refrigerated for 0, 60, and 120 d to investigate the changes in lipid mass spectrometry of dried shrimp with three packaging methods during chilled storage. Lipids were extracted from dried shrimp muscle tissue using a chloroform/methanol (2:1, v/v; −20 °C) solvent solution, as described by Tu et al. (2022). The extracted lipids were used for subsequent lipidomic analysis. The LC-MS/MS analysis was conducted using a Thermo UHPLC-Q Exactive HF-X Vanquish Horizon system.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using Origin 2022 (OriginLab Corp, Northampton, MA, USA) and IBM SPSS Statistics 26 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Two-tailed Pearson correlation coefficient tests were used to assess the correlations between basic physicochemical indicators, main microorganisms, and key volatile flavors, as well as the relationships between key volatile flavors and key differential lipids. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to evaluate significant differences, with Duncan's post-hoc test applied for pairwise comparisons. Statistical significance was determined at P < 0.05. Data were presented as the means ± standard deviation of the measurements for 3 replicates (n = 3).

Sensory evaluation is a vital method for assessing the quality of aquatic products. As depicted in Fig. 1B-D, the sensory quality of dried shrimp in all packaging groups declined as storage time increased, likely due to fat oxidation and microbial activity, resulting in off-flavors and color deterioration. Moreover, at 60 and 120 d of storage, the scores for appearance, odor, texture, and taste were higher in the VP and NP groups than in the CP group, as reflected in the appearance results in Fig. 1A.

The chromaticity changes shown in Fig. 1E-H indicated that, as storage time increased, the L*, a*, and W values of dried P. hardwickii treated with all three packaging methods decreased, while the b* value increased. Typically, the color of dried P. hardwickii changed from light red to dark yellow during storage, likely due to the deterioration of the dried shrimp over time (Cyprian et al., 2017), which further supported the appearance results shown in Fig. 1A. However, the degree of change in the VP and NP groups was lower than that in the CP group (P < 0.05), indicating that VP and NP treatments could delay the deterioration of dried shrimp quality, inhibit astaxanthin degradation, and contribute to better preservation. Jitpasutham et al. (2024) found that a green chitosan film with emulsified cinnamon oil nanoemulsion inhibited astaxanthin degradation and lipid oxidation in dried shrimp (Macrobrachium equidens), producing similar results to this study.

Texture change

Texture can be used to assess the extent of spoilage and deterioration in aquatic products. As illustrated in Fig. 1I-L, the hardness, springiness, chewiness, and gumminess of dried shrimp in all three packaging groups diminished as storage time extended. However, the decline was more pronounced in the CP group compared to the VP and NP groups (P < 0.05). This disparity was likely attributable to the continuous oxidative degradation of muscle protein in the CP group, driven by microbial and enzymatic activity, which subsequently resulted in a decline intexture characteristics (Bi et al., 2021). Similarly, Li, Liu, et al. (2020) reported that the texture of ready-to-eat shrimp (Macrobrachium nipponense) deteriorated over time during storage, aligning with this study's results.

pH change

pH is a key metric for reflecting changes in the storage quality of aquatic products and their processed items. As for Fig. 1M, during the storage process, the pH of the dried shrimp in all three packaging methods exhibited a pattern of first decreasing, then increasing, and finally decreasing again. This pattern may be attributed to the degradation of ATP and anaerobic glycolysis in the muscles of dried shrimp during the initial storage period (0–30 d), which produce acidic compounds such as phosphoric acid and lactic acid, causing a reduction in pH (Zhu et al., 2024). Subsequently, the pH of dried shrimp in the CP, VP, and NP groups gradually increased and reached maximum values at 90 d, which were 8.45 ± 0.03, 8.34 ± 0.03, and 8.40 ± 0.02, respectively. This increase might be due to heightened microbial activity and the production of alkaline compounds such as ammonia and trimethylamine by endogenous enzymes (Liu et al., 2013). Thereafter, the accumulation of organic acids from anaerobic fermentation reversed the trend, causing a secondary pH decrease. The amplitude of these fluctuations was markedly smaller in the VP and NP groups than in the CP group, confirming that vacuum and nitrogen atmospheres retarded spoilage and aligned with the texture findings. Comparable pH patterns were reported by Wattana et al. (2022) for VP-stored, cooked, and peeled Harpiosquilla raphidea during refrigeration.

TBARS change

The TBARS value serves as a metric for the content of MDA. During storage, unsaturated lipids in the shrimp underwent progressive oxidation and cleavage, generating MDA as a secondary product (Wu et al., 2023). Consequently, TBARS levels rose steadily over time in all packaging treatments (Fig. 1N), a trend attributed to the liberation of free fatty acids by bacterial lipase and phospholipase (Wu et al., 2023). Under identical chilled conditions, the CP group exhibited higher TBARS values than the VP and NP groups (P < 0.05), indicating accelerated lipid oxidation. This rapid deterioration was attributed to the accumulation of unstable peroxides from unsaturated fatty acids, which decomposed into alcohols, aldehydes, ketones, and short-chain hydrocarbons (Zhao et al., 2020).

TVB-N and TVC changes

TVB-N, an indicator of alkaline nitrogenous spoilage products such as ammonia and biogenic amines, was generated through the proteolytic action of endogenous enzymes and bacteria. Fig. 1O illustratedan initial TVB-N concentration of5.77 ± 0.40 mg/100 g in the dried shrimp.Over the storage period, this value rose steadily across all packaging treatments, with the ascent being more pronounced in the CP group than in the VP and NP groups (P < 0.05). Lan et al. (2022) attributed such increases to the growth of putrefactive bacteria and the consequent increase in microbial degradation products. The slower accumulation observed under VP and NP suggested that these atmospheres effectively suppressed microbial proteolysis, thereby preserving shrimp quality and extending shelf life.

TVC followed a similar pattern. Counts escalated in all treatments, yet remained significantly lower in VP and NP than in CP throughout the 120-day period. The nutrient-rich matrix of dried shrimp, particularly its high protein content, provided an ideal substrate for psychrotrophic spoilage organisms (Jia et al., 2019). By altering the surrounding gas composition, VP through oxygen removal and NP through nitrogen displacement, both treatments created an anaerobic environment that curtailed the growth of aerobic bacteria (Zhang, Huang, et al., 2025). This inhibition of microbial proliferation directly corresponded to the moderated rise in TVB-N levels observed under VP and NP.

SEM (Fig. 2A) revealed cracks within the muscle fibre bundles of samples stored for 0 d, an effect traced to prolonged drying that steadily removed moisture, denatured fibrillar proteins, and provoked fibre shrinkage (Zheng et al., 2025). VOCs analysis

Detection results of VOCs

Principal component analysis (PCA) was utilized to visualize the differences in VOCs among dried shrimp samples. Fig. S1A, D, and G illustrateed that the dried shrimp samples from the three packaging types were distinctly separated in the PCA score plot, indicating significant differences in the VOCs (P < 0.05). To achieve more effective lipid separation performance for shrimp samples and further eliminate irrelevant variables such as noise signals, projections of latent structure-discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) were employed for additional analysis. The results demonstrated that the model was stable and reliable, with good predictive capabilities (Fig. S1B, E, and H). Moreover, no overfitting phenomenon was observed (P < 0.05), confirming the model's suitability for analyzing flavor metabolite differences among dried shrimp samples with different packaging during storage.

Fig. 3A and Table S2 revealed the types of VOCs in dried shrimp samples. A total of 299 VOCs were detected, including hydrocarbons (58, 19.4 %), organic heterocyclic compounds (53, 17.73 %), ketones (44, 14.72 %), aldehydes (34, 11.37 %), esters (26, 8.7 %), alcohols (25, 8.36 %), and others. The relative contents of VOCs in dried shrimp with three packaging methods were shown in Fig. 3B and Table S3. With the extension of storage time, the contents of aldehydes and alcohols in dried shrimp samples treated with the three packaging types gradually increased, while the contents of hydrocarbons gradually decreased. The contents of phenols, terpenoids, acids, and phenyl ethers first decreased and then increased. Volcano plot analysis, a type of univariate statistical method and one of the most commonly used statistical analysis techniques, was conducted. The red dots and the blue dots represented significantly upregulated and downregulated VOCs, respectively, while gray dots indicated no significant difference in VOCs (Fig. 3C-K). With increased storage time, the number of up-regulated differential metabolites in dried shrimp with different packaging treatments was significantly greater than that of down-regulated VOCs. The CP group (120 d vs. 0 d) had 237 up-regulated VOCs, significantly more than the VP group (191) and NP group (156) (Fig. 3E, H, and K). This phenomenon suggested that both storage time and packaging treatments significantly affected the VOCs of dried shrimp.

To more intuitively observe the changes in VOCs in the samples, we selected the VOCs with the top 38 abundances for cluster analysis to achieve clearer, more stable, and biologically interpretable clustering results. The CP samples showed more pronounced relative expression levels than the VP and NP samples, especially in the red box area (Fig. 3L). The three types of packaged dried shrimp were clearly divided into two major clusters: VP and NP samples clustered together, while CP samples formed a separate cluster. This suggested that the VOCs in the VP and NP samples exhibited similar trends, whereas the CP samples displayed a distinct trend compared to the other two packaging types. This further highlighted significant differences in metabolites between the VP and NP samples and the CP samples. Additionally, the biological replicate samples clustered together, indicating strong consistency among replicates and further confirming the high reliability of the data and the effectiveness of the analytical method.

Relative odor activity value (ROAV) changes

ROAV is used to assess the extent to which a certain VOC contributes to the overall aroma of food, with higher ROAV typically signifying a more pronounced contribution to the sample's overall flavor profile. A total of 10 VOCs with an ROAV >1 were identified across the dried shrimp samples (Table S4). These compounds, which included 5 aldehydes (2,4-Decadienal, Hexanal, Isovaleraldehyde, Valeraldehyde, and Trans-2,Cis-6-Nonadienal), 2 ketones (1-Octen-3-One, 1-Hepten-3-One), 2 alcohols (1-Octen-3-Ol, Cis-5-Octen-1-Ol), and 1 organoheterocyclic compound (2-Pentylpyridine), were deemed the principal VOCs influencing dried shrimp flavor during storage. Notably, 1-Octen-3-One and 1-Hepten-3-One imparted negative flavors, whereas the remaining eight compounds enhanced the flavor positively. This suggested that dried shrimp samples may exhibit a complex odor profile, encompassing both desirable notes like fat, nuts, and melons, and less favorable ones such as soil, metal, and spoilage.

Lipids in meat can catalyze the decomposition of linoleic acid via lipoxygenase and hydroperoxidase, a process recognized as the primary pathway for alcohol compound formation in meat (Ma et al., 2012). 1-Octen-3-Ol, an oxidation product of 12-Hydroxyperoxyarachidonic acid, results from the thermal degradation of n-3 and n-6 polyunsaturated fatty acids. Although alcohol compounds have a relatively high sensory threshold and thus contribute less to the overall flavor of dried shrimp, they may still exert a synergistic effect on the final taste.

Aldehyde compounds, characterized by their low sensory threshold, typically enhance the aroma of meat products and are key volatile components in meat flavorings. Hexanal and 2,4-Decadienal, along with Valeraldehyde, arise from the auto-oxidation of linoleic acid and oleic acid. The auto-oxidation of linoleic acid produces 1,3-Hydroperoxides and 9-Hydroperoxides, which further decompose into form Hexanal and 2, 4-Decadienal, respectively, along with other by-products like oxygenated fatty acids. Sohail et al. (2022) noted that low concentrations of Valeraldehyde contribute positively to dried shrimp flavor, whereas high concentrations can intensify the fishy smell typical of aquatic products. Isovaleraldehyde, derived from isovaleric acid through reduction or dehydration, imparts a unique aroma.

Ketone compounds, which are mainly formed through the oxidation of unsaturated fatty acids and degradation of amino acids, generally exhibit a creamy aroma and delicate fragrance. Given their higher sensory threshold compared to aldehydes, ketones have a less pronounced effect on the flavor of dried shrimp (Zheng et al., 2015).

Composition of bacterial community analysis

The microbial community composition of dried shrimp samples, subjected to different packaging methods, was analyzed during chilled storage using HTS. The alpha diversity index was calculated, and all samples exhibited sequencing coverage exceeding 0.99, confirming the scientific reliability of the microbial diversity analysis results (Table S5). As illustrated in Fig. 4A-B, at the phylum level, four phyla had the highest relative abundance across all samples: Bacillota (43.44 %), Pseudomonadota (33.07 %), Actinomycetota (13.94 %), and Bacteroidota (5.99 %). Huang and Xie (2020) identified Pseudomonas as the main predominant spoilage bacteria in hybrid grouper stored at 4 °C, findings that aligned with the current study. The relative abundance of Bacillota first increased and then decreased in all packaging types but remained high in the CP group throughout storage. Dynamic changes in lipids in P. hardwickii

A comprehensive lipid spectrum analysis of dried shrimp subjected todifferent packaging treatments during storage was conducted using lipidomics technology based on UHPLC-MS/MS. Fig. 5A displayed the identification results of 2993 lipid metabolites across all samples, categorized into 5 major lipid classes: glycerophospholipid (GP, 53.4 %), glycerolipid (GL, 31.0 %), sphingolipid (SP, 13.3 %), fatty acyl (FA, 2.1 %), and sterol lipid (ST, 0.2 %), aligning with our previous findings (Zheng et al., 2025). Compared to the CP group (Fig. S2A), the VP (Fig. S2B) and NP groups (Fig. S2C) exhibited increased GL and FA quantities, while SP decreased, likely due to the specific packaging methods and storage conditions, which also explained the significant flavor changes discussed in Section 3.7. In addition, these lipid molecules were further divided into 43 subcategories (Fig. 5B), with phosphatidylcholine (PC, 449), TG (647), and ceramides (Cer, 141) being the most abundant, followed by acylcarnitine (AcCa, 26) and campesterol ester (CmE, 3).

To investigate the impact of various packaging treatments on the lipid subcategory content of dried shrimp during storage, bar charts were employed to visually analyze the changes in lipid data (Fig. S2D-F). Across different packaging types, the relative content of phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) decreased with storage time, while DG and lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC) increased. Ali and Karin (2018) demonstrated that lipid content, particularly TG, has a greater impact on aroma retention than lipid type, with PE, PC, Cardiolipin (CL), DG, Cer, and LPC following in order of importance. These substances likely played a crucial role in maintaining the flavor of dried shrimp.

Phospholipids, rich in unsaturated fatty acids, significantly contribute to meat flavor due to their tendency to oxidize and compatibility with aqueous reactants (Li et al., 2021). Jia et al. (2021) showed that oxidation of phospholipids damaged cell membranes and intracellular components, accelerating overall oxidation. The increase in DG may result from the breakdown of the phosphate group on the glycerol structure of PE during storage (Zhou et al., 2023). Chen et al. (2010) demonstrated that the generation of LPC led to cell membrane destruction, promoting the formation of droplets in meat. A higher Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) score indicates a greater contribution to classification and more precise lipid classification across sample groups. Key volatile flavors and key differential lipids

In the CP group (Fig. 6D), key volatile flavors such as 1-Octen-3-One, 2,4-Decadienal, 2-Pentylpyridine, Hexanal, Isovaleraldehyde, 1-Octen-3-Ol, Valeraldehyde, Cis-5-Octen-1-Ol, and Trans-2,Cis-6-Nonadienal were significantly negatively correlated with specific lipids, including PE (36:0/18:4), TG (18:4/18:4/20:5), and CL (18:2/16:0/18:0/18:1). Other lipids such as PC(18:1e/17:1), TG(16:1e/6:0/20:5), PE(26:1/22:6), TG(17:0/18:1/22:6), TG(20:5/14:2/20:5), TG(16:0/14:3/22:6), and TG (16:0/21:0/22:4) were also negatively correlated with all key flavors. In the VP group (Fig. 6E), with the exception of 1-Hepten-3-One, the other 9 key flavors were significantly negatively correlated with PC (17:0/24:1) and SPH (d14:0). Additional lipids such as PC (20:0/24:2), TG (16:0e/16:0/22:4), PE (26:1/22:6), TG (16:0/14:3/22:6), TG (22:6/14:3/22:6), PE (20:1e/23:0), and PE (20:4e/23:1) were negatively correlated with all key flavors. For the NP group (Fig. 6F), excluding 1-Hepten-3-One, the other 9 flavors were significantly negatively correlated with TG (4:0/8:0/11:1) and TG (17:0/20:5/20:5). Lipids such as PE (36:0/18:1), TG (18:4/18:4/20:5), PE (34:0/10:3), PE (33:0/18:0), and PE (20:3e/22:5) were negatively correlated with all key flavors. These findings strongly suggested that the degradation of specific lipids, acting as flavor precursors, played a fundamental role in governing the development of the characteristic volatile flavor profile in dried shrimp under different packaging conditions.

Analysis of key flavor substances and formation pathways

To better clarify the interactions among key lipids, dominant microorganisms, and characteristic flavor compounds of dried shrimp during chilled storage, the possible occurrence pathways were investigated (Fig. 6G). Fat oxidation, protein degradation, and microbial activity are the main factors impacting dried shrimp quality and flavor. Dominant microorganisms and key lipid metabolites may directly or indirectly affect the synthesis of key flavor compounds. Broekaert et al. (2013) found that the presence of ketones in aquatic products was primarily associated with the effects of Psychrobacter and Pseudoalteromonas. Furthermore, it has been reported that Acinetobacter played a significant role in the formation of spoilage flavors in aquatic products. Microbial activity can lead to protein degradation, as microorganisms break down proteins into smaller peptides or amino acids by secreting enzymes such as proteases and peptidases. Zhang, Wang, et al. (2025) demonstrated that free amino acids and their derivatives, produced by protein degradation, can act as precursors to interact with lipid oxidation products, leading to the generation of VOCs.

Conclusion

This study used multi-omics techniques to examine the impact of packaging types on the flavor and quality of dried P. hardwickii during storage. VP and NP effectively slowed the decline in L*, a*, W, hardness, springiness, chewiness, and gumminess, as well as the rise in TBARS, TVB-N, and TVC, preserving the shrimp's appearance, sensory quality, color, texture, and microstructure. HS-SPME-GC–MS analysis identified 10 key flavor compounds with ROAV >1. HTS revealed 4 dominant bacterial phyla and 9 genera, while lipidomics identified 30 differential lipids across different packaging types. These lipids and bacterial strains significantly influenced the flavor of dried shrimp during chilled storage. Correlation analysis indicated that fat oxidation and protein degradation likely contributed to declines in muscle quality, color changes, and the generation of key flavor compounds. Six dominant bacteria—Bacillota, Actinomycetota, Dietzia, Psychrobacter, Acinetobacter, and Photobacterium—along with 25 key differential lipids, played a crucial role in the quality changes and flavor formation of dried shrimp.

In summary, VP and NP were effective in preserving the quality of dried shrimp. Lipid oxidation, protein degradation, and microbial activity were the primary factors affecting flavor and quality during storage. In addition, VP and NP also offered a significant cost advantage. However, VP may have affected the appearance of dried shrimp, while NP required more storage space, which should be considered in future research. This study provided valuable insights into the flavor evolution of dried P. hardwickii and the impact of packaging methods, offering a theoretical basis for future research on flavor control and food preservation technologies.
