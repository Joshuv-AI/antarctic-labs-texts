Exploring perception of coarticulatory cues in childhood apraxia of speech and residual speech sound disorders: A pilot study
Authors: Molly Beiting, Navin Viswanathan, Nicole Caballero, Elaine Hitchcock, Tara McAllister, Jonathan L Preston
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction:Children with childhood apraxia of speech (CAS) exhibit speech production deficits beyond segmental errors, including atypical coarticulation. Broadly, children with speech sound disorders (SSD) also exhibit weaknesses in speech perception, which may directly relate to specific production difficulties. This study examined differences in categorical perception among children with CAS, residual SSD affecting /ɹ/ (rSSD), and typical speech (TD). We hypothesized that children with CAS would demonstrate weaker performance on a task requiring perception of coarticulatory cues—a defining weakness in their speech production—but perform similarly to rSSD peers on a segmental task.Methods:This study was designed as an exploratory pilot project, embedded in the baseline assessment of a series of speech treatment studies. Participants included 96 children (ages 8;0–15;11), 44 CAS, 39 rSSD, and 13 TD. In the segmental perception task, participants judged stimuli from a 5-step /al/ - /aɹ/ continuum. In the second task, /al/ and /aɹ/ endpoints were prefixed to a 5-step /ga/-/da/ continuum, creating /al/ (/alga/ - /alda/) and /aɹ/ (/aɹga/ - /aɹda/ ) conditions. Compensation for coarticulation was assessed at an ambiguous step, where listeners were expected to perceive /g/ after /al/ or /d/ after /aɹ/.Results & Conclusions:Compensation for coarticulation was significantly weaker among children with CAS, who also demonstrated less reliable perception of /ga/ - /da/ endpoints and segmental perception weaknesses on the /al/ - /aɹ/ task not seen in rSSD or TD. Findings contribute an initial characterization of perceptual compensation for coarticulation in children with CAS. Results highlight performance differences based on underlying SSD subtype and may be clinically useful for future development of diagnostic tools that bypass verbal output limitations.Keywords: Speech perception, Speech sound disorders, Childhood apraxia of speech, Compensation for coarticulation

Introduction

The primary deficit underlying childhood apraxia of speech (CAS) is impaired planning and programming of speech movements, impacting speech production and intelligibility (Shriberg et al., 2019). Although no single feature is sufficient for differential diagnosis from other speech sound disorders (SSDs), hallmark characteristics of CAS include inconsistent errors (e.g., Iuzzini-Seigel et al., 2017), prosodic deficits (e.g., Shriberg et al., 2017), and aberrant coarticulation (e.g., Nijland et al., 2002). Evidence of coarticulatory differences in speech production largely comes from acoustic investigations, reporting inconsistent and idiosyncratic patterns of coarticulation compared to peers without speech disorders (Maas & Mailend, 2017; Maassen et al., 2001; Nijland et al., 2002, 2003; Sussman et al., 2000; Terband, 2017), although lack of a non-CAS SSD control group is a notable limitation of many of these studies. In addition to speech production deficits, it is common for children with CAS to present with a constellation of symptoms affecting related skills (e.g., Chenausky et al., 2023), such as language (e.g., Zuk et al., 2018), phonological awareness, vocabulary, literacy (e.g., Gillon & Moriarty, 2007; McNeill et al., 2009; Miller et al., 2019; Stein et al., 2020), and speech perception (e.g., Groenen et al., 1996; Nijland et al., 2009). Given the large number of children with CAS who seem to be impacted by these deficits, it is unlikely that they are entirely coincidental or solely attributed to spurious factors.

Precise relationships and causal pathways have not yet been well defined, but it is possible that deficits in speech perception, phonological awareness, and/or literacy arise due to the cascading effects of impaired speech motor planning on these developing systems. Alternatively, impairments in foundational skills such as implicit learning (e.g., Bombonato et al., 2022) or speech perception (Nijland, 2009) may play an influential role in the onset and maintenance of CAS. Given the importance of perceptual skill in developing and monitoring speech motor plans (e.g., Nijland, 2009), there is particular interest in exploring how speech perception ability presents among individuals with various clinical speech profiles—and whether the nature of perceptual weaknesses relate to whether speech planning and programming is specifically implicated in the SSD. This knowledge is needed to advance both the theoretical understanding of speech development and disorders and the clinical management of SSDs.

Relationship between speech perception and speech production

Functionally, speech perception is the ability to extract a message conveyed by a speaker from a multimodal spoken signal. In order to do so in running speech, listeners must cope with a dynamic and highly variable signal resulting from overlapping gestures, or coarticulated speech. Coarticulatory effects are described as anticipatory when a speech sound is influenced by a later occurring segment, or perseverative when a later occurring speech sound shows traces of an earlier segment (Maassen et al., 2001).

In terms of their speech production, young children generally demonstrate more coarticulation than adults (Noiray et al., 2018). This is likely due to a combination of factors, including younger children having broader internally-based phonological representations (e.g., syllables or whole words vs. phonemes) and less mature motor control (e.g., protracted development of lingual coordination, Rubertus & Noiray, 2018). As children’s perceptual skills, access to phonetic structure, and motor skills develop, their internally-based phonological representations are refined and reorganized, leading to adult-like patterns of coarticulation (Cychosz et al., 2021; Nittrouer, 2002). Impairments in speech perception could disrupt sensitivity to subtle phonetic cues and subsequent encoding of phonological information, leading to “fuzzy” or poorly-specified target representations. Based on this reasoning, an impoverished representational system could lead to cascading impacts on skills that reference these representations (i.e., speech production, phonological awareness, language, and literacy; Marquardt et al., 2002).

Just as speech perception plays a role in the development and refinement of stored phonological representations, sensory feedback is thought to be integral in establishing motor plans for speech production. According to the Directions into Velocities of Articulators model (DIVA; Guenther & Vladusich, 2012), mature speech motor plans are developed through the integration of tactile, proprioceptive, and auditory feedback, accumulated over the course of many attempts and corrections. As feedforward commands become more accurate and stable, the speech production system relies less on sensory feedback, favoring a more efficient feedforward-driven system (Terband et al., 2009, 2014). Although speech perception ability precedes speech production ability to some extent (e.g., Berti et al., 2022), the relationship is thought to be bidirectional; how an individual perceives speech is influenced by how they produce speech, and vice versa (McAllister Byun, 2012).

Speech perception among children with SSDs

Perceptual deficits are quite common among children with SSDs broadly. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis conducted by Hearnshaw et al. (2019) found that, across 80 % of reviewed studies, speech perception problems were reported among some or all participants with SSDs. Some studies have found a direct relationship between specific misarticulated sounds and perceptual skills (e. g., Cabbage et al., 2016; Groenen et al., 1996; Hearnshaw et al., 2018; Hitchcock et al., 2020), although this is not an entirely linear relationship (e.g., Brosseau-Lapré & Schumaker, 2020) and impaired speech perception among children with SSD is not universal (Zuk et al., 2018). Discrepant findings could be due to participant differences related to SSD subtypes or co-occurring deficits (e.g., language ability; Zuk et al., 2018) or attributed to methodological differences (Cabbage & Hitchcock, 2022).

Studies conducted specifically with children with CAS have produced consistent findings to the broader SSD literature—impaired speech perception is common within this subgroup. (e.g., Groenen et al., 1996; Maassen et al., 2003; Nijland, 2009; Wong et al., 2023; Zuk et al., 2018). Children with CAS have been shown to differ in their perception of detailed phonetic information such as the presence or absence of aspiration of stop consonants (Froud & Khamis-Dakwar, 2012) and, among Cantonese-speaking children with CAS, lexical tones (Wong et al., 2023). Children with CAS may also have difficulty performing metaphonological tasks requiring the identification of sounds within words (Lewis, et. al, 2004; Marquardt et al., 2002; McNeill et al., 2009). Further, there is some evidence that the specific speech perception weaknesses observed among children with CAS directly relate to speech production errors that are characteristic of CAS. For example, Groenen et al. (1996) found that the ability to discriminate between place of articulation contrasts /b/-/d/ was poorer among children with CAS (N = 17) compared to children with typical speech (N = 16), and that there was a strong correlation between poor discrimination scores and more frequent place-of-articulation-errors in speech production. Likewise, Maassen et al. (2003) found that ability to identify and discriminate between vowels (/i/–/ɪ/ and /a/–/ɑ/) was poorer among children with CAS (N = 11) compared to children with typical speech (N = 12). Although the small sample sizes and lack of a non-CAS speech disorder comparison group are significant limitations of these studies, findings support the interdependence of the speech perception and production domains and the potential clinical value of perceptual tasks.

Tasks to assess speech perception

Speech perception tasks can be coarsely grouped as identification or discrimination paradigms. Identification tasks are considered to involve higher order processing in that they require the listener to compare stimuli to internally stored representations (e.g., mispronunciation detection). Discrimination tasks are considered to involve lower-level processing in that they require comparison between two or more auditorily presented stimuli without reference to a stored representation (i.e., same or different; e.g., ABX tasks). There is some research to support a relationship between higher-order speech perception impairments and higher-order production problems (e.g., phonological disorders), with individuals with CAS having both higher- and lower-order perception problems (Nijland, 2009).

Categorical perception is one of the most widely used paradigms to assess speech perception among children with CAS and SSDs broadly (e.g., Nijland, 2009; Zuk et al., 2018) and involves both higher order and lower order processing. In this paradigm, the listener is presented with tokens from an acoustic continuum, manipulated for a single contrast such as voicing or place of articulation, with endpoints representing distinct sounds (e.g., /ga/ and /da/). Listeners with unaffected speech perception reliably categorize ambiguous tokens from the continuum as belonging to one of the two endpoints, resulting in an abrupt shift around the middle of the continuum (Liberman et al., 1957). This results in a sharp identification boundary, with better discrimination across the boundary than within it. For example, Nijland (2009) used a 7-step continuum from ‘pop’ to ‘kop’ (Dutch words with a short, rounded vowel ‘o’) and a two-alternative forced choice categorical classification task to examine differences in performance between children with typical development and those with various speech disorders (including CAS). As expected, children with typical speech development showed a sharp discrimination curve at the midpoint of the continuum, while children with speech disorders displayed a shallower curve, indicating relatively greater difficulty discriminating stimuli from different categories. The categorical perception phenomenon is thought to relate to ability to perform in everyday listening situations, in which coarticulatory context and speaker differences regularly contribute non-meaningful acoustic differences that must be deemphasized in order to make sense of speech (c.f. McMurray, 2022 for an alternate view).

Most speech perception tasks center on the ability to perceive segmental information, such as a single consonant voicing or place of articulation contrast. If speech perception deficits mirror speech production difficulties, segmental perception tasks are unlikely to fully uncover differences in performance between groups with different types of SSDs. For example, children who have difficulty articulating /ɹ/ but are presumed to have different SSD-subtypes (e.g., residual articulation disorder, phonologically-based disorder, CAS) may perform similarly on a perceptual task focused on segmental perception of /ɹ/. A task that aligns closely with potentially discriminative features (e.g., for CAS, lexical stress or coarticulation) may be most sensitive to specific clinical profiles, thus revealing unique underlying deficits theoretically implicated in different SSDs (Wang, 2024).

Compensation for coarticulation (CfC)

Compensation for coarticulation is a phenomenon of typical speech perception in which characteristics of the speech production context influence how a sound is perceived (Mann, 1980). This mechanism enables listeners to understand sounds accurately, compensating for acoustic changes due to anticipatory or perseverative coarticulatory influences. For example, when presented with an ambiguous token from a /ga/ - /da/ continuum in an experimental context, listeners more often perceive the ambiguous token as /g/ when preceded by /al/ but are more likely to perceive /d/ when preceded by /aɹ/ (Mann, 1980). That is, perception of the same token changes based on whether an /l/ or /ɹ/ is presented first. There are competing accounts of what underlies such perceptual shifts. From a gestural perceptive (Mann, 1980; Fowler, 2006) such perceptual shifts reflect the fact that listeners are attuned to vocal tract dynamics that unfold over time due to co-production of gestures in running speech. Thus, the ambiguous segments of the stop continuum are heard by listeners as a coarticulatory shift in constriction location (i.e., as one that is pulled forward after /al/, making it more ‘g’-like, and pulled back after /aɹ/. An alternate explanation is that such boundary shifts arise due to the interaction of the acoustic signal with the auditory system. Specifically, listeners experience spectral contrast (Holt, 1999; Lotto & Kluender, 1998) such that the critical speech formants of the target continuum (F3) are heard as higher (i.e., more /d/) after a segment with low F3s (/aɹ/) and lower (more /g/) after a segment with high F3s (/al/).

Evidence of compensation for coarticulation has been found among both adults (e.g., Mann, 1980) and children (e.g., Fowler et al., 1990; Mann et al., 1985) with typical speech development, but the nature of changes across typical and atypical development is currently underspecified. While other work has tried to dissociate the aforementioned accounts (Kang et al., 2016; Viswanathan et al., 2009, 2010), for current purposes it is critical to note that both explanations are signal-centric in that they stem from different characteristics of the syllables. Thus, investigating whether and how groups of listeners with unique clinical profiles differ in their compensation for coarticulation has the potential to broaden these accounts to incorporate the abilities of the listener (Viswanathan et al., 2010; Kang et al., 2016).

Compensation for coarticulation in children with SSD

To date, only one study (Mann et al., 1985) has examined the phenomenon of compensation for coarticulation among children with SSDs broadly, and no published research has involved children with CAS. In their investigation of perceptual accommodation for vowel rounding on fricatives (i.e., /ʃ/ - /s/ continuum followed by /a/ or /u/), Mann et al. (1985) compared performance of adults and three groups of children with either intact production of /ʃ/ and /s/ (two groups: five- and seven-year-olds with typical speech) or impaired production of /ʃ/ and /s/ (one group: seven-year-olds with production difficulty limited to fricatives and affricates). Overall, listeners perceived fewer instances of /ʃ/ in the /u/ context, and there was no difference in performance between groups. Results indicated that—for children with a presumed peripheral articulation impairment or phonological disorder, characterized by intact coarticulation and misarticulation of a limited number of phonemes—perceptual ability was not related to productive deficits (Mann et al., 1985). It is presently unknown how children with CAS (who have differences in coarticulation) might perform on a task that requires perception of coarticulatory cues.

Current study

In the present study, we used a /ɹ/ - /l/ categorical discrimination task and a compensation for coarticulation paradigm to investigate differences in perception that extend beyond a misarticulated sound. We examined the performance of three groups of children: those with single phoneme production deficits (i.e., residual speech sound disorder affecting /ɹ/; rSSD), CAS, and typical speech development (TD). We expected that perception of coarticulatory cues would relate to the type of SSD and the presumed nature of the underlying speech disorder. As such, we predicted that children with CAS and rSSD would perform similarly on the /ɹ/ - /l/ categorical discrimination task, but that—given their broad deficits in coarticulatory production due to impaired motor control and more pervasive impairments in phonological representations—children with CAS would demonstrate greater perceptual difficulty on the compensation for coarticulation task.

Methods

Participants

Participants were 96 children between 8 and 16 years of age who participated in research studies at [redacted for peer review] University between 2018 and 2023. The perceptual study was designed as an exploratory embedded pilot project and was unrelated to the primary studies. All data reported in this secondary analysis were collected prior to any experimental treatment. Inclusionary criteria were: a) found to have TD, rSSD, or CAS (see criteria below), b) spoke a rhotic dialect of American English as their dominant language (per parent report), c) passed a pure tone hearing screening at 20 dB at 500, 1000, 2000, and 4000 Hz, and d) passed an oral mechanism examination. Participants with developmental disabilities such as autism and Down syndrome (as per parent report) were excluded. These criteria were set to reduce heterogeneity in the sample and increase the likelihood that any observed patterns of speech perception performance were due to speech disorder subgroup, rather than differences that may be uniquely attributed to other disorders (e.g., Chen & Peng, 2021). Measures of phonological awareness (Comprehensive Test of Phonological Processing-Second Edition [CTOPP-2], Wagner et al., 2013) and language ability (Peabody Picture Vocabulary Test-Fourth Edition [PPVT-4], Dunn & Dunn, 2007; Clinical Evaluation of Language Fundamentals-Fifth Edition [CELF-5, Wiig et al., 2013) were collected for descriptive purposes. We did not collect information about diagnostic history or prior speech treatment.

The CAS group included 47 participants (female = 14, male = 33) between the ages of 9–16. For this study, we only included children with CAS who misarticulated /ɹ/ on a word-level test of articulation (GFTA-3), resulting in a final CAS sample of 44 (female = 14, male = 30; age M = 10.52, SD = 1.84). This decision was made to reduce the possibility that differences in task performance between rSSD and CAS groups could be related to differences in production capabilities of /ɹ/. CAS diagnosis was based on three tasks: a) the Syllable Repetition Task (SRT; Shriberg et al., 2012); b) the Maximum Repetition Rate of Trisyllables (MRR-Tri) from the Maximum Performance Task (MPT; Thoonen et al., 1999); and c) an 80-item polysyllable picture-naming task (adapted from Gozzard et al., 2006). These tasks yielded four measures: a) number of sound additions on the 18-item SRT, b) Apraxia Score on the MPT, c) lexical stress accuracy on polysyllabic words and d) syllable segregations on polysyllabic words. CAS diagnosis required at least two of four criteria: 1) four or more sound additions on the SRT; 2) Apraxia Score of 2 on the MPT; 3) ≤ 70 % lexical stress accuracy in polysyllables; and 4) syllable segregation on ≥3 % of polysyllables (Murray et al., 2015). Two independent raters confirmed whether the child met inclusionary criteria, with a third rater serving as a tie-breaker if the first two disagreed on eligibility. Although not required for diagnosis of CAS (but as typical for children with this disorder), the CAS group had significantly lower CTOPP-2 Phonological Awareness composite scores than the rSSD and TD groups (TD: M = 110.20, SD = 12.84; rSSD: M = 96.59, SD = 16.93, CAS: M = 75.52, SD = 14.64; F(2,91) = 31.63, p = < 0.001).

The rSSD group included 39 participants (female = 15, male = 24), between the ages of 8–14 (M = 10.69, SD = 2.84), who demonstrated isolated difficulty with /ɹ/ production. Participants a) scored less than the 8th percentile on the Goldman-Fristoe Test of Articulation (GFTA-2; Goldman & Fristoe, 2000, or GFTA-3; Goldman & Fristoe, 2015), b) exhibited less than 30 % accuracy on a 50-item word probe list eliciting /ɹ/ in various phonetic contexts, and c) exhibited one or fewer CAS criteria on an abridged battery including the 1) SRT, 2) MPT (same criteria as described above), and 3) the Inconsistency Screener from the Linguisystems Articulation Test (Bowers & Huisingh, 2011; cutoff score of three or more inconsistencies considered consistent with CAS; Preston et al., 2017).

The TD group included 13 participants (female = 6, male = 7) between the ages of 9–14 (M = 12.07, SD = 1.71). Participants a) produced /ɹ/ with 100 % accuracy on a 50-item word probe list, b) scored at or above the 16th percentile on the GFTA-3, and c) exhibited one or fewer CAS criteria (described above). There were two notable imbalances across participant groups. First, there was a greater proportion of males in the CAS and rSSD groups compared to the TD group. This pattern is common in speech disorders research given the higher prevalence of SSD in males (Wren et al., 2016). However, previous research has not demonstrated sex-related differences in performance on categorical speech perception tasks (e.g., Hearnshaw et al., 2018), so we did not expect this difference to confound results. Second, the TD group size was considerably smaller than the disordered groups. This imbalance was a result of the preliminary nature of this study, which relied on data collected from participants involved in various speech treatment studies(see Participants). We proceeded with unequal group sizes because the performance of children in the TD group was expected to be relatively homogenous (Hitchcock et al., 2024; Mann et al., 1985) and we selected a modeling approach that was able to handle differences in group size (Generalized Estimating Equations; GEE, see Results).

Stimuli

The speech stimuli were synthesized using parameters based on Holt, Stephens and Lotto (2005) using Praat (Boersma & Weenik, 2024). An /aɹ/ - /al/ continuum was constructed for Task 1. A /ga/ - /da/ continuum was constructed for Task 2. Next, the endpoints from the /aɹ/ and /al/ continuum were prepended to each step of the /ga/ - /da/ continuum, creating the /alga/ - /alda/ and /aɹga/ - /aɹda/ continua used in Task 2.

Construction of /aɹ/ - /al/ continuum

The liquids /al/ and /aɹ/ were synthesized with the following parameters. Both syllables shared the same F1 (onset at 750 Hz, offset at 550 Hz), F4 (steady at 2800 Hz) and F5 (steady at 3750 Hz). The liquids differed in both their F2 and F3. The F2 of the liquids had a shared onset at 1220 Hz, with a high offset at 1500 Hz for /aɹ/ vs. a low offset at 980 Hz for /al/. Their F3 had a shared onset at 2475 Hz, with a low offset at 1650 Hz for /aɹ/ vs. a high offset at 2700 Hz for /al/. The total duration of both syllables was 550 milliseconds, and all formant transitions began 250 milliseconds from onset. Pilot testing indicated that these end point tokens were judged as good tokens of each liquid. The acoustic 5-step liquid continuum between /al/ and /aɹ/ was then synthesized by simultaneous linear interpolation of F2 and F3 of these end point tokens.

Construction of /ga/ - /da/ continuum

A similar procedure was used to create the /ga/ - /da/ continuum. These stops shared F1 (onset at 300 Hz, offset at 750 Hz), F2 (onset at 1650 Hz, offset at 1200 Hz), F4 (steady at 2850 Hz) and F5 (steady at 3750 Hz). These stops varied solely in their F3 which consisted of a low onset of 1800 Hz for /ga/ versus a high onset of 2700 Hz for /ga/ followed by a shared offset of 2450 Hz. The total duration of the target stop syllables was 350 milliseconds with transitions within the first 130 milliseconds. Pilot testing again indicated that these end point tokens were judged as good tokens of each stop. The acoustic 5-step stop continuum between /ga/ and /da/ was synthesized by solely interpolating the F3 of the end point tokens.

Experimental procedures

Perceptual tasks were administered in-person, in a private, quiet room in the [redacted for peer review] Lab at [redacted for peer review] University. Participants wore headphones (Sennheister HD 280 PRO) and completed the tasks seated in front of a desktop computer monitor. Tasks were presented in PowerPoint, with graphics (from Funny Monster Character Vector Set [clipart], 2016, Vector Characters) and audio embedded in the slides. Task 1 (/aɹ/ - /al/ continuum) was always administered first, followed by Task 2 (the /alga/ - /alda/ and /aɹga/ - /aɹda/ continua). This ordering ensured that participants were familiar with the precursor syllables before testing the disyllables. The examiner sat next to the participant and controlled the stimulus presentation using a mouse. In both tasks, participants indicated their responses by pointing, and the examiner recorded their responses in real time by circling the choice on a paper record. The order of stimuli was pseudorandomized (i.e., a random number generator used to generate a fixed sequence) and was consistent across participants. Two trials were presented for each of five steps of the acoustic continua, resulting in two data points for each step (i.e., 10 trials per each of the three continua) per participant. Given that the experimental tasks were designed as an embedded pilot study, steps were taken to reduce overall experiment length, minimize participant burden, and ensure feasibility. In contrast to past CfC studies (e.g., Viswanathan et al., 2009), steps in /ga/ - /da/ continuum were reduced from 10 to 5, and the number of trials was reduced from 400 to 30. The total time to complete both tasks was about 15 min.

In the first task, participants were introduced to a cartoon robot who “spoke” the names of two pictured planets: /al/ and /aɹ/. A brief training exposure was provided prior to administration of the first task. The examiner read from a script: “Name these items for me. This is /al/ (participant imitates /al/) and this is /aɹ/ (participant imitates /aɹ/). This is /al/ (point to left) and this is /aɹ/ (point to right). Again, this is /aɹ/ (right) and this is /al/ (left). Now you say it: This is (point to left) /al/ and this is (point to right)… /aɹ/. One more time. (Point to right, child says /aɹ/). Good. (Point to left, child says /al/). Good!” This brief familiarization training was the only time that a spoken response was required, and habitual production errors were accepted. Following the training, the task began with instructions to “point to the one you think the speaker is saying. If you aren’t sure, take your best guess.” Participants were required to make a binary categorical judgement (i.e., no gradient or “I don’t know” response options). On each slide, the examiner played the audio stimulus (i.e., “robot’s speech”) twice.

In the second task, participants were first introduced to two cartoon characters “from Planet Al:” /alga/ and /alda/. A similar training procedure was provided to that described for Task 1. The examiner read from a script: “Name these items for me. This is /alga/ . Can you say his name? /alga/. This is /alda/. Can you say his name? /alda/. This is /alga/ and this is /alda/. Again, this is /alda/ and this is /alga/. You say the names: This is (point to left, child says /alga/) and this is (point to right, child says… /alda/). One more time. Which is /alda/? Oh, good. Which is /alga/? Good!” As described above, the examiner played the audio stimulus twice on each slide and the participant was instructed to point to their response and take a guess if they were unsure. Following presentation of the ten trials with the /al/ precursor (i.e., two trials per step on the 5-step /ga/ - /da/ continuum), participants were introduced to two final cartoon characters “from Planet Ar:” /aɹga/ and /aɹda/. The same training procedure and directions were presented, and participants responded to ten trials with the /aɹ/ precursor (i.e., two trials per step on the 5-step /ga/ - /da/ continuum). As in the first task, participants were required to make a binary categorical judgement (i.e., no gradient or “I don’t know” response options).

Results

Analyses were conducted using a Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) modeling approach from the geepack (Højsgaard et al., 2006; Yan, 2002; Yan & Fine, 2004) package in RStudio (R Core Team, 2023) and the parameters: family = binomial and corstr = independence. This modeling approach is able to accommodate differences in group size, binary responses, and within-participant correlation in repeated measures data (Zeger & Liang, 1986). Plots were constructed using ggplot2 (Wickham, 2016).

Task 1: categorical perception of /aɹ/ - /al/

We predicted that children with CAS and rSSD would perform similarly on the /aɹ/ - /al/ categorical discrimination task. Contrary to our hypothesis, there was a significant difference in performance between the rSSD and CAS group at Step 4 (i.e., an intermediate token between /aɹ/ and /al/) and Step 5 (/al/ endpoint). The odds of responding /al/ were significantly lower in the CAS group compared to the rSSD group at Step 4 (β = −3.43, Wald χ2 = 8.04, p = 0.005) and Step 5 (i.e., /al/ endpoint; β = −4.13, Wald χ2 = 9.70, p = 0.002). There was also a marginally significant difference in performance between the CAS and rSSD groups at Step 3 (i.e., an intermediate token between /aɹ/ and /al/; β = −2.02, Wald χ2 = 3.10, p = 0.078). The CAS group performed similarly to the rSSD group at all other steps, including Step 1 (i.e., /aɹ/ endpoint; β = 1.73, Wald χ2 = 2.51, p = 0.113). There were no significant differences in performance at any step of the /aɹ/ - /al/ continuum between the TD and rSSD groups. [Fig. 1]

Discussion

The purpose of this investigation was to examine whether children with TD, rSSD, and CAS demonstrate differences in their perceptual accommodation to phonetic context effects. We predicted that differences in performance would relate to speech disorder profile. Due to their hypothesized impairments in speech motor control, we predicted that children with CAS—who uniquely exhibit aberrant coarticulatory transitions in their speech production—would exhibit weaker perceptual compensation for coarticulation compared to children with rSSD. Children with CAS not only showed significantly weaker compensation for coarticulation relative to the rSSD group, but they also demonstrated overall perceptual weaknesses in their discrimination of continuum endpoints.

Children with rSSD affecting /ɹ/ performed similarly to children without speech disorders (TD). It is possible that the present sample size was not large enough, or the task was not sensitive enough, to detect subtle differences between groups, which have been documented in the broader literature (see Cabbage & Hitchcock, 2022 for a review). However, some studies that have compared performance between TD and SSD groups on categorical perception tasks have revealed similar findings to the present results (Dugan et al., 2019; Preston et al., 2015). Both TD and rSSD groups demonstrated the expected perceptual accommodation for coarticulation. These findings replicate Mann et al. (1985); children who misarticulated /ɹ/ did not show significantly different CfC responses compared to children with typical articulation. This finding also highlights that differences in perceptual ability between SSD and TD groups may be task dependent (Cabbage & Hitchcock, 2022). In the present study, children with typical speech development demonstrated largely accurate and homogenous performance on both perceptual experiments, which is consistent with previous research (e.g., Hearnshaw et al., 2018). Although the imbalance in group sizes—with considerably fewer children in the TD group than the disordered groups—is worth restating, the low variability in performance within this group provides some justification for proceeding with the imbalance.

While these findings are provocative and worthy of additional study, interpretation is limited by two primary factors. First, given that this was an embedded pilot study, steps were taken to reduce overall experiment length, minimize participant burden, and ensure feasibility. The method of stimuli construction and experimental procedures closely replicated past CfC studies (e.g., Viswanathan et al., 2009), with these exceptions: 1) steps in /ga/ - /da/ continuum reduced from 10 to 5, and 2) number of trials reduced from 400 to 30. As a result, task sensitivity was reduced, and estimation of true perceptual boundaries for all groups of listeners was difficult. In alignment with traditional categorical perception models, and to facilitate simplicity and ease of comparison to past research (e.g., Viswanathan et al., 2009), participants were required to make binary categorical judgements rather than gradient decisions in this short, embedded pilot task. Future research, including gradient response options (e.g., using a visual analog scale), could reveal more subtle perceptual differences. Despite these limitations, the study still detected significant differences, suggesting that the effects observed are likely robust and that the design, while constrained, was sufficient to capture meaningful patterns in perceptual performance. Findings from this pilot study support the need for continued research, using a prospectively designed study with expanded experimental procedures to improve task sensitivity.

Second, although there is a theoretical rationale for interpreting task performance differences in relation to SSD subtype and presumed underlying impairment, precise identification of underlying impairments is far from an exact science, and clinical SSD subtype provides only one potential explanation for the results. We did not systematically control for diagnostic or treatment history, so it is possible that present SSD subtypes did not accurately reflect underlying etiologies for all cases (e.g., some children in the rSSD group may have had a history of CAS). Additionally, phonological awareness and language ability represent potential confounds. Poor phonological awareness is common among children with CAS (e.g., McNeill et al., 2009; Knežević & Maassen, 2025), and this profile was uniquely reflected by the CAS group in this study (as measured by the CTOPP-2). Correlation between CfC task performance and the CTOPP-2 was not explored due to the small number of tokens and power limitations. It is possible that differences in compensation for phonetic context could be attributed to broader deficits in phonological processing rather than SSD subtype. However, the nature and direction of a potential causal relationship cannot be specified, and it is possible that perceptual differences could impede the development of phonological awareness (Benway et al., 2021; Rvachew & Grawberg, 2006). Due to the preliminary and exploratory nature of this experiment, language ability was not measured consistently across the three studies from which participants were recruited. Under specification of phonological awareness and language skills are limitations that should be addressed through future prospective studies that include relevant control groups.

This investigation provides a first description of perceptual compensation for coarticulation among children with CAS, and preliminary evidence for differential performance based on underlying speech disorder subtype. Children with segmental articulation difficulty affecting only /ɹ/ (rSSD) performed similarly to children with typical speech (TD) on both perceptual tasks, while children with CAS exhibited general perceptual difficulties and lacked statistically significant evidence of perceptual accommodation for coarticulation. These differences in performance may relate to the distinct underlying profiles that are presumed to be associated with these pediatric speech disorders. By definition, children with CAS are expected to have difficulty producing smooth coarticulatory transitions. While this study did not collect the speech production data that would be needed to explore whether there was a direct correlation between perception and production of coarticulation, further research could examine this relationship. Further exploration is also needed to determine whether weaknesses in compensation for coarticulation are attributed to speech disorder profile or a related factor such as phonological awareness or language ability. Overall, study results have promising implications for future development of diagnostic methods that can circumvent limitations in verbal output—a significant clinical problem that complicates accurate differential diagnosis of speech sound disorders.
