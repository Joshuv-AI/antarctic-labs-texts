Machine learning framework for multidimensional assessment of urban quality of life
Authors: Ahmed A. A. Ahmed, Yahia Abdelghafur, Yusr Ahmed, Ayman Alzaatreh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study uses statistical and machine learning techniques to categorize and rank 99 high development cities according to multidimensional Quality of Life factors (QoL). We categorize cities into three unique clusters using hierarchical Ward.D2 clustering and Principal Component Analysis for dimension reduction. We discover clusters that bring together economically developed cities with strong social safety nets, high-income cities lacking in public amenities, rising cities of the future located in peripheral regions, and major population centers in the developing world, finding considerable structural similarities that lead to comparable outcomes across culturally and geographically diverse cities. Components of QoL are grouped into three Principal Components that reveal dynamic interactions between different variables influencing economic and experimental aspects of QoL. Clusters are assessed relative to one another using the three PCs, and crucial factors for classifying cities into different clusters are identified by a Decision tree to allow for tailored policy recommendations for cities of different clusters. Our findings give policymakers a framework to prioritize holistic urban development over GDP-centric models, highlighting the importance of striking a balance between objective standards of human development and subjective, experiential indicators of individual wellbeing.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-41350-4.

Introduction

Measuring, defining, and comparing the wellbeing of different societies has been a professed goal of social scientists and philosophers since antiquity. Enlightenment thinkers like Jean-Jacques Rousseau famously argued that societies are shaped by a combination of an intricate interplay of geography, culture, religion, and civic morality, forces that resist simple quantification1. Today, social scientists rely on social Quality of Life (QoL) indicators that reflect people’s objective circumstances within a given society, such as income, ecology, human rights, and education, each contributing to individual and societal wellbeing. Despite being widely recognized as an empirical tool for evaluating and comparing Quality of Life across different countries and regions, theoretical and methodological critiques have been raised with regard to what variables should be used and how they should be measured and aggregated in order to quantify wellbeing.“Cities are the embodiment of numerical patterns that contain and direct our lives. But all cities are invisible to start with2.”– Daniel TammetDiener and Suh3 argue that the inclusion of comprehensive and diverse social indicators allows for a deeper understanding of wellbeing in different regions that goes beyond simple economic yardsticks, while acknowledging that social indicators are indeed fallible and that even relatively objective measures can be selected, weighted, and interpreted subjectively. In his book Seeing Like a State, James C. Scott traces this problem to the history of the use of quantitative indicators, which are often designed with the demands of the state in mind, and not the experience of individuals: “The state’s attempt to make society legible... involves simplifying complex realities into standardized categories, often erasing local knowledge4.” Brock5 expands on this notion by identifying three competing frameworks for assessing QoL: normative judgment, preference satisfaction, and individual experience. Most QoL indices, like the OECD Better Life Index or the Human Development Index (HDI), lean heavily on the Normative Judgment approach, which establishes objective determinants of a high QoL and then sets out to measure them. Satisfaction of Preferences tries to measure the ability of individuals to meet their needs based on the assumption of utility maximizing rational individuals, often prioritizing economic agency. Individual Experience instead prioritizes the lived experience of citizens; factors such as feelings of joy, pleasure, contentment, and life satisfaction are paramount in this approach, which focuses on subjective wellbeing.

A persistent challenge is aggregating diverse indicators into coherent indices. Should health outcomes weigh as much as income? Is political freedom more important than life expectancy? The methodologies pursued by different researchers dramatically shift the results of Quality of Life assessments, as selecting different variables and assigning different weights based on subjective evaluations of their importance can lead to dramatically different rankings. In recent years, the information revolution has enabled the collection of immense volumes of data at incredibly granular levels and the construction of numerous indices to evaluate QoL between countries and across time, but the same theoretical and methodological critiques remain valid. The study’s analysis is concerned with analyzing QoL at a granular scale, looking at wellbeing within individual cities rather than across countries. To address this and achieve a more fine-grained perspective on urban wellbeing, we followed the empirical workflow summarized in Fig. 1. Theoretical and Empirical Contributions and Limitations

The decision to examine cities was motivated by a desire to combine the three approaches to evaluating QoL, combining normative social indicators that measure political stability and healthcare quality with crowd sourced data from citizens reporting on factors such as crime and the costs of groceries. Cities exhibit stark disparities within their boundaries and require hyper localized metrics that capture these disparities, while standardized country level indices often oversimplify complex realities. In addition, cities tend to evolve faster and in more unpredictable ways than states. Municipal policies, geopolitical circumstances, and population composition can rapidly change between cities, and two neighboring cities in the same country can have widely different outcomes when it comes to QoL.

Analysis of urban QoL the literature has mainly revolved around the use of geospatial techniques to analyze different dimensions of QoL7,8. A major focus has been improving the granularity of the analysis, down to the neighborhood level, to reflect dispensaries within urban environments. Our objective is instead to offer an abstract, generalizable approach towards classifying cities based on important metrics—in effect using dimension reduction techniques to tackle the different theoretical dimensions of QoL analysis.

We propose a data mining framework to cluster 99 high development cities using multidimensional indicators, combining normative metrics with crowd sourced subjective data to uncover latent patterns in urban QoL indicators, utilizing dimension reduction techniques across a broad range of variables to emerge with a holistic and comprehensive evaluation of the Quality of Life indicators across different clusters before analyzing the features that cause the best performing cities to stand out. This approach addresses aggregation biases noted by Stiglitz et al.9, who critique “composite indices that arbitrarily weight incommensurable dimensions.”

We propose a data mining framework to cluster 99 high development cities using multidimensional indicators, combining normative metrics with crowd sourced subjective data to uncover latent patterns in urban QoL indicators, utilizing dimension reduction techniques across a broad range of variables to emerge with a holistic and comprehensive evaluation of the Quality of Life indicators across different clusters before analyzing the features that cause the best performing cities to stand out. This approach addresses aggregation biases noted by Stiglitz et al.9, who critique “composite indices that arbitrarily weight incommensurable dimensions.”

By aggregating data on 99 high-performing cities, our objective is provide a big-picture analysis that manages to find institutional similarities and differences, and bottlenecks that different cities have in common despite differences in culture, geography and socioeconomic conditions, contributing to a growing literature on the role of institution within social data-science literature. We achieve this by combining data sources that reflect different theoretical approaches. Normative Judgment, Satisfaction of Preferences and Individual Experience are all represented through a combination of indices based on expert-judgment and crowd-sourced data from city residents, and the robustness of results encourages the combination of different data sources that address different approaches towards evaluating QoL. Using dimension reduction to combining crowd-sourced data poses problems of varying data density and quality, which we recognize. Our limited scope of high-performing cities ameliorates this problem due to a relatively high data coverage in the countries and cities we select.

Our choice of cities is restricted to the highest-performing cities with regard to QoL globally, which creates an inherent bias and somewhat limits the generalization of our findings. Cities follow a highly skewed distribution when it comes to population, with a small number of cities being disproportionately larger due to agglomeration effects. Our sample of 99 cities represents a population of 765,000,000 people, or roughly 15% of the world’s urban population. Despite this, there is considerable diversity in population, size, economic development, and other variables among our sample, and the final results we propose include a classification mechanism that could allow for the introduction of new cities into the clusters we delineate.

A second concern was data availability. While a parallel analysis could theoretically be carried out under similar motivations to discover the common binding constraints among low-performance cities, access to data from some of the poorest urban regions of the world is beyond the scope of our study.

Furthermore, the cross-sectional nature of our data prevents definitive causal inferences. Consequently, our analysis is limited to identifying trends and interrelationships among quality of life (QoL) variables. Any discussion of causality is strictly confined to explaining why cities cluster together, rather than how individual QoL factors influence one another over time.

A second limitation is the potential for unobserved heterogeneity. Although we control for a wide array of factors, unmeasured variables, such as cultural values, historical path dependencies (e.g., the enduring influence of colonialism on urban trajectories, and high levels of inequality may be correlated with our model variables. Our approach mitigates this common issue in the literature by incorporating diverse data sources. Nonetheless, subsequent studies could build on recent methodological advances, such as more sophisticated dimension reduction and clustering techniques, to better address this perennial challenge.

Literature Review

The measurement and study of Quality of Life is a multidisciplinary endeavor that encompasses different theories, frameworks, and metrics. It integrates both subjective and objective indicators10,11, as well as positive and normative elements, since the way quality is destined and whose preferences are taken into account are key normative choices that can dramatically alter the course of policy interventions12. This study aims to move towards a holistic, data driven understanding that aggregates economic and experiential aspects of QoL in order to evaluate urban standards of living.

This research approach is increasingly important in a world where “Smart Cities” that integrate digital and communication technologies as well as data analytics into the daily life of citizens are becoming a policy standard across the world13,14. Despite being propelled by incredible waves of techno optimism and widespread adoption across different continents, the Smart City concept has demonstrated a mixed effectiveness in solving human problems15–17. One of the most pressing agendas in this field is ensuring that research on urban quality of life does not reproduce existing models of urban policymaking that have, in some cases, led to structural inequalities and chronic neglect of alternative measures of wellbeing18,19. This approach tends to overemphasize factors such as economic prosperity over experiential aspects such as environmental wellbeing, leading to the implementation of Smart Cities that serve as engines for accelerating existing neoliberal patterns of growth, rather than allowing for critical evaluation or redirection of existing policies20. A holistic approach to the evaluation of quality of life encourages the development of a social data science that does not generate predictions and recommendations based on a thin slice of reality21, but enables different voices in urban environments to create community driven interventions that measurably improve quality of life22,23.

The second aim of this study is to provide a framework for intercity collaboration to solve common problems. A clustering framework is used to transform cities into multidimensional clusters that share common structural challenges despite differences in history, culture, and geography. This is done in hopes of encouraging collaboration through mutual learning and progress toward meaningful solutions to common problems24. The study begins with a review of the literature linking the variables of choice with changes in QoL in different cities.

Quality of Life Indicators

Economic Factors

Cost of living represents the relative cost of consumer goods and services like food, transport, and utilities, but excludes the housing costs like rent or mortgage. The relationship between cost of living and quality of life intricately relies on multiple factors. A higher cost of living could simply be a result of higher income and better urban amenities and thus correlate positively with a better QoL25. On the other hand, a higher cost of living might also be an indication of higher living expenses that reduce quality of life by lowering disposable income and increasing poverty rates26,27. It is essential to understand how variables at individual level, like relative income level, access to public services, and subjective wellbeing impact this relationship. People with relatively lower incomes suffer most due to cost of living crises, which affects their overall health and wellbeing28. Increases in living costs amplify the disproportion of accessibility of public services between higher and lower income neighborhoods29,30. Particularly in societies with high levels of income inequality, changes in living expenses can make people with low Subjective Wellbeing (SWB) feel further deprivation31. This study aims to provide tools for policymakers to explore the cost of living index in relation to other factors of quality of life. Such findings might encourage policymakers to build frameworks that optimize the quality of life in the city and offer residents basic needs regardless of income, location, or subjective wellbeing.

Groceries describes the cost of grocery items in a city relative to New York City. Grocery prices have a direct impact on the nutritional value and wellbeing of citizens by controlling accessibility to essential nutrients such as calories, protein, oils, and fats32. This effect is aggravated for those with lower socioeconomic status, since low income urban neighborhoods have a higher risk of diet related health problems due to high prices and low availability of affordable healthy food options33–36. Combining other conditions such as transportation and income, both of which tarnish access to food, higher prices at the store can drive those who cannot pay into poverty and hunger37,38. Grocery prices have key interactions with other factors in the study. Cities with higher crime rates, especially around grocery stores (evident in a lower safety index), can have higher grocery prices due to added costs of security measures and business losses39,40. Additionally, the impact is further magnified for those of lower socioeconomic status, with low income urban neighborhoods more likely to suffer from diet related illness due to the cost and lack of access to nutrition33–36. Local purchasing power indicates the relative purchasing power of residents in a city based on the average net salary. A strong positive correlation exists between local purchasing power and a city’s quality of life. Residents’ QoL is improved by having access to better housing, healthcare, and amenities as a result of increased purchasing power41. The tangible features of neighborhoods, such as amenities, social infrastructure, and accessibility to public transport, reflect living standards and purchasing power. These characteristics positively influence real estate values and QoL in city neighborhoods42.

Rent evaluates the average cost of renting apartments in a city in comparison to New York City. The relationship between rent prices and quality of life depends on various factors, including urban amenities, environmental conditions, and socioeconomic factors. Higher rents may simply be the result of a greater incentive to pay for high urban amenities and better living conditions, such as better weather, air quality, lower crime rates, and more accessible public transit25,43,44. These factors are positively correlated with higher quality of life45. However, rent has a different impact on people with a lower socioeconomic status who spend a larger portion of their income on rent due to a shortage of affordable housing, resulting in a greater financial burden and a lower quality of life46,47. There are significant correlations between rent prices and other variables in the dataset. For instance, certain cities would have a positive correlation with the safety and environment indices48. Rent prices would be positively impacted by proximity to cultural amenities like national landscapes or cultural sites49. Moreover, depending on the type of transportation facility, the impact of proximity to transportation infrastructure on rent prices will vary50,51. Finally, education and healthcare are additional factors that have a positive relationship with rent prices. Although there is some variation, higher rent prices are associated with hospitals and universities being closer and more accessible52.

Social Factors

Education is an important variable affecting QoL in a city, as higher educational attainment correlates with increased employment opportunities and economic growth. Cities with a higher concentration of educated individuals tend to experience faster productivity and income growth53. Not to mention that establishing high quality schools, including private and charter schools, can attract and retain professional families. These schools provide educational benefits that appeal to middle and upper class families, thereby encouraging them to settle in urban areas54,55.

Healthcare includes factors such as social determinants, urban planning, accessibility, and environmental health. While poor development increases health risks, as demonstrated during the COVID 19 pandemic in Jakarta56, emphasizing the significance of public areas, robust digital infrastructure, and equitable healthcare for urban health57. Integrated urban planning that incorporates green spaces, secure infrastructure, and orderly public spaces effectively promotes health and enhances livability58–61. In less developed neighborhoods, environmental health factors such as exposure to the natural environment and air and noise pollution, have a significant impact on wellbeing62. On the other hand, public green spaces enhance mental health outcomes and lower the prevalence of disease59. Linking healthcare to other variables of interest, wealthy neighborhoods are more likely to receive better services and have better health status than marginalized communities63, while and social and cultural ecosystem services decrease health risks by encouraging physical activity and reducing stress64.

Safety is represented as the inverse of the crime incidence rate index, which takes into account perceptions of crime levels within a given city, concerns about specific crimes (such as robbery, harassment, or assault), as well as the severity of property and violent crimes65. The link between crime levels and QoL is subject to different interpretations in the literature. Previous studies find a significant relationship between homicide and sexual assault rates and perceived quality of life, concluding that higher rates of violent crimes decrease the QoL of community inhabitants66. This negative impact likely results from actual harm to physical integrity and property67, but is also attributed to the psychological toll of crime exposure68, as well as the financial and emotional costs of taking precautionary measures against anticipated crimes69,70. Crime rates and their associated effects vary greatly across the cities in the sample. Both direct and indirect impacts of crime are recognized as contributing to lower QoL, given that perceptions of safety correlate with higher life satisfaction scores71,72.

Stability represents the absence of military threats in the form of terror attacks and active military operations. The physical, psychological, and institutional damage created by intense periods of instability is hard to estimate, an effect compounded by the negative impacts that fear of war and conflict has on civil society and economic performance73. Stable and predictable circumstances are necessary preconditions for exercising rights, like freedom of movement74, which are hampered by periods of instability. The feedback loops generated by periods of violence have long term effects on important factors that play a role even after conflicts conclude. Instability has been shown to affect both perceived QoL (such as environmental quality75 and family dynamics76) and recognized institutional indicators like access to services and employment availability77, as well as citizens’ mental health. A 2019 study in the Gaza Strip directly linked perennial instability with severe and chronic declines in perceived quality of life among university students78 due to the inhospitable environment resulting from a decade of ongoing military conflict.

Infrastructural and Environmental Factors

Environmental quality is generally defined in terms of the quality of access to natural environments in urban landscapes, which can be difficult to measure since interactions between citizens and natural environments are complex, taking place at different levels throughout a city’s urban environment79. In lieu of a comprehensive index measuring environmental quality, the study instead relies on a definition that captures the cleanliness and pleasantness of the environment through the absence of heavy pollution, using the inverse of Numbeo’s crowdsourced pollution index. It is important to capture the impact of interaction with the environment, even in this limited scope, due to its strong influence on quality of life80. Air and water pollution, garbage disposal and cleanliness, noise and light pollution, as well as the availability of green spaces are all factors used to create a relative metric for environmental quality81, which has important impacts on long term physical82 and mental83 health outcomes, in addition to reducing healthcare expenditures resulting from pollution84.

Infrastructure quality is another metric used to evaluate urban QoL, defined as the quality and accessibility of technical and transportation infrastructure available to a city’s inhabitants85. Consistent access to quality roads and transportation networks, public transportation, water and sanitation networks, heat and energy supplies, as well as waste management systems is critical for citizen wellbeing86, as is the successful scaling and maintenance of these services as a city’s population grows and its physical infrastructure deteriorates over time87. Investments in infrastructure create positive spillover effects on other mechanisms for achieving higher QoL. Well maintained transportation systems allow citizens to access essential services, and have been linked to social and economic development88 and productivity gains89.

Demographic Factors

The interplay between population size, population growth rate, and quality of life (QoL) in the urban environment is complex. Larger cities have the advantage of economies of scale, a more diverse environment, and a more diverse labor force that can enhance QoL. At the same time, rapidly growing populations can cause stress on infrastructure, housing markets, the environment, and public utilities, potentially causing overpopulation, congestion, deterioration of the environment, and shortages of resources90–94. High QoL cities which possess good healthcare systems, schools, economic opportunities, and environmental wellbeing only serve to attract more individuals, establishing a feedback loop by which the mobility of population brings about even more development and improvement in standards of living95–97.

Data Description

To construct the dataset, data was collected from 4 sources to include insights from formal yearly indices on city QoL as well as crowd sourced user data from Numbeo. The combined data includes the global livability index98, crime index65, pollution index99 and population index100. The data from the listed sources was merged into one dataset, by matching the city names across the different indexes manually. Cities that were not present in all 4 indexes were removed. In total and after removing cities with empty values, the dataset ended up with 99 of the top performing cities on the Global Livability Index. From the 5 data sources 11 features were chosen, while others were dropped because they were included within the features kept. The dataset incorporates multiple indicators to assess urban quality of life, categorized into economic, social, infrastructural, and environmental dimensions. Stability measures the threat of terror and military conflict, while Healthcare evaluates the availability and quality of medical services, including public, private, and over the counter drug access. Education assesses the quality of both private and public schooling systems. Infrastructure examines the quality of road networks, public transport, international connectivity, and utilities like energy, water, and telecommunications. Economic factors include the Cost of Living Index, which compares prices of goods and services across cities, the Rent Index (bench marked against New York City), the Groceries Index (measuring food prices relative to NYC), and Local Purchasing Power, indicating how much an average salary can buy compared to NYC. Demographic variables include Population (current city size). Environmental conditions are captured by Environment_Quality, representing pollution levels (inverted; higher values indicate cleaner cities), and Safety Index, which inverts crime perceptions to quantify security levels. Together, these variables provide a multidimensional framework for evaluating urban livability. Each observation is identified by the corresponding city name in a feature called “City”. On the other hand, high levels of multicollinearity make a comparative analysis difficult, and thus we turn to Principal Component Analysis as the natural solution to this challenge, allowing us to inform QoL metrics given all of our indicators while addressing the inherit multicollinearity in measuring codependent variables.

Methodology

All numeric variables were standardized to mean = 0 and SD = 1.Classifying cities based on multidimensional indices is the first goal of the data analysis. To determine the number of clusters the study will use, NbClust was run on the standardized dataset. NbClust is an R package that provides a comprehensive framework for identifying the optimal number of clusters by implementing up to 30 different validity indices and supporting comparisons across multiple clustering methods101. It was chosen for its flexibility and the breadth of its validation metrics, which enabled a more informed and robust selection process. Results from the complete linkage, Ward D2, and K means clustering methods were compared. Complete linkage and Ward D2 are hierarchical clustering approaches that differ in how they measure the distance between clusters. Complete linkage uses the maximum distance between points, while Ward D2 minimizes the total within cluster variance, whereas K means is a partition based method that aims to assign data points to a prespecified number of clusters by minimizing the variance within each cluster. Additionally, alongside t-SNE visualizations, dendrograms generated by the complete and Ward D2 methods were used to help guide the final choice of the number of clusters. T-SNE was used as it is a nonlinear dimensionality reduction technique that allowed visualization of the high dimensional structure of the data in two dimensions, making it easier to observe the separation and compactness of clusters102.

After introducing a new “cluster” feature to the standardized dataset, the study utilized Principal Component Analysis for dimensionality reduction, to allow ease of interpretation and visualization while maintaining all variables103. Kaiser Criterion was utilized to decide upon the fitting number of PCs to use. Then the loading matrix was used to ensure that the loadings were positive to preserve interpretability. Furthermore, to interpret the obtained PC scores, varimax rotation was utilized. Varimax rotation improves and simplifies the interpretability of PCA results by maximizing the squared loadings variance such that each variable loads strongly on one component and weakly on all others104. This makes it easy to identify and name important patterns in the data. After interpreting the clusters using PCA and varimax rotation, the clusters were interpreted using the original variables by using simple pie charts per continent and by using Tukey’s HSD method. Tukey’s HSD, which stands for Honestly Significant Difference, is a post hoc test applied following ANOVA to compare all possible pairs of group means while controlling the familywise Type I error rate105. It provides confidence intervals and p-values to decide which specific group differences are statistically significant, while giving familywise error protection. This was done to explore the differentiating aspects of each cluster in comparison with the others, supporting the PCA findings. Finally, decision tree and XGBoost models were trained using the training dataset, with the “cluster” being the target feature while splitting the data into training and testing datasets using stratified sampling to preserve the proportion of the classes, using an 80-20 split. XGBoost (Extreme Gradient Boosting) is an advanced ensemble learning method that builds multiple decision trees sequentially, where each new tree corrects the errors of the previous ones, optimizing a differentiable loss function through gradient boosting106. This approach significantly enhances classification accuracy while controlling overfitting through regularization and early stopping mechanisms. The XGBoost model was used to generate a variable importance plot based on the Gain metric, which quantifies the contribution of each feature to the model’s predictive performance. This helps identify the most influential features for classifying cities into clusters. Additionally, a single decision tree was visualized to illustrate the key distinguishing variables between clusters in an interpretable manner.

We chose to cluster our standardized dataset using Ward D2 clustering over other methods to obtain minimum variance between clusters. Additionally, our choice of Ward.D2 allows for a granular inspection of cluster formation using the dendrogram. This is important to our data analysis due to the exploratory nature of this research. Moreover, the NbClust method demonstrated more votes for three clusters using Ward.D2 clustering.

T-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) was employed to better visualize the structure of the high dimensional data in a lower dimensional space. Results of the PC Variables

Normative Judgement (PC1)

Positive loadings for Healthcare, Education, and Price Indices. This PC was interpreted as representing the Normative Judgment and Preference satisfaction approaches to the evaluation of QoL. Developed cities score well on this index for two reasons.

High-income cities benefit from agglomeration effects, attracting and increasing the efficiency of services like education and healthcare, but this comes at the expense of a high cost of living as more high-earners compete for limited resources. The second aspect is that these cities are willing and able to accurately measure the impact of these services, lacing them at an advantage when it comes to evaluating QoL from a Normative Judgment/Preference Satisfaction approach. Cities that score highly on this PC are what we would commonly associate with a high QoL in the general understanding of the term.

Cities known for high costs of living, like Geneva, Zurich and Osaka, scored highly on this PC, while cities in the developing world like Manila, New Delhi and Jakarta had large negative scores, supporting our interpretation.

Individual Experience (PC2)

Positive loadings for stability, safety, and infrastructure.

The second principal component likely accounts for variables that impact the experiential QoL in cities, combining crowdsourced data on safety with normative indices for stability and infrastructure. Stability was defined by the Global Livability Index to describe the effect of various kinds of unrest on city residents, reflecting geopolitical pressures on city dwellers. Infrastructure measures the quality of access to roads, public transportation, as well as energy, water and telecommunications infrastructure. Safety similarly measures the lack of criminal activity . These variables evidently contribute to the subjective well-being of communities and individuals, and their positive loadings suggest potential for virtuous cycles, where investment in factors which benefit experiential QoL can create positive feedback loops, such as safety correlating with higher levels of political stability.

North American cities like Washington, D.C., Chicago, and Detroit had high negative scores on this PC, while cities including Ljubljana, Abu Dhabi, and Tallinn, which are known for responsible and proactive municipal policies, the integration of smart city initiatives, and high relative levels of safety, scored the highest.

Human Ecology (PC3)

Positive loading for Environmental Quality. Negative loading for population. The third and final principal component may seem simplistic in relation to the other two, but captures the well-documented link between population growth and environmental impact. The negative loading of population can be interpreted theoretically and empirically. The empirical justification points to the fact that densely populated cities (which are biased towards lower-income countries in our dataset) are more common in developing countries where investment into the energy transition has been lagging. Additionally, densely populated cities naturally face difficulties in regulating environmental quality due to the required inputs (energy, etc.) and outputs (traffic volume, etc.) associated with a larger population, which contribute to a larger environmental footprint. Our preferred interpretation for this PC is the ability to a city to balance the perennial problem of population pressure with institutional frameworks that allow for a sustainable ecological footprint relative to their size.

Analyzing the range of performance on this PC reveals the complexity involved in managing human ecology. While the lowest ranking performers include Tokyo, Shanghai and Jakarta (some of the most populous cities in our sample), the performers include Durban and Cape Town where investment into real progress towards carbon neutrality has been made under the framework of national and municipal reforms at the national and municipal level.

Classifying Cities by Cluster

In order to push the analysis further towards meaningful conclusions, decision tree and XGBoost models were used to better understand how the quality-of-life indicators separated the three clusters.

The data were randomly partitioned into a training set and a test set using a stratified (70/30 split) using (createDataPartition in the caret package), ensuring that the relative proportions of the three clusters were preserved in both sets. The training set was used exclusively for model fitting and internal tuning, whereas the test set was used once to assess model performance.

We fitted a decision tree using the rpart package with the cluster labels as the response and all remaining variables as predictors. The tree relied on the Gini impurity as the splitting criterion and employed 10-fold internal cross-validation (xval = 10) to estimate the cross-validated error and guide complexity control. The complexity parameter was set to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$cp = 0.01$$\end{document}, so that only splits that reduce the relative error by at least 1% were allowed, which mitigates overfitting given the modest sample size. The minimum number of observations required to attempt a split was set to minsplit = 10, and the minimum terminal node size was minbucket = 3, preventing overly small leaves. The maximum tree depth was left at its default of 30, but the combination of cp and minsplit effectively constrained tree growth. Class priors were set to the empirical cluster frequencies in the data (0.30, 0.37, and 0.33 for clusters 1, 2, and 3, respectively), and a symmetric misclassification loss matrix (zero on the diagonal and one off-diagonal) was used. The main hyperparameter settings are summarized in Table 2, and the results of the confusion matrix are shown in Fig. 12. Although the tree was used primarily as an interpretable model, it achieved a training accuracy of 0.914 and a test accuracy of 0.931. Afterward, we ran the XGBoost model using the default hyperparameters from the XGBoost R package with stratified sampling (70/30 split) to get the variable importance plot.

Given the significant structural differences between cities in the dataset, the study hopes that new cities can be incorporated into the theoretically meaningful clusters developed through the use of classification models, benefiting from the experience of other cities in similar clusters to implement policy reforms that can address structural factors that may not be obvious at first glance, as well as identifying under examined aspects of QoL as defined by low scores on PC2 and PC3. The conclusions presented by the decision tree in Fig. 13 are interesting, as they suggest that the most important factor in identifying which of the three clusters a city belongs to is stability. This is theoretically meaningful since stability internalizes different variables and provides important intuition on the level of development of the country as a whole. Cities in cluster 3 exhibit lower levels of stability overall, which reminds us of the importance of examining urban QoL within the context of broader political and economic developments.

The next split highlights education as being an important metric in determining between the well-performing cities of Clusters 1 and 2. Higher levels of education are naturally correlated with cluster 1 cities, but this relationship is not straightforward, as the following splits highlight environmental quality and rent as important distinguishes between the two clusters. This is meaningful, since it highlights the fact that cluster 2 cities do not lag far behind in the provision of services like education, which partially explains their high QoL. Another advantage of this visualization is confirming the value of experiential and ecological measures of QoL. This is supported by examining the variable importance plot shown in Supplementary Fig. S5, confirming that variables like stability, education and infrastructure are highly relevant in classifying cities.

The decision tree / XGBoost model improve the validity of the findings by providing more theoretical links between the clusters, PCs and original variables, as well as highlighting clear and actionable steps that cities in different clusters can take in order to improve the QoL of their citizens. Discussion and Conclusion

The data analysis utilized different statistical methods of analysis in order to investigate the relationship between different social indicators measuring the QoL in cities. By combining data from yearly indices with crowdsourced information gathered at the individual level. Through the use of Ward D2 clustering, the dataset of 99 cities was classified into three meaningful clusters that correlate to three Principal Components, meaningfully combining insights from different approaches towards evaluating Quality of Life, bridging the gap between the normative judgments and individual experience. Furthermore, classification models were utilized to highlight important variables in distinguishing between clusters, highlighting key indicators that internalized many aspects of urban life, and laying the foundation for policy recommendations that would allow cities in different clusters to cooperate and share valuable insights that can be applied to their sister cities. The next stage of this analysis necessarily involves moving from descriptive, normative analysis to positive, causal investigation.

A novel application of these findings is the potential for the development of a data driven urban diagnostic model inspired by Hausmann and Rodrik108 Growth Diagnostics approach. Under this framework, the most common constraints and challenges towards increased QoL relative to other clusters could be identified, aggregated, and decomposed into contributing structural factors. Collaboration to study and resolve these challenges can be conducted on an intercluster and an intracluster basis. Under the former approach, cities in different clusters could learn from each other’s successes and shortcomings, identifying causes of divergence in different QoL regions. Under the latter, cities that face similar constraints can pool together resources towards developing efficient and scalable solutions that bring together expertise from different regions of the world and leverage economies of scale to develop solutions whose costs would otherwise be a barrier for an individual city.

A second practical application is the identification of leverage points and critical factors that distinguish between different clusters. These conclusions could be developed further to identify mechanisms for addressing these critical factors that would allow certain cities to achieve better outcomes. For example, cities like Abu Dhabi and Taipei that are grouped into cluster 2 are often used as benchmarks for cities in developing countries, and a granular analysis of the distinguishing features and policies that set these cities apart from other cities in the global south could provide dynamic road maps and policy recommendations for municipalities that hope to emulate the success of cities in other clusters.

The findings and methods in applying these statistical techniques to the study of cities have relevant applications in urban planning, urban economics, comparative urban analysis, as well as municipal and national policymaking initiatives. Future studies could examine a larger sample of more diverse cities (given that this study focused on top performing cities) using an expanded set of variables, explore the results of different clustering methodologies, as well as use techniques such as Factor Analysis to derive deeper insights building on the Principal Component Analysis. The analysis importantly confirms that while geography, culture and history play a critical role in shaping the QoL across different cities, structural elements and active policy interventions are just as important in describing similarities between cities. Additionally, the study recognizes that the clusters are not static. In the words of French poet Charles Baudelaire109 describing the transformation of his native Paris in the 19th century, “the form of a city changes faster, alas! than a mortal’s heart.” The use of data driven insights can help in ensuring that the form taken by cities of the future is one that prioritizes the wellbeing and satisfaction of their citizens.
