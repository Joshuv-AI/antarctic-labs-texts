Quality retention and energy optimization in ohmic vacuum concentration of white mulberry syrup
Authors: Soraya Kakaeie, Mahmoud Koushesh Saba, Hosain Darvishi, Sirvan Mansouri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study systematically compared hybrid ohmic-vacuum heating technology with conventional ohmic heating at atmospheric pressure (100 kPa, used as the control treatment) for white mulberry syrup concentration. The experimental design consisted of two operational modes: (a) conventional ohmic heating at atmospheric pressure (100 kPa, baseline control), and (b) hybrid ohmic-vacuum heating at reduced pressures (50 and 75 kPa). Each mode was evaluated at three voltage gradients (10, 20, and 30 V/cm). Multi-objective optimization was performed using NSGA-II to simultaneously maximize bioactive compounds while minimizing processing time and energy consumption. Results demonstrated that increasing voltage gradient from 10 to 30 V/cm reduced processing time by 75.3% in the control (atmospheric pressure), whereas under ohmic-vacuum conditions (50 and 75 kPa), processing times were 81–295% longer than the control treatment due to reduced electrical conductivity at lower temperatures. Total phenolic content decreased by 28–48% across all treatments, with best retention (33.1 mg GAE/100 mL) achieved at 50 kPa and 10 V/cm. Antioxidant capacity showed excellent preservation (62.13–86.12%), peaking at 86.12% under intermediate conditions (75 kPa, 20 V/cm). Total flavonoid content exhibited dramatic variability (60.22–171.20 mg CE/100 mL), with maximum retention at 50 kPa and 10 V/cm. Counterintuitively, the control treatment demonstrated superior energy efficiency, with specific energy consumption 2.5-3.7-fold lower than the hybrid ohmic-vacuum method. NSGA-II optimization identified the best compromise conditions at 83.47 kPa and 26.82 V/cm, yielding: TPC = 30.85 mg GAE/100 mL, DPPH = 84.73%, TFC = 78.14 mg CE/100 mL, processing time = 12.24 min, and SEC = 2.87 MJ/kg water evp.

Introduction

White mulberry (Morus alba L.) is a plant of significant agricultural and nutritional importance, traditionally cultivated for its leaves to feed silkworms but increasingly valued for its succulent and nutritious fruits. The berries are rich in bioactive compounds, including anthocyanins, flavonoids, and phenolic acids, which are associated with a range of health benefits such as antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-diabetic properties1. However, the high moisture content and delicate structure of fresh mulberries make them highly perishable, leading to substantial post-harvest losses and limiting their commercial shelf-life and distribution2.

Among various preservation methods for mulberries—including drying, freezing, and refrigeration—each has inherent limitations. Drying causes substantial thermal degradation of bioactive compounds, freezing requires energy-intensive cold chain infrastructure often unavailable in mulberry-growing regions, and refrigeration offers only short-term preservation3,5. Concentration into syrup was selected because it enables ambient-temperature storage, reduces transport volume, adds economic value to perishable seasonal fruits, and aligns with traditional practices familiar to local producers3. White mulberry syrup is commonly consumed diluted with water as a refreshing drink, used as a natural sweetener in tea and dairy products, incorporated into desserts and dressings, or taken directly as a health supplement.

Among conventional methods, thermal evaporation is the most widely used industrial technique. However, the application of prolonged high temperatures in such processes is a major drawback, often leading to the degradation of thermolabile nutrients4, non-enzymatic browning5, loss of volatile aroma compounds6, and undesirable changes in color and flavor7. These detrimental effects ultimately compromise the sensory and nutritional quality of the final product, diminishing its market value and health-promoting potential8.

In the quest for more efficient and gentle processing technologies, ohmic heating (OH) has emerged as a promising alternative to conventional methods. Ohmic heating, also known as Joule heating, is an electrothermal technique where an alternating electric current is passed directly through the food material, generating heat volumetrically due to its electrical resistance9. This internal generation of heat can result in rapid and uniform temperature distribution, minimizing thermal gradients and reducing the risk of localized overheating and burn-on, which are common in conventional heat exchangers10. Consequently, as demonstrated by researcher’s, OH can better preserve heat-sensitive compounds, leading to products with superior quality compared to those processed by conventional heating11.

Despite its advantages, ohmic heating at atmospheric pressure still involves high temperatures that can, over time, affect sensitive components12. To further mitigate thermal damage, the combination of ohmic heating with vacuum concentration (VC) presents an innovative solution. Vacuum concentration lowers the boiling point of the liquid by reducing the operating pressure, thereby allowing evaporation to occur at significantly lower temperatures. The synergy of ohmic heating under vacuum—often termed Ohmic-Vacuum Concentration (OVC)—creates a highly efficient and mild processing environment13. The rapid and uniform heating of OH, coupled with the low-temperature evaporation of VC, holds great potential for maximizing the retention of bioactive compounds while simultaneously improving energy efficiency14. The energy efficiency arises from the direct energy transfer in OH, which avoids the heat transfer barriers of traditional heating surfaces, and the reduced latent heat of vaporization at lower pressures15. Darvishi et al.12 reported that ohmic heating of mulberry juice resulted in 3–4.5 times higher total phenol content and 4.6–5.3 times lower energy consumption compared to conventional heating. Similar advantages have been documented for other fruit matrices. Alkanan et al.1 observed that ohmic-vacuum combination heating of tomato paste led to more regular cell structures and lower treatment time compared to conventional heating. Norouzi et al.27 reported that conventional heating caused greater color degradation in sour cherry juice compared to ohmic heating, while acidity showed no significant difference between methods. Darvishi et al.11 found that vitamin C loss in orange concentrate under ohmic-vacuum (10–29.2%) was significantly lower than under conventional vacuum heating (47.4%), and total phenol changes were only 8.0–21.3% for ohmic-vacuum compared to 49.6% for conventional vacuum heating. Additionally, Sabanci and Icier28 reported that ohmic-assisted vacuum evaporation reduced the total process time for sour cherry juice concentration compared to conventional vacuum evaporation. Vangapandu and Bitra50 also confirmed better retention of ascorbic acid and chromatic properties in guava juice concentrate using ohmic heating-assisted vacuum evaporation versus rotary vacuum evaporation. Similarly, Darvishi et al.10 demonstrated that ohmic-vacuum better preserved antioxidant capacity and ascorbic acid in kiwifruit juice compared to atmospheric ohmic heating. These comparative findings consistently indicate that ohmic-based methods, whether under atmospheric or vacuum conditions, outperform conventional heating in preserving nutritional quality and improving energy efficiency.

While the individual benefits of ohmic heating and vacuum concentration have been studied for various fruit juices, their combined application for the concentration of white mulberry syrup remains a relatively unexplored area. Therefore, the objectives of this study are to systematically investigate the Ohmic-Vacuum Concentration process for white mulberry syrup. This research aims to evaluate the effects of key process parameters on critical quality attributes—such as total phenolic content, antioxidant activity, and total flavonoid content—and on the specific energy consumption and processing time. Furthermore, the process will be optimized to identify the ideal operating conditions that achieve the desired concentration level while maximizing product quality and minimizing processing time and energy use, thereby contributing to the development of sustainable and high-quality food processing techniques.

Materials and methods

Sample preparation

Fresh white mulberries (Morus alba L.) were procured from a mulberry orchard located in Kermanshah Province, Iran, and immediately transported to the laboratory. Upon arrival, the fruits were stored at 4 °C under refrigeration for approximately 8 h until experimental procedures commenced.

Fresh mulberries were initially washed thoroughly with tap water to remove surface contaminants and debris. After washing, the fruits were air-dried at ambient temperature for 15 min to remove excess surface moisture. The prepared mulberries were then homogenized using a fruit blender (1200 W, 30,000 rpm, Bosch, Germany) for 5 min to achieve complete tissue disruption. The resulting homogenate was filtered through a fine mesh to obtain a uniform juice consistency and remove particulate matter.

For ohmic heating experiments, the prepared sample was diluted with distilled water at a ratio of 100 g sample to 25 mL water (4:1 w/v). This dilution was necessary to (1) enhance the electrical conductivity of the sample for efficient ohmic heating, as high-sugar juices exhibit low ionic mobility10,40,47, and (2) prevent premature sugar caramelization and electrode fouling during the initial heating stage48. The added water was subsequently removed during the vacuum concentration process, yielding a final syrup with the desired solid content. This dilution-before-concentration approach is well-established in fruit syrup production49.

Ohmic heating system

The experimental apparatus (Fig. 1) consisted of a rectangular Pyrex glass chamber (10 × 10 × 20 cm, 1 mm thickness) equipped with two 316 L stainless steel electrodes (1.5 mm thickness) installed on opposite sides of the cell. The ohmic cell was housed within a 4-liter vacuum chamber to ensure uniform pressure distribution across the sample and maintain safe operating conditions during reduced pressure treatments. The system was integrated with a voltage regulating transformer (1 kW, 0–330 V, 50 Hz, MST-3, Japan), a coated type-K thermocouple for temperature monitoring, and a power analyzer (Lutron DW-6090, Taiwan) for real-time electrical parameter measurements. The data recorded by the power analyzer (power consumption, current, and voltage passed through the sample) were used to calculate the specific energy consumption of the ohmic heating system. The vacuum system comprised a piston dry vacuum pump (HL model, Guanyu, China), a buffer tank to minimize operational pulsations, and a condenser unit containing an ice-water mixture to recover evaporated water from the concentrated product. The mass of evaporated water during the concentration process (mw) was determined by direct mass balance. The initial mass of the sample (mi) was measured before each experiment using a digital balance (accuracy ± 0.01 g). After the concentration process reached the target 60 °Brix, the final mass of the concentrate (mf) was measured using the same balance. For all vacuum‑assisted experiments, the vacuum pump was operated at a constant electrical power input. Prior to the experimental campaign, the actual power consumption of the pump was measured using a digital power analyzer (Lutron DW-6090, Taiwan). The measured value was found to be in good agreement with the rated power specified in the pump’s datasheet. During the experiments, the desired vacuum pressure inside the system was achieved and maintained by adjusting a throttling valve installed between the vacuum pump and the test section. As a result, the electrical power supplied to the pump remained unchanged across all vacuum levels tested, while the pressure was modulated solely by the valve setting. This method decouples the pump power from the instantaneous pressure, and the measured constant power value is used in the specific energy consumption (SEC) calculations reported in this study. All process parameters including temperature, sample mass, current, and voltage were recorded at 1 s intervals using a PC-based data acquisition system.

Experimental conditions

Ohmic heating experiments were conducted under two distinct operational modes: (1) reduced pressure conditions (50 and 75 kPa absolute) and (2) atmospheric pressure (100 kPa), with each mode evaluated at three voltage gradients (10, 20, and 30 V/cm). Due to the laboratory’s elevation of approximately 1459 m above sea level, the atmospheric pressure was measured at less than 101.3 kPa, corresponding to a water boiling point of 92.7 ± 0.5 °C.

For each experimental run, approximately 150 g of prepared sample was transferred into the ohmic heating cell. Prior to treatment initiation, the vacuum pump was operated for 2 min to achieve stable vacuum conditions at the juice surface. The solution was concentrated to 60°Brix, a target selected based on three converging criteria: (i) microbial stability, as soluble solid contents above 55–60 °Brix reduce water activity sufficiently to inhibit the growth of most spoilage microorganisms and enable ambient-temperature storage without preservatives10–12; (ii) alignment with commercial syrup standards, since white mulberry syrups marketed in the Middle East and Central Asia are conventionally produced and sold at 60–65 °Brix49; and (iii) physicochemical and sensory acceptability, as concentrations below this range result in a product with insufficient viscosity and sweetness intensity, while concentrations above 65 °Brix risk sugar crystallization during storage. After each experiment, the ohmic cell was thoroughly cleaned using distilled water and a soft brush to prevent system contamination and maintain consistent experimental conditions.

Quality assessment

Total phenolic content (TPC)

Total phenolic content was determined using the Folin–Ciocalteu method16. Frozen samples (0.5 g) were homogenized with 2 mL of cold 1% methanolic HCl (methanol: 1% HCl, v/v) in a porcelain mortar. The homogenate was transferred into 2 mL microtubes and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C (MICRO HETTCH, Germany). An aliquot of 330 µL of the resulting supernatant was mixed with 200 µL of distilled water and 25 µL of Folin–Ciocalteu reagent (diluted 1:9 with distilled water). The reaction mixture (total volume = 555 µL) was transferred into a cuvette, and the absorbance was measured at 750 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Unico UV-2100, USA) after 30 min of incubation at room temperature in the dark. A calibration curve was prepared using gallic acid standards at concentrations of 0, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35, and 40 µg/mL. The same procedure (including the same dilution of Folin reagent, sodium carbonate percentage – here, sodium carbonate was not used in this protocol – aliquot volume, final reaction volume, and incubation time) was applied to all standards. Total phenolic content was calculated from the gallic acid standard curve and expressed as mg of gallic acid equivalents per 100 mL of Syrup (mg GAE/100 mL syrup).

Antioxidant capacity (DPPH Assay)

The antioxidant capacity was evaluated based on the scavenging activity of the 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical, following the procedure of Sánchez-Moreno et al. (1998). DPPH (3.94 mg) was dissolved in methanol and made up to 100 mL with the same solvent to obtain a 0.01 mM DPPH solution. Then, 0.3 mL of the extract was added to 2.0 mL of this methanolic DPPH solution. The reaction mixture was kept in the dark for 30 min. Subsequently, the absorbance was measured at 515 nm using a Unico UV-Vis spectrophotometer (USA). A control sample was prepared by mixing 0.3 mL of 1% acidic methanol with 2.0 mL of the DPPH solution. Total flavonoid content (TFC)

The total flavonoid content was determined using the aluminum chloride colorimetric method as described by Zhishen et al. (1999). Briefly, 200 µL of the acid-methanol extract (prepared for TPC analysis) was mixed with 1280 µL of distilled water and 60 µL of sodium nitrite solution (5% w/v). After 5 min, 60 µL of aluminum chloride solution (10% w/v) was added. The mixture was allowed to stand for 6 min before 400 µL of sodium hydroxide solution (1 M) was added. The final solution was vortexed thoroughly, and after 5 min, the absorbance was measured at 510 nm against a prepared blank. The TFC was quantified using a catechin standard curve (0–500 ppm) and expressed as mg of catechin equivalents per 100 mL of syrup (mg CE/100 mL syrup).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in triplicate. Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The effect of processing conditions on selected properties was analyzed using univariate analysis of variance. Where ANOVA indicated significant differences (p ≤ 0.05), Duncan’s multiple range test was applied for post-hoc mean comparison. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 18.0).

Optimization methodology

All experimental conditions and their corresponding parameters are summarized in Table 1. The optimization of the ohmic-vacuum concentration process for white mulberry syrup was formulated as a multi-objective optimization problem, aiming to simultaneously enhance product quality attributes while minimizing process inefficiencies. A Non-dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm II (NSGA-II) was employed to address this complex optimization challenge due to its proven capability in handling competing objectives and generating diverse Pareto-optimal solutions43. The independent variables consisted of system pressure (P: 50–100 kPa) and voltage gradient (∇V: 10–30 V/cm), while five critical response variables were evaluated and categorized into two groups based on optimization objectives. Quality attributes—total phenolic content (TPC, mg GAE/100 mL), antioxidant activity measured by DPPH radical scavenging assay (%), and total flavonoid content (TFC, mg CE/100 mL)—were designated for maximization to ensure superior nutritional quality of the final concentrate. Process efficiency parameters—processing time (min) and specific energy consumption (SEC, MJ/kg water evp.)—were targeted for minimization to achieve economically viable and sustainable industrial operation.

Prior to optimization, mathematical modeling of the relationship between independent variables and each response was performed using TableCurve 3D software. This software systematically evaluated thousands of potential model equations, including polynomial, rational, logarithmic, and exponential functions, to identify the best-fit model for each response based on statistical criteria. Model selection was guided by three primary indicators: the coefficient of determination (R²) to assess the proportion of variance explained by the model, the fit standard error (FSR) to evaluate prediction accuracy, and the F-statistic to determine overall model significance. The NSGA-II algorithm was implemented with 100 chromosomes and 150 generations, using crossover rate of 0.8 and mutation rate of 0.2 to ensure robust convergence and solution diversity. Each chromosome encoded pressure and voltage gradient values within their feasible ranges.

The evolutionary process began with random population initialization. In each generation, solutions were evaluated using five objective functions from regression models. Fast non-dominated sorting classified solutions into Pareto fronts based on dominance principles, where Pareto-optimal solutions represent trade-offs where no objective improves without compromising others44,45. Crowding distance calculations-maintained diversity along the Pareto front. Binary tournament selection chose parents based on non-domination rank and crowding distance, while blend crossover and uniform mutation generated offspring.

Min-max normalization scaled all objectives to [0,1] range, ensuring equal importance45. For maximization objectives (TPC, DPPH, TFC), normalization used (Y-Ymin)/(Ymax -Ymin); for minimization objectives (Time, SEC), (Ymax-Y)/(Ymax -Ymin) was applied46. The best compromise solution, identified by highest composite fitness value from the Pareto front, provided optimal operating conditions balancing bioactive retention and process efficiency.

Results and discussion

Thermal behavior and processing time

The thermal profiles of the juice sample during the ohmic heating-assisted concentration process are presented in Fig. 2. Under conventional ohmic heating (100 kPa), the sample temperature reached a plateau of 86.5 ± 1.2 °C. In contrast, under ohmic-vacuum conditions, the plateau temperature was much lower. It was 66.2 ± 1.0 °C for 50 kPa and 76.5 ± 1.9 °C for 75 kPa, meaning the temperature dropped by 10 to 20 °C. This depression in the boiling point is a direct thermodynamic consequence of reduced pressure, a well-documented phenomenon that enhances the preservation of heat-sensitive compounds17.

Fig. 2Temperature profile of white mulberry syrups during ohmic-vacuum concentration.

Temperature profile of white mulberry syrups during ohmic-vacuum concentration.

The results demonstrate a decrease in processing time (Fig. 3) as the voltage gradient increases across all pressure levels. For instance, at atmospheric pressure (100 kPa), elevating the voltage gradient from 10 V/cm to 30 V/cm caused the heating time to plummet from 31.23 min to just 7.70 min. This represents a dramatic 75.3% reduction in time. A similarly strong trend was observed at an intermediate vacuum of 75 kPa, where the time decreased from 33.15 min to 14.58 min, equating to a 56.0% reduction. Even under the strongest vacuum of 50 kPa, where the overall times were longer, increasing the voltage still yielded a 23.1% reduction from 39.55 min to 30.42 min. This powerful effect is a direct consequence of the principles of Joule heating, where thermal energy generated within the syrup is proportional to the square of the electric field strength15. As shown in Fig. 2, higher voltage gradients produced steeper heating curves and shorter times to reach the boiling plateau, confirming accelerated heat generation. For example, at 100 kPa, increasing the voltage gradient from 10 to 30 V/cm reduced processing time from 31.23 to 7.70 min (75.3% reduction), reflecting the substantially higher heating rate at elevated voltage gradients18.

Fig. 3Effect of voltage gradient and pressure on processing time of white mulberry syrups in ohmic heating system.

Effect of voltage gradient and pressure on processing time of white mulberry syrups in ohmic heating system.

At a constant voltage gradient, intensifying the vacuum (reducing pressure from 100 kPa to 50 kPa) consistently led to an increase in processing time. For example, at 30 V/cm, the time increased by 295%, from 7.70 min at 100 kPa to 30.42 min at 50 kPa. This counter-intuitive trend may be explained by the interplay between vacuum conditions and electrical conductivity. While vacuum reduces the boiling point of the syrup—which is beneficial for preserving heat-sensitive compounds—it also lowers the operating temperature. The electrical conductivity of ionic solutions such as fruit syrup is known to be strongly temperature-dependent, generally decreasing as temperature decreases10,19. A reduction in conductivity would limit the flow of electrical current and thus reduce internal heat generation during ohmic heating10,13,15. Consequently, the decrease in heating efficiency may outweigh the advantage of the lower boiling point, leading to a slower evaporation rate and longer processing time14,20. It should be noted that electrical conductivity was not directly measured in this study; therefore, this explanation is based on established findings reported in the literature.

Total phenol content (TPC)

The TPC of the initially prepared mulberry sample was 46.2 mg GAE/100 mL. The TPC values of the final concentrated syrups ranged from 24.2 to 33.1 mg GAE/100 mL (Fig. 4), reflecting a 28–48% reduction attributable to the sensitivity of phenolic compounds to heat and environmental conditions10,35.

Fig. 4Effect of voltage gradient and pressure on total phenol content (TPC) of white mulberry syrups.

Effect of voltage gradient and pressure on total phenol content (TPC) of white mulberry syrups.

At 50 kPa, TPC declined monotonically with increasing voltage gradient, from 33.1 at 10 V/cm to 29.8 mg GAE/100 mL at 30 V/cm. Although vacuum conditions lower the boiling point and theoretically protect heat-sensitive phenolics, this benefit is progressively offset by localized hot spots generated at higher electrical fields, which accelerate oxidative degradation21,22.

At 75 kPa, a non-linear pattern emerged. TPC was lowest at 10 V/cm (24.2 mg GAE/100 mL), peaked at 20 V/cm (31.5 mg GAE/100 mL), then declined at 30 V/cm (28.2 mg GAE/100 mL). Prolonged exposure at slow heating rates causes cumulative oxidative damage, while moderate heating at 20 V/cm minimizes thermal exposure duration. Exceeding this threshold at 30 V/cm reintroduces localized overheating effects23–25. These findings align with Darvishi et al.10, who reported increasing TPC decline with higher voltage gradients in kiwi juice due to electrochemical effects. Optimal retention at 20 V/cm and 75 kPa mirrors results in black grape juice23, confirming that balancing electrical field intensity and pressure is critical for phenolic preservation.

At the molecular level, the observed changes in TPC can be attributed to the diverse chemical structures of phenolic compounds and their varying stability under processing conditions. Phenolics, including phenolic acids, flavonoids, and anthocyanins, differ in the number and position of hydroxyl groups, which directly influence their susceptibility to oxidation and thermal degradation. Compounds with ortho-dihydroxy structures are particularly prone to enzymatic and non-enzymatic oxidation, leading to quinone formation and subsequent polymerization or degradation. In addition, electrochemical reactions occurring during ohmic heating may promote the generation of reactive oxygen species, further accelerating phenolic breakdown10. Matrix interactions also play a significant role; phenolic compounds can bind to proteins, polysaccharides, and cell wall components, which may either protect them by limiting oxygen exposure or reduce their extractability, thereby lowering measured TPC53. Furthermore, the disruption of cellular structures under electric fields can enhance the release of bound phenolics, partially compensating for degradation under moderate conditions. Therefore, the net change in TPC reflects a dynamic balance between degradation reactions and matrix-mediated release mechanisms, strongly influenced by processing intensity and environmental conditions10,11.

Antioxidant capacity (DPPH assay)

The DPPH radical scavenging activity of white mulberry syrup ranged from 62.13% to 86.12% across different combinations of voltage gradient (10–30 V/cm) and pressure (50–100 kPa) during ohmic heating-vacuum concentration (Fig. 5), indicating substantial antioxidant capacity retention under various processing conditions.

Fig. 5effect of voltage gradient and pressure on antioxidant capacity (DPPH assay) of white mulberry syrups.

effect of voltage gradient and pressure on antioxidant capacity (DPPH assay) of white mulberry syrups.

At low voltage gradient (10 V/cm), reducing pressure from atmospheric (100 kPa) to vacuum (50 kPa) increased antioxidant activity from 62.13% to 80.65%. Vacuum conditions lower the boiling point, enabling concentration at reduced temperatures26, which minimizes thermal degradation of heat-sensitive antioxidant compounds such as flavonoids, anthocyanins, and phenolic acids24, and slows oxidative reactions that proceed more rapidly at elevated temperatures27.

Conversely, at moderate (20 V/cm) and high voltage gradients (30 V/cm), atmospheric pressure yielded superior antioxidant retention (85.01% and 82.63%, respectively) compared to vacuum conditions (68.16% and 68.09%). This reversal suggests that intense ohmic heating under vacuum creates unfavorable conditions, where the combination of rapid heating and low pressure accelerates volatile antioxidant loss through enhanced evaporation28 and potentially exposes antioxidants to oxidative damage despite reduced oxygen availability29.

Under atmospheric pressure, increasing voltage gradient from 10 to 30 V/cm substantially improved antioxidant activity from 62.13% to 82.63%. Higher voltage gradients shorten processing time, reducing cumulative thermal exposure31 and preserving thermolabile antioxidants more effectively than prolonged gentle heating32. Ohmic heating also generates uniform internal heat, minimizing localized overheating30, and may enhance cell membrane permeabilization, facilitating antioxidant release into the juice phase33. Under vacuum (50 kPa), however, voltage gradient effects proved negligible, with activities remaining relatively stable across all voltage levels, suggesting that pressure reduction dominates processing conditions and masks the benefits of faster heating rates.

The optimal condition was achieved at intermediate pressure (75 kPa) with 20 V/cm (86.12%) and 30 V/cm (85.04%), demonstrating synergistic effects between moderate vacuum and efficient ohmic heating. Similar trends have been reported for black grape juice40, kiwi juice10, and tomato juice35. Comparable findings in the literature confirm that ohmic-vacuum concentration consistently outperforms conventional methods in preserving antioxidant capacity across various fruit juices10,23,24,34.

Although antioxidant activity, TPC, and TFC are closely related, their responses to processing are not identical due to differences in reaction mechanisms and matrix effects. DPPH scavenging reflects the overall electron- or hydrogen-donating capacity of the system rather than the concentration of intact phenolic compounds. During ohmic heating, phenolics may undergo oxidation and structural transformation, forming intermediate species such as quinones that can still contribute to radical scavenging52. In some cases, these products may exhibit comparable or enhanced antioxidant reactivity, explaining the partial decoupling between phenolic degradation and antioxidant activity51,52.

In addition, matrix interactions significantly influence antioxidant behavior. Phenolic compounds may bind to macromolecules such as polysaccharides and cell wall components, affecting their accessibility, while electric field-induced cell permeabilization can enhance the release of bound antioxidants33. Furthermore, non-phenolic compounds, including Maillard reaction products and reducing sugars formed during concentration, can also contribute to DPPH scavenging independently of phenolic content51. Therefore, antioxidant activity reflects a balance between chemical transformation and matrix-mediated effects rather than direct phenolic concentration alone.

Total flavonoid content (TFC)

According to Fig. 6, the TFC of white mulberry syrup showed considerable variability under different combinations of voltage gradient (10–30 V/cm) and pressure (50–100 kPa) during ohmic heating-assisted vacuum concentration, ranging from 60.22 to 171.20 mg CE/100 mL. These results confirm that processing parameters strongly govern flavonoid preservation.

Fig. 6Effect of voltage gradient and pressure on total flavonoid content (TFC) of white mulberry syrups.

Effect of voltage gradient and pressure on total flavonoid content (TFC) of white mulberry syrups.

At the lowest voltage gradient (10 V/cm), vacuum conditions (50 kPa) yielded the highest TFC (171.20 mg CE/100 mL), outperforming atmospheric pressure (100 kPa, 159.70 mg CE/100 mL). Reduced pressure lowers the boiling point of water, enabling concentration at lower temperatures and thereby minimizing thermal degradation of flavonoids, which are highly susceptible to oxidative and structural deterioration at elevated temperatures36,37. At atmospheric pressure, the higher processing temperature required for evaporation prolonged thermal exposure, promoting greater degradation via oxidative pathways and thermal decomposition38.

At 20 V/cm, TFC dropped dramatically across all pressure levels, reaching its lowest values of 62.72 mg CE/100 mL (50 kPa) and 60.22 mg CE/100 mL (100 kPa). This severe decline reflects the synergistic damaging effects of moderate electrical intensity and thermal stress39. At this intermediate voltage, localized heating is sufficient to accelerate flavonoid oxidation and polymerization, while electrochemical reactions potentiate flavonoid breakdown40. The moderate processing rate further prolongs exposure to both thermal and electrical stresses, maximizing degradation41. Notably, vacuum conditions provided no meaningful protective advantage at this voltage, indicating that electrochemical stress became the dominant degradation factor, overriding the benefits of reduced pressure.

At the highest voltage gradient (30 V/cm), an unexpected reversal occurred: atmospheric pressure (100 kPa) yielded a higher TFC (142.21 mg CE/100 mL) than vacuum conditions (138.21 mg CE/100 mL). This is attributed to the extremely rapid and uniform volumetric heating at high voltage, which drastically reduces total processing time42 and thereby limits the duration of thermal exposure43,44. Under vacuum at this high voltage, the combination of reduced pressure and intense electrical field may promote turbulent evaporation, increasing oxygen contact and accelerating oxidative degradation15. At intermediate pressure (75 kPa) and high voltage, TFC fell sharply to 63.72 mg CE/100 mL, suggesting that this combination creates an unstable vapor-liquid equilibrium that further promotes oxidation.

Overall, the results highlight three key interaction patterns. First, low voltage (10 V/cm) combined with vacuum (50 kPa) represents the optimal condition for flavonoid preservation, achieving a maximum TFC of 171.20 mg CE/100 mL through minimized thermal and electrical stress. Second, intermediate voltage (20 V/cm) is consistently detrimental regardless of pressure, collapsing TFC below 63 mg CE/100 mL due to synergistic electrochemical and thermal degradation. Third, high voltage (30 V/cm) can achieve reasonable flavonoid retention (142.21 mg CE/100 mL) even at atmospheric pressure, demonstrating that minimizing processing duration can be as critical as controlling temperature33.

Although flavonoids are a major subclass of phenolics, TFC trends did not parallel those of total phenolic content (TPC), reflecting their distinct structural sensitivity and degradation pathways. Flavonoids possess a C6–C3–C6 backbone with multiple hydroxyl groups and heterocyclic rings54, making them highly susceptible to oxidative cleavage, ring-opening, and electrochemical degradation compared to simpler phenolic acids. These reactions are intensified under moderate electric fields, where electrochemical effects accelerate flavonoid transformation and polymerization55,56.

Matrix interactions further influence TFC, as flavonoids may bind to polysaccharides and cell wall components, reducing their extractability, while electric field-induced cell disruption can enhance their release33. Thus, TFC changes reflect a balance between structure-dependent degradation and matrix-mediated effects rather than simple thermal loss.

Specific energy consumption

The specific energy consumption (SEC) during ohmic-vacuum concentration of white mulberry juice was significantly influenced by the applied voltage gradient and system pressure (Fig. 7). The experimental data demonstrated a substantial 80% reduction in SEC, from 10.0 (MJ/kg water evp.) at the lowest intensity (50 kPa, 10 V/cm) to 2.3 (MJ/kg water evp.) at the highest (100 kPa, 30 V/cm), underscoring the critical impact of parameter optimization on energy efficiency.

The beneficial effect of increasing the voltage gradient on SEC was consistent across all pressure levels. At 50 kPa, increasing the gradient from 10 to 30 V/cm reduced SEC by 22% (10.0 to 7.8 MJ/kg water evp.). This effect was more pronounced at 75 kPa, with a 59% reduction (7.9 to 3.2 MJ/kg water), and remained substantial at 100 kPa, showing a 43% decrease (3.5 to 2.3 MJ/kg water evp.). This trend is strongly supported by the literature. Karakavuk et al. (2022) similarly observed a significant decrease in total energy consumption during the atmospheric ohmic evaporation of apple juice as the voltage gradient increased from 13 to 17 V/cm, attributing it to reduced processing times. The underlying mechanism is the principle of volumetric Joule heating, where the heat generation rate is proportional to the square of the electric field strength45. Higher voltage gradients accelerate ion mobility, thereby increasing the heating rate and shortening the process duration, which minimizes cumulative thermal losses46.

A pivotal finding of this study was the superior energy efficiency of atmospheric pressure (100 kPa) operation compared to vacuum conditions, which contradicts conventional thermal processing wisdom. At 30 V/cm, SEC decreased progressively from 7.8 (MJ/kg water evp.) at 50 kPa to 3.2 (MJ/kg water evp.) at 75 kPa, and further to a minimum of 2.3 (MJ/kg water evp.) at 100 kPa. This apparent paradox is resolved when considering the total system energy balance, including parasitic loads. Recent studies corroborate this finding. Mohammadi et al. (2025) reported that in vacuum ohmic systems, the energy consumed by the vacuum pump constituted 64.4–89.8% of the total energy input, making the process significantly more energy-intensive than atmospheric operation. Similarly, Darvishi et al.10, concentrating kiwifruit juice, found that specific energy consumption in ohmic-vacuum mode was 0.68–7.34 (MJ/kg water evp.) higher than in ohmic-atmospheric mode, with the vacuum pump alone consuming 59–81% of the total energy. Thus, the theoretical energy benefit of a lower boiling point under vacuum is negated by the substantial auxiliary energy required to maintain the vacuum15. The processing time data, showing an 81% reduction from 39.55 min (50 kPa, 10 V/cm) to 7.70 min (100 kPa, 30 V/cm), strongly correlates with the SEC trends. Shorter processing times not only reduce energy input but also limit the thermal exposure of heat-sensitive compounds, conferring dual advantages of efficiency and quality preservation47.

The best conditions identified in this study (100 kPa, 30 V/cm) achieved a minimum SEC of 2.30 (MJ/kg water evp.). This result demonstrates a remarkable efficiency compared to conventional thermal evaporation. Darvishi et al.12, for instance, reported that the conventional concentration of black mulberry juice consumed 17.50 (MJ/kg water evp.), which is 4.7-fold higher than the maximum SEC they recorded for ohmic heating and 8.0-fold higher than the optimal SEC achieved in the present work. This difference highlights the profound energy-saving potential of optimized ohmic heating technology for fruit juice concentration.

Optimization and validation

The mathematical models presented in Table 2, which exhibited high coefficients of determination (R² = 0.9357–0.9879), were employed as objective functions to predict system responses across the operational domain. The NSGA-II algorithm was configured with a population size of 100 chromosomes, operating over 150 generations with a crossover rate of 0.8 and a mutation rate of 0.2. Each chromosome encoded the two process parameters within their respective bounds, and fitness evaluation was performed by normalizing all five objective functions using min-max normalization to ensure equal weighting. The algorithm employed fast non-dominated sorting to classify solutions into Pareto fronts, with crowding distance calculations maintaining solution diversity throughout the evolutionary process. Binary tournament selection was utilized to choose parent chromosomes based on non-domination rank and crowding distance, while blend crossover and uniform mutation operators generated offspring populations.

The optimization convergence behavior demonstrated rapid improvement in the initial 80–100 generations, after which the best fitness value stabilized, indicating successful identification of the optimal region. The final Pareto front revealed inherent trade-offs between bioactive compound retention and process efficiency. Analysis of the non-dominated solutions showed that conditions favoring maximum bioactive preservation (low voltage gradient and reduced pressure) inevitably resulted in extended processing times and elevated energy consumption, whereas conditions optimizing process efficiency (high voltage gradient and atmospheric pressure) led to moderate reductions in certain bioactive compounds, particularly TFC.

The algorithm identified the optimal operating conditions that provided the best compromise among all five objectives. The optimal parameters were determined to be P = 83.47 kPa and ∇V = 26.82 V/cm, yielding predicted responses of TPC = 30.85 mg GAE/100 mL, DPPH = 84.73%, TFC = 78.14 mg CE/100 mL, processing time = 12.24 min, and SEC = 2.87 (MJ/kg water evp.). This solution achieved a composite fitness value of 0.782, representing an excellent balance between product quality and operational efficiency.

To validate the optimization results, triplicate experimental runs were conducted at the predicted optimal conditions (P = 83.47 kPa, ∇V = 26.82 V/cm). The experimental values obtained were TPC = 30.01 ± 0.87 (mg GAE/100 mL), DPPH = 85.38 ± 1.24%, TFC = 75.68 ± 3.45 (mg CE/100 mL), processing time = 11.85 ± 0.52 min, and SEC = 2.94 ± 0.15 (MJ/kg water evp.). Comparative analysis of the optimized conditions with individual optimal points for each response revealed the multi-objective nature of the problem. While 50 kPa and 10 V/cm provided maximum TPC (33.07 mg GAE/100 mL) and TFC (171.20 mg CE/100 mL) retention, these conditions resulted in the longest processing time (39.55 min) and highest specific energy consumption (9.96 MJ/kg water evp.), making them economically impractical for industrial-scale production. Conversely, 100 kPa and 30 V/cm achieved minimum processing time (7.70 min) and Sect. (2.30 MJ/kg water evp.) but with substantially reduced TFC (142.21 mg CE/100 mL) compared to the gentlest conditions. The optimized solution at 83.47 kPa and 26.82 V/cm successfully balanced these competing objectives, retaining 91.3% of the maximum achievable TPC, 98.4% of peak antioxidant activity, while reducing processing time by 70.0% and specific energy consumption by 70.4% compared to the most quality-preserving conditions. The optimization results provide clear operational guidelines for industrial implementation based on production priorities. For premium, health-focused markets, operating at 50 kPa and 10 V/cm maximizes bioactive compound retention despite elevated energy costs. Since vacuum pump energy dominates total consumption (64–90%) at these conditions, future industrial-scale studies should evaluate engineering interventions — including multi-stage concentration, variable-frequency drive pumps, mechanical vapor recompression, or heat integration strategies — to improve economic viability without compromising nutritional quality. For standard commercial production requiring a balance between quality and efficiency, the optimized conditions of approximately 75–85 kPa with 25–27 V/cm represent the ideal compromise, yielding antioxidant capacity of 84.73%, phenolic retention of 30.85 mg GAE/100 mL, processing time of 12.24 min, and specific energy consumption of 2.87 MJ/kg water evp. — all of which are natural outcomes of the NSGA-II multi-objective optimization, not predefined threshold constraints. For cost-driven industrial operations prioritizing throughput and minimal specific energy consumption, atmospheric pressure (100 kPa) with high voltage gradients (28–30 V/cm) offers the most economically viable solution while maintaining acceptable bioactive compound levels. These scientifically validated recommendations enable manufacturers to optimize their ohmic-vacuum concentration processes according to specific market requirements and economic constraints.

Conclusion

This study systematically evaluated conventional ohmic and hybrid ohmic-vacuum heating technologies for white mulberry syrup concentration, revealing critical trade-offs between bioactive compound retention and process efficiency. Increasing voltage gradient from 10 to 30 V/cm dramatically reduced processing time by 75.3% at atmospheric pressure, though vacuum conditions extended processing times by 81–295% due to reduced electrical conductivity at lower temperatures. Total phenolic content degradation ranged from 28 to 48%, with optimal retention (33.1 mg GAE/100 mL) at 50 kPa and 10 V/cm. Antioxidant capacity exhibited excellent preservation (62.13–86.12%), peaking at 86.12% under intermediate conditions (75 kPa, 20 V/cm). Total flavonoid content showed remarkable variability (60.22–171.20 mg CE/100 mL), with 20 V/cm proving universally detrimental regardless of pressure. Counterintuitively, conventional ohmic heating demonstrated superior energy efficiency, consuming 2.5–3.7-fold less energy than hybrid ohmic-vacuum method due to substantial vacuum pump energy requirements. Multi-objective optimization using NSGA-II identified optimal conditions at 83.47 kPa and 26.82 V/cm, achieving an excellent compromise between bioactive retention and operational efficiency. These findings provide manufacturers with clear operational guidelines to select processing parameters based on product quality priorities versus cost-efficiency requirements, advancing sustainable food processing technologies. For future work, it is recommended that additional measurements be included to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of process efficiency and product quality. These include: processing yield, color attributes, sugar profile, hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF) content, sensory evaluation, economic analysis, and storage stability studies under different packaging and temperature conditions. Furthermore, identification and quantification of characteristic metabolites (e.g., chlorogenic acid, rutin, quercetin) using advanced analytical techniques such as LC-MS/MS is strongly recommended.
