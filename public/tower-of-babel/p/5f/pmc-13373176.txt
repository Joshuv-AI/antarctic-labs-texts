Microstructural changes in irradiated teeth revealed by swept-source optical coherence tomography
Authors: Kumiko Matsuzaki, Hidenobu Matsuzaki, Tomoko Tabata, Hideki Aoyama, Kotaro Yoshio, Hirotaka Kosaki, Naoko Ohara, Alireza Sadr, Yoshihiko Soga, Yasushi Shimada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRadiation-induced caries is a late complication of radiotherapy for head and neck cancers. Although direct irradiation-induced changes in tooth structure may contribute to its development, these changes have not been fully characterized. This study evaluated irradiation-associated microstructural alterations and tissue density changes in human teeth using swept-source optical coherence tomography (SS-OCT). Eight extracted fully impacted human third molars were assigned to irradiated and non-irradiated groups (n = 4 each). The irradiated group received X-rays at a total dose of 70 Gy, a clinically relevant dose in definitive radiotherapy for head and neck cancer. A total of 64 SS-OCT cross-sectional images were obtained and analyzed, with 32 images from each group, including 16 from the coronal area on the occlusal side and 16 from the cervical area. Microcrack-like features were observed in 100% (16/16) of irradiated and 31.3% (5/16) of non-irradiated images in the coronal area on the occlusal side, and in 43.8% (7/16) and 0% (0/16) of images in the cervical area, respectively. Attenuation coefficients were significantly higher in irradiated enamel and dentin. These findings suggest that irradiation-induced microstructural changes in extracted human teeth in vitro may contribute to increased susceptibility to radiation-induced caries.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56971-y.Keywords: Radiation-induced caries, Dental enamel, Dentin, Radiation effects, Swept-source optical coherence tomographySubject terms: Cancer, Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Oncology

Introduction

Radiation-induced caries, characterized by rapid progression and destructive demineralization of the teeth, is a well-known late complication of radiotherapy for head and neck cancers1. To develop measures for its prevention, it is necessary to consider the specific factors underlying radiation-induced caries development. It has been reported that physical, chemical, and morphological changes in teeth are induced directly by irradiation2–9, and these changes have been suggested to contribute to the development of radiation-induced caries.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) is commonly used to examine the morphological microstructure of enamel and dentin. SEM analyses revealed that irradiation causes changes in the microstructure of human tooth enamel and dentin9. Several reports have shown the presence of prisms and crystals of the enamel in cross-sections of irradiated teeth on SEM. In addition, micromorphological alterations in the interprismatic region have been observed in irradiated enamel using SEM4,9–11. However, SEM requires invasive specimen preparation, including fixation with glutaraldehyde, dehydration through a graded ethanol series, critical point drying, and coating with gold.

Optical coherence tomography (OCT) has been developed for noninvasive cross-sectional imaging in biological systems10. Swept-source OCT (SS-OCT) uses a wavelength-tuned near-infrared laser as the light source, providing improved imaging resolution (approximately 10 μm) and scanning speed. With advances in technology, SS-OCT is now used in clinical practice, particularly for obtaining cross-sectional images of tissues from the vitreous body to the retina, choroid, and scleral border in ophthalmology. This technology also has been applied in dentistry. Laboratory studies have shown that SS-OCT can differentiate between carious and sound tissues, estimate lesion depth in demineralized tissue, observe gaps and defects in dental restorations, and detect tooth cracks11,12. In Japan, SS-OCT was approved for clinical dental use in 2020. Noninvasive cross-sectional imaging obtained by SS-OCT may provide insights into radiation-induced microstructural changes in teeth that cannot be observed by SEM.

In addition, the signals measured by SS-OCT can be used to calculate the attenuation coefficients, reflecting the tissue density. A previous study demonstrated a strong correlation between attenuation coefficients determined from SS-OCT signals and mineral changes of demineralized lesions in bovine enamel13. Previous studies have shown that the signal intensities and attenuation coefficients of SS-OCT are useful for detecting dental caries, noncarious cervical lesions, and defects in the enamel and superficial dentin layers11,14–16. SS-OCT can provide information on quantitative changes in the enamel, dentin-enamel junction (DEJ), and dentin induced by irradiation.

This study was performed to evaluate radiation-induced microstructural changes and quantitative changes in tissue density of enamel, DEJ, and dentin in irradiated human extracted teeth using SS-OCT in vitro.

Results

SS-OCT images

SS-OCT images of the enamel revealed characteristic bright linear features consistent with previously reported SS-OCT appearances of enamel cracks17,18, at the following frequencies: 100% (16/16) of images from the Ir group and 31.3% (5/16) from the NIr group in the coronal area on the occlusal side, and 43.8% (7/16) and 0% (0/16), respectively, in the cervical area. These differences between the Ir and NIr groups were significant in both regions (coronal area on the occlusal side: P < 0.001; cervical area: P = 0.007; Fisher’s exact test) (Fig. 1). The bright linear features ranged from 84.66 μm to 802.25 μm in length.

Frequencies of characteristic bright linear features in enamel. (A) Coronal area on the occlusal side. (B) Cervical area. The frequencies were significantly higher in the Ir group than in the NIr group in both areas (coronal area on the occlusal side: P < 0.001; cervical area: P = 0.007; Fisher’s exact test).

To further evaluate whether specimen preparation itself generated such findings, additional SS-OCT analysis was performed using preparation-only specimens subjected to the same protocol without irradiation. In these specimens, bright linear features in enamel were observed in 3/16 images in the coronal area on the occlusal side and 1/16 images in the cervical area. Representative SS-OCT images of the preparation-only specimens are shown in Supplementary Figure S1. These frequencies were substantially lower than those observed in the irradiated group, suggesting that specimen preparation alone is unlikely to account for the markedly higher frequency of bright linear features observed after irradiation. However, preparation-related microdefects cannot be completely excluded.

No bright linear features were observed in the dentin of either the NIr or Ir group. SS-OCT images of the cervical area in the Ir group also showed bright linear features in the enamel, similar to those seen in the coronal area on the occlusal side (Fig. 2B-e). The bright linear features in Fig. 2B-f, g and h were thicker than those in Fig. 2B-e.

Discussion

In the present study, SS-OCT revealed bright linear features in irradiated enamel, and their frequency was significantly greater in the Ir group than in the NIr group. In addition, the attenuation coefficients of both enamel and dentin were significantly higher in the Ir group, suggesting reduced tissue density after irradiation.

Previous SS-OCT studies have shown that enamel cracks can appear as high-intensity linear features due to increased backscattering at structural discontinuities. The bright linear features observed in the present study were consistent with these prior descriptions. In addition, complementary CLSM observations revealed localized linear structural discontinuities within enamel in sections prepared from the same specimens in which bright linear features were detected by SS-OCT, supporting the interpretation that these findings reflect underlying microstructural alterations consistent with microcrack-like features. Furthermore, in the additional preparation-only specimens subjected to the same protocol without irradiation, such bright linear features were absent or infrequent, suggesting that specimen preparation alone is unlikely to account for the markedly higher frequency observed in the irradiated group. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as microcrack-like features rather than definitively confirmed microcracks.

Radiation-induced tooth damage is thought to involve reactions between free radicals and the organic components of dental hard tissue. It has been reported that radiation does not show direct effects on the inorganic structure of human teeth, and the structural changes in enamel are due to alterations in the organic matrix of enamel19,20. Gonçalves et al. reported that SEM analysis (5,000× and 20,000×) revealed significant morphological alterations in the interprismatic region, which corresponds to enamel organic matrix, and suggested that alterations in the interprismatic region, which concentrates water, may result from the accumulation of free radicals and reactive oxygen species that react with and damage organic components9. Free radicals may alter and damage the organic matrix of the interstitial material between the enamel prisms, leading to localized structural discontinuities that appear as microcrack-like features on SS-OCT images.

To minimize potential confounding effects of storage conditions, all specimens were stored in normal saline solution (NSS) throughout the experimental period to prevent dehydration. NSS has been reported to have minimal effects on dental hard tissue structure in previous in vitro studies21,22. Furthermore, the additional preparation-only SS-OCT evaluation showed that bright linear features were absent or infrequent despite a longer storage duration, suggesting that storage and preparation procedures alone are unlikely to explain the observed differences.

Microcrack-like features observed using SS-OCT were found in the enamel and not in the dentin, whereas the attenuation coefficients of both enamel and dentin were significantly higher in the Ir group than in the NIr group (both P < 0.05). These findings suggest that irradiation affects both tissues, but the nature of structural alterations may differ. In the present study, the dentin ROIs were placed within 500 μm from the exposed dentin surface, rather than beneath the enamel layer, thereby minimizing the influence of signal attenuation associated with transmission through enamel. Therefore, the absence of bright linear features in dentin is unlikely to be explained solely by limited penetration through enamel-covered tissue. Nevertheless, dentin alterations may be more diffuse in nature and may not present as discrete linear features detectable by SS-OCT.

Another important consideration is the irradiation protocol used in this study. Although the total dose of 70 Gy is clinically relevant to definitive radiotherapy for head and neck cancer, the fractionation schedule used here (10 Gy/day × 7 days) differs substantially from conventional clinical radiotherapy. The present protocol was selected to deliver a clinically relevant cumulative dose within a controlled experimental timeframe. In an in vitro setting, prolonged fractionation over several weeks is difficult to standardize and may introduce additional variability related to specimen storage and handling. In addition, because dental hard tissues, particularly enamel, are acellular and lack intrinsic repair mechanisms, the influence of fractionation itself is likely limited, and the observed alterations may predominantly reflect cumulative dose-dependent effects. Therefore, the present model should be interpreted as a short-course, high-dose-per-fraction in vitro irradiation model rather than a direct simulation of standard clinical radiotherapy.

This study has several limitations. First, baseline SS-OCT imaging of the same specimens after preparation and before irradiation was not performed. Although a longitudinal within-specimen design could have provided stronger evidence regarding the temporal development of these structural alterations, the present study was originally designed as a comparative analysis between irradiated and non-irradiated specimens. To partially address this issue, we performed an additional SS-OCT evaluation using preparation-only specimens, in which bright linear features were absent or infrequent, suggesting that specimen preparation alone is unlikely to account for the markedly higher frequency observed in irradiated specimens. Second, although complementary CLSM provided supportive structural information, it does not constitute definitive ultrastructural confirmation of physical fracture. Third, the present study used a limited number of extracted teeth (n = 4 per group), which may limit statistical power and increase sensitivity to inter-specimen variability. Although the sample size was limited, the use of fully impacted third molars extracted for orthodontic reasons may have reduced heterogeneity related to tooth wear, long-term oral environmental exposure, and age-related structural changes. In addition, detailed baseline information such as donor age, sex, and initial tooth condition was not systematically recorded, limiting the ability to fully assess comparability between groups.

The bright linear features detected in this study were microscopic and would not be expected to be visible by visual inspection or conventional dental radiography. Because SS-OCT is increasingly being applied in clinical settings, it may serve as a useful modality for detecting irradiation-associated structural changes in teeth. Such microcrack-like structural alterations, together with reduced tissue density, may contribute to increased susceptibility to radiation-induced caries.

In conclusion, SS-OCT analysis revealed that X-ray irradiation at a total dose of 70 Gy, fractionated into 10 Gy per day, was associated with an increased frequency of microcrack-like features in enamel and with decreased tissue density in enamel and dentin in human teeth in vitro. These changes may contribute to the development of radiation-induced caries.

Methods

Specimen preparation

A schematic of the specimen preparation and subsequent analysis by X-ray irradiation and SS-OCT is shown in Fig. 5. Eight fully impacted human third molars, extracted before orthodontic treatment and without occlusal exposure, were used. The specimens were stored in normal saline solution (NSS) (Otsuka Normal Saline; Otsuka Pharmaceutical Factory, Inc., Tokushima, Japan) at 4 °C for a median of 110.5 days (range, 98–123 days) before the experiment. No systematic difference in storage duration was present between groups. Approximately one-third of the occlusal side of the crown was removed to expose a flat horizontal cross-section surface, including enamel, DEJ, and dentin, using a low-speed diamond saw (IsoMet low-speed saw; Buehler, Lake Bluff, IL, USA) under running water. Approximately half of the apical side of the root was then cut using a diamond saw to remove pulp. The flat surfaces of the tooth crown were ground with #600 grit silicon carbide paper under running water to remove the smear layer. The periodontal ligament was removed with a Gracey curette (Hu-Friedy Mfg. Co., LLC., Chicago, IL, USA) to expose the tooth surface at the DEJ. Prepared specimens (n = 8) were divided into the nonirradiated (NIr) group (n = 4) and irradiated (Ir) group (n = 4). Before further analysis, it was confirmed that all specimens had no cracks under a stereomicroscope (Keyence digital microscope VH-5500; Keyence Corp., Osaka, Japan). To prevent damage to the teeth due to drying, all specimens were kept in NSS throughout the experiment.

A schematic of specimen preparation and subsequent analysis by X-ray irradiation and SS-OCT. (A) Preparation of tooth specimens for analysis. The occlusal side and cervical side of eight extracted fully impacted human third molars were cut and the smear layer, periodontal ligaments, and pulp were removed. Tooth specimens including enamel, DEJ, and dentin on the surface were prepared for both NIr and Ir groups. The specimens in the Ir group were irradiated at a total dose of 70 Gy, fractionated into 10 Gy per day. Specimens from the NIr and Ir groups were kept in NSS during this period to prevent damage due to drying. (B) Observation with SS-OCT. The observation areas were set in the coronal area on the occlusal side and in the cervical region, each including enamel, DEJ, and dentin. Four sites were randomly selected in each region. (C) Setting of the region of interest. Regions of interest (ROIs), 1,000 μm in width and 500 μm in optical depth from the specimen surface, were set in the enamel, DEJ, and dentin areas in each image. The attenuation coefficients of these ROIs were calculated.

X-ray irradiation

Specimens were subjected to X-ray irradiation at a total dose of 70 Gy, fractionated into 10 Gy per day, with 4 MV X-rays provided by a linear accelerator (ONCOR; Siemens, Munich, Germany). Specimens from the Ir group (n = 4) and the NIr group (n = 4) were placed separately in two NSS-filled 12-well acrylic cell culture plates (Gentaur Ltd., London, UK), and the Ir group was subjected to X-ray irradiation (Fig. 5A). Specimens from both groups were kept in NSS during this period, which was changed daily.

SS-OCT system

An SS-OCT system with frequency-domain OCT technology (IVS-2000; Santec Holdings Corp., Komaki, Japan) was used. This system uses the magnitude and coherence of light reflected from the subject to generate depth-resolved images. This SS-OCT system operated at a central wavelength of 1,310 nm with a sweep rate of 20 kHz. The scanning light was projected onto the observation sites across the area of interest using a handheld probe. Two-dimensional cross-sectional images (5 × 7.481 mm) were created by converting the raw data into grayscale digital images.

Four observation sites were selected in each of the two areas for SS-OCT scans on each specimen: the coronal area on the occlusal side, observed from the surface of the horizontal cross-section, and the cervical area, observed from the cervical surface. In total, there were eight observation sites per specimen, encompassing enamel, DEJ, and dentin. The coronal area on the occlusal side was examined as a surrogate for clinically vulnerable occlusal worn surfaces, while the cervical area was included because of its clinical relevance to radiation-induced caries. These regions were selected for exploratory comparison based on clinical experience, although this site-specific tendency has not been systematically established in the literature. The sites in each area were randomly selected from predefined regions, and 64 two-dimensional cross-sectional images were created from SS-OCT signals, consisting of 32 from the Ir group and 32 from the NIr group (16 images from the coronal area on the occlusal side and 16 images from the cervical area in each group) (Fig. 5B). In addition, a signal intensity profile in dB units for each SS-OCT image was obtained to calculate the values of the signal attenuation coefficient.

SS-OCT image analysis

Three regions of interest (ROIs) with a width of 1,000 μm and an optical depth of 500 μm, located on enamel, DEJ, and dentin, were set in each created SS-OCT image. The signal intensity profiles of the SS-OCT images were obtained for each ROI. Microcrack-like features were operationally defined as bright linear or line-like features observed on SS-OCT images that were distinguishable from background speckle noise based on their continuity, relative signal intensity, and linear configuration.

All SS-OCT images were independently evaluated by five observers who were blinded to group allocation. Microcrack-like features were identified based on the predefined criteria described above. A majority rule was applied, whereby a finding was classified as present when at least three of the five observers judged it to be present.

Additional SS-OCT evaluation of preparation-related artifacts

To further assess whether the specimen preparation procedure itself generated crack-like SS-OCT findings, an additional set of extracted fully impacted human third molars was subjected to the same preparation protocol used in the main study, without irradiation. These specimens were stored in normal saline solution at 4 °C for a median of 208 days (range, 208–215 days) before preparation. After sectioning, grinding, smear-layer removal, and removal of the periodontal ligament, SS-OCT imaging was performed under the same conditions as in the main study. Bright linear features in enamel were evaluated in the coronal area on the occlusal side and in the cervical area in the same manner as in the main experiment. The frequency of microcrack-like features observed in this additional preparation-only group was compared descriptively with those in the irradiated and non-irradiated groups.

Complementary CLSM evaluation

After SS-OCT imaging, representative specimens were embedded in epoxy resin (EpoFix; Struers, Ballerup, Denmark). After polymerization, two sections were prepared from each tooth by sectioning perpendicular to the occlusal surface using a precision low-speed saw (IsoMet; Buehler, Lake Bluff, IL, USA) to obtain sections approximately 1.0 mm thick. Both sides of each section were examined, resulting in four observation surfaces per tooth. The cut surfaces were polished under water irrigation using silicon carbide paper until the final thickness was approximately 50 μm.

Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) was performed using an LSM 780 microscope (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) with a Plan-Apochromat 5×/0.16 objective lens at the Central Research Laboratory, Okayama University Medical School. CLSM images showing structural discontinuities within enamel were compared with SS-OCT images obtained from the same specimen. Structural discontinuities within enamel were qualitatively assessed as supportive findings for the interpretation of bright linear features observed in SS-OCT images.

Statistical analysis

The frequencies of enamel microcrack-like features were compared using Fisher’s exact test. Attenuation coefficients were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Statistical analyses were performed using JMP 12 software (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). In all analyses, P < 0.05 was taken to indicate statistical significance.
