First reported case of Moraxella lacunata bacteremia following pharyngitis in an adult with paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinemia receiving ravulizumab: A case report
Authors: Hanna Osawa, Koko Shibutani, Nobuyoshi Mori
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
We present a rare case of Moraxella lacunata bacteremia in a patient receiving ravulizumab, a complement inhibitor, for paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria (PNH). M. lacunata is typically regarded as a commensal organism of the upper respiratory tract, and invasive infections are exceedingly uncommon. Complement inhibition therapy, while effective in reducing hemolysis and thrombosis in PNH, predisposes patients to infections, including those by uncommon organisms. This report underscores the importance of clinical vigilance for atypical pathogens in patients receiving complement inhibitors.

Introduction

Paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria (PNH) is a rare, acquired clonal hematopoietic stem cell disorder characterized by complement-mediated intravascular hemolysis due to the absence of glycosylphosphatidylinositol-anchored proteins, which protect blood cells from complement attack. Terminal complement inhibitors, such as eculizumab and ravulizumab, have considerably transformed the PNH treatment landscape by effectively reducing intravascular hemolysis and thrombotic events [1]. However, inhibition of the terminal complement pathway increases susceptibility to certain bacterial infections, particularly with encapsulated organisms such as Neisseria meningitidis, Haemophilus influenzae, and Streptococcus pneumoniae. Vaccination mitigates but does not eliminate this risk, and some complement-sensitive pathogens lack effective vaccines [2]. Moraxella lacunata is typically a low-virulence, commensal gram-negative coccobacillus of the conjunctiva and upper respiratory tract and is rarely encountered as a cause of invasive disease [3], [4]. Here, we report the first case of M. lacunata bacteremia in an adult patient with PNH receiving ravulizumab, highlighting the clinical relevance of emerging opportunistic infections in this vulnerable population.

Case description

A 36-year-old man with PNH presented with a 2-hour history of fever (39 degrees Celsius), chills, and sore throat. He had been receiving ravulizumab for the past 5 years, following initial treatment with eculizumab. His vital signs were stable; physical examination revealed pharyngeal erythema with posterior pharyngeal follicles without exudates. He had received meningococcal group B (MenB) and quadrivalent conjugate (MCV4) vaccines 3 years prior, and pneumococcal vaccines (13-valent pneumococcal conjugate vaccine; PCV13, followed by 23-valent Pneumococcal Polysaccharide Vaccine; PPSV23) 4 years prior. He had not received the Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib) vaccine. He had no prior history of serious infections. There was also no history of aplastic anemia or exposure to immunosuppressants that could potentially cause aplastic anemia. Laboratory evaluation revealed a white blood cell count of 6200/μL with a normal differential, and CRP of 0.62 mg/dL. Two sets of blood cultures were obtained, and the patient received empirical intravenous ceftriaxone 2 g followed by oral amoxicillin-clavulanate plus amoxicillin. He defervesced within 48 h. However, on day 3, both sets of blood cultures grew pleomorphic gram-negative rods (Figure 1). He was admitted and treated with intravenous cefepime 1 g every 8 h. Contrast-enhanced CT of the chest and abdomen showed no focus of infection. Subsequently, blood culture growth of Moraxella lacunata was identified using MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry, with a score value of 1.84. Antibiotic susceptibility testing demonstrated sensitivity to beta-lactam antibiotics. Accordingly, therapy was de-escalated to oral amoxicillin-clavulanate and amoxicillin to complete a 14-day course. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed no evidence of endocarditis. The patient completed therapy uneventfully, with no recurrence of symptoms for the next 90 days.

Discussion

Ravulizumab is a long-acting monoclonal antibody that inhibits the cleavage of C5 into C5a and C5b, thereby blocking activation of the terminal complement pathway, including the formation of the membrane attack complex (MAC) [4], [5]. Studies have demonstrated its durable efficacy, safety, and favorable long-term survival outcomes in patients with PNH [5], [6]. Compared to eculizumab, ravulizumab offers an improved pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic profile, allowing for a reduced treatment burden through less frequent dosing [5], [6], [7].

However, similar to eculizumab, ravulizumab increases susceptibility to encapsulated and other complement-sensitive bacteria due to inhibition of membrane attack complex (MAC) formation and consequent loss of complement-mediated bactericidal activity. The risk of severe infections with encapsulated organisms, particularly Neisseria meningitidis, is well recognized in patients receiving C5 inhibitors, and appropriate vaccination is mandated prior to initiation. However, these vaccines do not provide coverage against all serogroups, and breakthrough infections have also been reported despite vaccination [2], [8]. In a 10-year pharmacovigilance analysis of eculizumab, reported infections were not limited to N. meningitidis but also included disseminated gonococcal infections and sepsis caused by other Neisseria species such as N. cinerea, N. subflava/sicca, and N. elongata [8]. These organisms are generally regarded as low-pathogenic or commensal in immunocompetent hosts; however, in complement-deficient patients, they are able to cause severe, invasive disease.

Our case of M. lacunata bacteremia illustrates this phenomenon. M. lacunata is a gram-negative coccobacillus that is generally regarded as a commensal organism of the conjunctiva and upper respiratory tract, although in rare cases it may cause invasive infections [3], [4]. Previous reports of invasive M. lacunata infections have included cases of sepsis, septic arthritis, and infective endocarditis. A 2014 literature review by Nakayama et al. [4] documented 12 cases of systemic infections caused by M. lacuna, noting that 8 patients had underlying cardiac conditions, 3 were receiving corticosteroid therapy, and 1 patient was HIV-positive. More recently, M. lacunata bacteremia has been reported in patients receiving complement inhibitors, including a pediatric patient with atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome treated with eculizumab (2021) [3] and an adult with generalized myasthenia gravis receiving eculizumab (2017) [9]. This suggests that M. lacunata, like Neisseria species, may exploit complement deficiencies to cause bloodstream infection in susceptible hosts.

Our case adds to this limited body of literature, highlighting M. lacunata as a potential opportunistic pathogen in adults receiving ravulizumab. To our knowledge, this is the first reported adult case of M. lacunata bacteremia in a patient receiving ravulizumab. Initial suspicion centered on a breakthrough infection caused by encapsulated bacteria, and empiric treatment was initiated under that assumption until blood cultures subsequently identified M. lacunata.

Importantly, the patient’s only identifiable infectious symptom was pharyngitis, and yet this preceded a bloodstream infection by M. lacunata. Whether the pharynx was the portal of entry remains speculative, but the timeline supports this possibility. This further reinforces the need for early blood culture collection in febrile patients on C5 inhibitors, even with mild presentation.

Given the increasing use of complement inhibitors in PNH, atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome, and generalized myasthenia gravis, surveillance for rare infections is essential. Moreover, while the role of vaccination remains paramount, this case underscores its limitations in preventing all potential pathogens in complement-deficient hosts.

Conclusion

M. lacunata should be considered a potential opportunistic pathogen in patients receiving terminal complement inhibitors, such as ravulizumab. Our case illustrates the need for heightened clinical vigilance for atypical infections in this population – even when typical signs of invasive infection are absent. Timely recognition and culture-based diagnosis are critical, and empirical treatment should account for a broad range of potential pathogens, including those not covered by standard vaccination protocols.
