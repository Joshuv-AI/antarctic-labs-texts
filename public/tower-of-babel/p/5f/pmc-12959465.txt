Preoperative Anxiety and Associated Factors Among Adult Patients Undergoing Elective Surgery at the Public Hospitals in Bahir Dar City, Ethiopia: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Wubet Tsega, Tesfaye Ayenew, Zemenu Shiferaw Yadita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsPreoperative anxiety affects many patients, impacting both their readiness for surgery and their postoperative recovery. A multi‐center study on preoperative anxiety is limited to the study area. Understanding patients' informational needs and other associated factors is crucial for developing effective strategies and enhancing patient outcomes. This research aims to assess the prevalence of preoperative anxiety and associated factors among adult patients undergoing elective surgery in public hospitals of Bahir Dar City, Northwest Ethiopia, 2023.MethodsThis multi‐center hospital‐based cross‐sectional study was conducted from September 1 to October 30, 2023. A systematic random sampling was used. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire, which included APAIS AND Oslo‐3 Scales, on the day before surgery, entered, and analyzed using EpiData version 4.6. and SPSS version 26. Descriptive analysis and binary logistic regression were performed, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.ResultsA total of 390 patients participated in the study, with a response rate of 95.8%. The prevalence of preoperative anxiety was 58.2% (95% CI: 53.1%–63.1%). Preoperative anxiety was significantly associated with fear of death (Adjusted odd ratio [AOR] = 5.21, 95% CI: 2.21–12.25), fear of anesthesia (AOR = 4.82, 95% CI: 2.12–10.93), concern about not recovering from anesthesia or illness (AOR = 3.00, 95% CI: 1.27–7.56), unaware of the planned surgery (AOR = 4.79, 95% CI: 2.01–11.43), Not knowing the type of anesthesia (AOR = 3.78, 95% CI: 1.71–8.37), having history of surgery (AOR = 2.48, 95%CI: 1.10‐5.60), and poor social support (AOR = 2.71, 95% CI: 1.13–6.51).Conclusions and RecommendationsIn this study, nearly one‐fifth of the patients (58.2%) experienced preoperative anxiety. The findings highlight the need for all surgical patients to visit a preoperative clinic where they can receive adequate information about their procedure, anesthesia, and overall condition. Strengthening social support systems and ensuring timely evaluation and management of preoperative anxiety are essential to improving patient outcomes.Keywords: Bahir Dar, elective surgery, Ethiopia, preoperative anxiety, public hospital

Introduction

Anxiety is a psychological condition marked by feelings of uncertainty, unease, and apprehension, often triggered by an unpredictable or unknown situation. While it is a normal response to stress or potential danger, excessive and prolonged anxiety can lead to severe postoperative complications, significantly increasing the risk of morbidity and mortality after surgery [1]. Anxiety affects every stage of the surgical process, including preoperative visits, anesthesia induction, perioperative care, and recovery. Symptoms are typically classified into three categories: physiological, psychological, and cognitive, all of which result from the activation of the autonomic nervous system and neuroendocrine changes in the body [2].

Surgery, by nature, is an invasive procedure that induces significant physiological, emotional, and psychological stress, leading to complications like bleeding, pain, nausea, and vomiting. These stressors can exacerbate preoperative anxiety, which in turn complicates the recovery process [1, 3]. Several tools, such as the Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale (DASS), State‐Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI), and Amsterdam Preoperative Anxiety and Information Scale (APAIS), are commonly used to assess anxiety in surgical patients [3, 4]. Among these, the APAIS is particularly effective for measuring anxiety related to anesthesia and surgery, offering a valid and economical method of assessment [5, 6, 7].

Preoperative anxiety has a profound impact not only on patients but also on healthcare professionals and institutions. Patients with preoperative anxiety, particularly those who are elderly or have cardiovascular or endocrine disorders, are at a higher risk for complications like increased morbidity, mortality, and postoperative issues. Physiological disturbances such as hypertension, tachycardia, and autonomic fluctuations can raise the risk of bleeding during or after surgery. Additionally, anxiety may contribute to gastrointestinal disturbances such as nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea, as well as endocrine issues that suppress the immune system, delaying wound healing [2, 8].

Anxiety further complicates anesthesia management, as it often leads to increased anesthetic and analgesic requirements, difficulty in establishing vascular access, delayed jaw relaxation, and challenges in preoxygenation at induction. Postoperative complications, such as prolonged recovery and extended hospital stays, are also more common in patients with preoperative anxiety [3, 9, 10]. The additional costs and resource burdens placed on healthcare institutions—due to extended stays and the treatment of anxiety‐related complications—further strain medical resources [4, 8]. Therefore, preoperative care should prioritize reducing anxiety through comprehensive patient evaluation, appropriate medications, and non‐pharmacological interventions [11, 12].

Globally, the prevalence of preoperative anxiety ranges from 60% to 80% among surgical patients [13]. In Ethiopia, studies show that the prevalence of preoperative anxiety varies between 47% and 70.3%, highlighting its common occurrence in surgical populations [9, 10]. Factors such as fear of postoperative pain, disability, complications, and medical errors significantly contribute to anxiety levels [3, 14]. Additionally, sociodemographic factors like age, sex, educational status, and income influence a patient's susceptibility to anxiety [15, 16]. Despite the growing body of research on preoperative anxiety in Ethiopia, most studies fail to examine the role of patients' informational needs about anesthesia and surgery, which can play a key role in reducing anxiety. Furthermore, many of these studies are conducted in single‐center settings, limiting the generalizability of their findings.

In Bahir Dar public hospitals, preoperative anxiety is a significant concern observed by clinicians and anesthetists (including the authors). It negatively impacts surgical outcomes, contributing to unplanned cancellations, complications, and delayed recovery. Despite its clinical relevance, no prior study has been conducted or published on this issue in the study area, highlighting the need for this study. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the prevalence of preoperative anxiety, assess patients' informational needs regarding anesthesia and surgery, and identify factors associated with anxiety among patients undergoing elective surgery in public hospitals in Bahir Dar City, Northwest Ethiopia, 2023.

Methodology

Study Design and Setting

A multi‐center hospital‐based cross‐sectional study was conducted in public hospitals in Bahir Dar City, Ethiopia, from September 1 to October 30, 2023. The study was carried out in three public hospitals: Tibebe Ghion Specialized Hospital (TGSH), Felege Hiwot Specialized Hospital (FHSH), and Addis Alem Hospital, located in Bahir Dar, Amhara Region. TGSH is a tertiary university teaching hospital in the southern part of the city, with a 450‐bed capacity, and performs approximately 400 elective surgeries monthly. FHSH, situated in the northern part of the city, has 400 beds and 9 operating tables, performing around 900 elective surgeries monthly. Addis Alem Hospital is a primary hospital, with approximately 100 adult elective surgeries performed each month. These hospitals serve patients referred from across the Amhara region, providing both outpatient and inpatient services in various specialties.

Population

The source population consisted of all patients scheduled for surgery at public hospitals in Bahir Dar City. The study population consisted of patients scheduled for surgery at these hospitals and presenting during the data collection period. Regarding the eligibility criteria, the study included patients aged 18 years and older who were able to comprehend and communicate effectively. Patients with diagnosed anxiety disorders or those currently using anti‐anxiety or antidepressant medications were excluded. Data collectors assessed psychiatric conditions and medication use through patient interviews, chart reviews, and physical examinations.

Sampling Procedure and Sample Size

Bahir Dar City has three public hospitals: TGSH, FHSH, and Addis Alem. Initially, a 2‐month adult surgical case flow was assessed, revealing that 1800 cases were operated at FHSH, 800 at TGSH, and 200 at Addis Alem during that period. Based on these numbers, the sample size was proportionally allocated to each hospital. To select study participants, a systematic random sampling technique was employed. The sampling interval (K th value) was calculated as N/n, where N is the total number of cases, and n is the desired sample size. The sampling interval (K th ) value was determined to be 7, meaning every seventh patient was selected for the study.

In determining the sample size, both the single population proportion formula and the double proportion formula were applied. However, the maximum sample size for this study was calculated using the single proportion formula with the following assumptions: a 95% confidence level, a 5% margin of error (d), and a preoperative anxiety proportion (P) of 59.6%, based on data from the University of Gondar Specialized Hospital [3]. The formula used was: n=Za22P(1−P)/d². After adjusting for a 10% non‐response rate, the final sample size was determined to be 407.

Data Collection Tools and Procedures

In our study, a structured questionnaire that included both interviewer‐administered and self‐reported components was used for data collection. The tool consists of four sections: sociodemographic and clinical factors, the APAIS (Amsterdam Preoperative Anxiety and Information Scale), the Oslo Social Support Scale, and potential causes of anxiety. The socio‐demographic and clinical information sections were conducted through interviewer‐administered methods to ensure completeness and clarity. The Amsterdam Preoperative Anxiety and Information Scale (APAIS) and the Oslo Social Support Scale (Oslo‐3) were administered with interviewers' assistance, depending on the participant's literacy level. The data collection was conducted on the day before surgery. The question about possible causes of preoperative anxiety was presented as a structured checklist with predefined response options (e.g., fear of surgery, fear of anesthesia, concern for family), where participants marked “yes” or “no” for each item. The tool was initially developed in English, then translated into Amharic, and subsequently back translated into English to ensure consistency and accuracy. The data collection process involved five anesthesiology residents and two anesthetists, who administered the interviewer‐guided questionnaires to the participants.

To ensure data quality, a 1‐day training session on the KoboCollect software was provided to the data collectors before the start of data collection. Additionally, a pretest was conducted with 20 individuals (5% of the sample population) who were not included in the study, to test the process before actual data collection in the study area. The principal investigator reviewed the collected data, assessed for any incomplete responses, performed data cleaning, and ensured the overall quality of the data before entering it into the system.

Measurements

Variables

The outcome variable (dependent variable) in this study is preoperative anxiety (Yes or No), while the independent variables include socio‐demographic characteristics, including age (in years), gender, educational status, occupation, residency, marital status, family condition, ethnicity, and presence of comorbid illness. Surgery‐related factors comprised fear of complications, fear of death, fear of pain, concern regarding surgical outcomes, previous surgical experience, and type of surgery. Anesthesia‐related factors included fear of anesthetic complications, fear of intraoperative awareness, and fear of not recovering from anesthesia. Information‐related factors involved whether the patient had received information about the disease, the type of surgical procedure, and the type of anesthesia.

Operational Definitions and Definitions of Terms

Surgery/Operation: A procedure that involves cutting or altering a patient's tissues [17].

Anxiety: A vague feeling of dread, stress, or apprehension [1].

Level of Anxiety: A patient is considered to experience anxiety if they score 11 or more on the anxiety scale of the Amsterdam Preoperative Anxiety Information Scale (APAIS) [5].

Major Surgery: Any surgical operation performed in a major operating room using regional anesthesia, sedation, or general anesthesia [18].

Preoperative: The time period between the decision to have surgery and the commencement of the surgical procedure [19].

Preoperative Anxiety: Anxiety experienced by a patient during the preoperative period [20].

Perioperative: The time encompassing the preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative periods [21, 22].

Amsterdam Preoperative Anxiety Information Scale (APAIS): is a validated self‐report tool consisting of 6 items, each rated on a 5‐point Likert scale (1 = not at all, 5 = extremely). It measures two dimensions:

Preoperative anxiety: four items (two related to anesthesia, two to surgery), total score range: 4–20. A score of ≥ 11 is commonly used to indicate clinically significant anxiety.

Preoperative anxiety: four items (two related to anesthesia, two to surgery), total score range: 4–20. A score of ≥ 11 is commonly used to indicate clinically significant anxiety.

Need for information: two items, total score range: 2–10. Patients scoring 2–4 are considered to have low information needs, 5–7 moderate needs, and 8–10 high needs.

Need for information: two items, total score range: 2–10. Patients scoring 2–4 are considered to have low information needs, 5–7 moderate needs, and 8–10 high needs.

The APAIS has been translated and validated in various languages, including Amharic (Cronbach's alpha = 0.87) [5, 7].

Oslo Social Support Scale (Oslo‐3): It consists of 3 items designed to assess the level of social support. The total score ranges from 3 to 14, with respondents categorized into three levels: a score of 3–8 indicates poor social support, 9–11 indicates moderate social support, and 12–14 indicates strong social support [23].

Data Processing and Analysis

After data collection, the data were reviewed for completeness, consistency, and accuracy. It was then sorted, categorized, summarized, and coded according to variable categories, with additional checks for errors. The data were subsequently exported to the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 26 for analysis. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and proportions, were calculated and presented using tables and text.

A binary logistic regression analysis was performed. Initially, bivariate logistic regression was conducted to examine the relationship between each independent variable and the outcome variable, with crude odds ratios (COR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) calculated. Variables with a p‐value less than 0.20, along with clinically significant variables, were included in the multivariable logistic regression model to assess their effect on the outcome and control for potential confounders. Variables with a p‐value less than 0.05 in the multivariable model were considered statistically significant, and adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% CI were computed to determine the associations. The Hosmer and Lemeshow test yielded a significance level of 0.351, while the omnibus test of model coefficients showed significance at 0.00, indicating a good model fit.

Ethical Approval and Consent to Participate

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Bahir Dar University following the approval of the research proposal (Protocol Number: 824/2023). A support letter was provided to the selected Hospitals. Written informed consent was obtained from all study participants after a thorough explanation of the potential benefits and risks. Participants were informed that they could withdraw from the study at any time without any impact on their treatment or other hospital benefits. To ensure confidentiality, all collected data was stored on password‐protected devices, including phones and computers, and was accessible only to the research team. All collected data were anonymized; personal identifiers such as names were not recorded. In cases where patients were identified as experiencing significant anxiety during the preoperative period, the data collectors provided counseling and reassurance. They also reported the findings to the relevant care providers (anesthesia providers, surgical residents, and nurses) to ensure appropriate treatment was administered (involving a psychiatrist).

Results

Socio‐Economic and Demographic Characteristics

A total of 390 patients participated in the study, resulting in a 95.8% response rate. Among them, 204 (52.3%) were female and 186 (47.7%) were male. A total of 163 participants (41.8%) were between 18 and 35 years of age, with a mean age of 41.7 years (a standard deviation of 14.8). Regarding religious affiliation, 289 participants (71.4%) identified as Orthodox Christians. Most participants were married (277; 71%). In terms of educational background, 90 (23.1%) had attended higher education, while 134 (34.4%) were illiterate. Additionally, 202 participants (51.8%) resided in rural areas (Table 1).

Prevalence and Reasons for Preoperative Anxiety

The prevalence of preoperative anxiety among adult patients attending public hospitals in Bahir Dar city was 58.2% (95% CI: 53.1%–63.1%). Of the 390 participants, 227 had an APAIS score of 11 or higher, indicating the presence of anxiety, while the remaining 163 (41.8%) did not experience preoperative anxiety. The mean APAIS score was 11.29 (SD = 3.93), with a median of 11 and a range from 4 to 20.

A structured checklist was used to assess possible causes of preoperative anxiety, with predefined response options such as fear of surgery, fear of anesthesia, and concern for family. Participants responded with “yes” or “no” to each item. The most reported causes of anxiety were fear of complications (47.7%), fear of anesthesia (40.5%), fear of not recovering (38.7%), and fear of death (35.9%). In terms of information needs, 22.3% of respondents reported a high demand for preoperative information. Based on the Oslo Social Support Scale, 196 participants (50.3%) had moderate social support (scores 9–11), while 106 (27.2%) had strong social support (scores 12–14) (Table 3).

Factors Associated With Preoperative Anxiety

In the binary logistic regression analysis, a bivariable analysis was initially performed, revealing about 19 variables significantly associated with preoperative anxiety at a p < 0.25. However, in the multivariable analysis, the factors that showed a statistically significant association with preoperative anxiety (p < 0.05) were only 11, namely; previous surgical experience, knowledge about the disease and planned surgery, knowledge of the anesthesia to be received, fear of death, fear of anesthesia, fear of complications, fear of not recovering, concerns about family, information requirements, and social support.

Patients who were unaware of the planned surgery they were undergoing were five times more likely to experience preoperative anxiety than those who were informed (AOR = 4.79, 95% CI: 2.01–11.43). Patients who did not know the type of anesthesia they would receive were four times more likely to experience preoperative anxiety than those who did know (AOR = 3.78, 95% CI: 1.71–8.37). First‐time surgery patients were twice as likely to experience preoperative anxiety compared to those with previous surgical experience (AOR = 2.48, 95% CI: 1.10–5.60). Patients who were concerned about death were five times more likely to experience preoperative anxiety than those who did not have such concerns (AOR = 5.21, 95% CI: 2.21–12.25).

Patients who feared anesthesia were five times more likely to develop preoperative anxiety than those without such fears (AOR = 4.82, 95% CI: 2.12–10.93). Patients who worried about not recovering from surgery or anesthesia were three times more likely to experience preoperative anxiety than those without such concerns (AOR = 3.00, 95% CI: 1.27–7.56). Concerns about complications and family issues were associated with three times and two times higher likelihoods of preoperative anxiety, respectively (AOR = 3.13, 95% CI: 1.48–6.62; AOR = 2.32, 95% CI: 1.02–5.32). Patients who desired moderate to high levels of information about their disease, surgery, and anesthesia management were three times and nine times more likely to experience preoperative anxiety compared to those who wanted little or no information (AOR = 3.46, 95% CI: 1.55–7.72; AOR = 9.18, 95% CI: 3.37–25.05). Patients with poor to average social support were six times and three times more likely to develop preoperative anxiety than those with strong social support (AOR = 5.61, 95% CI: 1.82–17.28; AOR = 2.71, 95% CI: 1.13–6.51) (Table 4).

Discussion

In this study, the prevalence of preoperative anxiety among adult patients undergoing surgery in public hospitals of Bahir Dar City was found to be 58.2% (95% CI: 53.1%–63.1%), as indicated by an APAIS score of 11 or greater. Factors significantly associated with higher levels of preoperative anxiety included lack of previous surgical experience, poor knowledge about the planned surgery and anesthesia, and fears related to death, anesthesia, surgical complications, and not recovering. Additionally, concerns about family, high need for medical information, and inadequate social support were predictors of anxiety.

The proportion observed in our study aligns with several comparable settings. It was comparable to that of many national and international studies, such as in Lithuania (57.7%), Saudi Arabia (55%), Pakistan (62%), Brazil (53.4%), and in Ethiopia from Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital (58.9%), the University of Gondar (59.6%), and Debre Markos and Felege Hiwot Referral Hospitals (61%) [3, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28]. This consistency suggests a comparable psychological burden among surgical patients across diverse healthcare settings, particularly in low‐ to middle‐income countries where preoperative education and psychological preparation may be limited.

However, the observed prevalence is lower than that reported in Sri Lanka (76.6%), Baghdad (68%), Tunisia (67%), Niger (90%), and southwest Ethiopia (70.3%) [3, 9, 27, 29, 30]. These higher rates may reflect differences in cultural perceptions of surgery, fear of medical procedures, lack of preoperative counseling services, healthcare quality, or the psychological preparedness of patients. Conversely, our findings indicate a higher prevalence of POA compared to studies conducted in Turkey (40%), Sweden (34%), Iran (47%), Nigeria (33%), south Ethiopia (47%), and Wollo, northeast Ethiopia (48.3%) [9, 10, 15, 31, 32, 33]. These differences could be attributed to well‐established preoperative education programs and better doctor‐patient communication, resulting in lower anxiety levels.

Patients who lacked adequate information about their surgery and anesthesia were five times more likely to experience preoperative anxiety. Those with moderate to high information needs were three to nine times more likely to feel anxious. These findings are consistent with studies from the U.S., Spain, the Netherlands, Canada, and other countries, highlighting the significant impact of unmet informational needs on preoperative anxiety [5, 7, 12, 26, 27, 29, 34, 35]. Similarly, a study from India found that well‐informed patients had significantly lower anxiety levels [36]. Evidence from Lebanon and elsewhere shows that simply providing information may not reduce anxiety if it doesn't match the patient's coping style or cultural background. Thus, patient education must be tailored to individual needs to effectively manage preoperative anxiety [5, 7, 12].

The study found that first‐time surgery patients were twice as likely to experience preoperative anxiety compared to those with prior surgical experience. This aligns with findings from Felege Hiwot, Debre Markos, and the University of Gondar, where previous surgery reduced anxiety risk by up to 61% [3, 14, 27, 29, 37]. Similarly, a study in the Czech Republic showed increased anxiety without prior experience. However, studies from Pakistan, Sri Lanka, Spain, and Jimma University found no significant link [9, 12, 25, 29]. Patients with poor and moderate social support were six and three times more likely, respectively, to experience preoperative anxiety than those with strong support. Strong social support helps reduce anxiety by improving emotional regulation, coping, and providing reassurance. This aligns with findings from Yirgalem, Ethiopia, where strong support reduced preoperative anxiety risk by 84% [10].

In this study, participants identified several causes of preoperative anxiety, with the most common being fear of death (83.6%), fear of not recovering (82.1%), fear of anesthesia (78.5%), fear of complications (78%), and family concerns (75.9%). Other frequently mentioned factors included fear of intraoperative awareness (77.2%), cosmetic issues (73.3%), postoperative pain (70.5%), and physical disability (70.2%). These concerns may stem from limited health literacy, poor communication between patients and healthcare providers, and cultural beliefs surrounding surgery and outcomes. Significant associations were found between anxiety and fear of complications, anesthesia, death, not recovering, and family concerns, aligning with studies from Ethiopia, Brazil, Pakistan, and others [3, 4, 10, 12, 27, 28, 37, 38]. Specifically, patients who feared complications were three times more likely to develop anxiety, while those who feared anesthesia or death were five times more likely. Fear of not recovering also increased the risk threefold, and family concerns significantly contributed to anxiety, consistent with findings from Gondar, Debre Markos, and Pakistan—though not observed in studies from Sri Lanka, Yirgalem, and Jimma [10, 12, 13, 27, 29, 37].

Conclusions and Recommendations

Nearly one‐fifth (58.2%) of the respondents experienced preoperative anxiety, which is consistent with findings from previous studies conducted globally. Fear of anesthesia, death, inability to recover from anesthesia or illness, complications, and family concerns were significantly associated with preoperative anxiety. Additionally, lack of information about the surgical and anesthesia management plan, poor social support, higher information requirements, and lack of previous surgical experience were also statistically significant factors linked to preoperative anxiety. This study recommends that government officials create guidelines for screening, prevention, and management of preoperative anxiety, alongside using media to raise public awareness about anesthesia and surgery. Hospitals should ensure all surgical patients visit a preoperative clinic to receive adequate information, with prompt attention to any anxiety. Training for healthcare providers on assessing and managing preoperative anxiety is essential. Future research should explore the causes and effects of preoperative anxiety, compare preoperative and postoperative anxiety levels, and investigate the timing of anxiety in relation to surgery.

Strengths and Limitations

A key strength of this study is its analysis of the underlying causes of preoperative anxiety, rather than focusing solely on its prevalence. It uniquely assesses both the adequacy and individual need for information, offering deeper insight into how information affects anxiety. The inclusion of social support also adds valuable context to the psychosocial factors influencing preoperative anxiety. Additionally, the exclusion of pediatric and emergency patients helped ensure a more focused and homogeneous study population, improving the reliability of findings.

Regarding the limitations, as a cross‐sectional study, this research cannot establish causal relationships between preoperative anxiety and the associated factors. Additionally, it did not assess or compare anxiety levels before and after surgery, limiting understanding of how anxiety may change postoperatively.
