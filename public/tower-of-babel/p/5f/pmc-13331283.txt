Patient, care partner, and oncologist perspectives on clinically important survival thresholds after considering time toxicity in advanced cancer
Authors: Arjun Gupta, Kendall Lin, Rick J Jansen, Samuel X Stevens, Manju George, Allison Breininger, Christopher M Booth, Helen M Parsons, Jeanette Y Ziegenfuss, Rachel I Vogel, Anne H Blaes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA treatment’s time toxicity (measured using contact days) can decrease days spent at home (home days). We evaluated the minimum clinically important difference (MCID) and non-inferiority margin (NIM) of home days, and treatment acceptance when all additional survival time was spent in health care by surveying people with lived (patients, n = 42; informal care partners, n = 29) and professional (oncologists, n = 8) experience of advanced cancer. All 3 groups reported similar MCIDs and NIMs (6-month survival; median MCID range, 14-15; P = .79; median NIM range, 5-11; P = .14). Care partners (48%) and patients (21%) more often accepted 2-week survival gains without an increase in home days than oncologists (0%), P = .09. Caregivers reported altruism guided their responses, and clinicians’ responses in stated-preference work may differ from revealed-preferences in the clinic. By empirically deriving MCID/NIM for home days and evaluating treatment acceptance, this work will aid trial design and decision studies using an endpoint of home days.

Background

The time toxicity of a treatment (measured using contact days) can decrease days at home (home days).1,2 The minimum clinically important difference (MCID; smallest change in an outcome considered meaningful) and non-inferiority margin (NIM; maximal difference considered no worse) of home days are unknown.3

Methods

This single-center cross-sectional in-person guided survey study enrolled adults with advanced stage solid cancer or lymphoma who had ever received systemic cancer-directed treatment, their informal care partner, and their primary oncologist. Patients could participate without care partners, and oncologists could care for multiple patients. Based on prior vignette-based and stated preference work,3,4 we first explained the concept of contact days/home days and MCID/NIM with teach-back confirmation. We then presented hypothetical vignettes (Table S1). Example vignettes stated survival of 6 and 12 months with standard treatment, of which 5 and 10 months, respectively, were home days, and asked the MCID/NIM of home days for a new treatment with the same survival. A separate vignette evaluated the acceptability of a new treatment if it increased survival by 2 weeks without an increase in home days. If not accepted, the threshold of acceptability was explored. The primary outcome was the distribution of MCID/NIM values, compared using Kruskal–Wallis, and treatment acceptance patterns, compared using Fisher’s exact test, by participant group. We did not perform formal power calculations in this pilot study. UMN IRB approved this study. We obtained written informed consent. We used REDCap and R (version 4.5.1) for data collection and analysis.

Results

Between November 2024 and August 2025, we enrolled 79 participants: 42 patients (median age, 62 years), 29 care partners (69% spouses), and 8 oncologists (38% women) Table S2.

All participant groups reported similar MCID estimates over both the 6- and 12-month survival periods (Figure 1A and B). The median MCID for a 6-month survival with 5-months of home days was 14 (patients), 15 (care partners), and 15 (oncologists) days; Figure 1A; P = .79. For a 12-month survival with 10-months of home days, the median MCID was 20 (patients), 23 (care partners), and 30 (clinicians) days; Figure 1B; P = .51. Likewise, participant groups reported comparable NIMs (median NIM; for 6-month survival: patients, 5; care partners, 7; clinicians, 11 days; Figure 1C; P = .14; for 12-month survival: patients, 10; care partners, 11; clinicians, 15 days; Figure 1D; P = .64).

Participant groups had distinct and diverse views on what guided their choices—oncologists more frequently prioritized milestone personal events such as attending a family wedding (oncologists, 25%; patients, 10%; care partners, 5%) and reported factoring in a patients’ preferences and context such as being a young parent (oncologists, 25%; care partners, 10%; patients, 5%), whereas patients and care partners reported they valued increased survival time by itself (care partners, 35%; patients, 25%; oncologists 13%). Discussion

Participant groups with lived and professional experience of cancer generally had similar MCID/NIM home day thresholds. Three specific points deserve discussion. First, the numerical estimates for MCID/NIM match prior work in the non-cancer context: 3-week MCID and 1-week NIM of hospital-free days for critically ill patients with similar thresholds across participant groups,3 and an MCID of 18.6 days for home days over 1 year for older adults.5 Second, despite similar median estimates across groups, we observed a wide range within groups. Individuals’ preferences may thus vary significantly. Third, an excessively high superiority margin in a trial may condemn it to a null finding. In one trial, investigators set the MCID for “good days” at 14 days over 90-days; the trial was unsurprisingly “negative” with an actual difference of 2.9 days.6,7 A more conservative MCID may not have changed the primary finding but made meeting the endpoint plausible.

Although not statistically different, oncologists had numerically higher thresholds for MCID/NIM and accepting treatments, aligning with prior survey-based work.8–10 However, use and uptake of treatments with marginal benefits in clinics is high. This tension between stated (what oncologists say in surveys) and revealed (what actually happens) preferences suggests that oncologists’ actual MCID in clinical environments is lower than in research settings. This may relate to a Hawthorne effect (oncologists were conscious of appearing rational), and they wore a regulatory hat, looking for population-level relevance versus a frontline oncologist hat.

Care partners were the most accepting of treatments, guided by altruism, not backing away from tasks, but also deferring the final decision to the patient. Almost 80% of patients themselves rejected treatments that increased survival by 2-weeks, but almost 20% were willing to accept treatments that increased survival by 1 week without an increase in home days. This again highlights the deeply personal nature of individual preferences. Oncologists most frequently stated that they would guide decisions considering patients’ preferences and milestone events. Oncologists may have found study scenarios more relatable (similar to clinical practice), but patients and care partners, who do not commonly consider such specific and emotional scenarios, may have found these challenging. All groups mentioned that actual decisions are complex, with multiple factors beyond simply contact days/home days.

Limitations include limited scope (single institution and geographic region; limited racial/ethnic diversity; only patients accepting systemic therapy and agreeing to participate; only assessing limited survival thresholds), MCID/NIM complexity (participants’ formal comprehension not assessed), assessing stated (vs revealed) preferences, and not considering other tradeoffs such as quality-of-life (home days do not necessarily represent high-quality time, and factors such as symptoms, pain, and functional decline are not captured by the home days measure alone).

Strengths include empirically deriving the first estimates of MCID/NIM for home days and evaluating treatment acceptance when considering survival offset by time toxicity across groups. Internal consistency (MCID > NIM) and logical open-ended explanations raise confidence. These findings will guide decision science studies.
