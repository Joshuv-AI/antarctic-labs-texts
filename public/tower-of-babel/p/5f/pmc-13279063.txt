Residential Greenness and Risk of Coronary Artery Disease Following COVID‐19: A Nationwide Cohort Study in South Korea
Authors: Dongjin Yeo, Juyeong Kim, Kyeongeun Kim, Sooji Lee, Hayeon Lee, Lee Smith, Damiano Pizzol, Ho Geol Woo, Selin Woo, Dong Keon Yon, Yerin Hwang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundResidential greenness has been associated with lower ischemic heart disease risk in general populations; however, its relevance after SARS‐CoV‐2 infection is unclear. We evaluated the associations between residential greenness and incident coronary artery disease following COVID‐19.MethodsIn this nationwide cohort study using the South Korean K‐COV‐N (Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency–COVID‐19–National Health Insurance Service) database, we included individuals aged ≥19 years with confirmed SARS‐CoV‐2 infection. The data set linked National Health Insurance Service health screening and claims data with Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency surveillance records. Residential greenness was measured using the normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI; low 0.2–<0.4, moderate 0.4–<0.6, high ≥0.6). The primary outcome was first diagnosis of coronary artery disease (acute coronary syndrome or chronic ischemic heart disease) ≥30 days after infection. Inverse probability of treatment weighting was used to balance covariates, and weighted Cox models were applied to estimate adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) with 95% CIs. Analyses were stratified by COVID‐19 severity, vaccination status, variant period, sex, age, and income.ResultsAmong 3 097 179 individuals (61.4% men), 25.3%, 59.7%, and 15.0% were classified as having low, moderate, and high NDVI exposure, respectively. Although NDVI was not associated with risk of acute coronary syndrome, higher NDVI was associated with lower risk of chronic ischemic heart disease (aHR, 0.86 [95% CI, 0.84–0.88] for moderate NDVI; aHR, 0.82 [95% CI, 0.80–0.84] for high NDVI). Associations were stronger in moderate to severe COVID‐19, vaccinated individuals, males, and younger individuals.ConclusionsResidential greenness was not associated with post‐COVID acute coronary events but was consistently associated with lower chronic ischemic heart disease risk, suggesting a potential role in long‐term cardiovascular health after SARS‐CoV‐2 infection.Keywords: acute coronary syndrome, chronic ischemic heart disease, COVID‐19, normalized difference vegetation index, residential greenness, vaccinationSubject Categories: Cardiovascular Disease, Epidemiology

What Are the Clinical Implications?

Residential greenness, often quantified using the normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI), has been consistently associated with lower coronary artery disease incidence and mortality in previous population‐based studies. 1 , 2 , 3 Residential greenness has been associated with acute improvements in physiological processes through reductions in oxidative stress and inflammation, which contribute to improvements in cardiometabolic profiles, including blood pressure, lipid levels, and glucose regulation. 1 , 3 In addition, as a component of the neighborhood built environment, residential greenness contributes to daily exposures involving environmental, social, psychological, and physiological factors that may influence long‐term cardiovascular health. 4 , 5

Meanwhile, COVID‐19 has been associated with increased risks of cardiovascular outcomes, including heart failure, stroke, arrhythmias, thromboembolic events, and coronary artery disease, many of which persist as postacute sequelae, 6 , 7 through mechanisms involving SARS‐CoV‐2–induced endothelial injury and hypercoagulability. 8 Although the biological pathways implicated in COVID‐19–related cardiovascular injury overlap with mechanisms through which greenness improves cardiometabolic and inflammatory processes, 9 previous studies have not examined whether greenness provides cardiovascular benefits in the context of COVID‐19. In addition, because mobility restrictions and increased time spent at home during the pandemic likely altered exposure to residential greenness, it is important to consider how this may have influenced the degree of benefits. 10

Therefore, we conducted a nationwide, population‐based cohort study to evaluate whether residential greenness, measured by NDVI, was associated with the risk of newly diagnosed acute coronary syndrome (ACS) and chronic ischemic heart disease (IHD) among individuals with confirmed SARS‐CoV‐2 infection using data from 2018 and 2023, and how the benefits of residential greenness differed among subgroups.

METHODS

The data used in this study are not publicly available due to data use agreements and privacy regulations but can be accessed through the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA) and/or the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) of Korea on approval. The analytic methods are fully described in the Methods section, and the statistical code may be made available on reasonable request. No materials requiring sharing were generated or used in this study.

Data Source

In the current study, residential greenness was estimated by matching individual residential coordinates with NDVI data derived from the Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) provided by NASA Earthdata. These measures were subsequently linked to the K‐COV‐N (Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency–COVID‐19–National Health Insurance Service) cohort, which was established through a collaboration between KDCA and NHIS of South Korea. The K‐COV‐N cohort integrates nationwide health screening data, NHIS health care utilization records, and COVID‐19 surveillance data from the KDCA, covering the period from January 1, 2018, to December 31, 2023. Leveraging Korea's single‐payer NHIS, this data set encompasses nearly the entire population and enables consistent longitudinal follow‐up of a wide range of clinical events, including those occurring outside of health care institutions. 7

Ethical approval for this human study was obtained from the institutional review board of Kyung Hee University (KHSIRB‐23‐241[EA]), with additional authorization from KDCA and NHIS (KDCA‐NHIS‐2024‐1‐280). All analyses were conducted using deidentified administrative data, and the requirement for informed consent was waived in accordance with institutional and national regulatory guidelines.

Study Population

We included individuals aged ≥19 years with confirmed SARS‐CoV‐2 infection, as determined through laboratory testing using real‐time reverse‐transcription polymerase chain reaction assays of nasal or pharyngeal swabs. 7 These tests were authorized by KDCA and adhered to World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines. In the overlap‐weighted COVID‐19 cohort, SARS‐CoV‐2 infection was defined as a positive reverse‐transcription polymerase chain reaction test result (Figure 1A). 7 To ensure cohort comparability and reduce confounding from residential mobility, only participants with stable residential address records during both the year preceding infection and the year of COVID‐19 diagnosis were retained. The index date was defined as the date of confirmed infection, and follow‐up continued until the earliest of: (1) death, (2) first occurrence of the outcome of interest, or (3) December 31, 2023. Individuals with a prior diagnosis of the outcome before the index date or missing data on key covariates were excluded. Preexisting conditions were identified using records from January 1, 2018, to the index date, and follow‐up was conducted between January 1, 2020, and December 31, 2023 (Figure 1B).

Residential Greenness Exposure

Exposure to residential greenness was assessed using the NDVI, a satellite‐based indicator of vegetation density. NDVI was calculated as the difference between near‐infrared and visible light reflectance divided by their sum, representing the extent of photosynthetically active vegetation. 11 , 12 NDVI data were obtained from NASA's Terra satellite using the MODIS product MOD13A3 (version 6.1), a global data set available monthly at a 1‐km spatial resolution. 13 Monthly MOD13A3 data were integrated according to administrative regions in South Korea to calculate annual average NDVI values. For this study, we calculated monthly NDVI values for all 282 administrative districts in South Korea, referred to as Si‐Gun‐Gu, the basic units of local governance. 14 NDVI categories were defined as follows: values from 0.4 to <0.6 indicated moderate NDVI, and values of ≥0.6 were classified as high NDVI. 15 NDVI values <0.4 were classified as the low NDVI group and used as the reference category. The spatial distribution of NDVI across South Korea is presented in Figure S1.

Outcomes

The primary outcome, identified using the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10), was defined as the first diagnosis of ACS (ICD‐10 codes I20, I21, and I24) 16 and chronic IHD (ICD‐10 code I25). 17 To minimize potential reverse causality, outcomes were restricted to diagnoses made at least 30 days after confirmed SARS‐CoV‐2 infection. 7 In addition, individuals with any recorded diagnosis of ACS or chronic IHD before the index date were excluded from the analytic cohort. This ensured that all participants were free of preexisting cardiovascular disease (CVD) at baseline.

Covariates

To account for potential confounding, we adjusted for a comprehensive set of demographic, clinical, and behavioral covariates. Demographic variables, obtained from the national health insurance database, included sex, age group (19–39, 40–59, and ≥60 years), and household income, categorized into tertiles (low [0–39th percentile], middle [40th–79th percentile], and high [80th–100th percentile]). Clinical parameters were obtained from routine national health screening records. Body mass index was classified according to Asia‐Pacific criteria as underweight (<18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5–22.9 kg/m2), overweight (23.0–24.9 kg/m2), or obese (≥25.0 kg/m2). 18 Blood pressure was defined as normal (systolic <140 mm Hg and diastolic <90 mm Hg) or elevated (systolic ≥140 mm Hg and/or diastolic ≥90 mm Hg). Estimated glomerular filtration rate was dichotomized at <60 versus ≥60 mL/min per 1.73 m2, and fasting blood glucose at <100 versus ≥100 mg/dL. All laboratory values were derived from fasting blood samples collected during health checkups. Additional health status indicators included the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), 19 antihypertensive medication use, and treatment history for diabetes or dyslipidemia. Lifestyle covariates comprised smoking status (never, former/current, or unknown), weekly alcohol consumption (<1 day, 1–2 days, 3–4 days, ≥5 days, or unknown), and aerobic physical activity. Adequate physical activity was defined as ≥600 metabolic equivalent minutes per week, consistent with WHO guidelines. 20 Missing data for all covariates were addressed using the missing‐indicator method.

Confounding and Mediating Factors

Covariate selection was guided by a prespecified directed acyclic graph that represented assumed causal relationships among NDVI, coronary artery diseases, and relevant demographic, socioeconomic, and clinical factors. The directed acyclic graph was used to identify a minimally sufficient adjustment set to control for confounding while avoiding adjustment for downstream mediators on the causal pathway. 21 Based on this framework, variables considered potential cardiometabolic mediators (body mass index, 22 blood pressure, 23 fasting blood glucose, 23 and aerobic physical activity 22 ) were excluded from the primary inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) model. The directed acyclic graph is provided in Figure S2.

Statistical Analysis

The index date was defined as the date of SARS‐CoV‐2 diagnosis, and participants were followed until the earliest occurrence of the outcome, death, or December 31, 2023. Associations were further stratified by COVID‐19 severity, vaccination status, viral variant, sex, age, and household income. Specifically, moderate to severe COVID‐19 was defined as cases requiring intensive care unit admission, oxygen therapy, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, cardiopulmonary resuscitation, or renal replacement therapy. 24 Vaccination status was determined by the number of vaccine doses received before the outcome date and categorized as 0, 1 or 2, or 3 or 4 doses. Viral variant type was classified based on the index infection date: the original strain (before July 31, 2021), the delta variant (August 1, 2021, to December 31, 2021), and the omicron variant (thereafter). 24

To ensure comparability among the 3 NDVI exposure categories, IPTW based on a propensity score model was applied. 25 The covariates included in the weighting model were age, sex, household income level, medication history, CCI, estimated glomerular filtration rate, smoking status, and alcohol consumption, as defined a priori based on the prespecified directed acyclic graph. Stabilized IPTW weights were derived and applied as analytic weights in all outcome models to reduce baseline differences among NDVI exposure groups, rather than applying sampling weights based on unequal selection probabilities. Covariate balance after weighting was assessed using standardized mean differences, with values <0.1 considered indicative of acceptable balance. Weighted Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to estimate adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) with 95% CIs for each outcome among the NDVI groups. 26 Cox proportional hazards models included the same covariate set used for estimating IPTW, ensuring consistency between the weighting and outcome models. Although IPTW was applied to balance baseline covariates among exposure groups, additional covariate adjustment in the weighted outcome model was performed to improve robustness and account for potential residual confounding. This approach corresponds to a doubly robust estimation framework, providing consistent effect estimates if either the propensity score model or the outcome model is correctly specified. 27 To formally assess dose–response relationships, NDVI was additionally modeled as a continuous variable in the Cox proportional hazards model, and the corresponding P value for its regression coefficient was reported as the P for trend. Interaction terms were also included to evaluate effect modification among prespecified stratification variables, and corresponding P values for interaction were calculated. In addition, additive interaction between NDVI and COVID‐19–related and sociodemographic factors was assessed using the relative excess risk due to interaction, attributable proportion, and synergy index. 28 To evaluate the robustness of the findings, sensitivity analyses were conducted using unweighted Cox models and alternative weighted models in which potential mediators were additionally included. Statistical significance was defined as a 2‐sided P value <0.05. All analyses were performed using SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc.).

RESULTS

Study Populations

We identified 7 999 898 individuals aged 19 years or older in South Korea with confirmed SARS‐CoV‐2 infection for initial inclusion. After excluding those without residential address data, those who died before the index date, and those with inconsistent address records during the year before infection, 3 097 179 participants remained eligible. These individuals were categorized into low, moderate, and high exposure groups according to residential NDVI levels. To balance baseline characteristics, 1:1:1 IPTW was applied, which resulted in final cohort sizes of 784 449 in the low NDVI group, 1 849 208 in the moderate group, and 463 522 in the high group (Figure 1 and Table 1).

Baseline characteristics of individuals excluded from the analytic cohort (n=4 902 719) are presented in Table S2. Overall, their characteristics were broadly comparable to those of the included cohort, with no substantial differences among major demographic, lifestyle, or clinical variables.

The median follow‐up duration was 1.68 years (interquartile range, 1.20–1.79 years) for both outcomes, yielding a total of 4 485 949 person‐years for ACS and 4 364 471 person‐years for chronic IHD.

Overall Associations of NDVI With ACS and Chronic IHD

NDVI was not associated with risk of ACS. Compared with the low NDVI group, the aHR was 1.00 (95% CI, 0.98–1.02) for both moderate and high exposure. In contrast, NDVI was associated with lower risk of chronic IHD, with aHRs of 0.86 (95% CI, 0.84–0.88) for moderate exposure and 0.83 (95% CI, 0.81–0.85) for high exposure (Table 2). A formal test for linear trend among NDVI categories supported a dose–response association for chronic IHD but not for ACS.

Sensitivity Analyses of the Association Between NDVI and ACS and Chronic IHD

In sensitivity analyses using unweighted Cox proportional hazards models, the overall patterns of association were consistent with the primary IPTW‐weighted analyses. NDVI remained not associated with the risk of ACS (aHR for moderate NDVI, 1.00 [95% CI, 0.97–1.03]; aHR for high NDVI, 1.00 [95% CI, 0.96–1.04]), whereas higher NDVI was consistently associated with a lower risk of chronic IHD (aHR for moderate NDVI, 0.86 [95% CI, 0.82–0.90]; aHR for high NDVI, 0.83 [95% CI, 0.78–0.87]) (Table S3).

When potential cardiometabolic mediators, including body mass index, blood pressure, fasting blood glucose, and aerobic physical activity, were additionally included in the IPTW‐weighted Cox models, the overall findings remained materially unchanged (Table S4). NDVI continued to show no association with the risk of ACS (aHR for moderate NDVI, 1.00 [95% CI, 0.98–1.02]; aHR for high NDVI, 1.00 [95% CI, 0.98–1.02]), whereas high NDVI remained consistently associated with a lower risk of chronic IHD, with only minimal attenuation of the effect estimates (aHR for moderate NDVI, 0.86 [95% CI, 0.84–0.88]; aHR for high NDVI, 0.82 [95% CI, 0.80–0.84]).

Subgroup Analyses by COVID‐19 Severity, Vaccination Status, and Variant Type

In analyses stratified by COVID‐19 severity, reductions in chronic IHD were observed among all exposure levels (Figure 2 and Table S5). Among those without moderate to severe COVID‐19, the aHR was 0.86 (95% CI, 0.84–0.89) for moderate exposure and 0.83 (95% CI, 0.81–0.85) for high exposure. Larger reductions were observed among those with moderate to severe COVID‐19, with aHRs of 0.71 (95% CI, 0.61–0.84) for moderate exposure and 0.53 (95% CI, 0.44–0.63) for high exposure. By vaccination status, no association was observed among unvaccinated individuals (Table S6). Among those vaccinated 1 or 2 times, the aHR was 0.86 (95% CI, 0.82–0.90) for moderate NDVI and 0.82 (95% CI, 0.79–0.87) for high NDVI. Among those vaccinated 3 or 4 times, the aHR was 0.86 (95% CI, 0.83–0.88) for moderate NDVI and 0.82 (95% CI, 0.79–0.84) for high NDVI. When examined by variant type, protective associations with chronic IHD were observed during omicron infection but not during original strain or delta (Table S7). For omicron, the aHR was 0.85 (95% CI, 0.83–0.87) for moderate NDVI and 0.82 (95% CI, 0.80–0.84) for high NDVI. Among categories of severity, vaccination, and variant type, NDVI was not associated with risk of ACS.

Subgroup Analyses by Sex, Age, and Income Level

In sex‐stratified analyses, NDVI was associated with lower risk of chronic IHD in both sexes, with larger reductions in men (Figure 3 and Table S8). Among men, the aHR was 0.85 (95% CI, 0.83–0.88) for moderate NDVI and 0.81 (95% CI, 0.78–0.83) for high NDVI. Among women, the aHR was 0.88 (95% CI, 0.83–0.93) for moderate NDVI and 0.90 (95% CI, 0.85–0.95) for high NDVI. However, NDVI was not associated with risk of ACS in either men or women. By age, NDVI was associated with lower risk of chronic IHD among all groups (Table S9). The largest reduction was observed in those aged 19 to 39 years (aHR, 0.66 [95% CI, 0.54–0.82]) for moderate exposure. Among those aged 40 to 59 years, the aHRs were 0.89 (95% CI, 0.85–0.93) for moderate exposure and 0.82 (95% CI, 0.78–0.85) for high exposure. Among those 65 years or older, the aHRs were 0.85 (95% CI, 0.82–0.87) for moderate exposure and 0.84 (95% CI, 0.81–0.86) for high exposure. For ACS, NDVI was associated with lower risk only among those aged 19 to 39 years, with aHRs of 0.84 (95% CI, 0.77–0.91) for moderate exposure and 0.71 (95% CI, 0.65–0.78) for high exposure. By income level, NDVI was associated with lower risk of chronic IHD in all groups, with progressively larger reductions in higher income categories (Table S10). In the lowest income group, the aHR was 0.83 (95% CI, 0.80–0.87) for moderate NDVI and 0.88 (95% CI, 0.84–0.92) for high NDVI. In the middle‐income group, the aHR was 0.88 (95% CI, 0.84–0.91) for moderate NDVI and 0.84 (95% CI, 0.80–0.87) for high NDVI. In the highest income group, the aHR was 0.86 (95% CI, 0.83–0.90) for moderate NDVI and 0.79 (95% CI, 0.76–0.82) for high NDVI.

Additive Interaction Between NDVI and COVID‐19–Related and Sociodemographic Factors

For ACS, additive interaction estimates were generally close to the null among all examined COVID‐19–related and sociodemographic factors, indicating no meaningful additive interaction between NDVI and these factors for ACS (Table S11).

In contrast, for chronic IHD, a strong negative additive interaction was observed between NDVI and COVID‐19 severity (relative excess risk due to interaction, −2.72 [95% CI, −3.92 to −1.59]; attributable proportion, −0.55 [95% CI, −0.79 to −0.31]; synergy index, 0.59 [95% CI, 0.41–0.78]), indicating that the absolute risk reduction associated with higher NDVI was substantially greater among individuals with more severe disease. In addition, a significant positive additive interaction by sex was identified (relative excess risk due to interaction, 0.12 [95% CI, 0.05–0.20]; attributable proportion, 0.37 [95% CI, 0.19–0.53]; synergy index, 1.64 [95% CI, 1.31–2.02]), suggesting that the association between NDVI and chronic ischemic heart disease differed by sex on the absolute risk scale.

DISCUSSION

Key Findings

In this nationwide cohort study of 3 097 179 individuals in South Korea, we evaluated the association between residential greenness, measured using NDVI, and the subsequent development of coronary artery disease among individuals with confirmed SARS‐CoV‐2 infection. Our study offers several key findings. First, residential greenness was consistently associated with lower risk of chronic IHD, whereas no significant associations were exhibited for ACS. Second, protective associations for chronic IHD were more pronounced in individuals with moderate to severe COVID‐19, indicating greater reductions among those with elevated baseline vascular injury. Third, risk reductions were present among vaccinated individuals and observed only for omicron infection. Fourth, in stratified analyses, reductions were larger in men than in women and were strongest among individuals aged 19 to 39 years. Fifth, although protective associations were observed among all income groups, higher income categories exhibited modestly larger risk reductions. Taken together, these findings indicate that greenness did not influence the short‐term prothrombotic effects of SARS‐CoV‐2 in acute coronary events but was consistently associated with lower risk of chronic IHD after COVID‐19, particularly in older, vaccinated, and severely affected individuals.

Comparison With Previous Studies

Several studies have examined the cardiovascular benefits of residential greenness, with most reporting protective associations for CVD, including coronary artery disease. In a cross‐sectional analysis of 24 845 individuals, higher NDVI within 500 meters of residence was associated with a 27% lower prevalence of CVD, although outcomes were based on self‐reported diagnoses, introducing the possibility of recall bias. 29 By contrast, our use of nationwide administrative data and ICD‐10 codes enabled the identification of incident diagnoses, thereby improving outcome validity. 29 Other large cohort studies have also reported protective associations. A study of 243 558 individuals aged 65 years and older found that higher NDVI was associated with lower incidence of cardiovascular conditions over time, 30 and another study comparing persistently high versus low greenness reported lower odds of incident CVD. 1 Both of these investigations, however, were restricted to older populations. 1 , 30 In contrast, our study included adults aged 19 years and older and showed that protective associations were not confined to older individuals, with the strongest associations observed among those aged 19 to 39 years for both ACS and chronic IHD.

To the best of our knowledge, there is a lack of previous research that has evaluated greenness and cardiovascular outcomes in the setting of SARS‐CoV‐2 infection. By examining disease severity, vaccination status, and viral variant, our analysis provides a possible relationship between greenness and coronary artery disease after COVID‐19. Importantly, whereas earlier studies have reported protective associations for both ACS and chronic IHD, 31 , 32 we observed protection only for chronic IHD, not for ACS. This finding may be explained by differences in how SARS‐CoV‐2 influences acute coronary events compared with the gradual development of chronic atherosclerotic disease, which has not been previously examined. 33

Plausible Mechanisms

Several biological pathways have been proposed to explain why residential greenness may be associated with a lower risk of both ACS and chronic IHD. 34 , 35 However, our findings suggest that greenness was not sufficient to counteract the strong prothrombotic effects of SARS‐CoV‐2 in acute coronary events. SARS‐CoV‐2 has been found to bind to angiotensin‐converting enzyme 2 receptors on cardiomyocytes, allowing direct myocardial injury, 36 , 37 while severe infection has been associated with cytokine storm from an imbalanced helper T‐cell response, 36 , 38 which destabilize atherosclerotic plaques and promote thrombosis, processes that lead to ACS. 8 , 36 The onset of these events is often abrupt, with prior studies indicating that the elevated risk of myocardial infarction is concentrated in the first few weeks after infection and declines over time. 39 Thus, this short period of elevated risk suggests that the longer‐term pathways through which greenness benefits cardiovascular health, 1 , 30 such as reductions in oxidative stress and systemic inflammation, 35 may not have sufficient time to influence the occurrence of acute coronary events after COVID‐19. In contrast, greenness has been reported to reduce coronary artery stenosis 40 and slow the progression of subclinical atherosclerosis, 3 which may explain its observed long‐term protective associations with chronic IHD.

Variant‐specific biology may also explain the differential associations we observed, with protective effects present for omicron but not for the original strain or delta. Omicron sublineages replicate predominantly in the upper airways and show markedly reduced replication in the lower respiratory tract, leading to limited infection of deep lung tissue. 41 In contrast, delta variants have been associated with extensive alveolar damage and widespread inflammatory infiltration. 42 Thus, reduced replication in the lower respiratory tract may lessen systemic inflammation, creating conditions in which greenness‐related cardioprotective mechanisms are more likely to be observed. 41

Clinical and Policy Implications

Our finding that residential greenness was associated with lower risk of chronic IHD, particularly among vaccinated individuals, suggests that vaccination may reduce systemic inflammation and vascular injury, thereby making cardiovascular benefits of greenness more apparent. Vaccination, although developed to prevent infection, has been associated with broader immune adaptations, as seen with the Bacillus Calmette–Guérin vaccine in strengthening responses to unrelated pathogens, improving cancer therapy, and delaying autoimmune disease. 43 In the context of COVID‐19, such immune effects may contribute to vascular stability and increase the likelihood that greenness‐related cardiovascular benefits are expressed. 44 These findings support Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices recommendations for COVID‐19 vaccination as part of strategies with potential impact on long‐term cardiovascular health. 45

Strengths and Limitations

This nationwide cohort study of 3 097 179 individuals with confirmed SARS‐CoV‐2 infection in South Korea examined the association between residential NDVI and subsequent IHD, using comprehensive administrative and satellite‐derived data. However, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, ACS and chronic IHD were identified using ICD‐10 codes from national insurance claims data. Although these codes have been validated in Korean claims data, they may not fully identify milder or undiagnosed cases. To reduce potential misclassification, we restricted analyses to first‐time diagnoses after COVID‐19 and utilized a large cohort to enhance statistical power. Second, outcomes could not be classified by clinical subtypes (eg, ST‐segment–elevation and non–ST‐segment–elevation myocardial infarction), and information on relevant parameters such as biomarker levels or angiographic findings were not available. 46 , 47 Nevertheless, because both subtypes arise from shared mechanisms such as inflammation, plaque instability, and thrombosis, the combined outcome provides meaningful information on the potential cardiovascular effects of greenness after COVID‐19. 4 Third, covariates related to the built environment, such as walkability and roadway density, 5 and additional environmental exposures, including air, noise, and light pollution or ambient temperature, 3 neighborhood‐level socioeconomic indicators such as the Area Deprivation Index, 48 could not be included because these data were not available. Fourth, residual confounding cannot be excluded despite adjustment for a wide range of demographic, behavioral, and clinical factors and the use of inverse probability of treatment weighting. Unmeasured variables such as diet, psychosocial stress, or occupational exposures may influence both greenness exposure and coronary artery disease risk, but these factors were not available in the data set. 49 Thus, cautious interpretation is needed, and causal relationships cannot be inferred from this observational study. Fifth, greenness was measured using NDVI, a satellite‐based index that primarily reflects vegetation density in larger public areas such as parks and forests. However, this metric does not account for smaller‐scale or private greenery, such as gardens, street trees, or greenbelts, which may also contribute to health benefits. 50 In addition, owing to limitations in the available data, we were unable to evaluate exposure robustness using alternative NDVI buffer zones (eg, 500 meters or 1000 meters) or to further adjust for regional‐level characteristics such as air pollution and urbanization. These unmeasured spatial factors may have influenced both residential greenness and the risk of CVD. Sixth, NDVI values were assigned according to residential address, assuming participants spent the majority of their time at home. While this assumption may not apply to all individuals, particularly those whose occupations required extended periods away from their residence, pandemic‐related restrictions on mobility likely reduced variability in daily exposures, making residential NDVI a reasonable approximation.

Despite these limitations, this study has several notable strengths. Using a nationwide cohort of 3 097 179 individuals with confirmed SARS‐CoV‐2 infection allowed for precise estimates and detailed subgroup analyses. The integration of insurance claims, COVID‐19 surveillance data, and satellite‐derived NDVI provided consistent outcome definitions and standardized exposure measurement, offering new insights into how residential greenness may influence ACS and chronic IHD risk differently following SARS‐CoV‐2 infection. In addition, the findings were robust among multiple analytic approaches, with similar effect estimates observed in IPTW‐weighted and ‐unweighted models, as well as in models that additionally included potential cardiometabolic mediators, supporting the stability of the observed associations.

CONCLUSIONS

In this nationwide cohort of 3 097 179 individuals with confirmed SARS‐CoV‐2 infection, residential greenness was associated with lower risk of chronic IHD but not ACS. Protective associations for chronic disease were stronger in individuals with moderate to severe infection, those vaccinated against COVID‐19, and those infected with the omicron variant, as well as in men, younger individuals, and higher income groups. These findings suggest that greenness was not sufficient to overcome the acute prothrombotic effects of SARS‐CoV‐2 but was consistently associated with reduced risk of chronic IHD, supporting the role of vaccination and access to residential greenness for long‐term cardiovascular health during pandemics.
