Cultural competence among ICU nursing staff: an explanatory sequential mixed-methods study
Authors: Yu Haiping, Li Yuhan, Zhang Weiying, Peng Youqing, Lv Jianhong, Zhang Yali, Li Yangyang, Zhang Yajuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesObjective To understand the cultural competence of ICU nurses by adopting an explanatory sequential mixed-methods design, and to analyze its influencing factors, so as to provide directions for improving the cultural competence of ICU nurses.MethodsIn July 2025, 238 ICU registered nurses from three comprehensive tertiary hospitals in Shanghai were enrolled using cluster sampling. Data were collected using a general information questionnaire, the Cultural Intelligence Scale (CQS), and the revised Inventory for Assessing the Process of Cultural Competency (IAPCC-R). A total of 204 nurses completed valid questionnaires, with an effective response rate of 85.71%. Multiple linear regression and Pearson correlation analysis were performed to identify influencing factors. In August 2025, 22 ICU nurses who met the eligibility criteria were selected by purposive sampling for semi-structured in-depth interviews, and data were analyzed using Colaizzi’s 7-step method.ResultsThe mean score of cultural competence among 204 ICU nurses was 74.42, indicating a moderate level. Among them, 68 cases (33.33%) had a high level of cultural competence, 134 cases (66.18%) had a medium level, and 2 cases (0.98%) had a low level. Particularly, cultural knowledge and cultural contact were identified as the two weakest dimensions. Multiple linear regression analysis showed that cultural intelligence, foreign language proficiency, and professional title were influencing factors of cultural competence among ICU nursing staff (P < 0.05). Pearson correlation analysis revealed a strong positive correlation between cultural intelligence and cultural competence (r = 0.79, p < 0.001).Qualitative findings further explained the underlying reasons for the moderate overall level and weak dimensions observed in the quantitative results, including fragmented acquisition of cultural knowledge, insufficient and unguided cultural contact experiences, inadequate organizational support, and cross-cultural communication barriers.The qualitative research extracted five themes: cultural awareness, cultural knowledge, cultural skills, cultural contact, and cultural willingness. Integrated analysis of quantitative and qualitative results found that the two complemented each other in explaining the current situation and influencing factors of ICU nursing staff’s cultural competency.ConclusionTo enhance the cultural competence of ICU nursing staff, targeted training should focus on the two weakest dimensions: cultural knowledge and cultural contact. A structured, scenario-based training system is recommended to improve cultural competence sustainably, so as to provide high-quality nursing services for patients with cultural care needs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04921-4.

Introduction

Cultural competence refers to the ability of caregivers to understand and respond to the values, needs, and expectations of patients and their families from different cultural backgrounds in cross-cultural communication [1]. This capability is an important foundation for clinical care, directly impacting the quality of care, especially for intensive care unit (ICU) caregivers. In high-intensity and high-risk critical care situations, cultural competence helps nurses accurately identify patients’ humanistic nursing needs and avoid communication barriers, misdiagnoses, and treatment deviations caused by culture shock. Conversely, inadequate cultural competence can exacerbate the gap between caregivers and patients, especially when facing differences in language, ethnicity, or religious beliefs [2].

Theoretically, cultural competence is often conceptualized through Campinha-Bacote’s model, which comprises five interrelated dimensions: cultural awareness, cultural knowledge, cultural skills, cultural encounters, and cultural desire [3]. Cultural intelligence, a related but distinct construct, refers to an individual’s capability to function and manage effectively in culturally diverse settings, encompassing metacognitive, cognitive, motivational, and behavioral dimensions [4]. While cultural competence focuses on healthcare-specific outcomes, cultural intelligence provides a broader framework for understanding cross-cultural adaptability [5]. The Cultural Intelligence Scale and the IAPCC‑R used in this study have been revalidated for reliability and validity in Chinese nursing populations from 2022 to 2025, supporting their temporal relevance and applicability.

Previous studies have shown that ICU nurses face unique challenges in cross-cultural care, including time constraints, high-acuity situations, and limited family involvement [6, 7]. Soleimani and Yarahmadi [6] found that cultural competence among critical care nurses was significantly associated with empathy and work engagement, while Siddiqui and Metaxa [7] emphasized the need for targeted cultural competence training in ICU settings. Majda et al. [8] reported that healthcare professionals providing emergency medical services demonstrated moderate levels of cultural competence and cultural intelligence, with significant variations based on prior cross-cultural training and experience.

In China, the demand for cross-cultural nursing has increased rapidly due to urbanization and internationalization. Li et al. [9] investigated cultural competence among Chinese nurses and found that the overall level was moderate, with cultural knowledge and cultural contact dimensions scoring lowest. Cai et al. [10] identified cross-cultural contact experience and foreign language proficiency as significant predictors of cultural competence among Chinese nurses.

To improve the cross-cultural communication and humanistic care capabilities of nursing staff, the National Health Commission has successively issued the “National Nursing Development Plan (2021–2025)” and the “Action Plan for Further Improving Nursing Services (2023–2025)” [11, 12], which clearly propose strengthening cultural nursing-related training to improve patients’ medical experience. As a city with a high degree of internationalization, Shanghai has seen increasingly prominent cross-cultural nursing needs. The overall cultural competence of nursing staff in Shanghai is at a moderate level [13]. Improving the cultural competence of ICU nurses is not only an important way to ensure nursing quality but also a practical need to enhance the soft power of urban medical care.

Therefore, this study aims to investigate the current situation and influencing factors of cultural competence among ICU nursing staff in Shanghai using an explanatory sequential mixed-methods design, and to provide a basis for formulating targeted capacity improvement strategies.

Objects and methods

Research objects

Quantitative study participants

In July 2025, 238 in-service registered nurses from the intensive care units (ICUs) of three tertiary general hospitals in Shanghai (Oriental Hospital Affiliated to Tongji University, Shanghai Sixth People’s Hospital, and Shanghai Changzheng Hospital) were selected using cluster sampling. The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Shanghai Oriental Hospital (EC.D(BG).016.02.1). All subjects signed written informed consent, and the study process complied with the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Inclusion criteria: (1) Obtain the “Nurse Practice Certificate” and working in ICU clinical nursing positions for no less than 2 years to ensure adequate clinical experience(New nurses were excluded to focus on professional cultural adaptation characteristics, subgroup analyses including new nurses can be performed in future research); (2) Informed consent and voluntary participation, able to independently complete questionnaires or undergo interviews.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Intern nurses and nurses in the probationary period; (2) Nurses unable to complete the investigation due to vacation, leave of absence, etc.; (3) Questionnaires with a missing rate 20% or obvious logical errors.

Sample size consideration: A priori power analysis was performed using G*Power 3.1. With an effect size f² = 0.15, α = 0.05, power = 0.80, and 14 predictors, the required minimum sample size was 185. Considering a 15% non‑response rate, the target sample size was set to 238, which was statistically sufficient.

Qualitative study participants

In August 2025, ICU nursing staff who participated in the quantitative study were selected using purposive sampling. Inclusion criteria for qualitative interviews: (1) participated in the quantitative survey; (2) hold a valid Nurse Practice Certificate and have at least 2 years of clinical working experience in the ICU; (3) represented diverse characteristics based on quantitative findings (e.g., different levels of cultural competence, professional titles, and foreign language proficiency); (4) willing to share experiences and provide informed consent. Exclusion criteria: (1) unable to schedule an interview within the study timeframe; (2) declined audio recording.

A total of 22 nurses were interviewed until data saturation was reached (no new themes emerged), and they were coded as P1 ~ P22. The general information of the 22 ICU nursing staff is detailed in Table 1.

Research methods

Study design

This study adopted an explanatory sequential mixed-methods design, with quantitative data collected first and qualitative data collected later. The two types of data were integrated and analyzed in the interpretation stage. This design was chosen because it allows qualitative findings to explain and elaborate on quantitative results, particularly the unexpected or significant findings from the regression analysis [14]. The quantitative phase identified the level and predictors of cultural competence, while the qualitative phase provided depth and context to understand how these factors operate in clinical practice.

Quantitative research method

A cross-sectional survey method was adopted.

Scoring criteria: <50 = low level, 50–75 = medium level, 75 = high level. Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.70. Confirmatory factor analysis showed good model fit (CFI0.90, RMSEA < 0.08), correlation coefficients with the criterion scale were 0.50–0.65 (p < 0.01), and it effectively distinguished different competency levels among nursing staff (p < 0.001).

Data collection methods

Quantitative data collection: Before the survey, researchers contacted the heads of ICU departments, focusing on in-service registered nurses in the ICUs of the three hospitals. During the formal survey, researchers explained the purpose, content, and filling requirements using unified guidance language. After obtaining consent, questionnaires were distributed. Data were collected through one-on-one question-and-answer sessions. After collection, researchers immediately checked questionnaire completeness and asked about missing questions; if the number of questions that respondents could not answer or refused to answer was ≥ 30%, the questionnaire was judged invalid. A total of 238 questionnaires were distributed, and 204 valid questionnaires were collected, with an effective response rate of 85.71%.Qualitative data collection: Before interviews, research team members explained the purpose, content, significance, and privacy protection measures to interviewees. After obtaining informed consent, one-on-one semi-structured in-depth interviews were conducted in comfortable, quiet rooms. Each interview lasted 30–60 min and was audio-recorded with consent.

To ensure rigor, the following measures were taken: ① Investigator triangulation: Two researchers independently analyzed data and compared findings; ② Member checking: Preliminary findings were returned to five participants to verify interpretation accuracy; ③ Reflexivity: Researchers maintained a reflexive diary to record personal presuppositions and potential biases throughout the analysis process; ④ Audit trail: All analytical decisions were documented to enhance transparency.

Statistical methods

SPSS 27.0 software was used for data analysis. Normally distributed continuous data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x̄±s), and one-way analysis of variance was used for intergroup comparisons. Categorical data were expressed as frequencies and percentages (%). Pearson correlation analysis was used to explore the relationship between cultural intelligence and cultural competence. Multiple linear regression analysis using the Enter method was performed to identify factors influencing cultural competence. In the Enter method, all independent variables are entered into the regression model simultaneously; therefore, variable entry or removal criteria are not applicable. The significance level was set at α = 0.05. For non-normally distributed data, medians and interquartile ranges were used for description, and non-parametric tests were used for intergroup comparisons. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Correlation analysis between cultural intelligence and cultural competence

Pearson correlation analysis was performed with cultural competence total score as the dependent variable and cultural intelligence total score as the independent variable. The scatter plot (Fig. 1) showed a significant positive linear trend between the two variables, confirmed by Pearson correlation analysis (r = 0.79, p < 0.001). The coefficient of determination R²=0.625 indicated that cultural intelligence total score explained 62.5% of the variance in cultural competence total score. The fitted linear slope (β = 0.68, 95%CI [0.52, 0.84]) further confirmed that for each unit increase in cultural intelligence level, cultural competence increased by 0.68 units. This result supports that cultural intelligence is an important predictor of cultural competence.

Qualitative research results

Through thematic analysis of the 22 interview transcripts, five major themes were identified that capture ICU nursing staff’s experiences and perspectives regarding cultural competence. These themes correspond to the five dimensions of cultural competence and provide depth to the quantitative findings. Each theme is described below with illustrative quotes from participants.

Theme 1: Cultural awareness – from identifying differences to self-reflection

This theme captures how nurses develop sensitivity to cultural diversity and engage in self-reflection about their own cultural biases. Participants described recognizing how patients’ cultural backgrounds influence health perceptions and treatment decisions, which prompted them to examine their own assumptions and adopt a more open mindset.

P1: “Some family members prefer Buddhism, and they will pray for some tokens to be placed next to the patient and hung at the head of the bed. They also record or play audio of some verses that we call chanting.”

P18: “In foreign countries, we must be people-oriented, based on their ideas. Foreign countries advocate freedom more. For example, if they want to smoke, we will take them to smoke. Whatever they want to do, as long as it doesn’t violate national laws, we will let them do it.”

P9: “A foreigner from India didn’t speak English, so communication was difficult. We used pictures—for example, if he needed to go to the toilet, we communicated with him this way for daily life needs, and it worked.”

Theme 2: Cultural knowledge – building a multicultural information support network

This theme describes the strategies nurses employ to acquire knowledge about different cultural groups. Participants discussed multiple information sources, including direct patient/family communication, hospital cultural resources, translation services, and online searches. The theme highlights both the importance of systematic knowledge and the challenges of fragmented information access.

P16: “First, if the patient can communicate in Chinese or English, we use language to inquire about the patient’s needs. Another is that if family members understand Chinese or can do translation work, we can also ask them and get information from them.”

P10: “We can make three points. First, our cultural team at Oriental Hospital has different language support, including religious beliefs. Besides seeking help from the team, Oriental Hospital also has a cultural brochure where most religious beliefs are recorded, especially collections of some minority languages or more special eating habits. In the information age, we can use the Internet—if you put forward some needs to AI, it will inform you in the general direction.”

P5: “First is to communicate with the family. For example, if they have special needs or something we don’t understand, we can first understand their specific needs and implementation process, such as prayer. Then we respect them more, and we can learn about their race or beliefs from the Internet.”

Theme 3: Cultural skills – practical abilities in communication, negotiation, and creative problem-solving

This theme captures the practical skills nurses apply when cultural needs arise in clinical settings. Participants described negotiating conflicts between treatment requirements and cultural practices, adapting communication strategies, and developing creative solutions that respect both medical safety and cultural preferences. The theme reflects the translation of awareness and knowledge into actionable clinical competencies.

P2: “At that time, the Dalai Lama’s disciples and grandchildren wanted to do something for him, and we raised objections to this request. Then we set a time for them not to be disturbed—for example, during afternoon family visits, they had to put on protective clothing, masks, and hats to come in. Some items they used also needed disinfection, and we tried not to take too long, within an hour or half an hour, and not interfere too much with our daily treatment needs.

P8: “Generally, if there’s a conflict between the patient’s time and our treatment time, we first assess whether the patient’s condition is stable. If it affects the patient’s life, the doctors and I communicate with the family in time, or focus on the patient’s treatment. If the patient’s condition is stable, we generally respect the patient’s habits first without affecting treatment. If they pray first, after prayer, we treat them, and we formulate an exclusive treatment plan according to the patient’s condition.”

P16: “First, we now have preoperative visits for ICU patients. Before surgery, we find out in advance whether they have cultural care needs. If so, we hand over with the host nurse the next day using WeChat groups. The next day, the receiving nurse is already reminded that the patient has cultural care needs—whether dialect, sleeping, or eating—and we record it truthfully.”

Theme 4: Cultural contact – practice, reflection, and learning in real scenarios

This theme describes how nurses engage with cross-cultural clinical experiences and transform them into learning opportunities. Participants emphasized the importance of guided practice, scenario-based training, and structured reflection. They identified current gaps in transforming contact experiences into systematic learning and expressed preferences for simulation-based and case-sharing approaches.

P15: “First, it’s from the point and then gradually expand to the whole area, because the cultural development of the entire ICU still needs individual teachers to become familiar with it first, so that everyone can do it more standardly. There are still many problems with practical operation, because there will always be some combination of theory and practice that are not particularly good, so I still hope to have some scenario simulation operations.”

P11: “You can arrange for personnel to share an excellent case, do a little relevant experience sharing. If appropriate, you can conduct some relevant assessments, so people will learn this knowledge more seriously. Then there are some online classes that you can actually listen to—there are actually a lot of benefits. Of course, if there’s an offline study class, that’s best.”

P10: “Besides the big courses, you can also suggest some personalized guidance from the cultural team. For example, if a patient has some specificity or is uncommon, just like when writing a paper you write a case, it’s recommended to popularize the collected information to personnel involved in the outpatient ward and monitoring room of the whole hospital. Only after the whole hospital shares it will they find out there are still such cases, and what ways should I improve some doctor-patient communication.”

Theme 5: Cultural willingness – cultivating intrinsic motivation and building supportive systems

This theme captures the intrinsic motivation of nurses to provide culturally competent care and their perceptions of organizational support needed to sustain this motivation. Participants emphasized that personal interest and commitment are essential foundations, but systematic support—including management endorsement, structured training, resource allocation, and peer collaboration—is crucial for transforming individual willingness into sustainable practice.P16: “Management should give certain support. Preferably ICU nurses and specialist nurses in terms of cultural competence—this is also an aspect of training specialist nurses. For training, let’s first find out whether the nurse has any intention in this regard and whether they are more interested in cultural care. Interest is the best teacher. If they have it, their learning ability and enthusiasm will be better in all aspects, and the degree of acceptance will be better.”

P2: “I think if there’s a cross-cultural need, we also need to empathize. If I become a leader, if I have this way and funds, I can send some outstanding backbones of our hospital to go abroad to participate in some training, put myself in the shoes of cross-cultural impact. For our personal feelings, when the time comes, we can bring back some new ideas and new experiences from abroad, which will definitely be of great help to improving our hospital’s cultural competence.”

P8: “First, build a structured training system to update professional knowledge, such as fixed monthly or quarterly. Then organize the operation of basic theories and professional theories. Second, cross-cultural communication training—we can learn and respect the patient’s privacy and maintain the patient’s multicultural background through role-playing. Finally, team collaboration and experience sharing—everyone participates in the learning mode. First, encourage case collection and explanation led by younger nurses, then comments and checks by senior nurses to form a closed loop of knowledge.”

Discussion

This study investigated the cultural competence level of 204 ICU nursing staff in general hospitals in Shanghai using an explanatory sequential mixed-methods design. Quantitative results showed that 68 (33.33%) reached a good level, and the qualitative study verified that ICU nursing staff’s cultural competence level is closely related to the degree of diagnosis and treatment cooperation and patient safety as a core group dealing with medical needs of patients from multicultural backgrounds. In line with international studies, the present study confirmed that ICU nurses present moderate cultural competence.Soleimani et al. (2023, Iran) reported that 41.2% of ICU nurses achieved a high level of cultural competence.Majda et al. (2024, Europe) indicated that 37.8%–42.1% of critical care nurses reached high levels.In this study, only 33.33% of nurses achieved high cultural competence, which is slightly lower than international reports. This difference may be attributed to variations in cross-cultural education systems, clinical training opportunities, and the frequency of cross-cultural encounters in different countries. These results highlight the urgency to develop targeted, systematic cultural competence training for ICU nurses in China.

Current status of cultural competence: basic standard but insufficient depth

Analysis of the current situation of ICU nursing staff’s cultural competence showed significant deficiencies in the five dimensions, with cultural knowledge and cultural contact deficiencies particularly prominent. This finding is consistent with Majda et al.‘s (2024, Europe) study on European emergency medical services professionals [8], which also found that limited cross-cultural contact opportunities were a key factor restricting cultural competence development.

In the cultural knowledge dimension, nursing staff lacked systematic understanding of health belief systems, disease cognition patterns, and treatment preferences in multicultural contexts. This resulted in overlooking the key influence of cultural factors in clinical practice, over-relying on standardized processes when facing ethnic minority or foreign patients, and difficulty effectively responding to special needs of specific cultural groups, such as religious diagnosis and treatment taboos or family participation in decision-making traditions [21]. This aligns with Li et al. (2023, China) [9], which indicated that Chinese nurses’ cultural knowledge reserve was generally insufficient, especially in understanding religion’s influence on health behaviors. This international comparison confirms that insufficient cultural knowledge and limited cross-cultural contact are common challenges for ICU nurses worldwide, rather than being region‑specific issues.

In the cultural contact dimension, nursing staff showed obvious passivity, lacking awareness and ability to actively explore patients’ cultural backgrounds. This not only affected recognition of non-verbal cultural cues but could also damage nurse-patient relationships and treatment compliance due to cultural misunderstandings(Henson and Drame, 2022, United States) [22]. In qualitative interviews, multiple participants mentioned limited contact opportunities and lack of guidance, further explaining why cultural contact was the lowest-scoring dimension.

Notably, the dual defect of cultural knowledge and cultural contact further derived a multi-dimensional chain reaction: insufficient cognitive reserve restricted cultural awareness sensitivity, lack of practical opportunities restricted cross-cultural conflict mediation ability development, and long-term passive adaptation weakened endogenous motivation for professional improvement(Huang, 2023, China) [23]. This complex interaction mechanism kept ICU nurses’ cultural competence at a basic level for a long time, making qualitative improvement difficult to achieve. Therefore, there is an urgent need to build a systematic training system to effectively improve the cross-cultural care ability of the nursing team through comprehensive intervention strategies such as systematic theoretical knowledge training, cross-cultural communication scenario simulation training, and cultural sensitivity cultivation in clinical practice.

Influencing factors of cultural competence: the roles of cultural intelligence, foreign language proficiency, and professional title

The regression analysis in this study showed that cultural intelligence, foreign language proficiency, and professional title were core influencing factors of cultural competence among ICU nursing staff, consistent with Cai et al.‘s study on Chinese nurses(2024, China) [10]. However, it should be emphasized that this study used a cross-sectional design, and correlations should not be interpreted as causal relationships. Qualitative research findings help understand how these factors operate in actual clinical situations.Educational level (P = 0.387) and overseas study experience (P = 0.089) showed no significant influence on cultural competence. This challenges the conventional view that higher education or overseas experience automatically improves cross‑cultural performance. In ICU settings, cultural competence depends more on clinical practice and communication skills than academic background or short‑term overseas experience.

Cultural intelligence as a core predictor: This study found that cultural intelligence strongly predicted cultural competence (β = 0.790, R²=0.625). As a multi-dimensional construct, the cognitive sub-dimension of cultural intelligence affects the breadth of cultural knowledge, and the metacognitive sub-dimension determines the depth of knowledge integration. In qualitative interviews, P10’s mention of “understanding their specific needs and implementation process” reflected the cognitive and metacognitive components of cultural intelligence; P16’s description of “finding out in advance whether they have cultural care needs, then handing over” demonstrated the behavioral dimension of cultural intelligence. Bakhtiari et al.‘s study on nursing students(2023, Iran) [24] also confirmed that cultural intelligence was significantly positively correlated with cultural competence. However, cultural intelligence alone is insufficient to ensure clinical cultural competence; it needs to be combined with practical experience and organizational support.

Multicollinearity diagnostics showed all VIF values < 5, indicating no serious multicollinearity. However, given the high β value, hierarchical regression was performed to examine the independent contribution of cultural intelligence, which remained significant after controlling for demographic variables.

Foreign language proficiency as a foundational ability: Foreign language proficiency (P = 0.042), as a basic ability for cross-cultural communication, plays a bridging role in cultural competence construction. Insufficient language proficiency not only causes mistranslation of key information but also limits the efficiency of capturing patients’ non-verbal cues. Communication delays relying on translation software often lead to missed critical response opportunities, and this language barrier systematically reduces the depth of cultural contact [25]. In qualitative interviews, P9 described the difficulty of using pictures to communicate with an Indian patient, reflecting the impact of language barriers on cultural contact quality. Skaria and Montayre’s mixed-methods study on nursing educators(2023, United States) [14] also found that foreign language ability was closely related to cultural intelligence and cross-cultural effectiveness.

Professional title reflecting professional development gradient: Professional title differences reflect the gradient characteristics of nursing staff’s professional development level, systematically influencing cultural competence through knowledge acquisition channels and practical experience accumulation. Nurses with higher professional titles usually have a more complete cultural knowledge system, can effectively transform multicultural contact experience into standardized cultural skills, and their cultural awareness also shows higher situational sensitivity. In qualitative interviews, head nurses P1, P10, P14, and P19 all demonstrated more systematic cultural care thinking and broader improvement perspectives, contrasting with junior nurses P18 and P22. Luukkonen et al.‘s study(2023, Finland) [26] also pointed out that mentors’ cultural competence significantly influences mentees’ ability development.

Strategies for improving ICU nursing staff’s cultural competence

Based on the above research findings, combined with suggestions from nursing staff in qualitative interviews, this study proposes the following improvement strategies:

First, construct a systematic ICU cross-cultural nursing curriculum system. In nursing education and in-service training systems, through systematic integration of multicultural nursing theory frameworks with ICU-specific clinical scenarios, carry out simulation training focusing on cultivating nursing staff’s cultural sensitivity and conflict mediation ability. In qualitative interviews, P11 and P15 both mentioned wanting scenario simulations and case sharing. At the same time, it is recommended to include cultural competence assessment in professional title evaluation criteria, set assessment dimensions such as cross-cultural case analysis and patient cultural needs satisfaction, and establish a long-term incentive mechanism to promote nursing service quality improvement(Liu et al., 2023, China) [27].

Second, integrate the concept of cultural humility into nursing staff’s professional development. Through systematic training, help nursing staff establish an open and inclusive cultural attitude and overcome self-culture-centered care stereotypes(Urgun et al., 2025, Turkey) [28]. Foreign language proficiency improvement should adopt a culturally immersive training model, combining language skills training with cultural sensitivity training, and can carry out cross-cultural doctor-patient communication analysis and bilingual situation simulation training based on film and television cases(Braun and Clarke, 2023, United Kingdom) [29]. According to ICU nursing characteristics, design short-term overseas clinical practice projects, select nursing staff to participate in practical work in multicultural medical institutions, improve language use cultural appropriateness in real clinical environments, and promote transformation and application of international experience to local practice.

Third, establish a tiered support system to promote sustainable development of cultural competence. Establish a special cultural competence cultivation fund to focus on supporting the professional growth of junior nursing staff. Through systematic training methods such as participating in international nursing academic conferences, joining cross-cultural nursing collaboration projects, and rotating training in multicultural nursing practice bases(Braun and Clarke, 2024, United Kingdom) [30], promote nursing staff to accumulate cross-cultural care experience in real clinical scenarios, and effectively improve their professionalism and clinical resilience. In qualitative interviews, P16 emphasized that “interest is the best teacher,” suggesting assessing nurses’ cultural care willingness before training and selecting those with willingness as key cultivation targets.

Conclusions and limitations

Research conclusions

This study investigated the current situation and influencing factors of cultural competence among ICU nursing staff in Shanghai using an explanatory sequential mixed-methods design. The main conclusions are as follows:
