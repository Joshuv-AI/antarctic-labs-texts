Drawbacks of antibiotic regimen following cesarean deliveries on beneficial bacteria in colostrum, a prospective observational study
Authors: Ahmed Bakr Mousa, Mazen Abdel-Rasheed, Raghda M.S. Moawad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe dramatic increase in Cesarean Section (CS) deliveries raises significant concerns due to risks such as unnecessary surgery, elevated healthcare costs, antibiotic overuse, and disruption of maternal breast milk microflora, which impacts infant gut microbiota.Objective and methodsThis study evaluates optimal antibiotic regimens for CS to preserve Lactobacilli and Bifidobacteria while ensuring aseptic wound conditions, thereby addressing antibiotic misuse and resistance. A cohort of 116 mothers received prophylactic antibiotics (third-generation Cephalosporins, Penicillin/β-lactam, or Linezolid) administered preoperatively and continued orally for 1, 3, or 5 days. Inclusion criteria comprised women with BMI 18–29 kg/m² undergoing elective CS for obstetric indications (malpresentation, placenta previa, recurrent CS). Colostrum samples were microbiologically analyzed using nutrient agar, De Man, Rogosa and Sharpe (MRS) agar, and Bile salt L-cysteine MRS (BL-MRS) agar to quantify total bacteria, Lactobacilli, and Bifidobacteria.ResultsCephalosporins caused the most substantial microbial reduction: 60.18% by day 5 and 59.2% by day 3. Linezolid for 5 days followed (48.7% reduction), while Penicillin/β-lactam for 3 and 5 days resulted in 33.93% and 36.9% reductions, respectively. Cephalosporins and Linezolid significantly reduced Bifidobacteria on days 3 and 5 (P < 0.05). Linezolid exhibited the most potent inhibition against Lactobacilli.ConclusionNo significant differences in wound healing outcomes were observed among antibiotic regimens (P = 0.916). Preoperative prophylaxis with Penicillin/β-lactam is recommended, as it minimally disrupts Lactobacilli and Bifidobacteria while maintaining aseptic conditions, offering a balanced approach to microbial preservation and infection prevention.

Introduction

The global rise in cesarean section (CS) rates is a pressing public health concern. In Egypt, data from the 2014 Demographic and Health Survey (EDHS) reveal that CS rates have surged by over 100% since 2005, accounting for 52% of all deliveries. Institutional-based CS rates in Egypt (67.3%) far exceed those of other Arab nations, such as Jordan and Saudi Arabia, positioning Egypt as the country with the third-highest CS rate globally, surpassed only by Brazil (55.6%) and the Dominican Republic (56.4%). Despite advances in surgical safety, peri- and postoperative complications remain significant, with early and late adverse outcomes becoming increasingly prevalent as CS rates climb [1].

Breastfeeding serves as a critical postnatal link between mother and newborn, fostering microbial colonization, immune system maturation, and metabolic programming essential for lifelong health [2]. Breast milk confers protection against infections (e.g., gastroenteritis, rotavirus, septicemia, urinary tract infections) and chronic conditions (e.g., allergies, obesity) in infants [3, 4]. Notably, breast milk contains probiotics and nutrients that shape the infant gut microbiome, particularly in cases of dysbiosis (e.g., preterm or CS-delivered infants) [5].

Colostrum, the nutrient-rich fluid secreted during the initial breastfeeding period (days 1–5 post-delivery), is enriched with immunomodulatory compounds, cytokines, and antimicrobial agents. It harbours a diverse bacterial flora (10³–10⁴ CFU/mL) and is a studious source of colonized bacteria for infants [6, 7]. Antibiotic consumption during pregnancy or breastfeeding can affect the number of probiotics. Probiotics transfer from mothers to their breastfed babies mainly during the first days of life (0–5 days). This supports the statement that early breast milk and colostrum have more lactic acid bacteria and Bifidobacterium. More than 200 bacterial taxa have been isolated from breast milk. Lactic acid bacteria like Lactobacillus, Leuconostoc, Lactococcus, some Bifidobacterium, and many others, including Streptococcus, Enterococcus, and Staphylococcus, were detected using traditional culture methods [3, 8].

Numerous studies have characterized the diversity of bacterial species in breast milk, even in aseptic samples. Specifically, Hermansson’s study isolated probiotic bacteria from breast milk samples of 47 healthy women, culturing specimens on selective media to identify distinct microbial groups [9]. Analysis of matched samples from 20 vaginally delivered, breastfed infants over three months post-birth revealed lower bacterial counts in mature milk compared to colostrum. Notably, colostrum exhibited higher concentrations of live Bifidobacterium and Lactobacilli—key taxa implicated in initial gut colonization—than mature milk [10, 11]. These findings underscore colostrum’s role as a primary source of probiotic transfer, shaping the infant gut microbiota during critical early developmental windows.

ACOG guidelines (2018) recommend administering broad-spectrum antibiotic prophylaxis within one hour before all Cesarean deliveries, unless equivalent coverage is already provided (e.g., for chorioamnionitis) [12]. However, antibiotic use during pregnancy and lactation may disrupt maternal and infant probiotic populations, with implications for microbial colonization and immune development. Prophylactic antibiotic regimens are distinct from therapeutic protocols for active infections [13] and remain contentious due to emerging antimicrobial resistance (AMR) risks. A UK Government-commissioned review projects AMR-related fatalities to reach 10 million annually by 2050, driven by multidrug-resistant strains proliferating in clinical settings [14, 15]. This has heightened scrutiny of antibiotic stewardship, particularly during lactation, as resistance genes and dysbiotic microbial shifts can transmit vertically via breast milk [16, 17].

This study evaluates the differential effects of Penicillin/β-lactam, third-generation Cephalosporins, and Linezolid on Lactobacilli and Bifidobacterium populations in colostrum during the first-day, third-day, and fifth-day post-Cesarean delivery. We hypothesize that these antibiotic classes show distinct inhibitory profiles against probiotic taxa, with implications for microbial preservation and surgical site infection prevention. Concurrently, we assess their clinical efficacy in wound healing and infection control to balance antimicrobial stewardship with best postpartum outcomes.

Patient and methods

Study population

This prospective study enrolled 116 women who underwent Cesarean deliveries at Minia Maternity and Children University Hospital, Minia University, Egypt, between January and December 2022.

All methods were carried out following relevant guidelines and regulations. All participants were informed about the study, and written consent was obtained from each participant. All participants received intraoperative antibiotic prophylaxis (1 g third-generation cephalosporin) within one hour before skin incision, followed by postoperative oral antibiotics. Cesarean sections were performed under standardized conditions by a single surgical team, with written informed consent obtained from all participants.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: Women aged 18–45 years with BMI 18–29 kg/m² undergoing elective CS for absolute obstetric indications (e.g., malpresentation, placenta previa, or earlier recurrent CS).

Exclusion criteria: Morbid obesity (BMI ≥ 40 kg/m² or ≥ 35 kg/m² with obesity-related comorbidities), third-trimester infections or hospitalizations, or pregnancy complicated by insulin-dependent diabetes, hypertensive disorders, or cardiac disease.

Experimental design

The participants were divided randomly into three main groups according to the type of antibiotic used (Cephalosporines third generation- Penicillin/sulbactam -Linezolid group). Each main group was subdivided into three subgroups according to duration and time of administration of antibiotics and, therefore, the time of collection of maternal breast milk samples. In the first subgroup, antibiotic prophylaxis was only used perioperatively, and breast milk samples were collected after one day. In the second subgroup, samples were collected on the third day, i.e., after three days of antibiotic intake, while in the last subgroup, samples were collected on the fifth day, i.e., after five days of antibiotic intake. The study depended on counting bacterial groups on selective media (MRS and B-LMRS), as well as taking an overall idea about the total bacterial count of the sample based on nutrient agar medium.

Sample collection

Breast milk was collected from mothers in specific sterilized tubes and then transferred into specific iceboxes to the microbiology laboratory in the Faculty of Agriculture, Minia University, to be analyzed. Each wound was checked at day 5 during follow-up for any sign of gapped wound or inflammation, which may need extra care. All patients who do CS came for follow-up and dressing 1 week after delivery at the outpatient clinic in MMUH, Faculty of Medicine, Minia University. The study design can be concluded in Fig. 1.

Microbiological analysis

One mL of each milk sample was serially diluted (101 to 105) in 0.1% sterile bacteriological peptone and water diluents. A pour plate technique was applied, and anaerobic jars were used to guarantee anaerobic conditions. Plates counting 30 to 300 colonies were recorded as colony-forming units (CFU) per mL of the milk sample. The total bacterial count was determined using a universal medium (nutrient agar medium) aerobically at 37 °C after 72 h [19]. Lactobacilli were enumerated on well-recommended selective medium De Man, Rogosa and Sharpe agar medium (MRS), plates were incubated at 37 °C for 72 h, and Bifidobacteria were enumerated on Bile salt L-cysteine De Man, Rogosa and Sharpe agar medium (BL-MRS) at 37 °C after 72 h [20]. The viable cell count was performed for each plate and was recorded as CFU/mL; the results were then calculated as log10 CFU/mL.

Statistical analysis

Each evaluation was carried out at least three times. Means and standard errors or standard deviations were calculated. Data were analyzed by one-way ANOVA analysis followed by the Tukey test for multiple comparisons as a post-test using GraphPad Prism 5 analysis software [21].

Reduction of beneficial bacterial count by tested antibiotic

Cephalosporins induced the most pronounced reductions in total bacterial counts: 60.18% (percentage decrease in bacterial count compared to day 1) by day 5 and 59.2% by day 3 (Fig. 2). Linezolid (5-day regimen) followed with a 48.7% reduction, while penicillin/β-lactam showed progressive declines (36.9% at 5 days, 33.93% at 3 days).

Lactobacilli were most suppressed by Linezolid (5-day: 48.7%; 3-day: 46.2%) and penicillin/β-lactam (5-day: 47.5%; 3-day: 35.5%), with minimal impact from cephalosporins (3-day: 6.6%). Bifidobacteria reductions were highest with cephalosporins (day 5: 84.86%; day 3: 76.5%) and linezolid (day 5: 83.7%; day 3: 82.2%), while penicillin/β-lactam had negligible effects (4.5% at 3 days).

Discussion

Effect of cephalosporines third generation, Penicillin/β-lactam or linezolid group on beneficial bacterial count

This study, involving 116 colostrum samples, showed that penicillin/β-lactam was associated with the highest Bifidobacteria counts in colostrum, while cephalosporins induced the most substantial microbial reductions: 60.18% and 59.2% by days 5 and 3, respectively. Linezolid (5-day regimen) followed with a 48.7% reduction, while penicillin/β-lactam showed progressively lower efficacy (36.9% at 5 days, 29.2% at 3 days). Notably, cephalosporins and Linezolid caused significant declines in Bifidobacteria over time (P < 0.05), with Linezolid showing the most potent inhibitory effect on Lactobacilli. Despite these microbial shifts, no significant differences in wound healing outcomes were observed among the antibiotic groups during the study period (P = 0.916), underscoring comparable clinical efficacy in infection prevention.

The protective role of breast milk in neonatal gut colonization is well-established, contributing 25–30% of gastrointestinal microbiota through its rich composition of glycoproteins, oligosaccharides, and probiotics. These components selectively promote Bifidobacteria growth, critical for immune and metabolic programming [3]. Our findings align with prior studies highlighting colostrum as a primary source of microbial inoculation, with bacterial loads (3.97–4.41 log₁₀ CFU/mL) mirroring Damaceno et al.’s reported range (1.5–4.0 log₁₀ CFU/mL) [10]. The narrower range observed here likely reflects our shorter evaluation window (5 days post-CS), emphasizing the need for longer-term studies to assess sustained microbial dynamics.

Hermansson et al. [9] previously linked CS deliveries and intrapartum antibiotics to altered milk microbiota, exacerbating neonatal dysbiosis. While Jost et al. focused on samples collected two months postpartum, our study bridges a critical gap by evaluating early postpartum changes and observed higher microbial loads in colostrum than in mature milk, with Streptococcus, Lactobacillus, and Bifidobacterium dominating early colonization. Our data corroborates that Lactobacilli and Bifidobacteria constitute ~ 10% and 4% of total breast milk bacteria, respectively [11].

The tension between antibiotic efficacy and microbiota preservation is clear: while cephalosporins and Linezolid effectively prevent infections, their pronounced suppression of beneficial taxa raises concerns for neonatal microbiome development. Penicillin/β-lactam appears as a balanced option, minimizing harm to Lactobacilli and Bifidobacteria without compromising wound outcomes. Future research should explore the longitudinal impacts of antibiotic regimens on infant health, including immune function and metabolic outcomes, to inform evidence-based guidelines for CS-associated prophylaxis.

Mechanism of action of the selected antibiotics and bacterial resistance phenomenon

Cephalosporins, a subclass of β-lactam antibiotics, inhibit bacterial cell wall synthesis by binding to penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs), disrupting peptidoglycan cross-linking. This mechanism targets both gram-positive and select gram-negative aerobic bacteria, though susceptibility varies across strains [22]. For instance, resistance to cefotetan was observed in 26% of tested strains, while Bifidobacterium longum and B. breve exhibited resistance to cefoxitin (MIC = 64 mg/L) [23]. Notably, no β-lactamase production was detected in Bifidobacterium strains, which may explain their pronounced sensitivity: in this study, cephalosporins reduced Bifidobacterium counts by 84.86% (3.26 ± 0.04 to 2.44 ± 0.05 log₁₀ CFU/mL) by day 5 (Table 2).

Cephalosporins demonstrated limited activity against Lactobacilli, with minor reductions of 6.6% and 7.8% observed on days 3 and 5, respectively. While cefotaxime showed broader inhibition zones (indicating lower MICs) than ceftriaxone and cefazolin, the molecular basis for Lactobacilli resistance remains poorly characterized [24]. This contrasts with their potent effects on Bifidobacterium, underscoring genus-specific differences in β-lactam susceptibility.

Amoxicillin-clavulanic acid, a widely used broad-spectrum β-lactam antibiotic, combines Amoxicillin (a penicillin derivative) with clavulanic acid (a β-lactamase inhibitor). Amoxicillin exerts bactericidal activity by inhibiting peptidoglycan biosynthesis, thereby disrupting cell wall integrity in both gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria. Clavulanic acid enhances this activity by irreversibly binding to β-lactamase enzymes, which are secreted by resistant bacterial strains to hydrolyze the β-lactam ring of penicillins. This inhibition restores Amoxicillin’s efficacy against β-lactamase-producing pathogens, expanding its therapeutic spectrum [25].

Studies report the sensitivity of Bifidobacterium strains to Amoxicillin, amoxicillin-clavulanic acid, and penicillin G at clinically relevant concentrations, underscoring the utility of this combination in targeting resistant infections [22]. However, its broad-spectrum activity necessitates cautious use to mitigate collateral damage to beneficial microbiota.

Contrary to conventional understanding, our findings indicate reduced susceptibility of Lactobacilli and Bifidobacteria to penicillin/β-lactam antibiotics, raising questions about potential resistance gene acquisition. This aligns with emerging evidence of resistance in these genera. For instance, Bifidobacterium animalis subsp. lactis, B. angulatum, and B. bifidum exhibit resistance to Oxacillin, a β-lactam antibiotic [26]. Similarly, ampicillin resistance has been documented in dairy-derived Lactobacillus spp [27, 28, 29]. and Bifidobacterium strains [30, 31].

Kusada et al. [32] propose that bile salt hydrolase (BSH) enzymes—critical for bile salt detoxification and gut colonization—may confer ancillary resistance to β-lactams. Their study identified a novel BSH (LpBSH) in Lactobacillus paragasseri JCM 5343T capable of degrading penicillin, suggesting a dual functional role in microbial survival. While plasmids are rare in Bifidobacterium, chromosomally encoded resistance genes have been identified in both Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium [33], though these do not always translate to phenotypic resistance [34].

The observed resistance patterns may reflect selective pressures from heightened antibiotic use during and after the COVID-19 pandemic, potentially driving mutations or horizontal gene transfer. However, the clinical relevance of these mechanisms warrants further investigation to distinguish intrinsic tolerance from acquired resistance.

Resistance to β-lactam antibiotics in Lactobacilli is primarily mediated through enzymatic hydrolysis via β-lactamases, including extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBLs) [23]. Although β-lactam resistance genes (blaZ) are relatively uncommon in this genus, in vitro studies demonstrate their potential horizontal transfer between Lactobacillus spp. and other gram-positive bacteria, including foodborne pathogens [24].

Linezolid, a first-generation oxazolidinone antibiotic, inhibits bacterial protein synthesis by binding to the 23 S ribosomal RNA of the 50 S subunit, thereby preventing the formation of the 70 S initiation complex. This mechanism effectively halts bacterial replication by blocking translation [35]. A recent study demonstrated significant reductions in Lactobacilli and Bifidobacterium counts by day 5 of linezolid administration. While antibiotics targeting gram-positive bacteria reportedly exert negligible effects on most Bifidobacterium strains [23], Linezolid exhibited potent inhibitory activity in this study. Notably, no linezolid resistance was observed among Bifidobacterium strains, with all strains exhibiting MIC₅₀ values ≤ 1 mg/L. However, susceptibility varied across species: B. breve displayed comparatively higher resistance, consistent with prior findings [22]. These findings align with our results, which demonstrate marked inhibition of Bifidobacterium populations by Linezolid, with counts declining to 2.54 ± 0.02 log₁₀ CFU/mL (day 3) and 2.50 ± 0.04 log₁₀ CFU/mL (day 5) post-administration.

Maternal obesity significantly alters the diversity and composition of the breast milk microbiome, reducing microbial richness and limiting the abundance of Bifidobacterium spp. Obese and overweight mothers exhibit a microbiome profile characterized by higher Staphylococcus and reduced Lactobacillus and Streptococcus populations compared to normal-weight mothers [36].

Intrapartum antibiotic exposure further disrupts this microbial balance, correlating with diminished Lactobacilli and Bifidobacteria levels in breast milk at one month postpartum. Additional factors exacerbating microbial depletion include antibiotic use during pregnancy or lactation and chemotherapy. Critically, antibiotic-resistance genes can transfer vertically via breast milk, exposing infants to both immediate dysbiosis and the legacy of prior maternal antibiotic use [9].

The study discusses not only the impact of antibiotics on colostrum bacteria but also the benefits of antibiotic usage to prevent surgical site infections after CS deliveries and, from this point of view, excluding many confounding factors that may affect healing and incidence of scar infection in the form of different surgeons, different surgical techniques or suture materials used.

Effect of tested antibiotics on wound healing

Open wounds provide an ideal environment for microbial colonization, though colonization does not invariably cause infection [37]. Globally, surgical site infections (SSIs) affect 0.5–15% of patients, primarily arising from perioperative bacterial contamination at the surgical site. SSI prevention reduces patient morbidity and alleviates associated socioeconomic burdens on families and healthcare systems. To restrict the risk of infection, modifiable risk factors in the preconception period, a sterile atmosphere, aseptic procedures should be addressed, hemostatic techniques utilized, and antimicrobial prophylaxis should be applied.

Implementing structured wound surveillance systems can significantly reduce surgical site infection rates, enhancing postoperative outcomes [38]. In East Asia, including China, Cesarean section (CS) rates surged from 13.4% (2000) to 28.8% (2015), marking one of the fastest increases globally (exceeding 15%) [39]. This trend underscores the urgency of developing nursing protocols tailored to post-Cesarean care to mitigate risks and optimize maternal health.

The widespread misuse of antibiotics, a primary driver of antimicrobial resistance (AMR), is not confined to developing nations but extends across both developing and developed countries, fostering dangerous dependencies at individual and systemic levels [40]. Addressing this requires stringent antibiotic stewardship—limiting unnecessary prescriptions and ensuring evidence-based usage—as an urgent global priority to curb AMR and preserve therapeutic efficacy.

Cephalosporins and Penicillins plus β-lactamase inhibitors may have the same impact on preventing mothers’ infections at cesarean sections. However, no obvious evidence existed for many important outcomes [41].

Current results suggest no statistical difference in wound infection between groups at the antibiotic type or duration level. Therefore, there is no need to apply antibiotics for 3 or 5 days postoperative, and prophylaxis antibiotics are enough in CS delivery.

Antibiotics were often used during postpartum periods, but use was highly variable by site and time in low- and middle-income countries. The antibiotics used varied substantially by time and site, but penicillin-type drugs, cephalosporin-type drugs, and metronidazole were used more frequently than other types [42].

A 2009 study in Eritrea evaluated postoperative antibiotic use among 39 patients, revealing that 26 patients (66.7%) received antibiotics for only one day, while just 6 patients (15.4%) completed a 5–7-day course. Notably, one-third of infected cases—including 11 clean surgical wounds and two non-clean wounds—did not receive guideline-recommended postoperative antibiotics [43]. Similarly, a study in Benin reported that 71.3% of patients were prescribed multiple antibiotics for 5–10 days post-surgery [44]. These findings underscore the urgent need for standardized antibiotic stewardship policies to curb overuse and misuse, which drive antimicrobial resistance (AMR) and pose critical public health risks.

Prophylactic antibiotic administration prior to surgery has significantly reduced postoperative infections, including endometritis, aligning with global recommendations for evidence-based perioperative care [44]. While the current study’s focus on beneficial bacteria (e.g., Lactobacilli, Bifidobacterium) limits broader genomic insights, it provides critical data on microbial groups essential for probiotic function and infant gut microbiota establishment.

The current study highlighted that Penicillin/β-lactam is considered the properly tested antibiotic that maintains probiotics and secures aseptic wounds.

Limitations faced by this study were a lack of genomic data, the short time duration of the study, the culture that not all mothers accepted to share in the study, following the patients, possible selection bias, short follow-up period, and a more in-depth exploration of the genetic and biochemical mechanisms involved in antibiotic resistance.

Conclusion

Penicillin/β-lactam emerged as the least disruptive antibiotic to Lactobacilli and Bifidobacteria—critical probiotics for infant gut colonization—while effectively preventing surgical site infections. Notably, no significant differences in wound healing outcomes were observed across antibiotic durations (P > 0.05), supporting short-course postpartum regimens. This approach balances maternal-infant microbial health with clinical efficacy, particularly in resource-limited settings where extended prophylactic courses are mandated.

Cephalosporins and Linezolid caused significant reductions in Bifidobacterium counts by days 3 and 5 (P < 0.05), with Linezolid showing the most potent inhibition of Lactobacilli. These findings advocate for antibiotic stewardship, prioritizing penicillin/β-lactam to preserve probiotic integrity. Beyond obstetrics, these insights may inform veterinary practices and dairy industries, guiding probiotic strain selection for microbial starter cultures.

Future studies should compare antibiotic impacts in vaginal versus Cesarean deliveries, explore longitudinal effects of prolonged exposure, and integrate genomic or biochemical analyses to elucidate strain-specific resistance mechanisms.
