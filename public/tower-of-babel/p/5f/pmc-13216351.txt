Migrasome-transported PTGES amplifies the PGE2 cascade in SPP1+ macrophages to drive oral leukoplakia carcinogenesis
Authors: Ming-Jing Jiang, Hao-Yu Zhou, Yu-Ting Bai, Xiao-Jie Chen, Gang Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOral leukoplakia (OLK) is a common oral potentially malignant disorder with a significant risk of transforming into oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC), yet the mechanisms remain poorly understood. Here we show that migrasomes, membranous organelles released by migrating cells, are detectable in epithelial cells of OLK and OSCC tissues and are more abundant in OSCC. Using carcinogenesis models, we transform human dysplastic oral keratinocyte (DOK) into oral carcinoma (DOK-TC) cells. Migrasomes derived from DOK-TC cells enhance interactions between DOK-TC cells and macrophages to promote carcinogenesis. Mechanistically, the uptake of prostaglandin E synthase (PTGES)-enriched migrasomes by macrophages increases PTGES expression and prostaglandin E2 secretion, which in turn induces an SPP1+ macrophage phenotype and promotes migration and proliferation. These findings uncover an unexplored migrasome-dependent immunomodulatory mechanism in OLK carcinogenesis and suggest migrasomal PTGES as a promising biomarker for early OSCC detection and a potential therapeutic target.Subject terms: Tumour heterogeneity, Oral cancer

Introduction

Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) is a prevalent malignancy originating from the oral mucosal epithelium, with over 380,000 new cases reported globally in 2022. Despite advances in treatment, the prognosis remains poor, primarily due to frequent late-stage diagnoses, underscoring the urgent need to investigate the early events involved in oral carcinogenesis1.

Oral leukoplakia (OLK), the most common oral potentially malignant disorder, with a global prevalence of 4.11%, serves as a precancerous lesion that can transform into OSCC2–4. A hallmark of OLK carcinogenesis is the formation of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, which develops during the precancerous stage and is characterized by the infiltration of protumor macrophages, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and regulatory T cells5,6. Among these, protumor macrophages are the predominant cells in the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, correlating with T-cell exhaustion, dysfunction of cytotoxic T lymphocytes, and the increased abundance of Tregs in higher dysplasia grades. These factors significantly contribute to carcinogenesis5,7,8. Nevertheless, the molecular and immunological mechanisms underlying the progression of OLK to OSCC remain incompletely understood9.

Advances in single-cell RNA sequencing technologies have revealed that protumor macrophage phenotypes within the tumor microenvironment are highly heterogeneous. Notably, specific subpopulations such as secreted phosphoprotein 1-positive (SPP1+) macrophages, which promote carcinoma progression by secreting TNF-α and IL-1β10, and indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1-positive (IDO1+) macrophages, which contribute to CD8+ T cell exhaustion5, have been identified. However, the key macrophage subsets driving the malignant transformation of OLK remain poorly characterized.

Emerging evidence suggests that intercellular interactions between epithelial-derived tumor cells and macrophages contribute to the formation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment and drive carcinogenesis. For instance, IL-4 from ovarian cancer cell11, and hepatocellular carcinoma-derived erythropoietin have been shown to reprogram macrophages to support immune evasion and tumor growth12. These interactions can occur via direct contact (e.g., adhesion molecules, gap junctions) or through indirect contact (e.g., cytokines, extracellular vesicles)13–15.

Migrasomes are newly recognized membranous organelles generated by retraction fibers during cell migration16. Prior to detachment, they function as secretion hubs for autonomous cellular processes17, and after detachment, migrasomes act as extracellular vesicles capable of transporting signaling molecules between cells17,18. Previous studies have demonstrated that migrasomes promote embryonic angiogenesis through the enrichment of vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA) and CXCL1219,20, regulate macrophage polarization through the secretion of milk fat globule-EGF factor 8 (MFGE8)21, and promote tumor progression in pancreatic cancer via the release of the cytokine TGF-β122. Migrasomes are increasingly recognized as potential contributors to the remodeling of the immune microenvironment. However, their role in precancerous immune reprogramming remains largely unexplored.

The progression from dysplasia to invasive oral cancer is histopathologically defined by the invasion of islands and cords of malignant epithelial cells, originating from the dysplastic epithelia, through the basement membrane into the lamina propria23. Based on the fact that migrasomes originate from migrating cells24, and the innate and pathologically enhanced migratory capacity of dysplastic epithelial cells in OLK carcinogenesis23, we hypothesized that the dynamic interactions between migrating dysplastic cells and immune components, particularly macrophages, in OLK carcinogenesis may be orchestrated, at least in part, by migrasome-mediated signaling.

This study identified elevated migrasome levels in OSCC compared to OLK. To clarify the role of migrasomes in enhanced epithelial cell–macrophage crosstalk during carcinogenesis, the DOK cells were transformed into oral carcinoma cells, including DOK-BD and DOK-4NQO, via carcinogenesis models. Migrasomes enriched in prostaglandin E synthase (PTGES) derived from DOK-4NQO and MOC1 cells were taken up by macrophages, leading to increased PTGES expression and prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) secretion. This in turn induced the SPP1+ macrophage phenotype and facilitated migration and proliferation, promoting carcinogenesis. Our findings uncover an unexplored mechanism driving OLK carcinogenesis and suggest migrasomal PTGES as a promising target for early screening and diagnosis of OSCC.

Results

Single-cell transcriptomic analysis revealed cellular heterogeneity and migrasome score changes during OLK carcinogenesis

To investigate cellular heterogeneity during OLK carcinogenesis, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing on three OLK and seven OSCC samples. After quality control, doublet removal, and batch integration, transcriptomic data from a total of 46,909 cells (26,100 from OLK and 20,809 from OSCC) were retained for downstream analysis (Fig. 1a). Dimensionality reduction using UMAP revealed 15 distinct clusters (Supplementary Fig. 1a), which were further categorized into nine major cell types based on hierarchical clustering and lineage-specific markers (Fig. 1b,c and Supplementary Fig. 1b). Immune cells were distributed across clusters 1, 3, 5, 7, 8, and 9, and were subclassified according to canonical gene signatures. T cells (cluster 1) exhibited high expression of T-lineage genes such as CD8A, CD8B, GZMH, CD3E, and CD7. Myeloid cells (cluster 3) were characterized by elevated expression of LYZ, FCN1, and macrophage-associated markers, including CD163, C1QA, C1QB, and C1QC. Plasma cells (cluster 5) expressed immunoglobulin-related genes such as IGHG1, IGLL5, and IGHM. B cells (cluster 7) showed enrichment of CD19, MS4A1, and BANK1. Mast cells (cluster 8) expressed HDC and MS4A2, while dendritic cells (cluster 9) were marked by FLT3, CCL19, and CCL22. Epithelial cells were mainly localized to cluster 2 and expressed markers such as DSP, COL17A1, and CSTA. Fibroblasts in cluster 4 showed enrichment for COL1A1, PRELP, and CLIP, and endothelial cells in cluster 6 were defined by expression of VWF and CDH5 (Fig. 1d).

We next analyzed the distributional changes in these cell types across individual samples during progression from OLK to OSCC. A global increase was observed in the proportions of dendritic cells (~9.6-fold), B cells (~7.0-fold), myeloid cells (~6.9-fold), T cells (~1.9-fold), and plasma cells (~1.1-fold) (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 1c). Cell type-specific comparisons also revealed substantial increases in immune subsets including dendritic cells, B cells, myeloid cells, and T cells (Supplementary Fig. 1d). Based on published literature, we constructed a gene set associated with migrasome activity25–28. After Harmony-based batch correction, migrasome scores were computed for each cell using the AUCell algorithm (Fig. 1f). Statistically significant changes in migrasome scores were observed across most cell types during disease progression, with increased scores detected in epithelial cells (p = 3.13 × 10⁻59), myeloid cells (p = 2.59 × 10⁻31), mast cells (p = 0.0003), and endothelial cells (p = 2.50 × 10⁻103), and decreased scores in plasma cells (p = 1.29 × 10⁻35), T cells (1.28 × 10⁻5), and B cells (p = 3.58 × 10⁻6), while dendritic cells showed no significant change (p = 0.6591), and fibroblasts (p = 0.0564) (Fig. 1g). Given that epithelial cells are central to OSCC development and that they displayed profound and statistically significant alterations in migrasome activity during the OLK to OSCC transition, we subsequently focused our investigation on the role of epithelial cell-derived migrasomes in malignant progression.

Identification of migrasomes in OLK and OSCC

Human OLK and OSCC tissue sections were examined for the presence of migrasomes in epithelial cells via TEM. In OLK tissues, retraction fibers-like structures (red arrow) extended from epithelial cells near the basement membrane, terminating in vesicles with intravascular vesicles (yellow arrow) (Fig. 2a). OSCC tissues displayed longer retraction fibers ending in vesicles of approximately 500 nm diameter (Fig. 2b). The morphology and size range of these structures matched reported migrasomes16. To confirm these findings, multiplex immunofluorescence staining was performed, which revealed abundant WGA+PIGK+ vesicles (yellow arrows) of approximately 2–3 µm diameter surrounding epithelial cells at the epithelia-lamina propria junction (Fig. 2c). Similarly, OSCC cells were surrounded by 2 µm vesicles exhibiting strong PIGK and WGA colocalization (Fig. 2d). These results confirm the presence of migrasomes in both OLK and OSCC tissues.

In virto, to simulate the transformation of dysplastic epithelial cells into cancer cells during the OLK carcinogenesis, carcinogen B[a]P/DMBA and 4NQO were used to transform DOK cells into DOK-TC cells, including DOK-BD and DOK-4NQO cell29–31. Primary assessment confirmed the drug’s safety (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). The malignant potential of DOK-TC cells was confirmed in vitro and in vivo experiments. Compared to DOK cells, DOK-TC cells exhibited weakened growth inhibition, increased nuclear-cytoplasmic ratio (Supplementary Fig. 2c, d), more spike-like protrusions (Supplementary Fig. 2e). Enhanced migration were observed in DOK-4NQO cells compared to DOK-BD cells (Supplementary Fig. 2f, g). Compared with DOK cells, DOK-TC cells exhibited reduced G0/G1 phase occupancy with concurrent G2/M phase accumulation (Supplementary Fig. 2h, i), and DOK-TC cells exhibited enhanced proliferative capacity (Supplementary Fig. 2j). Critically, nude mice injected with DOK-TC cells achieved a 100% tumor formation rate in vivo xenotransplantation experiments (Supplementary Fig. 3a, d), appeared with larger tumor volume and weight (Supplementary Fig. 3b, e), and displayed typical squamous cell carcinoma structures (Supplementary Fig. 3c, f). Collectively, these results demonstrate that carcinogenic drugs induced the malignant transformation of DOK cells, establishing the DOK-TC cells for subsequent research.

Further WGA staining revealed that normal oral epithelial cells HOEC produced fewer migrasomes than DOK cells, transformed DOK-4NQO and DOK-BD cells, or OSCC cells. Furthermore, the SCC4, SCC9, and DOK-4NQO lines generated an even greater number of migrasomes compared to the precancerous DOK cells. SCC4 cells exhibited a significant advantage in migrasome production over all other cells examined (Fig. 2e, f and Supplementary Table 2). Additionally, time-lapse imaging captured the dynamic processes of migrasome generation and detachment from retraction fibers in DOK-GFP cells (Fig. 2g). The results of scanning electron microscopy showed that many retraction fibers with long tubular structures were clearly visible at the tail of the cells, and vesicle-like structures were produced on the tips and intersections of the retraction fibers (Fig. 2h,i and Supplementary Fig. 4a). The above results confirm the presence of migrasomes in epithelial cells during OLK carcinogenesis.

Identification of the morphological and molecular characteristics of migrasomes

The typical characteristic of migrasomes is that they contain different numbers of vesicles and are shaped like an open pomegranate, as described previously16. To confirm this, we isolated migrasomes and exosomes from DOK, SCC4, and MOC1 cells by referring to established methods19,32, and exosomes were used as an extracellular vesicle control (Fig. 3a). Purified migrasomes were phospholipid bilayer organelles with diameters of 0.3 to 3 µm and some purified migrasomes were still attached to retraction fibers, and exosomes were around 100 nm in diameter observed by TEM (Fig. 3b, f). TEM imaging of ultra-thin sections demonstrated that migrasomes from DOK and MOC1 cells displayed a distinct pomegranate-like architecture averaging 500 nm in diameter and contained numerous smaller vesicles of 50–100 nm (Fig. 3d, h and Supplementary Fig. 4b). Western blot analysis detected calnexin exclusively in cell body fractions, confirming the absence of cellular contamination in migrasome and exosome preparations. Migrasome markers (ITGαV, EOGT, PIGK, NDST1, TSPAN4), found in previous findings33–35, showed varying expression in migrasomes but were absent in exosomes, confirming their specificity for distinguishing migrasomes from other vesicles like exosomes (Fig. 3c, e, g). While these results confirm the presence and characteristics of epithelial migrasomes in OLK and OSCC, their specific roles in OLK carcinogenesis remain to be elucidated.

Single-cell transcriptomic analysis found enhanced epithelial cells– macrophages interactions in OSCC

Immunosuppression is a hallmark of OLK carcinogenesis. Interactions between epithelial cells and immune cell populations play a pivotal role in inducing an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment to promote cancer development11,12. To explore this, we performed CellChat analysis and compared intercellular communication between OLK and OSCC groups. We observed a marked increase in interaction numbers between epithelial cells and both T cells and myeloid cells in the OSCC group (Fig. 4a, b and Supplementary Fig. 5a). Given that myeloid cells exhibited the most prominent proportional increase during the transition from OLK to OSCC (Fig. 1e), we next performed a detailed subcluster analysis of the myeloid compartment. After excluding contaminating clusters, four distinct myeloid subpopulations (Myeloid_0/1/3/5) were identified (Fig. 4c).

Based on canonical marker gene expression, Myeloid_0 was annotated as resident macrophages, characterized by high expression of complement components (C1QA, C1QB, C1QC), APOE, and MMP12, which are well-established markers of differentiated tissue macrophages. Myeloid_1 represented inflammatory macrophages, expressing NLRP3, IL6, CCL20, and TIMP1, consistent with an activated pro-inflammatory phenotype36. In contrast, Myeloid_3 corresponded to classical monocytes, as evidenced by enrichment for FCN1 and VCAN, canonical markers of circulating inflammatory monocytes. Myeloid_5 was identified as neutrophils based on the expression of CSF3R and FCGR3B37.

We then assessed the top 10 marker genes for each myeloid subcluster and compared their expression between OLK and OSCC (Fig. 4d). CellChat analysis showed that epithelial interactions with Myeloid_0 and Myeloid_3 were increased in cancerous tissues (Fig. 4e, f). Given that Myeloid_0 constituted the most abundant subpopulation (Fig. 4c), we further investigated potential interactions between epithelial and this subset. Notably, SPP1 was found to be highly contained in Myeloid_0 compared to other myeloid subclusters (Fig. 4g). Previous studies have identified SPP1 as a signature marker of pro-tumorigenic macrophages in emerging classification systems38,39. Moreover, differential expression analysis revealed significant upregulation of SPP1 in OSCC compared to OLK (Fig. 4h). Comparative assessment of myeloid subcluster proportions further demonstrated a pronounced expansion of the SPP1+ Myeloid_0 population in OSCC (53.34% vs 31.85% in OLK), representing the most substantial compositional shift among myeloid subsets. (Supplementary Fig. 5b).

The above findings indicate that SPP1+ Myeloid_0 is the principal myeloid population engaging epithelia in OSCC. We therefore examined macrophage infiltration and SPP1 expression by immunohistochemistry. Compared to normal mucosa and mild-to-moderate dysplasia OLK tissues, OLK with severe dysplasia and OSCC tissues exhibited increased macrophage infiltration with a consistent upregulation in the expression of SPP1 (Fig. 4i). Compared with normal tissues, the migrasome marker N-deacetylase and N-sulfotransferase 1 (NDST1) was upregulated only in grade I-II OSCC tissues (Supplementary Fig. 6a, b). To investigate whether migrasomes mediate epithelial regulation of macrophages, we treated macrophages with migrasomes isolated from DOK, DOK-BD, and DOK-4NQO cells and confirmed their uptake (Fig. 4j and Supplementary Fig. 6c).

Migrasome-mediated crosstalk between DOK-TC cells and macrophages promoted OLK carcinogenesis

Macrophages derived from THP-1 cells were treated with migrasomes from DOK-BD cells (DOK-BD-Mig), or DOK-4NQO cells (DOK-4NQO-mig) at varying concentrations for different durations, and the changes in mRNA levels of SPP1, and the classical protumor macrophages markers CD163 and arginase-1 (Arg-1), were examined. The results demonstrated that 5, 10, and 20 μg/mL migrasomes all induced SPP1 expression in macrophages at 12 h. Among these, the inductive effect of 10 μg/mL migrasomes was more pronounced than that of 5 μg/mL, while no significant difference was observed between the 10 μg/mL and 20 μg/mL groups (Supplementary Fig. 7a, d). Additionally, 10 μg/mL migrasomes induced Arg-1 expression in macrophages at both 12 h and 24 h (Supplementary Fig. 7b, e). Although 20 µg/ml migrasomes from DOK-BD cells promoted the expression of CD163, respectively, the fold changes are relatively low (Supplementary Fig. 7c, f). Furthermore, 10 µg/mL migrasomes derived from DOK-TC cells upregulated the SPP1 and Arg-1 protein levels in macrophages simultaneously (Fig. 5a), and promoted the recruitment of macrophages (Fig. 5b, c).

Next, 10 μg/mL migrasomes were selected for further investigation using the procedure outlined in Fig. 5d to verify their involvement in the DOK-TC cell-macrophage interaction (Fig. 5d). Colony formation assays found that macrophages treated by migrasomes of DOK-TC cells significantly enhanced proliferation of DOK-TC cells (Fig. 5e-g). Compared with migrasomes derived from DOK cells, migrasomes derived from DOK-4NQO cells exhibit a stronger migration and invasion-inducing ability on DOK-4NQO cells by educating macrophages (Fig. 5h–k). Macrophages treated with migrasomes derived from DOK-BD cells can induce the migration of DOK-BD cells, while this phenomenon was not observed in the group treated with migrasomes derived from DOK cells (Fig. 5l, m). In vivo, intratumoral injection of DOK-4NQO-derived migrasomes markedly accelerated xenograft tumor growth in nude mice (Fig. 5n, o), resulted larger tumor volume and weight (Fig. 5p). Ki67 and proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) staining showed that migrasome treatment enhanced tumor proliferation (Fig. 5q, r). These results demonstrate that DOK-TC cell-derived migrasomes drive OLK carcinogenesis by mediating interaction between DOK-TC cells and macrophages.

Quantitative proteomics characterized the proteome of migrasomes derived from DOK-TC cells

As shown in Fig. 4j and Supplementary Fig. 6c, migrasomes can be phagocytosed by neighboring cells16,40, which serves as an important mechanism for the lateral transfer of cellular contents, such as chemokines, growth factors, and mRNA19,20,34. To investigate whether proteins or mRNAs are the primary regulators of macrophage responses mediated by migrasomes, we followed the approach described by Zhu et al.34. DOK-BD-Mig and DOK-4NQO-Mig were pretreated with RNase A to degrade mRNA or proteinase K to degrade proteins, followed by co-culture with macrophages. Proteinase K pretreatment significantly attenuated both Arg-1 induction (Fig. 6a, b), and the increase in SPP1⁺ macrophages (Fig. 6c and Supplementary Fig. 8a–d), suggesting that migrasomal proteins, rather than RNA cargo, mediate the regulatory effects on macrophages.

To further characterize the protein cargo of migrasomes, 4D label-free quantitative proteomics was performed to compare migrasomes derived from DOK cells with those from DOK-TC cells. Differentially expressed proteins were identified by comparing DOK-Mig with DOK-BD-Mig or DOK-4NQO-Mig. Principal component analysis demonstrated a clear separation between DOK-Mig and DOK-BD-Mig or DOK-4NQO-Mig (Fig. 6d). Heatmaps illustrated the expression patterns of these differentially expressed proteins (Fig. 6e, f). Volcano plots further highlighted significant changes, revealing 107 upregulated and 363 downregulated proteins in DOK-4NQO-Mig, and 211 upregulated and 454 downregulated proteins in DOK-BD-Mig, based on a threshold of fold change >1.5 and adjusted p value (adj.p-Value) < 0.05. The top 10 upregulated and downregulated proteins in each group were labeled according to fold change (Fig. 6g, h). Intersection analysis of the top 10 upregulated proteins from DOK-4NQO-Mig and DOK-BD-Mig revealed four common proteins, RPEL1, PTGES, DPM3, and H2AX (Fig. 6i). Given that PTGES is a key synthase involved in PGE2 biosynthesis within the arachidonic acid metabolism and expressed in multiple types of cancers, including colorectal cancer, breast cancer, and leukemia, and promotes tumor growth and immunosuppression via the PGE2 receptors EP2 and EP441–43, we selected PTGES to further investigate its role in the migrasome-mediated interaction between DOK-TC cells and macrophages.

Migrasome-transported PTGES triggered macrophage PGE2 cascade to mediate intercellular crosstalk

To validate PTGES identified through migrasome proteomics, immunofluorescence assays were performed. The results showed a significant enrichment of PTGES in the migrasomes of DOK-4NQO cells (Fig. 7a). Western blot analysis was conducted to determine the protein expression levels of PTGES in cell bodies, migrasomes, and exosomes, respectively. The results showed that PTGES expression was comparable between DOK and DOK-4NQO but decreased in DOK-BD cell body (Fig. 7b). In contrast to exosomes, PTGES was exclusively detected in migrasomes derived from DOK-TC and MOC1 cells, but not in migrasomes from DOK cells (Fig. 7c).

We proceeded to establish stable PTGES overexpression and knockdown models in DOK or DOK-4NQO cells for further investigation. The results demonstrated that the overexpression level of PTGES in DOK cells exceeded 2-fold (Fig. 7d and Supplementary Fig. 9a). In DOK-4NQO cells, the knockdown efficiency of shPTGES#3 was the highest, resulting in more than a tenfold reduction compared with the control (Fig. 7d, e). PTGES overexpression in DOK cells increased migrasomal PTGES levels, while PTGES knockdown in DOK-4NQO cells decreased its levels in migrasomes (Fig. 7f). Migrasomes isolated from shRNA control-transfected (shCtrl-Mig) and shPTGES#3-transfected (shPTGES#3-Mig) DOK-4NQO cells were used for subsequent treatment of THP-1 cell-derived macrophages. Compared to shCtrl-Mig, treatment with shPTGES#3-Mig downregulated the levels of SPP1, PTGES, and Arg-1 in macrophages (Fig. 7g), and reduced PGE2 secretion (Fig. 7h). It was reported that PGE2 exacerbated the immunosuppressive microenvironment43. To investigate whether migrasome-delivered PTGES induces macrophage phenotypic changes by promoting PGE2 signaling, we treated macrophages with PGE2. Strikingly, the addition of 20 µM PGE2 was sufficient to upregulate the expression of PTGES, SPP1, and Arg-1, which confirmed our hypothesis (Fig. 7i).

Celecoxib, a selective cyclooxygenase-2 inhibitor, could attenuate the conversion of arachidonic acid to prostaglandin H2, thereby decreasing the subsequent PTGES-mediated conversion of prostaglandin H2 to PGE244. Celecoxib was used to confirm the role of the migrasome-mediated PTGES/PGE2 pathway. The CCK-8 assay demonstrated that celecoxib at concentrations below 50 µM had no impact on macrophage viability (Fig. 7j). We selected 12.5 µM and 25 µM celecoxib for further experiments. As expected, migrasomes from PTGES-overexpressing DOK cells (PTGES-OE-Mig) prompted PGE2 secretion (Fig. 7k) and upregulated SPP1, PTGES, and Arg-1 levels in macrophages (Fig. 7l). However, pretreatment of macrophages with 25 µM celecoxib effectively abolished both the PGE2 secretion (Fig. 7k), and the induction of SPP1 triggered by PTGES-OE-Mig (Fig. 7l).

Moreover, THP-1 cell-derived macrophages subjected to shPTGES#3-Mig showed a decreased ability to induce the migration and invasion of DOK-4NQO cells, and this reduction was reversed by the addition of 20 µM exogenous PGE2 (Fig. 7m,n). Furthermore, macrophages treated with shPTGES#3-Mig exhibited a diminished capacity to induce the proliferation of DOK-4NQO cells, and this impairment was also restored by 20 µM PGE2 (Fig. 7o). Conversely, PTGES-OE-Mig treatment strengthened the capacity of macrophages to induce the migration and invasion of DOK cells, whereas pretreatment of macrophages with 25 µM celecoxib attenuated this inductive effect (Fig. 7p, q). PTGES-OE-Mig also augmented the ability of macrophages to promote the proliferation of DOK cells, and this inductive capacity was inhibited when macrophages were pretreated with 25 µM celecoxib (Fig. 7r).

Migrasome-transported PTGES amplifies the PGE2 cascade in macrophage to promote OLK carcinogenesis

Primary BMDMs and the mouse OSCC cell line MOC1 were used to further validate that migrasomal PTGES/PGE2 signaling in macrophages promotes carcinogenesis. MOC1 cell migrasomes are enriched in PTGES (Fig. 8a), and can be internalized by BMDMs (Fig. 8b). 5 μg/mL or 10 µg/mL migrasomes derived from MOC1 cells (MOC1-Mig) significantly promoted the expression of PTGES, SPP1, and Arg1 in BMDMs (Fig. 8c). We proceeded to establish stable PTGES knockdown models in MOC1 cells. The shPTGES#3 construct was most effective, reducing PTGES levels by roughly threefold in MOC1 cells (Fig. 8d and Supplementary Fig. 9b), and it also markedly decreased PTGES abundance in the released migrasomes (Fig. 8e). Notably, compared to migrasomes from knockdown control MOC1 cells (shCtrl-Mig), exposure to migrasomes derived from shPTGES#3-transfected MCO1 cells (shPTGES#3-Mig) suppressed PTGES expression in treated BMDMs (Fig. 8f), and decreased PGE2 secretion (Fig. 8g). Adding PGE2 to BMDMs promoted the expression of PTGES, SPP1 and Arg-1 (Fig. 8h). BMDMs treated with PTGES-knocked-down migrasomes exhibited reduced ability to induce proliferation of MOC1 cell, which could be corrected by 20 µM PGE2 (Fig. 8i, j). Moreover, BMDMs subjected to migrasomes with PTGES knockdown showed a decreased ability to induce the migration of MOC1 cells, and this reduction was reversed by the addition of 20 µM exogenous PGE2 (Fig. 8k, l).

In vivo, the mouse OLK or local early OSCC model was established by feeding drinking water containing 100 µg/ml 4NQO for 16 consecutive weeks. After treatment with PBS, shCtrl-Mig, or shPTGES#3-Mig, mice were euthanized to observe the number of tongue lesions (Fig. 8m). Multiple immunofluorescences demonstrated the uptake of migrasomes by macrophages in vivo (Fig. 8n). The number of tongues lesions in shPTGES#3-Mig groups was less than that in the shCtrl-Mig group (Fig. 8o, p). Microscopic observation of hematoxylin-eosin staining showed that 25% of mice in the PBS control group had invasive carcinoma lesions (2 of 8). In the shCtrl-Mig group, 50% of mice had invasive carcinoma lesions (4 of 8), with fewer cases of mild-to-moderate dysplasia (early lesions). In the shPTGES#3-Mig group, 28.57% of mice had invasive carcinoma lesions (2 out of 7) (Fig. 8q, r). Of note, shCtrl-Mig injection promoted F4/80+SPP1+ macrophage infiltration, whereas shPTGES#3-Mig attenuated this induction (Fig. 8s). These results demonstrate that PTGES transported by migrasomes amplifies the PGE2 signaling cascade in macrophages, thereby promoting OLK carcinogenesis.

Discussion

Migrasomes, newly identified organelles generated by migrating cells, serve as critical carriers of bioactive signals that mediate intercellular communication24. Our study identified migrasome as a critical mediator of enhanced interactions between epithelial cells and macrophages that promoted OLK carcinogenesis. Mechanistically, upon uptake by macrophages, migrasomes enriched with PTGES upregulated PTGES expression and PGE2 secretion in macrophages. PGE2, in turn, induced an SPP1+ macrophage phenotype and facilitated migration and proliferation. These findings revealed an innovative migrasome-dependent immunomodulatory mechanism in oral carcinogenesis.

The carcinogenic progression of OLK to OSCC is defined by the invasion of dysplastic epithelial cells through the basement membrane into the lamina propria23. This pivotal step highlights the pathologically enhanced migratory capacity of dysplastic epithelial cells. Given that migrasome biogenesis is an intrinsic process of migrating cells16, and that single-cell data showed a consistently higher migrasome score in OSCC than in OLK epithelial cells, we examined clinical specimens and confirmed migrasomes surrounding epithelial cells in both OLK and OSCC. These observations support a role for migrasomes in advancing lesions from a precancerous to a malignant state.

In vitro model using DOK cells and their carcinogen-transformed derivatives allowed us to systematically trace changes in migrasome formation and cargo composition during malignant transformation. Migrasome abundance increased with malignant progression: highest in malignant SCC4 cells, intermediate in carcinogen-transformed DOK-4NQO cells (higher than DOK), and lowest in normal epithelial cells. This gradient aligns with reports that active cell migration and actin dynamics are essential for migrasome biogenesis16. Therefore, it can be speculated that the increase in migrasomes during malignant transformation is not merely a concomitant phenomenon of enhanced migratory phenotype, but may also reflect an augmented role of migrasomes in intercellular signaling and tumor microenvironment regulation.

Interestingly, molecular profiling of purified migrasomes showed that migrasomes derived from both DOK and SCC4 cells consistently expressed ITGαV, PIGK, and EOGT, while the expression of TSPAN4 varied markedly. Specifically, TSPAN4 was detectable in migrasomes from DOK and MOC1 cells but absent in those from SCC4 cells. Considering the functional compensation among tetraspanin (TSPAN) family members, such as TSPAN720, TSPAN945, and TSPAN2427, which can each independently promote migrasome formation, we hypothesized that SCC4 cells might compensate for the loss of TSPAN4 through other TSPAN members. Our supplementary experiments confirmed the presence of TSPAN7 and TSPAN9 in SCC4-derived migrasomes (Supplementary Fig. 4c), supporting this compensatory hypothesis and underscoring the molecular diversity and plasticity of migrasome formation.

Functionally, migrasomes from DOK-4NQO cells delivered PTGES to macrophages, thereby amplifying the PTGES/PGE2 cascade in macrophages. The resulting rise in PGE2 induced SPP1+ macrophages and increased DOK-4NQO cell migration and proliferation, ultimately driving OLK carcinogenesis in vivo. Treatment with the selective cyclooxygenase-2 inhibitor, celecoxib44, effectively blocked the migrasome-activated PTGES/PGE2 pathway, significantly reducing PGE2 production in macrophages and suppressing their migration and proliferation. This finding not only uncovers a mechanism by which migrasomes mediate intercellular signaling within the tumor microenvironment but also provides a potential strategy for pharmacologically modulating migrasome signaling to delay malignant transformation.

Protumor macrophages have traditionally been classified as M1 or M2, while this binary model is now recognized as overly simplistic46. Recent evidence suggests that macrophage polarization in tumors is better defined by distinct functional subsets, such as TREM2+SPP1+ and FOLR2+ macrophages38,39. In head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, the CXCL9:SPP1 axis has emerged as a key determinant of clinical outcomes, with an abundance of SPP1+ macrophages strongly associated with poor prognosis47. In this study, migrasome-transferred PTGES can induce PGE2 secretion to promote SPP1 expression in macrophages, uncovers a previously unrecognized role of migrasomes in orchestrating the polarization of SPP1+ macrophages.

Although this study delineates an immunoregulatory mechanism by which migrasomes drive OLK malignant transformation, important mechanistic and translational questions remain. Methodologically, we used THP-1–derived macrophage-like cells and murine BMDMs as scalable systems for mechanistic dissection and supported relevance using human OLK/OSCC specimens and in vivo models. Given that macrophage programs in premalignant lesions and tumors are shaped by tissue microenvironmental, future low-input 3D/organotypic and spatial approaches may enable functional assays in primary monocyte-derived and lesion-resident macrophages, as the limited tissue volume of clinically obtained OLK incisional or excisional biopsy specimens can constrain cell yield for downstream functional assays48. In addition, the absence of migrasome-specific markers hinders high-purity isolation from solid tissues, while reliable in vivo imaging techniques require further development49. Biologically, the triggers of excessive migrasome formation in premalignant cells and the principles governing selective cargo sorting, such as how key effectors like PTGES are preferentially packaged, remain unclear. Furthermore, while the large size of migrasomes suggests potential for drug delivery, challenges regarding their stability and efficient in vivo delivery need to be addressed. Addressing these methodological and biological gaps will be essential for harnessing the therapeutic potential of migrasomes.

The high prevalence and malignant potential of OLK underscore the urgent need for better early detection methods4. Effective strategies for early risk stratification are lacking due to the absence of reliable biomarkers to predict malignant transformation of OLK and the limited accuracy of non-invasive screening methods48. Our finding that migrasome-derived PTGES drives OLK carcinogenesis positions it as a promising biomarker for risk stratification and a therapeutic target, paving the way for shifting OSCC management from late-stage treatment to early prevention.

Methods

Antibodies and reagents

A list of all antibodies and reagents utilized in this study can be found in the Supplementary Data 1 and 2.

Cell lines culture

THP-1 (human monocyte cell from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd., China) was maintained in RPMI 1640 medium. Macrophages were differentiated from THP-1 cells using 100 ng/mL phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) for 48 h. OLK cell line DOK was a gift from Prof. Hong-Mei Zhou (West China Hospital of Stomatology, Sichuan University). MOC1 (mouse OSCC Cell Line obtained from Kerafast) was cultured in IMDM/F12 (2:1) with 5 μg/mL insulin, 0.04 μg/mL Hydrocortisone, and 5 ng/mL epidermal growth factor. Human OSCC Cell Line SCC4 and SCC9 (Human OSCC Cell Lines from ATCC) and SCC7 (mouse SCC Cell Line from Bowers Type Culture Collection) were cultured in F12/DMEM. All cells were supplemented with 1% penicillin/streptomycin and 10% fetal bovine serum.

Primary cells culture

Human oral epithelial cells (HOECs) were isolated from clinically normal gingival tissue (with ethical approval and informed consent) using explant outgrowth. Tissue fragments (1-2 mm³) were adhered to plates in KGM-Gold (Lonza)/DMEM mixed medium (2:1 ratio). Epithelial outgrowths (days 10-15) were expanded following mechanical removal of fibroblasts. Cells were passaged at 90% confluence using 0.05% trypsin-EDTA.

Murine bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were isolated from tibiae and femora of 6 to 8-week-old male C57BL/6 mice. Bone marrow cells were flushed with PBS, filtered through 70 µm strainers, and cultured in complete medium (DMEM + 10% Fetal Bovine Serum + 30 ng/ml M-CSF at 37 °C, 5% CO2. Non-adherent cells were removed after 24 h, with medium refreshed every 48 h.

Transformation of DOK cell by Benzo[a]pyrene (B[a]P) plus dimethylbenz[a]anthracene (DMBA) or 4-Nitroquinoline N-oxide (4NQO)

DOK cells were transformed into oral carcinoma (DOK-TC) cells, including DOK-BD and DOK-4NQO cells, via two methods. Following established protocols, DOK cells were treated intermittently (every 2 days) with 70 µM B[a]P plus DMBA mixture for 3 months to acquire DOK-BD cells29,30. DOK cells underwent acute exposure to 2.6 µM 4NQO for 3 h. This was followed by 30 cycles of intermittent 4NQO treatment over 2 months to acquire DOK-4NQO cells31. Both DOK-BD and DOK-4NQO cells were expanded and assessed for tumorigenicity in vitro and in vivo.

Stable cell line generation

The plasmid encoding short hairpin RNA (shRNA) targeting human and mouse PTGES, PTGES-overexpression plasmid pLV3-CMV-PTGES, lentiviral vector plasmid pLV3-CMV-MCS and pLV3-U6-MCS-shRNA, and the lentiviral packaging plasmids psPAX2 and pMD2.G were purchased from MiaoLing Plasmid Platform (Wuhan, China). DOK-4NQO and MOC1 cells were infected with 5 µg/mL polybrene and lentivirus supernatant, then selected with 2 μg/mL and 4 μg/mL puromycin separately. DOK cell was selected with 1 μg/mL puromycin. Cells were maintained in culture medium containing puromycin. The Protein levels were verified using a Western blot. The specific targeting sequences were listed in Supplementary Data 3.

Animal models

All mice were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions in individually ventilated cages (IVCs) facilities. Individual identification was achieved by ear punching. The mice were housed in an environment maintained at ~25 °C with 50% relative humidity and subjected to a 12 h light-dark cycle. They had free access to standard laboratory chow and water. The maximal tumor size permitted by the ethics committee was 15 mm in diameter. This limit was not exceeded in any experiment. Mice were euthanized if tumor size approached the predefined endpoint criteria or if signs of distress were observed. Only male mice were used; sex was not considered as a biological variable to reduce variability.

For the xenograft model, 4–5-week-old male BALB/c nude mice were purchased from the Center for Disease Control of Hubei province. Briefly, 5 × 10⁶ DOK, DOK-BD, or DOK-4NQO cells per site were subcutaneously injected into the axillary region to establish nude mouse xenograft tumor models. All mice were randomly assigned to 2 groups (6 per group). To assess the effects of DOK-4NQO cell-derived migrasomes on the tumorigenesis of DOK-4NQO cells, mice bearing xenografts were intraperitoneally injected with 20 µg of migrasomes derived from DOK-4NQO cells in 50 µL of PBS. Tumor volumes (length × width2/2) were monitored and compared. Injections were administered once every three days for a total of 7 injections. Then the mice were sacrificed, then weighed, photographed, fixed, and embedded in paraffin for hematoxylin-eosin staining and immunohistochemistry assays.

For the mouse model of 4NQO-induced oral carcinogenesis. 4–5-week-old male C57BL/6 J mice were purchased from the Center for Disease Control of Hubei province. 4NQO was dissolved in DMSO and diluted to a working solution (100 µg/mL) in drinking water every week. Starting at 6 weeks of age, male C57BL/6 mice were administered the working solution for 16 weeks. Representative tongues were collected for histopathological observation to confirm the successful establishment of different degrees of dysplasia in the mouse tongues. Subsequently, all mice were given regular drinking water and randomly divided into 3 groups (10 mice per group).

For migrasome injection. All injection instruments were prepared, including syringes pre-loaded with PBS or migrasomes. C57BL/6 mice were anesthetized by inhalation of 1.5% isoflurane. After anesthesia, an assistant exteriorized the tongue using sterile forceps. Mice in the control group were administered a 25 µL injection of PBS into the submucosa on both sides of the dorsal tongue using a disposable insulin syringe. For the shPTGES#3-Mig and shCtrl-Mig groups, migrasomes were isolated from shPTGES#3-transfected MOC1 cells and shRNA control-transfected MOC1 cells, respectively. The isolated migrasomes were then washed with PBS, resuspended, and an equal volume of the suspension (containing 30 µg of migrasomes) was injected into the corresponding tongue sites using the same methodology as the control group. Injections were administered once every three days for one month. Two weeks after the final injection, the mice were sacrificed. Their tongues and main organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were fixed and embedded in paraffin blocks for histological examination. Lesions were classified by hematoxylin-eosin staining examination as normal, dysplasia (mild to moderate dysplasia, severe dysplasia or carcinoma in situ), and invasive carcinoma. Tumor growth was monitored regularly.

Hematoxylin-eosin staining and immunohistochemistry

For tissue hematoxylin-eosin staining, tissues were dissected and fixed overnight with 4% paraformaldehyde. Samples were then embedded in paraffin. For the hematoxylin-eosin staining of cells, cells were simply fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min. Paraffin sections or cells were prepared and stained with hematoxylin-eosin for histology. Immunohistochemistry was performed according to the manufacturer’s guidelines for the Kit and quantified. Antibodies used in immunohistochemistry assays are listed in Supplementary Data 1.

Immunofluorescence staining

For tissue sections of human OLK and OSCC, after deparaffinization with xylene and rehydration with ethanol, sections were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin for 1 h, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting PIGK and E-cadherin. Sections were washed three times with PBS, followed by incubation with Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody, and Cyanine5 or CoraLite® Plus 594-Goat Anti-Mouse recombinant secondary antibody for 1 h at 37 °C. Subsequently, sections were incubated with 1 µg/mL wheat germ agglutinin (WGA)−488 dye for 10 min.

For leukoplakia tissues of the mouse tongue treated with migrasomes, after fixation with paraformaldehyde, paraffin sections were prepared. Primary antibodies F4/80 and SPP1 recombinant antibodies were incubated overnight. Secondary antibodies used were iFluor® 488 tyramide and iFluor® 647 tyramide. Finally, sections were stained with DAPI for 5 min and imaged using a spinning-disk confocal microscope (OLYMPUS, Evident Corporation, Japan).

To observe migrasome phagocytosis by macrophages in vivo, extracted MOC1 cell-derived migrasomes were pre-stained with 3 µg/mL WGA-488 for 10 min and washed twice with PBS. 12h post-injection into the mouse tongues, mice were sacrificed. Tongue tissues were prepared into frozen sections with a thickness of 8 µm. Primary antibodies CD45 and F4/80 were incubated at 4 °C overnight; secondary antibodies used were iFluor® 561 tyramide and iFluor® 647 tyramide. Finally, sections were stained with DAPI for 5 min and photographed using a Leica STELLARIS 8 confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany).

For migrasome uptake assays in vitro, migrasomes from DOK, DOK-BD, DOK-4NQO, and MOC1 cells were stained with WGA-555 for 10 min and PBS-washed twice. Stained migrasomes were added to THP-1-derived macrophages or BMDM. After 12h incubation, the medium was removed, and the cells were PBS-washed three times. Cells were fixed at room temperature for 10 min, blocked with goat serum for 1 h, and incubated overnight with CD68 or F4/80 primary antibody. Following incubation with Alexa Fluor® 488 AffiniPure® Goat Anti-Rabbit or Multi-rAb CoraLite® Plus 488-Goat Anti-Mouse secondary antibody for 1 h, samples were observed and imaged using a STEDYCON confocal microscope (Abberior, Abberior Instruments, Germany).

To detect the effect of migrasomes from DOK and DOK-TC cells on SPP1 expression in macrophages in vitro, migrasome-treated macrophages were fixed with paraformaldehyde, blocked with goat serum for 1 h, and incubated overnight with SPP1 and CD68 antibodies. Alexa Fluor® 488 AffiniPure® Goat Anti-Rabbit and Multi-rAb™ CoraLite® Plus 594-Goat Anti-Mouse secondary antibodies were incubated for 1 h at room temperature. After DAPI staining for 5 min, samples were imaged by spinning-disk confocal microscope (OLYMPUS, Evident Corporation, Japan).

To observe PTGES in DOK-4NQO and MOC1 cells in vitro, confocal dishes were coated with fibronectin at a concentration of 20 µg/ml and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After 2.5 × 105 cells were seeded in the dishes and incubated for 12–16 h, the cells were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 10 min. They were then blocked with goat serum at 37 °C for 30 min and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton-X-100 at room temperature for 10 min. Next, the samples were incubated with PTGES antibody (diluted at 1:100) overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with a secondary antibody (Alexa Fluor® 555 AffiniPure™ Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L), diluted at 1:200) at 37 °C for 60 min. WGA-488 staining was performed at a concentration of 2 µg/mL. After staining with DAPI for 10 min, excess stains were washed away, and the samples were imaged using a spinning-disk confocal microscope (OLYMPUS, Evident Corporation, Japan).

Antibodies and reagents information for immunofluorescence is presented in Supplementary Data 1 and Supplementary Data 2.

WGA staining

Confocal dishes were coated with fibronectin (10 µg/mL) and seeded with HOEC, DOK, DOK-TC, and OSCC cells. When cell density reached approximately 40% after 12 h of culture, cells were fixed with 2.5% gluta fixative solution, stained with 1 µg/mL WGA-488 for 10 min, and imaged using a STEDYCON confocal microscope (Abberior, Abberior Instruments, Germany). Migrasomes were quantified for each cell line.

Real-time live-cell imaging

DOK-GFP cells were seeded in 10 μg/mL fibronectin-coated 96-well plates, and maintained under 37 °C, 5% CO₂. Time-lapse imaging was performed at 10-minute intervals for 24 h using a High Content Screening System (Opera Phenix Plus, PerkinElmer, USA).

Exosome isolation

DOK, DOK-BD, DOK-4NQO, MOC1, and SCC4 cells were cultured until reaching 70-80% confluency. The medium was then changed to one supplemented with exosome-free fetal bovine serum. After 48 h, the supernatant was collected. Cells and large debris were removed by centrifugation at 2000 × g for 10 min followed by 12,000 × g for 30 min. Supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 µm filter and centrifuged at 110,000 × g for 70 min. Exosome concentrations were measured using a BCA protein assay kit.

Isolation of migrasomes from culture cells

DOK, DOK-BD, DOK-4NQO, SCC4, and MOC1 cells were plated in 150 mm dishes and incubated in complete medium for about 12 h to reach 60% confluence. Cultures were rinsed twice with PBS and briefly trypsinized using 0.25% trypsin. Trypsinization was stopped by adding complete medium as soon as detachment was observed, and the suspension was transferred to 50 mL conical tubes. Unless otherwise stated, subsequent steps were carried out at 4 °C.

The suspension was clarified sequentially by centrifugation at 1000 × g for 10 min and 4000 × g for 20 min. The resulting supernatant was centrifuged at 18,000 × g for 40 min to pellet migrasome-enriched material, a crude migrasome fraction. For OptiPrep separation, the crude pellet was resuspended in 500 µL PBS and adjusted to 19% OptiPrep by adding 317 µL OptiPrep and 183 µL 1× dilution buffer. Discontinuous gradients were assembled in ultracentrifuge tubes by layering, bottom to top, 30%, 25%, sample layer, 15%, 12%, 10%, 8%, 5% and 2% OptiPrep (1.5 mL each; sample layer 1 mL). Gradients were centrifuged at 150,000 × g for 4 h at 4 °C in a 42Ti rotor (Beckman Coulter XE-100). Fractions (1.5 mL) were collected from the top, diluted 1:1 with PBS, and centrifuged at 18,000 × g for 40 min at 4 °C to remove OptiPrep and recover particulate material. Pellets were resuspended in around 100 µL PBS. Migrasome-enriched fractions were identified by western blotting and TEM and used for downstream analyses.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Ultrathin-section TEM was performed on fresh OLK and OSCC tissues. Specimens were immediately immersed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde electron microscopy fixative for 2 h, rinsed in buffer, and post-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide for 1 h. Samples were dehydrated through an ascending ethanol series and embedded in Embed-812 epoxy resin. 100 nm ultrathin sections were cut on an Ultracut EM UC7 ultramicrotome (Leica) and mounted on Formvar-coated 230-mesh copper grids. Sections were contrasted with 2% phosphotungstic acid (PTA) and examined at 200 kV.

For negative staining, purified exosome or migrasome suspensions were placed as droplets on Parafilm, and copper grids were floated on the droplets for 20 s for adsorption. Excess liquid was blotted off, grids were stained with 2% PTA for 1 min, air-dried, and imaged on a Tecnai G2 20 TWIN TEM (FEI) operated at 200 kV.

Scanning electron microscope (SEM)

Cultured cells grown on culture dishes were gently rinsed with PBS three times and fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde electron microscopy fixative for 2 h at room temperature. After fixation, samples were washed with PBS and post-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide for 2 h at room temperature to enhance membrane contrast. Samples were then dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, and 100% ethanol, with the 100% step repeated twice). Following dehydration, ethanol was replaced with isoamyl acetate. Specimens were subsequently subjected to CO2 critical point drying, mounted on SEM (S4800, HITACHI, Japan) stubs using conductive adhesive, sputter-coated with gold for 30 s, and imaged by scanning electron microscopy at an accelerating voltage of 2.0 kV.

Human 4D-label-free quantitative proteomics

Proteomic analysis of migrasomes from DOK, DOK-BD, and DOK-4NQO cells was performed at Shanghai Hwayen Biotechnological, Inc., China. Three biological replicates were analyzed for each experimental group (n = 3 per group). No technical replicates were performed. Each migrasome sample contained 50 µL of purified migrasomes at approximately 1.5 µg/µL. Migrasome proteins were extracted using lysis buffer containing 7 M urea, 2% SDS, and protease inhibitor cocktail. Proteins from equal volumes of nine migrasome samples were acetone-precipitated. Fifty micrograms of protein were reduced with 10 mM DTT at 37 °C for 1 h and alkylated with iodoacetamide (IAA) at a 1:5 ratio to DTT for 40 min in the dark. Samples were precipitated, centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 1 h, and digested with trypsin overnight at 37 °C. Peptides were desalted using MonoSpin C18 columns. Peptide separation was performed using an Easy nLC1200 system coupled to an Orbitrap Exploris™ 480 mass spectrometer with FAIMS Pro™ (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) operating in data-independent acquisition (DIA) mode. MS1 scans were acquired over an m/z range of 350–1500 at a resolution of 120,000, with an AGC target of 48,000 and maximum injection time of 50 ms. HCD-MS/MS scans were acquired at a resolution of 30,000 with an AGC target of 2 × 10⁵ and collision energy of 32.

DIA raw data were processed using DIA-NN (v1.9.2) with default settings. Default mass tolerance, minimum peptide length settings, and protein inference parameters in DIA-NN were applied. and searched against the SwissProt Homo sapiens protein database (SwissProt release 2021_06). Trypsin was specified as the proteolytic enzyme with up to two missed cleavages allowed. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine was set as a fixed modification, while oxidation of methionine and acetylation of protein N-termini were set as variable modifications. Peptide and protein identifications were filtered at a false discovery rate (FDR) of 1% (Q value < 0.01; protein-level FDR = 0.01) using a target–decoy strategy. Protein quantification was performed based on MS2 fragment ion peak areas.

Raw protein expression data were log2-transformed and analyzed for differential abundance using the limma package (v3.60.6) in R (v4.4.1). Principal component analysis was performed with the base prcomp function and visualized in 3D using Plotly (v4.11.0) and geometry (v0.5.2). Volcano plots were generated with ggplot2 (v3.5.2) and annotated using ggrepel (v0.9.6). Heatmaps were created with ComplexHeatmap (v2.21.1) and circlize (v0.4.16). Data import, processing and export used openxlsx (v4.2.8), stringr (v1.5.1) and dplyr (v1.1.4). Significance thresholds were set at fold change >1.5 and adjusted p < 0.05. All plots were generated in R.

Single-cell RNA-seq data processing and analysis

Single-cell RNA-sequencing data from OLK and OSCC were processed using the Seurat R package (v5.3.0) in R version 4.4.1. Raw gene expression matrices were imported and converted into Seurat objects, with cells retained if they expressed >300 genes, >1000 UMIs, and <20% mitochondrial gene content. Genes detected in fewer than three cells or with fewer than 40 counts across all cells were excluded. Doublets were identified and removed using DoubletFinder (v2.0.4) with pN = 0.25, pK = 0.09, and an expected doublet rate of 8%.

Three OLK samples were downloaded from GEO (GSE196296) and processed individually from 10x Genomics outputs and merged into a unified Seurat object. Seven HPV-negative OSCC samples (#1, 7, 17, 30, 39, 46, 49) were downloaded from GEO (GSE234933), and sample- and cell-level metadata were added prior to merging. Following normalization, highly variable genes (n = 2000) were identified using the “vst” method, and data were scaled and projected via principal component analysis (30 components). Cells expressing >1% hemoglobin genes (HBA1, HBB, HBG1, etc.) were filtered out to remove red blood cell contamination.

Batch correction was performed using the Harmony algorithm (v1.2.3), based on sample identity. Clustering was conducted using the Louvain algorithm (resolution = 0.3) on Harmony embeddings, and dimensionality reduction was visualized with Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP). Cluster marker genes were identified using the FindAllMarkers function, with a minimum log fold change of 0.25 and expression in ≥25% of cells. Top marker genes were visualized using heatmaps. Cell types were annotated using canonical markers and merged into similar clusters.

For migrasome score quantification, to quantify migrasome-associated transcriptional activity, we calculated AUCell scores using the AUCell R package (v1.26.0). A curated gene set consisting of 12 known migrasome-related genes including: ITGB1, ITGA5, EOGT, CPQ, PIGK, NDST1, TSPAN4, PIP5K1A, RAB35, SGMS2, ROCK1, CD151 was used25–28. AUCell scores (“Migscore”) were assigned to each cell based on gene-ranking recovery. Group-wise comparisons between OLK and OSCC were conducted using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

For cell–cell communication analysis, intercellular signaling networks among epithelial and immune cells were inferred using the CellChat R package (v1.6.1). Separate CellChat objects were constructed for each group using log-normalized gene expression matrices (Seurat “data” slot, normalized by the NormalizeData function) and cell-type annotations. The CellChatDB.human ligand–receptor database was used. The resulting signaling networks were used to analyze group-specific intercellular interactions.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed via Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay in four experimental systems: (1) DOK cell was treated with 4NQO or B(a)P/DMBA at different concentrations for 24 h, and cell viability was detected through the CCK8 assay; (2) CCK-8 assay were used to evaluate the proliferation ability between DOK and DOK-TC cells; (3) DOK-4NQO and DOK cells were treated with supernatant from THP-1-derived macrophages exposed to PTGES-knockdown or -overexpression migrasomes, CCK-8 assay were used to evaluate the proliferation ability of the treated cells; (4) THP-1 cell-derived macrophages were treated with increasing concentrations of celecoxib. Cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 reagent according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

EdU assays

MOC1 cells were stimulated with supernatant from BMDM exposed to PTGES-knockdown migrasomes; subsequent procedures were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions using the EdU Cell Proliferation Kit with Alexa Fluor 488. MOC1 cell proliferation was quantified as the ratio of EdU-positive cells to the total number of cells.

Transwell assay

Cell migration and invasion were assessed via Transwell assay: (1) To evaluate the migration and invasion of DOK and DOK-TC cells, DOK and DOK-TC cells were seeded in upper chambers exposed to serum-free medium, with complete medium in lower chambers; (2) To evaluate migrasome-mediated macrophage recruitment, PMA-induced THP-1 cells in upper chambers were exposed to serum-free medium containing DOK/DOK-TC cell-derived migrasomes, with complete medium in lower chambers; (3) To observe migrasome-treated macrophages’ inductive effect on DOK-TC cell migration and invasion, these macrophages were cocultured with DOK-TC cells in upper chambers; (4) THP-1-derived macrophages after migrasome treatment with PTGES knockdown or overexpression were co-cultured with DOK-4NQO or DOK cells in upper chambers; (5) BMDMs exposed to PTGES-knockdown migrasomes were co-cultured with MOC1. Migrated cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min and stained with 0.2% crystal violet for 10 min. Chambers were rinsed with distilled water and photographed microscopically. For Transwell invasion assays, upper chambers were pre-coated with Matrigel.

Wound healing assay

DOK-4NQO cells, achieving approximately 90% confluence, were wounded by scraping with a 200µL pipette tip twice per well and washed three times with PBS. Wounds were observed and photographed at 0 h and 24 h.

Quantitative Real-Time Polymerase Chain Reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using the FastPure Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit V2 (Vazyme Biotech Co.,Ltd) and reverse-transcribed to cDNA with HiScript IV RT SuperMix for qPCR. qRT-PCR employed Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix. ACTB mRNA levels served as internal controls. Gene expression differences were calculated via the 2(−ΔΔCt) method and expressed as fold-changes. PCR conditions began with 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. A final dissociation stage included 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 60 s, and 95 °C for 15 s. Primers are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

PGE2 levels in supernatants of THP-1-derived macrophages or BMDMs after treatment with PTGES-knockdown or -overexpression migrasomes were assayed using the prostaglandin E2/PGE2 competitive ELISA Kit. All experimental procedures followed the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cell cycle assay

DOK and DOK-TC cells were trypsin-digested, centrifuged, and PBS-washed three times. Per the Cell Cycle Staining Kit protocol, 1 mL PI staining solution and 10 µL RNase A were added, vortex-mixed for 5 seconds, and stained in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. Samples were analyzed using flow cytometry, and cell cycle distribution was evaluated with ModfitLT 5 saainc software.

Cytoskeleton staining assay

5 × 10⁴ DOK, DOK-BD, and DOK-4-NQO cells were seeded into confocal dishes. After 24 h of culture, the medium was discarded, and the cells were PBS-washed three times. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 minutes, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 at room temperature for 15 minutes. Per actin-tracker red kit instructions, actin-tracker red was diluted in 5% BSA + 0.1% Triton X-100; 200 µL working solution was added per dish and dark-stained for 60 minutes. Cells were DAPI-stained for 5 min and imaged using an FV1200 confocal microscope (OLYMPUS, Japan).

Western blot

Cells were washed with ice-cold PBS and lysed on ice in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. Lysates were cleared by centrifugation at 4 °C and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein were denatured in SDS sample buffer, resolved by SDS-PAGE, and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, and signals were quantified after normalization to an appropriate loading control. Primary and secondary antibodies used are listed in Supplementary Data 1. Bands were visualized using chemiluminescent HRP substrate.

Flow cytometry

DOK-BD and DOK-4NQO cell-derived migrasomes were pretreated with 0.1% Triton-X-100 plus proteinase K to degrade proteins or 0.1% Triton-X-100 plus RNase A to degrade mRNA, followed by coculture with macrophages. A single-cell suspension of macrophages was stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies in PBS containing 1% BSA for 20 min at 4 °C per the manufacturer’s dilution ratios. SPP1 Monoclonal Antibody (2F10), eFluor™ 660, eBioscience™ was used. Measurement wavelength was 647 nm. Data analysis used FlowJo software (v10.8.1). The gating strategy corresponds to the flow cytometry data shown in Supplementary Fig. 8a,c.

Colony formation assay

A colony-formation assay was used to assess cell proliferation. DOK-BD and DOK-4NQO cells were seeded in six-well plates at 3000 cells per well and incubated with supernatant from macrophages treated with migrasomes from DOK and DOK-TC cells until obvious clonal proliferation was observed. The colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and stained with 0.1% v/v crystal violet for another 10 min. Images were acquired and the number of colonies was quantified for further analysis.

Statistics & Reproducibility

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0.0). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Wilcoxon rank-sum test (Figs.1g and 4h), and the unpaired Student’s t-test (Fig. 5p, r) were employed for comparisons between the two groups. In Fig. 4g, the overall group difference was assessed by the Kruskal-Wallis test; significant results were followed by pairwise Wilcoxon rank-sum test with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction, and p-values reported were FDR-adjusted. Other comparisons involving multiple groups, one-way ANOVA with either Tukey’s multiple comparisons test or Dunnett’s multiple comparisons (when the control group was specified) was used. Cell proliferation was analyzed by ordinary two-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test. Tumor growth curves were analyzed by two-way repeated-measures ANOVA with Geisser-Greenhouse correction. All statistical tests were two-sided. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

All experiments were independently repeated at least three times with similar results unless otherwise stated. The number of biological replicates (n) for each experiment is specified in the corresponding figure legends. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. No data were excluded from the analyses. The experiments were not randomized. The investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment.
