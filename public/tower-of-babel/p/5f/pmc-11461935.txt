Varicella pneumonia in a healthy 31-year-old female with no prior varicella immunity and no skin rush: A case report
Authors: Zineb Izi, Ibtissam El Bqaq, Khadija Laasri, Amine Naggar, Omar El Aoufir, Fatima Zahra Laamrani, Laila Jroundi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractVaricella pneumonia represents a relatively uncommon yet severe complication arising from initial varicella infection. Between 5% and 15% of instances of adult varicella infection may result in various pulmonary conditions. Predominantly afflicting adults, its severity is compounded by factors like smoking, compromised immune systems, pregnancy, age, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and male gender. We represent the case of an adult female lacking prior immunity to varicella, who experienced heightened oxygen needs with severe hemoptysis without skin symptoms. Swift improvement was observed just a 2 following valacyclovir administration. Varicella pneumonia stands out as a significant complication stemming from primary varicella infection in adults, with early administration of acyclovir linked to both symptomatic relief and reduced mortality rates.Keywords: Varicella, Varicella pneumonia, CPAP, Radiography of varicella pneumonia, Complications of varicella

Introduction

Varicella, caused by the Varicella-Zoster Virus (VZV), is a highly contagious disease that typically presents as a mild, self-limiting illness in children. However, it can pose significant risks to immunocompromised individuals, potentially resulting in life-threatening complications [1]. Although rare, VZV infections can also lead to severe and sometimes fatal outcomes in individuals with normal immune function, including varicella pneumonia, which can rapidly progress to acute respiratory distress syndrome despite standard supportive care [2]. We present the case of a 31-year-old woman who was admitted to the emergency room for hemoptysis and was subsequently diagnosed with chickenpox following exposure to her 4-year-old daughter. This report will discuss the respiratory manifestations of the Varicella-Zoster Virus, relevant imaging findings, and treatment approaches.

Case report

A 31-year-old female presented to the emergency room for slight hemoptysis and shortness of breath. The patient was exposed to varicella from her 4-year-old daughter. The patient stated that she had never been vaccinated against varicella and had never had a varicella infection before. The absence of prior immunity was her sole notable risk factor, given her immunocompetence and lack of smoking history. Clinically, upon admission she presented with, a fever of 39.5°C, rapid breathing at 35 cycles per minute, oxygen saturation of 75% while using a high-concentration mask at 15 L/min, bilateral wheezing, cyanosis around the mouth and extremities indicating respiratory distress, paradoxical breathing, normal blood pressure measuring 130/90 mmHg, a heart rate of 120 beats per minute, without any skin symptoms.

Despite therapy the patient experienced worsening tachypnea, hemoptysis, and reduced oxygen saturation with FIO2 of 100%. white blood count 10,700, cold agglutinin tests, and blood cultures were negative. Covid-19 and H1N1 tests yielded negative results, while VZV serology returned positive.

The chest X-ray revealed bilateral and diffuse alveolar interstitial opacities in both lung fields.

CT scan showed a diffuse micronodular pattern distributed throughout the lungs and some surrounding ground-glass attenuation with focal narrowing of the lower left lobular bronchus (Fig. 1).

The diagnosis of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) due to Varicella pneumonia was confirmed based on the combination of respiratory symptoms, the contagious context and positive Varicella Zoster Virus serology. Treatment was initiated with acyclovir at a dosage of 10 mg/kg every 8 hours.

Consequently, the patient was deemed fit for discharge from both the and Intensive Care Unit (ICU) to Infectious Disease service, with instructions to continue her valacyclovir treatment as an outpatient.

Discussion

Varicella, a highly communicable illness triggered by the VZV, is globally prevalent. Belonging to the "Herpesviridae" family of DNA viruses, it can induce both active and dormant infections. Transmission occurs primarily through inhaling droplets containing infected saliva or, less commonly, direct contact with skin lesions [3].

Varicella, typically considered a mild childhood illness, is also observed in 10%-20% of adults [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Risk factors for adult onset include smoking, advanced age, pregnancy, chronic lung disease, and immunosuppression [4]. Research indicates that pneumonia occurs in about 36.8% of adult chickenpox cases among smokers, compared to approximately 3% among nonsmokers [5]. This complication harbors significant morbidity and mortality for a vaccine-preventable disease. In our case, the lack of skin eruptions and the absence of these risk factors likely contributed to the favorable outcome of the disease.

Respiratory manifestations of Varicella pneumonia typically emerge 1-7 days following the onset of the rash. These symptoms often present mildly, characterized by a dry cough, hemoptysis, chest pain, dyspnea, fever, and in severe cases, acute respiratory distress [6]. Varicella-induced acute respiratory distress syndrome, though uncommon, poses a life-threatening situation.

In our case of varicella pneumonia, an unusual aspect is the lack of a skin rash, which usually accompanies this viral infection. This deviation from the typical presentation makes diagnosis more challenging and highlights the need to consider VZV pneumonia as a possible differential diagnosis, even when characteristic skin lesions are absent, especially in a contagious context.

Histological characteristics of pneumonitis associated with chickenpox and shingles comprise an interstitial infiltration of mononuclear inflammatory cells alongside intra-alveolar proteinaceous exudate, hyaline membrane formation, and hyperplasia of type II cells, indicating diffuse alveolar damage. In cases of remote chickenpox infection, pathological findings include spherical nodules dispersed irregularly throughout lung tissue, identifiable radiographically by widespread micronodular calcifications. These nodules exhibit an outer fibrous capsule, often layered, enclosing regions of hyalinized collagen or necrotic tissue. The degree of calcification varies in intensity [7].

Chest X-ray findings differ slightly from those of other viral infections. It causes the development of multiple 5- to 10-mm ill-defined nodules in the lungs, which may merge and appear and disappear in various lung regions. Hilar adenopathy and small pleural effusions are rare during the acute phase and were not seen in our case. The small consolidations usually clear within a week after skin lesions disappear but can persist for months.

High-resolution CT provided more detailed insights into varicella-zoster pneumonia findings compared to radiography. The small nodules observed on chest radiographs appeared as 5- to 10-mm poorly defined nodules on CT, with some showing surrounding ground-glass attenuation. Similarly, areas of consolidation on chest radiographs manifested as patchy ground-glass attenuation and merging of poorly defined nodules on CT, and that was the case in our patient.

Varicella pneumonia can resemble several other conditions, necessitating careful differential diagnosis. Pulmonary hemosiderosis, often linked to mitral stenosis or repeated hemorrhage conditions like Goodpasture syndrome, can show multiple small, dense nodules similar to those seen in varicella pneumonia. Idiopathic silicosis, resulting from pure silica exposure, leads to small, round nodules throughout the lungs, which may mimic the appearance of varicella pneumonia. Pulmonary alveolar microlithiasis, a rare condition involving the deposition of calcium phosphate microliths within the alveoli, can also cause diffuse, small nodular opacities. Additionally, other viral pneumonias, such as those caused by cytomegalovirus, influenza virus, or measles virus, can produce imaging findings that include nodules with surrounding ground-glass opacity and patchy ground-glass attenuation, further complicating the differential diagnosis [8].

The standard treatment for varicella pneumonia typically involves a 7- to 10-day course of Acyclovir, administered orally or intravenously depending on the severity of the condition [9]. The use of adjunctive steroid therapy remains a topic of debate but should be considered in cases of severe infection. Approximately 40% of patients with varicella-zoster virus (VZV) pneumonia may require mechanical ventilation. In instances of severe or refractory respiratory failure, the use of ECMO (extracorporeal membrane oxygenation) should be considered [10]. The mortality rate associated with varicella pneumonia has been declining, currently standing at around 6% according to recent data [5]. This improvement is likely attributed to early diagnosis, treatment with Acyclovir, and the availability of modern intensive care units.

Conclusion

Varicella pneumonia can be life-threatening in adults. Therefore, it is important to be aware of this possibility in adult patients exhibiting respiratory symptoms such as cough, difficulty breathing, chest pain, rapid breathing, and hemoptysis, alongside contagious context. Early administration of acyclovir therapy is crucial to manage the condition effectively. This atypical presentation underscores the diverse clinical spectrum of VZV infection and highlights the need for heightened vigilance among clinicians to recognize and manage such cases effectively.
