Arthroscopic Treatment of Focal Chondral Defects in the Hip Joint With Autologous Minced Cartilage, Allograft Cartilage Extracellular Matrix, and Platelet‐Rich Plasma
Authors: Austin G Cross, Ashley D Price, Anthony J Scillia, Matthew J Kraeutler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFocal chondral defects (FCDs) are often encountered during hip arthroscopy for the treatment of femoroacetabular impingement. Historically, arthroscopic treatment for FCDs in the hip joint has revolved around microfracture, though recent evidence has showed inferior outcomes with this approach. Open cartilage repair procedures in the hip joint require a surgical hip dislocation and carry increased morbidity to the patient. This technical note offers a single‐staged, arthroscopic treatment option for FCDs of the hip joint by combining autologous minced cartilage, allograft cartilage extracellular matrix, and platelet‐rich plasma.

Hip arthroscopy has become increasingly popular for the treatment of intra‐articular hip pathology and is considered the gold standard for the treatment of femoroacetabular impingement (FAI). The increasing incidence of hip arthroscopy for the treatment of FAI has been well documented, 1 as clinicians and surgeons have developed a better understanding of the clinical and radiographic findings associated with FAI. In addition to femoroplasty/acetabuloplasty and treatment of labral pathology, hip arthroscopy allows the surgeon to address focal chondral defects (FCDs) which are frequently associated with FAI.

Several treatment options exist for addressing cartilage defects of the hip. Debridement and microfracture (MFx) have been considered the mainstay of treatment for small, contained lesions, 2 , 3 though recent concern has been raised to the efficacy and longevity of this treatment option. 4 , 5 Other treatment options such as osteochondral allograft transplantation often require a more extensive surgical hip dislocation with added morbidity to the patient. As a result, it is important to identify arthroscopic treatment options in the hip joint which avoid MFx. This technical note describes a treatment option which allows for single‐stage arthroscopic treatment of FCDs in the hip joint (femoral head or acetabulum) by combining autologous minced cartilage, allograft cartilage extracellular matrix, and platelet‐rich plasma.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Postless hip arthroscopy is initiated as previously described. 6 If a full‐thickness FCD is identified on the acetabulum or femoral head, the decision is made to proceed with the following technique (Video 1). The lesion is first debrided with a shaver to create stable borders (Figure 1) and dimensions of the defect are recorded. Following treatment of labral pathology, traction is released and the planned femoroplasty is marked under fluoroscopic guidance. The GraftNet autologous tissue collector (Arthrex, Naples, FL) is connected to the shaver and cartilage overlying the cam lesion is debrided and suctioned into the GraftNet for later use (Figure 2). Femoroplasty is then performed. Following femoroplasty, capsular repair stitches are passed but not tied.

The patient is positioned supine on the operating table, and postless hip arthroscopy is initiated as previously described. 6 Once a focal chondral defect is identified, the decision is made to proceed with the described technique. A shaver is used to debride the cartilage lesion to achieve stable borders. After the labral pathology is addressed, traction is released and the femoroplasty portion of the procedure is completed. During femoroplasty, the GraftNet autologous tissue collector is used to obtain autograft cartilage. After femoroplasty is complete, stitches for capsular repair are passed but not tied until completion of the cartilage repair. On the back table, the autologous graft is combined with 1 cc of BioCartilage Extracellular Matrix and platelet‐rich plasma collected from the patient. Next, the joint is placed back on traction and all fluid is suctioned from the joint. The graft is then placed into the cartilage defect followed by fibrin glue for further stabilization. On the back table, the autologous cartilage (collected as described above) is combined with 1 cc of BioCartilage Extracellular Matrix (Arthrex, Naples, FL) and platelet‐rich plasma (PRP) prepared from 20 mL of autologous blood collected from the patient's intravenous line (Figure 3). PRP should be added to the cartilage mixture sequentially until a paste‐like substance is created.

On the back table, an assistant mixes autologous cartilage, BioCartilage Extracellular Matrix (Arthrex, Naples, FL), and PRP to create a paste‐like substance. (PRP, platelet‐rich plasma.)

The joint is then placed back on traction and all fluid is suctioned out of the joint using the shaver. The Hip Delivery Needle and Mixing and Delivery Syringe (Arthrex, Naples, FL) are connected, and the cartilage/PRP solution is advanced to the edge of the spatula at the end of the delivery needle. The needle is then passed through the cannula in the midanterior portal, with the spatula placed against the FCD. The solution is further advanced until the cartilage solution fills the defect (Figure 4). The delivery needle is then removed from the joint and saline is passed through the needle to clear out any remaining cartilage. A fibrin glue delivery system (VISTASEAL, Johnson & Johnson, New Brunswick, NJ) is then passed through the midanterior portal cannula. A small amount of fibrin glue is delivered over the cartilage solution in the FCD (Figure 5). Traction is released, the hip is slowly and carefully flexed to 45°, and the capsular repair stitches are tied. When this technique is utilized, changes to our postoperative rehabilitation protocol include toe‐touch weight bearing for 6 weeks postoperatively.

DISCUSSION

Treatment options for FCDs of the hip joint have mostly been limited to arthroscopic techniques due to the difficult accessibility of the joint without performing an extensive open surgical hip dislocation. Historically, arthroscopic treatment with debridement and MFx has been considered the mainstay of treatment for these defects. 7 However, there are numerous concerns associated with MFx, including deleterious effects of the subchondral bone and the inferior mechanical properties of the fibrocartilage tissue formed versus native hyaline cartilage. 8 , 9 , 10

A recent systematic review by Dhillon et al. 4 investigated the outcomes of hip arthroscopy patients treated with MFx compared with other cartilage repair procedures. The authors found that patients who were treated with MFx had a higher reoperation rate, and equivalent or inferior patient‐reported outcomes (PROs) compared with those treated with other procedures (including debridement, bone marrow aspirate concentrate, autologous matrix‐induced chondrogenesis, biopolymer, and microfragmented autologous adipose tissue transposition). Another systematic review published in 2024 by Akhtar et al. 11 found that although MFx was the most commonly performed procedure for FCDs in the hip joint, it had lower survivability and higher conversion rates to total hip arthroplasty than previously reported. The authors also reported that novel techniques which avoided MFx altogether had higher survival rates compared with MFx alone. A recent study by Sanz‐Reig et al. 12 found that a majority of athletes treated with MFx were unable to continue participating in the same sport at 5‐year follow up.

This technical note describes a method of single‐stage arthroscopic treatment of FCDs in the hip joint by combining autologous cartilage, allograft cartilage extracellular matrix, and biologics without causing damage to the subchondral bone. Advantages of this technique include the use of a single‐stage, all‐arthroscopic treatment which avoids MFx. Given the concerns with MFx, numerous studies have investigated alternative treatment modalities to address cartilage defects. Fortier et al. 13 found BioCartilage mixed with PRP to be superior to microfracture up to 13 months post‐implantation in equine knees. Brusalis et al. 14 found promising results with the combination of BioCartilage with PRP at 2‐year follow up in patients with cartilage defects in the knee. Other authors have previously described techniques and comparable results with particulated autologous chondrocyte implantation with traditional autologous chondrocyte implantation, while avoiding the 2‐staged procedure and also having the benefit of being more economical. 15 , 16 , 17 Another advantage of our technique is that there is no increased morbidity from the cartilage harvesting process, as this cartilage would be debrided routinely as part of femoroplasty. This technique is not without its challenges. Delivering the cartilage graft can be challenging arthroscopically depending on the anatomical location of the cartilage defect.
