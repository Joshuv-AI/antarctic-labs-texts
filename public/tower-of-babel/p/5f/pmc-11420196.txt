The roles of geometry and viscosity in the mobilization of coarse sediment by finer sediment
Authors: Marwan A. Hassan, Gary Parker, Yarra Hassan, Chenge An, Xudong Fu, Jeremy G. Venditti
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceIn gravel-sand rivers, coarser sediment is heavier and thus more difficult to move. It has been observed that additions of fine sediment can increase the mobility of coarse sediment. This effect can disrupt the riverbed stability and further affect infrastructure safety and aquatic habitat. Previous research indicates that both the addition of sand and finer gravel can enhance the mobility of coarser gravel, but the effect of sand is markedly larger. Our experiments show that a viscous effect plays a key role in scenarios of added sand but not added finer gravel. Our formulation of this problem should be universal but translates to different sizes on other planets due to differences in gravity, viscosity, and sediment submerged specific gravity.

The transport of mixed-size sediment is a vexing problem because the transport of such mixtures of sand and gravel is highly dependent on the sizes present on the bed and in the sediment supply (e.g., refs. 1–4). A seminal paper by Wilcock (5) showed that adding sand <2 mm to a gravel bed increases the mobility of the gravel. This effect was later verified (e.g., refs. 6 and 7) and encapsulated in a bedload transport relation in which the 2 mm size plays a specific role (8). However, the physical basis for selecting 2 mm was not obvious, beyond the naming conversion for sand and gravel.

Supply of material that is coarser than what the flow can transport results in aggradation and increased bed slope (e.g., ref. 9). On the other hand, an increase in supply of fine sediment to a gravel-dominated bed will fill the pore space between gravels, reduce the pivot angle of the coarse fractions, and increase the mobility of the gravel (5, 6, 10, 11). Such increased mobility of gravel with the existence of sand can play a role in many morphological phenomena and features, including riverbed degradation, bimodal bed grain size distribution, and gravel-sand transitions (12–15). While the phenomenon of sand enhancing coarse particle mobility is well documented (e.g., refs. 2, 5, 6, 8, and 16–18), the reasons why this occurs are not clearly known. Early research indicated the processes responsible for increased gravel mobility include a) smoothing of the bed surface roughness by the fine particles (reducing the drag on the coarse particles and increasing the flow velocity); b) protrusion of the coarse particles into a zone of higher velocity; and c) collision of moving gravel grains against finer grains (16, 17). Wilcock and collaborators showed a significant reduction of the critical shear stress needed to initiate gravel movement with the addition of sand. As the sand fraction in the bed increases from zero to about 30%, the threshold of motion decreases by ~40% (2, 8).

Finer particles entraining coarser particles is not just limited to sand (<2 mm) entraining gravel (19, 20). This suggests that the increased mobility may be a geometric effect. Adding fine gravels to a coarser mixture results in the entrainment of the coarser grains as fine gravel fills the interstitial pockets, smoothing the bed and accelerating near-bed flow (19, 20). This increases mean velocity and near-bed velocity, and reduces turbulence, which in turn increases the drag and lift forces on the coarse fractions, leading to their disproportionate entrainment and transport. The addition of particles near the median size of a gravel mixture has also been observed to cause entrainment from the bed, but mainly through replacement of the same-sized material (20, 21). Whether finer sediment causes enhanced mobility of a coarser bed depends on how fine sediment interacts with the bed surface and its propensity to accumulate, infiltrate into the surface, or percolate through the bed to the bottom of the coarse gravel layer (22). As the coarse fractions move, more fines are likely to infiltrate to the substrate which will likely decrease the stability of the coarse armor layer (23). The way finer sediment interacts with the bed surface layer is thought to be dependent on the ratio of the coarser sediment size (Dc) to the finer sediment size (Df) (22). If Dc/Df > 20, then the finer sediment will percolate through the gravel bed to the bottom and if Dc/Df < 2, the finer sediment will not infiltrate through the interstitial spaces of the gravel and the bed will aggrade (24, 25). If 2 < Dc/Df < 20, finer sediment tends to bridge across interstitial pockets of the bed, remaining near the bed surface (24–26). The depth of infiltration is typically 2.5 to 5 times the coarse sediment size, so it remains in the active layer where it can affect coarse sediment flux transport.

It has been recently argued that finer particles entrain coarser particles due to a viscous effect. Parker et al. (27) illustrated how adding sand to a gravel bed can encourage the development of a transitional hydraulically smooth turbulent boundary layer (where viscous forces become important). Gravel beds without much sand typically have hydraulically rough, turbulent bed boundary layers. Novak and Nalluri (28) have shown that a coarse particle placed on the bed under hydraulically smooth flow moves more easily than when placed on the bed under hydraulically rough flow. Here, we report a set of experiments where we explicitly test whether increased mobility of coarse sediment due to the addition of finer sediment is a purely geometric effect or whether it is also influenced by viscosity (SI Appendix, Text S1 and S2 for Methods). We use two mixtures with identical grain size ratios, Dc/Df, and vary the fine sediment feed rate relative to the estimated transport capacity. The first mixture has Df = 0.5 mm, Dc = 5 mm and (δv)cr > Df , so there should be enhanced mobility due to the viscous effect (Fig. 1). The second mixture has Df = 2 mm, Dc = 20 mm and (δv)cr < Df, so the viscous effect should be negligible. If the enhanced mobility is the same between the two mixtures, we can conclude that the increase in coarser material mobility is a purely geometric effect, and that the special significance of D = 2 mm is not physically meaningful. If the differential mobility is greater in the finer mixture than in the coarser mixture, the viscous effect plays a role in enhanced mobility, and the significance of the size D = 2 mm [shorthand for 1 to 5 mm, as explained in Parker et al. (27)] can be cast as a viscous effect in terms of dimensionless grain size D*.

We established the feed rates for our experiments by calculating the transport capacity for a given slope and transport stage from the Ashida and Michiue (29) bedload equation. The transport stage was kept identical for the finer and coarser mixtures by holding the nondimensional transport rate (q∗) constant. This was done to ensure that any mobilization effects were due to the addition of finer sediment and not due to the nonlinearity of the relation between bedload flux and shear stress. Nondimensional transport rate (q∗) is calculated asq∗=qbRgD1.5,

where qb is the dimensional transport rate and g is gravitational acceleration. The capacity transport rate from Ashida and Michiue (29) was q∗=0.00287. We varied the sediment supply as a percentage of capacity transport stage.

Each of our experiments consisted of two phases. In the first phase, a coarse sediment was fed into the channel until the bed had adopted a steady state slope and the sediment output was approximately equal to the input. In the second phase, coarse sediment supply was decreased, and a fine sediment supply was added (the total sediment supply kept about constant). Size-specific sediment flux, channel slope, bed surface grain size and infiltration were monitored during each experiment. The shear stress was calculated using average water depth and water surface slope in the streamwise direction.

Results

Observations.

During the first stage of each experiment, most of the coarse sediment supplied to the flume was deposited in the flume, increasing the bed elevation and slope until the gradient was sufficient to pass the feed through the channel. This produced a steady state transport condition without persistent erosion or deposition in the channel. Upon introduction of the fine sediment feed, the fines infiltrated into the coarse fraction near the upper-most part of the flume, and then this infiltration pattern migrated downstream. The fine sediment front progression rate depended on the fine feed rate. For both Mixture 1 and Mixture 2, it took about 5 h for fines to reach the sediment trap at low fine feed rates, and another 6 h for the sediment output to equal the feed rate. At high fine feed rates, the fines front reached the outlet after 30 min and the transport matched the feed rate after 1 to 2 h (SI Appendix, Text S3 for further details).

We explored the potential covariation in the coarse sediment transport rates and fine sediment feed rates, by focusing on the peaks in transport caused by the introduction of fine sediment (Fig. 3). We quantified the spike in transport as the maximum coarse material transport rate (qsmax). To test the robustness of our results, we also quantified it in terms of the mean transport rate of the maximum and the two adjacent measurements (qsmean) (the one before the maximum, the maximum, and one after the maximum). For Mixture 1, qsmax increased from 20 to 118 g/ms as the fine feed rate increased (Fig. 3A). For experiments with high fine feed rates, qsmax values ranged between 88 and 120 g/ms. For Mixture 2, qsmax also increased with fine feed rate but ranged between 100 and 141 g/ms (Fig. 3B). For both mixtures, the variation in qsmean was similar to qsmax, but the increase in qsmean is more subdued.

To compare between fine sediment feed rates and coarse material movement, we calculated the ratios qsmax/qftotal and qsmean/qftotal, where qftotal is the sum of fine and coarse fraction feed rates. In order to determine whether the increase in coarse sediment is truly entrainment or just replacement of coarse sediment on the bed surface, we also explored the ratio of qsmf/qftotal, where qsmf is the mean of the difference between the coarse fraction transport rate and the feed rate. Fig. 3 summarizes results for all 14 experiments with Mixture 1 and all 9 experiments with Mixture 2. For Mixture 1, qsmax/qftotal ranged between 2 and 4.5 with no clear relation to the value of fine material feed rate (Fig. 3C) and with a mean qsmax/qftotal = 3.33. In addition, qsmean/qftotal ranged between 1.6 and 3.2 with a mean value of 2.4 (Fig. 3C). Mixture 2 produced similar trends to Mixture 1; however, qsmax/qftotal and qsmean/qftotal were much lower (Fig. 3D). Overall, the mean qsmax/qftotal for Mixture 1 was more than twice that of Mixture 2 (3.33 versus 1.45). Comparison of mean qsmean/qftotal produces different values but the same overall tendency (2.4 vs. 1.09). Finally, we examined the influence of the fine coverage on the bed on the mobility of the coarse fractions (Fig. 3 E and F). The analyses yielded similar results to those obtained for the fine feed rate.

These results conclusively demonstrate a difference between the behavior of Mixture 1 and Mixture 2. In both cases, adding material from the finer phase increases the transport rate of the coarser phase, but the effect in the case of Mixture 2 is notably weaker than that of Mixture 1. We conclude that the tendency for the addition of a finer phase to a coarser phase to increase the transport rate of the coarser phase cannot be a purely geometric effect.

Discussion and conclusions

Our work provides broader insight into previously reported results indicating that sand feed enhances gravel transport (e.g., refs. 5, 8, 19, 20, 22, 25, and 26). We used two mixtures of finer and coarser sediment, both with grain size ratios of Dc/Df = 10 (Mixture 1: 0.5 and 5 mm, Mixture 2: 2 and 20 mm). If the enhanced mobility is the same between the two mixtures, we can conclude that the enhanced mobility of the coarser material is a purely geometric effect. If the differential mobility is greater in the finer mixture (Mixture 1) than the coarser mixture (Mixture 2), the effect of viscosity may play an important role (as outlined in ref. 22). We find the transport rate enhancement of 5 mm gravel caused by the addition of 0.5 mm sand is substantially larger than the corresponding transport enhancement of 20 mm gravel caused by the addition of 2 mm material. We can therefore conclude that the enhanced mobility of coarser material caused by the addition of finer material is not purely a geometric effect.

Our results confirm the theoretical prediction that the 2 mm division (shorthand for 1 to 5 mm) between gravel and sand is meaningful on Earth. Parker et al. (23) showed that D = 2 mm translates to D* = 51 [assuming R = 1.65 (quartz), g = 9.81 m/s2 (Earth) and ν = 1.0 × 10−6 m2/s (water at 20 °C)]. Our experiments confirm that sediment sizes with D* < 51 behave differently than sediment with D* > 51. If finer sediment with D* < 51 is added to a coarser gravel, the viscous and geometric effects should together enhance the mobility of coarser gravel. If D* > 51 the viscous effect should be negligible, and the enhancement of coarser sizes should be less. Our observation should occur universally with the threshold for the viscous effect occurring at D* = 51 but will translate to a different size on other planets. On Mars, the dimensional grain size threshold would be larger (~3 mm) and on planets with higher gravitational acceleration, it would be lower.

Lamb and Venditti (14) indicated a role for viscosity in grain size sorting because whether the flow is hydraulically rough or smooth affects the suspension threshold and therefore the amount of sand on the bed. The Parker et al. (27) theory suggests that in the case of sand-gravel mixtures, a shift in flow from turbulent rough to transitionally turbulent smooth as sand content increases in the bed, so enhancing the mobility of coarser material. It is reasonable to expect that this effect increases smoothly with increasing sand content, in accord with the Wilcock-Crowe (8) relation for sediment transport of mixtures. In that relation, reference Shields number decreases smoothly with increasing sand content in the bed.

Methods

The experiments were conducted in a sediment feed flume in the Mountain Channel Hydraulic Experimental Laboratory at the University of British Columbia. The flume is 6 m long, 0.5 m wide, and 0.9 m deep. Two mixtures of natural sediment grains, each consisting of two grain sizes, were used. Mixture 1 consists of 0.5 mm (ranged 0.35 to 0.7 mm) and 5 mm (4 to 5.6 mm) grains. Mixture 2 consists of 2.0 mm (1.4 to 2.8 mm) and 20 mm (16 to 22 mm) grains. The initial slope of the flume was set at 0.5%. The feed rate was set according to the Ashida and Michiue formula (29). The calculated capacity feed rate was 10.83 g/m/s and 86.59 g/m/s for Mixture 1 and Mixture 2, respectively. Sediment exiting the flume was trapped by a 0.25 mm mesh screen basket that would collect material over a 15-min period, and then its contents would be weighed and sieved to determine sediment transport rates and grain size distributions. Collected experimental data included centerline water and bed surface profiles, sediment transport rates of each grain size range, bed surface grain size distributions, and bed surface digital elevation models (DEMs). The data were used to calculate bed and water surface slopes. Water depth was calculated by differencing water surface and bed profiles, then averaging over the flume. Bed shear stress was estimated using the water surface slope and the hydraulic radius. The photos were used to estimate the bed surface grain size distribution. A total of 23 experiments with different sand and gravel feed mixtures were conducted. Each experiment began with a 0.10 m layer of loose gravel (5 mm or 20 mm, corresponding to the coarse phases of Mixture 1 and Mixture 2, respectively) with a flat surface. The coarser phase (5 mm for Mixture 1 and 20 mm for Mixture 2) was fed into the system at a constant rate. This feed rate was maintained until the sediment output at the end was nearly equal to the sediment input. Once this “equilibrium” was met, the finer phase (0.5 mm for Mixture 1 and 2 mm for Mixture 2) was added at a given rate (SI Appendix, Table S1). The total amount of sediment fed into the system was held constant throughout most of the experiments.
