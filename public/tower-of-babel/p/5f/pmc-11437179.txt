Survey-based qualitative exploration of user perspectives on the Philips Visual Patient Avatar in clinical situation management
Authors: Indre Radaviciute, Cynthia A. Hunn, Justyna Lunkiewicz, Petar Milovanovic, Jan F. Willms, Christoph B. Nöthiger, Emanuela Keller, David W. Tscholl, Greta Gasciauskaite
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Philips Visual Patient Avatar is an innovative approach to patient monitoring. Computer-based simulation studies have shown that it can improve diagnostic accuracy and confidence while reducing perceived workload. Following its integration into clinical practice, we conducted a single-centre qualitative study at the University Hospital Zurich to explore the views of anaesthesia, post-anaesthesia and intensive care providers on their experience with the technology. We used an online survey to assess its contributions in different clinical situations. We analysed the data thematically to identify key themes. Of the 510 healthcare providers contacted, 131 (25.7%) completed the survey and 154 comments were collected. Key themes included the detection of specific vital sign changes, focusing on temperature and oxygen saturation (41.9%, 34/81 comments in the operating room; 38.6%, 17/44 comments in the intensive care unit; 10.3%, 3/29 comments in the post-anaesthesia care unit). Additionally, the technology was perceived to support daily routines and situational awareness (28.4%, 23/81 comments in the OR; 9.1%, 4/44 comments in the ICU; 10.3%, 3/29 comments in the PACU). The study provides early, but strong evidence that the Philips Visual Patient Avatar assists healthcare providers in specific clinical situations in the perioperative and critical care settings.

Introduction

Patient monitoring is essential in perioperative and critical care settings, where early detection of pathological changes is necessary to identify and treat medical conditions1,2. While advances in information technology have greatly improved patient monitoring, they also pose challenges to clinicians due to the increasing cognitive load caused by the density of information and the complexity of interpreting vital signs3,4. The conventional approach to patient monitoring, where individual parameters are measured by a single sensor and displayed as discrete numerical values or waveforms, can create a mismatch between the amount of data available and the human ability to extract meaningful insights from it5,6. This can have a negative impact on the situational awareness of healthcare professionals, with poor situational awareness accounting for approximately 80% of treatment errors in anaesthesia and critical care7,8.

A user-centred design combines multiple parameters from different sensors into a single visual display, making the data more intuitive to view and interpret9,10. It considers the principles of human visual information processing and integrates patient monitoring data based on the brain's innate ability to quickly recognize changes in colour, motion and shape11–13. An example of a user-centred visualization technology designed to enhance situational awareness is the Philips Visual Patient Avatar (Royal Philips, Amsterdam, The Netherlands) developed at the University Hospital Zurich, Switzerland. The technology provides information on various vital signs through changes in colour, shape, and motion. The visual cues presented by the Visual Patient are designed to closely mimic reality while remaining simple enough to create an intuitive user interface that requires minimal training. Several computer-based and high-fidelity simulation studies have demonstrated that the use of the Visual Patient Avatar improves perception, verbalization, and diagnostic confidence in critical situations, ultimately leading to greater situational awareness14–16. Additionally, Visual Patient has been found to reduce perceived workload and increase performance in the presence of distractions14,16,17.

The recent introduction of the Visual Patient Avatar into clinical practice necessitates an examination of its use in the real world. This study aimed to investigate the impact of this novel technology in clinical practice, immediately after its first introduction, by gathering feedback from anaesthesia, post-anaesthesia care, and critical care providers on the specific clinical scenarios where the Philips Visual Patient Avatar has demonstrated its performance.

Methods

Approval and consent

The Ethics Committee of the Canton of Zurich, Switzerland, reviewed the study protocol and issued a declaration of non-jurisdiction (Business Management System for Ethics Committees number Req-2021-00756), which confirms that all methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines, regulations and in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. When accessing the survey, participants were informed of the scientific purpose of the survey and were assured that all responses would be anonymous and not linked to any identifying information. Participation was voluntary and without compensation. An informed consent was obtained from all participants. Participants consented by clicking on the link, completing the survey, and submitting it.

Implementation of the Philips Visual Patient Avatar in the study centre

University Hospital Zurich, where the Philips Visual Patient Avatar was originally developed, introduced the technology in its operating rooms, post-anaesthesia care units and two intensive care units simultaneously in March 2023. The Philips Visual Patient Avatar has been integrated into conventional patient monitoring as a split-screen feature (Fig. 1). Before integrating the technology into clinical practice, educational sessions were held to introduce the concept to the team. First, we held lectures for the whole staff, including all professional roles, to present the theoretical concepts and key usage instructions of the technology. Secondly, we held bedside teaching sessions in smaller groups where practical aspects of the technology could be discussed in more detail. Additionally, an instructional video and a brief reference guide were made available on the study centre’s intranet (Appendices A and B), which was announced to all personnel by e-mail. These resources were intentionally designed for easy access and consultation to provide support if any aspects needed further clarification. Our research team was readily available to provide support and guidance as needed.Fig. 1Split-screen display with a combination of conventional monitoring and the Philips Visual Patient Avatar.

Study design

This was an investigator-initiated, single-centre, qualitative descriptive study conducted at the University Hospital Zurich, Switzerland during two consecutive weeks in June 2023, three months after the integration of the technology into real clinical practice. We aimed to investigate the specific clinical situations and collect real-life experience data where the Philips Visual Patient Avatar demonstrates its performance, according to the care providers who are newly using the technology. The study included an online questionnaire with a combination of open-ended and closed-ended questions. The online survey invitation was emailed to all anaesthesia providers and post-anaesthesia care units as well as neurosurgical intensive care unit healthcare professionals (attending physicians, residents, and nurses). It was only possible to complete the survey once from the same account.

Online questionnaire

The online survey was created using Google Forms (Alphabet Inc., Mountain View, CA, USA). The initial invitation was followed by a reminder after one week (Appendix C). The questionnaire consisted of two parts. The first part contained two open-ended questions designed to elicit free-text responses about the clinical situations in which the Philips Visual Patient Avatar was helpful and whether the learning material was sufficient. The second part contained five closed-ended questions designed to collect demographic information from the participants (age, gender, professional position, and years of experience in anaesthesia or critical care). Participants were not required to provide self-identifying information. Responses to potentially personally identifiable questions were grouped together for this purpose. For example, participants were not required to indicate their specific age, but only to select a specific age group. In all cases, participants were given the option not to answer certain questions and to leave the field blank. In addition, we assessed the participants' familiarity with the educational materials provided. The translated questionnaire can be found in Appendix D.

We began by defining the objectives of the survey and sought expert input to assess content validity. Members of the anaesthesia team not participating in the study reviewed the questionnaire, identifying issues with question phrasing, clarity, and response options, which led to revisions based on their recommendations. The online interface, usability and technical functionality of the questionnaire were pre-tested with a target group in a pilot study. The responses collected during this pre-testing phase were deleted before the survey was distributed to participants and were not included in the statistical analysis.

Data analysis

First, the collected statements were categorized by department: operating rooms, post-anaesthesia care units and neurosurgical intensive care unit. We used an online translator, DeepL (DeepL GmbH, Cologne, Germany), to translate the responses from the original German to English. All translated statements are included in Appendix E.

We used a six-step thematic analysis method18 to examine the open-ended questions and find the dominant themes. After a thorough review of the collected data, our research team met to share their overall impressions and explore potential thematic concepts derived from the collected responses. Participants' responses were reviewed separately by three team members, JL, DWT, and GG, who coded the statements independently. Any discrepancies that arose during the coding process were discussed and resolved in a joint discussion.

Demographic information was analysed using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA). Information was presented as numerical values with their respective percentage distributions or as medians with interquartile ranges.

A total of 154 statements were collected during the survey. We identified two main themes within the responses: “I have experienced situations where Philips Visual Patient helped me” (88/154, 57.1%) and “I have not experienced situations where Philips Visual Patient helped me” (65/154, 42.2%). One of the 154 comments (0.7%) was not assigned to any group and was described as not codable. We further categorized the situations in which Philips Visual Patient Avatar helped healthcare providers by department and the identified sub-themes.

We collected 81 statements from anaesthesia personnel in the operating room and grouped them into three distinct sub-themes: identifying changes in specific vital signs, supporting situational awareness in daily use, and entertainment value. Discussion

This single-centre, qualitative, descriptive study allowed us to determine whether the Philips Visual Patient Avatar supports anaesthesia, post-anaesthesia care and critical care providers in various clinical settings, including the operating room, post-anaesthesia care unit and intensive care unit, and in which specific real-world situations they find the technology most useful.

The key findings indicate that the Philips Visual Patient Avatar, when used as a split-screen feature alongside conventional monitoring, is perceived as a technology that complements the conventional display and provides a quick overview in the operating room, post-anaesthesia care and intensive care settings. In total, almost 60% of the participants’ statements indicated that they had already experienced situations where the Philips Visual Patient Avatar helped them in their clinical practice (73% in the operating room, 48% in the neurosurgical intensive care unit and 28% in the post-anaesthesia care unit). Participants indicated that the technology significantly contributes to the assessment of changes in certain vital signs, particularly temperature and oxygen saturation monitoring, ultimately increasing the efficiency of information transfer. It was also stated that the Avatar helps senior caregivers who are supervising several operating rooms and thus more than one patient at a time. As demonstrated in previous computer-based and high-fidelity simulation studies15,16,19, the incorporation of numerical and waveform data presented visually through changing colours, shapes, and animations, as seen in the Philips Visual Patient Avatar, has been shown to accelerate decision making, improve the accuracy of clinical diagnoses, reduce perceived workload, and increase perceived diagnostic confidence. These results can be attributed to the user-centred design of the technology, which supports the use of direct visual representations of data to enhance situational awareness9. The visual cues representing the various states of patient status were specifically designed to present vital sign changes that healthcare professionals can easily associate with real-world situations. To ensure that all users, regardless of their background or experience, can effectively utilize and interpret the visual cues provided by the Avatar, ongoing training and support is provided at the study centre. Nevertheless, the technology’s intuitive and minimalistic design is targeted at all user groups working in the perioperative setting and is easily learned, as stated by 31/36 participants in a previous study19. Other investigations have also shown that healthcare providers performed better in vital sign recall when using the Philips Visual Patient Avatar compared to conventional monitoring after only a short educational session comprising of an introductory video for the novel technology14,16.

Other key principles in the design of the Visual Patient technology include Wittgenstein's Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus20 and the NASA publication "On Organization of Information: Approach and Early Work" by Degani et al.21. Wittgenstein stated that a logical image should have meaningful similarities to the reality it is intended to represent20, as embodied in the Philips Visual Patient Avatar. Degani et al. emphasize that the pinnacle of "order and wholeness" is achieved by integrating all essential data into a unified display21. This method allows healthcare providers to quickly assess whether all parameters are within the normal range, providing immediate reassurance and reducing the cognitive load associated with examining each numeric value individually. While the synthesis and simplification of information transfer might give rise to concerns about a potential trade-off between the quality of the represented information and the accuracy or precision of the technology, the Philips Visual Patient Avatar is intended as a supplementary tool to the conventional display. Consequently, the technology does not compromise the accuracy or precision of the numeric or waveform presentations, as these are always accessible. Instead, the Avatar aims to enhance users’ situational awareness in the early stages of perception and guides them to the measurements in question when these are outside of the normal range.

Other technologies that have been integrated into real-world clinical practice and translate alphanumeric patient data into visual representations include: the Dynamic Lung from Hamilton Medical AG22, the Alarm Status Visualizer from Masimo Corporation23, and the HemoSight and Physiology Screen from Edwards Lifesciences24. While the listed visualization technologies aim to improve clinical workflow and enhance patient monitoring by targeting specific areas of interest such as lung function, alarm status, or hemodynamic state, the Philips Visual Patient Avatar is the first to provide a holistic representation of patient condition by integrating multiple data sources into a single, intuitive graphical avatar.

Interestingly, the positive impact of the avatar in the post-anaesthesia care unit was rather low. The results suggest that this is the only area of the study where only nurses responded. The more negative responses from the post-anaesthesia care unit may have been influenced by differences in professional roles. However, it is also important to note that educational efforts aimed at nurses to familiarize them with the new technology at the post-anaesthesia care unit were less effective than in the other settings. This was reflected in a higher rate of participants at the post-anaesthesia care unit who had not consulted either of the educational materials. As we did not analyse the responses on the basis of different professional roles in the operating room and intensive care unit, this would be an interesting area for future studies.

Furthermore, participants highlighted the role of technology in supporting daily clinical practice by enhancing situational awareness, particularly in managing high workloads and complex, high-stress situations. It is common to observe a decline in performance during high-pressure situations25,26, which may contribute to psychological distress28–30. Challenging clinical scenarios require healthcare providers to process a significant amount of information in a short period of time29, potentially leading to cognitive changes that impair concentration and memory, thereby reducing task effectiveness26–28. Recent studies have also indicated that when cognitive resources are limited, the avatar-based technology can help alleviate cognitive load14–16. These findings suggest that the Philips Visual Patient Avatar may enhance situational awareness in clinical settings. This is particularly pertinent in the domains of anaesthesiology and critical care, where a lack of situational awareness has been linked to a significant proportion of medical errors8. If healthcare professionals are able to utilise the technology effectively in real-world clinical settings, this technology may be a significant contributor to enhancing patient safety.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the study has the inherent limitations of qualitative research. Qualitative analysis provides a detailed description without attempting to assign frequencies to the characteristics identified in the data—rare and more common phenomena are given equal attention. Second, survey results can be affected by non-response31, self-selection32, information bias33, leading question/interviewer bias34. If certain individuals choose not to participate in a survey, their absence can result in an incomplete and potentially biased dataset. Various reasons may underlie non-response of certain participants, such as personal motivation or temporal capacity. Consequently, the findings might not accurately reflect the broader population, and this limitation can impact the extent to which the results can be generalized. Conversely, when individuals decide to participate based on their personal experiences, interests, or motivations, the sample may not adequately represent the entire target population. While we have strived to develop a reliable data collection tool to maximize generalizability, information bias remains a significant factor that can impact survey results. When an interviewer asks leading questions that can systematically influence the respondent's answers. This bias can arise from attempts to 'clarify' questions, or by emphasising certain words differently when interviewing cases compared to controls (or vice versa). While this survey focused on the assessment of situations where the avatar has been found to be useful to anaesthesia providers, a previous qualitative analysis of the opinions of care providers regarding the new technology included a comprehensive thematic analysis of their views on both its strengths and limitations35. Hereby, a key objective was to identify areas for improvement, as identified by users, with the aim of guiding the future development of the Visual Patient Avatar. This in-depth analysis was conducted in order to ensure that the technology aligns with the needs of healthcare providers. As the avatar inventors are part of the same institution where the survey was conducted, this could also influence participants' responses. Furthermore, although it was only possible to complete the survey once from the same account, we could not prevent multiple responses from the same participant logging in using the survey link and a different, e.g. non-institutional email address. In addition, our study included clinicians with demanding clinical responsibilities, which may have discouraged participation due to time constraints. This issue, together with non-response, self-selection and information bias, may have contributed to the rather low response rate. Finally, this study was conducted at a single university hospital. Perceptions of healthcare providers may differ in different settings.

Conclusion

The Philips Visual Patient Avatar can support caregivers in assessing multiple vital signs, especially temperature and oxygen saturation, and provides greater situational awareness in complex, high-stress daily situations. Almost 60% of participants reported that the Philips Visual Patient Avatar was helpful shortly after its clinical introduction, with 73% working in the operating room, 48% in the neurosurgical intensive care unit and 28% in the post-anaesthesia care unit. Although some respondents had not yet experienced situations in which the new technology had proved beneficial, these preliminary findings suggest the usefulness of the avatar in real-world clinical settings. Future research is essential to explore these issues further and to assess opportunities for improving the technology in more detail.
