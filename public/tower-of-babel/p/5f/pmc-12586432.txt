Development of the SAVE study protocol to use lived experience to assess the value of serious health-related suffering and palliative care
Authors: Afsan Bhadelia, Natalie Greaves, Svetlana V Doubova, Felicia M Knaul
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSuffering generated by life-limiting or life-threatening conditions is a complex phenomenon inextricably linked with human experience. Most of this suffering can be ameliorated with adequate access to palliative care that is unavailable in much of the world, especially low-resource settings. Exploration of the definitional boundaries of and needs pertaining to suffering due to illness suggests that patients and families assign specific importance to the compassionate and supportive aspects of palliative care. However, the intrinsic and personal value of suffering alleviation has not been systematically studied in low- and middle-income countries, where it is direly needed.MethodsSAVE (Suffering Alleviation and the Value of Experiential knowledge) protocol is developed based on deliberation of a global expert group on limitations of quantitative measurement of suffering, literature reviews, and piloting in two countries – Barbados and Mexico – to support evidence generation that is anchored by lived experience.ResultsThe SAVE study protocol presents a methodological blueprint for study design, implementation, and analysis to conduct research on the value assigned to alleviation of serious health-related suffering by multiple stakeholders, especially patients and non-professional caregivers. The protocol incorporates an instrument for data collection. It also provides guidance on qualitative approaches and their importance to understanding lived experience to strengthen health system performance assessment.ConclusionsExploration of suffering based on personal experience is primarily focused on high-income contexts and/or clinical care management. Evidence generated using the protocol, which focuses on responsiveness, can advance development of people-centered metrics and bolster the investment case to equitably expand access to palliative care worldwide. Importantly, it can shift priority-setting and practice towards high-value healthcare.Subject terms: Palliative care, Chronic pain

Introduction

What is of value to individuals, especially patients and families, is often unaccounted for in health system priority-setting and practice. Value assessments are generally conducted in economic evaluation to compare and prioritize health interventions for effective resource allocation. The value-based approach measures the value of patient health outcomes achieved relative to cost, promoting multidimensionality in value assessment1. Taking this further, incorporating the intrinsic and personal value of healthcare to patients, the primary user, through a focus on lived experience can provide important insights on the complexity of the patient experience. Yet, patient complexity and heterogeneity in the patient experience2 to comprehensively examine value remain at the periphery of health services research3.

Patient complexity captures the triple burden faced by patients in terms of experiencing the disease itself, healthcare navigation, as well as self-management of care and its corresponding workload4. Individual patient value on different aspects of care and caring, including demands of healthcare, related preferences and priorities when interfacing with the health system and to achieve and maintain health goals that individuals themselves value, is interconnected with value considerations at the health systems level. Patient value is notably affected by sociocultural, political, historical, environmental and structural factors and related equity implications, which must also be accounted for5,6.

Palliative care inherently applies a patient-centered approach with a focus on patient values, preferences, concerns, needs, and expectations to improve quality of life from point of diagnosis through the end of life7. Alleviation of suffering due to illness is a central goal of palliative care8. Within this context, palliative care offers an opportunity to understand and increase the value generated from healthcare. The Lancet Commission on Global Access to Palliative Care and Pain Relief presented an indicator – serious health-related suffering (SHS) – to understand the burden or number of individuals living with or dying due to life-limiting or life-threatening conditions commonly generating SHS and associated need for palliative care9. Over 73 million people in 2021 experienced physical and psychological symptoms associated with SHS and which could be ameliorated with adequate access to palliative care. Yet, over 80% of individuals experiencing SHS live in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) with little or no access to such care that could reduce their suffering10. The gap in access to palliative care and pain relief represents both a major global public health inequity and a huge burden of avoidable suffering, concentrated in LMICs9,10. To address the inequities in access to palliative care, universal access to effective, affordable, and quality palliative care services worldwide must be prioritized. Nevertheless, priority-setting methods to achieve this are limited and would be of use for health systems strengthening.

Examining the lived experience of patients and non-professional caregivers, who play a critical role in care and live through various aspects of the patient journey, is essential to understanding the value of alleviating SHS to improve priority-setting and addressing the gap in palliative care. Qualitative evidence on lived experience with suffering and the value of quality palliative care services is primarily from high-income countries and/or focused mainly on clinical care management11–16. As the experience of pain and suffering can be strongly influenced by social and structural factors at the individual, household, and community levels, variations in lived experience and the value of suffering alleviation through access to palliative care remain poorly understood in many parts of the world. Evidence on these issues is limited or nonexistent from LMIC and resource limited settings. While qualitative methodology is often superseded by quantitative evidence that misses much of the nuances of lived experience, palliative care research has highlighted the importance of capturing lived experience through qualitative methodology. Building on this to systematically capture evidence on lived experience with SHS across different dimensions of suffering and corresponding value of healthcare can guide systems level priority-setting, not only simply clinical practice.

This paper presents the methodological development of the SAVE (Suffering Alleviation and the Value of Experiential knowledge) study qualitative protocol through testing in two pilot countries – Barbados and Mexico – for application in additional sites and countries. The final protocol offers guidance and resources to investigate the value of suffering alleviation to individuals, especially based on the lived experience of patients in need of palliative care, to contribute to comprehensive evidence for priority-setting. It follows the recommendation of the Lancet Commission to garner patient preferences and values on suffering across contexts to promote people-centered healthcare and increase investment in and access to palliative care9. While the value of suffering alleviation may be considered intuitive, a nuanced understanding of this value by different individuals and communities can help reshape strategies and mechanisms for improving health outcomes.

Methods

The methodological considerations for developing the qualitative research protocol for the SAVE study are detailed in this section, including steps undertaken to pilot in two countries – Barbados and Mexico – and related revision. The detailed country specific implementation is/will be detailed in the respective country manuscripts17. The results section presents the final SAVE protocol for implementation in future SAVE studies or related research. The protocol is intended to serve as a guide for research focused on more systematically capturing lived experience on SHS for adaptation by context.

Qualitative studies on lived experience of patients and/or caregivers are often guided by interpretative tradition and undertake either thematic or phenomenological analysis18. To ensure that the most appropriate framework for analysis was employed to qualitatively examine the constructs of interest – SHS and the value of alleviating SHS, a detailed comparison of theoretical traditions was conducted. A description of approaches considered to collect ethnographic data on suffering is documented for reference, especially as a primer for researchers interested in learning about and adopting qualitative methods in their research on SHS but lacking systematic guidance (Supplementary Data 1).

The multidimensionality of suffering can be best understood from different perspectives and lived realities. While patients and non-professional caregivers may provide insights on the immediate value of suffering alleviation, healthy individuals can offer insights on the potential future value of suffering alleviation when suffering is hypothetically considered. Additional parameters for study design were based on qualitative expertise of the global research team and collating guidance from review of studies on lived experience with chronic illness.

Instrument selection for performance status screening and health-related quality of life

A literature review was conducted on existing instruments to assess performance status to determine relevant options for screening potential participants. Specifically, the key terms “performance status assessment” and “performance status measure” were searched in PubMed and Google Scholar. Further, another literature review was conducted using the key terms “health-related quality of life” and measure,” also in PubMed and Google Scholar, to identify instruments to assess HRQoL to compare qualitative descriptions of suffering with corresponding health status and ability to engage in activities of daily living as captured by validated metrics. There was no time limitation to the search, and the search prioritized instruments that had been validated across different populations and countries, and notably ones that were field tested or implemented in LMICs contexts. The overall purpose was to identify robust tools that are culturally appropriate. A threshold for use of screening instrument(s) was established based on review with clinical experts with general and specialist training, including in palliative care, and guidance in literature, as reported in the results and detailed in Supplementary Note 1 and Supplementary Note 2.

Semi-structured interview guide – development and piloting

Three steps were followed in developing the interview guide. First, existing evidence and gaps were identified or informed by a) a literature review intersecting the individual patient and caregiver perspectives with that of health policy and systems research; and b) prior discussions and deliberations that highlighted gaps and methodological limitations of quantitative measurement of the SHS burden held by the Lancet Commission. The Commission convened a group of 35 commissioners and over 50 scientific advisors spanning various fields including public health, medicine, nursing, law, economics, epidemiology, policy, and anthropology, as well as incorporating perspectives from each world region9. For the literature review, the terms lived experience, palliative care, suffering, patient, caregiver and value were searched in various combinations in the grey and published literature through Google Scholar and PUBMED. An outline of core topics of interest was identified based on overarching themes from literature and prior expert deliberation on the overarching area of serious health-related suffering. This was used to develop a preliminary list of questions. Second, the initial iteration of the guide was reviewed by a global study team and pretested with 5 individuals – two patients, a caregiver, and two healthy individuals followed by cognitive debriefing interviews.

Third, the revised guide was piloted in two countries. Conceptual and semantic equivalence was assessed through the pilot interviews (n = 23 in Barbados; n = 48 in Mexico). The base language of question development was U.S. English. Conceptual equivalence pertains to the key constructs under investigation – SHS and value of suffering alleviation for the SAVE study – having similar meaning across groups of individuals19. Semantic equivalence is of relevance in the translation of constructs into another language to ensure integrity in meaning of source text20. Pilot interviews were conducted as part of two initial studies implementing the initial SAVE methodology in Barbados (U.S. English/Bajan English) and Mexico (Spanish). The pilot interviews in Barbados were conducted by one researcher using U.S. English (AB) and another using Bajan English (NG) with cross-comparison. The pilot interviews in Mexico were conducted by a researcher bilingual in English and Spanish (SV) with cross-comparison discussions with researcher versed in U.S. English (AB). Additional adjustments were made to the overall semi-structured interview guide presented in the results through an iterative process as research occurred in each country. A semi-structured format was maintained for adaptation of questions as emergent in conversation with the participant and by querying topics as they are raised.

Results

Here we present the SAVE study protocol, including methodological guidance on and an instrument for research on serious-health related suffering based on lived experience.

Value of qualitative approaches in understanding lived experience with suffering for health policy and systems research

Based on review of existing research philosophies, design strategies and analysis methods (Supplementary Data 1) and as initial work within a broader research agenda, the SAVE pilot studies apply the interpretive paradigm and thematic analysis to identify common themes across participants on the constructs of interest – SHS and the value of alleviating SHS. Thematic analysis offers great flexibility as a method and is particularly useful as a first step. Additionally, constant comparison can facilitate detection of patterns of commonalities of experience across the dataset, adding rigor to the analytic process21.

The protocol includes assessment of health-related quality of life, as detailed below, alongside the core qualitative data collection. Overall, the work highlights the utility of blurring philosophical and methodological boundaries to address real-world complex problems that call for development of new approaches to inquiry22,23. Indeed, combining approaches can offer the opportunity for “‘turning the prism’ via methodological triangulation”, generate novel synergies, and promote “complementary and mutually enriching perspectives.”23

Research questions and aims

This SAVE study focuses on three research questions: 1) How is SHS defined/described and understood based on lived experience? 2) How do patients, non-professional caregivers, and healthy individuals value the alleviation of SHS? 3) What opportunities exist for health systems to enhance the value of palliative care?

The SAVE study aims to: 1) Identify dimensions of SHS and the value of alleviating SHS based on lived experience through multiple perspectives – patients with a SHS associated condition, non-professional caregivers, and healthy individuals; and 2) Examine personal, interpersonal, social, structural, organizational, procedural, and contextual and/or circumstantial factors that influence the value of alleviating SHS.

Moreover, it is hypothesized that patients, non-professional caregivers, and healthy individuals greatly value the alleviation of suffering, and this added patient value can contribute to decision-making, including through metrics of suffering averted, in ways that will strengthen the capacity of health systems to improve patient-centeredness.

Participant population and sampling

Three core participant categories are identified as critical to understanding the patient or prospective patient experience with SHS: 1) patients with diagnosis of one or more SHS associated condition, 2) non-professional caregivers of patients with diagnosis of one or more SHS associated condition, 3) healthy individuals who do not have a diagnosis of an SHS associated condition and meet prespecified inclusion criteria to incorporate perceptions of suffering from individuals without current diagnosis of illness but who nevertheless may be at risk of a SHS associated condition in the future (see Box 1 for recommended criteria). Healthy individuals can offer the closest pre-illness perspective on SHS as well as provide a chance to capture barriers and concerns related to interfacing with the health system if the need to address SHS did emerge. All three categories can offer unique insights on suffering in terms of its impact or potential impact on health security – a sense of certainty in terms of healthcare and in this case, palliative services to alleviate such suffering for patients themselves – now or in the future – and their family members24. A fourth potential participant category – providers involved in the provision of palliative services – may be considered relevant, however, this participant category is not highlighted in this study given the focus on lived experience, notably individuals experiencing the health system currently or expecting to in the future as patients or non-professional caregivers.

Nonprobabilistic sampling, specifically purposive sampling is recommended to achieve the study aims based on four features or options of such sampling: 1) opportunity to target and identify individuals experiencing the phenomenon of interest25 – SHS; 2) permitted sampling emphasis on similarity and to select all cases meeting pre-established criteria26; 3) permitted sampling emphasis on maximum variation sampling to ensure that a diversity of perspectives is captured for identification of patterns and related significance as emerging from a heterogeneous sample26; and 4) permitted non-specific emphasis in sampling to include emergent opportunities for data collection27. The relevant purposive sampling approach can be used based on alignment with the aims of the specific study. An iterative approach of sampling and resampling28 is necessary for balancing theoretical29, thematic30, and meaning saturation30 without over recruitment and overburdening of vulnerable population groups. Sampling needs to ensure that the opportunity to participate in the study is not limited by race, ethnicity, religion, gender identification, sexual orientation, geography, or any other differentiating characteristic. As such, the sampling frame will need be based on review of representativeness of target populations, equity concerns (explicit inclusion of marginalized and minoritized communities and individuals in low socioeconomic strata), and feasibility of consistent implementation across countries.

Assessment of performance status for screening and health-related quality of life of participants

Participants will need to be of cognitive, emotional, and functional status to engage in the study. Based on review of the literature, two of the most commonly used participant screening instruments to assess performance status31, generally completed by providers, are the Karnofsky Performance Scale (KPS)32 and the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) Performance Status Scale (PSS)33. A comparison of their validity and reliability has generated either overall comparable results, with KPS faring slightly better in certain assessment34, or demonstrated variability in inter-rater reliability across instruments when including the Palliative Performance Scale (PPS), indicating challenges in determining the more accurate assessment of performance status35. Notably, additional studies cite discrepancies in ECOG PSS ratings when conducted by providers versus patients36,37. Additional instruments focused on specific populations, such as children or elderly include the Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA)38 and the Lansky Play-Performance Scale (adapted KPS for 1-16 year olds)39, respectively. The Functionality Assessment Flowchart (FAF) is a relatively newer instrument and despite high agreement between observers requires validation worldwide40. Limitations of performance status instruments include reliability and validity of self-examination when patients report41,42 and insufficient accounting of psychological factors43. Further, the prospects of using electronic activity monitoring devices as an alternative have been highlighted in the literature44. However, the cost implications, especially in LMIC contexts, and the need for patient familiarity and compliance with activity monitoring devices are cited concerns for use in the SAVE study.

Based on literature findings and the wider range of functional status options offered by the KPS to capture variations and given applicability for adult populations, KPS was selected as the instrument for screening prospective participants. Moreover, KPS has been used across varied settings, including Barbados and Mexico45–47. There are validated instruments to capture aspects of lived experience. With the purpose of assessing health-related quality of life (HRQoL) of selected participants through a validated instrument alongside the core qualitative data collection, EQ-5D-5L was selected. As such, the use of a patient reported outcome measure (PROM), notably EQ-5D-5L, was specifically incorporated for data triangulation and for complementarity with the qualitative data to describe the sample. Constructs within EQ-5D-5L were expanded upon through the qualitative data collection. The EQ-5D-5L questionnaire is a brief and short form, self-reported multi-attribute measure of overall health status across and specifically, HRQoL focused on five dimensions – mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression. Its use for the SAVE study is based on several considerations and criteria. Specifically, this quality-of-life assessment tool: 1) correlates well in comparison to instruments focused on symptoms amenable to palliative care such as pain48, 2) strong psychometric properties and validation in 25 countries across conditions, populations and country income groups49–51, including Mexico52,53 and with the full EQ-5D version validated in Barbados54 (the two countries where SAVE study was piloted and based on which this protocol for future studies was refined), 3) serves as a standard tool for comparison across study countries, 4) is available in multiple languages following standardized translation protocol in accordance with international guidelines for use across study sites worldwide55, and 5) poses low respondent burden. Any descriptive information provided by participants in administration of the EQ-5D-5L can be captured to further assess the qualitative findings of the study. Validated instruments assessing different dimensions of suffering, often used for screening in clinical settings, can be administered alongside the EQ-5D-5L to further examine the qualitative findings56–64.

Sample size and recruitment

Sample size determination in qualitative research commonly follows the principle of saturation–emergence of no new data with categories or themes reaching a point of repetition–as assessed through an iterative process with constant comparison29,65,66. This is the recommended approach for the SAVE study. Alternatives are presented later as potential future directions. SAVE study recruitment is recommended through a wide range of outlets–e.g., healthcare facilities, community centers and standard lists – to avoid selection bias and until saturation is achieved to only engage participants to the point of meeting study aims. Prospective participants identification is recommended with predetermined sampling inclusion and exclusion criteria (Box 1) and screening of prospective participants using a pre-established KPS threshold or alternate performance scale cut-off. For KPS and based on a proposed evaluation system of examining symptoms, need for assistance, and the level that the patient is bedridden67, a score of either 30 and above (Mexico) or 40 and above (Barbados) was determined as a threshold for the SAVE study to ensure that the patient could potentially participate without the need for active support. The KPS score of patients should ideally be provider reported given that it has strong predictors of identifying need for healthcare services such as the emergency room68, whereas caregivers and healthy individuals may self-report if KPS is included in screening this participant category. As the key focus of the research is on patients in need of palliative care, a minimum threshold for the KPS is set which includes patients who are disabled or severely disabled requiring “special care and assistance” but where “death is not imminent.”32 The application of sampling criteria will vary based on context, however, the core principle grounding the suggested criteria (Box 1) is to avoid undue harm to patients who may not have the functional status to participate while meeting the aims of the research as detailed in Supplementary Note 1.

In application, recruitment will require active communication of participation criteria (e.g., research information sheets, posters, and/or pamphlets) with collaborating healthcare facilities and community partners for open enrollment in community settings. For example, diagnosis of an SHS condition is best confirmed by the provider based on medical records. However, patients may respond to and express interest in participating based on research materials shared in community settings. As such, their diagnosis would be self-reported unless an explicit effort is made to restrict recruitment of patients to healthcare facilities and communicated as such in study materials that seek to recruit participants. The SHS conditions of focus (e.g., cancer, diabetes) and related specifications (e.g., type of cancer or whether type I and type I diabetes) will need to be explicitly detailed in the criteria. The pilot research in Mexico, for example, specified the criteria for enrollment of patients as diagnosis of advanced-stage cancer (ICD-10: C00–C97) or diabetes (ICD-10: E10–E11)17. Another consideration for applying the sampling criteria pertains to recruitment of caregivers. This may include anyone who is a nonprofessional caregiver or can be specified and limited to primary caregivers, as was done with the Mexico pilot study17.

Data collection

Semi-structured individual in-depth interviews (IDIs) and focus group discussions (FGDs) are the recommended mode of data collection to explore lived experience with SHS with a duration of approximately 60–90 min from point of greeting to close of interview, inclusive of the informed consent process and all other study procedures. While IDIs and FGDs can range from 30 min to several hours69, an upper limit of 90 min is established to balance respondent burden of individuals experiencing SHS alongside time necessary for qualitative data collection to reach saturation with the former taking priority. For FGDs, a range of 6–12 participants is generally suggested70.

Evidence indicates that use of phone interviews can increase opportunities for reaching underrepresented groups in research71 and virtual participation such as through Zoom has been overall rated above other alternatives as well as being cost-effective in terms of study costs, offering the potential for greater convenience and comfort for participants72,73. While the use of these alternatives became a necessity during the COVID-19 pandemic, studies both prior to and during the pandemic have reported similar to or better results than, in some cases, in-person data collection, including in research on mental health studies with vulnerable populations72,74,75. Considering the focus of the SAVE study on the complex and potentially sensitive topic of suffering, alternatives to face-to-face should be considered based on context and related implications. Notably, empathetic, and compassionate listening of concerns, particularly emotional distress, are necessary and extra vigilance is required when interviews are conducted by phone or virtually76,77.

The EQ-5D-5L questionnaire can be completed by the participant or administered by the interviewer after the IDI alongside collection of demographic information. If participants provide their own descriptions of or reactions to the EQ-5D-5L categories and/or their rationales for response selection while completing the questionnaire, these could be captured for potential qualitative analysis. Key demographic variables of interest, though not exhaustive, are age, sex, gender, education, race/ethnicity, religion, income, occupation, and marital status. Participant category (e.g., patient, caregiver) data will need to be confirmed. Health condition, healthcare access and utilization information of relevance and to collect includes primary condition of patient, co-morbidities, duration since diagnosis, healthcare access and/or health insurance status, and use and frequency of use of healthcare services, including palliative care. Additional variables may be relevant to collect based on context.

Additional considerations for data collection are presented in Supplementary Note 1 and Supplementary Note 2, including participant information and input, personnel and training, data collection modality and scheduling, supplementary data collection through ED-5D-5L and on demographics, and participant compensation. Further, potential biases that can emerge during research implementation and methods to address them are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Qualitative instrument

The semi-structured qualitative instrument and discussion guide, (Supplementary Data 2) outlines a list of topics and questions that can be explored with each participant or group of participants69. It has been developed to capture information on core terminologies and their definitions, experiential aspects of SHS and its alleviation, valuation of suffering alleviation, knowledge, attitudes and perceptions around palliative care, utilization of such services, health system considerations, comparative assessment of suffering experienced in society, contextual factors (e.g., sociocultural, historical, political) that may influence the experience of or valuation of suffering, as well as specific questions on caregiver burden and its impact on suffering.

The semi-structured guide provides the foundation for better understanding of SHS. Data collection is intended to follow an iterative process where initial points of an interview will determine the subsequent sequence and scope of questions to explore emergent topics. Not all questions are likely to be covered in any given session and the experiential journey of the participant should be the primary guide. Questions not serving the scope of the research in practice (how participants interpret or respond to certain questions) may be dropped. The order of the questions may also vary based on response to initial questions. Further, additional questions may be added as data collection proceeds and researchers learn more about the phenomenon of interest to further inquire on particular aspects highlighted by the participants. The conceptual parameters and definitions of relevant reference terms are listed in Table 1 as a resource for researchers, to train interviewers, and to guide data collection for certain aspects.

The final guide resulting from the pretest process and piloting in Barbados and Mexico includes modifications to account for varying interpretations of key concepts and to ensure that they do not diverge from the intended meaning. Further, missing areas identified better capture the narrative of suffering in its complexity and nuance. For example, SHS is deconstructed to suffering due to illness for conceptual and semantic equivalence as well as to ensure comprehension across education levels. The need for reference definitions, such as for palliative care, is made evident due to limited awareness of this type of care.

Prompts on valuation, including comparative assessments at the societal level, are reframed and additional questions to capture both monetary and non-monetary valuation of suffering alleviation are included. On the latter, specifically on participants’ willingness to pay to avert suffering. Willingness to pay data is primarily collected through quantitative surveys with incremental increase in values up to a certain threshold, e.g., a percentage or multiplier of GDP per capita, monthly or annual income, or pre-set benchmark78–81. However, qualitative data can offer informative insights to guide development of surveys or in general, e.g. defining affordability may be more pertinent information for action than specific value thresholds.

In review with caregivers, further clarification was necessitated to differentiate professional and non-professional caregiving, and the need for caregiver-specific questions made evident to explore an added dimension of suffering. Importantly, piloting has made clear that this type of research inquiry is rare but deeply appreciated by participants, especially those who feel neglected by the health system and do not have opportunities to express their concerns or to provide input.

Ethical considerations

All required aspects for the ethical conduct of research, including an informed consent process, voluntary and non-coerced participation, and protection of privacy and confidentiality should be adhered to. Specifically, this includes adherence to established international principles such as the Helsinki Principles and principles highlighted in the Belmont Report82,83. It is tantamount that research examining lived experience intends and seeks to do no harm. Ethical considerations and adequate risk-benefit analyses must be conducted in adapting and implementing the presented methodology. Moreover, proposed research should undergo formal review through a recognized institutional ethics board or panel and approval should be acquired prior to commencing any research activities.

Any research on this area must recognize the vulnerability of participants who are suffering and will need to ensure relevant interviewer training and have appropriate competencies to avoid any unintended harm. While the process of recounting the experience of suffering and expressing the value of suffering alleviation may generate some psychological discomfort, this research does not directly prompt sensitive topics and rather focuses on general prompts. Given the nature of the research topic, this may lead to discussion of memories and/or experiences of discomfort, pain, or other forms of distress. As such, it is critical that any emergent sensitive topics are monitored, participants are consulted on how they feel, are provided the autonomy to decide on whether they wish to continue, are provided access to timely psychosocial support as needed as well as reassured of confidentiality. A distress protocol is recommended such as the Qualitative Research Distress Protocol84 or the Research Interview and Distress Protocol85 for research on potentially sensitive issues such as suffering. Following consensus guidelines on conducting qualitative research with palliative and end-of-life care patients is recommended86,87.

The SAVE study is not expected to generate any direct benefits to participants. However, by partaking in related studies and sharing their experiences about SHS and the value of alleviating it, participants can inform patient-centered valuation of suffering alleviation to advance comprehensive evidence for priority-setting. This can help inform the design and reform of health systems through advocacy and policy changes, especially with respect to improving access to palliative care and pain relief, which participants may themselves benefit from in the future. Therefore, participants can play an important role in indirectly contributing to the health systems strengthening process, specifically one that advances patient- and people-centeredness, and to potentially improve access to and outcomes from palliative care. The study can allow participants a platform to express their voices and experience of engaging in the study may empower participants. This can potentially initiate or expand patient and caregiver advocacy efforts by participants.

Other ethical considerations include minimal potential undue harm due to the opportunity cost, logistics and/or other aspects of interfacing with the study. Acquiring consent from individuals who lack capacity should follow established international norms and available guidelines such as those developed by the Council for International Organizations of Medical Sciences (CIOMS), which are also an important reference for other considerations pertinent to research on serious health-related suffering that involves human subjects88. Further ethical considerations for data collection are presented in Supplementary Note 1 and Supplementary Note 2.

Analysis

The thematic analysis of qualitative data is recommended to be informed by the interpretivist philosophical paradigm based on appropriateness for research on lived experience with SHS (Supplementary Data 1). The constant comparative method – joint coding and analysis – can permit emergence of information throughout the analytic process89. A mixed deductive and inductive coding approach is recommended. As such, the transcripts can be subject to coding and concept mapping with the use of a preliminary codebook with a priori codes based on literature. This coding can be complemented with emergent codes, especially focusing on in vivo codes to directly capture participant’s voices, producing a continually expanding code list to reach saturation. An interactive process of coding can combine like codes, produce conceptual categories, and identify thematic patterns. Multiple independent coders should be employed to conduct collaborative coding with discussion and data triangulation for consistency in coding. At least two coders are needed for data triangulation to promote rigor and trustworthiness in the analysis90,91. It is critical that researchers practice reflexivity throughout the research process to minimize bias, including subjective interpretation of data92.

The evidence collected can be used to generate recommendations by health system function (stewardship, financing, resource generation, and service delivery). Descriptive statistical analysis of demographic data and data collected from the EQ-5D-5L questionnaire is suggested. The latter can be distinctly reported. Any detailed descriptions of the EQ-5D-5L categories (e.g., mobility or self-care) or participant status related to these categories reported alongside selection of a response option can be coded with other qualitative data.

Strengths and limitations

The concept of SHS is a complex phenomenon with widespread perspectives and interpretations lodged in the culture, history, and context that cannot be fully captured with one methodology or perhaps ever captured, which could be viewed as an overarching limitation of this study. Nevertheless, this does not mean that the effort should not be undertaken to improve our understanding of SHS. Multifaceted transdisciplinary approaches are necessary, especially to examine the heterogeneity of experience within any specific stakeholder group, e.g., patients, combined with the impact of intersectionality on the experience of suffering. Gaining a granular understanding of the phenomenon of interest requires acknowledging that research of this nature cannot be generalizable without building a substantial and rigorous repository of experiential knowledge worldwide and across diverse population groups. A core strength is the effort to build such a repository. Moreover, the SAVE protocol is designed to focus on adult populations. Additional considerations and appropriate study procedures would need to be implemented for use among vulnerable populations such as children.

The literature review of instruments to inform the protocol developed is based on a pragmatic approach with the aim of identifying robust instruments that have been applied and/or validated across different contexts and may be culturally appropriate in varied contexts. As such, PRISMA or other reporting guidelines for systematic review have not been applied. While the selected instruments presented in the protocol – KPS for screening and the brief EQ-5D-5L on HRQoL for data triangulation – have been validated in multiple contexts, additional tools may offer an opportunity for more nuanced assessment of functional capacity and HRQoL, respectively. Systematic processes to identify and assess psychometric properties of patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), such as those following PRISMA and COSMIN (COnsensus-based Standards for the selection of health Measurement INstruments) guidelines, are recommended for future research.

Discussion

This methodological paper presents the development of the SAVE study protocol, which highlights opportunities for monumental changes in how healthcare is prioritized, designed, and implemented. The protocol simultaneously responds to the call for greater value of and visibility for qualitative approaches to health and healthcare93. It offers guidance on systematic data collection and analysis following relevant qualitative research paradigms to harmonize varied approaches in current literature. It also places fundamental importance on capturing the voices of marginalized and minoritized populations who have experienced structural violence in healthcare to identify the added value of suffering alleviation in these communities. Indeed, the pain and suffering of communities experiencing historical trauma have not been given equal importance.

Failing to incorporate value into health systems analysis and response in a comprehensive manner can cause inadvertent negative consequences for those that these systems seek to serve. This is especially relevant for an area such as suffering that is so central to the trajectory of illness, from symptomatic disease through end of life, for both patients and non-professional caregivers. Palliative care can be delivered at low-cost ($3 per capita) and in most cases, at the primary care level, and yet it is not available to most people experiencing SHS worldwide9. Strengthening the investment case for palliative care to alleviate SHS requires novel evidence. The intrinsic and personal value derived from healthcare as reported by the intended beneficiaries – patients and their caregivers – and understood through their lens can provide such breakthrough evidence.

It is important to incorporate the lived experience and expertise of patients and non-professional caregivers in research. This is important not only in terms of what is researched but also how it is researched. Patient stories can capture and uncover illness trajectories, relevant nuances and value considerations, evolving values over the course of illness, and preferences therein, as well as related demands on the daily experience of living with or caring for someone living with an illness or disability4,94. Patient resiliency is reduced by the cumulative demands of care for co-morbidities and related workload95, which would exacerbate the experience of suffering. In addition, the perspective of individuals living without a life-limiting or life-threatening condition, and who could be considered healthy overall, can offer the benefit of examining the value trajectory prior to illness to link with the perspectives or patients and non-professional caregivers garnered through illness. Moreover, suffering can be indicative of structural causes and implications.

Research such as that advanced by the SAVE protocol can help not only pinpoint fractures and fissions that generate inequities but identify opportunities for ameliorating misguided health systems. While the study team developing the present protocol included lived experience expertise, future SAVE studies can seek to systematically engage individuals with lived experience in the design of research through participatory approaches that ensure equal partnership and following established guidance such as the World Health Organization’s framework for meaningful engagement of individuals with lived experience96,97. In low-resource settings where there is both limited knowledge regarding palliative care and limited access to palliative care, it may be an iterative process to effectively engage lived experience expertise, especially from within the context in which the research is being conducted. However, the ultimate goal should be the co-creation of knowledge and in doing so, acknowledging and affording the same respect to knowledge gained from lived experience as to technical knowledge98,99. Meaningful engagement and full integration of lived experience expertise is vital to the advancement of high-value and in turn, high-quality health systems. This is of particular relevance to responsiveness of health systems to the needs of different population sub-groups, especially minoritized and marginalized communities100–107.

The SAVE protocol offers an evidence-based and tested methodology to expand the call for research on this important area of work within the health sphere and for high-value healthcare108. As such, the SAVE protocol is intended as a blueprint for future research with content and methodological elements that can be locally adapted within respective study protocols. Application of the SAVE protocol in numerous countries, especially LMICs, can help build a global database on lived experience with SHS and the value of alleviating this suffering. The adaptation of the protocol across sociocultural and health systems settings will require locally anchored and driven efforts to ensure equivalency of content in different languages and to contextualize interpretations of suffering and its alleviation within the cultural and historical context. Specifically, and as was done with the pilot studies, the protocol will need to be adapted into the local language (Spanish for Mexico) and to account for core terminologies in languages variations (Bajan creole for Barbados). Some aspects of the protocol may require additional adaptation, including in settings where palliative care is isolated or non-existent. Further, to promote the inclusion of ethnic minorities, steps must be undertaken to include individuals who do not speak the primary language in the country, including through the use of interpreters and translation of study materials.

Future studies could consider methodological adjustments to data collection and analysis. For example, additional research may include patient-caregiver dyads to understand shared experiences or a third joint perspective to examine experiential distinctions, including patient and caregiver burdens109,110. Interviewing independently and jointly, instead of only jointly can ensure that one participant’s voice is not dominant and avoid confirmation bias. Ongoing discourse on systematically applying the principle of saturation is another avenue for methodological adjustment. For example, sample size determination and assessment of saturation can be guided by the information power model to follow the content of input rather than the magnitude of input111,112. Discourse on applying certain quantitative methods such as statistical modeling or empirical tests in qualitative research to establish an a priori estimate of saturation is also ongoing66. Transparency in this process is important for research integrity and replicability.

While not yet common, the use of combined qualitative analytic approaches is offered in the literature to promote multi-layered examination of a phenomenon and to promote analytic pluralism113,114. Indeed, a growing number of studies are using more than one qualitative approach on the same dataset to aid in understanding complex realities113–119. For example, interpretative phenomenological analysis (IPA) focuses on individual level analysis, only entailing the use of IDIs (not FGDs), and seeks to comprehensively understand an individual’s experience of an event or phenomenon while acknowledging the subjective nature of suffering120,121. IPA is of relevance to understanding pain and suffering by avoiding theoretical preconceptions and following an iterative “sense-making” process122. Given the advantage of applying IPA and thematic analysis on the same data113, research on suffering could be further anchored in lived experience and interpretation of that experience as it makes sense to the participant within their context. Dual analysis can examine shared realities across participants and explore individual level accounts, characteristics, and personal perceptions of suffering, including their underlying structure123. This could be especially helpful in examining value of alleviating SHS from individual and health systems levels, recognizing the challenges of simultaneously applying two approaches due to additional time, resources, and training required. This is, however, a promising area of future methodological discourse.

Based on an initial body of evidence on value of suffering alleviation, future research may also be informed by applying transformative participatory approaches to further advance a social justice and action-oriented agenda. This may be challenging at the onset, especially in LMIC contexts where there is limited access to palliative care and understanding of what such care ideally entails. Indeed, one of the aims of the SAVE study is to promote awareness and discussion as well as identify opportunities for engagement around alleviation of suffering through palliative care.

While the SAVE protocol is in alignment with the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) checklist to appraise qualitative research, future studies may consider explicit use of the COREQ (COnsolidated criteria for REporting Qualitative research) checklist for detailed reporting on qualitative design, methods, and analysis124. Other appraisal tools of relevance include the evaluation tool for qualitative studies (ETQS) and the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) tool125. As future studies apply the SAVE protocol, researchers are encouraged to report on practical methodological considerations, including the strengths and limitations of using proposed methods as well as relevant examples as they emerge within their respective research and research contexts.

The presented methodology builds on the recommendations of the Lancet Commission and is one step in a broader research agenda to develop a composite patient-preference-weighted measure of suffering averted - suffering-intensity-adjusted life year (SALY) - to compare interventions and to complement existing metrics to further strengthen priority-setting and practice9. Suffering is a particularly abstract concept that may not be as easily measured or observed as other latent constructs. Instruments to guide data collection on SHS may be hampered by a lack of awareness on palliative care in many contexts given the limited access to such care in most parts of the world. While the SAVE protocol has some gaps, it is an attempt to contribute to the science around suffering alleviation and the value designated to it by those experiencing SHS. Through a dominant focus on the value of qualitative research, it offers the opportunity to contribute to evidence and practice focused on and grounded by lived experience93. This SAVE protocol promotes knowledge co-generation to strengthen health systems to achieve universal palliative care and calls on collaborative partnerships to build a global dataset on lived experience with SHS. Indeed, condition-specific adaptation is being explored or reflected on for different cancer types126,127 as may be done with other conditions, countries, and/or based on other considerations.
