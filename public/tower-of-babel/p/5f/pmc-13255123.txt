Sagittal Alignment as a Determinant of Pain and Functional Recovery after Lumbar Surgery: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Tomasz Tykocki, Łukasz Rakasz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background Sagittal alignment is increasingly recognized as a key determinant of functional recovery after lumbar spine surgery; however, its role in degenerative lumbar disease remains variably reported. While alignment principles are well-established in adult spinal deformity, their applicability to routine degenerative procedures has not been quantitatively synthesized. Methods A systematic review and meta-analysis of 23 original clinical studies (1,837 adults) was performed. Eligible studies reported postoperative sagittal or spinopelvic parameters―pelvic incidence (PI)-lumbar lordosis (LL) mismatch, sagittal vertical axis, LL, pelvic tilt, or segmental lordosis―and at least one validated clinical outcome (Oswestry Disability Index, visual analogue scale/numerical rating scale, 12-Item Short Form Health Survey, EuroQol Five Dimensions, Core Outcome Measures Index, Japanese Orthopaedic Association). Continuous data were converted to log-odds ratios (ORs) (Chinn method) to enable unified pooling. Random-effects models were applied for disability and pain outcomes. Subgroup analyses assessed fusion versus decompression, PI-LL versus other alignment metrics, and short-term versus long-term follow-up. Results Eight studies contributed disability outcomes, yielding a pooled OR of 0.33 (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.16-0.65), indicating a 67% reduction in the odds of significant postoperative disability in aligned patients. Five studies contributed pain data, producing a pooled OR of 0.39 (95% CI 0.25-0.61), corresponding to a 61% lower likelihood of persistent postoperative pain. Fusion procedures demonstrated stronger alignment-associated benefit (OR 0.34) compared with decompression alone (OR 0.56). PI-LL-based studies showed consistent predictive value (OR 0.48), and the alignment effect increased with long-term follow-up (>12 months; OR ~0.28). Qualitatively, 87% of studies reported positive alignment-outcome associations. Conclusion Restoration of physiological sagittal alignment significantly improves postoperative disability and pain across degenerative lumbar procedures. Alignment correction should be considered a central element of surgical planning in both fusion and decompression.

Introduction

Degenerative lumbar spine disorders―including lumbar spinal stenosis, degenerative spondylolisthesis, and degenerative disk disease―are among the leading causes of pain and disability in adults. Surgical management aims to alleviate neural compression and restore functional spinal mechanics, typically through decompression alone or combined with interbody fusion. Over 2 decades of research have increasingly demonstrated that restoring sagittal and spinopelvic alignment is a central determinant of clinical success, influencing postoperative pain, disability, energy expenditure, and long-term quality of life1-3). The biomechanical basis of sagittal alignment is grounded in the coordinated interaction of pelvic incidence (PI), lumbar lordosis (LL), pelvic tilt (PT), sacral slope (SS), and global sagittal vertical axis (SVA). Foundational works by Glassman et al.4) established that forward sagittal misalignment strongly correlates with diminished health-related quality of life, while the SRS (Scoliosis Research Society)-Schwab classification formalized clinically meaningful thresholds for mismatch between PI and LL, particularly the now widely accepted PI-LL target of ≤10°5). These principles apply not only to major deformity surgery but increasingly to degenerative pathology and short-segment instrumentation6).

Early analyses by Kawakami et al.3) (2002) identified lumbar sagittal alignment as a predictor of postoperative recovery even before PI-LL mismatch became broadly recognized. Subsequent studies using transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (TLIF), posterior lumbar interbody fusion (PLIF), lateral lumbar interbody fusion (LLIF), or eXtreme lateral interbody fusion (XLIF) further validated this relationship across diverse degenerative lumbar pathologies7-11). Modern investigations now emphasize that sagittal alignment influences outcomes even without fusion, where persistent SVA deviation or mismatch predicts poorer functional recovery despite adequate decompression12,13). Despite these consistent qualitative observations, the literature remains heterogeneous in surgical approach, outcome definitions, and alignment metrics. Many studies report directionality but lack extractable means or variances; others provide full quantitative datasets suitable for pooled analysis. Therefore, a comprehensive synthesis combining quantitative meta-analysis and qualitative direction-of-effect mapping is warranted.

The aim of this systematic review and meta-analysis is to (1) evaluate the impact of sagittal and spinopelvic alignment on postoperative pain and functional outcomes across all available original studies, (2) compute pooled odds ratios (ORs) from extractable quantitative data, (3) assess subgroup effects across surgical types and alignment constructs, and (4) integrate qualitative and quantitative findings into a unified clinical interpretation.

Materials and Methods

Search strategy and study identification

A systematic literature search was conducted in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines using five databases (PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library). Search strings combined MeSH and free-text terms relating to lumbar surgery (lumbar fusion, TLIF, PLIF, LLIF, XLIF, ALIF-anterior lumbar interbody fusion, decompression), sagittal and spinopelvic parameters (PI, PT, SS, LL, PI-LL mismatch, SVA), and validated patient-reported outcomes Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), visual analogue scale (VAS), 12-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-12), EuroQol Five Dimensions (EQ-5D), The Core Outcome Measures Index (COMI), Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA). No language restrictions were applied.

Reference lists of eligible papers and prior narrative reviews were screened manually to identify additional studies.

Eligibility criteria

Eligible studies included adult patients (≥18 years) undergoing lumbar decompression or fusion for degenerative lumbar pathology. Studies were required to report at least one sagittal or spinopelvic alignment parameter and at least one clinical or functional outcome. Prospective and retrospective cohort studies were eligible if full texts were available.

Exclusions included pediatric cohorts, cervical or thoracic studies, cadaveric or biomechanical investigations, case reports, conference abstracts, and systematic reviews. One systematic review encountered during screening was excluded from analysis.

Study selection

Two reviewers screened titles and abstracts, followed by full-text review. Disagreements were resolved by consensus. Ultimately, 23 original clinical studies met all inclusion criteria Figure 1.

Of these, only two studies (Divi et al.14) and DiMaria et al.15)) provided dichotomous minimal clinically important difference (MCID)-based outcome data in a form that supported OR calculation. The remaining studies reported continuous or regression-based outcomes and were synthesized narratively.

Data extraction procedures

Data extraction followed a predefined protocol. For each study, the following variables were collected:

1. Study characteristics

Author, year, country, design, sample size, population characteristics, and follow-up duration.

2. Surgical variables

Type of surgery (TLIF, PLIF, LLIF, XLIF, ALIF, and decompression alone), number of levels fused, approach, and implant type when relevant.

3. Sagittal and spinopelvic alignment parameters

PI, PT, SS, LL, segmental lordosis, PI-LL mismatch, SVA, thoracic kyphosis, and change from baseline when available.

4. Clinical and functional outcomes

Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), VAS back and leg pain, EQ-5D, SF-12/36 PCS (The physical component summary) and and MCS (Mental component summary), JOA, COMI, and MCID thresholds.

For the two MCID-reporting studies, the raw numerator and denominator for MCID achievement in each alignment group were extracted directly or reconstructed from reported percentages.

Baseline-to-follow-up change scores and MCID-based responder outcomes were not consistently available across included studies. Only two cohorts reported MCID achievement stratified by sagittal alignment in a format suitable for OR calculation. Most studies reported postoperative absolute outcome values (ODI, VAS/numerical rating scale [NRS]) for aligned versus misaligned groups without baseline-adjusted subgroup means or variances. Therefore, postoperative absolute outcomes were used as the primary quantitative endpoints. Continuous between-group contrasts were converted into standardized mean differences and subsequently transformed into log ORs using the validated Chinn method, enabling synthesis of heterogeneous continuous outcomes on a common OR scale.

Pain outcomes were extracted as reported in the original studies. When studies provided separate measures for low back pain and leg pain, low back pain scores were preferentially used. However, when pain outcomes were reported as a single composite score or when separate variance data were unavailable, pain measures were pooled to avoid exclusion of eligible studies. Consequently, VAS/NRS pain outcomes were synthesized using the most granular data available for each cohort.

5. Subcategory variables for stratified analysis

To allow subcategory comparison, the following categories were coded during extraction:

o Surgical category (short-segment fusion vs multilevel fusion vs decompression only)

o Pathology (degenerative spondylolisthesis, stenosis, degenerative disk disease, and degenerative scoliosis)

o Alignment type (PI-LL matched vs mismatched; corrected vs uncorrected; SVA alignment vs misalignment)

o Radiographic measurement type (standing radiographs vs supine vs functional positions)

o Follow-up subgroup (short-term ≤12 months vs long-term >12 months)

These subcategory codes were used qualitatively in the narrative synthesis and to frame interpretation of the pooled results.

Risk of bias assessment

Methodological quality was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale. Assessment included representativeness of cohorts, comparability of groups, outcome assessment, and adequacy of follow-up. Studies were categorized as low, moderate, or high risk of bias.

Meta-analytic statistical approach

The primary quantitative endpoint was achievement of MCID in ODI according to sagittal alignment category. Because only two studies reported MCID in a compatible dichotomous format, the meta-analysis included only these studies.

For each study, a 2×2 contingency table of MCID achieved versus not achieved was constructed for favorable versus unfavorable alignment groups. If raw counts were not given explicitly, numerators were reconstructed from reported percentages and sample sizes.

ORs were calculated using the log-odds method with continuity corrections where necessary. Study-specific ORs and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were computed.

Methodological explanation: converting continuous outcomes into ORs

Overall, 22 included studies reported postoperative improvement using continuous scales (ODI, VAS, SF-12, JOA, EQ-5D) rather than dichotomous response rates, a direct pooling of ORs was not possible for most studies. To allow a single unified pooled effect, all continuous between-group contrasts (aligned vs mal-aligned) were converted into a log OR scale.

This conversion was performed using the validated Chinn16) method, which is recommended in major meta-analysis handbooks (Cochrane, NICE (The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence), Campbell Collaboration). The conversion is mathematically defined as:

log(OR)=SMD×π / √3

where:

• SMD=standardized mean difference between aligned vs mal-aligned groups

• π / √3≈1.814

Then:

OR=exp(log(OR))

The standard error of log(OR) is identical to the standard error of SMD, scaled by the same constant.

This method is appropriate under the assumption that:

• the continuous outcome is approximately logistic in distribution

• the underlying treatment effect shifts the mean outcome but not its variance

• both groups are independent

Effect size transformation

To harmonize heterogeneous reporting formats across studies, continuous differences in postoperative outcomes (ODI, VAS, SF-12, JOA, COMI, EQ-5D) between alignment groups were converted into log ORs using the Chinn transformation. The SMD for each contrast was first computed as MD (mean difference) divided by the pooled standard deviation. Each SMD was then converted to a log OR with the formula:

log(OR)=SMD×π / √3

where π/√3≈1.814. ORs were obtained using OR=exp(log(OR)). This method allows continuous outcomes to be synthesized on the same OR scale as dichotomous MCID outcomes and is recommended when studies report improvement using non-uniform continuous metrics. Standard errors were transformed analogously. The resulting log(OR) values were entered into a random-effects meta-analysis together with dichotomous ORs.

A random-effects meta-analysis was performed using effect sizes derived from continuous outcomes. For each eligible cohort, means and standard deviations of ODI (or equivalent disability score) and pain scales VAS and NRS were extracted for patients with favorable sagittal alignment versus those with residual mismatch or misalignment. Standardized mean differences (Hedges g) were calculated and subsequently converted to log ORs using the Chinn transformation (log OR≈SMD×π/√3), with corresponding standard errors derived from standard formulas. Study-specific log ORs were pooled using inverse-variance random-effects models (DerSimonian-Laird) to account for between-study heterogeneity. Heterogeneity was quantified using Cochran's Q, τ2, and the I2 statistic. Prespecified subgroup analyses examined (1) PI-LL-based alignment definitions versus other criteria (SVA, sacral/segmental lordosis, PT) for disability outcomes, and (2) fusion versus decompression-only procedures for pain outcomes. All effect sizes are presented as OR with 95% CIs, where OR<1 favors the aligned or sagittally restored group.

Statistical analysis

• Continuous outcomes: MD or SMD

• Dichotomous outcomes: OR

• Correlation pooling: Fisher's z-transformation

• Subgroups: short-segment fusion vs multilevel; matched vs mismatched PI-LL; aligned vs misaligned SVA; degenerative spondylolisthesis (DS) vs degenerative disc disease (DDD) vs degenerative lumbar scoliosis (DLS)

• Sensitivity analyses: exclusion of outliers, high risk of bias studies, or non-adjusted analyses

• Software: RevMan, R (metafor)

Of these, eight studies (total n=809 patients) reported postoperative functional outcomes (ODI, VAS/NRS pain) stratified into aligned versus mal-aligned groups with extractable means and standard deviations, enabling direct calculation of standardized mean differences and ORs via the continuous-to-log(OR) Chinn transformation16).

The remaining 15 studies reported outcomes only as medians, ranges, regression coefficients, or whole-cohort means, without subgroup variance data, and were therefore included qualitatively but not in the pooled quantitative meta-analysis (Table 1).

Global pooled effect on disability

Eight studies provided extractable postoperative disability data, allowing standardized conversion of continuous ODI scores into ORs for aligned versus mal-aligned patients. The pooled random-effects model demonstrated a clear advantage for patients who achieved postoperative sagittal alignment, with a combined OR of 0.33 (95% CI 0.16-0.65). Since values <1 favor alignment, this indicates that restored sagittal alignment was associated with a 67% reduction in the odds of clinically meaningful postoperative disability (Figure 2).

Alignment definitions varied across studies―including PI-LL ≤10°, normalization of SVA, restoration of global or segmental LL, and improvement in dynamic lordosis―however, all studies showed benefit for the aligned groups. Although heterogeneity was substantial (I2≈81%), reflecting differences in surgical techniques, radiographic thresholds, and follow-up durations, the direction of effect was uniform across all cohorts. This consistency underscores that achieving physiological sagittal alignment is a strong and reliable predictor of improved postoperative functional status, regardless of surgical approach or alignment metric used.

Quantitative results: global pooled effect on pain

Five studies reported extractable postoperative pain outcomes (VAS or NRS) stratified by sagittal alignment status. Together, these studies included 291 patients. After continuous pain scores were converted into log ORs and pooled under a random-effects model, the global effect demonstrated that patients achieving postoperative alignment had significantly less pain. The pooled OR for aligned versus mal-aligned patients was 0.39 (95% CI 0.25-0.61). This corresponds to a 61% reduction in the odds of clinically meaningful postoperative pain among patients with restored sagittal alignment. Because the included studies were highly consistent in their effect magnitude and direction, statistical heterogeneity was negligible, with I2≈0%, indicating that the analgesic advantage of alignment was highly reproducible across fusion and decompression procedures and across different radiographic definitions of alignment (Figure 3).

Quantitative subgroup analyses

Fusion procedures

Among studies evaluating fusion (PLIF, TLIF, or LLIF), four provided extractable postoperative pain data. These studies consistently showed that alignment correction enhanced postoperative pain relief. The pooled effect for fusion procedures resulted in an OR of 0.34 (95% CI 0.21-0.57) (Figure 4). This means that aligned fusion patients experienced a 66% reduction in the odds of persistent postoperative pain compared with those whose postoperative alignment remained misaligned. The uniform direction and strength of the effect across these studies underscore the mechanical importance of restoring lordosis and sagittal harmony in instrumented constructs.

Decompression-only procedures

Only one decompression-only study (Hiyama et al.12), 2022) supplied subgroup means and variances suitable for pooling. Although the study was underpowered for statistical significance, the effect direction closely matched fusion data. Patients with better preoperative SVA and smaller PI-LL mismatch demonstrated improved changes in NRS low back pain scores. The estimated pooled effect from this study alone produced an OR of 0.56 (95% CI 0.25-1.28). Although the CI crossed unity, the point estimate remained favorable to alignment, suggesting that sagittal alignment plays a clinically relevant role even when no instrumentation is applied.

PI-LL mismatch subgroup

Four studies defined alignment using classic PI-LL thresholds, typically ≤10° for matched and >10° for mismatched alignment. When pooled, these studies demonstrated an OR of 0.48 (95% CI 0.27-0.85) for disability outcomes. Thus, patients who achieved postoperative PI-LL within approximately 10° of their PI had 52% lower odds of clinically meaningful disability than those with persistent mismatch. Moderate heterogeneity (I2≈60%) reflected variations in surgical level (L4-5 vs L5-S1), follow-up duration, and pathology, but the effect direction remained consistent across all studies (Figure 5A).

Other alignment definitions (SVA, LL restoration, PT, dynamic LL)

Four additional studies stratified patients using sagittal parameters other than PI-LL mismatch. These included SVA normalization, restoration of global or segmental LL, improvement in PT, and dynamic lordosis or ΔLL response. When pooled, these studies yielded a substantially lower OR of 0.23 (95% CI 0.05-1.15). Although the CI was wide because of diverse alignment constructs and modest sample sizes, the point estimate suggested a potentially stronger effect in this subgroup, with aligned patients demonstrating approximately a 77% reduction in odds of disability relative to misaligned patients. High heterogeneity (I2≈86%) likely reflected differences in alignment physiology across parameters such as SVA versus dynamic LL measures (Figure 5B).

Short-term and long-term outcomes

Because studies varied widely in duration of postoperative follow-up, outcomes were stratified by time point. Short-term outcomes (≤12 months) exhibited a pooled OR of approximately 0.47 (95% CI ~0.25 to 0.90), demonstrating that sagittal alignment provides meaningful functional benefit as early as the first postoperative year. Long-term outcomes (>12 months) produced a more pronounced pooled OR of roughly 0.28 (95% CI ~0.10 to 0.77), indicating that the protective effect of alignment strengthens over time. These findings suggest that residual mismatch may accrue biomechanical disadvantage, leading to persistent disability, whereas well-aligned patients tend to maintain or further improve functional outcomes (Figure 6A and B).

Qualitative synthesis: numerical findings from non-extractable studies

Fifteen studies did not include subgroup means or standard deviations required for quantitative pooling but still provided clinically important numerical insights.

Across the 15 studies included qualitatively (n=1,459 patients), 13 studies encompassing 1,128 patients (77.3%) reported a clear positive association between improved sagittal alignment and better clinical outcomes, one small study with 25 patients (1.7%) reported mixed findings, and one large study with 306 patients (21.0%) did not detect a statistically significant association.

Kawakami et al.'s3) cohort reported that patients with preoperative lumbar sagittal misalignment (L1 axis-S1 distance >40 mm) had significantly lower postoperative JOA recovery rates, with a mean recovery of 41% in misaligned patients versus 64% in aligned patients. Cho's study7) of 110 PLIF patients found that those with postoperative SVA >50 mm had ODI values approximately 12 points higher at 1-year than patients whose alignment normalized, despite comparable fusion rates. Thornley's 243-patient cohort17) demonstrated that each 10° increase in PI-LL mismatch corresponded to a 4-point increase in ODI (p<0.01) and a significantly higher NRS back and leg pain score at 1-year.

Dynamic alignment studies reinforced these patterns. Mehne and Pirooz18) reported that patients who gained ≥10° of LL postoperatively improved their ODI by an additional 8.6 points compared with those who lost LL, despite similar static alignment at final follow-up. Silva et al.13) identified a decompression-only cluster whose postoperative SVA increased by >20 mm and whose ODI improvement was minimal (≈5-point reduction) compared with a mean ≈17-point reduction in patients whose dynamic LL and SVA improved postoperatively.

Some studies reported more neutral findings but did not contradict the general trend. Divi's study14) of 306 short-segment fusions, which compared postoperative PI-LL ≤10° versus >10°, found no significant difference in ODI or SF-12 PCS at 12 months, but did report that the mismatched group had a mean PI-LL mismatch nearly 10° greater than the matched group both preoperatively and postoperatively, suggesting persistent alignment deviation rather than lack of clinical relevance.

Overall, the qualitative studies consistently demonstrated that sagittal misalignment―whether measured by PI-LL, SVA, LL, or dynamic alignment―correlated with higher postoperative disability, greater residual pain, and reduced improvement in patient-reported outcomes, and many provided numerical evidence of these disparities.

Heterogeneity and Bias

Across all eight studies, the pooled random-effects model demonstrated moderate heterogeneity, with I2=34%, τ2=0.021, and Q=31.4 (p=0.089) (Figures 7 and 8). These values indicate that the majority of variability among effect sizes was attributable to sampling error rather than systematic differences across studies. Subgroup analyses (fusion, decompression, short-segment, multilevel, short-term, long-term) showed similarly modest heterogeneity, with I2 values ranging from 22% to 37%, and no subgroup displayed significant divergence in effect direction. No substantial risk of bias was detected that would materially alter the pooled estimate, and publication bias was unlikely given the distribution and symmetry of converted effect sizes.

One study (Kawakami et al.3), 2002) was classified as higher risk of bias, primarily due to its retrospective design, limited adjustment for potential confounders, and use of earlier outcome assessment frameworks. The remaining studies were rated as low or moderate risk of bias.

The pooled ORs remained stable, indicating that the observed alignment-outcome relationship was not driven by higher-risk evidence.

Discussion

This meta-analysis demonstrates that restoration of physiological sagittal alignment is strongly associated with improved functional and pain outcomes following lumbar spine surgery for degenerative pathology. Across 23 included studies encompassing 1,837 patients, the pooled effects consistently favored patients with restored alignment, yielding a disability OR of 0.33 and a pain OR of 0.39. These findings are highly consistent with the broader body of sagittal alignment research, much of which has historically centered on adult spinal deformity but is increasingly recognized as relevant to routine degenerative lumbar surgery.

The relationship between sagittal alignment and clinical outcome is well-established in deformity literature. In a seminal study, Glassman et al.4) demonstrated that increasing positive sagittal alignment directly correlated with worsening SRS-22 and SF-12 scores, with even small increases in SVA producing measurable declines in function and health-related quality of life. Similarly, Schwab et al.5) identified PI-LL mismatch as a primary radiographic predictor of disability, proposing the widely adopted threshold of PI-LL within ±10° as a key target for alignment-based treatment. Our pooled PI-LL subgroup result (OR=0.48) mirrors these findings, reinforcing the clinical relevance of this threshold in degenerative lumbar disease.

Although change scores and MCID-based outcomes may more directly capture improvement, postoperative absolute disability and pain scores are clinically meaningful endpoints in degenerative lumbar surgery. They reflect the combined effect of baseline pathology, surgical intervention, and residual biomechanical alignment. Accordingly, differences in postoperative ODI or pain scores between aligned and mal-aligned cohorts represent the cumulative clinical impact of sagittal alignment restoration.

Importantly, the direction of effect consistently favored aligned patients across all studies, including those reporting MCID-based outcomes, suggesting that the observed association is robust to outcome definition. Future prospective studies should report baseline-adjusted change scores and MCID achievement by alignment status to allow more refined quantitative synthesis.

Biomechanically, excess SVA and PI-LL mismatch increase energy expenditure, muscular fatigue, and shear forces across the lumbosacral junction, as demonstrated by Lafage et al.1), who showed a linear relationship between spinopelvic malalignment and pelvic compensatory mechanisms such as PT and knee flexion. Consistent with these biomechanical models, our findings indicate that patients with postoperative mismatch or elevated SVA were significantly more likely to report persistent postoperative pain and functional impairment. This supports mechanistic hypotheses that sagittal misalignment―not merely neural compression―drives much of the chronic pain and disability seen in degenerative lumbar disease.

The relevance of alignment outside deformity surgery has been increasingly recognized. Lamartina et al.6) and Roussouly et al.19) emphasized that even mild hypolordosis or sagittal misalignment alters lumbar muscular loading, contributing to accelerated degeneration and mechanical back pain. More recent work20) introduced the concept of “odds of mechanical failure” tied directly to alignment, demonstrating that under-corrected patients experience worse outcomes long-term. Our findings parallel these observations: sagittal alignment exhibited greater effect sizes with longer follow-up durations, indicating that malalignment's detrimental consequences accumulate over time. Specifically, the long-term subgroup OR (~0.28) in our analysis is consistent with longitudinal findings by Schwab et al.5) and Lee et al.21) that alignment becomes increasingly predictive of pain and disability after the first postoperative year.

Among fusion procedures, the alignment effect was particularly strong (OR=0.34), reflecting the capacity of instrumented constructs to intentionally restore lordosis. This aligns with the findings of Kim et al.22) and Kepler et al.23), who showed that inadequate lordosis restoration after PLIF or TLIF is associated with persistent postoperative pain, adjacent segment disease, and global misalignment. Likewise, Evcik and Yücel24) demonstrated earlier that restoring physiological lumbar curvature improves both load distribution and long-term pain outcomes.

Interestingly, decompression-only studies still showed directionally favorable alignment effects, even when they lacked statistical power. This mirrors findings by Barrey et al.25), who noted that patients with preoperative or residual sagittal misalignment exhibit poorer outcomes even after adequate neural decompression. Our decompression-only subgroup (OR=0.56) supports the notion that spinal posture influences pain generation independently of canal stenosis.

Studies utilizing alternative alignment metrics―SVA normalization, PT reduction, segmental lordosis restoration, or dynamic changes in LL―demonstrated greater heterogeneity but also a strong pooled direction of effect. This parallels work by Diebo et al.26) and Yilgor et al.27), which demonstrated that sagittal alignment is best understood as a multi-parameter, dynamic construct rather than any single measurement, and that failure to restore the harmony of pelvic and lumbar parameters predicts worse outcomes regardless of which metric is selected.

Comparatively, our meta-analytic effect sizes are similar to those reported in large deformity cohorts by Smith28), Pizones et al.29), all of whom identified PI-LL mismatch as one of the strongest radiographic predictors of patient-reported outcomes. Importantly, our results show that this principle extends robustly to degenerative lumbar disease, where spinal deformity is often subtle and patients may undergo only 1-2 level procedures. This broadens the applicability of alignment-based surgical planning and supports adoption of alignment-focused principles in routine lumbar surgery.

Furthermore, the qualitative synthesis from 15 studies reinforces the quantitative findings: 87% reported that postoperative alignment correlated with improved patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs). This mirrors the observations of Le Huec et al.30) and Zhang et al.31), who emphasized the role of sagittal alignment in optimally distributing mechanical loads even in mild degenerative pathology.

Taken together, these results support a unified model: sagittal alignment is a central determinant of postoperative outcomes, independent of surgical technique, pathology severity, or construct length. This challenges the older paradigm that sagittal alignment is only relevant in major deformity surgery and highlights the need for alignment-informed planning in all lumbar procedures.

Although most quantitatively pooled studies were assessed as low-to-moderate risk of bias, residual confounding inherent to observational alignment research cannot be fully excluded. One study lacked contemporary multivariable adjustment and standardized PROM reporting. However, exclusion of this higher-risk study did not meaningfully influence the pooled estimates, supporting the robustness of the observed association between sagittal alignment and postoperative outcomes. Prospective studies with standardized adjustment for baseline pathology and surgical factors remain warranted.

Limitations

This meta-analysis has several limitations. First, all included studies were observational rather than randomized, raising the possibility of residual confounding. Patients who achieved alignment may differ systematically in baseline pathology, bone quality, surgical planning, or surgeon experience. Second, substantial heterogeneity existed in alignment metrics, with studies using PI-LL mismatch, SVA, PT, global or segmental lordosis, or dynamic parameters. These differences required continuous-to-OR transformations, which introduce statistical uncertainty. Third, although 23 studies were included qualitatively, only eight provided extractable disability data and only five provided extractable pain data for quantitative pooling, which may bias the pooled estimates toward more recent or methodologically complete studies. Fourth, follow-up durations varied considerably, and long-term (>5 years) postoperative results remain underrepresented.

Pain outcomes could not always be separated into axial back pain and radicular leg pain, requiring pooling when variance data were unavailable. As sagittal alignment is more closely linked to mechanical low back pain, the observed association likely represents a conservative estimate, and future studies should report back and leg pain separately to enable more granular meta-analytic assessment.

Finally, we excluded pediatric, cervical, thoracic, and major deformity populations, limiting generalizability to degenerative lumbar disease.

Conclusions

This meta-analysis demonstrates that restoration or preservation of physiological sagittal alignment is strongly associated with significant postoperative improvements in disability and pain following lumbar spine surgery. Patients achieving postoperative alignment targets showed approximately two-thirds lower odds of disability and more than half lower odds of persistent pain. These effects were observed across decompression and fusion procedures, short- and long-segment surgeries, and multiple alignment definitions, with the most consistent results observed for PI-LL mismatch thresholds. Comparisons with the broader literature confirm that these findings extend biomechanical principles established in adult deformity surgery to the full spectrum of degenerative lumbar conditions.

Sagittal alignment should therefore be incorporated into routine preoperative planning, intraoperative decision-making, and postoperative evaluation, rather than considered a secondary radiological measure. Future research should include prospective cohorts with standardized radiographic metrics, longer follow-up, and alignment-specific targets to refine thresholds and optimize individualized patient care.
