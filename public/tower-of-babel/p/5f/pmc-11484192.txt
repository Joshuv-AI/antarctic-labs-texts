Maternal adiposity measures and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy: a meta-analysis
Authors: Caiping Xiang, Lun Sui, Xueteng Ding, Min Cao, Guoju Li, Zhanhui Du
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP) are a prevalent complication during pregnancy with significant implications for maternal and perinatal health globally. Maternal obesity is a known risk factor for HDP. This review sought to identify adiposity indicators in early pregnancy that are linked to the development of HDP.MethodsWe conducted a systematic search of the PubMed, Science Citation Index (Web of Science), and Embase databases. Screening and quality evaluation of studies was conducted independently by two reviewers. Both random effects meta-analysis and narrative synthesis were performed.ResultsThirty-one studies were included with a pooled sample of 81,311 women. The meta-analysis showed that the odds of HDP were significantly increased by higher pregnancy measures of adiposity (pre-pregnancy body mass index (OR = 2.14, 95%CI: 1.67–2.75), visceral adipose tissue (OR = 1.79, 95%CI: 1.01–3.16), subcutaneous adiposity thickness (OR = 1.67, 95%CI: 1.36–2.05), waist circumference (OR = 2.20, 95%CI: 1.37–3.54), waist to-hip ratio (OR = 3.08, 95% CI: 1.38–6.87), weight gain (OR = 1.69, 95%CI: 1.42–2.02) and percentage body fat (OR = 1.71, 95%CI: 1.16–2.53)). Epicardial fat thickness was significantly associated with HDP, although limited data were available.ConclusionsThe results of our study highlight the importance of investigating the predictive value of adiposity measures in identifying the risk of HDP to tailor care towards women at the highest risk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-024-06788-2.Keywords: Adiposity, Maternal health, Visceral fat, Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, Preeclampsia

Background

Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP) are a common complication of pregnancy in women, mainly including gestational hypertension (GH) and preeclampsia (PE), manifested by hypertension, edema, proteinuria, eclampsia and coma [1]. HDP constitute one of the leading causes of maternal and perinatal mortality worldwide [2] and it’s the global prevalence is about 10% [1]. There exist various risk factors for HDP, and unlike certain non-modifiable factors such as advanced maternal age, multiple pregnancies, renal disorders, and primiparity, obesity can be altered to help prevent the onset of HDP [3–6].

In recent years, there has been a notable increase in the global prevalence of obesity. A study conducted in the United States of America (USA) revealed a rise in obesity among women from 35.4% in 2007–2008 to 41.1% in 2015–2016 [7]. Common indicators of obesity include body mass index (BMI), waist circumference (WC), waist-to-hip ratio (WHR), percentage of body fat (PBF), and weight gain. Pre-pregnancy BMI is commonly utilized to classify weight status (obese) in pregnant women, aiding in risk assessment to enhance the health outcomes of both mothers and children. Nonetheless, a significant proportion of pregnant women with a pre-pregnancy BMI < 30 kg/m2 may experience adverse pregnancy outcomes at a higher rate, while those with a pre-pregnancy BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 may not necessarily be at such risks [8]. This indicates the limited accuracy and specificity of using pre-pregnancy BMI alone as an indicator of obesity in predicting HDP. Recent research has increasingly highlighted the significance of visceral adipose tissue (VAT) in reflecting obesity and the likelihood of developing HDP [9–11]. VAT is particularly indicative of central obesity and is known to secrete various thrombogenic and inflammatory substances, leading to disruptions in glucose metabolism, increased insulin resistance, and the development of metabolic syndrome [12, 13]. Observational studies have demonstrated elevated levels of activated macrophages and inflammatory adipokines in VAT, with greater VAT thickness correlating with an increased risk of PE [14–17]. Additionally, a research suggests a potential connection between age at menarche (AAM) and VAT, implicating a mediating role of VAT in the differential risk of developing PE [18]. Due to some inconsistencies in the observational data, further studies are warranted to establish more precise causal relationships. The objective of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to pinpoint early pregnancy adiposity measures, including VAT, as a factor that is potentially predictive of HDP.

Methods

Search strategy

The systematic review was registered on PROSPERO (CRD42023474213) and the Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) guidelines were followed [19].

We regularly searched the PubMed, Science Citation Index (Web of Science), and Embase databases from database inception to May 1st 2023, with no language restrictions. Using a mixture of controlled search vocabulary (e.g., MeSH) and free text, search terms were derived using the following concepts: “Intra-Abdominal Fat”, “Obesity”, “Waist Circumference”, “Waist-Hip Ratio”, “Body Fat Distribution”, “Adiposity”, “Abdominal Fat”, “Obesity, Abdominal” and “Hypertension, Pregnancy-Induced” OR “Preeclampsia”. We excluded high risk cohorts (e.g., obesity population and those with pre-existing conditions such as polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS) or type 2 diabetes), abstracts and unpublished studies. When duplicates occurred, the study with the highest number of cases was used. When different publications of the same study included data from different anthropometric methods, both were included, but each study was only included once in each analysis.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Upon careful examination of the selected articles, a secondary screening process was conducted based on the following criteria:

Inclusion Criteria: (1) Singleton pregnancy (2) Assessment of pregnancy adiposity measures (such as BMI, WC, WHR, PBF, weight gain, VAT, subcutaneous adiposity thickness (SAT)) conducted before 20 weeks of gestation (3) Pregnancy outcomes encompassing HDP (PE or GH) (4) Studies designed as cohorts or case-control studies.

Exclusion Criteria: (1) Pregnant women from specific populations with underlying conditions (e.g., limited to those with PCOS) (2) Groups consisting of residents in nursing homes (3) Studies solely focusing on BMI as the single adiposity measure (4) Research investigating low adiposity or undernutrition (5) pre-existing hypertension before pregnancy or before 20 weeks of gestation.

Outcomes measurement

The objective of this meta-analysis was to assess the prevalence of HDP, which predominantly includes GH and PE. GH was defined as blood pressure reaching 140/90 mmHg without the presence of proteinuria after 20 weeks of gestation (1 mmHg = 0.133 kPa). PE was characterized based on the criteria outlined in the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) guidelines: a systolic blood pressure of ≥ 140 mmHg or a diastolic blood pressure of ≥ 90 mmHg on two separate occasions at least 4 h apart occurring after 20 weeks of pregnancy in women with previously normal blood pressure levels, accompanied by the presence of proteinuria. Proteinuria was identified as having ≥ 0.3 g in a 24-hour urine specimen or protein/creatinine ratio ≥ 0.3 (30 mg/mmol) in a random urine specimen or dipstick ≥ 2 + if a quantitative measurement is unavailable [20].

Data extraction

Two reviewers autonomously collected data from each included study utilizing a standardized data collection sheet. Any discrepancies were settled through mutual agreement. For studies that reported a connection between maternal adiposity and the likelihood of developing HDP, the following details were extracted: last name of the primary author, publication year, study location, year of study execution, study design, participant demographics, sample size, variables adjusted for in the analysis, and risk estimates along with their respective 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Quality assessment

Two reviewers conducted independent quality assessments using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scales (NOS) for cohort and case-control studies to evaluate information bias, selection bias, and confounding [21]. Each study was evaluated based on three criteria: selection of the study group, comparability of the study groups, and assessment of the study outcome or exposure of interest. A rapid visual assessment assigned a rating to each criterion, with the top-quality study receiving a 9-star rating. Studies achieving a score of ≥ 7 stars were deemed to be of high methodological quality.

Statistical analysis

Risk ratios (RRs) or odds ratios (ORs) along with their corresponding 95% CIs were computed for each category utilizing random effects models that consider heterogeneity among studies. In this study, RRs were directly approximated as ORs [22]. The level of heterogeneity among the studies was assessed through the I2 and Q statistics [23]. The I2 statistic was categorized as follows: <25% (low heterogeneity), 25–50% (moderate heterogeneity), and > 75% (substantial heterogeneity). Given the incorporation of observational studies in this systematic review, high heterogeneity was anticipated. To analyze potential publication bias, Begg’s rank correlation test and Egger’s linear regression test were performed [24]. In instances of observed publication bias, the trim-and-fill method was employed for bias correction. Subgroup and meta-regression analyses were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. A sensitivity analysis was carried out by sequentially omitting one study at a time to examine the impact of individual studies on the overall findings. Statistical analyses were carried out using Stata software version 12.0 (Stata Corp, College Station, TX, USA), with statistical significance set at a two-tailed P-value of less than 0.05.

Results

Study characteristics

After removing duplicates, a total of 6,740 studies were identified, with 94 of these advancing to the full-text screening phase. From these, 31 studies [9–11, 25–50]met the inclusion criteria (Fig. 1). Out of these, 24 studies were observational (19 prospective and 5 retrospective cohorts), and 7 were case-control studies. The publication dates of these studies spanned from 2001 to 2023, with the majority (n = 26, 83.9%) being published between 2013 and 2023. Sample sizes varied from 108 to 32,531.

Study selection process

The analyzed studies encompassed a total of 81,311 pregnancies, with a combined sample size as detailed in Table 1. The majority of the studies originated from China (10 studies), followed by the USA (5 studies), Poland (1 study), Japan (2 studies), Iran (2 studies), Australia (3 studies), Sweden (1 study), Brazil (2 studies), Turkey (1 study), the United Kingdom (UK) (1 study), and Canada (3 studies). Geographically, the distribution of regions was as follows: 12 studies were conducted in Asia, 8 in the USA and Canada, 1 in the UK, and 10 in other regions.

The baseline characteristics of the participants included in the study are displayed in Table 1. The average age of the participants varied from 22.5 to 32.7 years, with mean BMI values ranging from 20.6 to 33.1 kg/m2. Weight gain was the most commonly documented parameter in the studies included (n = 12), followed by WC and SAT (n = 5 each), VAT and PBF (n = 4 each), and WHR (n = 3). The predominant focus of the outcome data was on PE (n = 18 studies), with GH following closely (n = 11 studies), and HDP examined in 9 of the studies.

The quality assessment scores of the studies included ranged from six to nine points, suggesting a good level of quality (Table 1). None of the studies were rated as low quality, with the majority being classified as high quality (67.7%). Cohort studies consistently received high scores across all evaluation criteria (75%), with all the included cohort studies fulfilling the requirement for an adequate follow-up period (100%). Two case-control studies achieved a score of eight, signifying high quality.

BMI

Eight [25, 30, 32, 33, 43–45, 47] studies reported associations between pre-pregnancy BMI and a diagnosis of HDP. The comparison of obesity with normal weight showed significant associations between obesity and an elevated risk of HDP (OR = 2.14, 95% CI:1.67–2.75, I2 = 98.4%, P < 0.001; Fig. 2A) with notable heterogeneity. In the subgroup analyses, the association was not significant in retrospective cohort study (OR = 1.22, 95% CI:0.55–2.66, I2 = 93.7%, P < 0.001). Studies with different designs could be sources of heterogeneity. No evidence was found of publication bias with Egger’s test (P = 0.063).

Displays forest plots illustrating the meta-analysis findings on the relationship between HDP and the risk associated with BMI. (A) pre-pregnancy BMI; (B) BMI during the first trimester

Ten [9, 11, 26, 27, 29, 34, 39, 46, 50, 51] reported between BMI during the first trimester and diagnosed of HDP. The OR for developing HDP was 2.75 (95% CI: 1.95–3.89) per 5-kg/m² increase in the first trimester BMI, with high heterogeneity (I2 = 92.8%, P < 0.001; Fig. 2B). Meta-regression analyses examining sources of heterogeneity, including sample size, participant age, study type and study location, showed that these did not contribute to the observed heterogeneity (P ≥ 0.05). Subsequent assessment of individual study effects revealed that the study by Ebrahimi-Mameghani et al. (2013) significantly influenced the results (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Upon excluding the study, the OR was 2.74 (95% CI: 2.19–3.44, I2 = 89.8%, P < 0.001), displaying a reduction in heterogeneity.

Three studies [35, 37, 49] reported measures of BMI during the second trimester. The summary OR was found to be 1.62 (95% CI: 1.43–1.85, I2 = 0.0%, P = 0.403). One [31] study measured BMI in pregnant women before delivery. The study, which was not pooled in the meta-analysis, reported that antenatal BMI was correlated with HDP.

Abdominal adipose tissue thickness

In all studies included, ultrasound assessments of SAT and VAT were conducted prior to the 20th week of gestation. Five studies [9, 11, 25, 37, 39] reported associations between the development of HDP and SAT. A significant positive correlation was observed between HDP and SAT (OR = 1.67, 95% CI: 1.36–2.05, I2 = 72.6%, P = 0.003; Fig. 3A) with significant heterogeneity. Additionally, one study indicated a positive but nonsignificant association between SAT and HDP (OR = 1.50, 95% CI: 0.49–4.55). The findings from a meta-analysis of four studies [10, 11, 25, 39] indicated that higher abdominal VAT, when treated as a continuous variable, was linked to an elevated risk of HDP (OR = 1.79, 95% CI: 1.01–3.16, I2 = 68.4%, P = 0.013; Fig. 3B). While two studies did not show statistically significant results (OR = 3.40, 95% CI: 0.90–13.4; OR = 2.60, 95% CI: 0.80-9.00), one study with a substantial sample size (n = 3,777) reported significant findings, ultimately indicating a positive association between visceral fat thickness and the risk of HDP.

WC and WHR

Five studies [26, 49–52] examined the relationship between WC measures and the diagnosis of HDP, involving a total of 7,392 participants. Four studies indicated significant positive associations, while one study showed no significant correlation (OR = 1.03, 95% CI: 0.97–1.10). Individuals classified as obese (WC ≥ 88 cm or WC ≥ 80 cm) had a higher likelihood of experiencing HDP (OR = 2.20, 95% CI: 1.37–3.54, I2 = 88.9%, P < 0.001; Fig. 3C) compared to those with normal WC measurements. There was no indication of publication bias based on Egger’s test (P < 0.001) or Begg’s test (P < 0.001), despite the limited number of studies. Three studies [27, 46, 51]presented data on WHR categories and their associations with HDP, all of which were included in the meta-analysis. A notable rise in the odds of experiencing HDP was observed for women in the high WHR category compared to those in the low WHR category (OR = 3.08, 95% CI: 1.38–6.87, I2 = 72.7%, P = 0.026; Fig. 3D) with significant heterogeneity. This indicates that WHR serves as a risk factor for HDP.

Weight gain

A total of 1,633 publications [30, 32–36, 41–45, 47] provided data concerning weight gain and the risk of HDP, resulting in a summary odds ratio of 1.65 (95% CI: 1.41–1.92, I2 = 90.1%, P < 0.001; Fig. 3F). Meta-regression analysis was conducted, revealing no identified source of heterogeneity. Further investigations were carried out. Assessing the impact of individual studies on the outcomes revealed that one study, conducted by Zhang et al. in 2022, influenced the results (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Upon excluding this study and conducting a revised meta-analysis, an OR of 1.69 (95% CI: 1.42–2.02, I2 = 83.7%, P < 0.001) was obtained, resulting in decreased heterogeneity.

PBF

The analysis included four cohort studies [29, 31, 38, 48] and one case-control study investigating body fat percentage and its association with the risk of HDP comprising 1,084 participants. The summary OR was found to be 1.71 (95% CI: 1.16–2.53, I2 = 94.7%, P < 0.001; Fig. 3E).

Epicardial fat thickness

Two studies [28, 40] indicated a significantly higher median sum of epicardial fat thickness (EFT) in women who subsequently developed HDP. Both studies reported that maternal EFT was associated with the level of risk for HDP, with OR of 12.34 (95% CI: 2.19–69.53) and 1.52 (95% CI: 1.21–1.90), respectively.

Publication bias

In the meta-analyses, more than 10 articles investigated the relationship between BMI during the first trimester and HDP, and the association between weight gain and HDP respectively, suggesting the presence of publication bias. The funnel plot and Begg’s test indicated potential publication bias (P = 0.001) in the analysis of HDP risk concerning BMI during the first trimester. In response, a sensitivity analysis was carried out utilizing the trim-and-fill method (Supplementary Fig. 2), which involves hypothetically imputing negative unpublished studies to balance the positive studies responsible for funnel plot asymmetry. The combined analysis, incorporating the hypothetical studies, reaffirmed a statistically significant association between BMI during the first trimester and the risk of HDP. However, there was no evidence of publication bias in articles investigating the correlation between weight gain and HDP, as indicated by Begg’s test (P = 0.742, Supplementary Fig. 2).

Discussion

This meta-analysis synthesized a comprehensive array of evidence from the 31 included studies that explored the association between early pregnancy adiposity measured before or at 20 weeks’ gestation and HDP. The analysis revealed strong associations of BMI, WC, and WHR with the development of HDP, a consistent finding supported by numerous prior studies. Furthermore, this meta-analysis showcased significant associations of VAT and SAT with the risk of HDP, although the extent of relevant studies was limited. Yet, the existing evidence often presents conflicting results or suggests a lack of strong associations between VAT, SAT, and maternal HDP, hinting at confined predictability of HDP risk based on these measures. Therefore, further investigations are warranted to elucidate and validate the utility of VAT and SAT in predicting the risk of HDP in pregnancy.

To our understanding, limited meta-analyses have delved into the relationship between maternal adiposity measures and the risk of HDP in pregnant women, particularly examining the association between ultrasound-measured abdominal SAT and VAT with HDP risk, a topic that has been infrequently addressed. In a meta-analysis conducted in 2021, an exploration of the correlation between ultrasound-measured abdominal SAT and VAT with HDP risk in pregnant women was mentioned, suggesting a potential association. However, this study was not subjected to meta-analysis due to the scarcity of pertinent literature and conflicting study outcomes [53]. In the present meta-analysis, alongside assessing the relationship between common measures such as BMI, WC, and WHR and HDP, we also investigated the correlation between ultrasound-measured abdominal SAT and VAT with the risk of HDP in pregnant women. The evaluation of abdominal SAT and VAT through ultrasound can be seamlessly integrated with routine fetal ultrasound in early gestation [54]. Notably, VAT is deemed to be a more precise indicator of central obesity compared to BMI and WC [9]. The positive association between VAT and the occurrence of HDP may be attributed to the inflammatory response of visceral fat and adipose-related bifactors [14, 16, 55]. Studies have highlighted the role of activated macrophages and inflammatory adipokines in VAT in the development of PE metabolic syndrome [56–58]. Additionally, evidence supports the premise that visceral fat contributes to the prevalence of PE, with adipose-related proteins potentially serving as PE biomarkers. However, disparities in research findings exist, underscoring the need for further investigations on the utility of visceral adiposity as a superior identifier of HDP risk compared to current clinical practices.

The present meta-analysis possesses both strengths and limitations. The strengths of this review encompass a meticulous search strategy, along with supplementary searches to enhance the pool of studies available for inclusion in the meta-analysis. The screening, data extraction, and quality assessment processes were carried out in duplicate to minimize potential human errors and subjective biases. Our objective was to optimize the number of studies consolidated in the meta-analyses by appropriately transforming the data where necessary. However, a notable limitation is the high heterogeneity of the data reported in the included studies, with substantial variability in result presentations encompassing OR, RR, mean values, and medians. Moreover, discrepancies in defining obesity also contributed to the observed heterogeneity. Furthermore, the limited number of studies included in the analysis precluded the use of meta-regression to explore sources of heterogeneity as originally planned. The constrained availability of studies for aggregation in each individual meta-analysis also impeded the effectiveness of investigating potential publication bias and executing sensitivity analyses.

Our meta-analysis has unveiled promising insights that can potentially guide clinical practice, particularly concerning the associations between VAT and SAT with the risk of HDP. Nevertheless, there is a pressing need for further investigation to ascertain whether these metrics are superior to BMI in predicting the risk of HDP, or whether they may be integrated into risk prediction models alongside BMI or other predictive factors. Notably, future research endeavors should prioritize the exploration of obesity measures that can be seamlessly incorporated into standard maternal care procedures. Subsequent studies should compare the efficacy of these measures to identify which metric serves as the most effective in guiding timely interventions for women necessitating early support to achieve optimal pregnancy outcomes.
