LncRNA MEG3 regulates the development of porcine skeletal muscle satellite cells by enhancing HMGA1 stability
Authors: Quan Liu, Honghong Zhou, Chong Fu, Su Xie, Mengxun Li, Changchun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPorcine skeletal muscle satellite cells (PSCs) are the core stem cell population for the development of porcine skeletal muscle. In postnatal piglets, PSCs can differentiate into myoblasts and fuse with existing muscle fibers, increasing muscle fiber volume. While the long noncoding RNA MEG3 (MEG3) has been shown to modulate PSC development, its mechanisms remain nebulous. Here, we aim to explore the mechanism whereby MEG3 modulates PSC development.MethodsCore interaction regions between MEG3 and CDC23 were detected using truncated constructs combined with RNA pull-down and RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP). Potential CDC23 target proteins were analyzed using coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC). The potential mechanism of MEG3 modulation was then further explored by employing MG-132 and cycloheximide (CHX) treatment and ubiquitination assays. Finally, downstream signaling pathways associated with MEG3 were detected by transcriptome sequencing (RNA-seq) and western blot analysis.ResultsThe 787–839 nt region of MEG3 and the 464–594aa region of CDC23 are necessary for binding, with MEG3 (Δ787–839 nt) showing a reduced inhibitory effect on proliferation and promoting effect on differentiation. MEG3 competitively binds CDC23, enhancing HMGA1 stability and protecting it from proteasome degradation. Background

Skeletal muscle is the largest tissue in pigs, accounting for 40–60% of the carcass mass and serves as the main meat source for consumers, providing a high-quality protein and other micronutrients [1]. Porcine skeletal muscle satellite cells (PSCs) are regarded as the sole source of stem cells for myogenic differentiation in adult skeletal muscle [2]. In mammals, the number of muscle fibers is determined during embryonic development. To promote fiber expansion and hypertrophy after birth, PSC activation is required. When stimulated by hypertrophic stimuli, satellite cells are activated, proliferate, undergo myogenic differentiation, and fuse with existing muscle fibers to enable growth or form new myotubes to regenerate damaged muscle fibers. However, a subset of these cells does not differentiate, but instead undergo self-renewal to replenish the PSC pool and return to a quiescent state [3]. Given that PSCs are important to promoting fiber growth, an in-depth understanding of the factors regulating PSC proliferation and differentiation and the molecular mechanisms modulating these processes is of great significance to improving swine muscle mass.

Skeletal muscle development is regulated by a variety of coding genes, noncoding genes, and epigenetic factors [4], with long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) found to be significant regulatory factors in skeletal muscle development by modulating myoblast proliferation [5], differentiation [6], and muscle regeneration [7]. Mechanistically, lncRNAs participate in myoblast proliferation and differentiation at multiple regulatory levels—transcriptional, post-transcriptional, translational, and epigenetic—using various mechanisms, including acting as molecular decoys and protein chaperones. Of these, interactions between lncRNAs and chaperone proteins is one of the most important regulatory modes. In chicken skeletal muscle satellite cells, lncRNA MYH1G-AS promotes proliferation while inhibiting differentiation by reducing FGF18 stability, thereby restricting chromatin accessibility at the SMAD4 promoter [8]. Moreover, in porcine myogenic cells, lncRNA MGR was shown to bind CDK9 and recruit it to the core promoter region of MYH1A, subsequently activating transcription [9].

During skeletal muscle development, protein-coding genes and noncoding genes modulate regulatory pathways by activating or inhibiting key molecular signaling molecules. In swine, LncRNA H19 promotes myogenic differentiation by activating the P38 signaling pathway via the upregulation of DBN1 [10]. Moreover, enhanced classical Wnt signaling can promote PSC differentiation and improve skeletal muscle regeneration [11]. In addition, lncRNA SYISL [12], LncRNA RMG [13], and lncRNA SOX6-AU [14] have all been reported to be closely related to PSC proliferation and differentiation.

LncRNA maternally expressed gene 3 (MEG3) is transcribed from the Dlk1-Dio3 locus and was first described as a tumor suppressor that inhibits proliferation [15], while in myoblast, MEG3 regulates proliferation [16], differentiation [17], and regeneration [18]. In bovine myoblasts, MEG3 upregulates the expression of MEF2C by sequestering miR-135 to promote differentiation [19]. In mouse skeletal muscle, MEG3 stabilizes PRC2 by binding to its subunit, SUZ12, thereby regulating muscle fiber type transition and skeletal muscle homeostasis [20]. Thus, MEG3 plays an important role in regulating the development of skeletal muscle, but its role in porcine muscle development requires further elucidation.

In our previous study, MEG3 and cell division cycle protein 23 homolog (CDC23) were found to interact [17, 21]. Meanwhile, another study indicated that a MEG3 haplotype inhibits ERK/MAPK signaling in PSCs [22]. However, whether PSC development is associated with MEG3–CDC23 binding or through which pathways MEG3 inhibits ERK signaling remains unclear. In this study, MEG3 was shown to protect high mobility group AT-Hook protein 1 (HMGA1) from proteasome degradation by competitively binding to CDC23. HMGA1 was shown to cause G1 arrest in PSCs by inhibiting the expression of cell cycle genes, including CCND2 and PLK2, and promote differentiation by upregulating the expression of myogenic differentiation genes, including CKM. Ultimately, MEG3 knockdown and the subsequent downregulation of HMGA1 was found to activate the GDNF–RET–ERK signaling axis, thereby promoting PSC proliferation and inhibiting differentiation. The findings presented herein not only further elucidate the mechanism of MEG3 regulation in PSC proliferation and differentiation, but also implicates potential genetic targets and provides a theoretical basis for accelerated porcine skeletal muscle development.

Materials and methods

Ethical statement

During this experiment, the handling methods of the animals were strictly carried out in accordance with the “Regulations on the Administration of Laboratory Animals” (1988), the “Guidelines for the Management and Use of Laboratory Animals” (1996), and the procedures stipulated by the Laboratory Animal Management and Ethics Committee of Huazhong Agricultural University.

PSC isolation and culturing

Porcine skeletal muscle satellite cells were isolated following the method of Li et al. [6]. Muscle tissue was obtained from the hind legs of 7-day-old male Yorkshire piglets. Samples were excised, transferred to cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 1% penicillin/streptomycin (15140-122, Gibco, USA), and minced using sterile scissors. Digestion was performed using type II collagenase (17101-015, Gibco, USA) at a volume ratio of 2:1, with sample incubated at 37 °C in a water bath with shaker for 2.5–3 h. The samples were then filtered through a sterile sieve with a defined pore size and subjected to differential adhesion culture to purify the PSCs. The PSCs were cultured in a proliferation medium containing 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS–AUS050, Wenren Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai), 0.5% chicken embryo extract (100–163P, GEMINI), 1% GlutaMax (35050-061, Gibco), 1% nonessential amino acids (11140-050, Gibco), and 1% antibiotic–antimycotic (15240-062, Gibco). After reaching an 90% confluence, the medium was changed to differentiation medium containing 2.5% horse serum (16050-122, Gibco), and 1% antibiotic–antimycotic (15240-062, Gibco).

Plasmid construction and oligonucleotides

For overexpression and interaction analysis, full-length MEG3 and mutators F1 (425–897 nt), F2 (425–531 nt), F3 (532–645 nt), F4 (646–763 nt), F5 (764–897 nt), and MEG3-Mut (Δ784–839 nt) were cloned into pcDNA3.1 vectors by used OK Clon DNA Ligation Kit（AG11803，ACCURATE BIOTECHNOLOGY(HUNAN）CO.,LTD，ChangSha，China）. The coding DNA sequence (CDS) for CDC23 and mutants CDC23 (Δ1–64aa), CDC23 (Δ65–147aa), CDC23 (Δ148–327aa), CDC23 (Δ328–463aa) and CDC23 (Δ464–594aa) were cloned into the pcDNA3.1-Myc vector by used OK Clon DNA Ligation Kit. For HMGA1, the CDS (NCBI RefSeq: NM_001185155.1) and mutants HMGA1 (K7R), HMGA1 (K15R), HMGA1 (K31R), HMGA1 (K46R), HMGA1 (K55R), and HMGA1 (K74R) were cloned into the pcDNA3.1-Flag vector by used OK Clon DNA Ligation Kit. The siRNAs of HMGA1 and CDC23, and ASO of MEG3 were obtained from Sangon (Shanghai, China). All oligonucleotide sequences are shown in the Supplementary Table.

RNA pull-down assay

The constructed full-length MEG3 and mutator plasmids were linearized using single enzyme digestion. Sequences were then transcribed in vitro using T7 RNA polymerase (10881767001, Roche, Basel, Switzerland) in the presence of biotin RNA labeling mix (11685597910, Roche). The biotinylated RNA was then incubated with total protein from satellite cells at 4 °C with rotation for 2 h. Next, the RNA–protein was captured using streptavidin magnetic beads, with the mixture incubated at 4 °C with rotation for 1 h. Finally, the magnetic beads were washed five times with wash buffer to remove nonspecific binding, followed by the addition of sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) loading buffer, and incubated for 10 min at boiling. The elutes were evaluated using western blot to identify proteins interacting with MEG3.

RNA immunoprecipitation

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) was carried out using an RNA immunoprecipitation kit (17-700, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Briefly, proliferative cells were lysed using RIP lysis buffer and subsequently incubated with A/G magnetic beads and CDC23 antibodies (1:50, abcam ab177148, USA) at 4 °C with rotation overnight. Finally, the RNA was extracted from the magnetic beads for further analysis.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using a SPARKeasy Tissue/Cell RNA Rapid Extraction Kit (AC0202A, Sparkjade Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shandong, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Reverse transcription was performed using gDNA Eraser and a SPARKscript II RT plus kit (AG0304-B, Sparkjade), with the addition of 2 × SYBR Green qPCR Mix with ROX (AH0104-B, Sparkjade) for quantification, according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Data was analyzed using the 2−∆∆CT method. Primer sequences were designed using Primer Premier5 and are shown in the Supplementary Table.

Western blotting analysis

Obtained protein samples from cells lysed with RIPA lysis buffer were evaluated. Proteins were separated via SDS-PAGE and transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. The membranes were then blocked using 5% skim milk in TBST and incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. The membranes were then washed and incubated in horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody. Finally, the membranes were incubated with a chemiluminescence substrate (34579, ThermoFisher, USA) and imaged by a fluorescent chemiluminescence imaging system (ChemiScope 6100Touch, Clinx, China). All the primary and secondary antibodies are shown in the Supplementary Table. SCH772984 (SJ-MX0112, Sparkjade) is used as an inhibitor of the ERK1/2 signaling pathway.

Immunoprecipitation of ubiquitinated proteins

IP/Co-IP was performed according to the instructions of the PierceTM Classic Magnetic Bead Method IP/Co-IP Kit (88804, ThermoFisher, USA). In brief, PSCs were cotransfected with HA-tagged ubiquitin and target protein and/or siRNA using jetPRIME (101000046; Polyplus, Illkirch, France) for 48 h. Then, 10 μm of MG-132, a proteosome inhibitor, was added to the cells and allowed to incubate for 8 h. After the incubation was completed, the cells were lysed in immunoprecipitation lysis buffer. Protein A/G magnetic beads and antibodies were then incubated for 6 h at 4 °C to allow antibody-bead complexes to form. These complexes were then combined with the lysates and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Next, samples were washed, combined with SDS-PAGE loading buffer, boiled and separated via SDS-PAGE. Finally, the bead complexes were evaluated via western blot analysis using anti-HA antibodies.

Cycloheximide (CHX) chase measurements of HMGA1 half-life

The plasmids or oligonucleotides were transiently transfected into PSCs using jetPRIME. After 24 h, CHX (20 ug/ml) was added to the culture medium, and incubation was continued for 0, 3, 6, 9 or 12 h. The cell lysates were submitted to western blotting using Flag-tag or HMGA1 antibody.

RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization

PSCs were washed twice with precooled PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, and then thoroughly washed in PBS to reduce potential background. Cells were then permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 10 min and washed with PBS. Subsequently, the probe and DAPI (1:1000, Invitrogen D3571, Shanghai, China) were incubated in accordance with the instructions of the fluorescence in situ hybridization Kit (#C10910; RiboBio, Guangzhou, China).

Immunofluorescence staining assay

For immunofluorescence (IF), cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 10 min. To reduce nonspecific binding, cells were incubated with blocking buffer at room temperature for 2 h. Samples were then incubated with primary antibody at 4 °C overnight. Next, cells were incubated with secondary antibody at room temperature, in the dark for 1 h. Finally, the cell nuclei were stained with DAPI for 10 min and rinsed with PBS. Images were obtained on a Nikon A1HD25 microscope.

CCK-8 assay

Cell proliferation was assessed using a CCK-8 kit (#RM02823, Abclonal, Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were seeded into 96-well plates, allowed to adhere and transfected HMGA1-siRNAs at a cell density of ~30%. CCK-8 reagent was added at 12 h, 24 h, 36 h and 48 h post-transfection, with cultures allowed to incubate for 3 h. A microplate reader was utilized to read absorbance values at 450 nm, with wells containing only medium or CCK-8 solution serving as blanks for background correction.

Flow cytometry analysis

The cell cycle was analyzed using a Cell Cycle Analysis kit (#C1052, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocols, followed by flow cytometry analysis. Briefly, cells were collected at 24 h post-transfection and fixed with 70% ethanol overnight at 4 °C. Samples were then incubated in the dark with propidium iodide solution containing RNase A for 30 min. Finally, flow cytometry analysis was performed using a Beckman Coulter FC500 Cytometer with data analysis performed using FlowJo v10.

5-Ethynyl-20-Deoxyuridine (EdU) assay

To further assess cellular proliferation, an EdU Cell Proliferation Kit with Alexa Fluor 555 (# MA0425-1, MeilunBio, Dalian, China, Cat1) was employed according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Briefly, cells were cultured in a 12-well plate and transfected HMGA1-siRNAs when reaching a density of ~30%. At 24 h post-transfection, EdU reagent was added at a final concentration of 50 μM/well and incubated for 2 h. Cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, washed with PBS and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 10 min at room temperature. Next, samples were incubated with Apollo dye solution for 30 min, followed by staining with DAPI solution for 10 min in the dark. Images were obtained using a Leica SP8 microscope with three fields randomly selected per sample.

Bioinformatics analysis

Interaction between MEG3 and CDC23 were predicted using catRAPID (http://s.tartaglialab.com/page/catrapid_group). Possible HMGA1 ubiquitination sites were predicted using PhosphoSitePlus® v6.7.7 (https://www.phosphosite.or), dbPTM (https://biomics.lab.nycu.edu.tw/dbPTM/) and qPTM (https://qptm.omicsbio.info/).

Bimolecular fluorescence (BiFC) complementarity assay

CDC23 (CDS) and its mutant were cloned into the VN173 vector to construct the VN173-CDC23 and VN173-CDC23 (Δ328–463aa) plasmids, while HMGA1 (CDS) was cloned into the VC155 vector to construct the VC155-HMGA1 plasmid. The constructed HMGA1 plasmid and either CDC23 plasmid were then cotransfected into 293 T cells using JetPRIME and incubated for 24 h. Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and imaged using a Leica SP8 microscope with three random fields selected per sample.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and differential gene expression (DEG) analysis

PSCs underwent HMGA1 and MEG3 knockdown separately, followed by a 48 h incubation in differentiation medium prior to total RNA extraction using a SPARKeasy Tissue/Cell RNA Rapid Extraction Kit (AC0202A; Sparkjade Biotechnology Co.) The RNA libraries were sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq6000 (Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co., Ltd, Guangzhou, China). The obtained raw sequences were then processed to remove low-quality reads and the sequences were aligned to the Sscrofa11.1reference genome. To evaluate sample variability and identify potential clustering, principal component analysis (PCA) and Pearson correlation coefficient analysis were performed. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2, with a significance threshold of p < 0.05 and |log2FC|> 1) required to be considered differentially expressed. To assess the biological functions and pathways affected by the knockdown, the identified DEGs were analyzed using Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA).

Statistical analysis

A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with a Student–Newman–Keuls post hoc test was performed to compare multiple sets of means. Statistical comparisons between two groups were conducted using a two-tailed Student’s t test. Results are expressed as a mean ± the mean standard error (SEM) with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Each statistical analysis included at least three biological replicates.

Results

MEG3 inhibits proliferation and promotes differentiation by binding to CDC23

In our previous study, CDC23 was found to endogenously bind MEG3 at the 425–897 nt position [17, 21]. To narrow down the exact region involved in this binding, smaller segments within this region were examined. First, a CDC23-Myc construct was generated and found to be specifically pulled down with the MEG3 sense transcript (Supplementary Fig. S1A). Truncated MEG3 mutants were then predicted and constructed (Supplementary Fig. S1C), with MEG3 (784–839 nt) shown to bind exogenous CDC23 (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Fig. S1B) while MEG3-Mut (Δ784–839 nt) did not (Fig. 1B). To evaluate the CDC23 binding site, several truncated CDC23 constructs were generated on the basis of the protein domain (Fig. 1C). RNA pull-down and RIP both showed that CDC23 (Δ464–594aa) was unable to bind MEG3 (Fig. 1D, E and Supplementary Fig. S1D). Thus, MEG3 (784–839 nt) and CDC23 (464–594aa) are the core binding regions.

To evaluate the role of MEG3-CDC23 binding in PSC proliferation and differentiation, wild-type MEG3 and MEG3-Mut (Δ784–839 nt) overexpression vectors were utilized. To evaluate proliferation, the genes CCND2, CDK4, and PCNA were evaluated at the transcriptomic and proteomic levels and an EdU assay was performed. The results showed that MEG3-Mut (Δ784–839 nt) had no effect on proliferation relative to the control, while MEG3 repressed proliferation (Fig. 1F–H, Supplementary Fig. S1E). When examining differentiation genes, MyoD, MyoG, and MyHC, levels were significantly upregulated following MEG3 overexpression, with MEG3-Mut (Δ784–839 nt) showed levels comparable to the control (Fig. 1I–K). In addition, MEG3 cells showed increased multinucleated muscle myotube formation relative to the control and mutant (Supplementary Fig. S1F). These results indicate that truncated MEG3 (Δ784–839 nt) is unable to bind CDC23 and subsequently loses the ability to effectively modulate PSCs differentiation and proliferation (Fig. 1L).

CDC23 interacts with HMGA1 and promotes its ubiquitination

To explore the downstream effects of MEG3-CDC23 and identify potential CDC23 targets, IP and mass spectrometry analyses were performed and 213 potential target proteins were identified. Enrichment analysis using GO and KEGG showed associations with actin regulation, amino acid synthesis, and spliceosomes (Supplementary Fig. S2A, B). When ranking the proteins identified following IP-MS, HMGA1 and RUNX1 related to muscle development showed the highest CDC23 binding rankings (Fig. 2A, B, Supplementary Fig. S2C). Furthermore, MEG3 knockdown was found to upregulate RUNX1 mRNA and protein expression, while only downregulating HMGA1 protein expression (Supplementary Fig. S2D, E).

Considering that CDC23 is a core subunit of the ubiquitin ligase APC/C and its actions would typically lead to a downregulation due to degradation [23], HMGA1 was selected for further verification. The interaction between HMGA1 and CDC23 was further confirmed via in vitro IP and BiFC (Fig. 2C, D, Supplementary Fig. S2F, G). Furthermore, IF and FISH images showed that CDC23, HMGA1, and MEG3 all colocalize in the nucleus (Supplementary Fig. S2H, I).

Next, whether CDC23 ubiquitinates HMGA1 in PSCs was investigated. Cell component isolation showed that CDC23 reduces HMGA1 expression in the nucleus in a dose-dependent manor (Fig. 2E, H). Furthermore, when combining CDC23 overexpression or knockdown (si-CDC23) and MG-132, HMGA1 expression was restored (Fig. 2F, Supplementary Fig. S3A), indicating that CDC23 regulates HMGA1 expression through a ubiquitin–proteasome pathway. Furthermore, after treatment with cycloheximide (CHX), the half-life of endogenous HMGA1 was significantly shortened in the presence of CDC23 overexpression and prolonged with CDC23 knockdown (Fig. 2G, Supplementary Fig. S3B), indicating that CDC23 reduces HMGA1 stability. Moreover, CDC23 overexpression promoted endogenous and exogenous HMGA1 ubiquitination (Fig. 2I, J), while CDC23 knockdown inhibited ubiquitination (Supplementary Fig. S3C, D).

To identify HMGA1 ubiquitination linkage types, the results show that HMGA1 identified associations with K48 and K11 linkages (Supplementary Fig. S3E). CDC23 overexpression promoted the HMGA1 K48 linkage, but not K11 (Figs. 2K, Supplementary Fig. S3F). Based on the ubiquitination sites that were jointly predicted by three post-translational databases, a series of K-R HMGA1 mutants were constructed (Supplementary Table), with HMGA1 (K7R) exhibiting the longest half-life (Supplementary Fig. S3G). Furthermore, CDC23 overexpression did not promote HMGA1 (K7R) ubiquitination (Fig. 2L, Supplementary Fig. S3H), indicating that the K7 site is an important ubiquitination site for HMGA1 stability. In addition, HMGA1 was found to bind to the 328-463aa region of CDC23 (Fig. 2M, Supplementary Fig. S3J), with CDC23 (Δ328–463aa) not downregulating HMGA1 expression (Fig. 2N, Supplementary Fig. S3I). Compared with wild-type HMGA1, HMGA1 (K7R) significantly inhibited DNA replication during the proliferation phase (Supplementary Fig. S3K), while promoting multinucleated myotube formation during differentiation (Supplementary Fig. S3L). These findings indicate that CDC23 promotes HMGA1 ubiquitination primarily through a K48 linkage, thereby reducing HMGA1 stability (Fig. 2O).

MEG3 stabilizes HMGA1 by competitively binding to CDC23

Given that MEG3 upregulates HMGA1 protein expression, but has no effect on mRNA expression, it would appear that MEG3 also modulates HMGA1 ubiquitination. In fact, MEG3 overexpression inhibited CDC23-HMGA1 binding when compared with MEG3-Mut (Δ784–839 nt) (Fig. 3A, B), while RNA pull-down showed MEG3 does not bind to HMGA1 (Supplementary Fig. S4A). Moreover, MEG3 overexpression weakened CDC23-HMGA1 binding in MEG3 knockdown cells, while the MEG3-Mut (Δ784–839 nt) had no effect (Fig. 3C, D). These results indicate that MEG3 competitively binds to CDC23 and weakens its interaction with HMGA1.

To assess the potential effect of MEG3 on HMGA1 protein stability, the half-life was examined. MEG3 knockdown significantly shortened the half-life of HMGA1 (Fig. 3E), while MEG3 overexpression prolonged it, and the addition of MG-132 restored HMGA1 regulation (Supplementary Fig. S4B, C). These findings indicate that MEG3 regulates HMGA1 expression through a proteasome degradation pathway. Furthermore, MEG3 was found to upregulate HMGA1 expression in a dose-dependent manner, while MEG3 knockdown downregulated HMGA1 expression of HMGA1 in the nucleus (Supplementary Fig. S4D, E).

To confirm whether HMGA1 ubiquitination is regulated by MEG3, ubiquitination assays were conducted. MEG3 overexpression was shown to inhibit endogenous HMGA1 ubiquitination (Fig. 3F), while the opposite trend occurred following MEG3 knockdown (Supplementary Fig. S4F). Similarly, MEG3 overexpression promoted Flag-HMGA1 ubiquitination, while HMGA1 (K7R) was unaffected (Fig. 3G). Moreover, MEG3 knockdown promoted K48-linked ubiquitination of Flag-HMGA1 instead of K11-linked (Supplementary Fig. S4G). In MEG3 knockdown cells overexpressing MEG3 and MEG3-Mut (Δ784–839nt), the results indicated that the 784–839nt region of MEG3 is required for HMGA1 stability (Fig. 3H). Furthermore, MEG3 overexpression alleviated HMGA1 ubiquitination and the downregulation of HMGA1 expression promoted by CDC23 (Fig. 3I, Supplementary Fig. S4H), while the opposite trend occurred after MEG3 knockdown (Supplementary Fig. S4I, J). HMGA1 overexpression can compensate for MEG3 knockdown or MEG3-Mut overexpression (Supplementary Fig. S4K). Interestingly, MEG3 overexpression did not inhibit the interaction between CDC23 (Δ464–594aa) and HMGA1, nor did it alleviate CDC23 (Δ464–594aa) driven HMGA1 ubiquitination (Fig. 3J, K). In conclusion, these results indicate that MEG3 inhibits HMGA1 ubiquitination at the K7 site by competitively binding CDC23, thereby protecting HMGA1 from degradation (Fig. 3L).

HMGA1 inhibits PSC proliferation and promotes differentiation

To explore the role of HMGA1 in PSC development, a temporal expression profile for HMGA1 was determined. The results showed that during differentiation, HMGA1 expression is significantly higher relative to the proliferation phase (Fig. 4A). When examining the tissue distribution of HMGA1, it was found to be highly expressed in the spleen and muscle tissues, but only present at low levels in the liver and subcutaneous adipose tissues (Supplementary Fig. S5A). In proliferative PSCs, HMGA1 knockdown significantly upregulated expression of the proliferation genes PCNA, CCND2, and CDK4 (Fig. 4B–D), while the opposite trend occurred following HMGA1 overexpression (Supplementary Fig. S5B–D). Proliferation was further explored using an EdU assay and showed that HMGA1 knockdown significantly increases the number of EdU+ positive cells (Fig. 4E), with a decrease noted after overexpression (Supplementary Fig. S5E), indicating that HMGA1 inhibits DNA synthesis. Furthermore, a CCK-8 assay showed that HMGA1 knockdown promotes PSC proliferation (Fig. S4G), while decreased proliferation was noted following HMGA1 overexpression (Supplementary Fig. S5G). In addition, flow cytometry analysis showed that in HMGA1 knockdown cells, the proportion of cells in the G1 phase significantly decreased, while the number in the S phase significantly increased (Fig. 4F). However, the opposite trend was observed following HMGA1 overexpression (Supplementary Fig. S5F), indicating that HMGA1 can arrest cells in the G1 phase. These results indicate that HMGA1 plays an inhibitory role during PSC proliferation.

Next, the role of HMGA1 in PSC differentiation was explored. In HMGA1 knockdown cells, both the mRNA and protein expression of the examined differentiation genes, MyHC, MyoG, and MyoD, were significantly downregulated (Fig. 4H–J), while the opposite trend was associated with HMGA1 overexpression (Supplementary Fig. S5H–J). Moreover, IF detecting MyoG showed that the number of positive cells significantly decreased in HMGA1 knockdown cells (Fig. 4K), with the opposite seen following overexpression (Supplementary Fig. S5K). IF staining of MyHC revealed a reduction in myotube numbers and thickness in HMGA1 knockdown cells (Fig. 4L), with the opposite observed in HMGA1 overexpression (Supplementary Fig. S5L). These findings indicate that HMGA1 plays a role in promoting PSC differentiation.

MEG3 and HMGA1 knockdown impacts myogenic differentiation

To further elucidate myogenic differentiation pathways associated with HMGA1, HMGA1 knockdown was performed during differentiation and cells were analyzed using RNA-seq to generate a transcriptional profile. When comparing the HMGA1 knockdown group and the control, Pearson correlation coefficients > 0.98 were observed and PCA results displayed distinct clustering between group (Supplementary Fig. S6A, B). DEGs were determined on the basis of q-value < 0.05, │FC│ > 2, with 221 upregulated and 253 downregulated genes identified (Fig. 5A, Supplementary Fig. S6C). Among these, genes related to cell cycle regulation, including CCND2, PLK2, and IGFBP3, were significantly upregulated, while genes related to muscle differentiation and development, including CKM, MYH11, and MYL4, were significantly downregulated (Fig. 5A). Next, GO and KEGG analysis of differentially upregulated genes showed enrichment in positive regulation of cell proliferation, positive regulation of RNA polymerase II transcription, and cell cycle pathways (Fig. 5B, C). For differentially downregulated genes, enrichment in muscle fiber assembly, regulation of myosin complexes, and regulation of actin cytoskeletons was noted (Supplementary Fig. S6D, E). Furthermore, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) showed that genes associated with positive regulation of cell cycle were significantly upregulated following HMGA1 knockdown (Fig. 5D, E), while genes associated with striated muscle contraction and musculoskeletal movement were significantly downregulated (Supplementary Fig. S6F, G). A subset of these genes that were associated with muscle differentiation and proliferation was further analyzed via qPCR and the same trends were observed (Fig. 5F). These results confirmed the reproducibility of the generated HMGA1 knockdown and showed that the HMGA1 knockdown leads to a restarting of the cell cycle, thereby inhibiting myogenic differentiation.

Next, RNA-seq was performed to generate a transcriptomic profile for MEG3 knockdown cells during differentiation. When comparing the MEG3 knockdown and control samples, the PCA results showed a distinct clustering between groups and correlation coefficients > 0.99, indicating a strong reproducibility (Supplementary Fig. S6H, I). Differential analysis identified a total of 601 DEGs (q-value < 0.05, │FC│ > 2), with 507 upregulated and 94 downregulated (Fig. 5G, Supplementary Fig. S6J). Among the upregulated genes, ERG1, VEGFC, and CCNB3, which are involved in the regulation of the cell cycle, were identified. The identified downregulated genes included MYH7, LMOD3, and MYL4, which are associated with myoblast differentiation and myofiber transition (Fig. 5G). GO and KEGG analyses of the upregulated genes revealed enrichment in positive regulation of growth factor activity and cell population proliferation (Fig. 5H, I), while downregulated genes showed enrichment in the regulation of actin cytoskeleton, extracellular matrix (ECM) receptor interaction, and myosin regulation (Supplementary Fig. S6K, L). The GSEA results showed that genes related to myocardial contraction were significantly downregulated, while genes related to chromatin structural composition were significantly upregulated (Supplementary Fig. S6M, N). A subset of these genes was further confirmed using qPCR, with genes related to muscle differentiation downregulated and genes related to cell proliferation upregulated (Fig. 5J). These results further indicate that MEG3 affects differentiation by inhibiting cell proliferation and influences genes associated with myotube formation.

Given that MEG3 upregulates HMGA1 expression by enhancing its stability (Fig. 3L), DEGs coregulated by MEG3 and HMGA1 were further explored. First, for the MEG3 knockdown group, the FC to be considered differentially expressed was lowered to a │FC│ > 1.5 and 987 DEGs were identified (Fig. 5K). To determine shared DEGs between the groups, a Venn diagram was constructed and 202 shared DEGs were identified, including 109 downregulated and 93 upregulated (Fig. 5L). For the shared upregulated genes, GO and KEGG analyses showed that the positive regulatory process of cell proliferation and the MAPK signaling pathway were significantly enriched (Fig. 5M, N), while the regulation of actin and the smooth muscle contraction process were significantly enriched in shared downregulated genes (Supplementary Fig. S6O, P). To further visualize these trends, a heatmap was constructed and showed that genes associated with the cell proliferation process and the MAPK signaling pathway were significantly upregulated (Fig. 5O). These results were further confirmed via qPCR, with CCND2, GDNF, RET, AREG, CSF1, and ORC1 expression examined and found to be upregulated after MEG3 and HMGA1 knockdown (Fig. 5P). These results suggest that the knockdown of MEG3 and HMGA1 may promote the expression of cell proliferation genes by activating the MAPK signaling pathway.

MEG3 regulates PSC development through HGMA1-mediated inhibition of MAPK signaling

Of the genes that were identified to be potentially coregulated by MEG3 and HMGA1 (Fig. 5N–P), GDNF and RET have been reported to affect cell proliferation by activating the ERK/MAPK signaling pathway [24, 25]. To confirm whether MEG3 regulates the ERK/MAPK signaling pathway through HMGA1, ERK1/2, GDNF, and RET expression was examined. The effect of HMGA1 overexpression on ERK1/2 was explored and found to downregulate phosphorylated ERK1/2 (p-ERK1/2) (Fig. 6A, B). Furthermore, the addition of a ERK1/2 inhibitor (SCH772984) significantly inhibited p-ERK1/2 upregulation following HMGA1 knockdown (Fig. 6C, D), indicating that HMGA1 inhibits the activation of the ERK/MAPK signaling pathway. Subsequently, MEG3 knockdown showed an upregulation of p-ERK1/2, GDNF, and RET and downregulation of HMGA1 (Fig. 6E, F). However, HMGA1 overexpression restored the expressional changes caused by MEG3 knockdown (Fig. 6E, F).

Next, a similar approach was used to further confirm that MEG3 regulates PSC proliferation and differentiation through a HMGA1-mediated mechanism. Proliferative genes, PLK2, PCNA, and CCND2, were evaluated at the mRNA and protein levels and showed a significant upregulation after MEG3 knockdown during the proliferative stage, while this upregulation was inhibited following HMGA1 overexpression (Fig. 6G, K, L). When examining the differentiation period, MEG3 knockdown significantly downregulated the mRNA and protein expression of the differentiation markers, CKM, MyoD, and MyoG, while HMGA1 overexpression reversed the downregulation (Fig. 6H–J). Furthermore, EdU staining showed that HMGA1 overexpression inhibited the increased number of EdU+-positive cells after MEG3 knockdown (Fig. 6M). Moreover, MyHC (IF) staining showed that HMGA1 overexpression reversed the reduction in multinucleated myotubes following MEG3 knockdown (Fig. 6N). Meanwhile, MYH7 (IF) staining showed that HMGA1 overexpression reversed the reduction in the number of slow muscle fiber-positive nuclei following MEG3 knockdown (Fig. 6O). These results indicate that MEG3 knockdown activates a HMGA1-mediated GDNF/RET/MAPK signaling axis, thereby promoting PSC proliferation and inhibiting differentiation (Fig. 6P).

Discussion

Skeletal muscle development is a highly regulated process, with lncRNAs gaining increased recognition for their roles in processes such as muscle regeneration, muscle atrophy, and myogenic proliferation/differentiation. MEG3 was first identified as a lncRNA with tumor-suppressor-like activity [26], but has more recently been associated with skeletal muscle development and myogenesis in mice [27], bovine [19], and sheep [28]. These studies have shown that MEG3 can function as a competing endogenous RNA that acts as a miRNA sponge, thereby upregulating target genes to ultimately promote myoblast differentiation while inhibiting proliferation. In our previous studies, MEG3 was shown to promote PSC differentiation and bind to CDC23 during this stage [16, 17, 21], but whether this interaction affects the regulatory function of MEG3 required further elucidation. Therefore, the aim of this study was to show that the interaction between MEG3 and CDC23 is essential to MEG3 exerting its role in muscle development. Specifically, MEG3 was found to competitively bind CDC23, thereby weakening the ubiquitination of HMGA1 and enhancing its stability. Moreover, HMGA1 was found to significantly inhibit PSC proliferation by inducing G1 phase arrest and upregulating the expression of genes associated with muscle differentiation. Ultimately, MEG3 was found to inhibit the ERK/MAPK signaling pathway by upregulating HMGA1 expression, thereby playing a role in inhibiting proliferation and promoting differentiation (Fig. 7).

A model for the regulatory mechanisms of LncRNA MEG3 in PSCs. Briefly, MEG3 promotes and stabilizes HMGA1 by disrupting the formation of the CDC23-HMGA1 complex. Meanwhile, MEG3 inhibits the GDNF/RET/MAPK signaling axis by upregulating HMGA1, thereby inhibiting PSC proliferation and promoting differentiation

LncRNAs can regulate gene expression at different levels and usually have four typical molecular regulatory mechanisms: signal, guide, decoy, and scaffold [29]. Signal lncRNAs, such as enhancer lncRNAs, modulate transcription through chromatin [30], with lncMyoD promoting myogenic gene expression by regulating chromatin accessibility [31]. As a guide, MEG3 inhibits metabolic gene expression in actin downregulated cells by binding to regions with elevated H3K27 acetylation to recruit chromatin-modifying complexes [32]. During the differentiation of bovine myoblasts, MEG3 upregulates MEF2C expression by acting as a molecular decoy and sponging miR-135 [19]. In addition, MEG3 can also act as a guide and scaffold to exert important biological functions [33, 34]. For example, MEG3 inhibits hepatoma cell proliferation by interacting with p53 protein to enhance the stability and transcriptional activity of p53-mediated [35]. These studies show that MEG3 has a rich variety of modes of action. Herein, MEG3 was found to act as a decoy, competitively bind to CDC23, and weaken the interaction between CDC23 and HMGA1. This disruption in CDC23–HMGA1 binding may be due to a spatial or physical hindrance due to MEG3 secondary structure.

Ubiquitination is one of the common post-translational modifications in vivo, with polyubiquitination of a target protein affecting its stability [36]. Several studies have shown that some lncRNAs can affect target protein stability by modulating ubiquitination. In one study, lncSAM was found to promote myoblast proliferation by binding to and stabilizing Sugt1 [37]. Furthermore, lncCRNDE binding was shown to improve Smad3 stability by inhibiting its ubiquitination, thereby promoting vascular smooth muscle cell proliferation [38]. In this study, CDC23 was found to bind HMGA1 and downregulates its expression through ubiquitination via a K48 linkage. Herein, the competitive binding of MEG3 to CDC23 slowed HMGA1 ubiquitination, thereby enhancing its stability and protecting it from proteasome degradation.

HMGA1 is a nonhistone chromatin associated protein that is mainly involved in the regulation of gene transcription. It has been implicated in myogenic differentiation of skeletal muscle [39], but its function has not been fully characterized. In mouse C2C12 myoblasts overexpressing HMGA1, myotube formation was inhibited and the expression of myogenic differentiation markers, MyoD and MyoG, was downregulated [40]. Meanwhile, studies have shown that miR-195/497 and miR-24-3p promote myogenic differentiation in C2C12 cells by downregulating HMGA1 expression [41, 42]. However, the results of this study showed that HMGA1 overexpression significantly downregulated the expression levels of proliferation markers, CCND2, CDK4, and PLK2, and reduced the number of cells in the S phase. Furthermore, HMGA1 overexpression upregulated the expression of differentiation marker genes, CKM, MYOD, and MYOG. These results fully demonstrate that HMGA1 induces cell cycle arrest and promotes myogenic differentiation, indicating that HMGA1 has specific functions in porcine skeletal muscle. These differences might be because even though gene sequences are conserved across species, their expression patterns and regulatory mechanisms can vary due to differences in transcription factors [43, 44], epigenetic modifications [40], or noncoding RNAs [20]. Previous studies have shown that HMGA1 acts as a transcription factor by binding to the promoter region of the muscle differentiation inhibitory gene ID3, forming the HMGA1–ID3 regulatory axis to inhibit myogenic differentiation in mouse [43, 44]. In our results, HMGA1 significantly upregulated the expression of the muscle differentiation promoting gene CKM [45], but had no effect on the expression of the ID3 gene. The results indicate that there is no HMGA1–ID3 regulatory axis for HMGA1 in porcine, but rather a HMGA1–CKM regulatory axis. Furthermore, the research results of Yao et al. [20] showed that the expression of HMGA1 in mouse muscles was not regulated by MEG3–CDC23 mediated interaction and ubiquitination, indicating that the MEG3–CDC23–HMGA1 regulatory axis is specific to swine.

In addition to these interactions, downstream signaling pathways play an important role in myoblast proliferation and differentiation. In one study, activation of the mTOR signaling pathway significantly inhibited muscle atrophy [46]. In addition, Notch signaling has been shown to promote myoblast proliferation and prevent premature differentiation [47], while the effects of JAK/STAT signaling have been shown to be stage-specific [48, 49]. Previous studies have shown that in PSCs, MEG3 inhibits ERK/MAPK signaling during differentiation [22], but its effects on signal transduction were not explored. In this study, the knockdown of both MEG3 and HMGA1 were shown to significantly enrich the MAPK signaling pathway. Moreover, MEG3 knockdown activated the GDNF/RET/MAPK signaling axis by downregulating HMGA1.

In conclusion, this research indicates that MEG3 competitively binds to CDC23, thereby leading to a reduction in HMGA1 ubiquitination and degradation. Furthermore, MEG3 was found to rely on HMGA1-mediated suppression of the GDNF/RET/MAPK signal axis to promote differentiation. The findings presented herein will aid in providing a better understanding of the mechanisms whereby MEG3 regulates PSC proliferation and differentiation.

Conclusions

This study confirmed that MEG3 enhances HMGA1 stability by competitively binding to CDC23 to damage the formation of the CDC23–HMGA1 complex. Furthermore, MEG3 inhibits the GDNF/RET/MAPK signaling axis by upregulating HMGA1, thereby promoting PSC differentiation and inhibiting proliferation. The results of this study provide important evidence for the significance of MEG3 and HMGA1 in skeletal muscle development and their potential use as targets for improving muscle growth traits in pigs.
