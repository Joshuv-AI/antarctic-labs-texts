Tumor Size-Dependent Clinical Outcomes and Complications After Stereotactic Radiosurgery for Sporadic Vestibular Schwannoma
Authors: Ji Woo An, So Young Ji, Kihwan Hwang, Ho Kang, Rahyeong Juh, Chae Yong Kim, Jung Ho Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) is an established treatment for vestibular schwannoma (VS), primarily for tumors smaller than 3 cm in diameter. Its role in larger tumors remains controversial and is generally limited to carefully selected patients. This study evaluated long-term outcomes of primary single-session SRS for VS, stratified by tumor size.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 160 patients with unilateral sporadic VS treated with SRS between July 2009 and September 2020. SRS was primarily indicated for tumors <3 cm in maximum diameter, while larger tumors were selectively treated in patients without significant brainstem compression or severe neurological deficits. Patients were stratified by Koos grade: Group A (I), Group B (II–III), and Group C (IV). Median follow-up was 96 months. Primary outcomes included progression-free survival (PFS), hearing preservation, and treatment-related complications.ResultsThe overall tumor control rate was 98.1%. The 10-year PFS rates were 100%, 97.4%, and 96.6% for Groups A, B, and C, respectively, with no significant difference (p=0.739). Among patients with serviceable hearing, 5-year preservation rates were 91.7%, 36.0%, and 53.6% (p=0.002). New-onset trigeminal neuralgia occurred in 0%, 0.9%, and 6.1% of patients in Groups A, B, and C, respectively. Post-SRS hydrocephalus requiring ventriculoperitoneal shunting was observed in 0%, 5.6%, and 27.3%, respectively.ConclusionWithin appropriately selected patients, SRS provides excellent tumor control across tumor sizes. However, the risks of trigeminal neuralgia and hydrocephalus requiring shunting increased with tumor size, underscoring the need for individualized treatment strategies.

INTRODUCTION

Vestibular schwannoma (VS) is a benign tumor arising from the Schwann cells of the eighth cranial nerve and represents the most common neoplasm of the cerebellopontine angle, accounting for 6%–8% of all intracranial tumors [12]. Although VS is generally slow-growing, its clinical impact is highly variable. Even small tumors confined to the internal auditory canal may lead to functional deterioration, particularly progressive hearing loss [3]. The widespread adoption of high-resolution MRI has increased the detection of VS across a broad size spectrum, including both small intracanalicular lesions and larger tumors identified incidentally in asymptomatic patients [34].

This shift has expanded the range of clinical scenarios requiring individualized treatment planning. Current management options include microsurgical resection, stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS), and conservative observation [5]. SRS has become firmly established as the primary treatment for small- to medium-sized VS, typically those less than 3 cm in diameter, offering tumor control rates exceeding 90%–95% at 5–10 years with low morbidity and favorable cranial nerve preservation [36].

In contrast, its role in the management of large tumors remains controversial. Microsurgical resection is generally preferred in patients with significant brainstem compression or life-threatening neurological symptoms. However, in selected patients with large but minimally symptomatic tumors, SRS may be considered as an alternative, although its long-term efficacy and complication profile in this setting require further clarification.

Although SRS has been applied across a broad range of tumor sizes in clinical practice, most previous studies have analyzed outcomes without systematic stratification by tumor size. Given that tumor size influences both radiosurgical planning, such as dose selection and radiation exposure to adjacent critical structures, and clinical outcomes, including functional preservation and the risk of treatment-related morbidity [78], its independent impact on treatment outcomes remains incompletely defined. Therefore, stratifying VS into clinically relevant size categories—intracanalicular, small-to-medium, and large tumors—is essential to better understand size-dependent differences in treatment efficacy and safety, and to support individualized treatment planning.

While several historical series have reported favorable tumor control for small-to-medium VS, the optimal management for larger masses remains highly controversial due to the increased risk of radiation-induced toxicity. A landmark large-cohort study by Klijn et al. [9] highlighted that initial tumor volume is the sole significant predictor of tumor control, with control efficacy noticeably decreasing in larger masses (>6 cm3). This necessitates a more granular evaluation of long-term functional and radiological outcomes stratified specifically by tumor volume and classifications.

Accordingly, this study evaluated the clinical outcomes of SRS for VS stratified by tumor size, with a focus on tumor control, functional outcomes, and treatment-related complications.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patients

We retrospectively reviewed 355 consecutive patients with sporadic unilateral VS who underwent SRS at our institution between July 2009 and September 2020. Among them, 160 patients with at least 24 months of radiological and clinical follow-up were included in the final analysis. Patients with neurofibromatosis type 2, prior microsurgical resection or radiotherapy, or insufficient clinical or imaging data were excluded. Also, patients presenting with life-threatening neurological symptoms due to brainstem compression were preferentially treated with microsurgical resection and were not included in this study. Baseline demographic and clinical data were collected, and neurological symptoms—including facial palsy and trigeminal neuralgia—were documented at baseline and during follow-up. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (IRB No. [B-2606-1051-102]). Human or animal subjects were managed in strict accordance with the ethical standards of the responsible committee on human experimentation and with the Helsinki Declaration. Informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study.

Tumor size was classified using the Koos grading system based on the tumor’s anatomical relationship to the brainstem [10]. Patients were categorized into three groups: Group A (Koos grade I, intracanalicular tumors), Group B (Koos grades II–III, small to medium tumors with or without brainstem contact), and Group C (Koos grade IV, tumors compressing or displacing the brainstem, excluding cases requiring urgent surgical decompression). All tumors were graded using pre-treatment MRI.

Treatment

SRS was performed using the Leksell Gamma Knife (Elekta). A stereotactic head frame was applied under local anesthesia, followed by high-resolution MRI. Treatment planning was performed using the Leksell GammaPlan system (Elekta) based on thin-slice MR images. The prescription isodose, maximum dose, and marginal dose were determined according to tumor volume using a best-fit isodose approach, with the marginal dose typically ranging from 12.0 to 13.0 Gy across all groups. For tumors with larger volumes (Group C), treatment planning required particular attention to minimize radiation exposure to adjacent critical structures, including the brainstem and cochlea, while maintaining adequate tumor coverage. Details of the institutional technique have been previously described [11].

Treatment assessment and outcome

Patients underwent scheduled follow-up with MRI and clinical evaluation at 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and annually thereafter. Additional imaging was performed as clinically indicated. Tumor volume was assessed on contrast-enhanced T1-weighted MRI using the Picture Archiving and Communication System (INFINITT Healthcare).

Transient volumetric expansion (TVE) was defined as any increase in contrast-enhanced tumor volume on serial MRI following SRS that was subsequently followed by regression. TVE was not considered treatment failure.

Tumor progression was defined as sustained and continuous tumor growth without subsequent regression on serial follow-up imaging. This definition was adopted because accumulating long-term evidence indicates that TVE may persist for several years before eventual regression. Although earlier studies frequently used threshold-based definitions of treatment failure, such as a 20% increase in tumor volume, these criteria may overestimate true tumor progression by classifying TVE as treatment failure. Conversely, tumor control failure was defined as continuous and progressive increase in tumor volume without subsequent regression through the last follow-up MRI [12].

Functional outcomes were evaluated in parallel with radiological changes. In patients with serviceable hearing before SRS, audiological evaluation was performed at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months and annually thereafter. Hearing was graded using the Gardner–Robertson (G-R) scale; serviceable hearing was defined as G-R grade I or II, and hearing deterioration as transition from G-R grade I or II to grade III or higher. Facial nerve function was assessed using the House–Brackmann grading system. The occurrence or progression of trigeminal neuropathy and hydrocephalus was also recorded.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27 (IBM Corp.). Continuous variables were summarized as medians with interquartile ranges or as means with standard deviations; categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons among the three Koos-based groups were conducted using one-way analysis of variance for normally distributed continuous variables and the Kruskal–Wallis test for non-normally distributed data.

Progression-free survival (PFS) and hearing preservation rates were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, with numbers at risk presented at each time interval; intergroup differences were evaluated with the log-rank test.

To further evaluate factors associated with hearing deterioration after SRS, an additional multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed in patients with serviceable hearing at baseline. Variables included age, sex, marginal radiation dose, and tumor size (Koos grade). A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

Patient demographics and baseline tumor characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Of the 160 patients, 20 (12.5%) were classified as Group A (Koos grade I), 107 (66.9%) as Group B (Koos grades II–III), and 33 (20.6%) as Group C (Koos grade IV). Median ages were 54, 56, and 59 years in Groups A, B, and C, respectively, with no significant difference among groups (p=0.421). Median SRS target volumes were 0.07 cm3, 0.93 cm3, and 5.31 cm3, respectively (p<0.001). Median marginal doses were 12.0, 13.0, and 12.5 Gy, with no significant difference (p=0.066). Median follow-up durations were 94, 96, and 89 months, respectively, with an overall median of 96 months (range, 29–181 months).

Pre-SRS serviceable hearing (G-R grade I or II) was present in 65.0%, 47.7%, and 24.2% of patients in Groups A, B, and C, respectively (p=0.010). Pre-existing trigeminal neuralgia was observed in 1 patient (0.9%) in Group B and in none of the patients in Groups A or C. Four patients (12.1%) in Group C had undergone ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunt placement prior to SRS due to tumor-related hydrocephalus, whereas no patients in Groups A or B had a pre-SRS shunt.

Tumor control

The overall local tumor control rate was 98.1% (157/160). Tumor progression was observed in 0 of 20 patients (0%) in Group A, 2 of 107 patients (1.9%) in Group B, and 1 of 33 patients (3.0%) in Group C. The Kaplan–Meier estimated 5- and 10-year PFS rates were 100%/100% in Group A, 98.9%/97.4% in Group B, and 96.6%/96.6% in Group C. There was no significant difference in PFS among the three groups (log-rank p=0.739) (Fig. 1). TVE was observed in 45.0% (9/20) of patients in Group A, 95.3% (102/107) in Group B, and 75.8% (25/33) in Group C.

Among the three patients with tumor progression, one underwent salvage surgical resection at 108 months after SRS. One patient underwent repeat SRS at 70 months. One patient was managed with observation.

Hearing preservation

Hearing outcomes were analyzed in 72 patients with pre-SRS serviceable hearing; 13 in Group A, 51 in Group B, and 8 in Group C. The crude hearing preservation rates were 92.3% (12/13) in Group A, 39.2% (20/51) in Group B, and 62.5% (5/8) in Group C (p=0.002).

No patient experienced improvement from non-serviceable to serviceable hearing after SRS. Kaplan–Meier analysis showed a significant difference in hearing preservation among the three groups (log-rank p=0.005) (Fig. 2). The 5-year hearing preservation rates were 91.7% in Group A, 36.0% in Group B, and 53.6% in Group C. Pairwise comparisons showed that Group A had significantly better hearing preservation than Group B (p=0.001) and Group C (p=0.041). There was no significant difference between Groups B and C (p=0.468).

Because baseline hearing status differed among the three groups, an additional multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed in the 72 patients who had serviceable hearing (G-R Class I or II) at the time of SRS (Table 2). Age was identified as the only independent predictor of post-SRS hearing deterioration (odds ratio [OR]=1.104, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.035–1.178, p=0.003). Although Koos grade was associated with a higher likelihood of hearing deterioration (OR=1.773, 95% CI: 0.919–3.422), this relationship did not reach statistical significance (p=0.088). No significant association was observed for sex or marginal radiation dose.

Pre- and post-SRS hearing status by group is shown in Fig. 3. The proportion of patients with serviceable hearing post-SRS was 60.0%, 18.7%, and 15.2% in Groups A, B, and C, respectively.

Complications

New-onset hydrocephalus requiring VP shunting after SRS occurred in 0 patients (0%) in Group A, 6 patients (5.6%) in Group B, and 9 patients (27.3%) in Group C (p<0.001). The median interval from SRS to shunt placement was 30.6 months (range, 12.0–100.9 months) in Group B and 6.4 months (range, 1.0–15.4 months) in Group C. In Group C, 4 patients (12.1%) had undergone VP shunt placement prior to SRS due to pre-existing hydrocephalus and were not included in the post-SRS hydrocephalus analysis.

New-onset trigeminal neuralgia after SRS occurred in 0 patients (0%) in Group A, 1 patient (0.9%) in Group B, and 2 patients (6.1%) in Group C. The first patient, from Group B, harbored a Koos grade III tumor and experienced symptom onset at 65 months post-SRS after receiving a marginal dose of 13 Gy. The second patient, from Group C, presented with a Koos grade IV tumor treated with 12 Gy and developed neurological symptoms during a 157-month long-term follow-up. The third patient, also from Group C, presented with a massive Koos grade IV tumor treated with 12 Gy and manifested neuralgia during a 131-month longitudinal tracking period. Notably, all three cases occurred in the absence of tumor progression, with serial neuroimaging demonstrating stable tumor controlled-state or volumetric reduction at the time of symptom onset (Supplementary Table 1 in the online-only Data Supplement).

No patient developed facial palsy (House–Brackmann grade II or higher). Treatment-related complications following SRS are summarized in Table 3.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we evaluated the long-term outcomes of primary SRS for VS stratified by tumor size. The principal finding is that SRS provides consistently high tumor control across tumor sizes, while functional outcomes and complication profiles vary in a size-dependent manner. These findings highlight that tumor size influences clinical outcomes primarily through functional preservation and treatment-related morbidity rather than tumor control itself.

The overall tumor control rate of 98.1% and comparable long-term PFS across groups confirm the established efficacy of SRS. These results are consistent with the ISRS Practice Guideline [13] and large systematic reviews reporting long-term tumor control rates exceeding 90% following single-session SRS [1415]. These findings should be interpreted cautiously in the context of treatment selection, as patients with severe brainstem compression or life-threatening symptoms were preferentially managed with microsurgical resection and excluded from this study [16]. Therefore, the present results reflect outcomes in appropriately selected patients rather than all large VS.

Hearing preservation differed significantly according to Koos grade. The favorable outcome observed in intracanalicular tumors suggests that early SRS, performed before tumor progression and hearing deterioration, may maximize the likelihood of functional preservation. This observation is consistent with previous reports demonstrating superior hearing outcomes in patients treated at an earlier stage with preserved baseline hearing [171819]. The relatively lower hearing preservation rate observed in Group B may be partly attributable to the higher marginal dose administered in this group along with a larger pool of patients inherently at risk for hearing deterioration. Although our multivariate analysis indicated that marginal dose was not independently associated with hearing outcomes, this finding should be interpreted cautiously. The prescribed marginal dose varied within a narrow range across our cohort, and cochlear dose data were not systematically available, both of which may have obscured a potential dose-dependent effect. Nevertheless, numerous previous studies have established that higher radiation doses, specifically marginal doses exceeding 12 Gy or elevated mean cochlear doses, are significantly correlated with post-radiosurgical hearing decline [20]. Therefore, meticulous dose selection and strict dose constraints for critical auditory structures remain vital to optimizing functional outcomes. By contrast, the preservation observed in Group C should be interpreted cautiously given the small and selected cohort. Nevertheless, the presence of serviceable hearing preservation even in Koos grade IV tumors is consistent with previous reports suggesting that functional preservation remains achievable in selected patients. Consistent with prior studies, pre-treatment hearing status and tumor size remain key determinants of hearing outcome after SRS [212223].

Complication profiles showed a clear size-dependent pattern. Post-SRS hydrocephalus requiring shunting increased markedly with tumor size and occurred earlier in larger tumors. This finding suggests that baseline tumor burden plays a critical role in the development of hydrocephalus. There were no obvious differences in baseline ventricular status on pre-treatment imaging among the three groups, and patients presenting with obstructive hydrocephalus were preferentially treated with microsurgical resection and were not included in this study. Therefore, the higher incidence of hydrocephalus observed in the large-tumor group is unlikely to be attributable to pre-existing obstructive hydrocephalus and may instead reflect a combination of greater tumor burden and delayed radiosurgical effects. Previous studies have identified tumor volume as an independent risk factor for communicating hydrocephalus following SRS [2425]. One potential mechanism is impaired cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) absorption associated with increased CSF protein levels following SRS-induced tumor necrosis and TVE [262728]. These effects may be more pronounced in larger tumors, in which greater tumor burden could result in increased protein release and more substantial alterations in CSF dynamics. These results underscore the importance of careful surveillance for hydrocephalus, particularly in patients with large tumors.

And new-onset trigeminal neuralgia was infrequent but observed more often in larger tumors. All cases occurred without tumor progression, suggesting a mechanism related to tumor shrinkage and nerve traction rather than direct compression. In larger tumors, the likelihood of pre-existing contact between the tumor and the trigeminal nerve root is higher. Additionally, even in tumors without initial contact, TVE following SRS may lead to temporary contact with the trigeminal nerve. Subsequent tumor shrinkage may then result in traction or tethering of the nerve root. These mechanisms may explain the increased occurrence of trigeminal neuralgia in larger tumors [2930]. This observation is consistent with previously proposed tethering mechanisms following SRS. Although the small number of cases precluded formal statistical analysis, the case-level descriptions presented support this mechanistic interpretation.

Taken together, these findings support a size-adapted treatment paradigm. SRS achieves durable tumor control across tumor sizes, but the risks of functional deterioration and treatment-related complications increase with tumor size. Therefore, treatment decisions should incorporate tumor size, baseline functional status, and the risk of complications.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective single-center design introduces potential selection bias. In particular, large tumors included in this study represent a selected subgroup because patients with severe brainstem compression or symptoms requiring urgent decompression were preferentially treated with microsurgery, limiting the generalizability of our findings. Second, the distribution of tumor size groups was unbalanced, and the number of patients with serviceable hearing in Group C was relatively small, potentially limiting statistical power. Third, cochlear dose information was not consistently available throughout the study period, precluding a detailed analysis of dose-related hearing outcomes. Future prospective, multi-center studies are warranted to validate these findings and further refine treatment strategies across tumor sizes.

Within appropriately selected patients, SRS provides excellent tumor control across all Koos grades. However, the risks of trigeminal neuralgia and hydrocephalus requiring VP shunting increase with tumor size, these findings confirm that tumor size plays a critical role in determining functional outcomes and treatment-related morbidity after SRS. Treatment decisions should incorporate Koos grade, baseline functional status, and the anticipated risk of post-treatment complications.
