Reconsidering operability in late-presenting Taussig-Bing anomaly
Authors: Bastien Provost, Francesco Bertelli, Rajiv Chaturvedi, Osami Honjo
Published: 2026

Three-dimensional computed tomographic reconstruction of the Taussig-Bing anomaly.

Central Message

Late surgical repair of Taussig-Bing anomaly may be possible in selected patients with reversible severe pulmonary hypertension.

Late presentation of Taussig-Bing anomaly is uncommon and often associated with pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH), which is considered a contraindication to definitive repair.1,2 However, medical therapy alone is not a long-standing solution.3 Management requires balancing the risks of chronic cyanosis against the perioperative hazards of surgery in the setting of elevated pulmonary vascular resistance while considering alternatives such as transplantation and palliation.4 Multimodal assessment and careful interpretation are critical to guide management. We report the successful repair of a late-presenting Taussig-Bing anomaly in a patient with hypoplastic aortic arch and patent ductus arteriosus (PDA).

Clinical Case

Institutional review board approval was not required, the need for consent was waived, and patient information has been anonymized to ensure confidentiality. A 14-year-old boy with a known unrepaired double-outlet right ventricle with transposition of the great arteries (Taussig-Bing anomaly) associated with severe aortic arch hypoplasia and a large PDA was referred for worsening symptoms. The diagnosis had been made 8 years earlier. Since then, the patient had been managed medically with furosemide, sildenafil, spironolactone, and acetylsalicylic acid.

His recent clinical course was marked by progressive exercise intolerance and episodes of profound desaturation, with oxygen saturation decreasing to 40%. He also had a history of brain abscess requiring neurosurgical drainage.

Resting oxygen saturation was 75%. Echocardiography confirmed the diagnosis and showed unobstructed outflow tracts, trivial semilunar valves regurgitation, and preserved biventricular function. Computed tomography with 3-dimensional reconstructions demonstrated the complex anatomy, including a 5-mm aortic isthmus.

Cardiac catheterization and magnetic resonance imaging flow assessment (Table 1) demonstrated at least partial pulmonary vasoreactivity, supporting operability. On the basis of these findings, anatomical repair combined with pulmonary flow restriction was undertaken.

Surgical Technique

Surgery was performed via a median sternotomy. After the initiation of cardiopulmonary bypass with aortic and bicaval cannulation, the PDA was ligated, and the patient was cooled to moderate hypothermia (28 °C). The aortic arch was first addressed, using a pulmonary homograft to establish unobstructed systemic outflow. The arterial switch was performed routinely with a Lecompte maneuver. The ventricular septal defect was left open, and a pulmonary artery band (PAB) was placed with a target peak gradient of 40 mm Hg. Cardiopulmonary bypass, crossclamp, and selective cerebral perfusion times were 218 minutes, 176 minutes and 53 minutes, respectively. The patient was successfully weaned from cardiopulmonary bypass with inhaled nitric oxide. The transition to sildenafil was performed on postoperative day 1, and the patient was extubated on postoperative day 4. He was discharged on postoperative day 9 and prescribed aspirin, sildenafil, captopril, and furosemide. Discharge echocardiography showed good ventricular function and peak gradient of 27 mm Hg across the PAB.

At 18 months of follow-up, the patient was receiving macitentan and tadalafil, and his resting oxygen saturation was 99%. The most recent echocardiogram showed good biventricular function, a peak gradient of 71 mm Hg across the PAB, and an estimated right ventricle systemic pressure of 60 mm Hg. Overall, these parameters were compatible with subsequent completion of biventricular repair.

Discussion

This case highlights key considerations in the management of late-presenting congenital heart disease with large shunts. First, the diagnosis of PAH requires careful interpretation. In our practice, a pulmonary-to-systolic blood flow ratio greater than 2:1 suggests potentially reversible pulmonary vascular disease and may support biventricular repair. Second, the combination of severe aortic arch hypoplasia and a PDA in this patient likely created a Potts-like physiology, limiting left-to-right shunting and pulmonary vascular remodeling. Finally, pre- and postoperative targeted PAH therapies may have contributed to the favorable outcome by enhancing pulmonary vascular reactivity and should be considered in selected patients as part of a comprehensive strategy.5

Conclusions

Late-presenting Taussig-Bing anomaly with PAH should not be considered uniformly inoperable. Careful multimodal evaluation, targeted PAH therapy, and a tailored surgical strategy may enable successful anatomical repair.
