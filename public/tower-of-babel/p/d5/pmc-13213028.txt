Novel eco-friendly nitrogen-rich-fluorinated porous polymers for high-performance removal of dyes from wastewater
Authors: Fatima Mahroos, Rasha Diab, Alizah Atif, Mohammad H Al-Sayah, Oussama M El-Kadri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSynthetic dyes discharged from industrial processes pose a persistent and severe threat to aquatic ecosystems, necessitating the development of advanced adsorbent materials capable of simultaneous and broad-spectrum dye removal. Herein, we report the synthesis of two novel nitrogen-rich, fluorinated porous organic polymers (POPs) – F-POP1 and F-POP2 – via a simple Schiff-base condensation strategy for the simultaneous and efficient removal of dyes with opposite charges, namely methyl orange (MO) and methylene blue (MB). The structural integrity and porosity of the POPs were confirmed by FT-IR, PXRD, TGA, and N₂ sorption analyses, revealing excellent thermal stability up to 360 °C, along with high surface areas of 691 and 1009 m²·g⁻¹ for F-POP1 and F-POP2, respectively. Systematic adsorption studies conducted under varying pH conditions and dye concentrations demonstrated significant performance toward both dyes, with maximum adsorption capacities reaching 319 and 427 mg·g⁻¹ for MO and 257 and 313 mg·g⁻¹ for MB on F-POP1 and F-POP2, respectively. Rapid adsorption kinetics were observed, with equilibrium reached within 6 h, achieving removal efficiencies of 48% and 75% for MO and 91% and 98% for MB using F-POP1 and F-POP2, respectively. Notably, despite the conventional tendency of fluorinated adsorbents to preferentially bind cationic dyes, both F-POPs in this work exhibited even higher affinity toward the anionic MO, attributed to the π-acidic framework induced by triazine and –CF₃ units that promotes strong donor–acceptor π–π interactions with the electron-rich aromatic structure of MO. Kinetic modeling followed a pseudo-second order rate equation, while equilibrium analysis using Freundlich, Dubinin–Radushkevich, and Temkin isotherms confirmed that dye uptake is governed predominantly by physisorption through weak noncovalent interactions, enabling efficient regeneration. Accordingly, the polymers exhibited excellent reusability over multiple adsorption-desorption cycles. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that fluorinated porous organic polymers can be chemically engineered to offer a versatile, reusable, and industrially relevant platform for the collective removal of both anionic and cationic dyes from wastewater.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-45753-1.Keywords: Porous organic polymers, Dye removal, Methyl orange, Methylene blue, Water treatment, Adsorption, Wastewater, Reusable adsorbentSubject terms: Chemistry, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

Wastewater treatment is a global concern, especially considering its hazardous effects on aquatic life. Synthetic dyes are one of the consequential pollutants of the textile1,2,3, papermaking4, cosmetic5,6, plastic7, and pharmaceutical8 industries, constituting around 17–20% of water pollution9. The textile industry, one of the prominent industries in Pakistan, India, and China, utilizes dyes at various stages of the manufacturing process, including printing, dyeing, and finishing. This includes a wide variety of dyes, such as rhodamine B, methylene blue, Congo red, methyl orange, and methyl red. Due to their toxic nature, their presence in the environment poses a hazard to aquatic environments and the food web10. Furthermore, the photo and thermal stability of such dyes disrupt photosynthesis processes, thus interfering with the food chain11. Methyl orange (MO) is an anionic azo dye, widely utilized in both the textile industry and as a pH indicator12. The inherently stable aromatic and –N = N– groups in its structure contribute to its resistance to chemical breakdown13, as well as its high toxicity, carcinogenicity, and teratogenicity14, making it an environmental pollutant. Methylene blue (MB), a cationic dye, is also commonly used in the textile industry, as well as in biological staining15. MB is recognized as a persistent organic pollutant16, and its contamination has been found to exhibit several health risks, including tissue necrosis, jaundice, and cancer, among others11,17. Their high water solubility and stability toward degradation processes greatly hinder their effective removal from environmental matrices. Due to their harmful nature, there is a need to develop methods for the effective and sustained removal of these contaminants.

Physical and chemical methods have been reported for the removal of dyes from aqueous solutions12,18,19,20. Though some physical techniques of dye removal, such as membrane filtration and nanofiltration, are effective at a lab scale, they are often non-recyclable and present challenges in the disposal of the resulting dye-laden effluents that typically require secondary treatment21. They are also limited by membrane fouling, as well as their high energy consumption, reliance on pressure-driven operations, and non-regenerability22,23. Furthermore, the use of enzymes for the biological degradation of dyes typically has low removal rates and is unable to degrade all types of dyes24. On the other hand, chemical methods, typically involving oxidation processes – such as advanced oxidation processes, coagulation-flocculation, ozonation, and Fenton reactions – can degrade dyes efficiently. Yet, they are associated with high operational costs and may result in the production of other aromatic pollutants that may be as harmful as the parent dyes25. Adsorption has been found to be a promising method as it is highly effective, economical, and simple26. Therefore, continuous efforts are focused on developing adsorbent materials with improved sustainability and lower environmental impact11.

Porous materials have been used for dye adsorption due to their high surface area and tunable properties. Of these, metal-organic frameworks (MOFs)26,27,28, activated carbons28,29,30, and porous organic polymers (POPs)31–33 have emerged as viable candidates for dye adsorption. While MOFs have high porosities and affinities for dye molecules, they may undergo framework collapse upon exposure to water, making them less desirable for wastewater treatment applications34. Furthermore, activated carbons are limited by their slow adsorption kinetics and challenging regeneration35,36. In contrast, POPs that are constructed primarily from light metals (such as C, H, O, N, etc.) have received increased attention due to their high surface area, excellent chemical and thermal stability, tunable structures, and ease of functionalization. Moreover, incorporating heteroatoms into their structures enhances their adsorption performance by introducing high-affinity binding sites, thereby making them more effective in dye removal37,38.

Among the different subclasses of POPs, triazine-based frameworks are particularly attractive due to their high nitrogen content. Nitrogen functionalities play a crucial role in adsorption processes, as their lone electron pairs can interact strongly with adsorbate molecules, thereby enhancing capture efficiency. In the context of dye removal, An et al. reported the successful synthesis and application of triazine-based frameworks for the adsorption of three different dyes through a combination of cation–π interactions, electrostatic attraction, and hydrogen bonding39. More recently, Liu et al. demonstrated that the high adsorption capacity of their covalent triazine framework (CTF) originates not only from its large surface area but also from the presence of triazine rings, which significantly reinforce surface interactions with methylene blue via electrostatic forces40. Beyond nitrogen incorporation, fluorine functionalization has also been widely explored to enhance adsorption performance, particularly toward charged contaminants. Wang et al. reported that surface fluorination significantly enhances both the adsorption capacity and binding strength for cationic dyes such as rhodamine and methylene blue41, while Byun et al. showed that fluorinated POPs can exhibit pronounced selectivity toward only positively charged pollutants42. Although the introduction of interactive sites can markedly improve adsorption efficiency, it may also compromise performance toward certain dye classes. For practical wastewater treatment, however, an ideal adsorbent should exhibit broad affinity toward a wide range of dye residues rather than strict charge selectivity. Guided by these considerations, we designed our polymers to enable the simultaneous capture of both cationic and anionic dyes.

Herein, we present the rational design and synthesis of two heteroatom-enriched porous organic polymers, F-POP1 and F-POP2, prepared via a simple Schiff-base condensation, and evaluate their adsorption behavior towards charged dyes. The polymer frameworks were engineered to integrate nitrogen- and fluorine-based functional motifs, which not only provide multiple interaction pathways, including electrostatic attraction, hydrogen bonding, π–π interactions, and donor–acceptor interactions, but also modulate the overall electronic nature of the polymer backbone32,43–45.

Comprehensive characterization techniques showed that F-POP1 and F-POP2 possess high thermal and chemical stability, along with high surface areas of 691 and 1009 m2∙g− 1, respectively. Systematic adsorption studies under varying pH, concentration, and contact time conditions revealed that both F-POPs exhibit rapid uptake kinetics and high adsorption capacities toward both dyes, with F-POP2 consistently outperforming F-POP1 owing to its larger accessible surface area and more favorable pore characteristics. At identical concentrations, both materials show higher adsorption capacities toward the anionic dye MO than the cationic MB, distinguishing these POPs from reported fluorinated adsorbents. This behavior demonstrates that fluorinated groups not only serve as interactive sites but also influence the overall electronic nature of the polymer framework, thereby governing adsorption performance. Collectively, these attributes position F-POP1 and F-POP2 as stable, versatile, and high-performance adsorbent platforms for realistic wastewater treatment and the removal of diverse charged contaminants.

Materials and methods

Materials

9,10-bis-(4,6-diamino-S-triazin-2-yl)anthracene (DTA) and 6,6′-(1,4-phenylene)bis(1,3,5-triazine-2,4-diamine) (BATB) were prepared according to previously published procedures46,47. The remaining solvents and reagents – including dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, anhydrous), tetrahydrofuran (THF), dichloromethane (DCM), methanol, ethanol, acetone, phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) – were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (Germany). Dyes were sourced separately: methyl orange (indicator grade; BDH Laboratory Chemicals, United Kingdom) and methylene blue (stain grade; Merck). All reagents were of analytical grade unless otherwise stated and used as received without further purification.

Experimental studies

Synthesis of F-POPs

F-POP1 and F-POP2 were synthesized via a Schiff-base condensation reaction using DTA and BATB as linkers, respectively. In each case, a 50 mL oven-dried round-bottom flask equipped with a condenser and stir bar was charged with 703 mg (4.04 mmol) of 4-(trifluoromethyl)benzaldehyde, 12 mL of DMSO, and either 400 mg (1.01 mmol) of DTA for F-POP1 or 300 mg (1.01 mmol) of BATB for F-POP2. Each mixture was degassed with argon for 15 min, then refluxed for 3 days at 180 °C under an inert atmosphere. After cooling to room temperature, the resulting precipitates were filtered and washed with THF, DCM, methanol, and acetone, and dried under vacuum to yield yellow (F-POP1) and white (F-POP2) powders.

Dye adsorption studies

To study the effect of pH on dye adsorption, 5 mg of F-POP1 or F-POP2 were added separately to 10 mL of MO (30 mg∙L− 1) or MB (10 mg∙L− 1) at different pHs using appropriate buffers; acetate (4.0), PBS (7.5), and bicarbonate (8.5). To allow adsorption to take place, the suspensions were agitated in a ThermoShaker at 25 ℃ for 6 h at 150 rpm, as shown in Fig. 1. Aliquots of the initial and final solutions were collected and analyzed using a dual-beam spectrophotometer (λMO = 464 nm, λMB = 664 nm) to determine MO/MB concentrations. Prior to final solution analysis, the suspensions were centrifuged to ensure separation of the adsorbent, and the supernatant was used for UV-Vis measurement.

To evaluate the influence of the initial dye concentration on adsorption performance, MO solutions at varying concentrations (10–100 mg∙L− 1) were prepared in PBS. 2 mg of F-POP1 or F-POP2 were added separately to 10 mL of each solution, and adsorption was carried out under identical conditions (25 ℃, 6 h, 150 rpm). Adsorption kinetics and isotherms

Adsorption kinetics were studied by adding 6 mg of F-POP1 or F-POP2 to 30 mL of MO (30 mg∙L− 1) or MB (10 mg∙L− 1) solutions in a 50 mL conical flask. The suspensions were agitated in a ThermoShaker at 25 ℃ and 150 rpm. Aliquots of the solution were taken at various intervals over 28 h, and dye concentrations were quantified using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer. E% was determined according to Eq. (2), and the data were fitted to pseudo-first order, pseudo-second order, and Elovich models.

Adsorption isotherms were studied by adding 2 mg of F-POP1 or F-POP2 to 10 mL of varying concentrations of MO or MB (10–100 mg∙L− 1) in a 20 mL glass vial. The suspensions were agitated in the ThermoShaker at 25 ℃ and 150 rpm until equilibrium concentration, after which the initial and final concentrations were determined using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer. Similarly, Qe was calculated according to Eq. (1), and the data were fitted to the following models to determine the best-fit model: Langmuir, Freundlich, Temkin, and Dubinin–Radushkevich (D-R).

Adsorption in tap water

To assess the performance of the adsorbents in complex media, adsorption experiments were carried out in tap water by adding 2 mg of F-POP1 or F-POP2 to 10 mL of MO (30 mg∙L− 1) or MB (10 mg∙L− 1) for 0.5 h. The removal efficiency in tap water was compared to that in PBS.

Recyclability and reuse

To evaluate the recyclability of the adsorbents, 20 mg of F-POP1 or F-POP2 were added separately to 100 mL of MO or MB solutions (50 mg∙L− 1). Adsorption was carried out in a ThermoShaker at 25 ℃ and 150 rpm for 6 h. Initial and final solution concentrations were determined using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer, and E% was calculated according to Eq. (2). The F-POPs were then recovered using vacuum filtration.

Desorption was performed by dispersing the recovered F-POPs in 60 mL of ethanol in the ThermoShaker at 25 ℃ and 250 rpm for 1 h. Ethanol was chosen as the desorption solvent because its polarity enables efficient disruption of dye-adsorbent interactions, removing the dye molecules from within the F-POP framework. The adsorbents were then recollected by vacuum filtration and dried in an oven at 80 ℃ for 2 h. This adsorption-desorption cycle was carried out for 5 cycles.

Instrumentation

Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra were obtained using a Shimadzu IRTracer-100 spectrometer equipped with a single reflection attenuated total reflection (ATR) accessory. 13C cross-polarized magic-angle spinning (CP/MAS) NMR was obtained using a Bruker Avance-HC 600 MHz spectrometer operating at a static field of 14.1 T using a 4.0 mm MAS probe. The thermal stability of the polymers was determined using a Perkin thermogravimetric analyzer with a temperature ramp rate of 5 ℃∙min− 1 under N2 flow. Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) data were obtained using a Panalytical X’pert3 pro multiuse diffractometer by Cu Kα radiation with a 2θ range of 20–50. The polymers’ morphology was investigated using Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM). SEM images were captured using a TESCAN-LMU field emission scanning electron microscope in ultra-high mode. Nitrogen adsorption-desorption measurements at 77 K were conducted using a Quantachrome Analyzer. From this, surface areas were calculated using the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) method, and the pore size distributions were determined using the density functional theory (DFT) method. Pore volumes were obtained through the single-point pore volume method at a relative pressure (P/P0) of 0.99. Dye concentrations were determined using a UV-Vis spectrometer (Shimadzu UV-1800) with a quartz cuvette. Zeta potential measurements were obtained using an Anton Paar Litesizer 500 particle analyzer. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra were collected using a Thermo Fisher QXi system (dual chamber) equipped with an Ag/Al monochromatic X-ray source (XR6, 650 μm spot size).

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization

DTA and BATB were prepared according to previously published procedures46,47. F-POP1 and F-POP2 were synthesized via a Schiff-base condensation reaction, as shown in Fig. 2, to yield yellow and white powders, respectively. This synthetic route is metal-free and does not require the addition of a catalyst, offering a practical and economically advantageous approach. The prepared polymers are insoluble in water and common organic solvents such as acetone, methanol, DCM, and THF. This suggests a highly cross-linked structure with high chemical and physical stability, which allows the polymers to withstand extensive washing with a variety of organic solvents during purification without structural degradation48.

Synthetic route for the preparation of F-POPs.

The structure, chemical connectivity, functional groups, crystallinity, morphology, thermal stability, and composition of the polymers were determined through spectroscopic and analytical techniques, including IR, PXRD, SEM, and TGA. The IR spectra (Fig. 3A) of both F-POP1 and F-POP2 indicate the successful formation of the aminal linkage between the building blocks by the presence of a broad peak at 3417 cm− 1 and 3414 cm− 1, respectively, resulting from the secondary amine (N–H) stretching vibration, as well as peaks between 2940 and 2980 cm− 1, and 2910 and 2920 cm− 1 associated with methyl (C–H) groups48. This is also confirmed by the inclusion of the fluorinated (C–F) group by the peak at ~ 1170 cm− 149. The peaks at ~ 1530 cm− 1 and ~ 1460 cm− 1 correspond to the incorporation of the triazine ring in both F-POPs48. Additionally, the lack of peaks at ~ 1620 cm− 1 associated with the imine (C = N) bond stretching vibration further confirms the formation of the aminal linkage. The absence of the carbonyl group (C = O) stretching at 1709 cm− 1 and primary amine (NH2) at ~ 3430 cm− 1 indicates a complete reaction of the starting material (Figure S1). 13C CP/MAS NMR spectroscopy (Figure S2) further supports the FT-IR results. The signal for the aliphatic carbon of the aminal linkage (–HN–CH–NH–) appeared at ~ 70 ppm48 and the trifluoromethyl carbon (–CF3) appeared as a broad peak at 130–140 ppm50. The spectra also show a broad peak at 160–180 ppm corresponding to the carbon atoms of the triazine ring48.

Characterization of F-POPs using various techniques: FT-IR spectra (A), TGA curve (B), SEM images for F-POP1 (C) and F-POP2 (D) at 10 μm.

As shown in Fig. 3B, both F-POP1 and F-POP2 exhibit high thermal stability, with decomposition initiating at approximately 360 °C. This thermal resistance infers a robust and densely cross-linked framework, which enhances the polymers’ structural integrity at raised temperatures. Additionally, Fig. 3C and D, and S3 show the surface morphologies of F-POPs as investigated through SEM. The flaky and irregular particles of F-POP1 point to a rough texture that could enhance surface exposure. On the other hand, the images of F-POP2 show a more compact and granular morphology. The observed morphological differences between the polymers may be attributed to the structural variation between the amine linkers employed in each of them. PXRD analysis (Figure S4) further confirms their amorphous nature, as shown by the broad peak centered at ~ 2θ = 20–25. Such an amorphous nature is in agreement with several known POPs of similar structures51.

The N₂ adsorption-desorption isotherms of F-POP1 and F-POP2 (Fig. 4A) exhibit a steep uptake at very low relative pressures (P/P₀ < 0.05), which is characteristic of micropore filling and corresponds to a Type I adsorption behavior. This initial microporous contribution is followed by a gradual increase in nitrogen uptake at higher relative pressures, with a pronounced rise near P/P₀ ≈ 0.9, indicating the presence of capillary condensation within mesopores and reflecting a Type IV contribution52. Together, these features confirm that both polymers possess a hierarchical micro–mesoporous structure.

The textural properties of both materials are summarized in Table 1. F-POP1 and F-POP2 exhibit high BET surface areas of 691 and 1009 m²·g⁻¹, respectively, along with large total pore volumes of 1.18 and 1.38 cm³·g⁻¹, confirming their highly porous nature. The pore size distribution derived from the DFT method (Fig. 4B) reveals a dominant pore size centered at 1.23 nm for both polymers, confirming the predominance of microporosity, accompanied by a secondary distribution extending into the mesoporous range. This mixed pore architecture provides an optimal balance between high surface area and efficient mass transport, which is beneficial for adsorption applications.

Performance of F-POPs for dye adsorption

The effect of pH on the F-POPs’ efficiency in removing MO and MB (Fig. 5A and B) was investigated by preparing solutions of MO (30 mg∙L− 1) and MB (10 mg∙L− 1) in three different buffer systems: acetate (pH 4.0), PBS (pH 7.4), and bicarbonate (pH 8.5). The presence of various functional groups on MO, including –N(CH3)2, –SO3, and N = N, as well as the –N(CH3)2 group and the S and N on MB, indicates that pH plays an important role in the adsorption process. Changes in pH influence the π orbitals and polarized sites in the dye molecules, as well as the hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions between the F-POPs and the dyes53. Additionally, it is worth noting that the initial pH of the solution remained unchanged during the adsorption process, as it was conducted in a buffered system.

Effect of pH (pH 4–8.5) on the F-POP’s removal efficiency of MO (A) and MB (B) in PBS. Effect of initial MO (C) and MB (D) concentration (C0 = 10–100 mg∙L−1) on the F-POP’s adsorption capacity (25 ℃, rpm = 150, 6 h) in PBS.

MO exhibited a higher removal efficiency at lower pH (Fig. 5A), with a notable decline as the pH increased. This behavior can be attributed to the protonation of active sites on MO under acidic conditions44, which generates positively charged sites and thereby promotes hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions with F-POPs53. At higher pH, deprotonation reduces these positive charges, leading to weaker electrostatic attraction and increased repulsion between MO and the adsorbent surface54. Nevertheless, the removal efficiency of MO remains relatively high across all tested pH values, likely due to the contribution of additional interactions such as π–π interactions and hydrogen bonding between the MO and F-POPs. To better understand the adsorption behavior at these pH values, zeta potential measurements were collected (Table S1). These measurements show that, under the tested buffered conditions, the F-POPs possess a negative surface charge, with the magnitude of the negative charge increasing as the pH increases. The slightly less negative surface charge observed at lower pH is likely the result of the protonation of the secondary nitrogen in the F-POP structure. In the range of 3.1 < pH < 4.4, MO exists in a zwitterionic form; however, as the pH increases above 4.4, the dye becomes deprotonated and carries a net negative charge55. Consequently, at higher pH, increased electrostatic repulsion between the MO− species and the negatively charged F-POPs results in slightly reduced adsorption.

MB, on the other hand, displayed the opposite trend (Fig. 5B); its removal efficiency increased with increasing pH. At low pH, protonation of the adsorbent lowers its affinity for the cationic MB molecules, which are competing with the H+ ions for the adsorption sites on the F-POPs56,57. As the pH increases and the concentration of H+ decreases, the adsorbent’s surface becomes more accessible for interaction with MB molecules58. Similar observations have been reported by Murthy et al.59. Zeta potential measurements further support this behavior, showing that the increasingly negative surface charge of the F-POPs at higher pH values enhances their interaction with cationic MB molecules, resulting in higher removal efficiencies. Both F-POPs exhibit relatively high removal efficiencies for MO and MB across the three pH conditions tested, which can be attributed to additional interactions, such as π–π interactions, which are pH-independent60, between the dye molecules and the adsorbent. Minimal differences in removal efficiency are observed between acetate and PBS buffers for MO and between PBS and bicarbonate buffers for MB. Hence, subsequent studies in this paper were conducted in PBS to ensure optimal removal of both dyes.

The effect of the initial dye concentration on adsorption capacity was investigated at various concentrations of MO and MB (10–100 mg∙L− 1) in PBS. Batch adsorption experiments were carried out in a ThermoShaker (T = 25 ℃, rpm = 150) for 6 h. For both dyes, the F-POPs display an increase in adsorption capacity with increasing initial dye concentration, as shown in Fig. 5C and D. As the concentration of dye in solution increases, the mass transfer driving force from the solution to the adsorbent is enhanced, resulting in higher adsorption capacities61. F-POP1 shows a saturation of its active sites at 40 and 60 mg∙L− 1 for MO and MB, respectively, after which there is no notable change in adsorption capacity. In contrast, F-POP2 displays no clear saturation point within the tested concentration range.

Dye adsorption kinetics and isotherms

To investigate the adsorption kinetics, the removal efficiency of each F-POP (6 mg) in 30 mL of MO (30 mg∙L− 1) and MB (10 mg∙L− 1) was monitored over 28 h while adsorption proceeded in the ThermoShaker (T = 25 ℃, rpm = 150), as shown in Fig. 6A and B, respectively. The pseudo-first order (PFO) and pseudo-second order (PSO) kinetic models were then fitted according to Eq. 362 and 455, respectively (Fig. 7).

Both F-POPs display rapid removal of MO and MB, with F-POP2 achieving higher removal efficiencies for both dyes compared to F-POP1. This enhanced performance can be attributed to the significantly higher BET surface area and pore volume of F-POP2 compared to F-POP1 (Table 1). The adsorbents reached equilibrium within the first 6 h of contact with the dyes, as the active sites of the F-POPs were readily available to interact with and adsorb the dye molecules. After the initial 6 h, no significant change in the removal efficiency was observed. Therefore, 6 h was selected as the optimal adsorption duration for both dyes. Within the first 6 h, 48% and 75% removal efficiencies were achieved for 30 mg∙L− 1 MO solutions using F-POP1 and F-POP2, respectively. For the 10 mg∙L− 1 MB solution, removal efficiencies of 91% and 98% were obtained with F-POP1 and F-POP2, respectively.

In solid-liquid adsorption systems, the process generally involves transport of the adsorbate from the bulk solution to the adsorbent surface, diffusion across the boundary layer, attachment to active surface sites, and subsequent intraparticle diffusion within the pores63. To gain further insight into the adsorption kinetics, the uptake behavior was subsequently evaluated using the PFO and PSO models.

Modeling of the adsorption kinetics (Fig. 7, Table S2) showed that the pseudo-second order model provides a significantly better fit than the pseudo-first order model, with correlation coefficients exceeding 0.99. Stronger correlations between the kinetic results and the PSO model are indicated by the lower chi-square values compared to the PFO model. This indicates that the adsorption rate is primarily governed by the availability of active adsorption sites rather than by simple mass transfer in the solution phase62. This further implies a monolayer adsorption system64. In both cases, higher values were obtained for MB than for MO, indicating a faster uptake of MB by both adsorbents. Furthermore, F-POP2 consistently exhibits higher values than F-POP1 for both dyes, suggesting more efficient interaction and faster surface uptake kinetics for F-POP2. where KL (L∙mg− 1) and KF (L∙mg− 1) are the Langmuir and Freundlich constants, Qm and Qe are the maximum and equilibrium adsorption capacities (mg∙g− 1), respectively, and Ce is the equilibrium concentration (mg∙L− 1).

Among the applied isotherm models, the Freundlich isotherm provided the best fit for the experimental data, indicating that the adsorption process proceeds via multilayer formation on a heterogeneous adsorbent surface64. This model assumes the existence of adsorption sites with different energies53, which is consistent with the structural heterogeneity of the polymer networks resulting from the presence of multiple functional groups and binding environments. Furthermore, the Freundlich intensity parameter n derived from the non-linear fitting exceeded unity (2.55 and 2.97 for F-POP1 and F-POP2, respectively), confirming that the adsorption of MO onto both materials is favorable57. This behavior is also consistent with the kinetic profiles, where an initial rapid uptake is followed by a slower adsorption stage. In systems governed by multiple weak interactions, the progressive formation of surface aggregates can lead to diffusion limitations and reduced adsorption rates at longer contact times73. Although the Freundlich model best described the equilibrium behavior, the Langmuir model provided Qm values of 318.5 and 427.4 mg∙g− 1 for the removal of MO with F-POP1 and F-POP2, respectively, which are comparable to the experimental maximum Qe values obtained.

As for methylene blue, the linear modeling shows Langmuir to be the best-fit model. The Langmuir model indicates a monolayer adsorption process characteristic of homogeneous adsorbent-adsorbate interactions64,48. The highly π-conjugated surface area of the adsorbent plays a major role in the adsorption of the dye, facilitating a monolayer adsorption process48. This also implies that there is no interaction between the MB molecules, leading to the completion of the adsorption process once the monolayer is formed74. This is also confirmed by the adsorption kinetics, as the abundance of active sites initially leads to rapid adsorption as molecules occupy available sites75. The Langmuir model showed a Qm of 257.1 and 312.5 mg∙g− 1 for the removal of MB with F-POP1 and F-POP2, respectively, similar to the maximum experimental Qe obtained in the isotherm studies. Based on the kinetic and equilibrium analyses discussed above, the adsorption mechanism is proposed and discussed in a later section.

The adsorption capacities for the F-POPs with the two dyes are shown to be higher than other previously reported adsorbents, as shown in Table 2. Generally, for both dyes, F-POP2 outperformed F-POP1, displaying a notably higher removal efficiency, which is likely due to its higher surface area and pore volume, which provide more accessible room for analyte adsorption. Furthermore, the adsorption capacity of MO by the F-POPs was greater than that of MB in both polymers, indicating a stronger binding affinity toward MO.

Proposed adsorption mechanism

To further elucidate the nature of the host–guest interactions, Temkin and D–R isotherm models were also applied and exhibited reasonable correlation coefficients (Figure S5). The D–R model yielded mean free adsorption energies (β) of approximately 0.2 kJ·mol⁻¹ for MO adsorption on both F-POPs, and similarly low values were obtained for MB. These values are well below 8 kJ·mol⁻¹, unequivocally confirming that the adsorption process is dominated by physisorption rather than chemisorption78. In agreement with this conclusion, the Temkin heat constant (heat of adsorption) did not exceed 45 J·mol⁻¹ for MO and MB adsorption, further indicating that the adsorption is governed by weak noncovalent forces such as van der Waals interactions79. Based on these results, the possible adsorption and interaction mechanisms are proposed in Figs. 11 and S6.

Schematic representation of the possible interactions between F-POP2 and MO; similar interaction mechanisms are proposed for F-POP1. Illustration created using Illustrae.com.

With regard to dyes, fluorinated polymers are generally reported to exhibit enhanced affinity toward cationic dyes due to fluorine-cation interactions, as supported by previous literature80–82. This explains the high adsorption capacities observed for MB on both F-POPs, arising from the favorable fluorine-cation interaction83. Interestingly, despite this typical behavior, both F-POPs in the present study exhibited even higher affinity toward the anionic dye MO. This unexpected preference suggests the involvement of additional interaction mechanisms. We propose that the simultaneous presence of electron-deficient triazine units and electron-withdrawing –CF₃ groups within the polymer backbone renders the aromatic framework π–acidic (electron-deficient). These π-acceptor domains can strongly interact with the π–electron-rich aromatic rings of MO through donor-acceptor π–π interactions84, while the negatively charged sulfonate group of MO further contributes to electrostatic stabilization. This synergistic effect likely accounts for the higher binding affinity observed for MO compared to MB at identical initial concentrations (i.e., ~ 91 mg⋅g−1 vs. 50 mg⋅g−1 by F-POP2 at C0 of 10 mg·L⁻¹). In both adsorption systems, additional π–π stacking and hydrogen-bonding interactions contribute to the overall binding85. Notably, the superior uptake of MO by F-POP2 can be attributed to a synergistic effect arising from its higher BET surface area, which provides a greater number of accessible adsorption sites, its larger pore volume, which facilitates molecular confinement, and its relatively more electron-deficient framework, which favors strong donor–acceptor interactions with the electron-rich MO molecules.

This interpretation is further supported by FTIR analysis (Figure S7, Table S4), where only shifts in characteristic vibrational bands were observed after adsorption, without the appearance of new absorption peaks. This confirms the involvement of noncovalent interactions at existing functional sites, rather than the formation of new chemical bonds between the dyes and the polymer frameworks.

XPS analysis before and after dye adsorption was further performed to elucidate the influence of MO and MB uptake on the surface chemistry of the F-POPs, and to confirm that the adsorption occurred without disruption of the polymeric framework. Survey XPS spectra (Figure S8, Table S5) for both F-POP1 and F-POP2 show a clear increase in the atomic percentage of sulfur after adsorption of MO or MB, indicating successful incorporation of the sulfur-containing dye molecules within the porous network. High-resolution C 1 s, N 1 s, F 1 s, and S 2p spectra and their deconvolutions are presented in Figure S9 and summarized in Table 3. The C 1 s spectra of the F-POPs before and after adsorption exhibit a minor contribution corresponding to C-OH species, which is attributed to partially reacted terminal ends of the 4-(trifluoromethyl)benzaldehyde, consistent with previous reports86. Importantly, no new chemical states or functional groups were detected in the C 1 s, N 1 s, or F 1 s spectra following dye adsorption. Instead, only slight shifts in the binding energies were observed, reflecting changes in the local chemical environment induced by dye-polymer interactions. The absence of new bond formation further supports that MO and MB adsorption predominantly via physical interactions rather than chemisorption, in agreement with previous studies87. As mentioned, these interactions likely arise from electrostatic interactions, π–π stacking, and dipole-dipole interactions involving the nitrogen-rich backbone and fluorinated moieties of the F-POPs. Furthermore, the XPS results underscore the critical role of surface functional groups in governing MO/MB adsorption, where surface chemistry rather than framework alteration appears to be the dominant factor controlling dye uptake88,89.

Adsorption from tap water

To assess the real-world applicability of the F-POPs, adsorption experiments were carried out in tap water, providing a more complex medium than the PBS-buffered system. The F-POPs were added to solutions of MO (30 mg∙L−1) and MB (10 mg∙L−1) prepared in tap water and PBS (Fig. 12). The results show a lower adsorption of MO from tap water compared to PBS, whereas MB exhibits an enhanced adsorption.

Adsorption of MO (30 mg∙L−1) (A) and MB (10 mg∙L−1) (B) from tap water and PBS using F-POPs (25 ℃, rpm = 150, t = 0.5 h).

The zeta potential measurements also clarify the differences observed between adsorption in PBS (pH 7.4) and tap water (pH 8.3). At the slightly higher pH of tap water, the F-POPs exhibit a more negative surface charge, which leads to a modest decrease in MO adsorption due to stronger electrostatic repulsion with the anionic dye. In contrast, MB adsorption increases under the same conditions because the more negative surface enhances its electrostatic attraction to the cationic dye. These shifts in surface charge between the two media therefore account for the lower MO removal and higher MB removal in tap water compared to PBS. These results highlight the potential for practical wastewater treatment applications of the F-POPs.

Recyclability and reuse

To assess the recyclability of the adsorbent, 20 mg of each F-POP were added to 100 mL of 50 mg∙L− 1 of MO and MB separately. Adsorption took place in the ThermoShaker (25 ℃, rpm = 150) for 6 h. After obtaining initial and final concentrations through UV-Vis spectrophotometry, the polymers were retrieved through vacuum filtration, then soaked in ethanol and shaken vigorously for 1 and 1.5 h for MO and MB, respectively. Ethanol was selected as a mild and effective regenerating solvent capable of disrupting the weak non-covalent interactions responsible for the dye adsorption, without damaging the polymer framework. The F-POPs were then thermally activated at 80 ℃ for 2 h. This procedure was carried out over 5 cycles for both dyes (Fig. 13). There was no significant decrease in the removal efficiency of the dyes over successive cycles, indicating their chemical stability and structural robustness.

F-POP recyclability over 5 cycles with MO (A) and MB (B) (C0 = 50 mg∙L−1, 25 ℃, 150 rpm).

FT-IR spectra after adsorption and after washing (Figure S5, Table S4) indicate the retention of the primary structural features of the polymers during the adsorption-desorption process. IR analysis of F-POP1 after adsorption of MO and MB shows a shift in the N–H stretching peak from ~ 3390 cm− 1 to ~ 3310 cm− 1. The F-POP2 spectrum displays a sharper peak at ~ 3420 cm− 1 corresponding to the secondary amine (N–H) stretching vibrations. Upon washing, this peak appears slightly less sharp, but still sharper than the pristine polymer as a result of residual dye remaining in the polymer matrix. The peaks at ~ 1540 cm− 1 and ~ 1370 cm− 1 also appear sharper upon dye adsorption, indicating the involvement of the triazine ring groups in the adsorption process. The IR analysis of the F-POPs after adsorption and desorption confirms interactions between the polymers and the dyes without disrupting their structural integrity. Such interactions are likely driven by van der Waals forces, such as hydrogen bonding, between the mentioned functional groups and the dye molecules. Additionally, the triazine rings in the polymers’ framework enable π–π interactions with the MO and MB, enhancing the adsorption efficiency96,97. The spectra show that the polymers possess multiple binding sites that contribute to the adsorption process. These sites likely act synergistically to facilitate the effective diffusion of dyes into the polymer pores98. Further, PXRD measurements after washing (Figure S6) show no observable changes, further confirming that the structural frameworks of both POPs remain intact throughout the adsorption and desorption cycles.

Conclusion

This study reports the synthesis and characterization of two novel porous organic polymers, F-POP1 and F-POP2. Comprehensive characterization confirms their excellent thermal and chemical stability, high surface areas, and hierarchical micro–mesoporous architectures, all of which are favorable for adsorption applications. Kinetic and equilibrium analyses reveal that dye uptake follows a pseudo-second order model and is governed predominantly by physisorption. Both polymers exhibit high adsorption capacities toward both dyes, with a clear preference for anionic MO over cationic MB at identical concentrations, distinguishing them from previously reported fluorinated adsorbents that typically rely on strong fluorine–cation interactions. The enhanced affinity toward MO further underscores the dual function of fluorinated groups as both interaction sites and regulators of the electronic character of the polymer backbone. These results demonstrate that fluorinated porous organic polymers can be deliberately engineered to overcome conventional charge selectivity and enable the simultaneous removal of anionic and cationic dyes. The weak host-guest interactions enable cost-effective and facile regeneration, confirming the practicality of these materials for repeated use. Collectively, this work establishes a clear design principle for developing broad-spectrum, reusable adsorbents for realistic wastewater treatment scenarios.
