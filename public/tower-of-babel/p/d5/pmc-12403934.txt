Determinants of puerperal sepsis among mothers who give birth at Jigjiga Public Hospitals, Jigjiga, Somali Regional State, Eastern Ethiopia: a facility-based unmatched case-control study
Authors: Hassan Nur Ali, Abdullahi Ahmed Tahlil, Mahad Ali Mohamud, Fuad Ismail Daud, Tesfaye Gobena, Chukwuma David Umeokonkwo, Ahmed Mohammed Ibrahim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPuerperal sepsis is a significant public health concern in low-resource settings, including Ethiopia, where it is a major contributor to maternal mortality, accounting for 13% of maternal deaths. We investigated the determinants of puerperal sepsis among mothers who give birth at Jigjiga Public Hospitals, Somali Regional State, Eastern Ethiopia.MethodsA facility-based unmatched case-control design was employed, with a sample size of 226 (76 cases and 151 controls). The study was conducted at two public hospitals in Jigjiga, Eastern Ethiopia, from January 2022 to December 2022. Data was collected using a standardized structured questionnaire and medical record review. Data were analyzed using SPSS software version 26. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was used to identify the determinants of puerperal sepsis. The level of significance was set at 0.05.ResultsThe mean age of the mothers was 30.2 ± 10.2 years for cases and 31.5 ± 6.7 years for controls. The results showed that Advanced maternal age (≥ 35 years) (AOR = 3.01; 95% CI: 1.02–8.83), fewer than three antenatal care (ANC) visits (AOR = 3.84; 95% CI: 1.75–8.43), undergoing five or more pelvic examinations during labor (AOR = 5.81; 95% CI: 1.68–20.13), and the presence of maternal anemia (AOR = 4.35; 95% CI: 2.33–8.13) were determinants of puerperal sepsis.ConclusionThis study found that advanced maternal age (≥ 35 years), attending fewer than three antenatal care (ANC) visits, undergoing five or more pelvic examinations during labor, and the presence of maternal anemia are significant determinants independently associated with an increased risk of puerperal sepsis. To reduce the risk of puerperal sepsis, efforts should focus on improving antenatal care coverage, minimizing unnecessary pelvic examinations, managing maternal anemia, and ensuring proper perineal care through adherence to clinical guidelines.Keywords: Case-control, Determinants, Eastern Ethiopia, Mothers, Puerperal sepsis, Somali Regional State

Introduction

Puerperal sepsis remains a serious and life-threatening infection that impacts women during the postpartum period, especially in low-resource settings such as Ethiopia [1]. It is a major contributor to maternal morbidity and mortality worldwide, accounting for around 11% of maternal deaths, with the highest incidence in developing countries where access to quality maternal healthcare is limited [2]. Puerperal sepsis, also known as postpartum infection, is defined as an infection of the genital tract that occurs within 42 days after childbirth or miscarriage, accompanied by two or more of the following symptoms: fever, chills, tachycardia, foul-smelling or purulent (pus-filled) vaginal discharge, uterine tenderness, pelvic pain, or delay in uterine ovulation [3, 4]. It can often arise from retained placental tissue, surgical complications, or contamination during delivery, and poor hygiene practices [5]. This condition is globally relevant as it not only affects women’s health but also has significant socio-economic implications for families and communities, further exacerbating health disparities, particularly in low-income countries [6]. It is estimated that there are between 15 and 19 million cases of maternal sepsis annually, resulting in approximately 75,000 maternal deaths, making it a significant public health concern [7–9].

In Africa, the prevalence of puerperal sepsis is higher than the global average, ranging from 8 to 15% [10, 11]. Studies revealed that the prevalence of puerperal sepsis was 11.5% in Tanzania [12], 10.7% in Nigeria [13], 22.4% in Uganda [14], 31.9% in Rwanda [15], 17.9% in Cameron [16], 16.3% in Malawi [17], and 30% in Zimbabwe [18]. In Ethiopia, where maternal mortality rates remain high despite recent improvements in healthcare access and quality, puerperal sepsis is the fourth leading cause of maternal mortality, contributing for 13% of all maternal death [6, 19]. According to the 2016 Ethiopia Demographic and Health Survey (EDHS) report, the maternal mortality ratio was 412 per 100,000 live births. Therefore, four women died during pregnancy, childbirth, or within two months of giving birth for every 1,000 live births [20]. Studies have revealed that various factors contribute to the prevalence of puerperal sepsis, including prolonged labor, perineal tears, postpartum hemorrhage, inadequate antenatal care, and poor hygiene practices during and after delivery [6–8].

Despite these alarming statistics, there is a significant research gap in our understanding of the specific determinants and risk factors associated with puerperal sepsis in Ethiopia. Current studies often focus on broader maternal health issues rather than investigating the unique context of puerperal sepsis in the Ethiopian healthcare environment. This gap in knowledge is particularly significant given the diversity of healthcare delivery systems and the varying cultural practices that may influence maternal health outcomes across different regions of the country. Understanding these local determinants is crucial for the development of culturally tailored and effective public health interventions aimed at reducing maternal mortality related to sepsis. Additionally, most existing literature does not adequately address the specific types of clinical practices, access to healthcare resources, and education levels of healthcare providers that may contribute to the prevalence of puerperal sepsis in Ethiopia. This study aims to fill these gaps by providing a detailed analysis of the determinants of puerperal sepsis among mothers who give birth at Jigjiga Public Hospitals in Jigjiga, Somali Regional State, eastern Ethiopia, thereby contributing to the existing body of evidence on maternal health and offering insights that can inform policy and practice in this critical area.

Materials and methods

Study design, period and setting

This study employed a facility-based unmatched case-control design to investigate the determinants of puerperal sepsis among mothers who delivered at Jigjiga public hospitals between January to December, 2022. The Somali Regional State is a regional state located in eastern Ethiopia, which holds the second-largest territory in the country after the Oromia Region. It is bordered by the Ethiopian regions of Afar and Oromia to the west, Djibouti to the north, Somalia to the northeast, east, and south, and Kenya to the southwest. Jigjiga is the capital city, located 621 km from Addis Ababa, Ethiopia’s capital city. The city has a population of approximately 304,400 people, comprising 151,422 males and 153,578 females [21]. The study was conducted at two public hospitals in Jigjiga, specifically Sultan Sheikh Hassen Yabare Comprehensive Specialized Referral Hospital and Karamara General Hospital. These hospitals provide health services to the local community as well as serving an estimated population of more than 7 million people living in the region, including neighboring districts of the Oromia region, and the region in northern Somalia (approximately 3.5-5 million according to the standard). In 2021, Sultan Sheikh Hassen Yabare Comprehensive Specialized Referral Hospital conducted 6,147 deliveries, while Karamara General Hospital reported conducting 2,486 deliveries. The hospitals are also responsible for training, teaching, and research for Jigjiga University’s College of Medicine and Health Sciences, as well as internship training for students from other health science colleges in the region.

Study population

The study population comprised all postpartum mothers who delivered at Jigjiga Public hospitals over 12 months. The case group consisted of mothers who were admitted with puerperal sepsis, while the control group comprised mothers who did not develop puerperal sepsis and remained hospitalized for more than 24 h.

Eligibility criteria

Cases

Postpartum mothers who developed puerperal sepsis within 24 h after delivery or within 42 days after delivery, admitted to Jigjiga Public hospitals during the data collection period, were included in the study. The diagnosis of puerperal sepsis was confirmed by a qualified healthcare professional, specifically an obstetrician, based on the World Health Organization (WHO) criteria for puerperal sepsis, which include the presence of a fever (temperature ≥ 38 °C) on two successive days, along with uterine tenderness, foul-smelling lochia, occurring within 42 days postpartum. Postpartum mothers who had a history of previous puerperal sepsis, who had pre-existing medical conditions, and who had incomplete or inaccurate medical records admitted to Jigjiga Public hospitals during the data collection period were excluded from the study.

Controls

Postpartum mothers who did not develop puerperal sepsis within 24 h after delivery or within 42 days after delivery, gave birth normally, and had a normal pregnancy outcome at Jigjiga Public Hospitals during the data collection period were included in the study. Mothers in the control group were followed through scheduled in-person clinic visits at 7, 14, 28, and 42 days postpartum and bi-weekly telephonic interviews conducted by trained research staff to assess the presence of any puerperal sepsis symptoms during the 42-day postpartum period. This multi-modal follow-up ensured accurate confirmation of sepsis-free status.

Sample size determination

The sample size for this study was determined using the double population proportion formula in Epi Info version 7, appropriate for unmatched case-control studies. The calculation was based on the following assumptions: an expected proportion of exposure among cases (p₀) with number of vaginal examinations during labor of 73.8%, derived from a previous study conducted in West Ethiopia [22]; an assumed odds ratio of 3.4; a 95% confidence level; 80% power; and a case-to-control ratio of 1:2. Based on these parameters, the minimum required sample size was 216 participants (72 cases and 144 controls). After adjusting for a 5% non-response rate, the final sample size was 226 participants (75 cases and 151 controls).

Sampling procedure

Consecutive sampling was used to select cases from postpartum mothers with puerperal sepsis admitted to the maternity wards of the two public hospitals, Sultan Sheikh Hassan Yabare Comprehensive Specialized Hospital and Karamara General Hospital. The selection of cases was based on their admission during the study period, rather than random selection. The selection of these two hospitals was based on their status as the primary public healthcare facilities in the region, where a significant number of puerperal sepsis cases are registered. Controls were selected from the same population as the cases, specifically mothers who did not develop puerperal sepsis during their postpartum stay, to ensure comparability. Controls were selected using simple random sampling from the list of postpartum mothers attending the hospital’s postnatal clinic during the study period. To ensure representativeness, controls were frequency-matched to cases based on age (± 2 years) and parity to reduce confounding. The total sample size was allocated proportionally to each hospital based on the number of registered puerperal sepsis cases in each hospital. Puerperal sepsis was defined in this study as a polymicrobial infection of the genital tract occurring during the postpartum period (within 42 days after childbirth), characterized by fever (temperature of 38 °C or higher), with or without uterine tenderness, and the presence of symptoms such as foul-smelling lochia and abdominal pain. Cases were selected from the medical records of mothers who developed puerperal sepsis during their postpartum stay, while controls were selected from the same population but consisted of mothers who did not develop puerperal sepsis during that time, until the desired sample size was reached.

Data collection tool and procedure

A standardized questionnaire was utilized to collect data for this study, which was adapted from a previous study conducted in Ethiopia [6, 8, 23]. The questionnaire was initially developed in English and subsequently translated into the Somali language, followed by a back-translation into English by independent language experts to ensure consistency between the two versions. The questionnaire comprehensively covers all relevant information necessary to meet the objectives of the study, categorized into the following sections: socio-demographic characteristics, obstetric factors, and maternal and co-morbidities. The data were collected from mothers who gave birth at Jigjiga Public Hospitals, within a period of 6 weeks after childbirth, to ensure relevant information regarding puerperal sepsis was obtained. All collected data were recorded on a specially designed data collection sheet for the study. A team of trained data collectors and supervisors was recruited for this study. Two midwives and two nurses served as data collectors, while a health officer acted as a supervisor. Prior to data collection, a comprehensive one-day training session was conducted by the principal investigator, emphasizing the study’s objectives, data recording methods, data quality control measures, and confidentiality protocols. The training also covered the contents of the questionnaire and the approach to be taken when interacting with study participants (cases and controls), with the aim of minimizing information bias and ensuring high-quality data collection.

Data quality control

To ensure the quality of the data collected, a comprehensive one-day training session was conducted for both data collectors and supervisors. The objective of this training was to clearly communicate the study’s aims and to enhance the skills necessary for effectively interviewing and engaging with the study participants during the data collection process. The questionnaire was pre-tested on 5% (4 Cases and 8 Controls) of the total sample size at Jigjiga public hospitals. The Questionnaires were checked for their completeness, validity and consistency by the principal investigator.

Data analysis

The collected data were coded and entered in EpiData version 3.1, with systematic data cleaning procedures implemented to ensure accuracy and consistency. These procedures included checks for missing data, outlier detection, and verification of data integrity, all of which were thoroughly documented to promote transparency and reproducibility. Subsequently, the cleaned dataset was exported to IBM SPSS version 26 for further analysis. Descriptive statistics were performed to characterize the distribution of variables, with results presented in narrative form, frequency tables, and proportions.

Binary logistic regression analysis was employed to identify independent determinants of puerperal sepsis, allowing for the adjustment of potential confounding variables. Variables with a p-value less than 0.25 in the bivariate analysis were selected as candidate predictors for multivariate logistic regression modeling. Model selection was guided by the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), both of which serve as model-fit indices used to compare competing models; lower values of AIC and BIC indicate a better balance between goodness-of-fit and model parsimony. These criteria facilitated the identification of the most appropriate model that optimally balances explanatory power with complexity. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test was used to assess the overall goodness-of-fit of the final multivariate model, with a p-value greater than 0.05 indicating an adequate fit. To evaluate multicollinearity among independent variables, Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) values were calculated, with VIFs below 5 suggesting minimal collinearity. The strength of associations between independent variables and puerperal sepsis was expressed using adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of the study participants

A total of 226 participants were included in the study (75 cases and 151 controls), yielding a 100% response rate. The mean age of the mothers was 30.2 ± 10.2 years for cases and 31.5 ± 6.7 years for controls. A majority of mothers, 37 (49.3%) of cases and 85 (56.3%) of controls were between the ages of 25–34 years. A significant proportion, 61 (81.3%) of cases and about half, 119 (81.0%), of controls, were married. Most of the participants, 31 (41.3%) of cases and 123 (81.5%) of controls, had no formal education. The majority of the participants, 42 (56.0%) cases and 87 (57.6%) controls, resided in urban areas (Table 1).

Obstetric characteristics of the study participants

Approximately three-quarters of the cases 53 (70.7%) and a similar proportion of the controls 108 (71.5%) were grand multiparous. Nearly three-quarters of cases 58 (77.3%) and controls 117 (77.5%) had received antenatal care (ANC) services. Of those who received ANC follow-up, in most cases 21 (36.2%) and controls 72 (61.5%) visited ANC three or more times. Regarding the duration of labor, 40 (53.3%) of cases and 77 (51.0%) of controls had experienced labor lasting 24 h or more. The majority of mothers, 58 (77.3%) of cases and 83 (55.0%) of controls, had a duration of rupture of membrane of less than 24 h. Most cases 70 (93.3%) and controls 116, (76.8%) had pelvic examinations performed fewer than five times. The majority of mothers, 49 (65.3%) of cases and 114 (75.5%) of controls, had spontaneous vaginal deliveries. 39 (52.0%) of cases and 54 (35.8%) of controls underwent episiotomy. A significant proportion of mothers, 61 (81.3%) of cases and 134 (88.7%) of controls, delivered in hospitals. Regarding previous obstetric history, 17 (22.7%) of cases and 29 (19.2%) of controls had a history of bad obstetric outcomes.

Additionally, 14 (18.7%) of cases and 24 (15.9%) of controls were referred from other healthcare facilities before giving birth. Among the study population, 20 (26.7%) of cases and controls 28 (18.5%) have hypertension. Similarly, 24 (32.0%) of cases and controls 22 (14.6%) have diabetic mellitus. Additionally, approximately two-fifths of the cases 28 (37.3%) and around a quarter of the controls 38 (25.2%) had a history of sexually transmitted infections. Furthermore, 57 (76.0%) of the cases and 63 (41.7%) of the controls had anemia (Table 2).

Determinants of puerperal sepsis

This case-control study aimed to identify the determinants of puerperal sepsis among postpartum mothers in Jigjiga public hospitals. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors, and the findings are reported using adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). The study found that maternal age was significantly associated with puerperal sepsis. Mothers aged 35 years and above were three times more likely to develop puerperal sepsis compared to those aged 15–24 years (AOR = 3.01; 95% CI: 1.02–8.83; p = 0.046).

The number of antenatal care (ANC) follow-up visits was a significant protective factor. Mothers who attended three or more ANC visits had a significantly lower risk of developing puerperal sepsis compared to those with two or fewer visits (AOR = 3.84; 95% CI: 1.75–8.43; p = 0.001). The results also revealed that the number of pelvic examinations was another significant predictor. Mothers who underwent five or more pelvic examinations during labor were nearly six times more likely to develop puerperal sepsis compared to those who had fewer than five examinations (AOR = 5.81; 95% CI: 1.68–20.13; p = 0.006). Mode of delivery was also associated with the risk of puerperal sepsis. Instrumental delivery significantly reduced the likelihood of puerperal sepsis compared to spontaneous vaginal delivery (AOR = 0.19; 95% CI: 0.06–0.56; p = 0.003). The study also found that mothers who did not undergo episiotomy were more than twice as likely to develop puerperal sepsis compared to those who did (AOR = 2.01; 95% CI: 1.14–3.54; p = 0.016). Anemia was strongly associated with puerperal sepsis. Anemic mothers had a significantly increased risk, being over four times more likely to develop the infection compared to non-anemic mothers (AOR = 4.35; 95% CI: 2.33–8.13; p < 0.001). The inverse odds ratio here reflects that anemia is a risk factor (Table 3).

Discussion

The present study investigated the determinants of puerperal sepsis among mothers who gave birth at Jigjiga public hospitals in Eastern Ethiopia. The results indicate that advanced maternal age (≥ 35 years), inadequate antenatal care (ANC) attendance (fewer than three visits), frequent pelvic examinations during labor (five or more), instrumental mode of delivery, absence of episiotomy, and the presence of maternal anemia were the determinants associated with increased risk of puerperal sepsis.

The results showed that maternal age emerged as a significant determinant, with women aged 35 years and above being nearly three times more likely to develop puerperal sepsis compared to younger mothers aged 15–24 years. This finding is consistent with studies conducted in Ethiopia [24, 25], which found that older maternal age independently predicted puerperal sepsis, possibly due to compromised tissue repair mechanisms and increased susceptibility to infections. This age group also experiences higher rates of obstetric interventions, which compound infection risk. The elevated risk may be attributed to age-related physiological changes and increased comorbidities, which could compromise the maternal immune response and wound healing processes during the postpartum period. Similarly, a study in Uganda found a significant association between maternal age and the risk of postpartum infections [26].

Antenatal care (ANC) follow-up was another important determinant. Mothers who attended ANC three or more times had a lower risk of puerperal sepsis, indicating the protective effect of regular maternal health monitoring. This finding aligns with studies conducted in Ethiopia and India, where adequate ANC visits were associated with early detection and management of infections and obstetric complications, thereby reducing postpartum sepsis [27]. ANC services provide critical health education, timely screening, and prophylactic interventions, which are essential for infection prevention.

Antenatal care (ANC) attendance demonstrated a protective association. Mothers who had three or more ANC visits were significantly less likely to develop puerperal sepsis compared to those with two or fewer visits. This finding aligns with previous studies from Tanzania and Uganda, which emphasized the importance of regular ANC follow-up in reducing maternal morbidity and mortality [28, 29]. Regular ANC attendance allows for early identification and management of infections, nutritional counseling, iron supplementation, and health education on personal hygiene and delivery preparedness, all of which are critical in preventing puerperal infections.

The number of pelvic examinations during labor was strongly associated with an increased risk of puerperal sepsis. This finding is consistent with research from Kenya and Bangladesh, where multiple vaginal examinations during labor were shown to increase the risk of ascending infections due to repeated disruption of the vaginal flora and potential introduction of pathogens [30, 31]. WHO guidelines recommend minimizing the number of pelvic examinations during labor to reduce the risk of maternal infections [32].

The association between mode of delivery and puerperal sepsis in this study showed that instrumental delivery was associated with a reduced risk compared to spontaneous vaginal delivery. Although this finding contrasts with previous research from Uganda and Ethiopia, where instrumental delivery was a risk factor due to tissue trauma and prolonged labor [24, 26], the protective effect observed here may reflect effective infection prevention practices during assisted deliveries or the early recognition and management of labor complications.

Interestingly, the absence of episiotomy was associated with higher odds of puerperal sepsis. This contrasts with studies from Ghana [33], Egypt [34], and United Kingdom [35], which identified episiotomy as a risk factor due to tissue injury and poor wound care (11,12). However, the observed association in our study may indicate that, in this context, episiotomy procedures were performed under aseptic conditions with appropriate postnatal care, while tears or neglected perineal trauma in those without episiotomy might have contributed to increased infection rates.

Anemia emerged as a strong determinant of puerperal sepsis, with anemic mothers being significantly more likely to develop the condition. This finding aligns with study in India, which have documented the immunosuppressive effect of anemia and its role in increasing susceptibility to infections during the postpartum period [36]. Anemia not only impairs tissue oxygenation and healing but also compromises the body’s ability to fight infections, thus exacerbating the risk of puerperal complications.

Conclusion

The present study identified several significant determinants of puerperal sepsis among mothers who gave birth at Jigjiga public hospitals in Somali Regional State, Eastern Ethiopia. The analysis of this study indicated that not attending antenatal care regularly, having fewer antenatal care follow-ups, membranes rupturing for less than 24 h, and having fewer pelvic examinations were all identified as determinants of puerperal sepsis. Healthcare providers and policymakers should prioritize improving access to quality antenatal care, enhancing maternal education and empowerment, and implementing evidence-based guidelines for managing obstetric complications. Furthermore, community-based education campaigns should be implemented to raise awareness about the importance of antenatal care and proper hygiene practices during childbirth.

Strengths of the study

This study offers a robust examination of puerperal sepsis determinants by employing a case-control design, allowing for the identification and comparison of various risk factors among mothers who have experienced this condition against a control group. The facility-based nature of the research adds to its relevance, as it focuses on the real-world settings of Jigjiga Public Hospitals, thus enhancing the applicability of the findings to similar healthcare contexts. Furthermore, the study’s systematic selection of participants can reduce biases, providing a clearer insight into the epidemiology of puerperal sepsis in the region, which is crucial for informing targeted interventions. The outcome measures are focused and well-defined, thereby enabling a detailed analysis of both biological and socio-economic determinants, which can contribute significantly to improving maternal health care practices in Somalia Regional State, Eastern Ethiopia.

Limitations of the study

The study presents several limitations that may affect the generalizability and interpretability of its findings. The use of an unmatched case-control design inherently limits the ability to establish causal relationships, as it cannot control for all confounding variables that might influence the outcome. Additionally, the reliance on medical records and self-reported data could introduce information bias, particularly if records were incomplete or inaccuracies occurred in participant responses. Furthermore, the study’s focus on only two hospitals may also restrict the generalizability of the findings to the wider population of the Somali Regional State, as it may not capture the experiences or health determinants of mothers who deliver at different healthcare facilities, particularly in rural or private settings. Lastly, potential gaps in the data collection process, such as incomplete medical records or lack of access to laboratory confirmations for sepsis diagnosis, could undermine the robustness of the conclusions drawn regarding the determinants of puerperal sepsis.

Written permission was secured from the relevant authorities of the study settings, including Sultan Sheik Hassan Yabare Comprehensive Specialized Hospital and Karamara General Hospital. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. For participants under 18 years of age, written assent was obtained from their and consent from their guardian. The data collectors were instructed to maintain patient confidentiality throughout the data collection process. To ensure the confidentiality of participants, personal identifiers such as names were not used, and codes were employed instead. Participants were informed of their right to refuse or terminate participation at any point during the interview.
