State Leadership Academies to reduce cigarette smoking among people with behavioral health conditions in the United States
Authors: M. Vijayaraghavan, C. Bonniot, J. Satterfield, B. Clark, F. Xia, C. Cheng, J. Safier, M. Pamatmat, S.A. Schroeder
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Cigarette smoking prevalence is high in behavioral health populations.•State behavioral health leaders can play a role in reducing smoking prevalence.•We implemented Leadership Academies in 24 states to increase their engagement in addressing smoking.•Using RE-AIM, we evaluated the 24 state Leadership Academies.•The 24 states implemented action plans that helped reduce smoking prevalence.

Introduction

In the United States (U.S.), the prevalence of cigarette smoking has declined among people with behavioral health conditions, but a substantial gap remains between those with and without these conditions (Han et al., 2022). In 2021, the Substance Abuse Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA) estimated that 57.8 million adults—22.8 % of the adult U.S. population—have mental illness (Substance Abuse Mental Health Services Administration, 2021). In 2021, 46.3 million (16.5 %) people aged 12 or older had a substance use disorder in the past year, including alcohol or illicit drug use.

Compared to the 11.5 % prevalence of cigarette smoking in the general population (Cornelius et al., 2023), the prevalence of cigarette smoking among people with any mental illness is 22.8 % (Loretan et al., 2022). Disparities exist among people with mental illness: American Indians or Alaskan Natives, gender and sexual minorities, those living below the federal poverty line, and those with justice involvement have much higher rates of cigarette smoking than their counterparts (Loretan et al., 2022). Among people with substance use disorders, the prevalence of cigarette smoking is 50 % to 70 % (Han et al., 2023, Guydish et al., 2019), and those living below the federal poverty line and with justice involvement have much higher rates of smoking than those without (Saloner et al., 2023). Over half of the 480,000 people who die from cigarette smoking yearly in the U.S. are estimated to be people with behavioral health conditions (Prochaska et al., 2017).

Barriers to providing cessation services among behavioral health populations include a lack of awareness among behavioral health providers of the high rates of smoking and limited state leadership buy-in to address smoking at systems and policy levels (Schroeder et al., 2018, Santhosh et al., 2014). To overcome these barriers, in 2007 the University of California, San Francisco Smoking Cessation Leadership Center (SCLC) partnered with SAMHSA to launch a series of initiatives dedicated to addressing smoking among behavioral health populations (Santhosh et al., 2014). One of these initiatives that began in 2010 included the state Leadership Academies for Wellness and Smoking Cessation (Schroeder et al., 2018, Santhosh et al., 2014).

The Leadership Academies were an inter-disciplinary statewide collaboration of representatives from public health, tobacco control, mental health, and substance use. Through a facilitated leadership convening, states used the Performance Partnership Model—a model focused on “low-cost, no cost” strategies—to establish baseline smoking prevalence, set a target for reduction in smoking prevalence, and develop strategies to reach and maintain those targets (Revell and Meriwether, 2011). Leadership Academies were initially supported by SCLC, and in 2011 SCLC received a contract from SAMHSA to provide technical assistance to states. In 2018, SCLC received a grant from SAMHSA to be the first Center of Excellence for Tobacco Free Recovery, continuing and expanding the work that it started in 2010 to new state Leadership Academies. In this manuscript, we use the RE-AIM framework (Glasgow et al., 1999) to evaluate SCLC’s Leadership Academies as a multi-layered framework to create and sustain engagement with state behavioral health leaders to reduce smoking in behavioral health populations.

Materials and methods

Leadership Academies procedures and setting

States were selected to become Leadership Academy participants based on the following criteria: an invitation from SCLC to participate because of a high cigarette smoking prevalence (Truth Initiative, 2017), and established state leadership buy-in to address smoking (Table 1). Each state selected to participate received a two-day facilitated summit of 30 to 40 key decision-makers, including interdisciplinary public health leaders, community champions, and peers with lived experiences of mental health and/or substance use. They were charged with developing an equity-oriented action plan to reduce smoking in their state behavioral health populations. In the pre-planning phase, SCLC worked with state planning committees to identify summit participants who represented key sectors for collaboration across state mental health, substance use, tobacco control, and public health. States then used the Performance Partnership Model, a leadership action model that involved taking a group through four simple questions: (a) Where are we now (baseline)?, (b) Where do want to be (target)?, (c) How will we get there (action plan with multiple strategies), and (d) How will we know we are getting there (the impact and measurement plan)? (Revell and Meriwether, 2011, Revell and Schroeder, 2005) Using this approach, state planning committees assessed their baseline smoking prevalence among people with behavioral health conditions using the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) (Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System Questionnaire, 2022). States then used BRFSS to identify 5-year target reductions in smoking prevalence among their behavioral health populations. During the two-day summit, participants jointly developed an action plan that included strategies across domains of data acquisition, education, policy initiatives, and systems change. After the summit, SCLC provided technical assistance to address roadblocks to achieving the action plan for up to three years and provided educational resources through a continuing education webinar program. Technical assistance included administrative support to facilitate ongoing work, provision of toolkits or resources to support action plans, and evaluating and addressing roadblocks to implementation.

Data sources and measures

States relied on two BRFSS measures, current cigarette smoking among those with frequent poor mental health and heavy drinking, which we report as primary effectiveness metrics at the time of the summit, 18 months after the summit (i.e., mid-point), and five years after the summit (Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System Questionnaire, 2022).

We asked all states to complete progress surveys after their participation in the Leadership Academies. Nine states completed their surveys six to twelve months after the summit, and 15 states completed the survey after one year. A state representative answered a Qualtrics survey on the extent to which: (a) participating in the Academy increased quit attempts among patients with behavioral health conditions, (b) their action plan was being implemented, and (c) awareness of the importance of tobacco dependence treatment among behavioral health providers had increased (responses assessed on a five-point Likert scale from strongly agree to strongly disagree). Respondents also answered in free text about benchmarks, successes, and challenges experienced when implementing their action plans.

Analysis

We used the RE-AIM framework (Glasgow et al., 1999) and a multi-methods approach to evaluate SCLC’s Leadership Academies (Table 1). We evaluated Reach by the number of states invited to participate in the Academies. We assessed Effectiveness by estimating annual prevalence declines in cigarette smoking among people with frequent poor mental health and heavy drinking. We used linear mixed-effects models, including time as a fixed effect and a random intercept for states to account for correlation, and estimated annual mean prevalence decline in smoking prevalence across the five-year time frame. The eight states that held Leadership Academies in the past 5 years have not yet reached their five-year time points, and therefore their annual decline is based on estimates between baseline and 18-month follow-up. We assessed measures of adoption at the organizational level using process metrics obtained from the progress survey, reporting the proportion among state respondents who agreed that the Leadership Academies had increased quit attempts among behavioral health populations, awareness among behavioral health providers on the need to treat tobacco dependence, and implementation of action plans. Other metrics of adoption included the number of providers trained, collaborations facilitated between organizations, and webinars hosted.

To evaluate Implementation and Maintenance, we qualitatively analyzed free text responses in the progress surveys on benchmarks, successes, and barriers to implementing state action plans using content analysis (Hsieh and Shannon, 2005). We examined responses for each of the questions and thematically coded them for progress made in key strategies including data acquisition, education, policy, and systems change. One author (BC) developed a codebook that reflected each of the four strategies and assigned fine-grained codes to text from the progress surveys. Using a process of directed and summative content analysis (Hsieh and Shannon, 2005), we summarized codes into themes and sub-themes and counted the number of instances keywords or codes were represented in the text using Excel. We report key themes and sub-themes as well as emblematic quotes from state respondents that address Implementation and Maintenance outcomes. This work was considered exempt by the University of California, San Francisco Institutional Review Board (IRB # 23-39017). Quantitative analyses were conducted using R, version 4.4.1.

Effectiveness

Using linear mixed models, the mean annual cigarette smoking prevalence decline among people with frequent poor mental health was 1.2 % (95 % CI 0.9 %–1.4 %) per year after enrollment into the academies (Fig. 2). Adoption

Of the respondents, 83 % (20 out of 24 respondents) agreed that awareness of the importance of treating tobacco dependence had increased among behavioral health providers across their state, and 58 % (10 out of 17 respondents) agreed that the academy had helped increase quit attempts. Of 12 respondents, three out of 12 states reported they had made over 50 % progress in implementing their action plans at the time of the progress survey and all had made some progress in implementing their action plans. Between 2010 and 2023, SCLC hosted 127 webinars, which were attended by 57,312 individuals across the US. Between 2018 and 2023, SCLC facilitated collaborations between 2773 organizations.

Implementation

As part of SCLC’s technical assistance, SCLC staff aided states with publicly available state-level data from SAMHSA to better track cigarette smoking prevalence outcomes for their own state and to identify facilities in their state that could benefit from increased outreach. SCLC’s website had a data dashboard that provided a snapshot of a state’s progress in cigarette smoking prevalence reduction relative to their own proposed target reduction and to other states.

Education

The primary efforts on education were around resource building, dissemination of training and toolkits, and improving accessibility of cessation resources to behavioral health providers in the community. Nineteen of the 24 states reported making progress around education in their action plans, with 13 states offering training for providers, three states offering training to patients around cessation, one state integrating a peer- and wellness-based model for training its peers, and two states increasing awareness around their pharmacists’ prescriptive authority to furnish medications for cessation without a prescription from a healthcare provider. Several states promoted media campaigns to advertise existing resources and developed webinars and learning opportunities. Another state developed a dedicated curriculum called Breaking the Habit in Behavioral Health (BH2) project, which trained 950 healthcare providers, administrators, and staff in behavioral health settings. One state devoted its efforts towards developing a peer program called Peer Recovery Specialists (PRSS) and established “A Wellness Coach Training for PRSS. The program has trained PRSS’s in assisting persons in establishing wellness goals of which tobacco cessation plays a significant role. Over 90 % of Community Mental Health Centers [in our state] have trained Wellness coaches.”

Policy

Eleven states reported making progress around policy initiatives, with tobacco-free grounds policies being the most common. Individual states reported policies focusing on expanding pharmacists’ prescriptive authority or tobacco 21 laws restricting the sales of commercial tobacco products to those less than 21 years of age. Another state reported leveraging funding from the American Lung Association to establish smoke-free housing and community mental health clinics. Participants in a Southern state had initiated discussions with legislators around smoke-free policy implementation in cities where casinos were located. Three states focused on policies to increase coverage of and access to the seven FDA-approved medications for cessation. Several states pursued policy changes to ensure that all Medicaid members received access to 12 weeks of any of the seven FDA-approved medications for cessation, including for individuals in residential substance use treatment.

Systems change

Systems change strategies included modifying the electronic health record to increase the delivery of cessation services, increasing billing for the provision of cessation services, standardizing the delivery of cessation services in healthcare settings, and better integrating state Quitline services with other health and behavioral health services. Sixteen of the 24 states reported making progress around systems change as part of their action plan. Two states described processes to better integrate smoking history questions and Quitline referral links within their electronic health record. Seven states discussed making changes or improvements to their Quitline services to better reach populations that were disproportionately impacted. One of the states expanded its “partnership with Medicaid [to] provide medication benefit via the Quitline for Medicaid participants and also the state received a Medicaid Administrative match for Quitline services.” Another state worked with its Department of Healthcare Services, Insurance Division, to expand Medicaid benefits for tobacco dependence for unlimited cessation attempts, when previously it only covered treatment for two 90-day trials.

Maintenance

While all states were able to sustain efforts to implement their action plans to some extent and many documented successes, several states highlighted themes around sustainability and maintenance of cessation services initiatives (Table 2).

Funding

Funding was one of the barriers to sustaining efforts: states mentioned frequent cuts to behavioral health services, which also limited the extent to which those states could address cigarette smoking. One state reported, “a challenge has been an enormous cut in funding for behavioral health services. While [behavioral health] and providers are struggling with survival, the momentum on tobacco issue has slowed.” However, the same state reported that leadership buy-in was critical to ensuring that cessation services was prioritized, “fortunately, the Director of Behavioral Health Services, understands how reduction of tobacco use can help…and she has made tobacco cessation a priority.” Several states mentioned that dedicated funding to develop cessation services guidelines and resources was not available, limiting states’ capacity to disseminate those resources.

Policy

Efforts to support tobacco-free grounds policies were hindered by a lack of support for such policies in some behavioral health facilities. Some of the specific concerns included “union opposition” to tobacco-free campus-wide policies in behavioral health facilities and barriers to adopting a wellness or recovery policy that included smoking, nutrition, and fitness. Moreover, legislative priorities sometimes did not match state action plans for tobacco control. Several states had moved forward with proposals to extend Medicaid benefits for NRT or expand cessation services coverage in private plans, but they were not passed by their state legislatures.

Staffing and logistics of convening committees

Leadership and staff turnover, as well as changes in state funding priorities that restricted staff capacity, were some of the major barriers to sustaining longitudinal engagement in the provision of tobacco treatment. One of the states reported “continuous change in staff leadership. Staff turnover within mental health/substance use partner organizations, along with capacity within current staff” was a barrier. The frequent changes in leadership and staff made it difficult for states to sustain their efforts.

Despite these universal barriers to program implementation, one of the key enablers of sustainability was leadership buy-in for reducing smoking prevalence and having a cessation champion that carried over even with changes in staff and leadership.

Discussion

Leadership Academies were an effective engagement and action framework to address cigarette smoking in behavioral health populations in 24 states. Most states had a one percentage annual decline in smoking prevalence among people who reported frequent poor mental health and heavy drinking after enrolling in the Leadership Academy. The Leadership Academy structure may have facilitated a reduction in smoking through (1) cross-collaboration between public health, behavioral health, and tobacco control; (2) an emphasis on identifying data sources to better track state initiatives in behavioral health priority populations; and (3) widespread dissemination of educational resources for service providers and community advocates in behavioral health. The leadership academies reached over 50,000 providers and community advocates through its national webinar series and facilitated collaborations among over 2000 organizations that were previously not collaborating. By implementing the Leadership Academies across states with different implementation and policy contexts, the Academies allowed for shared learning and partnership among states to further increase awareness and improve the provision of cessation services among behavioral health populations.

The Leadership Academy has several strengths. First, while previous work has shown the effects of individual statewide efforts to expand cessation services, including screening and treatment within behavioral health settings, we are aware of no others like the Leadership Academies that have approached this problem across states. For example, in 2016, Texas launched an evidence-based, multi-level, multicomponent implementation program called Taking Texas Tobacco Free (Correa-Fernandez et al., 2019, Taing et al., 2021, Samaha et al., 2017, Correa-Fernandez et al., 2017). This program facilitated the implementation of tobacco-free workplaces with cessation services in 18 mental health authorities across Texas, comprising 250 individual community behavioral health centers. Some of the accomplishments of Taking Texas Tobacco Free included training over 5,000 providers in behavioral health on smoking cessation, increasing providers’ assessments of cigarette smoking in mental health facilities, and reaching over 115,000 community members through outreach and education (Correa-Fernandez et al., 2019, Taing et al., 2021, Samaha et al., 2017, Correa-Fernandez et al., 2017).

As another example, the University of Wisconsin Center for Tobacco Research and Intervention has several state-wide initiatives including a pharmacist-led opt-out cessation treatment model that was integrated with the Wisconsin Tobacco Quit Line for people who were currently smoking and hospitalized in their inpatient hospital settings (Trapskin et al., 2022). The same program reported on its longitudinal state-wide successes in integrating cessation services within healthcare and particularly among behavioral health, providing training and technical assistance to their clinicians and health systems, increasing the availability of covered tobacco treatment by reducing barriers to treatment, and increasing the use of Quitline services (Redmond et al., 2010, Adsit et al., 2014).

While each of these programs made deep inroads in treating smoking among behavioral health populations in their states, the success of the Leadership Academy was in bringing together these early adopters to share lessons and best practices with other states that may have been further behind. These early adopter states highlighted similar issues around sustainability, calling for the inclusion of a cessation champion who oversees the implementation and maintenance of the tobacco-free workplace, offering cessation education as part of the workplace's new employee orientation, and continually offering educational resources for providers and patients (Correa-Fernandez et al., 2019, Redmond et al., 2010).

Second, the Leadership Academy provided a framework for an action plan that encompassed domains of data, education, policy, and systems change, without prescribing specific strategies. This approach increased the breadth and range of initiatives, allowing states the freedom to push forward agendas and collaborations by virtue of having all necessary stakeholders at the table.

Third, the Leadership Academy model facilitated the creation of a wide-reaching webinar series and educational resources that were at the forefront of current evidence for cessation services. The webinar series and educational resources were disseminated across our listservs that consisted of over 5000 invested community members, representing healthcare providers, behavioral health providers, educators, and community advocates. Through these resources, we were able to offer continuing education credits to thousands of individuals across diverse workforces including health educators, behavioral health providers, social workers, and clinicians. This approach allowed for sharing evidence-based best practices for cessation, thereby raising the bar, and setting community standards for cessation services across healthcare and behavioral health settings.

We highlight several limitations. The Leadership Academy was a multi-layer implementation strategy designed to enhance capacity and provide technical assistance to states to reduce tobacco use among their behavioral health populations. We are unable to draw causal inferences between declines in tobacco prevalence and participation in the Leadership Academies. Ideally, it would have been helpful to compare smoking rates over time between states with and without the Academies. However, because this was a service program with varying start and stop dates, we are unable to statistically link the activities of the program to declines in cigarette smoking. There was missing data on metrics of adoption. It is unclear if those missing data reflect a selection or respondent bias where states more likely to have sustained their action plan responded to the progress survey.

Conclusion

The strengths of the Leadership Academies outweigh these limitations by creating a framework for change that centers cigarette smoking at the forefront of behavioral health in the US. Focusing on behavioral health populations became one of the key strategies to reduce smoking prevalence overall, thereby improving population health. These efforts may have allowed for increased state and federal government investment and in the earmarking of funding for integrating cessation into behavioral health. The Leadership Academy, with sustained funding and commitment from the state and federal government, could promote further reductions in disparities in smoking and smoking-related health outcomes among behavioral health populations.
