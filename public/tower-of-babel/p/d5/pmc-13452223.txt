Assessment of planning organ at risk volume margins in non-adaptive pancreatic stereotactic body radiotherapy
Authors: Maxwell Robinson, Ahmed Eltinay, Ben George, Robert Owens, James Good, Somnath Mukherjee, Rebecca Muirhead
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and PurposeOrgan at risk (OAR) motion results in increased risk of radiotherapy treatment side effects due to the potential for greater dose than intended being delivered. This is of particular concern in stereotactic ablative body radiotherapy (SABR). The study aim was to assess the role of planning organ at risk volume (PRV) margins applied to OARs of the gastrointestinal (GI) tract over five fraction non-adaptive SABR for pancreatic cancer.Materials and MethodsThe clinical adaptive magnetic resonance-guided radiotherapy baseline planning for 10 patients was combined with retrospective replanning respecting constraints to OAR plus a margin of 2–5 mm. Baseline planning/replanning was applied to OAR contouring at treatment fractions without plan adaptation. The impact of PRV margin was assessed in terms of the encompassing of OAR motion, effect on delivered dose, and degree of associated compromise in planned treatment volume (PTV) dose coverage.ResultsWhilst variable across individual OARs, a 2–5 mm margin poorly encompassed motion in 40–70% of fractions. However, a 2–5 mm margin did effectively limit dose increases in most cases such that delivered biological effective dose (BED) did not exceed, on average, the baseline value. A margin of 2–3 mm resulted in minor PTV compromise (D70% median PTV BED decrease <10 Gy), whereas a 5 mm margin resulted in moderate compromise (D70% median PTV BED decrease ∼20 Gy).ConclusionsA PRV margin of 2–5 mm for OARs limits the increase in delivered dose. Consideration of such a margin is relevant in optimizing non-adaptive treatment practice.

Introduction

Stereotactic ablative body radiotherapy (SABR) has emerged as a promising treatment option for locally advanced pancreatic cancer. It has been shown to improve local control, progression free survival (PFS), and is suggestive of an overall survival (OS) benefit [1], [2], [3], [4], [5]. Treatment is characterised by a high dose per fraction and steep dose gradients to spare radiation sensitive organs at risk (OARs). Target coverage is often compromised to ensure that OAR constraints are achieved [6], and this is typical in pancreatic SABR due to the proximity of OARs of the gastrointestinal (GI) tract.

Traditionally, planning organ at risk volumes (PRVs) have been employed in radiotherapy for dose sensitive serial OARs, such as the spinal cord. In practice, PRV margins account for probabilistic, comparatively small, and quantifiable setup variations between radiotherapy fractions [7], [8]. Abdominal serial organs, such as those within the GI tract, are highly mobile [9]. They exhibit large deformations that increase the risk of higher dose than planned being delivered in any given fraction, with associated increased risk of treatment side effects. To address this challenge, advanced image-guidance techniques (such as magnetic resonance (MR) guided adaptive radiotherapy) have been adopted to enhance treatment precision, which has been shown to enable dose escalation to the tumour while reducing the risk of treatment side effects [10].

Despite the evident advantages of MR-guided adaptive radiotherapy, its availability remains limited. Whilst conventional computed tomography (CT) or cone beam CT (CBCT) adaptive radiotherapy platforms offer an alternative, they too are limited in availability. Additionally, CBCT image quality is inadequate for accurate adaptation in some instances. Consequently, most institutions use conventional platforms for SABR with no online treatment adaptation. This may not adequately spare GI tract OARs. Deformations of the GI tract cannot be modelled in the way, for example, spinal cord positioning errors can [8]. Despite this, one proposed solution to spare GI tract OARs in non-adaptive pancreatic SABR is the use of a PRV margin.

This retrospective study assessed the use of PRV margins ranging from 2 to 5 mm in non-adaptive SABR for pancreatic cancer. Baseline planning and repeat OAR contouring at treatment fractions were used retrospectively from patients previously treated using MR-guided adaptive radiotherapy. The primary objectives were to 1) investigate the degree to which different PRV margins encompassed OAR motion across treatment fractions, 2) evaluate the doses to individual OARs in each fraction with and without the use of a PRV margin and 3) examine the impact of PRV margins on PTV coverage.

Materials and methods

Patients received MR-guided adaptive SABR for inoperable pancreatic cancer on an MRIdian MR-linear accelerator (MR-Linac) (ViewRay Denver, Colorado, USA) at GenesisCare, Oxford, as part of an established referral pathway. Patients consented to their data being used anonymously as part of approved MR-Linac service evaluation between Oxford University Hospitals NHS Trust, GenesisCare Oxford and the University of Oxford. Patients were planned as per the local department treatment protocol. In brief, 40 Gy was prescribed in 5 fractions to a PTV grown by 5 mm from the gross tumour volume (GTV). Gastrointestinal tract OARs (stomach, duodenum and small/large bowel) were contoured as well as all other relevant OARs. Patients received a breath-hold planning MR and CT positioned supine with arms by their side or on their chest with a suitable headrest and knee support. Contouring was based on the planning MR. Patients were four hours nil by mouth prior to scan and treatment delivery. Patients were prescribed prophylactic anti-emetics, proton pump inhibitors and steroids if clinically indicated. Treatment was delivered using pseudo arc geometry and in exhale beath-hold using real time cine MR with direct tumour tracking. PTV coverage was compromised for overlapping GI OARs in order to respect mandatory UK consensus OAR constraints [11]. The original unedited PTV was split into two separate structures, one consisting of the region of PTV/OAR overlap and one not. For planning purposes, the volume of PTV not overlapping with an OAR was typically cropped back by a margin of 2 mm from any OAR. Treatment planning then aimed to cover this volume to prescription dose with SABR dose coverage intent (max dose <140%). Whereas the volume of PTV overlapping with an OAR was planned to the highest achievable dose whilst still meeting mandatory constraints following the approach described by Brunner et al [6]. A mandatory PTV prescription dose coverage criterion of 66.6% was used in accordance with a local planning policy constraint, i.e. V100% > 66.6%. Following routine adaptive radiotherapy clinical practice, recontouring at treatment fractions was limited to within a 3 cm radius of PTV [12].

Treatment data (contouring and planning) for the first 10 patients treated were exported as digital imaging communication in medicine (DICOM) and transferred to Oxford University Hospitals NHS Trust as part of clinical service review. Nine patients had locally advanced, and 1 had locally recurrent pancreatic cancer. For reasons unrelated to treatment, two patients did not receive their final fraction (receiving 4/5 fractions). Subsequently, resulting available data totalled 48 treatment fractions. For each patient, the following treatment data was available: baseline (simulation) MR images, contours and treatment plans as well as treatment fraction MR images, adapted contours, and adapted treatment plans.

Treatment data was imported into the local Eclipse (Varian, Palo Alto, California, USA) radiotherapy treatment planning system. Clinical baseline and treatment fraction adapted contours included a combined GI tract structure, as is commonly used in adaptive treatments to simplify the adaption process. For study purposes, individual OARs (stomach, duodenum, small bowel, and large bowel) were separated on all image datasets.

Additional baseline treatment plans were generated retrospectively. Plans respected UK consensus OAR constraints [11] applied to an OAR PRV grown with a margin of 2, 3, and 5 mm. Retrospective baseline planning followed the clinical planning protocol described above. Plans were generated on a non-adaptive Halcyon linac platform (Varian, Palo Alto, California, USA) using volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT). Clinically, MR-Linac treatment dose calculation used baseline CT imaging fused to MR images, however CT imaging was not available for retrospective replanning. Whilst definitively unsuitable for clinical dose calculation, for study dose calculation purposes only, the body outline was set to water equivalence for retrospective planning.

For each set of daily adapted contours, an assessment was made on whether the OARs were contained within the baseline PRV based on manual rigid image registration. Registration maximised overlap between the baseline and adaptive fraction contoured GTV; this simulated prospective online image matching. In instances where OARs were not contained within the baseline PRV, a further determination was made on whether any portion of the OAR that lay outside of the PRV was in the region of overlap with the unedited PTV. This was used to evaluate the PRV margin concept and as an initial proxy for likely increase in dose.

Results

With recontouring limited to within a 3 cm radius of the PTV, analysis excluded duodenum in 4 fractions, small bowel in 9 fractions, and large bowel in 14 fractions. While stomach was within a 3 cm radius in all 48 fractions. The percentage of fractions where OARs were contained within a 2 mm, 3 mm and 5 mm PRV margin respectively (i.e. did not have any portion falling outside of PRV) were as follows: stomach- 8.3%, 14.6% and 27.1%; duodenum- 0%, 2.3% and 13.6%; large bowel- 2.9% irrespective of margin size; small bowel- 0% irrespective of margin size. Discussion

This study assessed the suitability of PRV margins applied to OARs of the GI tract in pancreatic SABR through retrospective analysis using data MR-Linac data. The study demonstrated that large inter-fraction OAR motion was poorly encompassed using a 2–5 mm PRV margin. Uchinami et al. demonstrated that a margin encompassing GI tract motion over the course of 10–15 min in pancreatic SABR would be in the region of 10 mm or more, and a similar study by Hysing et al. identified a margin of comparable scale for bowel within the pelvis [13], [14]. In a high percentage of fractions duodenum was shown to fall outside the PRV in a region overlapping with PTV; at around 40% of fractions using a 5 mm margin and up to 70% of fractions using a 2 mm margin. Stomach was more moderate with 25–35% of fractions falling outside the PRV in a region overlapping with the PTV. Motion seen in small/large bowel was almost never encompassed by the PRV margin, but rarely (< 20%) was this in a region of overlap with the PTV. Overlap was assessed with unedited PTV (i.e. GTV plus 5 mm) and quantification of motion is dependent on categorisation of GI tract structure.

Delivered dose metrics were extracted for OARs under the scenarios of baseline clinical planning and retrospective baseline replanning with a 2–5 mm PRV margin, both applied without plan adaption. This determined OAR dose received under current non-adaptive practice with and without a range of PRV margins applied. Without a PRV margin, this study demonstrated comparable results to previous publications [15], [16]; with small volumes of duodenum in particular, and stomach to a lesser extent, showing an increase in delivered dose on treatment of approximately 10–30% from that at baseline planning. Bowel dose was largely unaffected. For retrospective replanning with a PRV margin, this study established that a PRV margin led to a reduction in delivered OAR BED3 from that at baseline planning when averaged over 5 fractions for most cases. Crucially, stomach and duodenum exhibited lower delivered dose on average when a moderate PRV margin of 2–3 mm was applied. McKenzie et al. [17] suggested an approach such that a PRV margin extend an OAR to “encompass completely” mean positional changes of an OAR and that the PRV DVH “will not underestimate the contribution of the high-dose components to the OAR” in 90% of cases. This study would place an emphasis on the PRV margin not underestimating contribution of the high-dose component rather than encompassing motion of GI tract OARs in five fraction SABR.

The median PTV BED10 D70% was 81 Gy in the baseline clinical plan. This reduced by between 4 and 16 Gy using a PRV margin. Evidence of a direct relationship between dose compromise and compromise in local control is lacking in SABR [18], [19] and PTV dose coverage compromise has not been found to affect outcomes in sites such as lung and oligometastatic SABR [20], [21]. However, this is in the context of highly non-uniform patient groups and delivery uncertainties. In principle, the higher the BED to the PTV, the better the outcomes [2], [10]. The balance of likelihood of tumour control and acceptable risk of treatment side effects is ultimately a clinical decision made with individual patients. However, data presented in this study would suggest adding a margin of 2–3 mm to stomach/duodenum maintains control on excessive OAR dose delivery without an implied large compromise in likelihood of tumour control based on the degree of PTV compromise.

Despite only 10 patients, analysis totalled 48 treatment fractions and subsequently provided considerable information regarding OAR motion and delivered doses. To the authors' knowledge this study represents the most comprehensive study of delivered OAR doses in pancreatic SABR under planning scenarios with and without use of a PRV. This study included only pancreatic cancer patients and may not be applicable to other sites. The analysis led to conclusions being drawn on the effectiveness of the PRV margin on average over 5 fractions. As seen in Fig. 4, outliers remain where large increases in delivered OAR dose do occur regardless of a PRV margin. Non-adaptive pancreatic SABR should unquestionably be delivered with some form of on-treatment approach to identifying such cases and managing them appropriately irrespective of any prospective strategy to control excessive dose. Further, Fig. 4 shows the delivered D0.1 cm3 OAR doses to be increased from that planned beyond simply outliers. Indeed, an increase was seen in ∼25% of cases when using a 2 mm margin PRV. Any form of radiotherapy has delivery uncertainties with known potential for some increase in delivered dose, including adaptive radiotherapy [22]. Determining definitive acceptable delivered doses is beyond the scope of this study. However, some moderate increase in a proportion of cases, whilst again controlling for outliers, does not negate a suitable role of 2–3 mm margin as outlined in this study.

All analysis was based on breath-hold treatment using cine MR with direct tumour tracking. Subsequently, conclusions drawn for non-adaptive radiotherapy should be in the context of breath-hold treatment and with consideration of the reduced accuracy when based on surrogates to tumour or other motion management strategies [23]. The reduced soft tissue image quality of non-MR imaging when not using fiducial marker matching is another consideration. Subsequently, analysis has uncertainties in the context of non-adaptive delivery that are not modelled here. However, this is not unique to non-adaptive radiotherapy, with increased adaptive radiotherapy delivery time associated with sources of dose uncertainty of equivalent concern [14], [22], [24], [25]. Further, image quality of traditional non-MR image guidance is increasing such that deficiencies in tumour matching without fiducial markers is reducing in concern. Finally, the use of PRV margins applied to GI tract in SABR remains controversial given the evidence on which SABR OAR dose constraints originated did not employ such margins. Leading many to reasonably conclude that adding such a margin is unnecessarily cautious. Nonetheless, residual concern on delivered dose uncertainty in non-adaptive SABR warrants study of mitigation strategies such as that presented here. MR-guided adaptive radiotherapy, with direct tumour tracking using cine MR, undoubtedly represents something of a gold standard in pancreatic SABR. However, studies such as this one drive the optimisation of treatment practice where no such platform is available.
