Early recognition and safe management of unintentional intrathecal placement of an epidural catheter during combined spinal-epidural labor analgesia: a case report in the context of the 2024 OAA guidelines
Authors: Hidenobu Watanabe, Choko Kume, Arisa Ijuin, Yoko Yamashita, Masaki Sato, Reiko Ohara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUnintentional intrathecal catheter placement is a recognized complication of labor neuraxial analgesia. Although continuous spinal analgesia (CSA) may be used as a management option, prompt recognition, careful catheter management, and close monitoring are required to prevent complications such as severe spinal anesthesia.Case presentationA 31-year-old primigravida underwent combined spinal-epidural analgesia for labor pain. Intrathecal catheter placement was diagnosed after a test dose was administered based on hypotension, motor weakness, sensory block, and glucose-positive aspirated fluid. The catheter was managed as CSA with fentanyl-based analgesia under strict monitoring. Although frequent breakthrough pain required rescue boluses, the vaginal delivery was achieved uneventfully. The patient developed a post-dural puncture headache that resolved after an epidural blood patch.ConclusionsThis case illustrates the importance of early recognition of inadvertent intrathecal catheter placement and demonstrates that safe labor analgesia can be achieved through conversion to CSA with careful catheter management and close monitoring.

Background

Unintentional intrathecal catheter placement is a rare but clinically important complication of neuraxial labor analgesia. Mistaking an intrathecal catheter for an epidural catheter can lead to the administration of excessive doses of local anesthetics, increasing the risk of high or total spinal anesthesia. Therefore, early identification of unintentional intrathecal catheter placement is essential. Management options include epidural catheter reinsertion or the use of an intrathecal catheter for continuous spinal analgesia (CSA). A recent UK survey reported that 59% of institutions chose CSA after accidental dural puncture (ADP) [1]. Furthermore, recent international guidelines and recommendations suggest that CSA may be considered an appropriate option when performed with adequate monitoring and management systems [1, 2]. Although these guidelines and surveys primarily address intrathecal catheters resulting from ADP with an epidural needle, the same principles of recognition, dosing, monitoring, labeling, and communication apply to an epidural catheter unintentionally placed intrathecally during CSEA. We apply them on that basis.

Here, we report a case of unintentional intrathecal catheter placement during CSEA, diagnosed early after a test dose and managed by conversion to CSA. We discuss its management in comparison with current international guidelines.

Case presentation

A 31-year-old primigravida was admitted at 37 weeks and 1 day of gestation for induction of labor, because fetal bradycardia had been detected during an outpatient visit. The patient’s height was 160 cm, weight was 81 kg, and body mass index was 31.6 kg/m2. Her medical history was notable for severe hearing impairment; however, communication was possible when speaking clearly in a loud voice.

When cervical dilation reached 3 cm, the patient reported a Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) pain score of 7 and requested labor analgesia.

CSEA was attempted using a CSEA kit (Perifix®, B. Braun, Melsungen, Germany). The initial approach at the L3/4 interspace, with the patient in the right lateral position, was unsuccessful after three puncture attempts. A fourth attempt was made at L4/5, where the epidural space was reached via a midline approach with cephalad needle angulation. After identifying the epidural space using the loss-of-resistance technique, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) reflux from the spinal needle was confirmed, and fentanyl 25 μg was administered intrathecally. Subsequently, after confirming the absence of CSF reflux through the epidural needle, an epidural catheter was inserted. A test dose of 3 mL of 1% lidocaine (30 mg) was administered. The epidural needle insertion depth was 7.5 cm, and the catheter was advanced 5 cm into the epidural space. Blood pressure at that time was 112/86 mmHg. Approximately 10 min after the test dose, marked analgesia and lower extremity weakness developed. Additionally, transient hypotension (91/56 mmHg) occurred and promptly improved after administration of phenylephrine 0.1 mg, with blood pressure increasing to 117/76 mmHg (Fig. 1). Concurrent clinical assessment revealed bilateral lower limb motor block (Bromage scale 2), a sensory level to cold extending to T7, and, on repeat aspiration, free flow of clear fluid from the catheter with a measured glucose concentration of 98 mg/dL, consistent with CSF. Based on these findings, the catheter was diagnosed as intrathecal.

The catheter was retained for use as an intrathecal catheter to provide CSA. The anesthesiology, obstetric, and midwifery teams were informed, verbally and in the medical record, that the catheter was intrathecal. A clearly visible label was attached to the catheter adjacent to the filter and on the infusion line.

After confirmation of recovery from lower extremity weakness, fentanyl 15 μg was administered through the catheter. CSA was initiated using an infusion pump (CADD-Solis®, Smiths Medical, Minneapolis, MN, USA) delivering fentanyl 2 μg/mL at 1 mL/h (2 μg/h). Maintenance with fentanyl alone was selected to avoid inadvertent administration of large doses of local anesthetics into the intrathecal space. Labor analgesia was subsequently managed with additional fentanyl and/or bupivacaine according to pain intensity (Fig. 2). Cold testing was performed hourly to assess the sensory block level. Monitoring included blood pressure every 15 min, as well as every 5 min for 15 min after each rescue bolus, with continuous pulse oximetry and cardiotocography.

No episodes of clinically significant motor block beyond Bromage 1, respiratory depression, hypotension, or signs suggestive of high spinal anesthesia were observed during CSA management (Fig. 2). The maximum sensory level to cold during CSA maintenance was T5, while the sensory block ranged between T5 and L1 throughout the course. Oxygen saturation was maintained between 97 and 99%, and the patient remained fully alert without pruritus, nausea, or vomiting. As labor progressed, the fetus developed a persistent occiput posterior position, and the patient adopted a hands-and-knees position, which she was able to do. No fetal heart rate abnormalities developed during the late stage of labor. Vaginal delivery was successfully achieved without major maternal or neonatal adverse events (Apgar scores 8 and 9 at 1 and 5 min, respectively). The neonate showed no respiratory depression and required no resuscitation. The total doses of fentanyl and bupivacaine administered were 104 μg and 8 mg, respectively. Total labor duration was 12 h and 27 min, and operative vaginal delivery was not required.

The catheter was retained until postpartum day (PPD) 2. The patient developed a post-dural puncture headache (PDPH) several hours after catheter removal. Although oral caffeine was prescribed, symptoms did not improve, and an epidural blood patch (EBP) was performed on PPD 3. After the EBP, the patient’s symptoms resolved, and she was discharged on PPD 8 with no neurological sequelae or further headache.

Although CSF glucose testing has an accuracy of approximately 97% [4], current guidelines emphasize that the absence of CSF aspiration does not exclude intrathecal catheter placement [1]. In this case, sufficient observation after the test dose allowed for the assessment of multiple clinical signs (hypotension, motor block, high sensory block, and glucose-positive aspirated fluid), facilitating early recognition of the intrathecal catheter placement.

CSA regimen: comparison between current guidelines and the present case

Current guidelines recommend maintaining CSA with continuous administration of a low-concentration local anesthetic in combination with an opioid (Table 1) [1, 2]. In the present case, however, fentanyl-only maintenance was initially selected to avoid inadvertent intrathecal administration of large doses of local anesthetics. Although no major cardiorespiratory complications occurred, additional fentanyl and low-dose bupivacaine were repeatedly required as labor progressed. A similar observation was reported by Hyuga et al., in which opioid-based CSA alone did not provide sufficiently stable analgesia throughout labor, and supplemental local anesthetic administration was required [5]. This observation is consistent with these guideline recommendations and with the report by Hyuga et al., suggesting that fentanyl-only maintenance may have limited analgesic efficacy. It should be interpreted with caution, however, as it derives from a single case. The principal contribution of the present case lies not in the analgesic regimen but in the early, multimodal recognition of unintentional intrathecal catheter placement during CSEA—based on the combined assessment of motor block, sensory level, hemodynamic change, and glucose-positive aspirate after a test dose—and the subsequent conversion to CSA with guideline-concordant safety measures under strict monitoring.

In the present case, anesthesiologist-managed dosing and continuous monitoring enabled safe labor management without evidence of high spinal anesthesia.

Prevention of PDPH and duration of catheter retention

Griffiths et al. recommend removal of intrathecal catheters at the earliest opportunity due to the risks of infection and inadvertent drug administration [1]. In contrast, Orbach-Zinger et al. suggest considering catheter retention for 24 h to reduce the risk of PDPH [2] (Table 1). In the present case, because 24 h had not yet elapsed by PPD1, the catheter was retained until PPD 2 in an attempt to prevent PDPH; however, PDPH still developed, and an EBP was ultimately required. A recent international consensus likewise concluded that intrathecal catheter placement does not clearly reduce the incidence of PDPH or the need for EBPs [6]. Decisions regarding catheter retention should therefore balance the risks of infection and medication errors.

Importance of multidisciplinary communication

Recent guidelines repeatedly emphasize the importance of clear labeling, communication during handovers, and avoidance of drug administration by non-anesthesiologists (Table 1) [1, 2]. In the present case, these measures of multidisciplinary information sharing, documentation, and labeling helped prevent inadvertent local anesthetic administration.

In conclusion, this case highlights how early recognition of unintentional intrathecal catheter placement, clear identification of the catheter, and strict management protocols can prevent medication errors and serious neuraxial complications. Furthermore, careful management of CSA enabled safe labor analgesia and delivery.
