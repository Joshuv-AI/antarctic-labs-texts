Comparison of fang replacement rate in Viperidae snakes
Authors: Jaim Sivan, Itay Tesler, Shlomo Hadad, Abraham Allan Degen, Ido Tsurim, Michael Kam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOnly a few studies have estimated fang replacement rate (FRR) in free-ranging snakes. We compared FRR between Crotalinae and Viperinae, two major clades of Viperidae. Crotalinae species differ by having pit organs which allow efficient strike at the prey, while, based on an earlier study, Viperinae species have relatively longer fangs. We hypothesized that FRR is related to the risk of fang damage and predicted that: (1) FRR is related positively to fang length; and (2) the FRR of Viperinae is faster than that of Crotalinae. To test these predictions, we determined the FRR, fang length, body size and hunting strategy of 21 Viperidae species, 7 Viperinae and 14 Crotalinae, controlling for allometric and phylogenetic effects. The average FRR of Viperinae was 2.6 times faster than that of Crotalinae, supporting our second prediction. However, in contradiction to our first prediction, FRR within subfamilies was related negatively to fang length. The phylogenetic analysis indicated that FRR and fang length covaried throughout the lineage, and that the relationship between FRR and fang length persisted beyond phylogenetic relatedness, implying that fang replacement of the two subfamilies followed different evolutionary pathways.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46398-w.Keywords: Fang replacement rate, Fang length, Viperinae, Crotalinae, Phylogeny, Hunting strategy, Post-strike behaviorSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Evolution, Zoology

Introduction

Fangs of Viperidae snakes are the longest among venomous snake species, that is, rear-fanged taxa and elapids, and, evolutionarily, are the youngest among families of venomous snakes1. The maxillary bones that hold the fangs are folded backwards to accommodate the long fangs when the mouth is closed and are rotated forward at approximately midstrike as the mouth begins to open2–4. After striking a prey, Viperidae can either hold the prey until it is paralyzed (strike and hold) or release it (strike and release) and follow the wounded prey by using strike-induced chemosensory searching5,6. Broeckhoven and du Plessis1 suggested that the evolution of longer fangs, with a closed, fused venom-conducting channel in viperids, enables maximum injection of venom into the prey and is related to the strike and release hunting strategy, especially when striking a large prey6,7. Many species employ both hunting strategies, depending on the size of the prey and the context. The strike and release strategy led to the “dangerous prey” hypothesis8 which states that prey is released due to the risk of injury from the prey6.

The two large sub-families within Viperidae are Viperinae, the true vipers, and Crotalinae, the pit vipers. Viperinae species are distributed in the Old-World, Asia and Africa, while Crotalinae are distributed in the Old- and New-World. The two sub-families diverged from their common ancestor approximately 50 mya; Viperinae most likely in Africa and Crotalinae in Asia. Crotalinae then colonized the New World following a single invasion approximately 24 mya9. Crotalinae are distinguished from Viperinae by the presence of a pair of heat-sensing organs located in “pits” between the eyes and nostrils and are considered to be more efficient than Viperinae in sensing or locating an injured prey10.

In an earlier study, which analyzed 30 species across five genera of both Viperinae and Crotalinae, Viperinae species were reported to have relatively (relative to SVL or skull length) longer fangs than Crotalinae10. The evolution of relatively longer fangs and larger venom yields in many Viperinae species, such as the Gaboon viper, Bitis gabonica, with the longest fangs11, ensures that a single strike is highly effective at incapacitating prey. Deep penetration enables the venom to spread quickly through the prey’s internal tissues. However, longer fangs of venomous snakes, specifically in the family Viperidae, are extremely fragile12,13, and are replaced frequently to maintain continued optimal function. Consequently, fang replacement rate (FRR) may have high adaptive (fitness) importance; however, only a few studies have estimated FRR in free ranging snakes.

Klauber2, an important pioneer in studying fang replacement, raised two major questions: (1) is fang replacement a random process; and (2) what is the frequency of fang replacement? He concluded that fang replacement in New-World viperid species within the subfamily Crotalinae, namely rattlesnakes, is not synchronized between the two sides of the maxilla (right-left) and that the position of the functional fang alternates between the internal and external sockets of the maxilla, so that a replacement fang is always positioned in the vacant socket. No such information is currently available for Viperinae and while some information is available on FRR14,15, little is known about the factors affecting FRR among species.

The aim of this study was to fill this gap by determining FRR in some Viperinae species and comparing the results with published FRR in Crotalinae. Based on the earlier study of Pough and Groves10, we predicted that the fangs of the Viperinae species in the present study would be longer than in the Crotalinae species. We hypothesized that FRR is related to the risk of fang damage and predicted that: (1) FRR is related positively to fang length as longer fangs are more fragile than shorter ones; and (2) if the fangs in Viperinae are longer than in Crotalinae, as predicted, then the FRR of Viperinae is faster than that of Crotalinae. As an additional aim, and based on the findings in Crotalinae2, we tested whether fang replacement in Viperinae is also random between the sides of the maxilla (right-left), and that the replacing fang alternates between internal and external sockets, in each side of the maxilla.

Methods

Measurements on Vipernae species

In the present study, we made measurements on six adult Viperinae species; specimens below 30% of average SVL of a species were excluded to avoid ontogeny effects (Table 1).These species included Cerastes vipera (n = 38), Cerastes cerastes (n = 21), Cerastes gasperettii (n = 21), Pseudocerastes fieldi (n = 19), Echis coloratus (n = 26), and Daboia palaestinae (n = 25). No live vertebrate specimen was used. Most of the specimens that were examined were obtained from the Zoological collection of the Hebrew University, Jerusalem, and some specimens were obtained from the Zoological collection of Ben-Gurion University of the Negev, Beer Sheva, Israel (Appendix 1).

Each specimen’s maxilla was examined visually for the presence and position of functional and replacement fangs (Fig. 1). When the two alternative fang-sockets on either the right or left side of the maxilla were occupied by fangs, the larger one attached solidly was considered to be the “functional fang”, whereas the one still loose, before fully calcifying in the socket, was considered to be the “replacement fang”. When two fangs on the same side of the maxilla were similar and both attached solidly in their socket, no decision was made as to which was the functional or replacement fang. This status was considered to be an advanced stage of fang replacement, where both the functional and the replacement fangs were functional, shortly before the old fang was shed14. For each specimen, we also measured body length (SVL; snout-vent-length) and fang length following Ernst16. Estimation of fang replacement rate

Several methods, reviewed in Tesler et al.17, have been used to estimate FRR in free-living viperids, mainly in the subfamily Crotalinae. In the present study, we used the indices developed by Fitch14 and Ernst15. Fitch14, studying copperheads (Agkistrodon contortrix: Crotalinae), developed an FRR index, here denoted as ‘F’, which he defined as “the time on the average each fang is in process of replacement”. Ernst15 also developed a FRR index, here denoted as ‘E’, which he defined as the “percentage of snakes having at least one replacement fang on either side of the maxillae”. Although Fitch and Ernst calculated their indices (F and E, respectively) based on the percentage of the population replacing a fang, they referred to their indices as the “true replacement rate”.

The FRR of 13 adult Crotalinae species, both adults and juveniles, (Table 1) were obtained from Ersnt15. An additional taxon with the common name copperhead14(here denoted as A. contortrix*), conspecific to A. contortrix15, was included in the analysis as it presented a population with distinct morphological characteristics. FRR of an additional Viperinae species – Daboia (russellii) siemensis was taken from a previous study16. In their studies, Fitch14 and Ernst16 did not include sex as a variable in the analyses, and Ernst commented that the sexes probably do not differ in FRR14,15. Fang length did not differ between males and females in the Agkistrodon-complex14,18 or in rattlesnakes2. Hence, we did not include sex in the analysis and assumed that our data were representative of the species. In addition, it should be noted that calculating an average FRR per species (Fitch14 and Ernst16 and the current study) does not allow addressing factors affecting FRR within species.

Habitat and geographic distribution of Viperinae were taken from Werner19, and of Crotalinae were taken from Holding et al.20, while hunting strategy of Viperinae was based on our unpublished data and discussions with researchers studying these species and of Crotalinae was based on Gloyd and Conant21(Appendix 1).

Statistical analyses

Fang replacement with respect to side of the maxilla and between the internal and external sockets in Viperinae

For each species, we counted the number of individuals that were in the process of fang replacement on either side of the maxilla, that is, both functional and replacement fangs occurred concomitantly on the same side of the maxilla. We used a Chi-square contingency test to test the null hypothesis that fang replacement is independent (asynchronized) between the sides of the maxilla (right-left). In addition, we counted all individuals in each species having a functional fang on the internal or external socket of each side of the maxilla separately. Cases in which the two sockets of the same side of the maxilla were occupied by functional fangs were excluded from the analysis. We used a Chi-square goodness of fit to test the null hypothesis that a similar proportion of internal and external sockets of the maxilla, in each side separately, were occupied by functional fangs.

Factors affecting fang replacement rate (FRR)

We compared FRR (indicated by ‘E’ or ‘F’) between and within the two viperid subfamilies (7 Viperinae and 14 Crotalinae taxa), as a function of body size (snout-vent length ; SVL), fang length, and hunting strategy. To evaluate whether fang length differs between the two subfamilies, we also tested the relationship between fang length, SVL, and subfamily affiliation using a phylogenetic generalized least squares (PGLS) model. When analyzing the effect of fang length on FRR we controlled for possible allometric effects by using the residuals of fang length on SVL×Subfamily; subsequently denoted as relative fang length. The residuals were extracted using a PGLS model to control for phylogenetic non-independence (see below).

Control of phylogenetic non-independence among species

We used PGLS to account for non-independence among species traits due to phylogenetic relationships22–26, estimating Pagel’s λ as a measure of the strength of the phylogenetic signal. Pagel’s λ rescales the off-diagonal elements of the phylogenetic covariance matrix and is biologically defined on the interval 0 ≤ λ ≤ 1, where λ = 1 corresponds to Brownian-motion expectations (strong phylogenetic structuring of residual covariation), and λ = 0 corresponds to a star phylogeny in which trait covariation is independent of shared ancestry26–28. In practice, maximum-likelihood optimization may occasionally calculate values outside this biologically meaningful range, including negative estimates, which reflect numerical behaviour of the optimiser rather than interpretable evolutionary structure. Because values outside the range 0 ≤ λ ≤ 1 do not represent valid covariance structures for trait evolution models, we constrained interpretation of λ to its theoretical range, treating estimates below 0 as absence of detectable phylogenetic signal (λ ≈ 0) and values above 1 as full Brownian-motion signal (λ ≈ 1). For the PGLS analyses, we used the following R29 packages: nlme30, ape31, and phytools32. Accompanying graphs were generated by ggplot233 and ggtree34.

Our PGLS analysis was based on a phylogenetic tree generated by Alencar et al.35, with two additional taxa (Fig. 2): (1) Gloydius himalayanus, based on Shi et al.36, as a sister taxon to the branch of G. strauchi (G. halys, G. intermedius), (G. blomhoffii, G. ussuriensis) with a branch length equivalent to 15 mya, matching the additive evolutionary time within its sister branch of the tree; and (2) A. contortrix*14 representing a population of Agkristrodon contortrix with distinct morphology and FRR, treated as a sister taxon to A. contortrix with a branch length of 2 mya, based on Guiher and Burbink’s37 estimate of 1.5-2 mya for the deep divergence between A. contortrix main lineages.

Results

Fang replacement in Viperinae is independent of side of maxilla

We examined 150 Viperinae specimens (see Table 2 for sample size distribution by species). No specimen lacked all fangs on both sides, only 7 (4.6%) specimens did not have a fang on one of the sides, and 96 (64%; range 46–84%; Table 2) specimens were in the process of replacing at least one fang. The χ2 contingency test (Table 2) supported the null hypothesis for each of the six Viperinae species, indicating that the presence of a replacement fang, in addition to the functional fang, was independent of the side of the maxilla (left-right), and that fang replacement was asynchronized between the two sides of the maxilla.

Fang replacement rate (FRR) indices

The E and F indices produced different values (Table 1), however, these indices were correlated strongly in our dataset (Pearson correlation coefficient = 0.994, t = 41.14, df = 19, p < 0.0001). Therefore, effects of fang length, body size and phylogeny on FRR using either E or F provided similar relationships. Since most cited data were from Ernst15 and his method was more recent, we used E in all further analyses. The FRR of Cerastes vipera was the fastest (E = 84.2) and that of Daboia siamensis was the slowest (E = 10.1) among all species, with both species belonging to Viperinae (see Table 1 for detailed FRR list).

The PGLS analysis indicated a significant effect of subfamily on FRR (Fig. 3; t = 4.77, p = 0.0001), with Viperinae having a faster FRR (mean ± sd: E = 54.3 ± 23.27, range: 10.1 to 84.2, n = 7) than Crotalinae (E = 20.7 ± 7.52, range: 11.8 to 36.4, n = 14). The estimated Pagel’s λ was 0.028, indicating a very weak phylogenetic signal in the residual variation after accounting for subfamily effects. Model diagnostics indicated a good fit (AIC = 168.07; residual SE = 14.57; n = 21), and standardized residuals exhibited no major violations of model assumptions.

We used PGLS to test whether relative fang length, Subfamily and their interactions affect the variation in FRR across species (Table 5; Fig. 5). The unrestricted PGLS model resulted in a negative Pagel’s λ value (λ = -0.318). Hence, we repeated the analysis with restricted λ = 0 (see Methods). Discussion

Fang replacement synchronization and pit organs

In Viperidae, the fangs are extremely important for their fitness and, therefore, investment in their maintenance is expected to be high. In the 150 Viperinae specimens in this study, no speciman lacked fangs completely, only 4.6% of the specimens did not have a fang on one of the sides of the maxilla, while 64% of the specimens were in the process of replacing at least one fang. This latter percentage was substantially greater than the percentage reported by Klauber2 in Crotalinae, suggesting that the FRR in Viperinae is faster than in Crotalinae. Interestingly, 38 Viperinae specimens, or 40% of the individuals that exhibited fang replacement, were in a state in which both fangs were functional, at least on one side of the maxilla. A single functional fang on each side is probably preferable for a Viperidae2 and the state of having two functional fangs on the same side is only a short transitional state. Hence, the observed high number of two functional fangs on the same side further indicates a very fast FFR. Consistent with previous findings by Klauber2 in a number of New-World Crotalinae species, fang replacement of Viperinae was independent of the side of maxilla. Our analysis also indicated that, as in Crotalinae, replacement fangs entered the empty socket, developed and waited, and, therefore, the internal and external sockets were occupied alternately.

A major difference between the two subfamilies is the paired loreal pit organs which are located between the eyes and nostrils and exist only in the evolutionary younger Crotalinae. These pits are specialized heat-sensing organs that enable infrared detection of warm-blooded animals and a more efficient tracking of a prey, particularly in the dark38. Consequently, we reasoned that Crotalinae should favor strike and release hunting and then track the animals, which would also result in less risk to the fangs, and that the prey should be mainly endotherms. However, in our study, only 3 of the 14 Crotalinae species used solely strike and release hunting compared to 1 of the 7 Viperinae species. Based on data provided by Holding et al.20, we calculated that the dietary intake of 53 species of Crotalinae consisted of 55.1 (± 31.05) % mammals and 4.3 (± 11.51) % birds, whereas of 18 Viperinae consisted of 45.6 (± 35.59) % mammals and 3.0 (± 3.86) % birds and, therefore, endotherms comprised approximately 59% of the diet for Crotalinae and 49% for Crotalinae. There were large differences among species in both subfamilies as can be noted from the large standard deviations of the means, and much of the difference between subfamilies was due to 3 Causus spp in Viperinae with no mammals or birds in the diet. Our reasoning that endotherms should comprise a larger proportion of the dietary intake in Crotalinae compared to Viperinae could not be supported, as differences between subfamilies did not differ statistically.

Fang length vs. Body Size (SVL)

A significant positive relationship emerged between SVL and fang length, suggesting an allometric relationship. In the present study, the species from the two subfamilies were similar in their range of sizes (SVL), and within the species examined, the fangs in Viperinae were longer than in Crotalinae. This relationship persisted even after controlling for potential phylogenetic relatedness, suggesting that the two subfamilies may have undergone different selective forces that shaped the relationship between SVL and fang length. Nonetheless, the medium to high Pagel’s λ of 0.876 indicated a strong phylogenetic signal and that the two traits (SVL and fang length) did not change independently with respect to each other throughout the lineage.

Pough and Groves10 also reported this difference in fang length relative to SVL between subfamilies, but did not provide eco-physiological rationale. In addition, these authors reported that the relative “circumference at the quadrates” was greater in Viperinae than Crotalinae, which suggests that, in similarly-sized snakes, Viperinae have a larger gape and, therefore, can swallow larger prey than Crotalinae. A relationship between fang length and prey size was reported in 199 viperid species20 which would suggest that Viperinae, with longer fangs, hunt larger prey than Crotalinae. The longer fangs would enable deeper penetration of the fangs in a large prey and rapid immobilization that would lessen the chance of the prey’s escape. In addition, deep venom injection facilitates digestion as proteolytic enzymes in the venom weaken the gut and body wall of the prey10,39. Thus, dietary habits may play a role in the evolution of fang length and, consequently, in FRR.

Fang replacement rate (FRR)

The two subfamilies differed in FRR, with Viperinae having longer fangs and a FRR that was 2.6 times faster than Crotalinae, which supported our predictions. Fang length affected FRR, even after controlling for allometric effects by using the relative fang length. Body size, despite its significant effect on fang length, did not influence FRR, with only a moderate to low phylogenetic effect on the correlation between the two traits. However, contrary to our prediction, FRR was correlated negatively with relative fang length within each subfamily. Our analysis also indicated that the pattern between the two traits differed between the two subfamilies, with Viperinae having a much steeper more significant negative slope in the regression than Crotalinae. Interestingly, our model estimated a negative Pagel’s λ, hence restricted to λ ≈ 0, indicating no or very low phylogenetic signal and that the relationship between FRR and relative fang length varied independently throughout the lineages. It also suggests that the two subfamilies may have undergone different selective pathways in shaping the relationship between FRR and fang length.

Our initial hypothesis assumed that longer fangs should be more fragile and, therefore, would be replaced more frequently. At the broad phylogenetic scale, our results were consistent with this prediction: fangs (after accounting for SVL) were longer and FRR was substantially faster in Viperinae than Crotalinae, suggesting that lineages with longer fangs underwent stronger selection to maintain a rapid replacement system. However, the pattern within-subfamily contradicted the simple “longer fang = more fragile = faster replacement” model. After controlling for body size and phylogeny, FRR was associated negatively with relative fang length, particularly in Viperinae. A key insight from a recent biomechanical study is that fang length alone does not necessarily imply greater mechanical stress. Finite-element simulations across snake fang morphotypes revealed that maximum von Mises stress was correlated negatively with fang length, indicating that longer fangs can display lower peak stress under standardized loading and may, therefore, be biomechanically optimized rather than inherently more failure-prone1,40. This optimization likely reflects multiple structural reinforcements. For example, high curvature in viperid fangs has been proposed to reduce fracture risk1, and measured material properties suggest that some viper fangs have comparatively high hardness and elastic modulus – properties expected to improve resistance to crack initiation and propagation1. In addition, developmental and histological evidence indicates that the venom-conducting canal is structurally stabilized by dentine attachments, including a distinct suture/lamellar-like connection between dentine layers, which provides added support during impact and loading41. Together, these findings support the notion that longer fangs may be accompanied by compensatory design features that reduce breakage probability, potentially weakening or even reversing the expected positive relationship between fang length and FRR within lineages. Ecology may further decouple fang length from replacement dynamics. In a comparative analyses across vipers, relative fang length was associated with diet, with mammal-heavy diets tending to be linked to longer fangs (controlling for other factors), consistent with selection for deeper venom delivery into prey with a low surface-area-to-volume ratio20. Concomitantly, many vipers employ a bite-and-release strategy—particularly for larger prey—which reduces the duration that fangs are exposed to mechanical loads from prolonged struggling, potentially lowering breakage risk even when fangs are long1. This context dependence also aligns with the notion that short fangs may be advantageous against mechanically “tough” prey (e.g., resistant integuments or extended struggles), meaning that fracture risk may depend more on prey properties and handling time than on fang length per se20. Finally, fang morphology is part of a broader venom-delivery system under strong selection in predator–prey interactions; such selection can drive rapid adaptive change in venom systems and associated delivery traits, but not necessarily in a way that couples replacement rate tightly to fang length within every lineage42. Overall, these lines of evidence suggest a scale-dependent interpretation of our results: subfamily-level differences (Viperinae vs. Crotalinae) may be consistent with selection for maintaining functional fangs in lineages with generally longer fangs, whereas within-subfamily, variation may reflect biomechanical optimization, structural reinforcement, ecological differences in prey/handling, and developmental constraints – factors that can jointly produce a negative association between relative fang length and FRR within lineages.

The Saharan sand viper (Cerastes vipera), the smallest viperid, had the fastest FRR of all snake species examined in this study. This species employs a strike-and-hold hunting strategy, whereas most other Viperinae employ a strike-and-release or mixed hunting strategy15. The decision whether to hold or release prey after striking is not restricted to small species. For example, A. contortrix43 and the puff adder (Bitis arietans; Viperinae)6 tend to strike and hold small prey, but release large prey. The Iranian spider-tailed viper, Pseudocerastes urarachnoides (Viperinae), a large viperid that preys on birds, employs a strike-and-hold strategy, as released prey would be difficult to follow44. Hunting strategy may affect the risk of fang loss and, thus, the evolutionary pathways of fang length and FRR. We reasoned that strike-and-hold species have faster FRR than strike-and release and, thus, greater selective pressures toward faster FRR. However, our results did not support this premise.

Hunting strategy and FRR

We reasoned that FRR could be related to hunting strategy; faster in the strike-and-hold due to the struggle of the prey, and slower in the strike-and-release species. The data distribution did not allow standard statistical analysis; however, based on the visual inspection of Fig. 6, FRR does not appear to be related to hunting strategy. Of the 21 species, 3 species used only strike-and-hold, 4 species used only strike-and-release and 14 species used both strategies. Viperinae, which comprised a third of the species, also comprised a third to a quarter of the species in each of the hunting strategies, which is approximately their proportion of the species pool (Table 1; Fig. 6).

Limitation of the study

Our sample size was a limitation in the present study, as 13 of 280 species of Crotalinae and 7 of 100 species of Viperinae were included in the analysis. The Viperinae species were all adults whereas the Crotalinae species included both adults and juveniles and therefore, the results could have been biased by ontogenetic effects. FRR of Vipera russelii was relatively slow among Viperinae species, which was also noticed by Ernst16. He suggested it might be slow because “some loose fangs may have dropped out during the period of preservation”16. Although we were careful with the specimens during our measurements, there is a possibility that some preserved specimens were damaged. Further studies on viperid species are warranted to determine: (1) whether the conclusions in this study are applicable for the subfamilies in general: and (2) the drivers affecting FRR and fang length evolution in relation to hunting strategy.

Conclusions

With the advanced, sophisticated fang system in Viperidae and their dependency on intact functional fangs, fang replacement rate plays a major role in determining fitness. Viperinae species replaced functional fangs faster than Crotalinae species indicating different evolutionary pathways and suggesting different selective forces. In addition, Viperinae tended to have longer fangs relative to body size than Crotalinae. However, even though longer fangs are likely more fragile, our analysis revealed a negative correlation between fang length and FRR within subfamilies, with the slope between relative fang length and FRR even steeper in Viperinae than in Crotalinae. Further studies on viperids are warranted to determine the factors affecting fang length and FRR.
