Inhibition of circulating glycocholic acid-regulated signaling potentiates immune checkpoint therapy in colorectal cancer
Authors: Senlin Zhao, Jing Zhang, Yushuai Mi, Bin Quan, Zijuan Hu, Xinyang Zhong, Ye Xu, Sanjun Cai, Xinxiang Li, Ping Wei, WanJun Chen, Yuping Zhu, Jianqiang Tang, Dawei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Serum bile acids (BAs) emerge as risk factors for cancer, but their roles in colorectal cancer (CRC) remain unclear. We show that glycocholic acid (GCA), a primary BA, is elevated in the serum of CRC patients. In a mouse CRC model, GCA promotes tumor programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression in tumors, suppressing CD8⁺ T cell-mediated antitumor immunity and facilitating tumor growth. Mechanistically, GCA inhibits the BA receptor farnesoid X receptor (FXR), a transcriptional repressor for SRY-box transcription factor 14 (SOX14). Loss of FXR repression upregulates SOX14-mediated expression of zinc finger DHHC-type palmitoyl transferase 9 (DHHC9), thereby reducing PD-L1 palmitoylation and stabilization. Silencing SOX14 or DHHC9, or activating FXR, synergizes with anti-PD-1 therapy, reducing tumor growth in GCA-treated mice. These findings uncover a mechanism that GCA remodels the tumor microenvironment to mediate CRC resistance to immunotherapy, highlighting therapeutic opportunities targeting the FXR-PD-L1 axis in CRC patients with elevated serum GCA.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) represents one of the main causes of mortality.1,2 Previous studies have shown that programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1)-expressing tumor cells attenuate CD8+ T cell infiltration, which results in tumor progression.3 Immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapies, including anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)/PD-L1 or anti-cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 therapies, have changed the therapeutic landscape and provided an unprecedented clinical response in various types of cancer.3–5 Although ICB therapy has durable effects toward a subset of microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) CRCs, accounting for ~15% of all CRC cases, only 30–50% of these patients can benefit from this therapy.6 Regrettably, patients with microsatellite instability stable (MSS)/proficient mismatch repair (pMMR) (~85%) merely have a 5–10% response rate. Metabolic reprogramming contributes to tumor immune evasion and progression, which is also considered as an important hallmark in tumors. It has been shown that metabolic reprogramming in CRC regulated by cholesterol or fatty acids is sufficient to reshape the tumor microenvironment (TME), leading to resistance towards ICB therapy.7–10 Therefore, the identification of key metabolites in regulating resistance to PD-L1 therapy is necessary to combat an immunosuppressive TME in CRC.

The expression of PD-L1 in tumor cells is regulated by various processes, including transcription and post-transcriptional modifications (PTMs).11 During cell malignant transformation and tumor initiation, the transcription level of PD-L1 is associated with oncogenic pathways such as aberrant activation of WNT/β-catenin and RAS/MAPK signaling.12,13 Moreover, PTMs, including palmitoylation, are critical for regulating the protein stability and function of PD-L1, and consequentially leading to reduced anti-tumor immunity in the TME.14 It has been demonstrated that metabolic stress alters palmitoyl transferase-induced protein palmitoylation.14,15 Notably, the palmitoyl transferases zinc finger DHHC-type palmitoyl transferase (DHHC)3 and DHHC9 have been reported as key regulators for mediating the palmitoylation of PD-L1, which contributes to lysosome sorting during PD-L1 degradation in several types of cancer.14,16 Thus, to identify whether and how metabolic alterations in CRC regulate the effects of PD-L1 palmitoylation is important for improving ICB therapy.

Bile acids (BAs) are considered as important regulators of intestinal epithelial homeostasis in the gastrointestinal tract. In addition to their detergent properties,17 BAs serve as ligands to nuclear (hormone) receptors such as the farnesoid X receptor (FXR), thus controlling numerous important cellular metabolism, regulatory and signaling functions, as well as immune responses.18 In CRC, circulating levels of BAs are strongly associated with tumor initiation and progression.19,20 However, activation of FXR negatively regulates intestinal cancer stem cell proliferation, leading to a slower CRC progression.21 Therefore, the role of BAs in regulating tumor initiation and development is still controversial, and it remains unknown whether alterations in BAs-mediated signaling in tumor cells may affect immune surveillance and ICB sensitivity.

The present study demonstrated that FXR inactivation mediated by the serum primary BA glycocholic acid (GCA) in CRC is necessary and sufficient to inhibit the antitumor response of CD8+ T cells. Mechanistically, GCA increased the expression of the transcription factor (TF) SRY-box transcription factor 14 (SOX14) by inhibiting FXR transcription-suppressing function, and consequentially promoted the DHHC9-induced palmitoylation and stabilization of PD-L1. Notably, activation of FXR with the agonist GW4064 sensitized ICB-resistant CRC to anti-PD-1/PD-L1 treatment. Taken together, the present findings indicate that targeting the FXR signaling pathway is a potential strategy for alleviating immunosuppressive TME and enhancing the sensitivity to anti-PDL-1 treatment in CRC.

Results

Increased serum GCA in patients with CRC is associated with poor clinical prognosis

It has been suggested that the elevation of serum BAs is associated with higher risk for multiple cancer types22; however, the association between BAs and CRC remains to be elucidated. To investigate whether BAs and which BA is associated with CRC tumorigenesis, we collected serum samples from 80 patients with CRC (Cohort1) and 20 healthy individuals, and measured the levels of 33 kinds of BAs including primary free, secondary free, primary conjugated and secondary conjugated BAs by ultra-performance LC-MS. It was found that only 6 BAs, that is, glycocholic acid (GCA), taurochenodeoxycholic acid (TDCA), taurocholic acid (TCA), taurochenodeoxycholic acid (TCDCA), glycochenodeoxycholic acid (GCDCA) and glycodeoxycholic acid (GDCA) were differently expressed; what’s more, all of these 6 BAs were significantly upregulated in the sera of patients with CRC compared with healthy individuals. Notably, the concentration of GCA was mostly upregulated compared with the other five types of BAs in patients with CRC (Fig. 1a). It was also found that the levels of GCA in patients with CRC with stage III/IV or distal metastasis were higher than those found in patients with CRC with stage I/II or without metastasis (Fig. 1b, c). In addition, increased levels of GCA were positively associated with poor 5-year disease-free and overall survival (Fig. 1d, e). Univariate and multivariate survival analyses further indicated that serum GCA served as an independent prognostic biomarker for poor outcome in patients with CRC (Fig. S1A, B). Furthermore, we validated our results by using an additional CRC cohort, and confirmed that the serum concentration of GCA was increased in patients with stage III/IV or distal metastasis (Fig. S1C, D), leading to worse disease-free and overall survival (Fig. S1E, F). In addition, higher serum GCA concentrations remained significantly associated with both shorter disease-free survival (DFS) and overall survival (OS) in CRC patients without liver metastasis (Fig. S1G, H). GCA promotes tumor growth in a mouse model of CRC via inhibiting FXR activity

As BAs mainly function by regulating FXR signaling in the intestine,17,19 we then examined the effects of FXR with its agonist GW4064 on the CRC cell lines MC38, CT26, HCT116 and SW620. As expected, activation of FXR by GW4064 significantly promoted FXR activity (Figs. 1f and S2A). FXR was knocked out in MC38, CT26, HCT116 and SW620 cells with the CRISPR/Cas9 system (Fig. S2B), and it was found that GW4064 failed to promote FXR activity in Fxr-deficient mouse and human CRC cells (Fig. S2C). Next, we cultured MC38 cells with 0–200 µM of GCA and observed that GCA suppressed FXR activity in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. S2D). Of note, 100 nM of GCA robustly decreased the activation of FXR without affecting the proliferation of MC38 cells, suggesting that this dosage of GCA is optimal for in vitro experiments (Fig. S2D). Consistently, same tendency was observed in other CRC cells (Figs. 1f and S2A). And GCA inhibited the transcriptional expression of the FXR downstream genes SHIP and IBABP in these CRC cell lines, confirmed by qPCR (Figs. 1g and S2E). Given that BAs regulate FXR function by altering the expression of FXR or the ability to bind to RXR, which is required for its downstream transcriptional activity23, we performed qPCR as well as immunoprecipitation experiments, and found that GCA inhibited the interaction between FXR and RXR, without affecting the mRNA level of FXR itself (Fig. S2F, G). In addition, we also observed that the FXR agonist was sufficient to restore the expression of SHIP and IBABP in GCA-treated MC38 and HCT116 cells (Fig. S2H), suggesting a negative role of GCA in FXR signaling in CRC.

To further explore the roles of GCA in CRC, MC38, CT26, HCT116 and SW620 cells were incubated with GCA or GW4064, and it was observed that the treatment had little effect on tumor cell proliferation in vitro (Fig. S2I). Consistently, the proliferation rate was also comparable in both FXR-deleted (FXR-KO) and control CRC cells (Fig. S2J). However, by using intestinal epithelial-specific Fxr-knockout (Fxr-ko, Fxr−/−) mice, it was found that Fxr was successfully deleted in intestine tissues and AOM/DSS-induced tumors (Fig. S2K, L). Loss of FXR promoted the development of CRC, as evidence by a decreased the colorectal length in the Fxr-ko mice (Fig. 1h). Histological analysis showed that tumors derived from Fxr-ko mice exhibited enlarged size and poor differentiation (Fig. 1i). We also generated another orthotopic mice model of CRC by implanting Fxr-knockout and control MC38 cells into colon mucosa. Consistently, mice with Fxr-knockout tumors showed an increased tumor growth and a worse overall survival compared to that of control tumors (Fig. S3A), suggesting that FXR acted as a tumor suppressor in CRC tumorigenesis in vivo. To further confirm this result, we inoculated MC38 cells into C57BL/6 mice and observed that 400 mg/kg of GCA treatment (intragastric gavage; ig) robustly increased tumor burden without evident side effects as measured by weight loss during the cause (Fig. S3B). We next measured the concentrations of BAs significantly elevated in the serum of CRC patients in mice following GCA administration (400 mg/kg). While serum and tumoral GCA levels were barely detectable in tumor-bearing mice without GCA treatment, GCA-treated mice exhibited measurable accumulation, with serum levels reaching approximately 150 nM and tumoral concentrations around 82 nmol/g (Fig. S3C, D). Notably, the median serum GCA concentrations in human Cohorts I and II were approximately 96.4 nM and 96.7 nM, respectively. These values are comparable to, or slightly lower than, those observed in GCA-treated mice, supporting the physiological relevance of our experimental model.

Of note, GCA significantly increased the tumor growth in AOM/DSS-induced CRC models, as evidenced by an increased tumor numbers and a short colon length in mice treated with GCA (Fig. S3E). However, GCA-treatment could not further promote the tumorigenesis in Fxr-ko C57BL/6 mice, suggesting that FXR is the functional target of GCA-induced tumorigenesis. While GCA significantly promoted tumor growth and decreased overall survival in immunocompetent mice by subcutaneously inoculating MC38 or CT26 cells (Fig. S3F, G), no significant difference was observed in terms of tumor size or survival rate between the GCA-treated and control groups when MC38 or CT26 cells were inoculated in immunodeficient BALB/c nude mice (Fig. S3H, I). qRT-PCR showed that GCA suppressed FXR signaling pathway in vivo, as shown by decreased expression of SHIP and IBABP in MC38 or CT26 tumors derived from immune-competent or nude mice treated with GCA (Fig. S3J, K). To avoid the potential effects of bacterial transformation of GCA in intestine, we intraperitoneal injected (i.p.ed) GCA twice a week in MC38 tumor-bearing mice and found an increased tumor growth in mice with GCA treatment (Fig. S3L). Collectively, our data indicated that immune responses played an important role in the GCA-induced development of CRC.

Based on the ability of GCA to inhibit FXR-activity, the present study next investigated whether altering the function of FXR could affect GCA-mediated tumor growth. Indeed, increased tumor size and decreased survival rate were observed in immunocompetent mice, but not in BALB/c nude mice, when treating the animals with the FXR antagonist T-βMCA (Figs. 1j and S4A, B), which mimics the behavior of tumor inoculated in immunocompetent mice or in immunodeficient mice treated with GCA. Consistently, the suppressive role of FXR in cancer was confirmed when immunocompetent, but not immunodeficient, BALB/c nude mice were treated with the FXR agonist GW4064 (Fig. S4C–G). To explore whether impaired FXR signaling was critical for GCA-increased tumor growth in vivo, the mice were treated with GCA in the absence or presence of the FXR-agonist GW4064 after inoculating MC38 or CT26 cells. As expected, mice receiving GW4064 showed decreased tumor size and prolonged overall survival compared with mice treated with GCA alone (Figs. 1k and S4H). Of note, GW4064 reverted the expression of FXR target genes I-BABP and SHP that inhibited by GCA (Fig. S4I, J). Taken together, these data suggested that impairment of FXR-mediated tumor suppression was critical for GCA-induced tumorigenesis, and immune responses were involved in regulating the GCA-FXR axis mediated tumor development.

GCA treatment inhibits CD8+ T cell infiltration and antitumor response

To further investigate the effects of tumor-infiltrated immune cells on GCA/FXR-mediated tumor progression, five independent cell clusters of CD45+ immune cells, including B cells, CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, macrophages and natural killer cells, were identified based on scRNA-seq (Fig. 2a left). An increased total number of CD8+ T cells and macrophages was observed in tumors treated with GW4064 compared with those without treatment (Fig. 2a right). The expression of markers of immune subtypes was shown by using a panel of 21 markers (Fig. S5A). Next, MC38 tumors were isolated from mice receiving GCA (i.g.) for 10 days, and the percentages of subpopulations in immune cells were analyzed by flow cytometry. Consistently, CD8+ T cells and macrophages, but not CD4+ T cells or CD4+ Foxp3+ regulatory T cells (Treg), were downregulated in tumors treated with GCA (Fig. 2b). To explore whether the suppressive effect of FXR on tumors was dependent on CD8+ T cells or macrophages, CD8+ T cells or macrophages were depleted in mice by using an their specific anti-CD8α antibody or clodronate liposomes (Fig. S5B), respectively, before inoculating the mice with MC38 cells with or without GCA or GW4064 treatment. Notably, GCA treatment did not further increase tumor growth in CD8+ T cell-depleted mice compared with untreated controls (Fig. 2c). Moreover, it was also found that the FXR agonist failed to inhibit CRC tumor progression in CD8+ T cell-depleted mice (Fig. 2d). However, tumor growth was comparable in both macrophage-depleted and control mice regardless of GCA or GW4064 treatment (Fig. 2e, f). In addition, MC38 or CT26 CRC cells were inoculated into Rag1−/− mice (which are deficient in T and B cells), and it was observed that treatment with FXR antagonist or agonist had a limited effect on tumor growth in Rag1−/− mice (Fig. S5C, D). We next explore the signaling pathway enriched in GCA-induced tumorigenesis. Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis revealed that genes upregulated in FXR signaling-mediated activated tumors were mainly associated with immune responses, including type I/II IFN responses (Fig. S6A, B). We examined the expression of IFN-stimulated genes (ISGs), including CXCL9, CXCL10, BST2, STAT1 and IFITM3, by qPCR. As expected, the mRNA levels of these ISGs were significantly upregulated in MC38 and CT26 tumor tissues derived from immunocompetent mice treated with GW4064 (Fig. S6C), whereas the expression of ISGs was decreased in tumors treated with GCA (Fig. S6D). However, the alteration of the mRNA levels of ISGs was not observed in tumor tissues derived from immunodeficient BALB/c nude mice when treating with GW4064 or GCA (Fig. S6E, F). Interestingly, MC38 and HCT116 cells were also treated with GW4064 or GCA in culture, however, no significant increase in the transcriptional expression of these ISGs was observed in GW4064- or GCA-treated CRC cells (Fig. S6G, H), suggesting a dependency on IFN signaling-associated immune responses in vivo.

As IFNγ is critical for tumor suppression mediated by CD8+ tumor-infiltrated T cells, we further used FACS analysis to evaluate IFNγ-expressing CD8+ T cells. It was found that IFNγ-expressing CD8+ T cells were significantly decreased in GCA-treated MC38 or CT26 tumors (Figs. 2g and S6I). However, an increased frequency of exhausted PD-1+ TIM3+ CD8+ T cells was observed in tumors treated with GCA compared with controls (Figs. 2g and S6I). On the contrary, tumors treated with FXR agonist GW4064 showed an enhanced population of CD8+ IFNγ+ T cells, accompanied with attenuated exhausted CD8+ T cell infiltration (Figs. 2h and S6J). Thus, the present results suggested that GCA-induced FXR impaired the antitumor activity of CD8+ T cells in the TME.

Increased expression of PD-L1 is required for GCA-mediated tumorigenesis

To investigate the underlying molecular mechanisms of GCA-mediated suppression of T cell immune responses, data-independent acquisition MS-based proteomics was performed in MC38 cells treated with or without GCA. Heatmap showed that PD-L1 was the most significantly increased protein among the top 20 deferentially expressed proteins (FC ≥ 2, p ≤ 0.05) annotated to both cytokine production and regulation of immune response pathways in GCA-treated MC38 cells (Fig. 2i). Next, CRC cells were treated with GCA or GW4064 in vitro, and the changes in the mRNA and protein levels of PD-L1 were evaluated. It was found that the levels of protein and cell surface expression of PD-L1, but not its mRNA levels, were upregulated in tumor cells treated with GCA (Figs. 2j and S7A, C). By contrast, PD-L1 protein levels were decreased in MC38 cells treated with the FXR agonist GW4064 compared with untreated cells (Figs. 2j and S7B, C), which suggests that the GCA/FXR-mediated increase in PD-L1 was regulated in a post-transcriptional manner. Functionally, tumor-infiltrated CD8+ T cells were co-cultured with MC38 cells or CT26 cells that had been pre-treated with GCA under anti-CD3 and anti-CD28-stimulating conditions, and it was found that FXR-inactivation rendered CRC cells more resistant to CD8+ T cells in T cell-mediated tumor cell-killing assays (Fig. S7D). Furthermore, tumor cell killing capacity was increased in wild-type (WT), but not in Cd274-KO, CRC cells pre-treated with a FXR agonist (Fig. S7E). We further corroborated the results with in vivo subcutaneous tumor growth model and found that knockout of Cd274 in MC38 cells or CT26 cells significantly reverted GCA-induced tumorigenesis (Fig. 2k, l). qRT-PCR analysis revealed that CD274-knockout did not affect GCA-induced suppression of FXR downstream genes I-BAPA and SHP (Fig. S7F, G). While low-dose anti-PD-L1 (10 mg/kg) therapy has minimal effect on tumor suppression in GCA-treated mice with subcutaneous tumors or in Fxr−/− mice of AOM/DSS-induced CRC model, blockade of PD-L1/PD-1-signaling with a high dose of anti-PD-L1 (100 mg/kg) effectively reversed GCA-induced tumorigenesis (Fig. S7H–J), suggesting that increased PD-L1 expression in tumors is critical for GCA/FXR-mediated dysfunction of CD8+ T cells during tumor growth.

GCA accelerates PD-L1 palmitoylation via DHHC9 in tumor cells

Similarly, increased expression of PD-L1 was observed in CRC cells treated with palmostatin B, the inhibitor of acyl protein thioesterase 1, which promotes global palmitoylation, in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 3c). To explore the key palmitoyltransferases for GCA-mediated PD-L1 accumulation, the transcriptional levels of palmitoyltransferases were screened by RNA-sequencing analysis, and it was found that the expression levels of DHHC9, DHHC1, DHHC12, DHHC18 and DHHC24 were on the top rank of differentially expressed genes in GCA-treated cells compared with controls (Fig. 3e). Then we knocked down these candidates in MC38 and HCT116 CRC cells by using specific siRNAs and found that GCA-induced accumulation of the total and cell surface PD-L1 protein was significantly reduced in DHHC9-knockdown CRC cells but not in other palmitoyltransferases-knockdown CRC cells (Fig. 3f, g). In addition, the transcriptional levels of DHHC9 were significantly upregulated in GCA-treated CRC cells confirmed by qPCR (Fig. S8A). To further investigate the function of DHHC9 in regulating the palmitoylation modification on PD-L1, DHHC9 was overexpressed in CRC cells. Increased protein, but not mRNA, expression of PD-L1 was observed in DHHC9-ovexpressing CRC cells (Figs. 3h and S8B). Notably, the palmitoylation modification on PD-L1 was significantly accumulated with the overexpression of DHHC9 (Fig. 3i). Next, we evaluated whether DHHC9 was critical for GCA-mediated PD-L1 expression. As expected, a decreased protein expression, but not transcriptional expression, of PD-L1 was observed in DHHC9-knockdown cells treated with GCA (Fig. S8C, D). Functionally, it was found that the tumor cell killing capacity of tumor-derived CD8+ T cells was significantly increased when co-cultured with DHHC9-knockdown CRC cells even without treatment with GCA (Fig. 3j). By contrast, overexpressing DHHC9 in MC38 or CT26 cells decreased the killing capacity of CD8+ T cells, leading to increased tumor growth (Fig. 3k). Furthermore, this tumor promoting capacity could not be reversed by treatment with the FXR agonist GW4064 (Fig. 3k). Collectively, these results indicated that DHHC9-mediated palmitoylation modification was critical for the GCA/FXR-induced stabilization of PD-L1 and tumor growth.

The SOX14-DHHC9 axis regulates PD-L1 expression in CRC cells

To identify the TF(s) which is (are) responsible for the GCA-mediated regulation of tumor PD-L1 expression, we analyzed all the differentially expressed transcriptional factors with fold change (FC) ≥ 2 in CRC cells treated with GCA, and it was observed that SOX14 was on the top list of the candidate TFs in terms of FC in expression (Fig. 4a). Loss-of-function screenings were then performed using specific siRNAs target to top 5 candidate TFs. The results showed that knockdown of SOX14, but not other candidates, could significantly diminish the expression of PD-L1 protein in CRC cells (Fig. 4b). Moreover, the mRNA expression of FXR downstream gene IBABP was negatively associated with SOX14 expression in tumor tissues derived from 80 patients with CRC (Fig. 4c upper). Subsequently, we explored whether FXR directly inhibited SOX14 transcription. The promoter region of human SOX14 was screened for FXR-binding site by using the JASPAR database, and ChIP assay was performed with an anti-FXR antibody under GCA or GW4064 treatment conditions. It was observed that FXR directly bound to the promoter of SOX14 (site 2), as evidenced by the enrichment of the target sequence (Fig. S9A). Moreover, GCA inhibited the DNA-binding capacity of FXR, whereas the FXR agonist GW4064 promoted its binding with DNA in CRC cells. (Fig. S9B). Finally, luciferase reporter constructs were generated to test the transcriptional activity of WT and FXR-binding sequence deficiency mutated regions for FXR-binding sites (site 2). As expected, the luciferase activity was significantly increased in HCT116 and SW620 cells transfected with WT region constructs upon GCA stimulation (Fig. S9C). By contrast, GW4064 significantly decreased the luciferase activity of CRC cells with WT region constructs, but not of cells transfected with mut region constructs (Fig. S6C), suggesting that FXR acts as a transcriptional suppressor for SOX14 expression. It was also found that knockdown of SOX14 decreased PD-L1 expression by using shRNA targeting SOX14 in multiple CRC cell lines even though treated with GCA (Figs. 4d, e, and S9D–F). Of note, increased protein levels of PD-L1 were observed in SOX14-overexpressing cells (Figs. 4f, g, and S9E), suggesting that increased SOX14 expression may be involved in GCA-induced PD-L1 expression. Moreover, we also investigated whether SOX14 was involved in regulating GCA-mediated tumor-promoting function by inoculating SOX14-knockdown cells into the subcutaneous tissue or colon mucosa of mice treated with GCA. Then, we found that Sox14 deficiency inhibited GCA-induced tumor growth, which was accompanied by an increased frequency of tumor-infiltrating total CD8+ T cells, as well as CD8+ IFNγ+ T cell subset, but a decreased number of exhausted CD8+ T cells in both subcutaneous and orthotopic CRC model (Figs. 4h, i, S9G, H). To identify whether SOX14 directly regulates DHHC9 expression, we firstly analyzed the correlation between the mRNA expression of SOX14 and DHHC9 in 80 human CRC samples, and found that SOX14 was positively correlated with DHHC9 mRNA expression (Fig. 4c down). It was also validated that GCA significantly increased the transcription and protein expression of SOX14 and DHHC9, as well as PD-L1 protein expression, in murine MC38 and human HCT116 cells (Fig. 4d, e). However, knockdown of SOX14 decreased the transcriptional expression of DHHC9, as well as the protein levels of both DHHC9 and PD-L1 in CRC cells when treated with GCA (Fig. 4d, e). By contrast, increased DHHC9 and PD-L1 expressions were observed in CRC cells with SOX14 overexpression compared with control cells (Fig. 4f, g). Most importantly, FXR-mediated DHHC9 and PD-L1 inhibition could be reversed by overexpression of SOX14 in cells incubated with a FXR agonist (Fig. 4f, g), suggesting that SOX14 is a potent FXR downstream TF for inducing DHHC9 transcriptional expression. Next, we analyzed the JASPAR database and identified two SOX14-binding sequences on the promoter region of human DHHC9 by ChIP assay. Only the predicted site1 binding sequence was enriched in ChIP-enriched promoters (Fig. 4j, k). Moreover, luciferase reporter constructs with SOX14-binding deficiency mutated regions for SOX14-binding were generated to evaluate transcriptional activity, and found that luciferase activity was significantly increased in SOX14-overexpressing MC38 and HCT116 cells transfected with DHHC9 WT region constructs, but not in cells transfected with DHHC9mut region constructs (Fig. 4l). This indicated that SOX14 was critical for DHHC9 expression.

Next, we examined whether DHHC9 was involved in SOX14-mediated tumor progression by overexpressing DHHC9 in SOX14-knockdown CRC cells. We found that overexpression of DHHC9 increased the expression of PD-L1 in CRC cells that exhibited downregulated PD-L1 expression by SOX14-knockdown (Fig. S9F). And overexpression of DHHC9 blocked SOX14 deficiency-mediated tumor inhibition (Fig. S9I). In addition, overexpression of DHHC9 in SOX14-deficient cells led to increased infiltration of total CD8+ T and CD8+ IFNγ+ T effector cells, but decreased infiltration of exhausted PD-1+ TIM3+ CD8+ T cells (Fig. S9J). Taken together, these data revealed that DHHC9 was the transcriptional and functional target of SOX14.

The GCA-mediated SOX14/DHHC9 axis correlates with decreased efficacy of immunotherapy in CRC patients

To further understand the role of the GCA-regulated SOX14/DHHC9 axis in immunotherapy in patients with CRC, LC-MS analysis was performed in the serum of 41 patients with MSI-H mCRC who received PD-1 blockade alone or combination with chemotherapy. The majority of patients with mCRC (15/20) with high levels of GCA were predicted non-responsive for immunotherapy, while patients with mCRC (19/21) with low concentrations of GCA showed response to immunotherapy (Table S2). Magnetic resonance imaging and computed tomography showed that patient 27 had a relatively high level of serum GCA and tumor progression after PD-1 blockade treatment (Fig. 5a, left and c), whereas patient 14, with a low concentration of serum GCA, showed tumor regression (Fig. 5a, right and c). We also examined SOX14 and DHHC9 proteins by using IF in formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tumor tissues from patients with mCRC who received PD-1 blockade. The majority of mCRC patients with high concentrations of serum GCA showed strong SOX14, DHHC9 and PD-L1 staining accompanied by low number of CD8+ T cells and often insensitivity to anti-PD-1 therapy (Fig. 5b, c). On the contrary, patients with low levels of serum GCA exhibited opposite results (Fig. 5b, c). Furthermore, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed a significant correlation between the high levels of GCA, SOX14, DHHC9 and PD-L1 and shorter progression-free survival (PFS) rates of patients with mCRC who received anti-PD-1 therapy (Fig. 5d–g). Conversely, higher number of infiltrated intratumor CD8+ T cells were accompanied by prolonged PFS rates in patients with CRC who received anti PD-1 therapy (Fig. 5h). Spearman correlation analysis showed that SOX14 protein expression was positively correlated with DHHC9 and PD-L1 protein expression in CRC tumor samples according to the IF staining scores (Fig. 5i, j). DHHC9 protein expression was also positively associated with PD-L1 protein (Fig. 5k). We further confirmed our results that the serum level of GCA was associated with the expression of DHHC9 or SOX14 in tumors of CRC patients in another cohort (Fig. S10A, B). Additionally, DHHC9 staining was correlated with SOX14 staining in these CRC tumors (Fig. S10C). Inhibition of GCA/FXR-induced SOX14 and DHHC9 expression sensitizes CRC to anti-PD-1 therapy

To explore whether the SOX14-DHHC9-PD-L1 axis is critical for GCA/FXR-mediated immunosuppression, it was hypothesized that Sox14-knockdown (Sox-KD) could ameliorate anti-PD-1 treatment in CRC under high levels of GCA in the TME. Then, GCA-treated immunocompetent mice were engrafted with control or Sox14-KD CRC cells and found that knockdown of Sox14 inhibited MC38 colon cancer outgrowth in the presence of anti-PD-1 therapy (Fig. 6a). Moreover, increased infiltration of CD8+ T and CD8+ IFNγ+ T effector cells, while decreased exhausted CD8+ T cells were observed in Sox14-KD cells compared with control cells in mice with and without anti-PD-1 antibody (Fig. 6b). Consistently, knockdown of Dhhc9 also significantly suppressed tumor growth in mice subjected to anti-PD-1 therapy compared with mice with monotreatment. In addition, Dhhc9-deficiency enhanced the CD8+ T cell anti-tumor function in the TME, as evidenced by the proportion of CD8+ T and IFNγ+ CD8+ T enriched in Dhhc9-knockdown MC38 tumors with anti-PD-1 treatment (Fig. 6c, d). As GCA negatively regulated FXR activity, we further investigated whether a FXR agonist could synergize CRC cells to anti-PD-1 therapy. As expected, combined GW4064 and anti-PD-1 treatment robustly slowed the tumor growth, which as associated with a higher number of CD8+ IFNγ+ T effector cell infiltration and lower percentage of exhausted T cells in tumors compared with those receiving monotherapies, suggesting that the activation of FXR sufficiently promoted the antitumor effects of anti-PD-1 therapy (Fig. S11A–D). In addition, anti-PD-1 therapy slightly decreased the AOM/DSS-mediated CRC tumorigenesis in both C57BL/6 and BALB/c mice (Fig. S11E, F). Of note, we observed that mice treated with combined anti-PD-1 and GW4064 were most likely to benefit from therapy, as evidenced by an increased colon length with a decreased tumor numbers and areas in mice receiving combined treatment compared to mono-treatment (Fig. S11E, F). To strengthen the present conclusions, anti-PD-1-resistant MC38 (MC38R) colon cancer was generated by three rounds of in vivo/in vitro selection. Anti-PD-1 antibody had a limited effect on the growth of MC38R cells in both subcutaneous and orthotopic CRC models (Figs. 6e, f, and S11G, H). Discussion

Accumulated evidence supports that BAs are critical for CRC development.20,21 However, the effects of BA in remodeling the TME are still unknow. The present study revealed that GCA suppressed antitumor CD8+ T cell infiltration and function in experimental and clinical CRC. Mechanistically, GCA increased the expression of tumor PD-L1 by promoting the FXR-SOX14-DHHC9 axis-mediated palmitoylation of PD-L1. In addition, blocking the FXR signaling pathway with the FXR-specific agonist GW4064 sensitized ICB-resistant CRCs to anti-PD-1 therapy. These results revealed an underlying molecular mechanism of GCA in regulating tumor immune evasion, and provided a compelling rationale for combination therapy in the treatment of patients with CRC with high levels of BAs.

Humans and mice exhibit substantially different bile acid pool compositions. This difference in endogenous bile acid composition presents a challenge when studying the physiological effects of GCA in mice, as their natural bile acid environment differs significantly from that of humans24,25. Due to the low endogenous levels of GCA in mice, which are highly amenable to studying the mechanisms, function or therapeutic effects of GCA by exogenously administered GCA.24 Our studies revealed that GCA inhibited the transcriptional function of the Farnesoid X Receptor (FXR) in both human and colorectal cancer (CRC) cells, promoted tumor growth in mice, and acted as a high-risk factor for CRC tumorigenesis. These results highlight the relevance of using mouse models to study the therapeutic effects of GCA, despite the differences in bile acid composition between species.

The most important pathway of BA signaling for regulating the development of intestinal cancer is through the nuclear receptor FXR, which suppresses colorectal tumorigenesis with a tumor cell intrinsic mechanism.21,26,27 For instance, in mouse models, loss of FXR expression was associated with increased size and number of CRC tumors via promoting Wnt signaling or increasing the proliferation of Lgr5+ intestinal stem cells.21,27,28 Conversely, the FXR agonist GW4064 counteracted tumorigenesis21,29. However, knockout of FXR (FXR-KO) in CRC cells, such as HT29 cells, reduced proliferation and increased apoptosis, depending on decreased MYC protein18. In addition, we did not observe a significant alteration in proliferation between FXR-KO and control MC38, CT28, HCT116 and SW620 CRC cells, suggesting a diverted function of FXR in regulating the growth of CRC cells with different genetic backgrounds.

Given the difference in the growth of tumor cells between in culture and in vivo observed in our previous studies, we found that inactivation of FXR significantly promoted CRC tumorigeneses in a cell-extrinsic mechanism by suppressing the infiltration and antitumor function of CD8+ T cells. IFNg secreted by CD8+ T cells suppressed tumor cells through inhibiting proliferation and inducing apoptosis, differentiation, migration, and cell-surface antigen expression, which give at least partially explanation on the suppressive function of FXR in regulating cell proliferation in vivo.

Furthermore, secondary bile acids like deoxycholic acid (DCA) and lithocholic acid (LCA) exacerbate CRC through cytotoxicity and genotoxicity, increasing oxidative stress and DNA damage in colonic epithelial cells30. Gut microbiota plays a critical role by converting primary to secondary BAs, with dysbiosis linked to higher CRC risk30,31. CRC patients often exhibit increased microbiota-mediated BA deconjugation and 7α-dehydroxylation, resulting in harmful BA accumulation32. GCA treatment p.o. or i.p. significantly promoted tumor growth in our study; the role of gut microbiota in GCA-induced tumorigenesis is indeed a critical field of interest for future research. Nevertheless, based on our findings, we exerted the tumor-inhibitory effects of FXR in vivo to the dependency of host conditions. In our study, it was found that the inactivation of FXR significantly suppressed the infiltration and antitumor function of CD8+ T cells by increasing the expression of PD-L1 in tumor cells. It has been reported that primary BAs and FXR ligands negatively regulate the IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α release from LPS-primed macrophages.33 Although increased infiltration of F4/80+ macrophages was also observed in tumors with GCA treatment, the faster growth of GCA-treated tumors was abolished only in CD8+ T cells-depleted, but not in macrophages-depleted, mice. This indicated that impeding the antitumor function of CD8+ T cells in the TME was essential for GCA-mediated tumor outgrowth.

Since PD-L1 is a critical molecule for suppressing CD8+ T cell function, the oncogenic or metabolic signaling pathway of various cancer types affect PD-L1 expression to mediate the immune escape of tumor cells.3 Although a negative correlation between the expression of FXR and PD-L1 has been reported in human hepatocellular carcinoma specimens,34 the detailed mechanism has not been illustrated. The current findings revealed that palmitoyltransferase DHHC9 acted as a key functional target of BA signaling to post-transcriptionally regulate PD-L1 by modifying its palmitoylation. Protein palmitoylation is a lipid modification that mainly involves in the covalent addition of the 16-carbon fatty acid palmitate to cysteine thiols.14 It has been reported that DHHC3 palmitoylates PD-L1 at the C272 site and sensitizes CRC cells to T cell-mediated killing in vitro.16 However, our present study observed that FXR inactivation had a limited effect on modulating the expression of DHHC3. Instead, we demonstrated that DHHC9 was the key modulator on regulating the protein level of CRC upon GCA treatment. As other reported that DHHC9 is involved in palmitoylateing the expression of PD-L1 in breast cancer.14 The current study identified that DHHC9-induced palmitoylation acted as a key mechanism governing BA signaling-mediated PD-L1 stability in CRC.

Recently, ICB therapy has revolutionized MSI-H/dMMR CRC treatment options. However, 50% of these patients had poor results.35 Notably, patients with MSS/pMMR (>85%) merely have a 5–10% response rate, even though 30% of the MSS cases exhibit a level of CD8+ T cell infiltration that is comparable with that of MSI tumors.33 As the most important heterogeneity, immune repressive microenvironment remodeling is observed in several tumors, evidenced by accumulation of exhausted CD8+ T, Treg and immunosuppressive myeloid cells. Advances in metabolism research suggest that a variety of metabolites in the TME are required for the immune escape of tumor cells.36 The present study identified that serum GCA-mediated FXR inactivation-associated SOX14-DHHC9 axis promotes immune escape. Moreover, the FXR agonist GW4064 or knockdown of SOX14 and DHHC9 increased the infiltration and antitumor function of CD8+ T cells in the TME, and sensitized CRC to anti-PD-1 therapy. The current study highlights that an FXR agonist may enhance GCA-induced resistant tumors to ICB therapy. The present findings may provide a scientific basis for clinical application in the future.

In summary, the current study revealed that the GCA-regulated FXR-SOX14-DHHC9-PD-L1 axis is critical for CRC progression by impairing the infiltration and function of CD8+ T cells. Of note, re-activating FXR signaling by using GW4064 sensitized ICB-resistant CRC to anti-PD-1 therapy. Thus, these findings provide an insight for improved understanding of the pathogenesis and immunoediting of human CRC, and for developing more effective strategies for the treatment of patients with CRC with ICB therapy.

Methods

Patient biopsies

A total of 80 paired CRC tissues, adjacent normal mucosa tissues and serum collected between January 2018 and September 2018 from patients with CRC who were diagnosed at Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center (FUSCC) were used for measuring the concentrations of a series of BAs and for tissue immunofluorescence staining (cohort Ⅰ). In addition, the serum of 20 normal healthy individuals was collected in October 2018. Furthermore, 41 patients with MSI-H or dMMR with recurrence or distant metastasis who received anti PD-1 immunotherapy between February 2019 and September 2021 at FUSCC were enrolled in the present study (cohort Ⅱ). A cohort of 308 patients (Cohort III) with colorectal cancer (CRC) was enrolled between January 2008 and September 2009 at Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center. Their clinicopathological characteristics of the patients are summarized in Supplementary Data 1–3. All patients from these 3 cohorts did not receive any treatment for cancer before surgery. Human serum specimens were collected prior to tumor resection, while normal mucosa and paired cancer tissues, as well as metastatic tissues, were collected immediately after surgical resection. Written informed consent was obtained from each patient, and the study protocol both conformed to the ethical guidelines of the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of FUSCC (approval no. 050432-4-2108).

Cell lines, plasmids, lentivirus production and transduction

The HCT116 and SW620 human colon cancer cell lines, the CT26 mouse colon cancer cell line, and the 293T cell line were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection, while the MC38 mouse colon cancer cell line was purchased from the Cell Bank of the Institute of Biochemistry and Cell Biology of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. Cell lines were cultured at 37 °C in 5% CO2 in DMEM or in RPMI1640 supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum, 100 µ/ml penicillin and 100 µg/ml streptomycin. Cells were tested monthly and found to be free of mycoplasma by PCR-based screening. Full-length SOX14 and DHHC9 fused with Flag-tag were amplified via PCR from cDNA of HCT116 cells, and then recombined into the pLVX plasmid (cat. no. 125839; Addgene, Inc.). The primers used are listed in Supplementary Data 4. Short hairpin RNA (shRNA) sequences targeting SOX14 and DHHC9 were cloned into the pLKO.1 vector (cat. no. 10878; Addgene, Inc.), and the primers used are listed in Supplementary Data 5. Single guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting FXR or CD274 were constructed into the lentiCRISPR v2 vector (cat. no. 52961; Addgene, Inc.). CRISPR/Cas9 targeting sequences are listed in Supplementary Data 5. Lentivirus was produced by transiently transfecting 293T cells with Lipofectamine® 3000 transfection reagent (cat. no. L3000075; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The viral supernatants were then incubated with 8 μg/ml Polybrene for 24 h, and the viral supernatants were then exchanged for fresh media. Cells were incubated for 48 h before puromycin selection. For small interfering RNA (siRNA) transfection, tumor cells were seeded into 6-well plates at ~1.5 × 105 cells per well and incubated at 37 °C overnight. Specific siRNAs (5 nM; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) were transfected into cells and incubated for 6 h. Next, the medium was replaced with complete DMEM for 48 h. The siRNA sequences are listed in Supplementary Data 6. The anti-PD1-resistant MC38R tumor cells were developed as previously described.37 In brief, 6-week-old C57BL/6J mice were injected subcutaneously into the right flank with 1.0 × 106 MC38 cells (n = 6). At 10 days post-injection, mice were treated twice weekly with 100 μg anti-PD-1α antibody (Bio X Cell) per mouse via intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection for 1 week. Next, tumors were selected and transplanted into the right flank of 6-week-old C57BL/6J mice. After 3 repeated cycles of tumor selection, nonresponsive tumors were digested into single tumor cells, namely MC38R tumor cells.

Sample preparation and HPLC-MS/MS-based metabolomic approaches

100 μl serum were spiked with 0.51 ml of ice-cold MeOH-ACN (1:1, v:v). The sample was vortexed and incubated for 1 h at −20 °C, following by centrifuging at 14,000 rcf at 4 °C for 15 min to precipitate proteins. The supernatant was aspirated, evaporated using a vacuum concentrator (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The extracts were reconstituted in 100 μl of MeOH and deionized water (85:15, v:v), and centrifuged at at 15,000 × g at 4 °C for 15 min to remove insoluble debris. The supernatant was collected at UPLC autosampler vials (Thermo Scientific, MA, USA) and stored at −80 °C prior to UPLC-MS analysis. Tumor samples were homogenized in 100% acetonitrile and were centrifuged twice at 15,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C for removal of precipitated proteins and other particulates to remove precipitated proteins and particulates. The supernatant was then diluted with 0.1% formic acid. The quality control sample was pooled by mixing 5 μL of extracted solution from each sample.

Liquid chromatographic separation and mass spectrometric detection were conducted using the Thermo Scientific TSQ Quantum Ultra LC-MS/MS system (Thermo Fisher, Palo Alto, CA). Chromatographic separation was achieved on a Poroshell SB-C18 analytical column (particle size, 1.7 μm; 50 mm (length) × 2.1 mm (i.d.)) equipped with a BEH C18 VanGuard pre-column (5 × 2.1 mm, i.d.; 1.7 μm). The mobile phase consisted of aqueous buffer containing 1 mM ammonium formate and formic acid (pH 4.39) (A) and acetonitrile/isopropanol (1:1 v/v) (B), with a total flow rate of 0.4 ml/min. The gradient profile for the LC pumps was as follows: 20–30% B (0–3 min), 30–40% B (3–4 min), 50–70% B (4–5 min), 70–90% B (5–5.2 min), continuing at 90% B up to 6 min. The injection volume for all samples was 2 μl. The column temperature was set to 55 °C, and the sample tray temperature was maintained at 8 °C. For mass spectrometric detection, the electrospray ionization (ESI) source was operated in negative ion mode to produce MS/MS spectra (Thermo Fisher). The parameters were as follows: spray voltage 2 kV, cone voltage 30 V, source temperature 120 °C, desolvation temperature 500 °C, cone gas flow 50 L/h, desolations gas flow 900 L/h.

For preparation of standard stock, 33 bile acid were dissolved to 1 mg/mL by appropriate amount of methanol as standard stock solutions and then further diluted to obtain standard solutions containing 0.05, 0.5, 5, and 50 mg/mL of each bile acid. The supernatant was collected and 70 μl was injected into the LC–MS/MS system for analysis. Cholic acid (CA), chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA), sodium glycochenodeoxycholate (GCDCA), sodium taurochenodeoxycholate (TCDCA), glycocholic acid (GCA), taurolithocholic acid (TLCA), sodium taurodeoxycholate hydrate (TDCA), and taurocholic acid sodium salt hydrate (TCA) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Saint Louis, MO). Ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA), deoxycholic acid (DCA). Lithocholic acid (LCA) and glycoursodeoxycholic acid (GUDCA) were procured from Cayman Chemical Company (Ann Arbor, MI, USA). Glycodeoxycholic acid (GDCA) and glycolithocholic acid (GLCA) were acquired from Toronto Research Chemicals (TRC, Canada).

Data-independent acquisition mass spectrometry (MS)-based proteomics

MC38 cells were treated with or without GCA for 24 h and processed by Data Independent Acquisition6 individually to assess the proteome. In brief, for sample preparation, cells were lysed in lysis buffer (1% SDS and 8 M urea) and digested with iST Sample Preparation kit (PreOmics) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Peptides were eluted with elution buffer (2 × 100 µl) and then lyophilized by SpeedVac. Protein samples were reconstituted in 0.1% FA and subjected to UltiMate 3000 liquid chromatography (LC) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.). In total, 200 ng peptide was separated by an analytical column with a 60 min gradient starting at 4% buffer B (80% ACN with 0.1% FA), followed by a stepwise increase to 28% in 25 min, 44% in 10 min, 90% in 10 min, hold for 7 min, and then equilibration at 4% for 8 min. The column flow rate was maintained at 500 nl/min with a column temperature of 50˚C. DIA data were acquired in the diaPASEF mode. Raw data of DIA were analyzed by Spectronaut 17 (Biognosys AG) with default settings. The normalization strategy was set to local normalization. Peptides which passed the 1% Q-value cut-off were used to calculate the major group quantities with the MaxLFQ method. Differentially expressed proteins were selected based on P < 0.05, fold-change (FC) > 1.5 or FC < 1/1.5.

Dual luciferase reporter assay

For measurement of FXR activity, cells were seeded into 24-well plates at ~1.5 × 104 cells per well and transfected with the FXR luciferase reporter plasmid FXRE-Luc (200 ng; cat. no. 11764ES03; Shanghai Yeasen Biotechnology Co., Ltd) and 20 ng Renilla pRL-SV40P plasmid (cat. no. 27163; Addgene, Inc.) per well with Lipofectamine® 3000 according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Next, cells were treated with GCA (100 nM, cat. no. G2878; Sigma-Aldrich; Merck KGaA) or GW4064 (5 μM, cat. no. G5172; Sigma-Aldrich; Merck KGaA) for 24 h, and the relative luciferase units (RLUs) were measured using the Dual-Glo Luciferase Assay System (Promega Corporation) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For monitoring SOX9 or DHHC9 expression, cells were seeded and transfected with 200 ng pGL3-SOX14, pGL3-SOX14-FXR mutant15, pGL3-DHHC9 or pGL3-DHHC9-SOX14 mut vector, along with 20 ng Renilla pRL-SV40P plasmid per well with Lipofectamine® 2000. After 24 h, the RLUs in cells were measured using the Dual-Glo Luciferase Assay System. The firefly RLUs were normalized by the RLUs from the Renilla-containing vector.

Mice

Fxr floxed mice were purchased from Cyagen Biosciences, Inc. (cat. no. S-CKO-04890). Villin-Cre (#004586) transgenic mice were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA) to create conditional knockout mice and silenced the expression of Fxr in intestinal epithelial cells. Briefly, two synthesized sgRNAs targeting introns 3 and 4, Cas9 mRNA and LoxP-containing oligonucleotide flanking with 50-bp homology arms were injected into mouse zygotes and then transferred into pseudo-pregnant mice. Fxr floxed mice were identified by genotyping and sequencing. Fxr floxed mice were then crossed with villin-Cre transgenic mice to obtain intestinal epithelial-specific Fxr knockout mouse models. The mice were randomly assigned into experimental groups. For primary mouse models, animals were chosen based on correct genotypes and were co-allocated in the same cages and used for the studies. All mice used in the study were gender- and age-matched. Investigators were blinded to the treatments during the experiments. All mice were housed in the specific-pathogen-free animal facility of FUSCC. Animals were observed for general health and signs of distress (reduced weight, ruffled fur, or evidence of tumor ulceration/bleeding). Mice were euthanized when body weight loss exceeded 20% or when tumor volume exceeded 1500 mm³. Euthanasia was performed in accordance with institutional and guidelines by CO₂ inhalation followed by cervical dislocation to ensure death. The maximal tumor burden permitted by our ethics committee is 1500 mm³, and this limit was not exceeded in any experiment. All animal procedures were approved by the Animal care and Use Committee of FUSCC.

For tumor engraftment, 6-week-old C57BL/6J or BALB/c mice were injected subcutaneously into the right flank with 1.0 × 106 MC38 or CT26 cells, respectively. BALB/c nude mice were also used to establish a subcutaneous CRC model by using MC38 or CT26 cells. The tumor volumes were calculated by using the formula “length × width × width/2” at the indicated days. Body weight was recorded at the time of tumor measurement. For survival analysis, mice were considered moribund and euthanized when tumor volume reached approximately 1000 mm³ or when severe signs of distress were observed. For endoscopy-guided orthotopic mouse model, wild-type 10–12 weeks old C57BL/6 mice were examined by endoscopy Coloview® system (Storz, Tuttlingen, Germany) with a zero-degree optic (diameter 1.9 mm) to ensure the colonic mucosa was healthy prior to implantation after anesthesia. For tumor cell implantation, the colon was first washed with PBS at 37 °C and 0.5 × 106 MC38 cells) diluted in 50 μL of PBS were injected very slowly towards the lumen of lower mid and distal colon ~30 mm from the anal verge to induce tumor growth by using endoscopic injection. The tumor volume was endoscopically monitored weekly by endoscopic score. For the AOM/DSS-induced colitis-associated cancer model, 8 to 10-week-old male mice were injected i.p. with 10 mg/kg AOM (cat. no. A5486; Sigma-Aldrich; Merck KGaA). After 5 days, 2% DSS (cat. no. D8906; Sigma-Aldrich; Merck KGaA) was added to the drinking water for 5 days, followed by regular drinking water for 2 weeks. The mice were sacrificed after 3 repeated cycles of DSS. For the macrophage-depletion experiment, the mice were pretreated with clodronate liposome (150 μl; Max Scientific) 3 days before inoculating with tumor cells, and then the indicated mice were administered with clodronate and control liposomes, with or without GCA [400 mµg/kg, intragastric gavage (ig) or GW4064 (50 mg/kg, i.p.)] twice per week during the course of the experiments. For the CD8-depletion experiment, mice were treated with an anti-CD8 antibody (200 µg per injection; clone 2.43; Bio X Cell) twice weekly during the course of the experiments. For anti-PD-1 antibody treatment, mice were treated with 100 μg anti-PD-1α antibody (Bio X Cell) per mouse twice weekly or the corresponding IgG control via i.p. injection for the indicated days. For anti-PD-L1 antibody treatment, mice were treated with 10 mg/kg or 100 mg/kg anti-PD-L1 antibody (Bio X Cell) per mouse twice weekly or the corresponding IgG control via i.p. injection for the indicated days. For GCA (400 mg/kg, intragastric gavage), GW4064 (50 mg/kg, i.p.) or T-βMCA (400 mg/kg, i.p.) treatment, mice were treated with GW4064 or T-βMCA twice per week after 6 days of being inoculated with the indicated CRC cells.

mRNA extraction and reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells or tumors by using TRizol® reagent (cat. no. 15596026CN; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Next, the extracted RNA was reversely transcribed to cDNA with PrimeScript® RT Reagent Kit (cat. no. RR037B; Takara Bio, Inc.). RT-qPCR was conducted with a fast two-step amplification program using FastStart Universal SYBR Green Master Mix (cat. no. 04913850001; Roche Diagnostics GmbH). Data were normalized to GAPDH. The primers used are listed in Supplementary Data 7.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

HCT116- and SW620-expressing SOX14-Flag CRC cells, or CRC cells treated with GCA or GW4064 for 24 h, were subjected to ChIP assay with SimpleChIP® Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit (cat. no. 9003S; Cell Signaling Technology, Inc.) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Mouse monoclonal antibody specific for FXR (cat. no. 72105; Cell Signaling Technology, Inc.) and rabbit monoclonal antibody specific for SOX14 (cat. no. PA1-28074; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) were used for ChIP assay. SYBR Green RT-qPCR was performed by using the following primers: FXR binding site 1 forward: 5′-TTTGTATTTCAAGCCCTAA-3′ and reverse: 5′-GACTTCGGTTCCACATTGT-3′; FXR binding site 2 forward: 5′-CAGTCTAGAGCTAGCCTCT-3′ and reverse: 5′-CGCACCTGCCAACACGCCGG-3′; SOX14 binding site 1 forward: 5′-AGGGAGATGGGTGGAGGAGT-3′ and reverse 5′-CGGCCGCCCCTATC-3′; and SOX14 binding site 2 forward: 5′-GGAGGCGCCGAGGCC-3′ and reverse: 5′-CCCACCACTGGCCAAAT-3′.

Immunoblotting

For immunoblotting, samples harvested form cells or tumors were lysed with RIPA buffer (cat. no. 89901; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) supplemented with a protease inhibitor (cat. no. 05892970001; Roche Diagnostics GmbH) and a phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (cat. no. 04906845001; Roche Diagnostics GmbH) for 1 h. The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were collected, and equal quantities of cell lysates (100 μg) were mixed with 5X SDS-PAGE loading dye. Next, the samples were separated using 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (MilliporeSigma). The membranes were then incubated with the indicated primary and secondary antibodies and visualized using the SuperSignal West Dura Extended Duration Substrate (cat. no. 34076; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.). The antibodies employed are listed in Supplementary Data 8.

Immunofluorescence microscopy and multiplex immunofluorescence staining

For immunofluorescence microscopy assay, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, followed by permeabilization with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 15 min. Samples were blocked with 1% goat serum for 30 min, and then incubated with the indicated primary antibodies at 4 °C for 12 h before being incubated at room temperature with secondary antibodies conjugated to Alexa Fluor 488/594. DAPI (cat. no. ab104139; Abcam) was used to stain the nuclei for 30 min before subjecting the samples to Zeiss Axiovert-200 fluorescence microscope (Zeiss AG).

For multiplex staining, samples were treated following the Opal protocol staining method. The reagents used were as follows: Anti-SOX14 (cat. no. #ab214041; 1:500; Abcam), anti-DHHC9, anti-PD-L1 and anti-CD8 (cat. no. 98941; Cell Signaling Technology, Inc.; RRID: AB_2756376, 1:500) antibodies were visualized by using fluorescein-cyanine (Cy) 7 (1:50), fluorescein-Cy5 (1:50) and fluorescein-Cy3 (1:50). In addition, DAPI (1:2000) was used for nuclei staining. All sections were cover slipped using VECTASHIELD Hardset Antifade Mounting Medium (cat. no. #H-1400; Vector Laboratories). The slides were scanned, and the mean fluorescence intensity of the indicated markers was analyzed with a Vectra slide scanner (PerkinElmer, Inc.) under fluorescence conditions.

Co-culture assay

MC38 or CT26 cells were injected (1.0 × 106 cells) subcutaneously into the right flank of C57BL/6 or BALB/c mice for 2 weeks. Next, the CD8+ T cells in MC38 or CT26 tumors were isolated by FACs. The number of indicated MC38 cells or CT26 cells was counted after 3 days of co-culture with CD8+ T cells (1.0 × 104 cells) in the presence of anti-CD3 (5 μg/ml) and anti-CD28 (5 μg/ml) antibody.

Tumor digestion, cell isolation and flow cytometry analysis

Tumors derived from the indicated mice were sheared and digested by using digestion buffer [RPMI medium supplemented with 5% FBS, intravenous collagenase I (0.5 mg/mL, cat. no. C1-22; Sigma-Aldrich; Merck KGaA) and DNase (1 µg/ml; cat. no. D5025; Sigma-Aldrich; Merck KGaA) for 30 min at 37 °C. Next, the mixture was filtered, and red blood cells (RBCs) were lysed using RBC lysis buffer (cat. no. 00-4300-54; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.). The precipitates were washed with buffer II (PBS with 2% FBS) and blocked with anti-CD16/32 antibody for 30 min. The single-cell suspensions were washed and incubated with the indicated antibodies before being subjected to LSRII (BD Biosciences) for analysis. Data were analyzed using FlowJo. The antibodies used are listed in Table S8. Cell populations were identified based on the following expression markers: CD4 T cells: CD45+/CD4+; CD8 T cells: CD45+/ CD8+; MDSC cells: CD45+/CD11b+/Gr-1+; and macrophages: CD45+/CD11b+/F4/80+. For intracellular staining, cells were stimulated with phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (20 ng/ml; cat. no. p8139; Sigma-Aldrich; Merck KGaA), ionomycin (1 μg/ml; cat. no. 13909; Sigma-Aldrich; Merck KGaA) and brefeldin A (5 ng/ml; cat. no. B5936; Sigma-Aldrich; Merck KGaA) for 4 h and then stained according to the intracellular cytokine staining procedure. Gate strategy (Fig. S12) and representative image of tumors (Fig. S13) were shown in Supplementary Figures.

Single cell sorting, library preparation and sequencing and data preprocessing

Tumor single-cell suspensions were prepared and filtered over 40-µm cell strainers. Next, cell concentration and viability were evaluated by Trypan Blue staining. The isolated cells were subjected to single-cell RNA analysis. In brief, cells were loaded to prepare single-cell gel beads in emulsions (GEMs) by using Chromium Single Cell Controller Instrument (10× Genomics). GEMs were encapsulated using the Single Cell 3′ Library & Cell Bead Kit (10x Genomics). Completed libraries were sequenced by Illumina NovaSeq 6000 using high-output 75-cycle kits with 150-bp paired-end reads. The data files were analyzed with the R package SingleR. Gene transcript counts per cell were determined by the unique molecular identifier (UMI; R package Seurat, version 3.1.1). After removing low-quality cells and likely multiplet captures, Seurat (normalizeData function) was used to normalize the expression values for each cell by the total expression and log-transformed the results. FindVariableGenes function in Seurat was performed to identify the highly variable genes. Then, these genes were subjected to RunPCA function and FindClusters function of Seurat to culculate the significant principal components. The clusters were visualized in a two-dimensional t-SNE algorithm using the RunTSNE function in Seurat and the marker genes of each cluster were identified by The FindAllMarkers function in Seurat. Additionally, differentially expressed genes (DEGs, P < 0.05 and |log2 (fold-change)| >0.58) was filtered by the FindMarkers function. Then, SingleR was used for unbiased cell type recognition of scRNA-seq, with the reference transcriptomic datasets ‘Human Primary Cell Atlas’ to infer the origin of each single cell independently and to identify cell types.38 Gene Ontology enrichment pathway enrichment analyses of DEGs were carried out using the hypergeometric distribution of R software. For immune cells, CD45+ cells were gated, and five major clusters of immune cells were identified, including CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, macrophages, B cells, neutrophils and NK cells by using markers for inferring immune cell populations in time-of-flight (CyTOF) analysis as previous study39. Sequencing and bioinformatics analysis were performed by Shanghai OE Biotech Co., Ltd.

PD-L1 palmitoylation

Tumor cells were co-cultured with complete medium containing 100 μM Click-iT Palmitic Acid-Azide (cat. no. C10265; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 6 h. Next, the cells were harvested and lysed with lysis buffer (1% SDS in 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0) supplemented with a protease inhibitor and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail on ice for 0.5 h. After sonication, the cell lysate was centrifuged at 12,000 × g at 4 °C for 10 min. The supernatants of cell lysates were collected and subject to Click-iT assay by using the Click-iT Protein Reaction Buffer Kit (cat. no. C10276; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The biotin-alkyne (cat. no. 901995; Sigma-Aldrich; Merck KGaA) connected palmitic-protein complex was followed by streptavidin (cat. no. 3419; Cell Signaling Technology, Inc.) pull-down and immunoblot assay for PD-L1.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed by using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software; Dotmatics). No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. No data was excluded from the analyses. For data showing normal distribution, the statistically significant differences between 2 groups of samples were estimated by applying an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests. One-way ANOVA and Dunnett’s post hoc test were used for comparison between ≥3 groups. The log-rank test was also applied to assess statistical significance in survival data. Statistical significance (p-values) was defined as not significant (ns): p > 0.05; *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001. All of the experiment was independently performed 3 times, each yielding similar results; data from one representative independent experiment is presented as the mean ± SD.
