Ferroptosis-related TFRC: a potential therapeutic target in sepsis and regulatory effect of γ-Tocotrienol
Authors: Yandong Yao, Gege Ke, Yuanxin Wu, Haili Li, Yingchun Hu, Muhu Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFerroptosis, an iron-dependent regulated cell death, plays a critical role in the pathophysiology of sepsis. This study aimed to identify core targets and therapeutic agents related to ferroptosis in sepsis.MethodsDifferential analysis of peripheral blood RNA-sequencing data from 19 patients with sepsis and 10 healthy controls was performed. Ferroptosis-related hub genes were identified via a PPI network and validated by Mendelian randomization, protein cohort studies, and survival/meta-analyses. Single-cell sequencing localized core genes, and in vitro/in vivo experiments explored targeted drugs.ResultsHigh TFRC expression correlated with poor sepsis prognosis. TFRC was predominantly expressed in monocytes and B cells, with more monocytes in non-survivors. γ‑Tocotrienol (γ‑T3) treatment was associated with reduced TFRC expression, lower ROS and IL‑1β levels, and improved survival in septic zebrafish and mice.ConclusionTFRC is a potential therapeutic target for ferroptosis in sepsis. γ‑T3 alleviates LPS-induced TFRC upregulation and improves survival in septic mice, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic agent.Study Registration: ChiCTR1900021261 (Chinese Clinical Trial Registry), registered February 4, 2019. Type of study: retrospective observational study using archived biological samples.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40246-026-01003-z.

Introduction

Sepsis is a life-threatening syndrome caused by a dysregulated host response to infection, resulting in systemic inflammation, multi-organ dysfunction, and increased mortality rates [1]. The pathophysiology of sepsis involves complex interactions among immune cells, oxidative stress, and cellular injury, with emerging evidence underscoring the critical role of ferroptosis, an iron‑dependent, regulated cell death driven by lipid peroxidation and iron overload [2–5]. Pathological iron accumulation amplifies inflammatory cascades and immune dysregulation [6–8]. In sepsis, disrupted iron homeostasis and heightened oxidative stress exacerbate ferroptosis, resulting in cellular membrane damage and organ failure, particularly in the liver, kidneys, and lungs [5, 9–11]. Recent studies have demonstrated that macrophage ferroptosis aggravates sepsis-induced lung injury and contributes to poor clinical outcomes[12, 13], and that ferroptosis also mediates sepsis-associated cardiac injury [14]. Conversely, inhibiting ferroptosis attenuates sepsis-related organ damage [15–18], highlighting its dual role as a driver of pathology and a therapeutic target. Given the profound influence of ferroptosis on sepsis severity and prognosis [19], this study aimed to identify ferroptosis‑related prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets in sepsis, and to explore potential drugs against these core targets (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Research workflow. Methods

Instruments

An artificial climate incubator (RGX-70ES), microscope (Bayer), stereoscopic fluorescence microscope (Mingmei MZX81), water bath (HWS-24), automated zebrafish behavior analysis system (Zebralab), stereoscopic three-dimensional fluorescence imaging system (ZE2004, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), microplate reader (Thermo Fisher), and chemiluminescent gel imaging system (Tannon-5200) were used.

Reagents

For details, please refer to the appendix.

Collection of samples

This study was conducted from January to December 2019 in the intensive care unit (ICU) of the Affiliated Hospital of Southwest Medical University in Luzhou, Sichuan Province, China. Peripheral blood samples were collected from 19 patients with sepsis and 10 healthy individuals. These samples were used for RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between sepsis patients and healthy controls were identified using the RNA-seq data. The datasets generated and analyzed during this study are available in the China National GeneBank Database (CNGBdb) [20]

(https://db.cngb.org/) under accession number CNP0002611.

The inclusion criteria for sepsis cases were: (1) meeting the 2016 Sepsis‑3 diagnostic criteria jointly published by the Society of Critical Care Medicine (SCCM) and the European Society of Intensive Care Medicine (ESICM) (infection + ΔSOFA score ≥ 2) [21]; (2) no history of severe infectious diseases or immune dysfunction; and (3) provision of signed informed consent. The exclusion criteria were: (1) presence of malignant tumors; (2) immunosuppressive therapy within the three months prior to the study; and (3) unwillingness to participate. All participants provided written informed consent, and the study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Southwest Medical University (Ethics Approval Number: ky2018029). This study was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Registration Number: ChiCTR1900021261). The study involving human participants adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki [22].

Preprocessing of PAXgene samples

Whole blood collection is the first step for measuring multiple intracellular RNA molecules. The PAXgene® Blood RNA System (BD Biosciences), comprising the PAXgene® Blood RNA Tube for collection and the PAXgene® Blood RNA Kit for nucleic acid purification, was used in this study. The PAXgene® Blood RNA Tube contains a proprietary reagent mixture that stabilizes the entire transcriptional profile of endogenous genes immediately upon blood collection [23]. When used with the PAXgene® Blood RNA System, this tube ensures accurate identification and quantification of gene expression.

Before sample collection, the PAXgene® Blood RNA Tubes were stored refrigerated at 18 °C to maintain stability, and each tube was labeled with detailed patient information for traceability. Blood samples were collected by standard venipuncture and transferred directly into the tubes. After collection, the tubes were gently inverted ten times to mix the blood thoroughly with the stabilization reagent, then stored at 18 °C to preserve RNA integrity. Three days after collection, total RNA was purified using the PAXgene® Blood RNA Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. This process yielded high‑quality RNA suitable for downstream transcriptomic analyses.

RNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from blood samples using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). RNA quality and purity were assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Following the manufacturer’s protocol, ribosomal RNA (rRNA) was depleted using target‑specific oligonucleotides and RNase H to minimize interference in downstream analyses. This step enriches target RNA species, improving capture efficiency for non‑coding RNAs (e.g., lncRNA and miRNA) and mRNA in high‑throughput sequencing.

After RNA purification, residual impurities and small RNA fragments were removed using SPRI beads. RNA was then fragmented at high temperature in the presence of divalent cations to generate appropriately sized fragments for library construction. First‑strand cDNA was synthesized from fragmented RNA using reverse transcriptase and random primers, and second‑strand cDNA was synthesized using DNA polymerase I and RNase H to produce a double‑stranded cDNA library.

Library quality and quantity were assessed by two methods: (1) fragment size distribution was examined using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer to ensure uniformity and appropriate size range; (2) library concentration was quantified by quantitative PCR (qPCR) with TaqMan probes to verify sequencing suitability. Libraries that passed quality control were subjected to paired‑end sequencing on the BGISEQ‑500/MGISEQ‑2000 platforms (BGI, Shenzhen, China), which provide high‑throughput, high‑precision sequencing for deep data generation.Raw sequencing data (including lncRNA, mRNA, and miRNA) were processed with SOAPnuke software (https://github.com/BGI-flexlab/SOAPnuke) [24]. for stringent quality control and removal of low‑quality reads. The filtered “clean” reads were saved in FASTQ format to ensure accuracy and reliability of subsequent analyses.

Bioinformatics analysis section

Differential expression analysis

Raw RNA‑seq data were processed using the online analysis platform IDEP2.0 (http://bioinformatics.sdstate.edu/idep/) [25]. Data were uploaded to the “LoadData” interface with the type specified as read counts. After successful upload, the platform performed log transformation and normalization. The processed data were visualized using box plots and principal component analysis (PCA), both generated with the ggplot2 package in R. Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 software package [26]. Differentially expressed RNAs between the normal control (NC) and sepsis groups were identified using a threshold of P < 0.05 and |log2FC|> 1. Results were visualized with the ggplot2 package.

Identification of ferroptosis-related genes in sepsis

First, tables of ferroptosis-related driver, suppressor, and marker genes were downloaded from the FerrDb database (http://www.zhounan.org/ferrdb/current/) [27]. Only validated ferroptosis-related genes were retained. The list of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was then intersected with the ferroptosis gene set to identify ferroptosis-related genes associated with sepsis. The overlap was visualized using the ggplot2 and VennDiagram packages in R.

GO analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) is a classification system describing gene and gene product functions across organisms. It comprises three ontologies: Molecular Function (MF), Cellular Component (CC), and Biological Process (BP). Each GO term represents a specific biological attribute.For functional analysis, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were mapped to GO terms, and the number of genes associated with each term was calculated. A hypergeometric test was applied to identify significantly enriched terms (P < 0.05), revealing primary biological functions and pathways associated with the DEGs [28].Enrichment analysis was performed using the clusterProfiler R package after gene ID conversion. Z‑scores for enriched terms were computed using the GOplot package based on the gene expression data. Results were visualized with the ggplot2 package.

KEGG pathway analysis

Pathway‑based analyses help elucidate how genes function together in biological systems. The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) is a widely used public database providing comprehensive information on biological pathways. Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using a hypergeometric test to identify KEGG pathways significantly enriched among the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) relative to the whole genome [29]. A significance threshold of P ≤ 0.05 was applied. After ID conversion, enrichment analysis was conducted using the clusterProfiler R package. Results were visualized with the ggplot2 package.

Identification of hub genes

To further identify hub genes associated with ferroptosis in sepsis, the overlapping genes were submitted to the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/) to construct protein‑protein interaction (PPI) networks [30], with a minimum required interaction score of 0.4. The resulting networks were exported to Cytoscape (version 3.10.2) for further analysis [31]. The cytoHubba plugin was used to score and rank nodes using the Degree, EPC, MCC, and MNC methods. The top ten hub genes from each method were identified, and their intersection was taken as the set of ferroptosis‑related hub genes in sepsis.

Mendelian randomization analysis

To explore causal relationships between core genes, sepsis susceptibility, and 28‑day mortality in sepsis, we performed a two‑sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis using the DMRdb platform (http://www.inbirg.com/DMRdb/#/base) [32]. Exposure type was set to “gene” and the hub genes were entered as exposures. Outcome type was set to “disease”, and the following outcomes were entered: sepsis, sepsis (under 75 years), sepsis (critical care), sepsis (28‑day death in critical care), sepsis (Phecode 994.2), and sepsis (28‑day death). Analysis type was set to eQTL‑disease. Genetic variants associated with hub gene expression were selected as instrumental variables (IVs), with sepsis as the clinical endpoint.The primary analysis method was inverse‑variance weighted (IVW) regression with multiplicative random effects, accounting for potential heterogeneity across genetic variants. Statistical significance for causal estimates was set at P < 0.05. This analytical framework adhered to the STROBE‑MR guidelines [33] and satisfied the three core MR assumptions: (1) relevance of IVs to exposure, (2) independence from confounding factors, and (3) exclusion restriction (no direct effect of IVs on the outcome).

Protein cohort validation

A proteome‑wide cohort analysis was performed using the Proteome‑Phenome Atlas platform (https://proteome-phenome-atlas.com/) [34] to investigate associations between hub genes and sepsis. In the disease‑wide analysis module, the following parameters were configured: (1) disease type was set to “incident”; (2) the population was set as the full population; (3) the significance level was set at 0.05; and (4) to ensure precise case ascertainment, “explicit sepsis” was used as the diagnostic criterion, strictly adhering to the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‑10) coding standards for sepsis identification [1].

Survival analysis

To further identify genes associated with sepsis prognosis, we downloaded the publicly available dataset GSE65682 [35], which contains gene expression profiles and clinical outcome data from 478 patients with sepsis (365 survivors and 113 non‑survivors). We used the survival package to test the proportional hazards assumption and fit the survival regression model; results were visualized using the survminer and ggplot2 packages. Patients were divided into high‑ and low‑TFRC expression groups based on the median TFRC level. The log‑rank test was used to assess the association between TFRC expression and patient survival. A P‑value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and genes with significant survival differences were identified as potential therapeutic targets for sepsis.

Meta analysis

To further investigate differences in transcript‑level expression of core genes between sepsis survivors and non‑survivors, we downloaded three peripheral blood RNA‑seq datasets from the GEO public database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/): GSE33118, GSE95233, and GSE185263. After normalizing each dataset with log2 transformation, we performed a meta‑analysis using the R package MultiMeta20. This analysis assessed the expression of individual genes across datasets to validate the expression trends observed in our study.

Single-cell RNA sequencing

To explore immune cell differences between sepsis survivors and non‑survivors and to examine hub gene expression across cell types, we collected two peripheral blood samples (one from a survivor and one from a non‑survivor). Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated by density gradient centrifugation and processed using the 10 × Genomics platform. This platform uses microfluidic technology to encapsulate cells in droplets with barcoded beads. After cell capture, droplets are broken, allowing cellular mRNA to bind to the bead barcodes, forming single‑cell gel beads in emulsion (GEMs).cDNA libraries were generated within droplets by reverse transcription, and sample index sequences were added to distinguish different samples. Raw sequencing data (FASTQ format) were processed with 10 × Genomics Cell Ranger software for quality control. Cells were excluded if they contained fewer than 200 genes, fewer than 1,000 unique molecular identifiers (UMIs), a log10(genes per UMI) value below 0.7, more than 10% mitochondrial UMI content, or more than 5% of reads mapping to hemoglobin genes. Remaining high‑quality cells were further processed with DoubletFinder to remove potential doublets.Single‑cell clustering was performed using the Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) algorithm, and cells were clustered using a shared nearest neighbor (SNN) algorithm. Optimal clustering was achieved with a resolution of 0.4, yielding 10 distinct clusters. Cell types were identified using marker genes.

Cellular localization of hub genes

Differentially expressed hub genes between sepsis and control groups were identified from RNA sequencing data. Single‑cell RNA sequencing was then used to assess the expression of these upregulated hub genes in sepsis survivors versus non‑survivors, and to identify immune cell types showing the most pronounced expression differences between the two groups. Hub gene expression levels were examined across the identified cell clusters. By integrating these analyses, we pinpointed the cellular expression sites of ferroptosis‑related genes associated with sepsis prognosis. This approach provides valuable insights into the roles of specific immune cell types in sepsis and their potential as therapeutic targets.

Cell experiments

Macrophage culture

RAW264.7 cells were purchased from Luzhou Micro‑O Biotech Co., Ltd. (Cat. No. M‑C2017, Mcellbank). Cells were cultured in high‑glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% (v/v) heat‑inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% (v/v) penicillin‑streptomycin (100 U/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin). Cultures were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ and 95% relative humidity in a HEPA‑filtered CO₂ incubator.

Drug preparation

LPS was dissolved in DMSO to 5 mg/mL and then diluted to the working concentration in DMEM containing 10% FBS without penicillin/streptomycin. γ‑T3 was dissolved in DMSO to 100 mM and diluted to the working concentration in DMEM with 10% FBS without penicillin/streptomycin.

CCK8 cytotoxicity assay

RAW264.7 cells were seeded in 96‑well plates at 1 × 104 cells per well. After 24 h, varying concentrations of LPS (0, 100, 200, 400, 800, 1000, 1500, 2000 ng/mL) or γ‑T3 (0, 5, 10, 20, 40, 80, 100, 200 μM) were added. Following 24 h of treatment, 10% CCK‑8 reagent was added to each well and incubated for 2 h. Absorbance at 450 nm (OD₄₅₀) was measured using a microplate reader, and cell survival rates were calculated. The experiment was repeated three times.

qRT‑PCR

For the normal control group (NC), cells were cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS (no penicillin/streptomycin) for 3 h, then the medium was replaced with fresh 10% FBS/DMEM (no antibiotics) and cells were incubated for another 4 h. For the LPS stimulation group, cells were first incubated in 10% FBS/DMEM without antibiotics for 3 h, then the medium was replaced with 10% FBS/DMEM containing 1000 ng/mL LPS (no antibiotics) and incubated for 4 h to induce an inflammatory response. For the γ‑T3 intervention group, cells were pretreated with γ‑T3 at 10, 20, or 40 μM in 10% FBS/DMEM without antibiotics for 3 h, then the medium was replaced with 10% FBS/DMEM containing the same concentration of γ‑T3 plus 1000 ng/mL LPS (no antibiotics) and incubated for 4 h.After treatment, the supernatant was removed, and total cellular RNA was extracted using an RNA extraction kit in a sterile, RNase‑free biosafety cabinet. Reverse transcription was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions, followed by qPCR using a qPCR kit. β‑Actin served as the internal reference gene. Relative mRNA expression was calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate, and the experiment was independently repeated three times.

ELISA

RAW264.7 cells were seeded in 6‑well plates and grown to 70–80% confluence before treatment. For the NC group, cells were incubated in DMEM with 10% FBS (no antibiotics) for 3 h, then the medium was replaced with fresh 10% FBS/DMEM (no antibiotics) and cells were cultured for an additional 13 h. For the LPS stimulation group, cells were incubated in 10% FBS/DMEM without antibiotics for 3 h, then the medium was replaced with 10% FBS/DMEM containing 1000 ng/mL LPS (no antibiotics) and incubated for 13 h. For the γ‑T3 intervention group, cells were pretreated with γ‑T3 at 10, 20, or 40 μM in 10% FBS/DMEM without antibiotics for 3 h, then the medium was replaced with 10% FBS/DMEM containing the same concentration of γ‑T3 plus 1000 ng/mL LPS (no antibiotics) and incubated for 13 h. After treatment, the cell culture supernatant was collected, and IL‑1β concentration was measured using an ELISA kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Western blot analysis

For the NC group, cells were first cultured for 3 h in DMEM with 10% FBS (no penicillin‑streptomycin). The medium was then replaced with fresh 10% FBS/DMEM (no antibiotics) and cells were cultured for another 6 h.For the LPS stimulation group, cells were incubated for 3 h in 10% FBS/DMEM without antibiotics, then the medium was replaced with 10% FBS/DMEM containing 1000 ng/mL LPS (no antibiotics) and incubated for another 6 h.

For the γ‑T3 intervention group, cells were pretreated for 3 h with γ‑T3 at 10, 20, or 40 μM in 10% FBS/DMEM without antibiotics. The medium was then replaced with 10% FBS/DMEM containing the corresponding concentration of γ‑T3 plus 1000 ng/mL LPS (no antibiotics) and incubation continued for 6 h.After treatment, the supernatant was discarded, and cells were washed twice with ice‑cold phosphate‑buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4). Total cellular protein was extracted using lysis buffer supplemented with 1% protease inhibitor and 1% phosphatase inhibitor. Cells were lysed on ice for 30 min, and the lysate was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatant containing soluble protein was collected, and protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay.Equal amounts of protein were mixed with 5 × SDS loading buffer and denatured by heating at 98 °C for 8 min. Denatured proteins were separated by 12% SDS‑PAGE (80 V for stacking gel, 120 V for resolving gel) and transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane using a wet transfer system at 300 mA for 90 min. The membrane was blocked with TBST containing 5% non‑fat milk for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After three 10‑min washes with TBST, the membrane was incubated with secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature, washed three more times with TBST, and protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection kit.

Zebrafish experiments

Laboratory animals

Transgenic zebrafish line CZ98 (ihb20Tg, Tg(mpeg1:EGFP)) was used. Zebrafish were obtained from the Zebrafish Experimental Center of Southwest Medical University. According to European Union legislation on animal protection, zebrafish embryos and larvae within five days post‑fertilization (dpf) are not classified as “animals.” Therefore, zebrafish embryos serve as a valid alternative model to mammals. The present study used zebrafish embryos for γ‑tocotrienol experiments while adhering to the 3R principles of laboratory animal welfare (Replacement, Reduction, Refinement).

Zebrafish breeding

Adult zebrafish were reared in a recirculating water system under a 14 h light/10 h dark cycle at 28 °C and fed three times daily. To obtain embryos, male and female zebrafish were paired at a 1:1 ratio in the evening and separated by a baffle. The next morning, the baffle was removed, and spawning occurred within 1 h after lights on. Embryos were placed in 10 cm Petri dishes containing E3 solution and cultured in an artificial climate incubator (14 h light/10 h dark) at 28.5 °C until experimental treatment.

Screening of LPS concentrations for zebrafish sepsis model

To establish a reliable in vivo model for studying the effect of γ‑T3 on sepsis, we first screened the appropriate LPS concentration and exposure time for inducing sepsis in zebrafish embryos. Embryos at 6 h post‑fertilization (hpf) with consistent development were selected, randomly grouped, and transferred to 6‑well plates (50 embryos per well). An LPS‑induced sepsis model was used with the following groups: blank control (NC; E3 medium only), low‑dose LPS (10 μg/mL LPS in E3), medium‑dose LPS (100 μg/mL LPS), and high‑dose LPS (1000 μg/mL LPS). All groups were incubated at 28.5 °C, and fresh treatment solution was replaced every 24 h. Morphological changes were observed under a stereomicroscope, and the number of dead embryos was recorded every 6 h to calculate survival rates.

Effect of γ‑T3 on survival in the zebrafish sepsis model

Based on the above model, a γ‑T3 intervention group was added. The groups were: NC (E3 medium), LPS (10 μg/mL LPS in E3), and γ‑T3 + LPS (10 μg/mL LPS plus 10,20,40 μg/mL γ‑T3 in E3).The treatment solution was replaced every 24 h, and survival status was recorded every 6 h until no further deaths occurred in the LPS group.Survival curves were plotted for statistical analysis.

Observation of macrophages in septic zebrafish

Because macrophages were increased in sepsis non‑survivors, we examined the effect of γ‑T3 on macrophages in septic zebrafish. Embryos at 6 hpf were randomly assigned to groups (10 embryos per well, three biological replicates per group). Groups were: NC (E3 medium), LPS (10 μg/mL LPS), and γ‑T3 + LPS (10 μg/mL LPS plus 10 μg/mL γ‑T3). All groups were cultured at 28.5 °C with daily medium replacement until 72 hpf. At sampling, three zebrafish were randomly selected from each well, and fluorescence images were captured using a stereoscopic three‑dimensional fluorescence imaging system. Macrophage numbers were quantified with ImageJ software by measuring fluorescence intensity.

Detection of ROS in septic zebrafish

Macrophage ferroptosis is closely linked to ROS production. Therefore, we measured ROS levels using the same experimental groups and treatments. At 72 hpf, embryos were removed, gently washed twice with PBS, and incubated with 20 μg/mL DCFH‑DA fluorescent probe at 37 °C in the dark for 1 h. After incubation, embryos were washed three times with PBS, and fluorescence images were collected (excitation 488 nm, emission 525 nm). Average fluorescence intensity in the macrophage area was quantified with ImageJ to reflect ROS production. The experiment was repeated three times.

Mouse experiment section

Animal preparation

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Southwest Medical University (Approval No. swmu20250029) and conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines. Female C57BL/6 J mice (8–10 weeks old, 20–25 g, specific pathogen-free) were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center of Southwest Medical University (Production License: SCXK (Chuan) 2024-0046; Use License: SYXK (Chuan) 2023–0065). Mice were housed under controlled conditions (temperature 22 ± 2 °C, humidity 55 ± 5%, 12 h light/dark cycle) with free access to sterile maintenance diet and water. After one week of acclimatization, mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups.

Drug preparation

For in vivo experiments: Lipopolysaccharide (LPS, catalog No. L2880, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare a 24 mg/mL stock solution, and further diluted with sterile saline to the required working concentrations. γ‑T3 (catalog No. T19768, TargetMol, Boston, MA, USA) was dissolved in DMSO to an 80 mg/mL stock solution and diluted with saline to 4 mg/mL before administration.

Dose selection and survival study

To evaluate the protective effect of γ-T3 against LPS-induced lethality, mice were randomly divided into six groups (n = 10 per group): control (NC), LPS (30 mg/kg), and LPS + γ-T3 (20, 50, or 100 mg/kg). LPS was administered intraperitoneally (i.p.), and γ-T3 was given i.p. 2 h after LPS. The NC group received vehicle (5% DMSO in saline). Survival was monitored every 12 h for 192 h. Based on preliminary results showing a trend toward improved survival with 100 mg/kg γ-T3 (P = 0.0838), a second experiment with larger groups (n = 15 per group: NC, LPS, LPS + 100 mg/kg γ-T3) was performed.

Bioinformatics analysis section

Discussion

Sepsis remains a formidable clinical challenge in critical care medicine, with its pathophysiological complexity arising from dysregulated immune responses, compromised oxidative stress homeostasis, and progressive organ dysfunction, which collectively contribute to persistently high mortality rates [36–38]. Emerging mechanistic insights reveal the pivotal involvement of ferroptosis—an iron‑dependent, regulated cell death modality—in dictating disease progression and clinical outcomes in patients with sepsis [12]. The pathognomonic features of iron overload, manifested through uncontrolled lipid peroxidation cascades and disrupted iron metabolism, synergistically potentiate cellular injury and ultimately culminate in multi‑organ failure [39, 40]. Clinical observations consistently demonstrate that circulating iron homeostasis imbalance correlates with deteriorating clinical outcomes and heightened susceptibility to sequential organ dysfunction in patients with sepsis [5, 41].

In this study, TFRC was identified as a key ferroptosis‑related hub gene in sepsis, highlighting its central role in disease progression. RNA‑sequencing analysis revealed that TFRC was highly expressed in the sepsis group and expressed at low levels in healthy controls. Protein cohort validation showed that upregulation of TFRC expression is associated with increased sepsis riskMendelian randomization analysis indicates that upregulated TFRC expression is associated with an increased risk of 28‑day mortality in patients with sepsis. And survival analysis demonstrated that patients with high TFRC expression had lower survival rates than those with low TFRC expression. Furthermore, meta‑analysis results confirmed that TFRC expression is higher in sepsis non‑survivors and lower in survivors, further supporting its association with sepsis prognosis. TFRC (transferrin receptor) is a critical regulator of iron metabolism [42].

Single‑cell sequencing results revealed a higher proportion of monocytes in the non‑survivor sample compared with the survivor sample (descriptive observation, n = 1 per group). Cellular localization analysis demonstrated TFRC expression in monocytes and B cells. As pivotal mediators of septic immune responses, macrophages play a dual role in inflammation and tissue repair [43]. Emerging evidence indicates that exacerbated ferroptosis in macrophages is associated with multi‑organ injury in patients with sepsis, whereas pharmacological inhibition of macrophage ferroptosis alleviates such pathological damage [12, 15, 44, 45].

Our experimental results show that γ‑T3 is associated with reduced TFRC expression in macrophages (both mRNA and protein levels) and improved survival in septic zebrafish and mice, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic agent for sepsis. TFRC is a key receptor for cellular iron uptake; its overexpression leads to accumulation of intracellular ferrous iron (Fe.2⁺), which catalyzes lipid peroxidation and ROS production via the Fenton reaction. In colorectal cancer cells, high TFRC expression is directly associated with increased levels of intracellular iron, malondialdehyde (MDA), and ROS [46]. In esophageal squamous cell carcinoma cells, TFRC regulates iron metabolism through the TFRC‑STEAP3‑DMT1 signaling axis, promoting ROS accumulation [47]. Additionally, hypoxia‑inducible factor HIF‑1α can bind to the TFRC promoter and activate its transcription; silencing HIF‑1α significantly downregulates TFRC expression and reduces intracellular ROS levels [48]

We also observed that γ‑T3 reduced ROS production in septic zebrafish and decreased NLRP3 and IL‑1β expression in macrophages. Mitochondria‑derived ROS are core signaling molecules for NLRP3 inflammasome activation [49, 50]. ROS trigger inflammasome assembly and activation by promoting NLRP3 phosphorylation and binding to thioredoxin‑interacting protein (TXNIP) [50, 51]. Previous studies have shown that corilagin inhibits mitochondrial ROS production and blocks TXNIP‑NLRP3 binding, thereby suppressing NLRP3 activation and macrophage pyroptosis; the ROS agonist imiquimod reverses this effect [50]. SRT1720 reduces IL‑1β release by decreasing ROS production and inhibiting the ROS‑NLRP3 axis, an effect counteracted by the SIRT1 inhibitor EX527 [52]. Azithromycin inhibits NLRP3 component expression and IL‑1β maturation by reducing virus‑induced mitochondrial ROS and intracellular calcium levels [53]. The NLRP3 inflammasome, as an activation platform for caspase‑1, directly cleaves pro‑IL‑1β to generate mature IL‑1β [54–57]; thus, inhibiting NLRP3 activation directly reduces IL‑1β secretion. In a Cd/Mo co‑exposure model, ROS scavengers reduce NLRP3‑related factor expression and IL‑1β release, alleviating blood‑testis barrier damage [58]. Elimination of ROS using the NADPH oxidase inhibitor apocynin downregulates NLRP3 complex protein expression and reduces IL‑1β secretion [59].

γ‑T3 antagonizes oxidative stress‑related pathways. For example, it may indirectly reduce TFRC activation by upstream factors such as HIF‑1α through enhancing extracellular matrix organization, inhibiting mitochondrial dysfunction [60], or suppressing NF‑κB‑mediated inflammation [61]. By reducing ROS levels, γ‑T3 can inhibit M1 macrophage polarization [48] and alleviate tissue inflammatory damage. In a muscle atrophy model, γ‑T3 significantly restored mitochondrial function by reducing ROS and iron accumulation [60].

In summary, TFRC is a potential therapeutic target protein for ferroptosis in sepsis. This study is exploratory in nature. No prior statistical power analysis was conducted, and the sample size collected in this research is limited. The findings remain to be verified with a larger sample. γ-T3 can alleviate the upregulation of TFRC expression in macrophages induced by LPS. The potential mechanism by which γ-T3 improves the survival rate of sepsis is as follows: γ-T3 may regulate macrophage function by inhibiting the expression of TFRC in macrophages, thereby reducing the accumulation of intracellular ferrous ions (Fe2⁺). The decrease in intracellular ferrous ion levels can further reduce the production of mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS), thereby inhibiting the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome. After the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome is inhibited, the secretion of the pro-inflammatory cytokine interleukin-1β (IL-1β) is subsequently reduced, ultimately suppressing the pro-inflammatory activation of macrophages and thus improving the survival rate of sepsis.

This study is exploratory research on ferroptosis in sepsis. The overall research design focuses on verifying core scientific questions, and there is still room for further improvement. First, no systematic statistical power analysis was conducted in the early stage of this study, resulting in a relatively limited overall sample size. In particular, only a single sample was included in each group for single-cell RNA sequencing experiments, which may to some extent affect the universality and stability of the omics data. Second, direct mechanistic evidence that γ-T3 regulates macrophage ferroptosis mediated by TFRC has not been obtained. In summary, the molecular regulatory mechanisms inferred in this study are mainly correlative conclusions with a certain degree of speculation. Subsequent studies can further refine and deeply validate the conclusions of this research by expanding the sample cohort and conducting in-depth specialized mechanistic experiments. Despite the inherent limitations of exploratory research mentioned above, the existing experimental results are stable and the conclusions are reliable, possessing important theoretical value and potential clinical application value.

Relying on multi-omics analysis, this study confirmed that TFRC is a potential core target for macrophage ferroptosis in sepsis, providing a target basis and research ideas for the study of ferroptosis mechanisms in sepsis. In vitro cellular experimental results verified that γ-T3 can effectively alleviate LPS-induced inflammatory responses in macrophages and inhibit the abnormal upregulation of inflammatory pathway factors including TFRC, NLRP3, and IL-1β. In vivo animal experiments further validated that γ-T3 can effectively ameliorate abnormal macrophage migration and oxidative stress damage in zebrafish sepsis models and significantly improve the survival rate of zebrafish and mouse sepsis models. The above findings indicate that the natural active ingredient γ-T3 may exert a prominent anti-sepsis protective effect by targeting and regulating TFRC-mediated ferroptosis and inflammatory pathways. It is a promising candidate natural drug for targeted intervention of sepsis, laying a solid experimental foundation and theoretical support for the subsequent research and development of novel preventive and therapeutic drugs for sepsis as well as the formulation of clinical targeted intervention strategies.
