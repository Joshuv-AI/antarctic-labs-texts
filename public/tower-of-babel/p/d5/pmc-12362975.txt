Evolution of postgraduate medical education: lessons from history to shape the future
Authors: Gohar Yerimyan, Davit Abrahamyan, Anna Isahakyan, Gevorg Yaghjyan, Alexander Bazarchyan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnsuring professional competencies of medical specialists remains a major and ongoing concern. This study focuses on the historical evolution of personnel development in different countries, emphasizing postgraduate specialization and the educational models that emerged as a result. The study goes beyond a chronological analysis of events, seeking to identify and compare the major turning points in the postgraduate medical education (PGME) systems in different developed countries. In parallel, the events related to the formation of the postgraduate education system in Armenia were also considered, since a number of procedures related to the postgraduate education system are currently being revised in Armenia. This study also includes the overview of the Soviet educational model, since it also had a significant impact on the formation of the Armenian model. Such comparisons provide insight into the influence of these milestones on the development of modern educational models. Through a comprehensive analysis of various sources, including academic publications, reports, books, archival materials and legal documents related to the history of medicine, the study reveals significant historical trends, events and ideas in the field of medical education (ME). Models operating in different countries have undergone their own developmental journeys, during which they have been refined, improved, and shaped specific cultures and traditions. The historical analysis of PGME provided a broader understanding of the systems operating in different countries, revealing similarities, differences, and key lessons from each. Specific turning points in the different systems were identified that played a transformative role in the evolution of educational models and practices. A comparative analysis of models used in countries with developed PGME systems and the current structure of Armenian PGME system has identified key areas where revision could help shape the future of Armenian system and bring it in line with leading global educational standards.Keywords: Postgraduate medical education, Health workforce, History of medicine

Background

Many countries around the world have medical education (ME) institutions. The 2024 World Directory of Medical Schools lists 3,995 ME institutions across 186 countries [1]. The duration and content of educational programs vary across different countries, and numerous tools are used to monitor their compliance with existing standards. Although there is no universal model for the regulation of ME, examining the models employed in various countries provides an opportunity to identify innovations and regulatory approaches [2]. Educational programs cover many professional areas of undergraduate, postgraduate and continuing ME. Approaches and procedures vary even within existing postgraduate education systems for different professional fields: in some countries internships are part of the postgraduate program in surgery (e.g., Australia, Denmark, and the United Kingdom), whereas in others, they are not (e.g., Germany, Greece, and Russia) [3]. However, all systems are aimed at one goal: training qualified personnel to meet the patients and healthcare system needs. To incorporate all these processes into a cohesive framework, a number of structures, such as the World Federation for Medical Education (WFME), the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME), the Accreditation Council for Continuing Medical Education (ACCME), the General Medical Council (GMC), and the European Accreditation Council for Continuing Medical Education (EACCME), have developed standardization documents and recommendations [4–8].

The evolution of PGME models across various countries has followed distinct trajectories, and examining these developments can illuminate several issues while providing valuable insights for others to learn from these noteworthy advancements. To assess the current situation in Armenia, we studied the history of the development of PGME in several countries, including Armenia. Flexner's analysis, as presented in his reports, also served as a basis for comparing historical developments [25, 39].

In general, the developments in education included the following areas. In ancient times, informal education systems relied on storytelling, apprenticeship, and religious teachings. The invention of writing and the creation of alphabets became the first impetus for the formation of prototypes of classical educational systems. In ancient Greece and Rome, the first formal education systems were formed. The first schools were established where mathematics, philosophy and rhetoric were taught. In this period, education was a privilege reserved for specific strata of society, namely the nobility and the affluent. Access to education was denied to the poor, slaves and women, and broader accessibility was not a topic of discussion [9, 10].

Until the Middle Ages, the realm of education was largely controlled by religious institutions, with the predominance of monastic schools. This practice continued until the Renaissance, when critical thinking, literature, and the arts were emphasized. The earliest universities were formed on the basis of classical education models, thereby establishing a solid foundation for academic education [11]. The Industrial Revolution further accelerated the systemic processes of introducing mass education, driven by a considerable increase in demand for education and, consequently, the need for its accessibility. Expanding access to education, in turn, led to the establishment of universities, community colleges, and vocational training programs [12]. These processes continued to develop rapidly until the twentieth century, when the technological revolution had an impact, increasing the use of technological achievements in the field of education. Rapid developments in technology inevitably lead to their large-scale integration into the education system, which ensures access to education at the global level, with a focus on personalized learning, STEM education (STEM—science, technology, engineering, and mathematics) and lifelong learning opportunities. Advances in artificial intelligence, machine learning, and virtual reality continue to transform technology from education to healthcare.

The genealogy of terminology across different nations is also compelling, given that a number of historical events contributed to the shaping of terms in educational systems formed there. In some cases, the terms were the logical expressions of specific events, whereas in other cases they were simply borrowed. For example, in the context of PGME, the term “residency” has its roots in the Latin word “residere”, which means “to dwell” or “to stay”. In the clinical context, “residency” originally denoted the period during which a physician-in-training stayed or resided in a hospital setting for practical clinical training and education. It was first used in the United States at the beginning of the twentieth century. In the context of PGME in Russia and several other post-Soviet states, “clinical ordinatura” is the term denoting postgraduate specialization. The Russian word “ordinatura” is derived from the Latin word “ordinatus”, meaning “ordered” or “organized”.

These variations not only reflect differences in healthcare systems and educational structures, but also highlight the historical contingencies that have shaped the evolution of PGME. To investigate these developments, this study employs a qualitative historical approach, analyzing key trends, milestones, and conceptual shifts in medical education. The research draws on a broad range of sources including scholarly literature, policy reports, archival records and legal documents selected for their relevance to the historical evolution of medical training. By examining this wide range of materials, the study seeks to uncover how various social, institutional and policy-driven factors have influenced the structure and philosophy of PGME over time. By examining critical moments in the development of PGME systems, policymakers and educational institutions can better understand the factors that have driven change and use these insights to refine and strengthen current frameworks. The study thus provides both a foundation for historical reflection and a resource for guiding future reforms in medical education.

Europe

The history of PGME in Europe presents a multifaceted and diverse landscape. Some academics contend that a general analysis is the only viable approach, as some institutions possess an extensive body of work on the subject, whereas others have done little in this direction. For example, until the 1970s, medical history faculties were active in Germany and France; in a number of institutes in Spain and Italy, and in all medical schools in Switzerland, the history of medicine was on the list of compulsory subjects, whereas in the United Kingdom the history of medicine as an academic science was less developed in those years [13]. In 1993, in its report “Tomorrow’s Doctors”, the GMC advocated for the incorporation of medical history into a revised interdisciplinary curriculum [14]. Nonetheless, we were able to investigate some archival resources and publications by other authors covering the general trends in PGME in Europe.

During the Middle Ages, ME in Europe was conducted primarily through apprenticeship and informal mentoring. During the Renaissance, formal medical schools emerged in Europe, particularly in Italy, France, and Germany, but postgraduate training continued to be largely based on apprenticeships and clinical experience [15]. The formal system of ME in Europe began to take shape in the late Middle Ages, when the first universities were founded. From the eleventh to the nineteenth centuries, Europe was home to academic physicians and practically trained surgeons. Practical clinical instruction was first recognized as an essential feature of university medical training at Leyden: there, as early as 1630, the student was expected to examine the patient in the presence of the professor, who thereupon criticized his findings and opinions. The sensitiveness of the students prevented the system from taking root at that time; but early in the eighteenth century it was revived by Boerhaave, under whom the medical clinic of the University of Leyden became the most famous in the world. With Boerhaave's clinic, university instruction in clinical medicine may be said to begin. His pupils transplanted his ideas and methods into fruitful soil at Paris, Padua, Edinburgh, and Vienna [39]. Academic physicians were educated gentlemen with only theoretical knowledge and possibly some skills in making medicines. The programs for trained surgeons (a diverse group of practitioners, including barber-surgeons, ship surgeons, and tooth extractors) were exclusively practical, with varying levels of academic training. Some programs were quite rigorous and included academic components; for example, the training at the Amsterdam Guild of Surgeons lasted five years and concluded with a final examination where the trainees had to prove their practical skills in bloodletting and cranial trepanation. Here, before the practical exam, the student had to pass a theoretical exam. The functions of controlling the education of doctors were gradually taken over by the state bodies, but their primary goal was the interest of public health, not the interests of doctors. Until the late nineteenth century, governments did not care about the content or duration of ME. PGME included internships of varying duration and content [15]. Founded in 1809 under Wilhelm von Humboldt's influence, the University of Berlin tested whether medieval universities could meet modern needs. The adjustments forced by the invasion of the university by the scientific spirit touch equally the faculty, the curriculum, the teaching method, the examinations, the equipment, and the expense. Germany's rapid advancement in medicine stemmed from its integration into universities during a pivotal era of new, transformative ideals. In contrast, England and France, though scientifically productive earlier, lacked strong university ties for their leading scientists. Figures like Davy, Faraday, Darwin, Huxley, and Tyndall worked independently, receiving little recognition or support from universities. Germany's ME balances structured guidance with “Lernfreiheit”, a qualified freedom where students follow examination requirements rather than fixed courses. They must pass two exams—the “physicum” after five semesters of lectures and labs, and the “state examination” after clinical training. In both Germany and Britain, education is shaped by exams rather than curricula, but Britain’s system is regulated professionally by the General Medical Council (GMC), which supervises exams without direct control over schools. As in Germany, the curriculum is shaped by examination requirements rather than independent decisions by the schools. While British exams vary in difficulty, London University’s degree is considered the most rigorous, and the GMC plays a key role in maintaining educational standards. University budgets do not fully reflect the cost of ME, as student fees supplement or even constitute faculty salaries, with examination fees adding further income. ME is expensive, though fee waivers, delays, and scholarships provide some relief. In Britain, medical schools operate independently, with little government oversight or funding, historically relying on tuition fees and minimal endowments [39].

In 1975, the goal of creating an international market of specialists in Europe led to the introduction of the European Union Directive, which defined the nomenclature of PGME programs and their minimum durations, but it did not address the necessary competencies of the specialists [16, 17].

Despite attempts to set standards by the European Union of Medical Specialists, the requirements and content of postgraduate specialization vary significantly across European countries [18].

For example, in the Netherlands, individual time variability due to the competency-based model of ME is more the rule rather than the exception. However, to mitigate health care costs, the Dutch government, which covers most of the costs of PGME, decided to reduce the duration of the programs, bringing them closer to the minimum durations set by the European Union. That decision led the medical professional federation to implement individualized, competency-based variation via the concept of credible professional practice rather than a simple reduction in training time [19–21].

In recent decades, there has been a shift toward competency-based education in postgraduate medical training. European countries are increasingly adopting competency frameworks and assessments to assess trainees’ knowledge, skills and professional attributes. European ME also emphasizes interdisciplinary education, encouraging collaboration between different healthcare professionals. This approach aims at improving patient outcomes through teamwork and shared decision-making [22].

With the advancement of digital technologies, training programs in several European countries are increasingly incorporating electronic platforms, simulation-based learning and telemedicine. These technologies offer innovative ways of learning and skill development [23, 24].

United States of America

In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, medical practice in the United States (U.S.) was unlicensed, unorganized, and practiced by individuals with varying levels of education, from laypersons to physicians. However, the existence of classical educational units contributed to the formation of a number of committees, thanks to which many processes began to be implemented in accordance with their recommendations. The existing ME institutions were revising their admission criteria and educational programs. Gradually, an awareness of close cooperation with clinics developed. These changes inevitably had financial implications. Educational institutions were increasingly investing substantial resources in the laboratory sector. In 1910, Dr. Abraham Flexner published a report (“Flexner Report”) that critically assessed the quality of ME in the United States and Canada. The report led to significant reforms in ME, notably the standardization of medical school curricula and an emphasis on postgraduate training. At that time, postgraduate training was regarded as a “compensatory adjustment” to medical school education, aimed at bridging existing gaps in medical schools and, if properly organized, in postgraduate education [25].

Since the early twentieth century, the structure of medical care has increasingly depended on classifying physicians into distinct specialties-surgery, pediatrics, ophthalmology and so forth. In the twenty-first century, medical care became regulated, systematically organized, and primarily provided by allopathic physicians trained in a standardized set of theoretical principles. Additionally, healthcare has grown into a significant part of U.S. society, accounting for approximately 17% of the gross domestic product (GDP) a figure that is expected to keep increasing [27].

Flexner sought advice from William Welch (1850–1934) at Hopkins, who played a key leadership role in American ME. Welch’s influence reinforced Flexner’s admiration for the German ME model, already adopted at Hopkins. There, students spent two years in laboratory sciences before clinical training, with admission requiring a university education. Hopkins became Flexner’s gold standard for evaluating medical schools, as its principles of excellence had already been established by Welch and his colleagues a decade earlier [37].

Sir William Osler (1849–1919) was the first to establish a postgraduate residency at Johns Hopkins Hospital. His proposed residency model had a pyramid structure: many interns, fewer residents, and at the top of the pyramid a chief resident who could stay at that hospital for up to seven or eight years. Thus, in this model, the medical staff of the teaching hospital consisted of physicians in training [15]. Osler played a key role in advancing Flexner’s mission by helping establish the Interurban Clinical Club in 1905. This organization fostered idea exchange and camaraderie among medical professors at top Eastern schools. It aligned with Flexner’s vision by promoting scientific disease investigation and improving teaching methods, significantly shaping the scientific foundation of American medicine. [37]

In the early twentieth century, as medical practice began to be coordinated, postgraduate training, specifically the internship—a year-long program of increasing responsibilities—became more widespread. In this period, one could meet several types of internship: “rotating” (specialists rotated in different departments of the hospital), “straight” (specialists trained in the same department of the hospital) and “mix”. After a short period of time, internment became standard, and in 1914, the capacity of hospitals was sufficient for only 50% of medical graduates to complete internship. In the 1930s, the internship became mandatory for everyone. Specialists were also called “house staff” because most of the hospitals were converted houses. Doctor Osler saw the main mission of these “home residents” in serving the sick. The residency model was one option to prepare for better delivery of these services, but other models could be considered. It was deemed reasonable for a specialist to have a basic ME, subsequently allowing them to develop their skills in any professional domain under the supervision of a specialist in a postgraduate training institution. Prior to the 1930s, these processes were optional, but in the decades that followed, they became mandatory. The formation of the American Board of Medical Specialties in 1933 was another significant step toward the formalization of the PGME. It was created to oversee the practice standards of medical professions in the United States. Pioneers of this process were representatives of professional councils of ophthalmology, dermatology, obstetrics and gynecology, and otolaryngology. They jointly initiated the processes of building a national system of standards and the recognition of specialists [26].

Clinical hospitals began to be formed where the internship was carried out. Before the First World War, the presence of “residents” was considered a luxury for the hospital, but this image changed dramatically after the Second World War, when the presence of “residents” was essential for the hospital. This was followed by a re-evaluation of the role of residents, when they ceased being viewed as hospital trainees and began to be viewed as cheap labor performing essential work for the medical organization. Residents provided medical services in exchange for acquiring skills and engaging in scientific work. There were no formal national standards on work hours and residents frequently worked over 100 h per week, often without adequate supervision. The unfortunate passing of an 18-year-old female patient at a New York hospital in 1984 led to modifications in the working hours of medical residents. In 1989, New York State implemented regulations limiting resident work hours to 80 per week, with no more than 30 consecutive hours of work and mandated 24-h supervision [27, 41].

Professional associations and councils, accrediting bodies, and legislators used their power to influence the length of postgraduate education programs to their advantage. Powers to regulate residency duration, content and qualifications were transferred in the 1980s to the established powerful national body, ACGME. The ACGME, in collaboration with the Royal College of Physicians and Surgeons of Canada, pioneered the competency-based postgraduate education initiative in the early twenty-first century, highlighting the variability present in the duration of clinical experience within the discipline. In 2000, Dr. Long recommended, and Drs. Caraccio, Keith, and others echoed that the existing approach to resident education with a fixed number of years of specialization should be replaced with a competency-based approach. This new approach would allow residents to continue their specialization until they demonstrate proficiency in the required knowledge and skills, and are able to apply them independently. According to the authors, the flexibility debate between resident work hours and program hours restrictions is likely to be vital in the coming years [15, 28]. In 2003, the ACGME also set duty hours regulations to avoid resident fatigue and patient safety issues. These regulations govern resident work hours in accredited programs [29].

In the twenty-first century, more emphasis has been placed on interprofessional education, which encourages collaboration between different healthcare professionals. This approach aims to improve patient care outcomes through teamwork and communication. During this period, technological solutions in both ME and healthcare and medical care increased and became more widespread [30–33]. Current reforms in ME are restoring the central role of service in the doctor–patient relationship. While the Flexner model remains the foundation of American medicine, these changes emphasize its core principles.

Soviet Union

Healthcare activities in prerevolutionary Russia were also not coordinated. In those years, 8,600 students studied at medical faculties, and the annual number of graduates was 1,000. The curriculum of the Faculty of Medicine, approved back in 1884, provided for the training of such a doctor whose activities were limited to the therapeutic function (healer), while preventive and practical skills were of secondary importance. Established in 1885 in St. Petersburg, the Clinical Institute of the Grand Duchess Elena Pavlovna was the first Institute of Doctors’ Improvement in Russia. Over its 32-year history, the Institute had qualified 9,906 doctors, yet its capacity had not met the existing demand. In 1914, the number of physicians was 23,143, and the mid-level practitioner staff was 50,000, whereas the population they served was approximately 153 million. More than half of those doctors were in private practice. The establishment of the People’s Commissariat of Health in Soviet Russia in 1918 was a prerequisite for the formation of a healthcare system that prioritized preventive medicine as its core function. Over the next two years, a number of divisions dedicated to resolving concerns related to health, sanitation, and occupational hygiene of the working class were created. In 1924, the state budget was responsible for the predominant share of expenditures necessary for sustaining the rural healthcare network. The number of scientific research institutes was also actively increasing with the expansion of the scope of their activities. During this period, 16 new medical faculties were opened. The growing demands of medical care and services, coupled with the systemic activities of the health sector, necessitated new conditions for personnel training. A total of 13 new medical universities were opened from 1930 to 1934. In the other countries of the Soviet Union, the process of establishing medical training institutes started in 1931 in Minsk (Belarus), in 1932 in Tashkent (Uzbekistan), and in 1935 in Tbilisi (Georgia) and Baku (Azerbaijan). To facilitate the formation and allocation of medical personnel, relevant bodies of the Communist Party were also established, notably the Personnel Department and republican, regional and district party organizations assigning personnel. These developments continued until the Second World War, when all healthcare efforts were directed towards wartime needs. In 1946, the Council of People’s Commissars of the Soviet Union acknowledged the substantial contribution of Soviet medicine to the victory of the Red Army. More than 35,000 Soviet medical workers were awarded medals and orders. During the war, the Soviet Union already ranked first in the world in terms of the number of medical personnel and indicators of personnel availability. After resolving the challenges associated with the provision of healthcare personnel and the end of the war, the widespread enhancement of service quality was prioritized. This shift in focus led to an increased emphasis on the specialization and improvement of health workers. In 1991, after the collapse of the Soviet Union, its Ministry of Health was transformed into the Ministry of Health of the Russian Federation, which assumed responsibility for the development of health policy and regulation of the field in Russia, including ME and postgraduate training. Russia continues to modernize its healthcare system with ongoing efforts to improve ME and postgraduate training, including the introduction of competency-based education and the integration of digital technologies into training programs.

Armenia

Armenian medicine has a history of approximately three millennia. Having emerged in ancient times, it created a unique amalgamation of theoretical and practical medical knowledge. Over the centuries, this body of knowledge has interacted with the cultures of different civilizations; enriching itself while steadily retaining its unique identity. Esteemed Armenian scientists, including G. Inchichyan, A. Soukryan, V. Torgomyan, R. Parsadanian, and L. Hovhannisyan, have contributed to the study of the history Armenian medicine, as have distinguished foreign historians such as Y. Karst, K. Neyman, L. Shulan, E. Legren, and H. Virkhov, among others. Furthermore, these studies explored the history of the scientific heritage of institutions, hospitals, pharmacies, medical schools and individual doctors operating in the Armenian Highlands, as well as in Armenian communities and the diaspora [35].

The period known as the Armenian medieval renaissance (tenth–fourteenth centuries) also marked notable advancements in the field of medical science. This era saw the establishment of medieval academies that provided education in medicine alongside various other disciplines. Moreover, the great teachers of the time also highly valued practical knowledge. In particular, Hovhannes Sarkavag (1045–1129, literally translated from Armenian as “John the Deacon”, also known as Hovhannes Imastaser—literally translated from Armenian as “John the Wisdom-lover”) wrote: “Knowledge cannot be accurate without experience.” Historical evidence confirms the existence of a healthcare system that formed and evolved in the city of Ani, the capital of Armenia at that time. The established healthcare system, coupled with the rich repository of literature also allowed for the education of doctors and the transfer of knowledge across generations through apprenticeships with renowned doctors in monastic academies. Notably, the writings of many Armenian doctors contain not only medical texts, but also descriptions and definitions of the image of a doctor—a specialist endowed with a deep sense of professional duty and high moral standards [35].

During the fifteenth to eighteenth centuries, Armenia experienced a series of rather unfavorable political events, which inevitably left their mark on the cultural life. However, it was at the beginning of this period that the comprehensive works of Amirdovlat Amasiatsi brought quite serious vitality to the development of medical literature, significant impacting the thinking of doctors of subsequent generations. Thereafter, the pace of development experienced a relative decline until the seventeenth century, although the printing of medical literature continued, witnessing a resurgence at the beginning of the eighteenth century. An important circumstance in the history of Armenian medicine was the study of a number of Armenian doctors at European medical universities at the end of the eighteenth century. The medical writings of many among them were already crafted in the spirit of European medicine. In general, during this period, the development of Armenian medicine and medical thought progressed beyond the borders of Armenia, in Armenian communities abroad [36]. These developments continued until the period of Soviet Armenia (1920). The advancements taking place within the Soviet Union were reflected in the union republics. It was the period of establishment of formal ME in Armenia. A school for training medical personnel was established in Yerevan in 1921. In 1922, to ensure a structured educational process, the Yerevan Medical School was transformed into the Faculty of Medicine of Yerevan State University. In 1927, the faculty graduated its first 32 students. In 1930, independent institutes were established based on the university's faculties, including the State Medical Institute.

Additionally, the Yerevan State Institute for Postgraduate training of Medical Professionals was established in Soviet Armenia in 1963 and implemented many postgraduate educational and research programs for physicians. In a fairly short period of time, the institute became a training center of all-Union importance, attracting numerous medical practitioners and professors from across the Soviet Union to engage in the exchange of expertise. This mechanism of specialization and enhancement of doctors’ professionalism was actively functioning and undergoing continuous refinement until the fall of the Soviet Union [38].

After regaining its independence in 1991, the Republic of Armenia continued the efforts to establish the PGME system. A list of medical specialties was introduced, according to which doctors could specialize during their clinical residency. Postgraduate specialization in clinical residency became a prerequisite for physicians wishing to engage in independent professional activities. Owing to its structure, content of educational programs and organizational format, clinical residency was more in line with the Soviet PGME model. The basis for these developments was the relevant laws and legal acts developed during that time. However, these new legal regulations introduced residency on a paid basis. Under this system, the resident is considered a student under the supervision of a physician rather than an employee of the clinic, as was the case in the Soviet PGME model. Nevertheless, current medical education in Armenia does not impose restrictions on the number of medical residents. Each graduate of a medical university can pursue PGME on a fee-based basis by passing the entrance exams. There are no requirements regarding the number of clinical cases or mandatory interventions performed by residents during their training, raising concerns about their clinical competencies. The significant increase in the number of students has always been a key focus of Flexner's studies. According to Flexner, professors could deliver academic lectures to large student groups, but the same approach was not feasible for clinical skills training [39]. Quality assessment tools in the PGME system of Armenia are limited to internal assessment mechanisms and there is a lack of external quality monitoring mechanisms. Meanwhile, based on European and U.S. models, residents are considered employees of their teaching hospital and receive a salary during their residency. The clinical resident in Armenia is not an employee of the teaching hospital and is not paid for his clinical work.

Discussion

This historical perspective allowed examining not only the evolution of medical practice, but also parallel developments and advances in history, education, and technology (Fig. 1). Historical analysis shows that PGME has deep historical roots, yet the most vigorous efforts toward its coordination, documentation and standardization took place in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. In all the studied countries, the initiatives launched by the professional community acted as the driving force behind these processes, which progressively developed and refined, transforming into a defined and mandatory requirement. At present, the processes aimed at upgrading and modifying this system are still in progress.

Education and technology development timelines

The emphasis on competency-based learning, interprofessional education, the application of digital tools, and the tailoring of education to align with the needs of the population and the requirements of health systems is becoming more pronounced. Furthermore, the well-being of the healthcare workforce, including mental health and ensuring optimal working conditions, remains a focal point of consideration. Our research indicates that prior to the nineteenth century, the regulations governing the working conditions of medical workers were still in an embryonic state and often adhered to widely accepted general norms. Professional bodies have been pivotal in overseeing these processes, which has allowed concerns to be voiced and fostered the establishment of regulatory mechanisms for the sector and the initiation of their legal regulations. By studying and analyzing historical sources and diverse documents, we were able to examine medical practices through a historical lens, pinpointing key events that shaped the PGME system in multiple countries.

Now Armenia is in the midst of revising its PGME system, making this analysis particularly relevant for Armenia. Moreover, these findings may also offer valuable insights for other countries currently revising their PGME systems, aiding in the effective implementation of necessary reforms.

The development of PGME in Armenia underwent significant transformations during two key historical periods. The first pivotal phase occurred during Soviet Armenia (Second Armenian Republic), when the foundation for structured PGME was established. A notable milestone was the establishment of Yerevan State Institute for Postgraduate training of Medical Professionals in 1963, which introduced postgraduate improvement and specialization programs for medical practitioners. This marked the beginning of a stable and formalized approach to PGME in the country. Systemic approaches during this period are also reflected in the number of medical graduates, which in Soviet Armenia remained relatively stable, ranging from 1 to 1.2 per 10,000 population [40].

The second transformative period coincided with the independence of Armenia and the establishment of the Third Republic in 1991. This era was characterized by the drafting and adoption of numerous legal frameworks, including laws and by-laws, many of which continue to govern the field of PGME today. These regulations laid the groundwork for adapting the education system to the needs of an independent republic while ensuring compliance with emerging global standards. It was during this period that fluctuations in the number of medical graduates began, with the figure reaching 2.6 per 10,000 population in 2016. These trends have continued to persist [40].

In contrast to developments in Europe and the United States, where the medical community and educators played a central role in shaping PGME, Armenia's approach has historically relied on legal and governmental oversight. In Western contexts, active contributions from medical professionals led to the creation of international organizations dedicated to regulating and improving PGME systems. These bodies facilitated continuous analysis and refinement of educational practices. In Soviet Armenia and the Third Republic, however, the regulation of postgraduate education has been predominantly achieved through comprehensive legal frameworks, with legislative bodies playing a leading role. While prominent specialists in the field contribute to the development of these legislative initiatives, Armenia lacks dedicated organizations composed of medical professionals tasked with ongoing evaluation and improvement of PGME. The absence of such bodies may limit the agility and adaptability of the system to meet the evolving demands of medical practice.

Today, the process of specialization of healthcare personnel in Armenia is carried out in two ways: within the framework of the state target program and on a paid basis. The state target program reflects the Soviet model, wherein individuals who specialize at the expense of the state receive a scholarship during their studies, with the obligation to work for three years under the appointment of the Ministry of Health. In contrast, students in the paid system receive their education with their own funds.

Our comparative analysis reveals significant differences between the components of PGME systems in leading systems and in Armenia. The comparative analysis was based on the history of ME and its developments in various countries, along with key findings from the Flexner Report and his proposed model of PGME. We identified and compared four main components: the selection of specialists, funding, educational programs, and quality control. These components involve fundamentally different approaches to planning and implementation. Conclusions

In this study, we investigated the developments of the PGME systems including Armenia. This historical review also allowed us to observe that PGME systems across different countries faced similar challenges at various stages of their development (such as growth number of students, imperfection of regulations on different stages of ME, curriculum), which served as impetus for their advancement. PGME, like other aspects of healthcare systems, has been significantly shaped by historical developments, often evolving in response to societal and systemic changes. These historical transformations created a growing demand for the development and enhancement of health human resources. In earlier periods, physicians trained primarily through apprenticeships, which sufficed to meet the limited demand for medical professionals in times when healthcare access was sparse, and structured healthcare systems were absent. However, the advent of the Industrial Revolution, coupled with advancements in science, catalyzed the establishment of coordinated healthcare and educational systems. These changes led to the creation of medical universities, which provided a formal and standardized approach to medical training. The emergence of organized healthcare systems introduced healthcare indicators which underscored the necessity of advancing the knowledge and skills of medical practitioners. This realization marked the inception of PGME systems, aimed at addressing the growing complexities of medical practice. In the current era, the digital revolution continues to reshape healthcare and education. The expanded capacity for data analysis not only enhances understanding of healthcare trends, but also transforms educational strategies for training medical professionals. Digital tools enable more adaptive, personalized, and evidence-based approaches to PGME. These observations highlight the importance of informed and targeted recommendations for policymakers and institutions aiming to reform or enhance their PGME systems. By critically analyzing historical milestones in their PGME systems, countries can identify key turning points that shaped their development and leverage these insights to optimize existing frameworks.
