Profiles and outcomes of untreated people with HIV in French Guiana: progress towards 95-95-95 targets
Authors: Mathieu Nacher, Aude Lucarelli, Fanja Vergeade, Sebastien Rabier, Loïc Epelboin, Philippe Abboud, Paul Le Turnier, Myriam El Guedj, Félix Djossou, Antoine Adenis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough treatment is recommended for all people living with HIV, not all individuals in the French Guiana cohort were receiving antiretroviral therapy (ART). We aimed to describe trends and characteristics of people living with HIV who were not receiving ART in French Guiana.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study evaluating the most recent treatment status and associated variables.ResultsBetween 2016 and 2023, there were 246 (6.1%) persons who had stopped ART at last news, 527 (13%) who were ART naive, and 3283 (80.9%) who were on ART. Over time, the proportion of treated individuals increased (+ 8% per year after 2010), while the proportions of ART-naive individuals (-3% per year) and treatment interruptions (-2% per year since 2017) declined. ART-naive individuals were less likely to report drug use (0.4%) compared with those on treatment (3.6%) or those who had stopped treatment (8.5%, P < 0.0001). The viral load among those not receiving treatment gradually declined. Persons having stopped treatment were older and more immunosuppressed. Remaining ART-naive persons were younger and had higher CD4 counts. Individuals who were ART-naive or who had interrupted treatment had the highest mortality.ConclusionsThe proportion of untreated persons has been gradually decreasing towards the target of 95% on treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13690-026-01963-7.

Background

French Guiana is a French territory in South America and has the highest HIV incidence and prevalence of all French territories [1]. For more than three decades, HIV prevalence has remained above 1%, and HIV has been a major cause of premature mortality [2, 3]. Drivers of the epidemic include multiple sexual partnerships and transactional heterosexual sex, occurring in a context of significant social vulnerability and high migration from HIV-endemic countries [4–7]. In this context, HIV prevalence among pregnant women has exceeded 1% since the 1990s, and the sex ratio has remained balanced between men and women. Another feature of the epidemic is that about three-quarters of people living with HIV are foreign-born. The HIV regional strategic plans emphasized primary prevention, HIV testing once a year, efforts to increase treatment adherence. Several non-governmental organizations deployed community health workers to support implementation of the strategic plan. Intensive media campaigns on HIV transmission and the importance of testing complemented field-based outreach efforts. We have recently shown that with continuous efforts to improve the cascade of care all indicators have been improving in French Guiana: there has been a decreasing trend for diagnostic delays, incidence and mortality have dropped, and the proportion of undiagnosed people living with HIV is shrinking fast [8]. These improvements correspond to the expansion of antiretroviral treatment (ART) eligibility to all people living with HIV [9]. Treatment as prevention likely played a major role in reducing transmission, although reduced diagnostic delays and the introduction of pre-exposure prophylaxis in vulnerable groups may also have contributed. We have estimated the cascade of care to be at 95% of people living with HIV being diagnosed according to the ECDC modelling tool, 92% of diagnosed people on ART, and 94% of treated people virologically suppressed, which seems close to UNAIDS’ target of 95 × 95 × 95 [8, 10]. However, given the circa 4000 people living with HIV in French Guiana, this suggests that 732 (200 not diagnosed + 304 not on treatment + 228 not virologically controlled) persons are at risk of disease progression and can still transmit the virus to their sexual partners, 532 of these persons are aware of their infection. These estimates suggest that further reductions in transmission should focus on optimizing care and treatment among these individuals. First, it requires sustaining the intense testing efforts [11, 12], and secondly, it requires maximizing the proportion of people with HIV on effective ART. While recommendations aim to treat all people living with HIV, it was not clear why 8% of French Guiana’s cohort were not receiving combination antiretroviral therapy, a question that deserves further investigation. Our main hypothesis is that, for a long time, clinicians preferred waiting until the patients were psychologically “ready” for treatment and, as long as CD4 counts were high, they could wait to initiate treatment. Despite changes in recommendations, we thus assume that among prescribers the switch for a more proactive therapeutic attitude was in fact gradual. In this perspective, our primary research objective was to describe the trends and characteristic profiles of people living with HIV who are not receiving antiretroviral therapy. Specifically, we aimed to analyze both those who remain ART-naïve and those who have interrupted treatment, identifying the clinical and demographic factors associated with each group to better understand the remaining hurdles to achieving the 95-95-95 targets.This retrospective hospital cohort study ultimately aimed to provide insights to optimize the cascade of care, notably the proportion of diagnosed people receiving ART.

Methods

HIV care in French Guiana

French Guiana is a French territory and benefits from a universal healthcare system, with insurance covering all diagnostic procedures and ART for all people living with HIV. Individuals without residence permits may obtain temporary residency for medical reasons if they have HIV and lack access to appropriate care in their country of origin. Outpatient care is mostly given in the 3 hospitals of Cayenne, Kourou, and Saint Laurent du Maroni. More than 200 patients are followed in remote health centers and are not included in the present hospital cohort. Similarly 1 in 6 patients is followed by a private practitioner and thus these are also not included in the DAT’AIDS hospital cohort [13].

Data source

Data from the retrospective, anonymized DAT’AIDS hospital cohort were aggregated into annual statistics spanning 1992–2023. There were no exclusion criteria all patients in the cohort were analyzed. The timing of assessments was by year. Given the aggregation most variables studied were available. Missing data were not imputed. For drug or alcohol use, individuals without documented reports were considered non-users. Because ART indications changed since the beginning of the epidemic the proportions on treatment also evolved. Because WHO guidelines expanded ART eligibility to all patients in 2016, we focused particularly on this period, when all people living with HIV were theoretically eligible for treatment.

Variables studied

Variables included treatment status (on ART, ART-naive, or having stopped treatment), age, sex, nationality, documented alcohol or drug use, CD4 count at diagnosis, CD4 nadir, most recent CD4 count, and last known HIV-1 viral load. The patient situation is a key variable that is carefully filled by trained clinical research technicians. Because treatment initiation is legally restricted to hospital settings, and all HIV clinics operate within hospitals, verifying whether a patient ever received ART was straightforward using documented treatment history. Furthermore, this can also be extracted from the medical observation as physician usually report such important information about the patient’s history. Whether they interrupted ART can also easily be verified from the electronic medical records both in the section regarding treatment details and from the medical observation where the physician also usually report this major information about patient care. For those lost to follow-up, the absence of records cross-matched with the outpatient’s planning records allows to confirm that the patient was not seen. The treatment status was cross-tabulated with vital status, being followed somewhere, or being lost to follow-up.

Statistical analysis

We calculated frequencies and proportions for categorical variables, and annual median values for continuous variables to visualize temporal trends. The average annual rate of change for treatment naïve, on treatment, and off treatment was computed. We used chi square tests for crosstabulations and t-tests to compare quantitative variables. We hypothesized that different nationalities may have different likelihoods to be on treatment (French Guianese being more afraid to be recognized at the hospital thus avoiding care, foreigners being poorer and having administrative difficulties, or being discriminated against could also have reasons to interrupt follow up). Multinomial regression was thus used to test whether different nationalities had different probabilities to be on treatment. Sensitivity analyses used logistic regressions models for naïve vs. treated and interrupted treatment versus treated, they also excluded those with non-specified country of origin, and finally restricted the analysis to after 2015; finally, bootstrapping with 500 replications was used to check the stability of the model estimates. Linear regression was used to compare quantitative variables between groups when qualitative variables had more than 2 categories, with a significance level at 5%. 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) for the median of CD4 counts and viral loads were obtained using non-parametric bootstrap resampling with 5,000 iterations. Kruskal-Wallis tests were used to compare the 3 groups.

Ethics and regulatory aspects

Anonymized patient data are included in the DAT’AIDS cohort, which is shared with the French Hospital Database on HIV. The Dat’AIDS cohort has received regulatory approval by the Commission Nationale Informatique et Libertés (CNIL 2001/762876). As required by law all persons included in the cohort gave written consent.

Results

Between 1992 and 2024, 6,531 patients were included in the database, of whom 596 had stopped ART at their last recorded visit, 1740 were ART-naive upon last visit, and 4195 were on ART at the last visit. With expanded treatment eligibility and an increasing therapeutic arsenal, these proportions changed gradually, with a steadily increasing proportion of individuals receiving ART (Fig. 1). The mean annual decline in ART-naive individuals was approximately 3% per year (P < 0.0001). Between 2017 and 2023, the proportion of individuals interrupting ART decreased by 12.2 absolute percentage points (from 17.2% to 5.0%), which corresponds to an average annual absolute reduction of approximately 2.0% and a relative annual decline of approximately 24%. The annual proportion of patients on ART increased by 16% overall, although a plateau was observed between 1999 and 2010, there was an 8% annual increase after 2010 (P < 0.0001).

The general progress was temporarily reversed in 2017 during the blockade of cities and 10-week hospital strike which hampered access to care (Fig. 1).

Figure 2 shows that the evolution of the duration between HIV diagnosis and ART gradually declined notably since 2014, and continued to decline. The median time between HIV diagnosis and ART initiation decreased from approximately 6 years in 1996 to less than 0.2 year in 2023, corresponding to a mean proportional decline of ~ 14.5% per year, with the most rapid reduction occurring after universal test-and-treat recommendations in 2015. Calendar year explained 79% of the observed variance (R² = 0.786), indicating a substantial acceleration in treatment initiation over time, the slope of this decline was highly significant (P < 0.0001). Between 1996 and 2005 there was a 58% reduction in treatment delay after diagnosis, between 2006 and 2015 there was an 80% reduction in treatment delays and between 2016 and 2023 another 80% reduction in treatment delay.

When zooming on the period 2016 and beyond, when antiretroviral therapy was in theory recommended for all, there were 246 (6.1%) persons who had stopped ART at last news, 527 (13%) who were antiretroviral naive, and 3283 (80.9%) who were on ART. ART-naive individuals had higher CD4 counts at diagnosis and higher CD4 nadirs and were younger than individuals in the other treatment groups. Those having stopped ART on the contrary had lower CD4 counts at diagnosis, at nadir, and were older than the other groups. Among the 773 persons (19.1%) not on ART Fig. 3 shows that nationality was not a factor of discrimination, with French nationals being most likely to be ART-naive or having stopped treatment.

Multinomial regression, adjusting for age, CD4 count, period, found that Haitians were less likely to have stopped treatment than French nationals (prevalence rate ratio = 0.5 (95%CI = 0.3–0.8)), there was no significant difference for ART-naive. Different sensitivity analyses did not change this finding.

Since 2016, ART-naive individuals were less likely to have reported drug problems (0.4%) than those on treatment (3.6%) or those having stopped ART (8.5%, P < 0.0001), the most likely to have such problems. For those for whom alcohol consumption was documented (N = 605), alcohol was more common among those who stopped ARTs (11% vs. 5.4%, P = 0.04).

When comparing ART-naive patients, patients who had stopped ART, and those on ART Fig. 4 shows that those who had stopped ART tended to have lower median initial CD4 counts, lower median CD4 nadirs, and lower last median CD4 counts. Overall, at treatment initiation, median first CD4 counts were 256 (95% CI = 221–291) in the stopped-ART group, 302 (95% CI = 289–314) among those on ART, and 362 (95% CI = 331–393) in ART-naïve individuals. This difference was statistically significant P < 0.0001. At nadir, median CD4 counts were lowest among individuals who had stopped ART (97 cells/mm³; 95% CI = 78–115), followed by those on ART (205; 95% CI = 196–214) and ART-naïve participants (281; 95% CI = 263–299). This difference was statistically significant P < 0.0001. At the last measurement, median CD4 counts increased to 222 (95% CI = 182–261) for those who had stopped ART, 511 (95% CI = 499–523) among participants on ART, and 270 (95% CI = 245–296) in the ART-naïve group. This difference was also statistically significant P < 0.0001.

In contrast, Fig. 4 shows that ART-naive tended to have greater median initial, nadir, and last CD4 counts. Kruskall Wallis tests confirmed this (P < 0.0001) for the initial CD4 count, nadir, and last CD4 count: for ART-naïve, the median initial CD4 count was 362 (IQR = 134–597), the median nadir was 281 (IQR = 86–481), and the median last CD4 count was 270 (IQR = 62–527]; for ART-treated, the median initial CD4 count was 301 (IQR = 130–480), the median nadir was 205 (IQR = 69–310), and the median last CD4 count was 511 (IQR = 296–733]; for ART-interrupted, the median initial CD4 count was 256 (IQR = 99–500), the median nadir was 97 (IQR = 22–227), and the median last CD4 count was 221 (IQR = 66–477]. The ART-naive persons were significantly younger (mean = 41.8 years (SD = 14), p<0.0001) than those on treatment (mean = 45.4 years (SD = 14)) and those having stopped treatment were older (mean = 47 (SD = 15)) than those on treatment (p = 0.01). By contrast, the age at the time of diagnosis was youngest among those on ART (mean = 35 years (SD = 12) vs. ART-naive (mean = 37.5 years (SD = 13), p < 0.0001) and those having stopped ART (mean = 38.3 years (SD = 14), p < 0.0001).

For the most recent HIV-1 viral load, ART-naive individuals had higher median values (median = 1895 copies/ml (first quartile = 20 third quartile = 75356, p < 0.0001) than those on treatment (median < 20 copies third quartile = 62) or those having stopped ART (median = 230 first quartile = 20 third quartile = 90297, p < 0.0001). Supplementary Fig. 1 shows that overtime the viral load of those not receiving ART (both naive or having stopped treatment) has declined.

Relation with AIDS and deaths among person living with HIV

Since 2016 among those having stopped ART half were at the AIDS stage (136/270), nearly a quarter of those on ART (815/3371), and only 24/527 (4.5%) of the ART-naive were at the AIDS stage.

Since 2016 there were 217 deaths of persons living with HIV. Among persons that had stopped ART 183/270 (67.8%), among those on ART 2/3371 (0.06%) had died, and 32/527 ART-naïve (6%) had died. Overall, 160 of 194 deaths (82.5%) since 2016 occurred among individuals who had discontinued ART, and 32/194 (16.5%) of those who died were persons who were ART-naive, with only 2/194 (1%) of persons on ART dying.

Since 2016 among those having stopped ART, 20/246 (8.1%) had interrupted follow-up; among ART-naive persons 55/527 (10.4%) had interrupted follow-up.

Discussion

Here we show that the proportion of persons not receiving ART has gradually declined over time. We also show clear differences between ART-naive individuals and those who discontinued treatment: ART-naive individuals were younger and had higher CD4 counts but higher viral loads. Among persons who stopped ART we found substantially more reports of alcohol or drug addiction than those on treatment or ART-naive. We also show that most deaths of persons living with HIV occurred among those who had stopped treatment, but we also show that 6% of ART-naive persons died, which emphasizes that delaying ART has a cost. The strikingly high mortality observed among individuals who discontinued ART likely reflects advanced disease progression following treatment interruption. Indeed, half of these individuals were at the AIDS stage at their last visit, and they exhibited significantly lower median CD4 counts at diagnosis (256 cells/mm³) and lower CD4 nadirs (97 cells/mm³) compared to both the ART-naïve and treated groups. This suggests that those who stop treatment are often those with the highest degree of immunosuppression and clinical vulnerability, potentially compounded by social barriers or comorbidities that led to the interruption in the first place.

The reasons why some patients remained ART-naive may vary, some physicians may have wished to wait for the patient to be ready or to solve housing and health insurance problems to start in better conditions to achieve optimal adherence. However, we have shown that not receiving treatment was associated with follow-up interruption in French Guiana [14], and here again, over 10% of the ART-naive were lost to follow-up, which may lead to immune suppression and its potentially fatal complications. However, over time the median viral load among the ART-naive and off treatment persons seemed to decline and median CD4 counts tended to increase. Furthermore, the delay between HIV diagnosis and antiretroviral initiation has constantly decreased. Overall, these gradual changes converge towards a situation where the proportion of persons not on antiretroviral today has been minimalized. The right time to start for prescribers may have gradually shifted from “give the patient enough time” to “start as soon as possible”.

These observations are partly consistent with the few studies that have focused on patients not receiving treatment. The link of treatment interruption with addiction was not very surprising and was found elsewhere [15]. However, whereas some studies found that lower incomes, females, and aboriginal or migrant populations were less likely to have received ART [16, 17], we did not find evidence for such differences in our hospital cohort. In fact, the opposite was observed for Haitian immigrants who were less likely to have interrupted ART than French nationals, a finding that refutes common preconceptions about treatment adherence in this population.

Among the study limitations we emphasize that the variables that were studied only give a coarse view of the patients. Although the gradual improvement of ARV coverage is plausible, the descriptive analysis does not allow to establish causality; time-varying confounders such as psychosocial stress, healthcare access, and socioeconomic status are important but are invisible in the aggregated data; furthermore, key biases, such as selection, misclassification, and survivor biases may impact the results. Qualitative data on patient experience and motivations for not being on treatment, or for the physician’s reasoning for not treating the patient were not available. There have been reports of challenges in optimizing the cascade of care in rural areas and French Guiana also faces these challenges [11, 12]. The usual barriers in rural areas are lack of provider expertise, transportation barriers, inadequate support services, and stigma. Our cohort did not allow us to discriminate between rural and urban residents. Furthermore, patients from the most rural areas and are followed in remote health centers are not all included in the hospital cohort. The data represents a snapshot of the last known situation of patients. However, some of the patients last on treatment may have since then stopped treatment or for those not on treatment may have initiated or restarted treatment elsewhere—i.e. with their private practitioner [13]. Our information systems are blind to ART-naive persons living with HIV who are not followed at the hospital, or whether treatment has been interrupted; thus we cannot rule out the fact that they are different from the majority of patients followed in the hospital and that the above trends may not necessarily apply. Finally, in a territory where personnel turnover is frequent, the data exhaustiveness may have fluctuated when research technicians’ positions were vacant or when physicians changed, notably in Western French Guiana. Despite these limitations the present study describes the situation for over 80% of the outpatient cohort and shows clear evolving trends that are instructive for the evolution of the cascade of care.

In conclusion, the data show that ART-naive persons were younger, had greater CD4 count, and were less likely to have alcohol or drug addiction problems; by contrast, those having stopped treatment were older and more likely to have alcohol or drug addiction problems, and had lower CD4 counts than other groups. Although treatment is recommended for all since 2016, a number of patients are not receiving treatment. However, their proportion has been gradually decreasing towards the target of 95% on treatment. Furthermore, the viral load among those not receiving treatment gradually declined. We hypothesize that this suggests that over time clinicians became less likely to withhold ART in persons with viral replication. The data also shows that deaths of persons living with HIV mainly concerned persons not receiving ART, including ART-naive persons emphasizing the importance of expanding the proportion of persons on treatment to further reduce complications and transmission.
