Revisiting the cognitive potential of lithium in alzheimer’s disease: the role of cholinergic system and brain connectivity
Authors: Lorenzo Pini, Bruno Pietro Imbimbo, Nunzio Pomara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent evidence demonstrating a marked reduction in endogenous brain lithium levels in mild cognitive impairment and Alzheimer’s disease (AD) has renewed interest in lithium as a physiological modulator of brain aging and neurodegeneration. Experimental data indicate that lithium deficiency accelerates amyloid-β deposition, tau pathology, synaptic and myelin loss, whereas physiological lithium replacement mitigates neuropathology and cognitive decline. Here, we argue that lithium’s cognitive potential in AD has been underestimated due to the historical neglect of two interrelated mechanisms: modulation of the cholinergic system and preservation of brain connectivity. Lithium has long been shown to inhibit cholinesterase activity and to increase choline and glycine availability, suggesting a capacity to enhance central cholinergic tone. Given the established role of cholinergic dysfunction in AD, this mechanism may represent an unrecognized contributor to lithium’s neurocognitive effects. In parallel, converging human neuroimaging evidence indicates that lithium modulates functional brain networks and is associated with improved white matter integrity, supporting a role in maintaining structural and functional connectivity. We propose brain connectivity as a systems-level integrator of lithium’s effects on synaptic plasticity, neuroinflammation, myelination, and cholinergic signaling. Future trials in early AD should incorporate physiological-dose lithium, candidate cholinergic biomarkers, and multimodal connectivity imaging to clarify lithium’s translational potential as a network-stabilizing intervention. To bridge prior clinical disappointments and the renewed lithium hypothesis, we further propose a de-risked trial strategy centered on biomarker-confirmed early disease, physiological-dose exposure, structured safety surveillance, and imaging-based target engagement.Subject terms: Clinical pharmacology, Diseases

A recent article published in Nature reported that endogenous brain lithium levels were markedly reduced in mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and Alzheimer’s disease (AD) patients, and that dietary lithium depletion in mouse models accelerates amyloid-β (Aβ) deposition, tau phosphorylation, synapse and myelin loss, and cognitive decline [1]. They further showed that replacement therapy with lithium orotate, a lithium salt with reduced amyloid sequestration, ameliorates neuropathology and cognitive deficits in transgenic AD mice. These findings renew interest in the possibility that physiological lithium replacement could be protective in the earliest stages of AD. We believe there are two mechanistic dimensions not discussed in Aron et al. [1] that could explain the encouraging effects of lithium in the AD brain, namely lithium’s interaction with the cholinergic system and lithium’s effects on brain connectivity. This framework holds also the potentiality of addressing the historical gap between theoretical potential and clinical disappointment. We interpret previous negative or inconclusive trials as informative but not definitive, because they largely tested symptomatic-stage AD, used heterogeneous salts and exposure ranges, were short relative to the expected biology of network repair, and did not include direct measures of brain lithium distribution, cholinergic engagement, or connectivity preservation [2–5].

Lithium’s interaction with the cholinergic system

In 1980, Choi and Derman demonstrated that lithium carbonate (Li₂CO₃) administered to patients with endogenous depression produced a significant and reversible inhibition of serum cholinesterase activity within hours of ingestion, without confounding psychotropic co-medications [6]. This biochemical effect was consistent across repeated measures at 7, 14, and 21 days of treatment. Given the central role of acetylcholine in cognition and the established symptomatic benefit of cholinesterase inhibition in AD, we propose that lithium’s impact on cholinesterase represents a plausible contributing mechanism to the cognitive protection observed by Aron et al. [1].

Although Choi and Derman’s study was small, their results align with earlier pharmacological evidence that lithium can modulate acetylcholine synthesis and release at the neuromuscular junction and may interact with central cholinergic signaling. In the decades since, the cholinergic hypothesis of AD has gained strong clinical validation, with multiple approved cholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine) improving cognition and daily function in mild-to-moderate AD. It is therefore striking that lithium’s intrinsic cholinesterase-inhibiting activity has been almost entirely overlooked in the context of AD therapeutics. Here, cholinesterase refers broadly to the enzymes regulating acetylcholine availability, all relevant to AD.

The relevance of this mechanism is further supported by observations from our previous investigator-initiated pilot study of lithium supplementation in AD patients [7], in which we examined not only its effects on memory but also potential mediators of cognitive effects and its impact on extrapyramidal signs (EPS). Although this small trial did not demonstrate statistically significant cognitive improvement, likely due to limited power, it did reveal notable biochemical and clinical effects [8]. Most strikingly, lithium treatment was associated with large increases in erythrocyte (RBC) choline and glycine levels [9, 10]. These peripheral elevations were hypothesized to reflect parallel changes in brain choline and glycine, given known erythrocyte–brain equilibria for small metabolites. Both choline and glycine are relevant to neurotransmission: choline as the precursor of acetylcholine, and glycine as a co-agonist at NMDA receptors. Preclinical studies suggest that elevated brain choline availability can support cholinergic tone, while glycine can modulate glutamatergic signaling and potentially exert neuroprotective effects. Since anticholinergic drugs diminish EPS, we hypothesized that lithium would exacerbate EPS by enhancing central cholinergic tone, particularly in patients with elevated baseline EPS [11]. This hypersensitivity to lithium’s motor side effects may reflect underlying nigrostriatal vulnerability in AD and would require careful safety monitoring in any future trials.

The convergence of these lines of evidence suggests that lithium’s potential benefits in AD may extend beyond the GSK3β inhibition, synaptic maintenance, and microglial modulation emphasized by Aron et al. [1]. An additional pathway may involve enhancement of central cholinergic function through direct cholinesterase inhibition, increased choline availability, and suppression of microglial pro-inflammatory activation via cholinergic anti-inflammatory mechanisms [12]. Indeed, Aron et al. demonstrated that lithium deficiency promotes a reactive microglial phenotype and that physiological lithium restoration suppresses microgliosis [1]; these microglial effects could plausibly be amplified by an increased cholinergic tone, which is known to downregulate pro-inflammatory cytokine release. Notably, Aron et al.’s mouse experiments used lithium orotate at doses yielding serum lithium concentrations within the physiological range, avoiding overt toxicity [1].

Given that past lithium supplementation trials in AD have been small, heterogeneous in dosing, and not designed to capture cholinesterase-related endpoints, we argue for a renewed, mechanistically informed clinical exploration [5]. A modern trial should incorporate early AD subjects with AD biomarker confirmation (amyloid-PET, plasma p-tau217/Aβ42 ratio), physiological-dose lithium orotate to minimize amyloid sequestration and toxicity, measurement of serum and, if feasible, CSF cholinesterase activity before and during treatment, parallel assays of RBC choline and glycine as potential surrogate markers of central metabolite changes. The study should adopt cognitive outcomes sensitive to cholinergic modulation (e.g., verbal learning, attention, working memory) and systematic monitoring for EPS and other neurological adverse events. Indeed, the safety signal from our earlier study (lithium-induced worsening of EPS) [11] warrants particular vigilance, possibly via exclusion of patients with significant baseline parkinsonism or the use of quantitative motor assessments.

Enhanced central cholinergic activity may be a negative modulator of brain GSK-3β expression via activation of the α7 nicotinic receptors [13]. Previous work suggests that lithium may increase central cholinergic tone. Therefore, as per Aron et al. [1], lower free lithium levels associated with increased brain amyloid plaques might decrease central cholinergic tone.

The mechanistic plausibility of a cholinergic contribution is strengthened by several additional considerations. Endogenous lithium deficiency in AD [1] would be expected to release any tonic cholinesterase inhibition, potentially compounding the cholinergic deficit. Restoration of lithium to physiological levels might therefore partially reverse this deficit without reaching toxic concentrations. Cholinergic signaling modulates microglial phenotype via α7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptors, leading to reduced production of IL-1β, TNF, and other pro-inflammatory mediators, paralleling the anti-inflammatory transcriptomic shifts observed by Aron et al. with lithium restoration [1].

From a translational standpoint, the intersection between lithium’s newly appreciated physiological role in the brain, its long-known cholinesterase inhibition, and its effects on key AD-relevant metabolites (choline, glycine) suggests a broader pharmacodynamic profile than currently appreciated. The high-affinity binding of lithium carbonate to amyloid, demonstrated by Aron et al. [1], could potentially limit its efficacy via sequestration in plaques; lithium orotate’s reduced amyloid binding and superior elevation of non-plaque lithium may thus enhance access to cholinergic synapses and cortical microcircuits.

The cholinergic basis of brain connectivity

The basal forebrain cholinergic system (BFCS), centered on the nucleus basalis of Meynert (NBM), provides the principal cholinergic innervation to the cerebral cortex and hippocampus through two anatomically distinct white matter pathways. A lateral pathway traverses the external capsule and uncinate fasciculus to reach frontal, parietal, and temporal cortices, while a medial pathway courses through the cingulum bundle to innervate the cingulate cortex, retrosplenial cortex, and hippocampus [14]. This anatomical organization indicates that cholinergic function and brain connectivity are not independent phenomena but rather complementary perspectives on the same neurobiological substrate. Recent diffusion MRI studies have shown that the microstructural integrity of these NBM white matter projections predicts cognitive performance in aging and neurodegenerative diseases more strongly than NBM volume itself [15]. Reduced integrity of NBM pathways, observed in both AD and dementia with Lewy bodies, is associated with an increased risk of progression from MCI to dementia [16]. Together, these findings identify cholinergic white matter tracts as a critical structural substrate through which cholinergic dysfunction may manifest as large-scale network disconnection.

Beyond structural connectivity, functional MRI studies have demonstrated that cholinergic depletion alters default mode network (DMN) connectivity and reduces functional coupling between the hippocampus and neocortical regions [17, 18]. Conversely, cholinesterase inhibitor treatment in AD patients has been associated with normalization of functional connectivity patterns [19]. This bidirectional evidence establishes that cholinergic tone can modulate functional brain architecture. Together, these findings identify cholinergic pathways as a critical substrate through which cholinergic dysfunction may manifest as both structural tract degradation and large-scale functional network disconnection, with direct implications for cognitive decline in AD.

Lithium’s effects on brain connectivity

The role of brain connectivity in cognitive decline and AD is well defined. Functional alterations within the DMN, altered hippocampal and posterior cingulate coupling, and loss of network segregation are common findings along the AD continuum [20]. Structural disconnection develops in parallel through axonal injury, demyelination, and small vessel disease, which are reflected by reduced fractional anisotropy and higher white matter hyperintensity burden. A lithium effect that improves or stabilizes myelin, reduces neuroinflammatory injury, or enhances synaptic resilience could, in principle, preserve both functional network organization and structural tract integrity. This is biologically plausible because lithium inhibits GSK 3 signaling and modulates pathways that influence tau phosphorylation, synaptic plasticity, and oligodendrocyte lineage biology. The key question is whether human imaging shows network level changes consistent with these expectations, and whether such changes extend to people with cognitive impairment.

Resting state functional MRI (fMRI) studies in humans show that lithium can change network level communication over time. A particularly informative design is within person longitudinal scanning before and after lithium initiation. Spielberg and colleagues studied medication free bipolar patients who received lithium monotherapy and were scanned at baseline, 2 weeks, and 8 weeks [21]. They used graph theory metrics to quantify connectome properties and reported lithium associated shifts that moved mania linked network indices toward healthier patterns. They also showed proportionality between network metric changes and symptom changes, which supports a treatment linked network modulation rather than a static trait difference. This is direct evidence that lithium can alter functional connectivity at the whole brain network level in humans.

A broader synthesis of fMRI work supports the idea that lithium tends to normalize circuit function and connectivity in bipolar disorder. Bergamelli and colleagues reviewed recent functional MRI studies and concluded that lithium commonly modulates prefrontal and interconnected limbic circuitry involved in emotion regulation, with some studies also showing resting state connectivity changes after lithium exposure [22]. While the included studies vary in design and analysis, the convergent message is that lithium has measurable effects on functional networks, not only on regional activation. This matters for dementia research because it demonstrates that lithium is not connectivity neutral in humans, which justifies asking parallel questions in cognitive decline.

On the other hand, relevant findings are reported for white matter microstructure. In older adults with bipolar disorder, Gildengers and colleagues reported that longer lithium exposure was associated with higher overall white matter integrity measured by fractional anisotropy, after controlling for age and vascular disease burden [23]. Fractional anisotropy is a tract level proxy for axonal and myelin organization and therefore a structural connectivity surrogate. Although this study was not designed around dementia outcomes, it directly links lithium exposure to better preservation of white matter microstructure in an older human cohort, a context that overlaps with age related cognitive vulnerability.

More mechanistic diffusion work goes further by linking white matter integrity to the spatial distribution of lithium in the brain. Advances in lithium sensitive MRI, using Lithium-7 (7Li), the most abundant stable isotope of lithium detected by magnetic resonance (MR), can improve the interpretability of connectivity studies because they enable mapping where lithium actually accumulates in different clinical conditions, such as bipolar disorder [24]. Stout and colleagues, using 7Li MRI, reported accumulation patterns that included prominent hippocampal signal in treated bipolar patients [25]. Notably, Necus and colleagues combined diffusion MRI with 7Li MRI reporting a positive association between generalized fractional anisotropy and the spatial distribution of lithium signal [26]. This moves beyond exposure duration toward a target engagement concept. If higher lithium presence in particular brain regions or tissue compartments relates to better microstructural integrity, then lithium related connectivity preservation becomes a biologically anchored hypothesis rather than only a clinical association.

The clinical trial literature shows repeated attempts to test lithium in AD, but imaging outcomes are uncommon. Hampel and colleagues conducted a 10-week, randomized, single blind, placebo-controlled multicenter study over 10 weeks in 71 mild AD participants [4]. No treatment effect on GSK-3 activity or CSF-based biomarker concentrations was observed. Lithium treatment did not lead to change in global cognitive performance as measured by the ADAS-Cog subscale or in depressive symptoms. The short trial duration and small samples limit the likelihood of detecting biological or clinical changes even if they exist. Unfortunately, brain connectivity was not studied.

Longer and lower dose strategies have also been explored. Nunes and colleagues tested a microdose lithium approach in AD and reported clinical course signals in a 15-month study involving 113 participants [27]. Again, neuroimaging connectivity was not measured. Still, this line of work is conceptually important for connectivity because microdose regimens aim to reduce toxicity risk while targeting long term neurobiological modulation. Connectivity outcomes are well suited to such regimens because they can detect subclinical brain effects that may precede measurable cognitive change.

Lithium has been repeatedly associated with measures consistent with preserved brain structural integrity, including increased gray matter and white matter volume in healthy volunteer studies after several weeks of lithium. Monkul and colleagues reported increases in dorsolateral prefrontal and anterior cingulate gray matter volumes and increased total white matter volume after 4 weeks of lithium in healthy individuals [28]. While volume is not connectivity, increased white matter volume and regional gray matter support can contribute to preserved tract conduction and local circuit processing that underpin network organization.

In older cohorts at risk of cognitive decline due to age and vascular burden, lithium exposure correlates with diffusion metrics that usually worsen in dementia. The Gildengers’ findings are notable here because they were controlled for vascular disease burden [23]. Since small vessel disease is a major driver of disconnection and executive dysfunction, any association between lithium and better white matter integrity in the presence of vascular risk strengthens the idea that lithium could preserve structural connectivity in older adults more generally.

A more recent study using 7Li-MRI and 3 T scanner and involving 9 healthy subjects receiving 5 mg daily for up to 28 days, demonstrated detection of lithium in the brain at very low concentration, in the range of approximately 10–60 mM [29]. This matters for AD because many proposed dementia regimens use subtherapeutic or low therapeutic dosing, and a frequent criticism has been uncertainty about brain exposure. If brain lithium can be measured in vivo, then future dementia trials can test whether individuals who achieve higher brain lithium signal show better maintenance of connectivity metrics over time.

A recent meta-analysis of clinical studies on lithium in AD emphasized the heterogeneity of trials and the need for better designed studies [5]. From a connectivity perspective, the key implication is that clinical endpoints alone may be too blunt in small samples. Potential imaging biomarkers such as DMN integrity, hippocampal connectivity, and diffusion-based tract measures can provide higher sensitivity to detect lithium related neurobiological effects, especially over intermediate time frames.

Putting this together, we can state that lithium can modulate functional brain networks and can be associated with better preservation of white matter microstructure, at least in bipolar disorder cohorts and in healthy volunteer studies. The AD literature provides proof of clinical interest and feasibility, but it lacks connectivity endpoints. The mechanistic bridge is plausible and testable. AD involves progressive network disintegration with early default mode network disruption and later global loss of segregation. Lithium, through synaptic resilience and potential myelin supportive effects, might slow these network changes. Diffusion MRI suggests a structural substrate for this hypothesis because lithium exposure correlates with higher fractional anisotropy in older adults and with better white matter integrity in relation to lithium distribution.

Bridging historical clinical disappointments with a de-risked trial strategy

Any renewed proposal to test lithium in AD must face with disappointing results of earlier trials. We argue, however, that those results should be read as informative rather than definitive. Hampel et al. tested lithium for 10 weeks in mild AD, without considering brain lithium exposure, cholinergic biomarkers, or connectivity endpoints [4]. While the design was appropriate for detecting short-term symptomatic or CSF biochemical effects, it did not test long-term restoration of a deficient trace element during the MCI or preclinical window. By contrast, the low-dose lithium studies by Forlenza and colleagues in amnestic MCI reported reduced CSF p-tau, better cognition, good tolerability, and 91% adherence after 12 months, and later showed clinical stability over two years with increased CSF Aβ42 after 36 months [2, 3]. The recent randomized pilot by Gildengers et al. supports the feasibility and tolerability of low-dose lithium in older adults at risk of cognitive decline, although none of the six co-primary endpoints met the prespecified significance threshold [30].

A second, related lesson concerns the therapeutic window. AD pathobiology begins years before the dementia stage at which most lithium trials have enrolled. Contemporary biomarker frameworks [31] define preclinical and prodromal phases in which amyloid, tau, synaptic dysfunction, and network abnormalities are detectable while irreversible disconnection is comparatively limited [32]. This is the stage at which a network-stabilizing intervention is most likely to be effective [33] and at which candidate connectivity-based biomarkers retain the dynamic range needed to detect target engagement. The low-dose MCI lithium experience also demonstrates that intervention at this stage is feasible and tolerable in older adults.

At a causal-chain level, the high-affinity binding of Aβ to lithium sequesters lithium in amyloid plaques and reduces the pool of biologically active lithium [1]. Reduced free lithium then increases vulnerability of synapses, myelin, and microglia homeostasis, while loss of lithium tonic cholinesterase inhibition and reduced choline availability further weaken cholinergic signalling. These changes manifest at the network level as degradation of Nucleus Basalis of Meynert cholinergic projections, altered hippocampal connectivity, and loss of DMN segregation, features characteristic of prodromal AD [34]. Restoration of physiologically available lithium acts upstream of GSK-3β/tau signalling, α7-nicotinic anti-inflammatory pathways, cholinergic tone, and white matter integrity. Brain connectivity therefore functions as an intermediate mechanistic endpoint rather than only a descriptive imaging readout.

Future trials should use low starting doses, slow titration, predefined upper serum targets below standard bipolar-disorder ranges, renal and thyroid monitoring, ECG screening when clinically indicated, drug-interaction review, hydration counseling, and systematic assessment of tremor, gait worsening, EPS, confusion, gastrointestinal symptoms, and cardiac conduction abnormalities [35, 36]. The goal is not to repurpose conventional psychiatric lithium dosing for dementia, but to test carefully monitored physiological or low-dose restoration with direct target-engagement measures, including serum lithium, 7Li-MRI where available, cholinesterase activity, RBC choline/glycine, p-tau217, NfL, GFAP, and brain connectivity.

Conclusions

In conclusion, Aron et al. have provided compelling evidence that endogenous lithium is a physiological modulator of brain ageing and AD pathology.1 We contend that a full understanding of lithium’s cognitive effects in AD requires integration of its cholinergic properties and its ability to modify brain connectivity (Fig. 1). The convergence of lithium’s cholinergic and connectivity-related effects may therefore operate through a shared anatomical pathway. If lithium enhances cholinergic tone, it may concurrently support the functional and structural integrity of NBM projections. Preclinical evidence supports this integrated view. Lithium modulates muscarinic receptor–dependent facilitation of synaptic plasticity and theta–gamma coupling within hippocampal–prefrontal circuits [37], demonstrating that lithium’s cholinergic interactions have direct consequences for circuit-level communication. The white matter preservation reported with long-term lithium treatment [23] may thus reflect, at least in part, maintenance of cholinergic tract integrity. Future clinical studies should explicitly assess cholinergic endpoints, metabolite changes, and microglial phenotype in the context of physiological-dose lithium supplementation, with an emphasis on early intervention and safety. Crucially, this framework does not dismiss prior negative trials. It reinterprets them as evidence that lithium must be tested at the appropriate biological stage, dose range, and target-engagement level, with explicit safety safeguards and network-sensitive endpoints.

Lithium as a physiological modulator of cognitive function in Alzheimer’s disease through convergent effects on the cholinergic system and brain connectivity.

Reduced endogenous brain lithium levels observed in mild cognitive impairment and Alzheimer’s disease are associated with neural disconnection. Restoration of lithium may exert multi-level effects, including modulation of cholinergic pathways, which in turn trigger a cascade of downstream effects on microglial activation and tau accumulation, together with preservation of white matter integrity within cholinergic tracts. These cascading effects may ultimately extend to large-scale functional networks, particularly cortico-hippocampal circuits and the default mode network. Brain connectivity is proposed as a systems-level integrative readout linking lithium’s effects on synaptic plasticity, myelination, neuroinflammation, and cholinergic signaling, and as a promising sensitive biomarker of target engagement in early clinical trials.

Brain connectivity may represent the systems level readout of many of the effects of lithium on synaptic plasticity, neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, mitochondrial function, and myelination. Future lithium trials in MCI or early AD should incorporate resting state fMRI connectivity and diffusion MRI as primary or key secondary endpoints, alongside quantitative measures of brain lithium exposure using 3D 7Li MRI where available. This design would allow direct tests of target engagement to network preservation and would clarify whether lithium benefits, if present, operate by stabilizing connectivity rather than by producing short term symptomatic effects.
