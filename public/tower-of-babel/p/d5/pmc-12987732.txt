The multifunctional RNA helicase DDX39A drives glioblastoma progression by modulating WISP1 alternative splicing that induces an immunosuppressive macrophage polarization
Authors: Yan Zhang, Zhiwei Xue, Naibin Zhang, Yuehua Zhu, YanZhao Wu, Meilin Lv, Zhihan Zhang, Feiyu Mu, Wenchen Xing, Ziyi Tang, Chunjie Wang, Zhiyi Xue, Wenjing Zhou, Xiaofei Liu, Xingang Li, Rolf Bjerkvig, Bin Huang, Mingzhi Han, Jian Wang, Donghai Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Glioblastoma (GB) is a highly complex ecosystem characterized by numerous interactions between tumor cells and the surrounding tumor microenvironment (TME). Splicing factors play a pivotal role in processing nascent pre-mRNA and are important in the progression of cancer, making them promising molecular targets. In this study, we demonstrate that the DEAD-box helicase 39 A (DDX39A), a RNA helicase with several important roles in RNA metabolism and cellular processes, is significantly upregulated in GB and is primarily expressed in tumor cells, leading to an immunosuppressive macrophage polarization. Through in vitro and in vivo studies, we demonstrate that reducing DDX39A expression in GB results in reduced tumor growth and invasion. Mechanistically, through RNA-seq and RIP-seq, we identified WISP1 as a critical downstream effector of DDX39A. DDX39A stabilizes WISP1 pre-mRNA through alternative splicing regulation, thereby activating the AKT signaling pathway. We further demonstrate that WISP1, when secreted by tumor cells, functions as a paracrine signaling molecule that promotes the development of immunosuppressive tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs). Additionally, we demonstrate that Fluphenazine hydrochloride binds to and inhibits DDX39A, thereby suppressing GB growth, invasion, and the immunosuppressive function of macrophages. DDX39A thus represents a potential candidate for glioma-targeted therapy.

Introduction

Glioblastoma (GB) represents the most aggressive and common form of primary brain tumors, characterized by rapid growth, a high recurrence rate, and resistance to conventional therapies [1]. Their molecular characteristics have been extensively studied, and it is now widely recognized that their cellular complexity and plasticity are closely related to the tumor cells themselves, as well as to influences mediated by the brain microenvironment [2–4].

Alternative splicing (AS) is an essential post-transcriptional mechanism that enables a single gene to produce multiple mRNA isoforms, thereby increasing the diversity of the proteome [5, 6]. AS in normal cells is crucial for regulating gene expression, cell differentiation, and tissue-specific functions [7]. However, in the context of cancer, aberrant AS has emerged as a significant driver of tumor progression, metastasis, and drug resistance [8–10]. Disruption of AS often leads to the generation of oncogenic isoforms that can impact key pathways, including cell cycle regulation, apoptosis, migration, and invasion [11–13]. It has been demonstrated that AS plays a crucial role in shaping cancer-related phenotypes by modulating the functions of key proteins. For instance, alterations in splicing factors such as SRSF1, SF3B1, and PTB are closely associated with changes in splicing patterns that promote tumorigenesis and metastasis [14–16]. These splicing factors are often overexpressed or mutated in various cancers, activating splicing programs that support GB cell survival and proliferation. In the present work, we show that the multifunctional RNA Helicase DDX39A is highly expressed in GB, which correlates with patient prognosis. Since DDX39A also acts as a splicing factor up-regulated in GB, the present work aimed at understanding its function and role in GB.

We show by single-cell sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis and mechanistic studies that DDX39A is highly expressed in GB cells exhibiting rapid proliferation as well as in in tumor associated macrophages (TAM) that promote tumor progression. Knockdown of DDX39A significantly inhibited malignant traits such as proliferation and invasion in GB cells while also altering the immunosuppressive microenvironment. Mechanistically, DDX39A binds to and mediates the alternative splicing of WISP1 pre-mRNA, enhancing its transcript stability. This post-transcriptional regulation drives PI3K-AKT hyperactivation in GB cells. Importantly, GB-secreted WISP1 functioned as a paracrine effector to induce AKT-dependent TAM M2-like polarization. We further show that the small molecule drug Fluphenazine binds to DDX39A and inhibits its function. Given its ability to cross the blood-brain barrier, Fluphenazine should therefore be a potential candidate for GB treatment.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

This study protocol was approved by the Scientific Research Ethics Committee of Qilu Hospital, Shandong University (Jinan, China; approval number: KYLL-202207-018-1). Patients and volunteers provided written informed consent for their participation in this study, which was conducted in full adherence to relevant regulations and guidelines.

Database searches and analysis

RNA-seq transcriptome data and the associated clinicopathological factors were collected from the TCGA glioma dataset (http://cancergenome.nih.gov/). The CGGA RNA-seq dataset along with the corresponding clinicopathological parameters were retrieved from the CGGA database (http://www.cgga.org.cn/). The expression data were analyzed using R software (Version 4.2.3). Heatmaps were generated with OmicStudio tools (https://www.omicstudio.cn/tool), while volcano plots and bar charts were constructed with GraphPad Prism (Version 8.3). Survival analyses were conducted using GlioVis (http://gliovis.bioinfo.cnio.es/).

scRNA-seq analysis

The scRNA-seq raw sequencing data were processed through the Cell Ranger pipeline, where cells were aligned to the mouse reference genome (mm10) and quantified for unique molecular identifiers (UMIs). Subsequent analyses were conducted using the Seurat package. Following normalization, genes with high variance across cells were identified for downstream analysis. For clustering analysis, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was first performed to identify the most significant principal components (PCs). The top PCs with the most significant contributions were selected for further clustering. A graph-based clustering algorithm was employed, where a K-nearest neighbor (KNN) graph was constructed based on Euclidean distances, and cells were grouped using the Louvain algorithm for modularity optimization. The clustering results were visualized using t-SNE or UMAP algorithms, which projected cells into a two-dimensional space, with cells in the same cluster represented in identical colors. Cell type annotation was performed based on canonical marker genes. Results were visualized through UMAP plots, violin plots, dot plots, and heatmaps. Statistical analyses were performed using R software (v4.1.0), and data visualization was carried out with Seurat’s built-in tools combined with the ggplot2 package.

Patients and samples

Glioma specimens and adjacent normal tissues were obtained from Qilu Hospital of Shandong University (Jinan, China). Samples of human glioma tissues were collected from patients during surgeries at Qilu Hospital. Nonneoplastic brain tissue samples were harvested during operations on patients with traumatic brain injury. Two experienced clinical pathologists assessed and diagnosed glioma specimens according to the latest WHO tumor classification (Table S1). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant regulations and guidelines.

Cell lines and cultures

The normal human astrocyte, along with human and mouse glioma cell lines (T98, U118, A172, LN229, and GL261), were purchased from ATCC and cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Waltham, MA, USA), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Patient-derived primary GB cells (GB#P3, GB#BG5, and GB#BG7) [17] were obtained from GB primary specimens and provided by Professor Rolf Bjerkvig at the Department of Biomedicine, University of Bergen (Bergen, Norway). Short tandem repeat (STR, Table S2) profiling was conducted to confirm the identity of the glioma stem cells (GSCs). These cells were maintained in Neurobasal Medium (Gibco/Thermo Fisher Scientific), supplemented with 2% B-27 Neuro Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 20 ng/mL epidermal growth factor (EGF; PeproTech; East Windsor, NJ, USA), and 10 ng/mL basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF; PeproTech). THP-1 cells were obtained from the Department of Hematology, Qilu Hospital of Shandong University, and cultured in RPMI 1640 Medium (MACGENE, USA) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Thermo Fisher Scientific). All cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

Cell transfection

Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) were transiently transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. For plasmids, transient transfections were similarly conducted with Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the same manufacturer’s guidelines. Stable knockdown or overexpression of DDX39A in cell lines was achieved through lentiviral transduction. To generate the lentivirus, HEK293T cells were transfected with the lentiviral packaging plasmids psPAX2 and pCMV-VSV-G, along with the lentiviral expression construct; lentiviral supernatants were collected 48 h later. Target cells were cultured with a supernatant containing lentivirus for 24 h, after which they were selected for puromycin treatment 72 h later. The sequences of the siRNAs and shRNAs used are shown in Table S3, 4. The plasmids and vector employed are listed in Table S5.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted following the manufacturer’s instructions using a kit from Yishan Bio (ES-RN001, Yishan Bio). cDNA was synthesized from total RNA using the ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Kit (11141ES60, Yeasen). qRT-PCR was conducted with SYBR Green Master Mix on the Roche 480II Real-Time PCR Detection System (11201ES08, Yeasen). The expression of target mRNA was normalized to GAPDH mRNA expression. The primer sequences utilized are provided in Table S6.

Western blotting analysis

Proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer containing a cocktail of protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Beyotime, China). Protein concentration was measured with the Enhanced BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Equal amounts of protein per sample (30 µg) were separated by SDS‒PAGE and then transferred onto PVDF membranes (Merck Millipore; Billerica, MA, USA). Membranes were blocked for 10 min in QuickBlockTM Blocking Buffer (Beyotime), incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, and then treated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (ZSGB-BIO; Beijing, China) for 1 h. ECL (Merck Millipore) was used to detect immunoreactivity. GAPDH served as a loading control. The primary antibodies used are listed in Table S7.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

For cellular immunofluorescence staining, 2000 cells per well were seeded overnight into Slide 8 Wells (Ibidi GmbH, Germany). The next day, treatments were added to each well, and after 72 h, each well was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS, blocked for 1 h using 1% BSA. Cells were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C at the appropriate dilution, followed by incubation with species-appropriate, fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature at a dilution of 1:2000 for detection. DAPI (blue) (Beyotime Biotechnology) was used as a nuclear counterstain with a 30 min incubation. Images were captured using a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems; Wetzlar, Germany).

For immunofluorescence staining of tissue sections, paraffin sections were incubated at 70 °C for 30 min, dewaxed in xylene and 100% ethanol, then air-dried. The sections were washed three times with PBS. Antigen retrieval was then carried out using EDTA Antigen Retrieval Solution (C1034, Solarbio) for 30 min at 95 °C, and the sections were allowed to cool naturally to room temperature for 1 h. They were incubated with 3% H2O2 for 10 min, followed by blocking for 30 min with a solution containing 0.1% saponin, 1% BSA, and 10% donkey serum. Incubations with primary and secondary antibodies were then performed as previously described. The primary antibodies used are listed in Table S7.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Paraffin sections were incubated at 70 °C for 30 min, dewaxed in xylene and 100% ethanol, and then air-dried. The sections were washed three times in PBS. Antigen retrieval was performed using EDTA Antigen Retrieval Solution (C1034, Solarbio) for 30 min at 95 °C, after which the sections were allowed to cool naturally to room temperature for 1 h. The sections were treated with 3% H2O2 for 10 min and subsequently blocked for 30 min. The primary antibody was added to the sections for overnight incubation at 4 °C. Detection was achieved by incubating with a species-appropriate horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody and the DAB substrate. The sections were scanned, and images were captured using the WISLEAP Scanning System (WISLEAP Medical Technology Co. LTD). Staining was evaluated independently to establish the histological score based on the proportion of positively stained cells and staining intensity. The primary antibodies used are listed in Table S7. The IHC staining of DDX39A in GB tissue samples was evaluated based on both staining intensity and percentage of positively stained cells. Percentage of positive cells: 0% = 0 points 1–25% = 1 point 26–50% = 2 points 51–75% = 3 points 76–100% = 4 points. Positive staining intensity: No colour was scored as 0 points, pale yellow as 1 point, yellow as 2 points, and brown as 3 points. The product of these two scores constituted the expression level: 0–4 points indicated low expression, while 5-12 points signified high expression.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) was performed using a Pierce™ Classic Magnetic IP/Co-IP Kit (88804, Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cell lysates were incubated with primary antibodies or anti-IgG (3420S; Cell Signaling Technology) overnight at 4 °C, then mixed with Protein A/G magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and incubated for 2 h at room temperature. The proteins were eluted, separated by SDS‒PAGE, and analyzed by western blotting. The primary antibodies used are listed in Table S7.

RIP-seq and RIP-PCR

RIP-Seq and subsequent bioinformatics analyses were conducted by LC-BIO Biotech (Hangzhou, China). DDX39A-overexpressing GB#P3 cells were utilized for RIP-seq using the EZ-Nuclear RIP (Cross-Linked) Kit (Merck Millipore). Briefly, the cells were harvested and cross-linked with 0.3% formaldehyde. Nuclei were extracted and lysed in nuclear lysis buffer, and the DNA was subjected to sonication to shear it into fragments of 500–1000 bp. Magnetic beads coated with anti-FLAG antibody (Cell Signaling Technology) were incubated with the nuclear lysates, and DNase I was applied to degrade the DNA. The library was constructed and paired-end sequenced by LC-BIO Biotech (Hangzhou, China) on an Illumina platform (Illumina, CA, USA). RIP-PCR was performed using a Magna RIP™ RNA-Binding Protein Immunoprecipitation Kit (17-700, Millipore). The primer sequences used are listed in Table S6.

RNA-seq

GB#P3 mRNA sequencing was performed by LC Bio Corporation in Hangzhou, China. Bioinformatics analysis employed the OmicStudio tools at https://www.omicstudio.cn/tool. Differential gene expression was identified using a fold change (FC; |log2(FC)|) greater than 1 and a P-value of less than 0.05.

RNA pull-down assays

RNA pull-down experiments were conducted using the Pierce™ Magnetic RNA-Protein Deposition Kit (#20164, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Biotin-labeled WISP1 pre-mRNA was synthesized by RiboBio (GenePharma). GB#P3 and GB#BG5 cell lysates were incubated with the biotinylated WISP1 probe, followed by streptavidin-conjugated agarose magnetic beads for 30 min at room temperature. Proteins bound to DDX39A were identified using Western blotting.

RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization

Cells were detected and analyzed using NovoExpress (Agilent). 2000 cells per well were seeded overnight into Slide 8 Wells (Ibidi GmbH, Germany) and then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. FAM modified FISH probes were used to detect the WISP1 pre-mRNA following the manufacturer’s protocol (GenePharma). Immunofluorescence staining was performed to determine the subcellular localization of DDX39A. DAPI (blue) (Beyotime Biotechnology) was used as a nuclear counterstain with a 30 min incubation. Images were captured using a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems; Wetzlar, Germany).

Flow cytometry

Cells were washed with PBS, resuspended in a staining solution, and treated with Annexin V-FITC and PI (BD Biosciences) for 15 min to detect apoptotic death. Anti-CD163-PE (ab95613, Abcam) antibody was used to stain the cells to identify CD163+ macrophages.

ELISA

For the ELISA assay, 3 × 105 GB#P3 cells transfected with lentivirus-shNC, shDDX39A-1, or shDDX39A-2 were seeded into each well of a 6-well plate. After 48 h of incubation in a cell culture incubator, the supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove any remaining cells or debris. The clarified supernatant was then immediately used for the ELISA assay. The concentrations of WISP1 in cell culture supernatants were measured using WISP1 ELISA kits (KE00236, Proteintech). The ELISA was conducted following the manufacturer’s guidelines. Absorbance was recorded at 450 nm. Protein content was assessed by comparing the relative absorbance of the samples to that of the standards.

Cell proliferation assays

Following the manufacturer’s instructions, a CCK-8 kit (40203ES76, Yeasen) was used to assess cell viability. Briefly, 8 × 10^3 cells were seeded in 96-well plates, cultured for 24 h, and then treated with CCK-8 reagent for 1 h at 37 °C. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm, and the transfection time point was recorded as 0 h.

For the EdU assay, cells were treated according to the standard protocols of the Yefluor 488 and 594 EdU Imaging Kits (40275ES76, 40276ES76, Yeasen). Representative images were captured using a Leica inverted fluorescence microscope. The ratio of EdU-positive cells (red or green) to Hoechst-positive cells (blue) was calculated to assess the cell proliferation rate.

Cell invasion assays

Transwell assays were performed in 24-well plates. The upper chamber was seeded with cells (3 × 104 cells/300 µL) in FBS-free medium, while the lower chamber was filled with medium supplemented with 10% FBS. Cells that passed through the 8 µm membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with crystal violet. Brightfield microscopy images were captured, and the cells were counted. For the 3D tumor spheroid invasion assays, cell spheres cultured in low-attachment U-bottom 96-well plates were embedded in Matrigel (Trevigen; Gaithersburg, MD, USA) and cultured for 48 h at 37 °C. The diameter of the spheres was considered the starting point for quantification. In the invasion ex vivo assay. 21-day rat fetal brain organoids were cultured according to the GB-brain organoid co-culture invasion ex vivo technique as previously described [18]. GFP-transfected GB#P3 cells were cultured until glioma spheroids formed, and then co-cultured for 48 h with mature brain organoids. Confocal microscopy images of GB cell invasion were obtained to evaluate invasion (Leica TCS SP8; Wetzlar, Germany).

Tumor sphere formation assay

For the tumor sphere formation experiment, 2000 GB#P3 cells were seeded into each well of a low-attachment 6-well plate, and the number of tumorspheres was measured 6 days later.

In vitro limiting dilution assays

GB#P3 cells were plated into one well of low-attachment 96-well plates at specified densities (2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, and 128 cells), with 10 repetitions for each density. The presence and quantity of tumorspheres in each well were counted 6 days later.

Animal studies

All animal experimental protocols used in this study received approval from the ethics committee of Qilu Hospital, Shandong University (Jinan, China; approval number: DWLL-2022-094). To minimize experimental error as much as possible, BALB/c nude and C57BL/6 J mice (male, 4 weeks; GemPharmatech; Nanjing, China) were randomly divided into groups of 5 mice each. GB#P3 or GL261 cells expressing luciferase (5 × 10⁵ cells suspended in 10 µL PBS) were transplanted into the frontal lobes of the nude mice using a stereotactic frame (KDS310, KD Scientific; Holliston, MA, USA). Tumor growth was monitored weekly through bioluminescence imaging (IVIS Spectral In Vivo Imaging System, PerkinElmer; Hopkinton, MA, USA). The animals were euthanized with anesthetics when they exhibited any signs of persistent discomfort, such as severe hunched posture, reduced movement, apathy, leg dragging, or loss of weight exceeding 20% of the initial body weight. The mouse brains were perfused, and brain tissues were sectioned for HE/IHC/IF analysis. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant regulations and guidelines.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 8.3; GraphPad Software, CA). The results are presented as mean ± SD. For independent samples, differences between two groups with equal variance were assessed using a two-tailed Student’s t-test. One-way ANOVA was utilized to evaluate differences among multiple groups with equal variance. All statistical data were derived from biological replicates. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated and compared using the log-rank test. P-values of < 0.05 were considered statistically significant and are indicated as follows: *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001.

Results

DDX39A is overexpressed in GBs and increases with glioma grade

We have earlier identified >300 proteins potentially involved in mRNA splicing using Gene Ontology annotations [19]. Among these, and also by retrieving expression data from the TCGA database, we found DDX39A to be significantly overexpressed in GB (n = 528) compared to normal brain tissue (n = 10) (Fig. 1A, B). This was also confirmed in the CGGA, showing that DDX39A expression increases with glioma grade, with the highest levels observed in GB (Fig. 1C). To confirm these results, we conducted IHC staining of DDX39A on non-neoplastic brain tissue (n = 3) and glioma biopsy specimens (n = 11). The results demonstrated that DDX39A expression was significantly elevated in GB. Since GB represents a highly complex ecosystem, which includes not only tumor cells but also immune cells such as microglia, macrophages and T cells, alongside tumor microenvironmental cells such as oligodendrocytes and endothelial cells among others [3], we analyzed single-cell sequencing data of glioma tissues (GSE131928) and found DDX39A to be primarily expressed in tumor cells and macrophages (Fig. 1E). Collectively, a cellular functional diversity was observed within the GB, spanning genes involved in putative metabolic reprogramming (METTL7B), drug resistance (MGST1), neuronal differentiation (STMN2), stress adaptation (TRIB3), stemness (DLL3), cell proliferation (MKI67), and immune modulation (CCL3L3) (Fig. 1F). Among these, DDX39A was higher expressed in GB cell populations with high MKI67 expression, indicating a highly proliferative state (Fig. 1F, right panel). Several studies have shown that macrophages with increased S100A4 expression can promote tumor proliferation, invasion, and metastasis [20, 21]. Interestingly, DDX39A was also highly expressed in macrophages with elevated MKI67 and S100A4 levels (Fig. S1A).

To confirm these results, we assessed by immunofluorescence staining the expression of DDX39A and the murine macrophage marker F4/80 in patient-derived orthotopic GB#P3 xenografts established in nude mice as well as in a human GB biopsy (Figs. 1G, S1B). Immune cell infiltration scoring analysis using RStudio to assess bulk RNA-seq using the CGGA database revealed that tumor tissues with high DDX39A expression exhibit increased infiltration and abundance of M2 macrophages compared to cell types with low DDX39A expression (Fig. S1C). This indicates that DDX39A may exert its effects through tumor cells and influence the glioma microenvironment, thereby promoting malignant progression. We further assessed DDX39A protein expression in various cell populations, including normal human astrocytes (NHA), GB cell lines (T98, U118, A172, and LN229), and patient-derived primary GSCs (GB#P3, GB#BG5, and GB#BG7) using western blotting. The results revealed that GB cell lines, along with patient-derived primary GSCs exhibited higher DDX39A expression levels compared to NHA (Fig. S1D). Among these GB models, GB#P3 and GB#BG5 were selected for further investigation. Immunofluorescence staining showed a nuclear localization of DDX39A, consistent with its role as a splicing factor (Fig. S1E).

In conclusion, the integrated analyses suggest that DDX39A overexpression is associated with a poor prognosis. In addition, increased DDX39A expression is associated with a tumour immunosuppressive microenvironment.

DDX39A down-regulation inhibits GB progression in vitro and in vivo

To determine the functional significance of increased DDX39A expression in GB cells, we reduced its expression in the human GB#P3 and GB#BG5 cancer stem cell lines using two independent siRNA sequences (Table S3, Fig. S2A, B). We then conducted RNA sequencing on GB#P3 cells transfected with siDDX39A and siNC, and found both upregulated and downregulated genes (Fig. S2C). Following GO enrichment analysis, the most affected gene clusters were related to the cell cycle, cell division, cell differentiation, innate immune response, apoptosis, and DNA replication (Fig. S2D-H). These processes are closely associated with glioma proliferation and the remodeling of the glioma immune microenvironment, indicating that DDX39A has a key role in GB progression. Therefore, we validated biological processes related to cell proliferation, invasion, apoptosis, stemness, and innate immune response through experiments. First, we show that silencing DDX39A reduces cell proliferation and viability in GB#P3, GB#BG5, and GL261 cells, as assessed by CCK8 and EdU assays. (Figs. 2A-C, S2I-M). Next, we investigated whether silencing DDX39A affected the invasive ability of GB cells. We assessed the invasive properties of GB cell lines transfected with siRNAs targeting DDX39A using a 3D tumor sphere invasion assay in collagen gels. The results revealed a significant decrease in invasion (Fig. 2D). To better replicate the physiologically invasive tumor microenvironment, we also employed a co-culture invasion model using tumor spheroids and normal rat brain organoids, as previously described [18]. Also in this invasion assay GB#P3-siDDX39A tumor cells showed a reduced invasive capacity into the organoids, compared to controls (Fig. 2E). The migration ability of GL261 was further assessed using the Transwell assay, yielding the same conclusion (Fig. S2N). Next, we used a flow-cytometric apoptosis assay in GB#P3, GB#BG5, and GL261 cells transfected with siDDX39A, as assessed by an increased Annexin-V and PI staining. An enhanced apoptotic cell death was observed following siDDX39A transfection (Figs. 2F, S2O). Additionally, silencing DDX39A led to a reduction in the protein expression levels of the full-length Caspase-3 protein, and an increase in the protein expression of Cleaved-Caspase-3, further indicating that silencing DDX39A promotes GB cell apoptosis (Fig. S2P). Additionally, silencing DDX39A impaired the self-renewal capacity of GSCs, as demonstrated by in vitro limiting dilution assays (Fig. 2G, H) and tumor sphere formation assays (Fig. 2I). DDX39A promotes the malignant progression of GB by influencing the alternative splicing of WISP1

DDX39A is a member of the DEAD-box protein family, a group of RNA-dependent ATPases that belong to the helicase superfamily 2 (SF2). It participates in multiple aspects of RNA metabolism, including pre-mRNA splicing [22]. IHC and immunofluorescence analyses of GB tissue samples and cells showed that DDX39A is predominantly localized in the cell nucleus (Fig. S1E). We used RIP-seq and RNA-seq to identify the downstream target RNAs of DDX39A. As shown in Fig. 3A, DDX39A has a binding specificity for exon-intron boundaries, with continuous exonic occupancy and concentrated binding at splice sites, indicating its role as a splicing regulator that likely facilitates exon recognition and splice site definition during pre-mRNA processing. The results revealed five types of AS events (Fig. 3B). From this, it was evident that skipped exon events represent the predominant form of differential alternative splicing, accounting for over 60% of all detected changes, indicating that exon inclusion/exclusion is the primary mechanism of splicing regulation in this system (Fig. 3C). Thus, DDX39A shows a rather precise binding specificity for exon-intron boundaries, with continuous exonic occupancy and concentrated binding at splice sites, indicating its role as a splicing regulator that likely facilitates exon recognition and splice site definition during pre-mRNA processing (Fig. 3A-C). Through deep sequencing analysis of DDX39A-bound RNAs, we found that DDX39A is predominantly mapped to exons and introns, which were quantified and subjected to differential gene analysis (Figs. 3C, D, S4A). To further confirm the downstream RNAs, we intersected the RNAs that DDX39A can bind with the downregulated genes from RNA-seq following DDX39A knockdown and the genes significantly upregulated in glioma (Fig. 3E). This resulted in 12 genes, which we displayed on the volcano plot of the RNA-seq data post-DDX39A knockdown (Fig. 3F). We performed PCR validation of the top-ranked genes and found that WISP1 had more pronounced trends (Fig. S4B, C). At present, there is no direct evidence for that DDX39A is involved in Wnt/ß-catenin signaling and thus WISP1 expression. However, DDX39A may influence gene expression globally, including potentially splicing or export of mRNAs encoding Wnt pathway components. In this context, a few studies of RNA helicases (including DDX39A paralogs, like DDX39B) have shown to have an impact on cancer progression, involving Wnt signaling activation [23]. Our current results (Fig. 3A-F) support this notion. Previous studies have shown that WISP1 affect clinical prognosis through associations with tumor purity, immune cell infiltration, and macrophage M2 polarization in pan-cancer [24]. Furthermore, WISP1 has been reported to promote GSC maintenance through an autocrine loop and, importantly, to promote the survival of M2 tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) in a paracrine manner [25], which is highly consistent with our current results regarding DDX39A. Therefore, we selected WISP1 as a candidate for further in-depth investigation. The DDX39A-binding motif shown in Fig. 3G illustrates the consensus RNA sequence preferentially recognized by DDX39A, as identified from our RIP-seq dataset. Furthermore, we show that knocking down DDX39A alters the RNA-seq profile of the WISP1 gene, likely reducing its proper splicing or steady-state transcript levels (Figs. 3H, S4D). DDX39A-WISP1 regulation activates the Akt pathway to promote GB progression

Dysregulation of the PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling axis is a well-recognized driver of oncogenic transformation, promoting tumor progression. DDX39A alters the tumor microenvironment

Tumor tissues with high DDX39A expression, as mentioned previously, showed increased infiltration and abundance of tumor-supportive macrophages (Fig. S1C). A previous study has shown that WISP1 promotes the survival of tumor-supportive M2-like macrophages by activating the Integrin α6β1-Akt signaling pathway [25]. To assess the effects of DDX39A on the immune microenvironment, we incubated supernatants from GB#P3-shDDX39A or GB#P3-shNC cells with THP1-induced M0 macrophages (Fig. 6A). We analyzed the expression levels of macrophage phenotypic markers using flow cytometry, qRT-PCR, and Western blot. Fluphenazine dihydrochloride targets DDX39A: a promising therapeutic strategy for GB

We screened potential drugs targeting the DDX39A-WISP1-AKT axis. Using the cMap database used for small molecule in silico drug screening, by using the differential gene expression between GB#P3-shDDX39A and GB#P3-shNC. Based on molecular docking results, we selected Fluphenazine dihydrochloride (FPZ), an effective and orally active phenothiazine-class dopamine receptor antagonist with good blood-brain barrier permeability [27–29], making it a promising candidate for GB treatment. The optimal crystal structure of the DDX39A protein was retrieved from the PDB database, and the 3D structure of FPZ in SDF format was obtained from the PubChem database. To assess the binding affinity of this candidate drug to its target, we performed molecular docking analysis. The binding pose of the candidate drug with the protein and the binding energy of the optimal protein-ligand complex were determined using AutoDock Vina 1.2.2. We systematically analyzed the binding interface of the protein-ligand complex using PLIP, and interaction details were supplemented with PyMOL 2.5 software. The affinity score of FPZ with the protein was −8.4 kcal/mol. It formed hydrogen bonds (blue solid lines) with GLY-316, LEU-314, SER-59, and HIS-341, along with hydrophobic interactions (gray dashed lines) with GLU-165, THR-198, and ASP-337 (Fig. 7A, B). Furthermore, we employed SPR to validate whether FPZ directly binds to DDX39A. The results showed that FPZ and DDX39A can interact directly (KD = 1.46 ± 0.03 μM) (Fig. 7C). In cultured cells, the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of FPZ was 21.25 μM for GB#P3, 19.04 μM for GB #BG5, and 15.43 μM for GL261 (Figs. 7D, S5A). We subsequently selected concentrations of 15 μM and 20 μM for GB#P3 and GB#BG5 cells, and 10 μM and 15 μM for GL261 cells for functional assays. The mRNA and protein levels of WISP1 were both decreased in GB#P3, GB#BG5, and GL261 cells treated with FPZ (Figs. 7E, F, S5B, C). Furthermore, RIP assays demonstrated a significant reduction in the binding affinity between DDX39A and WISP1 mRNA after FPZ (15 μM) treatment, indicating that FPZ interferes with the interaction required for efficient splicing regulation (Figs. 7G, S5D). The p-AKT(Ser473) protein expression was decreased in GB#P3 and GB#BG5 cells treated with FPZ (Fig. S5E). Moreover, FPZ inhibited the proliferative capacity (GB#P3, GB#BG5 and GL261) (Figs. 7H, S5F-I), invasive ability (GB#P3) (Fig. 7I), and self-renewal potential (GB#P3, GB#BG5) (Figs. 7J, S5J), while promoting their apoptosis in the three cell lines (Fig. S5K-M). The addition of FPZ to the co-culture system of GB#P3 and THP1 significantly inhibited M2-like polarization of THP1 cells and promoted their polarization towards M1 (Fig. S5N). Based on the in vitro experiments, we assessed the anti-GB efficiency of FPZ in vivo using two GB models (the human GB#P3 and the syngeneic GL261 mouse model). In both models, FPZ significantly inhibited tumor growth (Figs. 7K, S6A) and significantly prolonged the overall survival (OS) (Figs. 7L, S6B). Discussion

DDX39A, an RNA helicase belonging to the DEAD-box protein family, is involved in RNA splicing, transport, and stability regulation [30–33]. Our study demonstrates that DDX39A is significantly upregulated in GB tissues, with expression levels correlating with tumor grade. This is consistent with findings in other cancers, where DDX39A has been implicated in tumor progression, including hepatocellular carcinoma [31], tongue squamous cell carcinoma [34], lung squamous cell carcinoma [35], and ovarian cancer [36]. The single-cell sequencing and immunofluorescence analyses revealed that DDX39A is primarily expressed in highly proliferative tumor cells and macrophages within the glioma ecosystem. This dual expression pattern suggested DDX39A might influence both the intrinsic properties of tumor cells and the tumor microenvironment, which we confirmed through further functional experiments.

Silencing DDX39A in GB cells significantly inhibited malignant traits essential for tumor progression. RNA sequencing of DDX39A-down-regulated cells revealed altered gene expression related to the cell cycle, cell division, innate immune response, apoptosis, and DNA replication. Additionally, DDX39A down-regulation suppressed proliferation in vitro and in vivo, invasion, and self-renewal capacities while promoting apoptosis in GB cells. These findings establish DDX39A as a critical factor in maintaining the aggressive behavior of GB cells.

Previous studies have shown that WISP1 has been implicated in GB progression and macrophage polarization [25, 26]. In the present work, our RNA-seq, RIP-seq, and minigene reporter assays provide convergent evidence that DDX39A directly modulates WISP1 exon inclusion, generating a specific isoform that more strongly promotes immunosuppressive macrophage polarization. In our mind, this mechanistic layer represents a novel connection between DDX39A and WISP1 activity that aligns with our results on the DDX39A regulatory network. WISP1 is overexpressed in GB and is associated with poor patient outcomes, which is consistent with our findings regarding DDX39A.

WISP1, a secreted protein in the CCN family, has been linked to advanced stages of various solid tumors and the inflammatory tumor microenvironment. Previous research has shown that glioma stem cells establish autocrine/paracrine signaling through WISP1 secretion, activating AKT pathways in both themselves and TAMs, supporting stem cell and macrophage survival [25]. Our study on the KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the RNA-seq data revealed a significant association between DDX39A-related genes and the PI3K/AKT signaling axis. Western blot analyses showed that the knockdown of DDX39A decreased the Akt phosphorylation (pAkt-Ser473) level in GB cells. Our rescue experiments confirmed that WISP1 overexpression could counteract the anti-tumor effects of DDX39A silencing, restoring proliferation, invasion, self-renewal capabilities, and activation of AKT in GB cells. This establishes the DDX39A-WISP1-AKT axis as a critical pathway in GB progression.

Furthermore, we uncovered DDX39A’s role in shaping the tumor microenvironment. DDX39A knockdown altered the polarization of macrophages from a tumor-promoting M2-like phenotype toward a tumor-suppressive M1-like phenotype, evident both in vitro and in orthotopic xenograft models. These findings highlight the multifaceted role of DDX39A in promoting tumor progression, both directly through tumor cells and indirectly by modulating the immune microenvironment. However, a limitation related the present work is that we have not determined a direct role of DDX39A in macrophage phenotype transformation despite its high expression in these cells in the current study. Future research should explore a regulatory role by knocking out DDX39A specifically in macrophages and by utilizing macrophage-specific knockout mice to understand its contribution within the tumor microenvironment better.

Based on the mechanistic insights provided, we screened for potential therapeutic agents targeting DDX39A regulation and identified FPZ as a promising candidate. This antipsychotic drug can cross the blood-brain barrier effectively and has shown anti-cancer potential in various tumors [27–29, 37–39]. Our molecular docking, surface plasmon resonance, and functional studies confirmed that FPZ binds directly to DDX39A, inhibiting its function and thereby reducing WISP1 expression across three GB cell models. In both in vitro and in vivo experiments, FPZ inhibited GB cell proliferation and invasion, promoted apoptosis, suppressed M2-like macrophage polarization, and significantly extended survival in orthotopic xenograft models. Mechanistically, our results also demonstrated that Fluphenazine could inhibit the activation of the PI3K-AKT pathway. Several studies have shown the important role of the AKT pathway in glioma progression [40–43], indicating that FPZ may have clinical translational significance.

In conclusion, our study reveals that the splicing factor DDX39A promotes GB progression through the DDX39A- WISP1-AKT axis, affecting both tumor cells and the immune microenvironment. FPZ, which targets DDX39A, shows promise as a repurposed drug for GB treatment. These findings offer a novel therapeutic approach for GB patients, potentially addressing the limitations of current treatment options.
