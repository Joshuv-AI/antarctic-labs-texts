Rhythmic movements mimicking tremor under different metronome conditions
Authors: Handan Uzunçakmak-Uyanık, Fatma Gökçem Yıldız, Gül Yalçın-Çakmaklı, Bülent Elibol, Çağrı Mesut Temuçin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRhythmic movements mimicking tremor are commonly used as a control condition in tremor physiology studies, yet the influence of different metronome modalities on performance has not been systematically examined. We compared auditory, visual, and combined auditory–visual metronome guidance in terms of frequency accuracy, temporal stability, and persistence following cueing removal to identify a suitable metronome condition for tremor simulation. Nineteen healthy volunteers performed 2 Hz thumb abduction–adduction movements under auditory, visual, and combined auditory–visual metronome conditions. Each trial included a 30 s metronome-guided phase followed by a 30 s post-metronome phase. Surface EMG recordings were analyzed using repeated-measures ANOVA and correlation analyses. Frequency accuracy and temporal stability did not differ significantly between metronome types. However, auditory and combined auditory–visual cueing descriptively showed lower frequency error than visual cueing during metronome guidance. Guided-to-unguided temporal stability correlations were significant for auditory and combined auditory–visual cueing, but not for visual cueing, with combined cueing showing a relatively higher correlation. Although no significant group-level superiority was observed, the overall pattern of findings suggested that the combined auditory–visual metronome may represent a suitable metronome condition for rhythmic movements mimicking tremor. Introduction

The metronome response task is a sustained attention task that requires generating a response synchronized with an auditory or visual metronome1. Many tremor physiology studies, particularly those investigating pathological tremors such as essential tremor and parkinsonian tremor using tremor resetting paradigms, involve voluntarily mimicked rhythmic tremor/voluntary rhythmic movements as a control group activity2–5. Although rhythmic movements mimicking tremor and the metronome response task originate from different experimental traditions, both paradigms involve rhythmic motor output synchronized to an external rhythm and require sustained attention and motor control. Conceptually, rhythmic movements mimicking tremor could be considered as a specific form of the metronome response task, suggesting a potential overlap in the underlying sensorimotor and attentional mechanisms.

A metronome, which visually represents a rhythmic hand movement, may act as a stimulus that activates the mirror neuron system through the perception of movement. The mirror neuron system reflects the activity of specialized neurons within motor regions and is activated not only during the performance of manual actions but also during their visual observation or auditory perception, a mechanism referred to as the resonance account6,7. Motor facilitation during action perception is supported by both top-down predictive coding and bottom-up resonance explanation7. In humans, motor system activity has been shown to be modulated by both visual and auditory action-related stimuli8,9. According to observation–execution matching theory, watching a motor action increases the excitability of the corresponding motor representation in the primary motor cortex (M1)10. Supporting these, motor evoked potential (MEP) amplitudes in hand muscles were found to be greater which indicates the task related excitability increase of M1 when observing hand and arm movements of others7,11,12.

Cross-frequency coupling is believed to organize neural timing across multiple timescales13, whereas cross-modal phase reset al.igns this timing to the external sensory events14. These two mechanisms, controlled by attention, are responsible for the functional communication between the auditory and visual cortices14–17. Intracortical alpha-band power, a cross-frequency coupling, is modulated by the delta phase. This cross-frequency coupling is influenced by attention. Entrainment is the basic mechanism of selective attention, and it is not limited to the local sensory cortex but also extends to the widespread cortical regions of the brain including auditory, visual, parietal, and frontal cortices13,18. These findings suggest that the entrainment is under the control of higher order attentional networks13. Accordingly, different metronome modalities (auditory, visual, bimodal such as combined auditory-visual) may theoretically engage these networks differently and potentially influence M1 excitability to varying degrees.

This study was designed as a preparatory step to determine an appropriate tremor simulation methodology in healthy participants for a main prospective study investigating the relationship between different tremor types and cortical silent periods using transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS). We aimed to compare auditory, visual, and combined auditory–visual metronome guidance during rhythmic movements mimicking tremor in healthy participants. Specifically, we evaluated frequency accuracy, temporal stability, and persistence of rhythmic performance following cue removal to identify a suitable metronome condition for the main study.

Based on previous findings from sensorimotor synchronization and multisensory integration research16,19, we hypothesized that metronome conditions with auditory cueing could result in more stable rhythmic performance. We further expected that combined auditory–visual cueing might provide a favorable balance between synchronization stability and theoretical considerations regarding cortical excitability.

Although auditory and visual guidance have been extensively studied in conventional sensorimotor tasks19,20, their effects on rhythmic movements mimicking tremor and post-metronome persistence have not been systematically examined. Therefore, the present study extends previous synchronization research into the context of tremor simulation and contributes to the methodological investigation of tremor physiology.

Methods

Participants and ethical approval

The study included a total of 19 healthy, right-handed volunteers aged 29–69 years (11 female, 8 male). None of the participants had any disease affecting their cognitive processes, and none had any prior experience with the study task. This sub-study is a preparatory study for the tremor simulation methodology for healthy volunteers to be included in the prospective study regarding evaluation of relationship between different tremor types and cortical silent period by TMS.

The study protocol was approved by the Hacettepe University Ethics Committee (Approval No: SBA 26/168). All procedures were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant national and international guidelines and regulations. Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Procedure

All studies were conducted in a semi-lit environment with individuals seated in a comfortable, adjustable chair designed for individual comfort, with elbow and wrist joints that could be fixed during the study. Ag–AgCl surface electrodes were used for the recordings. Active electrodes were placed on the skin over the muscle bellies of the abductor pollicis brevis and adductor pollicis muscles of the right hand; reference electrodes were positioned on the corresponding tendons, and a ground electrode was placed proximally to the active electrode. The four fingers excluding the thumb were secured together at the level of the metacarpophalangeal joints using a Velcro strap, and the forearm was stabilized to the arm-support apparatus of the chair using a second Velcro strap.

An auditory, visual, and combined auditory-visual metronome system was developed by Dr Ç.M.T. using an HTML-based software interface. Each metronome type was set to 2 Hz. Rhythmic broadband white noise presented at approximately 70 dB SPL was used as the auditory stimulus. Visual stimulation consisted of an animated illustration showing rhythmic right-thumb abduction and adduction movements, synchronized with the metronome frequency (Fig. 1). The visual stimulus was presented on a 24-inch LCD monitor positioned 70 cm in front of the participant. The visual stimulus was presented as a high-contrast image of a right hand against a dark background. In the combined condition, auditory and visual cueing were presented simultaneously.

Visual metronome interface used for tremor simulation.

Each participant was first shown and taught alternating and continuous tremor simulation movements in the form of thumb abduction and adduction for 30 s. Following this, participants performed rhythmic movements mimicking tremor, each lasting 30 s, guided by auditory, visual, and combined auditory-visual metronomes. When the 30-second interval of each metronome type ended, the corresponding metronome was turned off, and participants were asked to continue the tremor simulation movement for another 30 s at the frequency they had learned. This unguided phase was used to evaluate the regularity of the learned rhythm after removal of the cueing. The interval between each metronome type session was 1 min. The order of metronome conditions was randomized across participants to minimize potential order, learning, and fatigue effects. Tremor simulation signals were recorded and amplified by EMG machine (Digitimer D440-4) and stored using a CED 1401 data acquisition system (Cambridge Electronic Design, Cambridge, UK) and Signal software (version 6.06). Signals were sampled at 1 kHz and band-pass filtered using a finite impulse response filter at 10 and 1000 Hz. Signal analysis was performed offline on the same device at the end of the session.

Data acquisition and analysis

Tremor simulation EMG recordings obtained from each participant using all three metronome types were divided into two 30-second metronome-guided and post-metronome phases traces. Each tremor period was measured from the onset of one burst to the onset of the next and recorded (Fig. 2). Burst onsets were identified off-line by visual inspection of the EMG traces using Signal software. Measurements were performed manually based on the initial deflection of each EMG burst from baseline activity. For each phase (metronome-guided and post-metronome), the absolute deviations of all tremor periods from a 500 ms-target period (corresponding to 2 Hz) were calculated, and then the absolute deviation of their mean value from the 500 ms-target period was calculated. This error metric, calculated to assess frequency accuracy, was called AbsError. Temporal stability was assessed using the coefficient of variation (CV), defined as the ratio of the standard deviation of the tremor periods to their mean periods (SD/mean). CV provided a normalized measure of rhythmic variability.

EMG traces from the right adductor pollicis (ADC 2) and abductor pollicis brevis (ADC 3) muscles showing simulated tremor bursts. Simulated tremor period measurement is demonstrated in the abductor pollicis brevis (ADC 3) channel.

Data analyses were conducted using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 25.0 for Windows (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Descriptive data are reported as mean ± standard deviation. The statistical significance level was set at α = 0.05.

Separate two-way repeated-measures analyses of variance (RM-ANOVAs) were conducted for frequency accuracy (AbsError) and temporal stability (CV). Both analyses included two within-subject factors: state (metronome-guided vs. post-metronome) and metronome type (auditory, visual, and combined auditory-visual). Mauchly’s test was used to assess sphericity, and Greenhouse–Geisser corrections were applied when the sphericity assumption was violated. Partial eta squared (η²p) values are reported as measures of effect size.

Considering the performance during metronome-guided phase could more directly reflect pacing effectiveness, an additional Metronome-guided_Only RM-ANOVAs sub-analysis was conducted separately for AbsError and CV to compare metronome types during active synchronization. Estimated marginal means were examined to determine the direction of the observed effects.

To assess the persistence in the post-metronome phase, AbsError values of the metronome-guided and post-metronome phases were compared separately for each metronome type. To evaluate whether temporal stability changed in the post-metronome phase, CV values of the metronome-guided and post-metronome phases were also compared. Paired sample t-tests and Wilcoxon signed rank tests were applied for these comparisons, depending on the normality of the distribution.

Associations between metronome-guided and post-metronome phases were assessed using Spearman correlation analyses to examine whether rhythmic performance was preserved in post-metronome phase. Additional Spearman correlations were performed between AbsError and CV values from metronome-guided epochs to explore the relationship between frequency accuracy and temporal stability.

Frequency accuracy (AbsError)

The RM-ANOVAs (type × state) with AbsError revealed no significant main effect of metronome type on frequency accuracy (F (2,36) = 2.56, p = 0.091, η²p = 0.125), indicating that auditory, visual, and combined auditory-visual metronomes did not differ significantly regarding their ability to reduce frequency error. Similarly, no main effect of state (metronome-guided vs. post-metronome) was observed (F (1,18) = 0.277, p = 0.605, η²p = 0.015), suggesting that removal of the metronome did not significantly deteriorate frequency accuracy. The type × state interaction was also not significant (F (1.487,26.771) = 1.259, p = 0.29, η²p = 0.065), showing that the effect of metronome presence did not differ across the types. Metronome-guided_Only RM-ANOVAs sub-analysis showed a borderline effect of metronome type under the sphericity assumed condition (F (2,36) = 3.257, p = 0.05, η²p = 0.153). Although pairwise comparisons did not reach significance after Bonferroni correction, the main effect of metronome type showed a moderate effect size. Estimated marginal means demonstrated descriptively higher frequency error with the visual metronome, whereas auditory and combined auditory–visual metronomes showed lower and similar AbsError values.

Because AbsError values showed normal distribution according to the Shapiro–Wilk test, paired sample t-tests were performed to compare metronome-guided and post-metronome epochs for each metronome type separately. These analyses revealed no significant differences between the metronome-guided and post-metronome phases in AbsError for auditory, visual, or combined auditory-visual metronomes (all p > 0.05).

Strong correlations between metronome-guided and post-metronome phases were observed for AbsError in all metronome types (auditory: ρ = 0.944, p < 0.001; visual: ρ = 0.904, p < 0.001; combined auditory-visual: ρ = 0.938, p < 0.001), supporting the preservation of individual frequency accuracy profiles after metronome was switched off.

Temporal stability (CV)

The RM-ANOVAs (type × state) on CV revealed no significant main effects of metronome type (F (2,36) = 0.359, p = 0.701, η²p = 0.02), state (F (1,18) = 0.837, p = 0.372, η²p = 0.044), and type × state (F (2,36) = 0.622, p = 0.542, η²p = 0.033) interaction. An additional metronome-guided_Only RM-ANOVAs with CV also demonstrated no significant differences among metronome types (F(2,36) = 0.851, p = 0.435, η²p = 0.045). Additionally, as CV values deviated from normality, Wilcoxon signed rank tests were applied. They showed no significant differences in CV between the metronome-guided and post-metronome phases for any metronome type.

Significant correlations between metronome-guided and post-metronome CV values were observed for the auditory (ρ = 0.548, p = 0.015) and combined auditory–visual metronomes (ρ = 0.685, p = 0.001), whereas the correlation was weak and non-significant for visual metronome (ρ = 0.370, p = 0.11).

Relationship between frequency accuracy (AbsError) and temporal stability (CV)

Significant positive correlations were observed for auditory metronome (ρ = 0.723, p < 0.001) and combined auditory-visual metronome (ρ = 0.636, p = 0.003). In contrast, the association between AbsError and CV during visual metronome was weaker and did not reach statistical significance (ρ = 0.386, p = 0.10).

Discussion

Our main results showed that neither the metronome state nor metronome type altered the frequency accuracy. Furthermore, frequency accuracy also remained stable during the post-metronome phase, and this persistence did not differ across metronome types. During metronome-guided performance, synchronization accuracy was similar for auditory and combined auditory–visual types, whereas visual pacing descriptively showed higher deviation from the target frequency. Although group-level differences were not statistically significant, subtle modality related effects may have remained undetected, particularly given the relatively small sample size. In this context, the borderline metronome type effect observed during the metronome-guided phase may still be informative for selecting metronome conditions in tremor simulation studies despite the absence of clear differences across metronome conditions. Temporal stability was not significantly affected by metronome type or state at the group level. However, guided-to-unguided correlations for temporal stability were significant for auditory and combined auditory–visual metronomes, whereas the correlation did not reach significance for the visual metronome. Descriptively, the combined auditory–visual metronome exhibited a relatively higher guided-to-unguided temporal stability correlation. In addition, auditory and combined auditory-visual metronomes showed strong correlations between frequency accuracy and temporal stability, while visual metronome exhibited a dissociation between these two dimensions, suggesting potential type-specific differences in timing mechanisms.

Above findings are generally consistent with the previous research on type specific differences in sensorimotor synchronization and stability. Whitton and Jiang demonstrated that synchronization to external rhythmic stimuli is significantly more accurate for auditory than for visual flashing sequences19, in line with our descriptive findings. Similarly, Vaz et al., reported reduced stability during visually guided bimanual coordination tasks21. Moreover, Comstock and Balasubramaniam showed that visual sensorimotor synchronization is characterized by a weak coupling between error detection and motor correction22. This pattern parallels the dissociation regarding frequency accuracy and temporal stability observed with visual cueing in our study. With respect to visual cueing, Iversen et al., suggested that a visual metronome can only integrate strongly with the motor system when an event-based (movement and collision-involved) visual structure is provided; otherwise, stable synchronization cannot be achieved20. Accordingly, we used a visual metronome incorporating hand movement illustrations. However, even under this condition, the visual cueing descriptively showed lower synchronization performance than auditory cueing. Although persistence did not differ significantly between metronome types during the post-metronome phase, Kennedy et al., reported that retention performance in an auditory–visual model condition was more stable than in an auditory-only model condition23.

Gamma-band increases and signal amplification occur in the locus where attention is focused24, whereas the alpha-band is associated with the suppression of disregarded information25. Furthermore, the phase/amplitude cross-frequency coupling mechanism, in which the amplitude of faster rhythms (alpha, beta, and gamma) is modulated by the phase of slower oscillations (delta or theta), is thought to regulate the “excitability state” of both widespread cortical networks and local neural clusters26. The attended stimulus aligns with the excitatory phase13, and that entrainment-based timing, which forms the basic mechanism of selective attention13, operates most effectively within the delta frequency range (1–3 Hz)13. Considering these, our metronome types were set to 2 Hz.

Audiovisual interactions involve both facilitatory and suppressive mechanisms that depend on attentional demands and task context. Integration of information across sensory modalities extends from early sensory regions to higher order association areas27,28. Early evidence from Romei et al., showed that applying TMS to the visual cortex in the presence of only auditory stimuli shortened reaction time, suggesting that visual cortex stimulation can enhance auditory perception29. In the same study, no additional effect was observed when combined auditory and visual stimuli were presented, possibly reflecting the brain’s natural integration of these two senses29. Subsequent studies further demonstrated cross-modal facilitation. Romei et al., reported that structured looming sounds considerably enhanced visual cortex excitability30 and Bolognini et al., showed that auditory stimulation could facilitate the perception of subthreshold TMS-induced phosphenes27.

In addition to facilitation, audiovisual interactions also involve inhibitory mechanisms. Gomez-Ramirez et al., found relatively increased alpha-band amplitude in the auditory cortex during visual attention, coinciding with auditory stimuli that were meant to be ignored13. This interpretation was supported by later findings showing that increased alpha power in the auditory cortex during visual tasks reflects active suppression of auditory information31. More recent evidence from Pomper et al., highlighted the dominance of auditory cueing, demonstrating faster response times and higher hit rates with rhythmic auditory stimulation compared to visual stimulation16. They further showed that auditory stimulation produced similar effects across both auditory and visual target discrimination tasks, whereas visual stimulation was largely restricted to the visual target modality16. Together, these findings demonstrate that audiovisual interaction exhibits a flexible balance between facilitation and suppression to optimize perceptual performance.

To minimize potential excitability related effects, white noise was used as the auditory stimulus in our study. Notably, among the stationary and structured sounds used by Romei et al., stationary white noise produced the smallest increase in visual cortex excitability relative to baseline30.

Because our study task involved movement, findings from motor imagery literature are also relevant. Motor imagery is known to increase motor cortical excitability through accessing the motor system32. Notably, in a motor imagery study of index finger abduction guided by visual, auditory, and combined auditory-visual cueing, it was shown that auditory cueing elicited the highest excitability in older healthy participants, whereas visual cueing was more effective in younger participants32. They also noted that combined auditory-visual cueing is less effective than a single strategy32. In contrast, Ikeda et al., reported higher MEP amplitudes during combined auditory–visual cueing than during single modality conditions33. Cengiz et al., showed that simultaneous motor imagery and action observation significantly increased corticomotor excitability more than action observation alone34. Simultaneous visual and auditory stimuli that are congruent with a perceived action significantly increase excitability35,36. Together, these previous findings provided a theoretical framework regarding the potential excitability related effects that might accompany different metronome conditions combined with movement.

On the other hand, Alaerts et al., demonstrated that adding an incongruent auditory stimulus to a visual action failed to increase corticomotor excitability35. Specifically, MEP amplitudes under the incongruent bimodal condition [VcrushAwater] did not differ statistically from those obtained under the unimodal visual condition [Vcrush]35. Conversely, a combination of an incongruent visual stimulus with an auditory action sound led to a borderline decrease in corticomotor excitability35. MEP amplitudes during [VfootAcrush] were slightly lower than those obtained under the unimodal auditory condition [Acrush]35. Bolognini et al., declared that suprathreshold TMS stimuli, suppressed the phosphene perception in the cross-modal trials (occipital TMS with auditory stimulus)27. It seems that incongruent audiovisual stimuli may either have no effect on or even decrease motor excitability. In our study, we based our methodology on the incongruent combined auditory-visual stimulus condition which is likely to produce a moderate level of interaction. This inference is partly supported by animal data showing maximal modulation in the auditory cortex (but in the direction of suppression) when the visual stimulus was presented 20–80 ms before the auditory stimulus28.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the present findings. First, the relatively small sample size may have limited the statistical power to detect subtle differences between metronome conditions, particularly given the generally small-to-moderate effect sizes observed in some analyses. Therefore, the predominance of non-significant group-level findings should not necessarily be interpreted as evidence of complete equivalence between metronome modalities. Second, this study was designed as a preparatory methodological investigation in healthy participants rather than a definitive comparison study. Finally, although exploratory correlation analyses suggested modality related differences in persistence and temporal stability, these findings should be confirmed in larger samples.

Conclusion

This preparatory study aimed to explore suitable metronome conditions for tremor simulation by integrating our findings with existing excitability evidence from the literature prior to proceeding to the main experimental protocol. Although frequency accuracy and temporal stability were not significantly influenced by metronome type at the group level, a borderline type effect was observed during metronome-guided phase. Auditory and combined auditory–visual metronomes descriptively showed lower frequency error compared with visual metronome. In addition, exploratory correlation analyses suggested potential differences across metronome modalities at the individual level. Visual metronome was associated with larger frequency errors and weaker preservation of temporal stability following metronome offset, whereas auditory metronome showed better persistence. Notably, among the tested conditions, the combined auditory–visual metronome showed a relatively consistent overall pattern, including low frequency error and significant temporal stability correlations following metronome offset. Taken together with theoretical considerations from previous multisensory and cortical excitability literature, these preliminary findings suggest that the combined auditory–visual metronome may represent a suitable candidate for the main study protocol. Additionally, considering that tremor related motor output may reflect transient neuromuscular states and physiological modulation37,38, establishing a stable and reproducible tremor simulation paradigm may be important for future studies investigating state-dependent motor system changes.
