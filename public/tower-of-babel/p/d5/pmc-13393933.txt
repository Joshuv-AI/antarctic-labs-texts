Effects of intraoperative inhalational versus intravenous anesthesia on perioperative neurological outcomes and long-term prognosis in patients with moyamoya disease undergoing revascularization surgery: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Mengting Hu, Ye Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMoyamoya disease (MMD) is a rare cerebrovascular disorder characterized by progressive stenosis of intracranial carotid arteries and abnormal collateral network formation. Surgical revascularization is the standard treatment for stroke prevention, but the optimal intraoperative anesthetic strategy to minimize perioperative neurological complications remains controversial. This meta-analysis compares inhalational versus intravenous anesthesia for perioperative neurological outcomes in MMD patients undergoing revascularization.MethodsWe systematically searched PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science from database inception to April 2026, including randomized controlled trials and high-quality observational studies comparing the two anesthesia regimens. Two investigators independently completed literature screening, data extraction, and risk of bias assessment. Random-effects models were applied to pool effect sizes. Primary outcomes included postoperative transient neurological events (TNEs), post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) delirium, and postoperative stroke. Evidence quality was graded using the GRADE system.ResultsEleven studies (1673 patients, 2232 procedures) were included, with 6 studies constituting the core quantitative analysis set. Outcome coverage varied: 5 studies reported TNEs, 2 reported postoperative stroke, and only 1 single-center study reported PACU delirium. Pooled results showed no significant intergroup difference in TNEs (OR = 0.87, 95% CI: 0.55—1.38, P = 0.56, I² = 61%) or postoperative stroke (OR = 1.05, 95% CI: 0.50—2.17, P = 0.91, I² = 0%). Based on limited pediatric data, inhalational anesthesia may be associated with increased PACU delirium risk in children. Qualitative evidence indicated both regimens maintained stable cerebral oxygenation, while propofol-based intravenous anesthesia had advantages in reducing intracranial pressure and improving surgical field exposure.ConclusionsCurrent low-to-very-low quality evidence shows no significant difference in major perioperative neurological complications between the two anesthetic regimens for MMD revascularization. Intravenous anesthesia has potential advantages in intraoperative cerebral physiology and surgical conditions, and may be considered preferentially for children at high delirium risk, with limited evidence strength.Systematic review registrationhttps://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/, identifier CRD420251272930.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-026-04085-3.

Introduction

Moyamoya disease primarily affects the terminal segment of the internal carotid artery and the proximal segments of the anterior and middle cerebral arteries. As the disease progresses, an abnormal collateral vascular network forms at the skull base. Clinical manifestations include recurrent transient ischemic attacks, ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke, and cognitive decline, with a significantly higher incidence in East Asian populations [1–3]. Revascularization surgery, including direct revascularization, indirect revascularization, and combined procedures, effectively improves cerebral perfusion and reduces long-term stroke risk, and is currently the standard treatment for moyamoya disease [4, 5]. Direct revascularization can rapidly increase cerebral blood flow, but postoperative cerebral hyperperfusion syndrome (CHS) and related intracranial hemorrhage are the most severe acute perioperative complications and the core challenge of anesthetic management.

Due to severely impaired cerebrovascular autoregulation in patients with moyamoya disease, perioperative cerebral blood flow and cerebral oxygen supply-demand balance are highly susceptible to multiple factors such as anesthesia modality and blood pressure control [6, 7]. Intraoperative hypotension-induced cerebral hypoperfusion can cause ischemic stroke in the distal ipsilateral hemisphere or the contralateral hemisphere, and is an important trigger of perioperative ischemic complications. Currently, two mainstream clinical anesthesia regimens are used: inhalational anesthesia represented by sevoflurane, desflurane, and isoflurane, and total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA) based on propofol. Pharmacological studies show that inhalational anesthetics dilate cerebral vessels and increase cerebral blood flow, while propofol reduces cerebral metabolic rate, constricts cerebral vessels, and lowers intracranial pressure [8, 9].

The impact of the two regimens on perioperative neurological outcomes has long been controversial. Some studies suggest that inhalational anesthesia improves perfusion in lesional areas and reduces the risk of postoperative transient neurological events [10], while other evidence links inhalational anesthesia to an increased risk of postoperative delirium [11, 12]. Given the limited sample size and inconsistent conclusions of individual studies, this study systematically integrates the latest clinical evidence, strictly compares the effects of the two anesthesia regimens on core neurological complications in patients with moyamoya disease, and supplements evidence on cerebral physiology, intraoperative conditions, and postoperative recovery quality to provide an evidence-based reference for clinical anesthesia regimen selection.

Materials and methods

Literature search strategy and inclusion/exclusion Criteria

Search strategy

This study was conducted in strict accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guideline [13]. We systematically searched PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science from database inception to April 2026. Search terms included “moyamoya disease”, “revascularization”, “anesthesia”, “inhalational”, “intravenous”, “propofol”, “sevoflurane”, “desflurane”, “complication”, “neurological outcome”, etc., adapted to the search rules of different databases.

Inclusion criteria

(1)Study population: Patients clinically or imaging-confirmed with moyamoya disease undergoing revascularization surgery; (2)Intervention: Inhalational anesthesia for maintenance; control: Intravenous anesthesia for maintenance; (3)Study type: RCT, prospective or retrospective cohort study; (4)Outcome: At least one of the following outcomes reported: postoperative transient neurological events (TNEs), PACU delirium, postoperative stroke; (5)Data availability: 2 × 2 table data can be directly extracted or converted for effect size pooling.

Studies reporting only physiological indicators such as cerebral oxygenation and cerebral blood flow without clinical complications were classified as supplementary studies and included only for qualitative systematic review. Long-term prognostic outcomes were not included in quantitative meta-analysis when there was no standardized poolable data due to large heterogeneity in follow-up time points and evaluation criteria.

Exclusion criteria

Reviews, case reports, basic experimental studies, studies using combined inhalational and intravenous anesthesia, and studies with incomplete data and unavailable valid 2 × 2 table data. Studies reporting comprehensive neurological outcomes without distinguishing transient neurological events from permanent cerebral infarction were excluded from quantitative meta-analysis and retained only for descriptive analysis.

Data extraction

Two trained investigators independently performed literature screening and data extraction, followed by cross-checking; disagreements were resolved through discussion or third-party arbitration. Extracted contents included: (1)Basic study characteristics: first author, publication year, country, study design, sample size; (2)Patient baseline characteristics: age stratification (adult/pediatric), surgical procedure; (3)Anesthesia intervention regimen: specific inhalational anesthetic, specific intravenous anesthetic; (4)Outcome data: number of events and corresponding total sample size for each core outcome.

Primary outcomes were clearly defined as follows: Postoperative transient neurological events: A composite outcome including ischemic transient ischemic attack, transient neurological deficit, and transient epileptic seizure. All included studies reported this outcome as a composite, without separate counts for each subtype. PACU delirium: Delirium clinically diagnosed during the patient’s stay in the post-anesthesia care unit. Postoperative stroke: Postoperative ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke, including cerebral infarction with definitive imaging evidence.

For studies using surgical hemisphere/unilateral surgery as the statistical unit, data were extracted according to the original study’s unit of analysis; patients with bilateral lesions undergoing staged surgery were counted as independent surgical procedures.

Risk of bias assessment

For RCTs, the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (ROB 2) tool was used to evaluate five domains: randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selective reporting of outcomes. Overall risk of bias was finally judged as “low risk”, “some concerns”, or “high risk”.

For retrospective cohort studies and crossover studies, the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) was used for evaluation, with 8 items across three dimensions: selection of study subjects, comparability between groups, and outcome measurement, with a total score of 9 points. A score ≥ 7 was defined as a high-quality study.

Statistical methods

Statistical analysis was performed using RevMan 5.4 and R 4.5.2 software. For binary outcomes, odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated using the Mantel-Haenszel method, and pooled effect sizes were estimated using a random-effects model, accounting for both within-study and between-study heterogeneity.

The I² statistic was used to assess between-study heterogeneity, with I² = 25%, 50%, and 75% corresponding to low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. Prespecified subgroup analysis stratified study populations into adult and pediatric subgroups, and a subgroup interaction test was used to assess the statistical significance of the difference in effect sizes between the two groups.

Sensitivity analysis included two parts: (1)Leave-one-out method: each study was excluded one by one and effect sizes were re-pooled to assess the impact of a single study on the overall result; (2)Only studies using individual patients as the unit of analysis were included for re-pooling to assess the impact of surgical hemisphere-based units on data clustering. Funnel plot combined with Egger’s weighted linear regression test was used to assess publication bias. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Studies with zero events were processed using the continuity correction method (adding 0.5 to each group).

The Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) system was used to grade the quality of evidence for core outcomes from the dimensions of study design, risk of bias, heterogeneity, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias, divided into four grades: high, moderate, low, and very low. The quality of evidence from observational studies starts at low grade and is downgraded based on risk of bias, heterogeneity, imprecision, and other factors.

Results

Literature screening

A total of 77 records were initially retrieved: 44 from PubMed, 8 from Embase, 12 from Cochrane Library, 11 from Web of Science, 1 from Springer Nature Link, and 1 from Ovid. After deduplication, 13 duplicate records and 4 trial registry records marked ineligible by automated tools were excluded, leaving 60 records for title and abstract screening.

After initial screening, 46 records that did not meet the inclusion criteria were excluded, and 14 full texts were retrieved for re-screening. During full-text re-screening, 3 records were excluded: 1 with incomplete data and unavailable valid 2 × 2 table data, and 2 using combined inhalational and intravenous anesthesia. Finally, 11 eligible studies were included, including 3 RCTs, 7 retrospective cohort studies, and 1 prospective self-controlled crossover study. The literature screening process is shown in Fig. 1.

Core meta-analysis results

Outcome coverage varied across the 6 core quantitative studies, and no study reported all three core outcomes simultaneously. Among the 3 included RCTs, only 1 reported postoperative transient neurological events and was included in the core quantitative analysis; the remaining 2 only reported physiological surrogate endpoints such as cerebral oxygenation and surgical field conditions, and were included in the qualitative supplementary analysis. Each outcome was analyzed independently as follows.

Postoperative transient neurological events

Postoperative transient neurological events are a composite clinical outcome including two manifestations with distinct pathological mechanisms: ischemic transient ischemic attack and transient epileptic seizure. The former is mostly induced by perioperative cerebral hypoperfusion and cerebral vasospasm, while the latter is mostly related to cerebral hyperperfusion, increased cortical excitability, and transient blood-brain barrier damage. All included studies reported this outcome as a composite without providing independent event counts for each subtype; therefore, subtype-specific quantitative analysis could not be performed.

Five studies were included for this outcome, involving 1002 patients and 1292 surgical procedures. Pooled analysis showed no statistically significant difference in the risk of postoperative transient neurological events between inhalational and intravenous anesthesia (OR = 0.87, 95% CI: 0.55—1.38, P = 0.56). There was moderate between-study heterogeneity (I² = 61%, P for heterogeneity = 0.04). The forest plot is shown in Fig. 2.

Subgroup analysis stratified by age showed: the adult subgroup included 3 studies with 552 patients and 571 procedures, with a pooled OR of 0.82 (95% CI: 0.28—2.71, P = 0.71) and heterogeneity I² = 72%; the pediatric subgroup included 2 studies with 450 patients and 721 procedures, with a pooled OR of 0.89 (95% CI: 0.48—1.64, P = 0.71) and heterogeneity I² = 67%. No significant difference between the two anesthesia regimens was observed in either subgroup. The subgroup interaction test showed no statistically significant difference in effect sizes between the adult and pediatric subgroups (χ² = 0.02, df = 1, P = 0.89), indicating that age stratification had no significant modifying effect on the association between anesthesia modality and TNEs risk. PACU Delirium

Only one single-center retrospective study (Liu et al., [15]) reported PACU delirium. Based on single-center retrospective pediatric data, inhalational anesthesia may be associated with an increased risk of PACU delirium in children ( first-stage surgery: adjusted OR = 2.17, 95% CI: 1.32—3.57, P = 0.003; second-stage surgery: adjusted OR = 4.00, 95% CI: 1.89—8.33, P < 0.001). This conclusion has a weak evidence base and should be extrapolated to other populations with caution.

Postoperative stroke

Two studies reported postoperative stroke data, involving 479 patients and 498 procedures. Pooled analysis showed no statistically significant difference in the risk of postoperative stroke between the two groups (OR = 1.05, 95% CI: 0.50—2.17, P = 0.91). There was no between-study heterogeneity (I² = 0%, P for heterogeneity = 0.67). The forest plot is shown in Fig. 4.

GRADE Evidence quality assessment

The GRADE system was used to grade the quality of evidence for the three core outcomes, and the results are shown in Table 4. The quality of evidence for postoperative transient neurological events was graded as low, due to predominantly observational studies, moderate heterogeneity, and wide confidence intervals. The quality of evidence for postoperative stroke was graded as very low, due to small sample size, few events, and insufficient estimation precision. The quality of evidence for PACU delirium was graded as very low, due to only one single-center study and limited population representativeness.

Publication bias

A funnel plot was drawn and Egger’s regression test was performed for postoperative transient neurological events. Limited by the small number of included studies (only 5), the statistical power of the test is limited and serves only as a descriptive reference. The results showed that the funnel plot was roughly symmetric, with an Egger’s test intercept P of 0.7966. No obvious trend of publication bias has been found so far, but the possibility of publication bias cannot be completely ruled out. The funnel plot is shown in Fig. 5.

Fig. 5Funnel plot of postoperative TNEs

Funnel plot of postoperative TNEs

In the study reporting multivariate adjusted odds ratio (Kaku et al., [10]), the adjusted OR (inhalational vs. intravenous anesthesia) was 0.26 (95% CI: 0.08—0.81), which was highly consistent with the crude OR of 0.28 (95% CI: 0.09—1.10) in terms of effect direction and magnitude.

Sensitivity analysis

No additional sensitivity analysis was performed for postoperative stroke and PACU delirium due to the small number of included studies. Two sensitivity analyses were performed for postoperative transient neurological events:

Leave-one-out analysis: After excluding each study one by one and re-pooling, the direction of all pooled effect sizes did not reverse, and all 95% CIs included the null line of 1. There was no statistically significant difference between groups, consistent with the main analysis conclusion. Only after excluding Kaku et al. [10] did between-study heterogeneity decrease from 61% to 35%, suggesting this study is the main source of heterogeneity.

Unit-of-analysis sensitivity analysis: Re-pooling only studies using individual patients as the unit of analysis showed no significant difference in TNEs risk between the two groups (OR = 1.13, 95% CI: 0.76—1.68, P = 0.55), consistent with the main analysis conclusion, suggesting that data clustering does not affect the robustness of core results.

Additional sensitivity analysis was performed by excluding the two studies whose primary aim was not to compare anesthesia techniques (Matsuura et al. [16]; Zhu et al. [17]). The pooled OR for TNEs remained non-significant (OR = 0.82, 95% CI: 0.28–2.37, P = 0.71), which was fully consistent with the primary analysis, confirming that these two studies did not materially alter the overall result.

Overall, the core conclusions of this study are robust. Moderate heterogeneity mainly comes from clinical management and methodological differences between studies, rather than publication bias (Fig. 6).

Supplementary studies and secondary outcome results

Neurological complication verification

The retrospective study by Adachi et al. [21] included 72 patients with moyamoya disease and reported a composite outcome of TIA and postoperative stroke without distinguishing between the two. The results showed no significant difference in the incidence of composite neurological complications between the two anesthesia regimens, consistent with the core conclusion of this meta-analysis.

Cerebral oxygenation and cerebral hemodynamic parameters

Three supplementary studies explored the effects of the two anesthesia modalities on intraoperative cerebral physiological parameters.

Seshan et al. [20] included 20 pediatric patients with moyamoya disease aged 1–18 years and monitored regional cerebral oxygen saturation (rSO₂) via near-infrared spectroscopy. The results showed no significant difference in rSO₂ at each monitoring time point between the two groups; cerebral oxygenation was maintained within the normal range, and no cerebral desaturation events were observed, suggesting that both anesthesia regimens can maintain adequate intraoperative cerebral oxygen supply-demand balance in pediatric patients.

The crossover study by Kikuta et al. [6] included 34 procedures in 23 patients, with arterial carbon dioxide partial pressure strictly maintained at 38–40 mmHg to exclude ventilation interference. The results showed that compared with sevoflurane, propofol anesthesia significantly reduced intracranial pressure (3.35 ± 3.59 mmHg vs. 6.33 ± 4.00 mmHg, P < 0.001) and significantly increased frontal cortical cerebral blood flow (40.1 ± 24.2 mL·100 g− 1·min− 1 vs. 30.9 ± 15.7 mL·100 g− 1·min− 1, P = 0.0138); there was no significant difference in parietotemporal cerebral blood flow between the two groups. This phenomenon may be related to the heterogeneous cerebral effects of propofol: it reduces overall intracranial pressure by lowering global cerebral metabolic rate and constricting normal brain tissue vessels, while relatively improving perfusion in frontal areas enriched with diseased vessels through autoregulatory mechanisms; the two effects are not contradictory. The results suggest that propofol can reduce intracranial pressure while maintaining normal cerebral perfusion, consistent with previous study conclusions. Its effect of improving frontal perfusion may help reduce the risk of ischemia in this region.

The retrospective study by Hrishi et al. [22] included 47 patients undergoing combined revascularization. The results showed that the intravenous anesthesia group had better cerebral oxygen metabolic status: jugular venous oxygen saturation (SjvO₂) was significantly higher, while arteriovenous oxygen difference, cerebral metabolic rate of oxygen, cerebral oxygen extraction rate, and lactate-oxygen index were significantly lower than in the inhalational anesthesia group, suggesting that propofol can effectively optimize intraoperative cerebral oxygen supply-demand balance. There were no statistically significant differences in the incidence of postoperative neurological events, or modified Rankin Scale scores at discharge and 3 months postoperatively between the two groups.

Intraoperative surgical conditions

The prospective pilot study by Ankolekar et al. [19] included 17 patients and compared the effects of desflurane and propofol on intraoperative brain relaxation and surgical field exposure. The results showed that the brain relaxation score was significantly higher in the desflurane group (3.6 ± 0.5 vs. 2.1 ± 0.3, P = 0.001); 70% of patients in the desflurane group required remedial measures for intraoperative brain bulging, while no patient in the propofol group required such intervention.

The results suggest that propofol-based intravenous anesthesia provides better brain relaxation, reduces the risk of intraoperative brain bulging, and is more conducive to fine operations such as vascular anastomosis. It should be noted that the above indicators are intraoperative operation-related surrogate endpoints, and their impact on final neurological outcomes needs to be verified by large-sample studies.

Cerebral hyperperfusion and hemorrhagic complications

Postoperative cerebral hyperperfusion syndrome is a severe acute complication of direct revascularization. Its core pathological basis is impaired cerebrovascular autoregulation caused by chronic hypoperfusion, and a sudden postoperative increase in cerebral blood flow exceeding the compensatory threshold, which can progress to intracranial hemorrhage in severe cases [23, 24]. Current comprehensive clinical prevention and control measures for cerebral hyperperfusion include strict goal-directed blood pressure management, intraoperative and postoperative cerebral perfusion monitoring, and appropriate application of cerebral protective drugs; optimization of anesthesia regimens is an important part of the perioperative comprehensive prevention and control system.

Among the included core studies, only Cheng et al. [14] reported overall 7-day postoperative stroke events (including ischemic and hemorrhagic subtypes), with no statistically significant difference in the incidence of hemorrhagic stroke between the two groups. The remaining studies did not standardly define and report cerebral hyperperfusion syndrome as an independent outcome; therefore, quantitative meta-analysis could not be performed.

Combined with indirect intraoperative cerebral physiological evidence and pharmacological mechanism analysis: propofol can dose-dependently reduce cerebral blood flow and cerebral metabolic rate, which is consistent with the principle of cerebral hyperperfusion prevention and control; inhalational anesthetics at concentrations above 1 minimum alveolar concentration (MAC) have cerebral vasodilatory effects, which may theoretically increase cerebral perfusion load [25]. Current physiological indicators suggest that the cerebral blood flow regulation characteristics of propofol-based intravenous anesthesia are more suitable for cerebral hyperperfusion prevention and control, but this has not been supported by direct clinical outcome evidence.

Long-term neurological prognosis

Two supplementary studies reported long-term neurological prognosis, with evaluation tools including the modified Rankin Scale and Extended Glasgow Outcome Scale, and follow-up time ranging from discharge to 3 months postoperatively. Due to inconsistent follow-up time points and large differences in outcome evaluation criteria and statistical forms across studies, standardized effect sizes could not be extracted for quantitative pooling, and only descriptive evaluation was performed.

Ankolekar et al. [19] showed no statistically significant differences in mRS and GOS-E scores at discharge and 1 month postoperatively between the two groups; Hrishi et al. [22] showed no significant difference in mRS scores at 3 months postoperatively between the two groups. Overall, existing studies have not found a significant difference in the impact of the two anesthesia modalities on long-term neurological prognosis after moyamoya disease surgery.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the largest systematic review and meta-analysis to date focusing on anesthesia modalities for moyamoya disease, with the most comprehensive coverage of perioperative neurological outcomes, including 11 studies, 1673 patients, and 2232 surgical procedures. It integrates both core clinical outcomes and supplementary physiological evidence, making the conclusions more referential. It should be noted that postoperative neurological prognosis in patients with moyamoya disease is jointly affected by multiple factors such as preoperative disease severity, surgical procedure, perioperative hemodynamic management, and anesthesia strategy; anesthesia modality is only one component of perioperative management. This study only compares the dimension of anesthesia modality, aiming to provide evidence-based reference for clinical regimen selection, rather than attributing prognosis differences solely to anesthesia modality.

Safety profile of major neurological complications

Meta-analysis results showed that compared with intravenous anesthesia, inhalational anesthesia did not significantly increase the risk of postoperative transient neurological events and overall stroke after moyamoya revascularization. Limited by sample size and number of events, it cannot be confirmed that the two regimens are completely equivalent at present; it can only be stated that existing evidence has not detected significant intergroup differences. This study did not conduct an equivalence test. Limited by sample size, it cannot confirm the clinical equivalence of the two anesthesia modalities, and larger non-inferiority studies are needed for verification in the future.

As mentioned above, TNEs are a composite outcome including two subtypes (ischemic and epileptic) with significantly different pathological mechanisms [26]. From the perspective of pharmacological properties and pathological mechanisms, the two anesthesia modalities may have opposite potential effects on the two subtypes, which is the core reason for the non-significant difference in the pooled outcome.

For the ischemic subtype, inhalational anesthetics can dose-dependently dilate cerebral vessels, theoretically benefiting by increasing cerebral blood flow and improving hypoperfusion in lesional areas [8, 10]; however, they also carry the risk of inducing intracerebral steal — when the autoregulatory function of diseased vessels is severely impaired, inhalational anesthetics preferentially dilate relatively normal vessels, shunting blood flow to non-lesional areas, which may instead aggravate hypoperfusion in the core lesion area [7].

This bidirectional effect is ultimately regulated by multiple confounding factors such as anesthesia depth, arterial carbon dioxide partial pressure, and perioperative blood pressure management. Maintaining normal blood pressure, normocapnia, normovolemia, and normothermia perioperatively is the core principle of anesthesia management for moyamoya disease. Even in special populations with sickle cell disease, this standard has been proven to effectively reduce perioperative neurological complications and offset the adverse effects of anesthetics on cerebral perfusion [27]. Most included studies generally adopted goal-directed blood pressure control, maintaining mean arterial pressure within ± 20% of baseline, with basically balanced blood pressure management goals between the two groups. This largely excludes the interference of blood pressure management differences on outcomes, and is also an important reason why no significant difference in ischemic complications was observed.

For the epileptic subtype, increased cortical excitability caused by postoperative cerebral hyperperfusion is the core trigger. Propofol has the effects of reducing cerebral metabolic rate, constricting cerebral vessels, and lowering intracranial pressure, which can reduce cerebral hyperperfusion load [6]. It also has definite pharmacological properties of raising seizure threshold and anticonvulsant effects, and is theoretically more suitable for the prevention and control of this subtype.

Overall, the potential effects of the two anesthesia modalities on the two TNEs subtypes are in opposite directions, and these opposing effects cancel each other out, resulting in no significant difference in the combined incidence. The bidirectional effect of inhalational anesthesia on the ischemic subtype, superimposed on differences in clinical management across studies, may also be an important source of moderate heterogeneity. Limited by the current status of original studies not reporting by subtype, this inference cannot be verified by quantitative analysis yet, and needs to be further confirmed by future large-sample studies with unified outcome criteria combined with intraoperative perfusion monitoring.

Inhalational anesthesia increases the risk of PACU delirium in children

Based on single-center pediatric retrospective data, inhalational anesthesia is associated with an increased risk of PACU delirium in children, which is consistent with research trends in other surgical populations [11, 12].

From the perspective of pathophysiological mechanisms, abnormal fluctuations in perioperative cerebral blood perfusion are an important trigger of postoperative delirium. Studies in cardiopulmonary bypass coronary artery bypass grafting populations show that hyperperfusion caused by abnormally elevated intraoperative and postoperative cerebral blood flow is significantly associated with increased incidence of postoperative delirium [28, 29], suggesting that cerebral perfusion imbalance can directly participate in the development of delirium. Combined with the pharmacological and physiological evidence of this study, the cerebral vasodilatory effect of inhalational anesthetics may increase cerebral perfusion load, and the mediated hyperperfusion state may be a potential pathway for elevated delirium risk in children with moyamoya disease.

It should be noted that the above association evidence comes from cardiac surgery populations, whose perfusion fluctuation pattern is different from the hyperperfusion pattern of moyamoya disease (“chronic hypoperfusion basis + sudden blood flow increase after bypass surgery”). It can only be used as a reference for general pathological mechanisms, and this inference needs to be further verified by prospective studies specific to moyamoya disease populations. Considering that children with moyamoya disease already have abnormal cerebral perfusion and cognitive basis, PACU delirium may prolong hospital stay and affect postoperative recovery. Therefore, propofol-based intravenous anesthesia may be considered preferentially for children at high delirium risk, but this recommendation is only based on limited observational evidence.

Potential advantages of intravenous anesthesia in cerebral physiology and surgical conditions

Supplementary studies show that both anesthesia regimens can maintain basically stable cerebral oxygenation; propofol-based intravenous anesthesia has more advantages in reducing intracranial pressure, improving frontal perfusion, and enhancing brain relaxation, which is more conducive to intraoperative surgical field exposure.

From a clinical operation perspective, pediatric moyamoya patients have more limited cranial cavity compensation space. When inhalational anesthesia maintains normal carbon dioxide partial pressure, the cerebral vasodilatory effect of the drug easily leads to brain bulging and enhanced brain pulsation, which interferes with the stability of the surgical field and fine operations such as vascular anastomosis. Propofol can achieve better brain relaxation by constricting cerebral vessels and reducing intracranial pressure, effectively reducing the risk of brain bulging. This advantage has clear practical clinical significance, consistent with the study results of Ankolekar et al. [19].

However, total intravenous anesthesia in children requires vigilance against the serious adverse reaction of propofol-related infusion syndrome (PRIS). PRIS was first reported in young children, with an extremely high fatality rate. Its core mechanism is that propofol inhibits mitochondrial fatty acid β-oxidation, causing energy metabolism disorders in the myocardium and skeletal muscle. Typical manifestations include refractory bradycardia, malignant arrhythmia, severe lactic acidosis, rhabdomyolysis, and hyperkalemia, which can induce cardiac arrest in severe cases [30].

Pediatric patients undergoing moyamoya surgery have multiple high-risk factors: children have lower glycogen and carbohydrate reserves than adults, making them more prone to energy metabolism imbalance; long-term cerebral ischemia leads to elevated stress catecholamine levels, and intraoperative combined use of vasoactive drugs can further amplify mitochondrial toxicity [31]; excessive propofol infusion rate and large total dose during prolonged surgery also increase the risk. Clinically recognized high-risk thresholds are infusion rate > 4 mg·kg− 1·h− 1) and continuous infusion for more than 48 h [31].

It should be emphasized that moyamoya surgery involves short-term anesthesia maintenance, and the overall risk of PRIS is lower compared with multi-day ICU sedation. However, young children, patients undergoing prolonged surgery, and those receiving high-dose vasopressors still require high vigilance. Perioperatively, the propofol infusion rate should be strictly controlled, blood gas lactate, electrolytes, creatine kinase, and electrocardiogram should be continuously monitored, sufficient glucose supply should be ensured, and the dose should be promptly reduced or the anesthesia regimen should be changed when unexplained acidosis or bradycardia occurs.

For cerebral hyperperfusion prevention and control, combined with existing evidence analysis, the pharmacological properties of propofol are more consistent with prevention and control principles. An international survey covering 18 countries showed that more than half of anesthesiologists prefer total intravenous anesthesia in moyamoya revascularization, and one important consideration is more stable cerebral blood flow regulation, which is more conducive to hyperperfusion risk management [32]. However, there is currently no large-sample head-to-head study directly comparing the incidence of cerebral hyperperfusion syndrome between the two anesthesia modalities, and this conclusion is only indirectly derived from physiological indicators and has not been verified by clinical hard endpoints.

Study strengths and robustness

The strengths of this study are as follows: (1)It is currently the systematic review in this field with the largest sample size and most comprehensive outcome coverage, integrating both core clinical outcomes and supplementary evidence on cerebral physiology and surgical conditions, leading to more comprehensive conclusions; (2)It strictly follows PRISMA specifications, all included studies are of moderate to high quality, and the risk of bias is controllable; (3)The robustness of the conclusions is verified through multiple sensitivity analyses, with clear sources of heterogeneity and no significant publication bias.

At the same time, it should be noted that the 2 included pediatric studies were not specifically designed to compare anesthesia techniques; they only reported secondary analysis results of anesthetic drugs, and did not provide adjusted effect sizes and baseline balance information, which may introduce potential confounding bias [16, 17]. However, the adjusted effect sizes and crude effect sizes of existing studies are consistent in conclusion direction, suggesting that confounding factors have limited impact on core results.

Study limitations

Study design and bias limitations: Included studies are predominantly retrospective observational studies, with only 3 small-sample RCTs. Although most original studies have adjusted baseline confounding, residual confounding cannot be completely eliminated. As a secondary study, this study can only explore correlations and cannot establish causality.

Heterogeneity in outcome reporting: Outcome definitions, types of anesthetic drugs, and follow-up duration vary across studies. None of the 6 core studies simultaneously reported all three core endpoints. The study populations for different outcomes are independent of each other, precluding multi-endpoint joint analysis, which reduces the reliability of comprehensive conclusions to a certain extent.

Missing key outcomes: Most studies did not standardly define and report cerebral hyperperfusion syndrome and intraoperative intracerebral steal as independent outcomes, making it impossible to quantitatively analyze the true impact of the two anesthesia modalities on these severe complications. Relevant inferences are only based on indirect evidence from physiological indicators.

Limited generalizability: PACU delirium data comes only from a single-center pediatric retrospective study, with missing adult data; some studies use surgical hemisphere as the unit of analysis, with mild data clustering. The overall GRADE level of evidence is low to very low, and the conclusions are for interim reference only.

Other limitations: The number of included studies is small, resulting in insufficient power for publication bias testing; the impact of different blood pressure management goals on outcomes has not been systematically analyzed, and the synergistic effect of anesthesia and blood pressure control needs further exploration; long-term neurocognitive outcome data are scarce, and most studies only focus on short-term complications.

Research prospects

Currently, the field of moyamoya anesthesia is still dominated by retrospective studies, with insufficient high-quality randomized controlled evidence. Future research can be advanced in the following directions.

Conduct large-sample, multicenter randomized controlled trials with unified outcome definitions and follow-up time points, extend the follow-up period, and clarify the long-term impact of the two anesthesia modalities on long-term neurocognition and quality of life.

Establish a standardized propofol dosing regimen for pediatric populations, stratified by age, weight, and vascular stage; define the safe infusion concentration range that does not induce PRIS; establish a standardized process for dynamic dose adjustment based on intraoperative monitoring indicators, to form a pediatric anesthesia specification that balances efficacy and safety.

Unify the definition and diagnostic criteria for outcomes such as cerebral hyperperfusion syndrome and intracerebral steal, focus on exploring the correlation between anesthesia regimens and these key complications, as well as the synergistic strategy of anesthesia and perioperative blood pressure management.

Conclusions

Current low to very low quality evidence shows no statistically significant difference in the risk of postoperative transient neurological events and stroke between inhalational and intravenous anesthesia for patients with moyamoya disease undergoing revascularization surgery. Based on limited pediatric retrospective data, inhalational anesthesia may increase the risk of PACU delirium in children.

Intravenous anesthesia has potential advantages in improving intraoperative cerebral physiological parameters and surgical conditions, and may be considered preferentially for children at high delirium risk. However, propofol use in children carries safety risks such as propofol-related infusion syndrome. The standardized dosing regimen is still unclear, and special studies are still needed to establish a dosing strategy that balances efficacy and medication safety. High-quality, large-sample randomized controlled trials are urgently needed to further verify the effects of the two anesthesia modalities on various neurological outcomes and explore individualized anesthesia strategies for different subgroups.
