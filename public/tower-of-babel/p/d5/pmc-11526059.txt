Blood and local eosinophil levels in chronic rhinitis: Comment☆
Authors: Hinpetch Daungsupawong, Viroj Wiwanitkit
Published: 2024

Dear Editor,

We write about the publication on “Blood and local eosinophil levels in chronic rhinitis: Observations during seasonal allergen exposure and non-exposure."1 This study aimed to investigate the characteristics and factors influencing the distribution of systemic and local eosinophils in patients with non-allergic rhinitis (NAR), perennial allergic rhinitis (PAR), and seasonal allergic rhinitis (SAR). A cross-sectional study was conducted in Beijing during different pollen seasons. In the study, 558 patients with chronic rhinitis provided serum allergen samples, underwent blood tests, nasal cell cultures, and completed a questionnaire on symptoms. The results showed that patients with chronic rhinitis had the highest blood eosinophil levels and the most severe symptoms. In addition, higher eosinophil counts were associated with more severe nasal and ocular symptoms, as well as higher incidences of concomitant asthma and allergic conjunctivitis. In particular, patients with chronic rhinitis had higher eosinophil levels during the pollen season, which was positively associated with pollen exposure. Several weaknesses in the study method should be addressed. First, the cross-sectional design limited the ability to infer causal relationships, as data were collected at a single point without tracking changes over time. Furthermore, the reliance on self-reported data on symptom severity and history may lead to bias, as patients may have different levels of symptom awareness or acceptance. This study therefore did not take into account confounding factors, such as other environmental exposures or genetic predispositions that may affect eosinophil levels.

Patients with persistent rhinitis showed greater eosinophil levels during pollen season, which was linked to pollen exposure. Several flaws in the study methodology should be addressed. First, the cross-sectional design restricted the capacity to establish causal linkages because data were collected at a single point without being tracked over time. Furthermore, relying on self-reported data on symptom severity and history may result in bias because patients may have varying levels of illness awareness or acceptance. As a result, this study did not account for confounding variables such as other environmental exposures or genetic predispositions that could influence eosinophil levels. Furthermore, while the sample size appears to be appropriate, the study's conclusions may be limited if the patient demographics do not accurately reflect the general population.

There are methodological limitations to consider, such as the time period during which data were obtained, which may not have captured the full range of eosinophil responses to environmental stimuli outside the season under investigation. Because pollen exposure varies seasonally and regionally, a lack of long-term follow-up may overlook significant changes in eosinophil levels that could yield clinical insights. Furthermore, the lack of a strong control group prevents the association between eosinophil levels and symptoms from being accurately compared to patients who did not attain chronic rhinitis (CR). Controlling for variables such as participants' treatment plans and medication adherence is difficult when analyzing eosinophil changes and outcomes without precise information.

To further understand eosinophil dynamics in CR, future research should use longitudinal designs that follow eosinophil levels and symptom severity over time, particularly before, during, and after pollen season. Furthermore, future studies could benefit from a bigger and more diverse cohort of individuals to improve the generalizability of the results. The inclusion of a control group of patients who did not attain CR would allow for comparisons to better understand the disparities in eosinophil distribution and clinical outcomes. Other potential causes, such as environmental factors, dietary habits, and genetic risk, may potentially shed light on the etiology of eosinophilia. In the search for novel techniques, researchers could investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying the link between eosinophil counts and allergic reaction, potentially uncovering biomarkers that predict symptom intensity. Advanced approaches, such as genomic and proteomic studies, may uncover novel elements of eosinophil biology in various rhinitis subtypes. Improving research by incorporating technologies, such as wearable devices that monitor daily allergen exposure and symptoms, could give real-time data, increase the intensity of analysis, and lead to more tailored treatment methods. Finally, looking into the role of novel therapeutic methods that target eosinophil activity could lead to new management techniques for chronic rhinitis patients.
