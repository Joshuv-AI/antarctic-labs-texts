Acute Neurological Complications After Transplantation in Methylmalonic Acidemia: A 35‐Patient French Cohort
Authors: Adélaïde Vissac, Eugénie Sarda, Charles‐Joris Roux, Manuel Schiff, Claire‐Marine Bérat, Juliette Bouchereau, Jean‐Baptiste Arnoux, Chloé de Puyraimond, Margaux Gaschignard, Tristan Mekdade, Ugo Cucinotta, Claire Mayer, Valérie Barbier, Jean François Benoist, Mehdi Oualha, Carmen Capito, Christophe Chardot, Myriam Dao, Aude Servais, Claire Francoz, Laurène Dehoux, Florence Lacaille, Isabelle Desguerre, Nathalie Boddaert, Pascale de Lonlay, Anaïs Brassier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTransplantation is an effective therapeutic option to improve quality of life in patients with severe methylmalonic acidemia (MMA). However, data regarding neurological complications following transplantation remain limited. A retrospective, single‐center study was conducted at Necker Hospital (France), including MMA patients who underwent kidney (KT), liver (LT), or liver‐kidney transplantation (LKT) between 2007 and 2022. Our aim was to evaluate acute neurological complications occurring after transplantation, focusing on clinical features, laboratory parameters and neuroimaging. Sensorineural complications were also assessed. Thirty‐five patients were included, 21 had undergone LKT, 10 LT and 4 KT. The median age at transplantation was 10.1 years, with a median follow‐up of 5 years. Tacrolimus was used in 91% of patients. Acute neurological complications likely related to MMA occurred in 17% of patients. They include Leigh syndrome with identifiable triggers, observed in 4 patients (2 early and 2 late‐post‐transplantation), leading to one early‐ and one late‐onset death. Stroke‐like episodes occurred in 2 patients. Non‐epileptic myoclonus, likely related to calcineurin inhibitor (CNI), were reported in 31% of patients. Pre‐transplant brain MRI showed nonspecific abnormalities in 31% of patients and remained stable afterwards. Post‐transplant ophthalmological data available for 17 patients showed 3 optic atrophies. No acute vision or hearing loss was reported. Although transplantation improves metabolic control in MMA, acute neurological complications can still occur following a triggering event, even years after transplantation. The risk may arise from sensitivity to CNI‐induced neurotoxicity. Pre‐ and post‐transplant neuroimaging, emergency metabolic protocols, and tailored immunosuppression are essential for long‐term management.

Introduction

Methylmalonic acidemia (MMA) is a rare inborn error of metabolism caused by a defect in the methylmalonyl‐CoA mutase enzyme or its cofactor, adenosylcobalamin [1]. This condition leads to a toxic accumulation of methylmalonic acid (MMA) and secondary mitochondrial energy dysfunction. Current management includes a protein‐restricted diet, L‐carnitine supplementation and a strict prevention of catabolic states [2]. Despite these interventions, long‐term prognosis remains poor, with progressive multi‐organ involvement. Kidney impairment is common and frequently progresses to end‐stage kidney disease [3]. Neurological complications are also prominent and can be severe, including Leigh syndrome (symmetric basal ganglia and/or brainstem lesions), global developmental delay, and, more recently recognized, sensorineural impairments [4, 5].

Organ transplantation as “enzyme replacement therapy” was proposed as a therapeutic option for patients with severe forms of MMA [6], initially with isolated kidney transplantation (KT) to treat progressive kidney failure [7]. Subsequently, combined liver‐kidney transplantation (LKT) was introduced to restore higher levels of enzyme activity, particularly via the hepatic graft, which contains more enzyme than the kidney. Isolated liver transplantation (LT) was proposed preemptively in young patients, before the onset of kidney failure [6], an approach we recently adopted in an attempt to prevent long‐term kidney and neurological complications. Overall, transplantation improved metabolic stability and enhanced quality of life [8]. However, data on post‐transplant acute neurological episodes remain limited, particularly regarding short‐, medium‐, and long‐term follow‐up.

Our primary objective of this study was to describe the occurrence and nature of acute neurological complications in MMA patients following transplantation. Secondary objectives were to evaluate the occurrence of sensorineural impairments (ophthalmological and auditory) pre and post‐transplantation.

Patients and Methods

We conducted a retrospective, single‐center study at Necker Hospital (Paris, France), including all patients with MMA who underwent LKT, LT or KT between July 2007 and August 2022. MMA diagnosis was confirmed in all patients exhibiting biallelic variants either in MMUT or in MMAB.

Clinical data were extracted from patients' medical records. Collected variables included age at disease onset (neonatal or late‐onset forms), genotype, type of transplantation, age at transplantation, and immunosuppressive regimen. Immunosuppression depended on transplant type: tacrolimus alone was generally used in LT, whereas it was combined with another drug (mycophenolate mofetil or azathioprine) and steroids in LKT and KT. Post‐transplant acute neurological episodes—regardless of their direct relation to MMA—were recorded. Brain Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) scans performed after transplantation were re‐evaluated by a single pediatric neuroradiologist.

Leigh syndrome (LS) was defined by acquired bilateral, symmetrical lesions involving the basal ganglia and brainstem most often associated with lactate peak on brain spectroscopy (SRM). Stroke‐like episodes (SLE) were defined as the onset of acute or subacute neurological deficits mimicking a stroke, and imaging lesions incongruent to a main vascular territory. In Posterior Reversible Encephalopathy Syndrome (PRES), reported in patients with renal failure, high blood pressure, certain drugs including calcineurin inhibitors, immune dysfunction [9], acute neurological symptoms were headaches, seizures, visual disturbances, altered level of consciousness. A metabolic decompensation event (MDE) was defined by acidosis with or without ketosis, or hyperammonemia > 100 μg/L.

Ophthalmological and auditory examination data were also collected.

In accordance with French legislation, patients or their legal representatives were informed during routine consultations that their medical data could be used for research purposes, with the absence of opposition systematically recorded. All data were anonymized and securely stored in a password‐protected database, adhering to French and European data protection regulations.

Statistical analyses were conducted using either means or medians, as appropriate.

Of the 35 patients, 60% (n = 21) underwent combined LKT, 29% (n = 10) LT and 11% (n = 4) KT. Two patients, P13 and P24, required a second combined LKT due to graft failure following an initial KT.

The median age at transplantation was 10.1 years (range: 1.3–30) with subgroup median ages of 12.7 years for LKT, 5.8 years for LT, and 13.3 years for KT.

The median post‐transplant follow‐up duration was 5.3 years (range: 0–16.5), with subgroup median ages of 5.7 years (range: 0–10) for combined LKT, 3.8 years (range: 1.4–7.3) for LT, and 12.3 years (range: 11.2–16.5) for KT.

Five patients died during follow‐up (3 after LKT and 2 after KT): 3 due to multiple organ failure early after LKT, 1 related to LS 9.4 years after KT, and 1 following a MDE in a context of sepsis secondary to pneumonia, with altered consciousness but no Leigh syndrome or stroke‐like episodes, associated with a metabolic acidosis with a pH of 7.29, and hyperammonemia, 12 years after KT.

The most common immunosuppressive drug was tacrolimus, a calcineurin inhibitor (CNI) (n = 32; 91%). Tacrolimus alone was administered in all LT, whereas it was combined with steroids, and either mycophenolate mofetil (n = 13; 37%) or azathioprine (n = 9; 26%) for LKT and KT. Three adults who underwent LKT received another CNI, ciclosporin, in combination with mycophenolate mofetil. Tacrolimus was switched to a mTOR inhibitor (sirolimus or everolimus) after several months in 16 out of 32 patients, for kidney protection. Additionally, tacrolimus was replaced by ciclosporin in four patients after acute neurological complications, including SLE, LS or severe myoclonus (P11, P21, P27, P30).

Acute Neurological Events Most Likely Related to Secondary Mitochondrial Dysfunction and/or MMA Neurotoxicity

Prior to transplantation, only one patient (P28) had exhibited LS at 2 years of age, with a clinical full recovery. Post‐transplantation, four patients developed LS (Figure 2), two cases occurred shortly after transplantation (one 1 month after LT [P7], and one 3 weeks after LKT [P 30]) and two others more than one‐year post‐transplant (one 1 year after LT [P6] and one 9 years after KT [P33]). The age at transplantation was from 1.3 to 25 years of age. All four patients had tacrolimus blood levels within the therapeutic target range, no ketoacidosis was observed, and plasma ammonia ranges went from 60 to 90 μmol/L (Table 2). Two of these patients died, one at 2 months (P30) and the other one 9 years post‐transplant (late onset, P33)—respectively 1 month and 3 days after the first neurological signs. Potential triggering factors were identified in the cases of the 2 late onset LS (more than 1 year after transplantation): Hepatic graft failure in one patient and severe influenza infection in the non‐vaccinated other.

SLE were observed in two patients after LKT (Figure 2). P11, within 1 month post‐transplantation, developed diffuse erratic myoclonus first of right upper limb and then of left upper limb and head, associated with bilateral pyramidal syndrome. Brain MRI showed SLE in the central region (Figure 3). As possible trigger, tacrolimus blood level was elevated (18.7 μg/L, target 10–15) 5 days prior to symptom onset. No other triggering factor was identified, such as arterial hypertension. Tacrolimus was then switched to ciclosporin, and the patient was treated with oral clonazepam and piracetam. Despite treatment, persistent myoclonus of the upper limbs and head remained and finally disappeared 3 years later. P21 presented, 2 months post‐LKT, with partial continuous epilepsy, characterized by right hemicorporeal myoclonus more pronounced in the right upper limb. Electroencephalography (EEG) showed a pseudo‐periodic slow wave focus with left central spikes. Brain MRI revealed lesions consistent with SLE (Figure 2). No clear triggering factor was identified. Tacrolimus was switched to ciclosporin, and seizures resolved following the initiation of levetiracetam, with no further recurrence.

No association was found between plasma and urinary MMA levels and the likelihood of exhibiting a severe acute neurological complication (data not shown).

Acute Neurological Events Probably Not Related to MMA Without Energetic Failure

Two patients (P11 and P14) developed intensive care unit (ICU) acquired transient motor weakness in accordance with critical illness polyneuropathy. Two other patients (P17 and P21) were diagnosed with central pontine myelinolysis, confirmed by MRI lesions, attributed to rapid fluctuations in plasma sodium levels immediately after transplantation (including one patient, P17, treated with ciclosporin with moderate variation in plasma sodium levels).

Proximal and diffuse non‐epileptic myoclonus were observed in 31% patients (n = 11; P6, P7, P9, P11, P12, P17, P23, P27, P28, P29, P34) with a median onset of 2 months post‐transplantation (range: 15 days to 18 months). They can be distinguished from tremors frequently described with tacrolimus, based on their more proximal distribution, greater intensity, and poorer response to treatment. Tacrolimus was switched to ciclosporin in two patients. Five patients were treated with piracetam, with one of them receiving a combination of piracetam and clonazepam. Clinical improvement was observed in 3 out of the 5 treated patients. Five of the 11 patients developed at a different moment neurological signs: LS (n = 2), SLE (n = 1), or centropontic lesion (n = 2).

No PRES syndrome was diagnosed in our study.

All patients assessed brain MRI before transplantation, 28 after transplantation. Nonspecific brain MRI abnormalities were identified in 31% of patients (n = 11) before transplantation: leukoencephalopathy (n = 4), hippocampal atrophy (n = 3), nonspecific periventricular white matter hyperintensities (n = 7). All abnormalities remained stable after transplantation over time when compared to pre‐transplant imaging. Three of these patients experienced an acute neurological event after transplantation: one SLE and two LS (P11, P7, P30). Nevertheless, acute neurological events seems to be independent of pre‐exiting MRI abnormalities. In two patients, new nonspecific brain MRI abnormalities appeared after transplantation: P10 developed periventricular white matter hyperintensities, and P29 showed mild bilateral lesions of the globus pallidus, without any clinical or radiological evidence of LS.

Sensorineural Impairments

Three of the 6 patients with pre‐transplant ophthalmological data had normal visual assessments. One patient had early‐stage retinal dystrophy without optic atrophy, along with a mildly enlarged foveal depression. Two patients experienced acute‐onset blindness, which improved following pre‐transplant dialysis.

Post‐transplant ophthalmological data were available for 17 patients. Of these, 65% (n = 11) were normal. One patient had hypertensive retinopathy, and another one a mild unilateral abnormality in optical coherence tomography (OCT), without associated visual symptoms. Three patients had visual impairment associated with optic atrophy. One patient experienced a transient scotoma following LKT.

No patient underwent auditory assessment before transplantation, and no impairment was found among the 8 patients who had one after transplantation. No hearing loss was reported.

Discussion

Transplantation, whether LKT, LT or KT, is a therapeutic option for patients with severe MMA, leading to improved quality of life through better metabolic control, and, in most cases, diet liberalization (mild protein restriction), with discontinuation of enteral nutrition [10]. However, although rare, MDE and acute neurological complications were reported after transplantation [10, 11].

We reported here the first study on acute neurological complications in a large cohort of patients transplanted for MMA. Age at transplantation did not appear to influence the occurrence of acute neurological complications. Four patients developed LS, 2 SLE, and 11 non‐epileptic myoclonus. The pathophysiology most probably involves the secondary mitochondrial energy dysfunction in the brain, with possible direct MMA toxicity. This also could make these patients especially more prone to CNI‐induced neurotoxicity, in particular tacrolimus. Even if both mechanisms can be associated, it seems that tacrolimus neurotoxicity and energy failure play a role separately in terms of clinical and neuroimaging patterns of expression.

This could also be related to the accumulation of MMA, methylcitrate, glycine and glutamine in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) [12]. Martinelli et al. showed that the MMA levels in the CSF did not significantly change after transplantation, with a median level of 744 μmol/L [11].

CNI, tacrolimus more than ciclosporin, are potentially neurotoxic and may contribute to these complications [13, 14]. Although the underlying mechanisms are not fully understood, mitochondrial dysfunction has been implicated [14]. Molema et al. demonstrated a decrease in plasma FGF‐21 levels, a marker of mitochondrial dysfunction, in patients transplanted for MMA, with a subsequent increase in patients who developed neurological complications. This suggests that pre‐existing mitochondrial dysfunction may exacerbate CNI‐induced neurotoxicity. However, their most typical and frequent neurological side effects include distal tremors [15, 16], and PRES [14], seen in 0.5%–5% of liver transplants [13], where high blood pressure is an important co‐factor [17], and that we did not see in our patients. Tremors secondary to tacrolimus affect 22% of transplanted patients whatever the organ, in a review by Shukla et al. [18]. Central pontine myelinolysis has been reported in a child treated with ciclosporin, but with also hydroelectrolytic disorders [19]. Non‐epileptic myoclonus, LS and SLE were not previously described in transplanted patients without MMA.

Non‐epileptic myoclonus, different from classical distal tremors, were identified in one‐third of our patients; they were important and sometimes very difficult to treat, but mostly disappeared after months or years.

In our cohort, four patients developed LS after transplantation, compared to only one case before the procedure. The reported cases occurred within weeks of the transplantation [8, 11], but it also could occur late, at a time when medical attention was relaxed.

Two patients experienced SLE post‐transplantation whereas non had such events before transplantation. The main differential diagnosis for SLE is PRES [13], and distinguishing both can be challenging. PRES is typically characterized by vasogenic oedema (increased ADC) on MRI, while stroke‐like shows cytotoxic oedema, with diffusion restriction. However, Durrleman et al. observed a diffusion restriction in only 52% of SLE, while 8% showing increased ADC and 40% no ADC modification [20]. In our patients, blood pressure was normal and MRI was in favor of SLE. SLE happened within a year post transplantation, without identifiable triggers. In the hypothesis of a PRES, tacrolimus was switched to ciclosporin. Notably, no data exist on SLE in patients with MMA pre‐ or post‐transplant. The role of tacrolimus in one reported case of SLE was suspected [21]. Its mitochondrial toxicity, associated with the initial metabolic disorder, could participate in the energy failure.

Tacrolimus is the most common first‐line immunosuppressive drug, as monotherapy following LT, and in combination with steroids, and azathioprine or mycophenolate mofetil after LKT or KT. It allows a better patient and graft survival than ciclosporin [22]. Several months after transplantation, it may be replaced by less nephro‐ and neurotoxic agents such as mTOR inhibitors (Sirolimus or Everolimus). In these patients who already have a neurological fragility, ciclosporin can be considered, on a case‐by‐case basis, as a first‐line drug instead of tacrolimus, as we now decided for LKT according to the usual practice of the medical teams. It is advisable to perform a liver biopsy in cases of suspected rejection or before modifying immunosuppression to determine the most appropriate immunosuppressive regimen.

The enzyme replacement with transplantation does not completely cure MMA, and patients remain at risk of MDE. Unlike MDEs occurring before transplantation, characterized by biochemical markers in plasma and urine such as ketosis, hyperammonemia, acidosis, and elevated MMA levels, post‐transplant MDEs often present with minimal or no significant changes in these metabolic parameters. In our patients, no ketosis was observed, and plasma ammonia levels were normal or only mildly elevated. Urinary and plasma MMA concentrations may moderately increase, but results are not usually available in emergency. This diagnosis delay may contribute to the under‐recognition of MDEs when neurological or gastrointestinal symptoms arise. It is essential to maintain a high level of suspicion during a catabolic stress, whatever the time after transplantation. Prompt initiation of an emergency protocol with intravenous glucose infusion and the administration of supportive cofactors, particularly thiamin (vitamin B1), is advisable. This is especially relevant in cases of lactic acidosis, where thiamin serves as a critical cofactor in mitochondrial energy metabolism [23]. An emergency management protocol for transplanted patients with MMA is currently being developed by the French national network for inherited metabolic diseases (G2m) [24].

Many patients showed nonspecific abnormalities in brain MRI, both before and after transplantation. Some of these abnormalities were already described [4, 25], and are thought to be more related to chronic toxicity rather than to sequelae from acute neurological events [26]. In our patients, these abnormalities did not disappear after transplantation. A systematic brain MRI performed prior to transplantation may help characterize the nature of acute neurological events occurring post‐transplantation, and guide appropriate treatment strategies.

Transplantation does not seem to change the cognitive outcome in our patients (data not shown), as previously described in literature [27, 28], although further detailed studies are required to determine the extent of any cognitive benefits, with more structured neurocognitive evaluations.

Ophthalmological manifestations have only recently been described [29]. Two of our patients presented with sudden blindness, likely related to MMA, before transplantation, and none afterwards. Both patients showed improvement in visual function after dialysis. This suggests that systematic screening and a fundus follow‐up may be necessary, particularly in patients with renal failure and those who have presented neurological complications, in order to take early action and potentially restore visual function.

This study has several limitations. First, it is a retrospective, single‐center analysis, with 3 patients lost to follow up. The transplant subgroups (LKT, LT and KT) are not comparable, as our approaches evolved over time (initially KT, then LKT, and more recently LT), resulting in differences in patients' age and clinical status. Systematic ophthalmologic screening was only recently implemented. We did not report sensorineural hearing loss due to limited available data; however, such complications should systematically be monitored both before and after transplantation.

In the future, transplantation may no longer be the only alternative to improve quality of life in these patients. A clinical trial is currently underway using messenger RNA therapy [30]. Several pre‐clinical gene therapy studies already ongoing [31]. Most viral vectors studied so far have a hepatic tropism [32], but targeting other affected organs, such as kidneys and brain, remains a challenge. AAV9 is a promising candidate, as it can cross the blood–brain barrier and potentially address central nervous system involvement [33].

Conclusion

Transplantation allows an improvement in metabolic balance and quality of life of patients with severe MMA. Current recommendations support early preemptive LT, or combined LKT in cases of renal failure, in light of MMA's natural history and long‐term complications. However, transplantation does not prevent early or late acute neurological complications, such as LS or SLE, most probably related to secondary mitochondrial energy failure. Proximal non‐epileptic myoclonus possibly related to tacrolimus were first described in this study. They are different from “classic” tacrolimus induced tremor, which happened quite frequently after transplantation. MMA may demonstrate increased susceptibility to CNI, likely due to their underlying mitochondrial dysfunction.

These acute neurological complications may occur long after transplantation with possible sequelae. It is thus important to remain cautious, particularly in situations of catabolism (including fasting, infection, fever and drug reactions) and to anticipate possible acute neurological events by initiating emergency regimens, even in the absence of clear metabolic decompensation. Systematic brain imaging before transplantation is important for future comparison in case of post‐transplant neurological complications. Given the probable increased susceptibility of neurotoxicity of CNI, particularly Tacrolimus, immunosuppressive strategies in these patients should be discussed.
