Hypofractionated radiotherapy across cancer sites: a narrative review of evidence and implementation
Authors: Masahiro Inada, Takuya Uehara, Yukinori Matsuo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHypofractionated radiotherapy (HFRT), which delivers higher doses per fraction in fewer treatment sessions, represents a practical form of de-escalation in radiation by shortening overall treatment time and reducing patient burden. This narrative review summarizes the current evidence for HFRT across various malignancies. For breast cancer, randomized trials including the START trials and JCOG 0906 have established moderate HFRT (40–42.56 Gy in 15–16 fractions) as standard care after breast-conserving surgery. In localized prostate cancer, multiple trials have demonstrated equivalent biochemical control with moderate HFRT compared to conventional fractionation. For early-stage glottic cancer, HFRT achieves comparable or superior local control with acceptable toxicity. In elderly glioblastoma patients, HFRT with concurrent temozolomide has become a standard treatment option. For vulnerable patients unable to tolerate standard chemoradiotherapy, HFRT provides a valuable alternative, although caution is required when combining with chemotherapy. In palliative settings, single-fraction radiotherapy is established for painful bone metastases, while split-course regimens such as QUAD shot offer effective palliation for bulky tumors. Despite robust evidence, HFRT adoption varies across regions, with barriers including reimbursement structures and institutional capacity. HFRT reduces healthcare costs while maintaining outcomes, making it an attractive option in resource-limited settings. Continued efforts are needed to promote evidence-based HFRT implementation worldwide.Keywords: hypofractionated radiotherapy, de-escalation, breast cancer, prostate cancer, palliative radiotherapy

Introduction

Treatment de-escalation has been actively discussed in the field of oncology. In clinical practice, de-escalation has been applied in various forms: shortening treatment duration and reducing drug dosages in medical oncology, and reducing the extent of surgical resection in surgical oncology. Ferrero et al. outlined the primary objectives of de-escalation as follows: improving patients' quality of life by reducing treatment-related toxicity, minimizing inherent toxicities and long-term sequelae associated with anti-cancer therapies, and reducing the significant costs linked to often-expensive cancer treatments [1].

In the field of radiation oncology, de-escalation strategies such as reducing prescription doses or decreasing irradiation volumes have been explored, particularly in head and neck cancers [2]. However, the evidence supporting de-escalation of treatment intensity remains insufficient. In contrast, hypofractionated radiotherapy (HFRT), which uses a higher dose per fraction with fewer fractions, has been investigated for many years, and accumulating evidence has established its efficacy and safety in specific malignancies. In this context, hypofractionation can be regarded as a form of de-escalation in radiotherapy when it preserves tumor control while reducing overall treatment burden and improving patient convenience.

Coutard and colleagues refined and systematized the principle of fractionated irradiation [3], and thereafter curative radiotherapy for malignant tumors has been based on fractionation as the standard approach. Conventionally fractionated radiotherapy (CFRT) is defined as 1.8–2.2 Gy per fraction, delivered once daily on weekdays [4]. Overall treatment time (OTT), particularly for definitive-intent regimens, typically ranges from 4 to 8 weeks [5], requiring numerous outpatient visits or extended hospital stays. The Veterans Affairs Evidence Synthesis Program defined HFRT as regimens using a dose per fraction exceeding 2.2 Gy. Regimens using a dose per fraction over 2.2 Gy and <5.0 Gy are commonly described as moderate hypofractionation, while those with ≥5 Gy per fraction are referred to as ultra-hypofractionation [4].

Although escalation of the dose per fraction is challenging in diseases requiring large irradiation fields due to concerns regarding increased acute toxicity, clinical trials have demonstrated that in diseases with limited target volumes, such as early-stage glottic cancer, glioblastoma, breast cancer, and prostate cancer, HFRT can be safely and effectively implemented [6–12]. In this narrative review, we overview the evidence of HFRT as implemented in clinical practice and discuss future perspectives.

Materials and methods

This narrative review is based on a literature search of PubMed and relevant references, without restriction on publication period, focusing on studies addressing the usefulness and evidence of HFRT. Additional information was obtained from national and international guidelines, as well as expert consensus documents. The literature selection was intended to provide a comprehensive overview of current knowledge. Studies specifically addressing stereotactic radiotherapy for small tumors, such as early-stage lung cancer or brain metastases, were excluded, as these topics have been extensively reviewed elsewhere and involve distinct treatment paradigms. Instead, this review emphasizes evidence and guidelines related to CFRT and moderate HFRT applied to major cancer sites.

Results

Breast cancer

The role of radiotherapy for non-metastatic breast cancer is in the post-operative setting following breast-conserving surgery for early-stage disease or mastectomy for locoregionally advanced stage. In early breast cancer, post-operative whole-breast irradiation reduced the risk of local relapse and breast cancer mortality after breast conserving surgery [13]. Historically, the most frequent radiotherapy schedule was 50 Gy in 25 fractions over 5 weeks for adjuvant therapy following breast-conserving surgery. Radiobiological studies have indicated that breast cancer tissues possess a relatively low α/β ratio, implying potential advantages from higher fraction doses; consequently, several clinical trials have been initiated to evaluate the efficacy and safety of HFRT [14, 15]. In 2002, the first large-scale randomized controlled trial from Canada compared 50 Gy in 25 fractions over 5 weeks with 42.5 Gy in 16 fractions over 3 weeks and demonstrated equivalent outcomes in terms of local recurrence, survival, and cosmesis [16]. Following the results of the UK Standardisation of Breast Radiotherapy (START) trials demonstrating the equivalence of a 3-week HFRT schedule to CFRT, HFRT has been globally implemented as a standard approach for postoperative whole-breast irradiation after breast-conserving surgery [8, 9, 17]. Following the widespread adoption of HFRT schedules worldwide, a single-arm phase II study, Japan Clinical Oncology Group (JCOG) 0906, was conducted in Japan to validate the efficacy and safety of hypofractionated whole-breast irradiation in the Japanese population [18, 19]. In JCOG 0906, whole breast irradiation with 42.56 Gy in 16 fractions yielded a 5-year ipsilateral breast relapse-free survival of 98%, with grade ≥ 2 late toxicity reported in 12.4% of patients. While only moderate HFRT has been adopted for whole-breast irradiation after breast-conserving surgery in Japan, extensive research is ongoing internationally on HFRT regimens following mastectomy and on ultra-HFRT approaches after breast-conserving surgery. Supplement Table 1 presents an overview of representative investigation of ultra-hypofractionated whole-breast irradiation performed worldwide following breast-conserving surgery. In the settings of postmastectomy radiotherapy and ultra-hypofractionated whole-breast irradiation, an increased risk of skin toxicity has been reported in several studies, highlighting the need for careful patient selection and cautious implementation [23–25]. Moreover, local therapy for breast cancer has been evolving remarkably, with recent advances in reconstructive techniques and non-surgical approaches such as radiofrequency ablation. Continuous research is required to build robust evidence for postoperative radiotherapy in the context of these emerging treatment modalities.

Localized prostate cancer

Definitive radiotherapy is widely used for prostate cancer and is considered a treatment option equivalent to radical prostatectomy in terms of curative potential [26]. In definitive radiotherapy for prostate cancer, dose escalation beyond 70 Gy has been shown to improve biochemical relapse-free survival [27, 28]. The use of intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) allows high-dose delivery while minimizing rectal and other toxicities, making prostate cancer one of the most frequently treated with radiotherapy [29, 30]. Traditionally, definitive radiotherapy for prostate cancer has been delivered with CFRT, consisting of 1.8–2.0 Gy per fraction to a total dose of 74–78 Gy over 8 weeks. As prostate cancer tissues also have low α/β ratio, HFRT with decreasing total radiation dose may be potentially beneficial [31]. Advances in IMRT and image guidance technologies have enabled the safe escalation of fraction dose and have contributed to the widespread adoption of HFRT. Table 2 summarizes the major randomized trials of moderate HFRT for localized prostate cancer conducted worldwide [10, 11, 32–37]. Although all studies have shown equivalence in biochemical relapse-free rates, a few trials have reported a modest or statistically significant increase in gastrointestinal late adverse events among patients receiving HFRT [33, 36]. The safe delivery of HFRT requires careful treatment planning, including adherence to dose constraints. In a Japanese population, a prospective phase II trial of HFRT delivering 70 Gy in 28 fractions was conducted by Nakamura et al [35]. Five-year biochemical relapse-free rates exceeding 90% were achieved across low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups, with grade 2 toxicities reported in ~5% of patients, confirming the safety and efficacy of this regimen in Japanese patients.

In prostate cancer, several studies have explored ultra-HFRT regimens delivering more than 6 Gy per fraction. Both the HYPO-RT-PC trial (42.7 Gy/7 fractions) and the PACE-B trial (36.25 Gy/5 fractions) reported equivalent tumor control and late toxicity compared with CFRT, although a slight increase in acute genitourinary toxicity was observed [38, 39]. These results suggest that ultra-HFRT may become one of the standard treatment options in the future.

Early glottic cancer

Glottic laryngeal cancer is one of the common head and neck malignancies. Because hoarseness is an early and characteristic symptom of glottic cancer, the disease is often detected at an early stage. Early-stage glottic cancer has a low risk of lymph node metastasis, and definitive radiotherapy to the primary site without chemotherapy is one of the standard treatment options that offers excellent voice preservation [40–42]. The CFRT schedule for early glottic cancer typically involves a total dose of 60–70 Gy administered in daily 2-Gy fractions [43, 44]. From the results of biological and clinical investigations, OTT is known as the one of the factors affecting the local control [43, 45, 46]. To enhance local control and treatment efficiency, multiple retrospective and prospective studies have evaluated regimens with increased fraction size and shorten OTT, demonstrating promising results with manageable toxicities (Table 3) [47–54]. Numerous studies have shown that HFRT regimens provide superior local control and comparable survival outcomes without an increase in severe adverse events. The JCOG 0701 trial was a large-scale randomized study performed in Japan that compared CFRT (66–70 Gy in 33–35 fractions over 7 weeks) and HFRT schedules (60–64.8 Gy in 25–27 fractions over 5 weeks), showing trends toward improvement in 3-year local failure (15.9% vs 10.3%) and similar 3-year progression-free survival (79.9% vs 81.7%) with HFRT in Japanese population [12, 54]. In the JCOG 0701 study, the non-inferiority of HFRT could not be confirmed, as the lower bound of confidence interval failed to exceed the non-inferiority margin, however, a trend toward reduced toxicity with HFRT was observed in the long-term follow-up study [55]. Thus, HFRT has become recognized as one of the treatment options for early-stage glottic cancer. Reducing the number of treatment fractions has contributed to lowering medical costs and alleviating the burden on patients, which proved particularly beneficial during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Several investigations have explored further reduction in the number of radiotherapy fractions. Although good local control has been reported in studies employing three- to five-fraction regimens, an increased rate of severe late toxicities also has been reported [56–58]. In moderate HFRT using a fraction size of 2.25–3.5 Gy, severe late complications are rare (Table 3); nevertheless, a prospective phase I/II trial exploring ultra-HFRT using 30–36 Gy in three fractions was discontinued because 18% of patients developed soft-tissue or bone necrosis [57]. A retrospective study using 35–42.5 Gy in five fractions also reported a 9.0% incidence of severe soft-tissue or chondronecrosis [58]. Given the high incidence of severe complications, further reduction in the number of fractions should be approached with caution. Clinical data from a phase II prospective trial suggested that small target volume size and exclusion of active smoker yield promising safety outcome [56].

Glioblastoma in the elderly

Glioblastoma is one of the most aggressive primary malignant tumors of the brain. Standard of care for glioblastoma is maximal safe surgical resection followed by adjuvant radiotherapy with concomitant chemotherapy [59]. Several prospective studies have established the effect of postoperative radiotherapy delivered with CFRT schedule of 60 Gy in 30 fractions (2 Gy per fraction over 6 weeks) [60, 61]. In contrast, among patients over 70 years of age, survival outcomes remain poor despite surgery followed by standard radiotherapy and concomitant temozolomide, raising concerns regarding the justification of applying 6 weeks of CFRT to all elderly patients [62]. The efficacy of HFRT using 40 Gy in 15 fractions over 3 weeks for elderly patients with glioblastoma was first demonstrated by Roa et al. in 2004 [62]. Later, another randomized controlled trial by Malmström A et al. confirmed its efficacy and safety using 34 Gy in 10 fractions over 2 weeks in 2012 [63]. Subsequently, Perry et al. demonstrated the survival advantage of the addition of temozolomide to HFRT [6], establishing HFRT as a standard treatment option in this population. Although no prospective trials have directly compared CFRT and HFRT in combination with temozolomide, several retrospective analyses have demonstrated the non-inferiority or equivalence of HFRT plus temozolomide compared with CFRT [7, 64] (Table 4). Minniti et al. also reported that shortening the treatment course did not compromise overall survival, while contributing to the preservation of quality of life, including cognitive function [65].

Following the evidence supporting moderate HFRT, subsequent studies have explored whether even shorter treatment courses could provide comparable efficacy while further reducing treatment burden. Several trials have examined 1-week radiotherapy schedules compared with the conventional fraction 6-week approach or the moderate HFRT 3-week approach, demonstrating encouraging results without apparent compromise in survival or safety [66, 67]. However, due to the limited number of clinical trials and the lack of sufficient safety data regarding concomitant temozolomide, this 1-week regimen has not yet been widely adopted in clinical practice in Japan. Accordingly, a phase III trial has been initiated by JCOG to directly compare 1-week radiotherapy (25 Gy in five fractions) with concomitant temozolomide against the standard 3-week schedule (40 Gy in 15 fractions) with temozolomide in elderly glioblastoma patients [68].

Vulnerable patients

In patients with non-metastatic head and neck, lung, and esophageal cancers, surgery or chemoradiotherapy represents the standard of care, including for unresectable cases. However, due to its high treatment intensity, elderly patients often have difficulty completing the planned therapy [69–72]. In patients who cannot tolerate surgery or chemoradiotherapy, radiotherapy alone is considered. In this setting, radiotherapy fractionation is commonly based on standard chemoradiotherapy regimens; in other words, CFRT is widely used. In such de-intensified radiotherapy-alone approaches, several studies have investigated HFRT schedules aimed at shortening OTT.

For head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, HFRT with 2.5–3 Gy per fraction (total 50–60 Gy) has demonstrated favorable local control rates (>70%) with acceptable toxicity, particularly with modern IMRT techniques (Suppl Table 2). However, concurrent chemotherapy with fraction sizes above 2.4 Gy is discouraged due to increased acute toxicity, as recommended by ASTRO/ESTRO consensus and NCCN Guidelines [73, 74].

For esophageal cancer, HFRT regimens with ≤3 Gy per fraction have been investigated, as higher doses (≥5 Gy) are associated with severe acute toxicity (Suppl Table 3). In Japan, a phase II study is ongoing for patients ineligible for concurrent chemotherapy [75].

For locally advanced non-small cell lung cancer, HFRT at 2.4–2.5 Gy per fraction appears relatively safe as monotherapy, though clinical outcomes remain suboptimal (Suppl Table 4). Dose escalation with ≥3-Gy fractions increase severe esophagitis, and the IASLC recommends cautious patient selection for accelerated HFRT [76].

As discussed above, HFRT for vulnerable patients appears to be a promising option that may reduce patient burden while maintaining treatment efficacy. However, robust evidence remains limited, particularly when compared with the solid data available for breast and prostate cancers, including large randomized controlled trials. Therefore, the use of HFRT in these settings should be individualized and implemented in accordance with each patient’s circumstances and preferences.

Palliative radiotherapy

Palliative radiotherapy is a cornerstone of oncologic care, aimed at alleviating the symptomatic burden of advanced cancer to improve patient quality of life. A guiding principle in this setting is the minimization of treatment-related toxicity and logistical challenges, including travel and time spent in the hospital. Consequently, hypofractionation is of paramount importance. Historically, moderately hypofractionated regimens such as 30 Gy in 10 fractions became a widely accepted standard of care for many palliative indications, including painful bone metastases [77].

The drive to further minimize patient burden has led to the widespread adoption of single-fraction radiotherapy (SFRT) as the ultimate form of hypofractionation. The Dutch Bone Metastasis Study randomized 1171 patients to receive either a single 8 Gy fraction or 24 Gy in six fractions, demonstrating no significant difference in pain response [78]. Although SFRT was associated with a higher retreatment rate (25% vs. 7%), it offered profound benefits in patient convenience and conservation of healthcare resources. These findings have been validated by subsequent trials and meta-analyses [79, 80]. The ESTRO ACROP guidelines establish that SFRT is as effective for pain control as multi-fraction regimens, achieving pain relief rates of 50%–80% with complete pain relief in 20%–40% of patients [81, 82].

The efficacy of SFRT is well established for painful bone metastases, while its role in other palliative indications remains less clearly defined. To address this, accelerated split-course regimens such as the “QUAD SHOT” (14–14.8 Gy in four fractions over 2 days, repeated at 3–6 weeks intervals for up to three cycles) have been developed (Suppl Table 5). This approach resolves the limitations of SFRT by enabling the delivery of a higher biological effective dose (BED) per cycle, which can induce a greater tumor response. Furthermore, the planned treatment breaks allow for clinical assessment of tumor response and patient tolerance, providing a flexible and adaptive framework that aligns well with the dynamic nature of palliative care. Although originally applied to pelvic tumors, its use has been extended to head and neck cancer. Toya et al. reported a retrospective study of 105 patients with incurable head and neck cancer treated with the QUAD SHOT regimen using VMAT [83]. Seventy-three percent of patients completed three cycles. Tumor response was observed in 88% and symptom relief in 71% of patients, with an overall response rate of 93%. All patients who completed three cycles achieved a response. Grade 3 toxicity (mucositis) occurred in only 2% of patients, with no Grade 4 or higher toxicity observed.

Discussion

In breast and prostate cancers, moderate HFRT has achieved oncologic outcomes and toxicity profiles equivalent to those of CFRT while substantially shortening treatment duration, thereby reducing patient burden. Similar trends have been observed in early-stage glottic cancer. Notably, concurrent chemotherapy was not used in HFRT for these diseases. When chemotherapy is combined with HFRT, the risk of acute toxicity increases, potentially negating the benefits of treatment de-escalation.

Radiobiological background

The biological effect of radiotherapy is known to depend not only on the total dose but also on the dose per fraction and radiation sensitivity. Therefore, to compare the biological intensity of radiotherapy delivered with different fraction sizes, the BED is commonly used as calculated as follows [84];

n: fraction, d: dose per fraction [Gy], α/β: α/β ratio [Gy].

The α/β ratio is a parameter that reflects the radiosensitivity of tumor and normal tissues. Conventional malignant tumors and early-responding normal tissues typically have a high α/β ratio, while late-responding normal tissues are considered to have a low α/β ratio. In tumors with a high α/β ratio, the use of a small size dose per fraction with an increased total dose enables an increase in tumor BED while minimizing the BED to late-responding normal tissues. Consequently, CFRT is characterized by a small dose per fraction. In contrast to most malignant tumors, accumulating evidence indicates that certain tumors, including prostate cancer, breast cancer, and glioblastoma, are characterized by a low α/β ratio [85–87]. When the tumor α/β ratio is low, HFRT schedules with a higher dose per fraction may offer a radiobiological advantage. Based on this radiobiological background, accumulating evidence from clinical trials has demonstrated the efficacy of HFRT for glioblastoma, breast cancer, and prostate cancer [6–11]. Notably, reported α/β estimates for glioblastoma vary substantially across studies, and some datasets support higher α/β values, implying that HFRT may not confer a uniform radiobiological advantage for all glioblastomas [87, 88].

Nevertheless, HFRT can be useful in selected cases of tumors with a high α/β ratio. Because of accelerated tumor cell repopulation, prolongation of OTT has been associated with decreased local control [89]. For example, in laryngeal cancer, prolongation of OTT is associated with an estimated dose loss of ~0.6 Gy per day, and unnecessary treatment delays are therefore discouraged, as follows [45];

Tov: overall treatment time, Tlag: treatment lag.

Adoption of hypofractionated radiotherapy

Despite the robust clinical evidence supporting HFRT, its adoption varies considerably across geographic regions and cancer sites. Akimoto et al. reported that the utilization of HFRT in the Asia-Pacific region, including Japan, South Korea, Singapore, and Australia, lags behind that in Western countries [90]. The authors identified several barriers to adoption, including pay-per-fraction reimbursement models that financially disadvantage shorter treatment courses, concerns about long-term toxicity and efficacy data, and professional culture within multidisciplinary teams where radiotherapy may be underutilized.

Recent data from Japan demonstrate encouraging trends. Ikawa et al. conducted a multicenter study in Osaka Prefecture examining HFRT utilization among patients with breast and prostate cancer [91]. For breast cancer patients undergoing partial mastectomy, HFRT use increased dramatically from 41% in 2019 to 81% in 2023, likely driven by the National Guideline revisions in 2022 [92]. For prostate cancer, HFRT (including hypofractionated IMRT and SBRT) increased from 25% in 2019 to 48% in 2023, although adoption was notably lower at medium- and low-volume facilities (30% and 17%, respectively, in 2023) compared to high-volume centers (79%). These findings suggest that economic incentives and equipment requirements may represent significant barriers to implementation at smaller institutions.

In the palliative setting, Koide et al. analysed nationwide claims data and reported that SFRT courses increased from 1730 in fiscal year 2014 to 5642 in 2022, with outpatient treatments surpassing inpatient treatments by 2022 [93]. This steady increase reflects the growing recognition of SFRT as an effective and patient-centered approach to palliative care.

Cost considerations

HFRT has significant potential for healthcare cost containment. Hunter et al. conducted a systematic literature review demonstrating that HFRT can reduce treatment costs by 20%–40% compared to CFRT, with potential annual savings estimated at over 100 million dollars in the United States for breast cancer alone [94]. However, the authors also identified important challenges, including reduced hospital revenue under fee-for-service payment systems, changes in workforce demand for radiation oncologists and radiation therapists, and the need for reimbursement reform to align financial incentives with evidence-based practice.

Within the medical service system in Japan, costs for radiotherapy are determined by procedure-based reimbursement. The total costs depend on several treatment-related factors, including the radiotherapy technique, the prescribed number of fractions, the application of immobilization systems, and the methods of image-guided radiotherapy incorporated into the treatment. For example, in conventionally fractionated IMRT for glioblastoma, the patient incurs costs including a management fee of 50 000 Japanese yen (JPY), an immobilization system creation fee of 10 000 JPY, plus a treatment fee of 30 000 JPY and a CT-based image guidance fee of 4500 JPY per treatment fraction (30 fractions) (Table 5). Therefore, HFRT schedules, by reducing the total number of fractions, have the potential to lower the overall treatment cost. In breast and prostate cancer, HFRT receives an additional reimbursement per fraction; however, the overall treatment cost is still lower than that of CFRT (Table 5). Furthermore, decreased hospital visits can reduce transportation costs and lost wages from work absences, providing a significant reduction in patients’ overall financial burden.

Conclusion

HFRT represents a valuable strategy for treatment de-escalation in radiotherapy, offering the potential to maintain or improve oncologic outcomes while reducing treatment burden and healthcare costs. Although adoption has increased substantially in recent years, particularly in Japan, continued efforts are needed to address barriers related to reimbursement structures, institutional capacity, and professional education to ensure that all patients can benefit from evidence-based hypofractionated treatment approaches.
