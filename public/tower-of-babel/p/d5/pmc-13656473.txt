Quality of life and health challenges among individuals with spinal cord injury in Türkiye: findings from the InSCI dataset
Authors: Belgin Erhan, Bilinc Dogruoz Karatekin, Kevser Gumussu, Basak Bilir Kaya, Hulya Sirzai, Yasemin Yumusakhuylu, The Spinal Cord Paralytics Association of Turkey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionSpinal cord injury (SCI) is frequently accompanied by secondary health conditions that can compromise participation and overall quality of life (QoL). Using the Turkish arm of the International Spinal Cord Injury (InSCI) Community Survey, we described the sociodemographic and clinical profile of adults with SCI in Türkiye and examined cross-sectional factors associated with higher QoL.MethodsCross-sectional analysis of the official Turkish InSCI survey. Community-dwelling adults with traumatic or non-traumatic SCI were recruited via five Istanbul-based institutions that serve patients from multiple regions of Türkiye. Overall QoL was assessed with a 5-point Likert item and analysed both as a 5-category distribution and as a binary outcome (high QoL = 4–5 vs. low QoL = 1–3). Self-rated overall health was entered as an ordinal explanatory variable. Multivariable logistic regression (complete-case) explored independent correlates of high QoL.ResultsAmong 405 participants (mean age 43.95 years; 70.5% male), paraplegia was reported by 77.9% and traumatic SCI by 88.7%. Secondary health conditions were common, including spasticity (61.9%), bladder dysfunction (61.1%), and bowel problems (60.1%); 67.3% reported clinically significant pain (VAS ≥ 4). QoL was rated bad/very bad by 19.7%, neither good nor bad by 56.1%, and good/very good by 24.2%. In adjusted analysis (n = 251), worse self-rated health was consistently associated with lower odds of high QoL (OR 0.25 per one-level worsening; 95% CI 0.15–0.42; p < 0.001), whereas age, sex, time since injury, traumatic etiology, and income were not independently associated.DiscussionNeutral QoL ratings predominated and symptom burden was high. The association between self-rated health and QoL suggests that perceived health is closely related to overall well-being; however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously because both constructs were assessed using single-item subjective measures.ConclusionSecondary health conditions are common among people with SCI in Türkiye, and perceived overall health showed the most consistent association with higher QoL after adjustment.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12891-026-10217-0.

Introduction

Spinal cord injury (SCI) is a life-altering condition with profound consequences on an individual’s physical, emotional, and social well-being. Beyond the initial trauma, individuals often face a wide range of secondary health conditions, such as pain, spasticity, bladder and bowel dysfunction, sexual problems, and cardiovascular instability, which significantly affect daily functioning and quality of life (QoL) [1].

The International Spinal Cord Injury (InSCI) Community Survey was developed to systematically capture the lived experiences of individuals with SCI across multiple countries, using a standardized framework grounded in the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) [2]. This global initiative has generated valuable comparative data on how health, personal, and environmental factors shape life with SCI across diverse contexts. InSCI-based studies show that secondary health conditions are linked to poorer QoL and life satisfaction, with access to treatment and environmental conditions influencing this association [1].

International comparisons are informative, but country-specific data are essential to address local disparities and sociocultural factors. The Turkish arm of the InSCI survey provides one of the first large standardized datasets describing community-dwelling individuals with SCI in Türkiye. By using the common InSCI framework, the present study enables a country-specific description and cautious comparison with international findings regarding sociodemographic characteristics, secondary health conditions, employment, perceived health, and QoL.

Documenting lived experiences and patient-reported outcomes in SCI is essential for person-centered care and better rehabilitation. Incorporating patient perspectives helps align interventions with real needs. Previous longitudinal and rehabilitation-based studies have shown that life satisfaction and selected QoL domains may change during and after inpatient rehabilitation, emphasizing that QoL after SCI is dynamic and influenced by adaptation, functional independence, and rehabilitation context [3, 4].

The specific contribution of this study is not limited to reporting national InSCI descriptive data. By analysing the Turkish InSCI cohort within the standardized international InSCI framework, this study identifies how perceived health, secondary health conditions, employment-related indicators, and socioeconomic characteristics intersect with QoL in a rehabilitation context that has been underrepresented in the SCI literature. In this way, the study provides a country-specific but internationally comparable profile that may inform long-term rehabilitation planning, service prioritization, and future comparative research.

The present study aims to (1) describe the sociodemographic and health characteristics of individuals with SCI in Türkiye, (2) examine the relationships between these factors and perceived QoL, and (3) clarify the service-relevant implications of secondary health conditions within the Turkish rehabilitation context.

Methods

Study design and data source

This study was a cross-sectional, observational analysis based on data from the Turkish arm of the International Spinal Cord Injury (InSCI) Community Survey [5, 6]. The InSCI Community Survey is a multinational, cross-sectional survey designed to describe the lived experience, functioning, health conditions, participation, environmental factors, and well-being of people living with SCI in the community. Its conceptual structure is based on the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health and the International SCI Core Data Sets, allowing harmonized data collection and comparison across participating countries [5, 6]. The Turkish InSCI dataset, used in this study, is part of the global comparative database and adheres to the core structure and methodology used in all participating countries.

This study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of X University on December 22, 2021 (Approval No: 2021/0681). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment.

Data collection

Survey responses were obtained through self-administered paper questionnaires, electronic forms, or structured interviews when necessary. Data collection adhered to the standardized procedures defined in the InSCI protocol to ensure international comparability.

Participants

A total of 475 community-dwelling individuals with SCI were assessed for eligibility. As illustrated in the enrollment flow diagram (Fig. 1), 405 individuals met the inclusion criteria and were enrolled in the study. Inclusion criteria were: (1) history of traumatic or non-traumatic SCI; (2) community residence in Türkiye; (3) ability to provide informed consent and complete the survey independently or with assistance. Exclusion criteria included congenital causes of SCI, neurodegenerative conditions, or inability to understand the purpose of the study. Recruitment and survey administration followed the standardized InSCI protocol. A small subgroup reported being “fully recovered” after SCI; these cases were retained for descriptive reporting and presented as a separate category.

Participants were recruited from five major centers in Türkiye: one university hospital (public), 3 specialized rehabilitation centers (2 public, 1 private), 1 disability association (The Spinal Cord Paralytics Association of Türkiye). All institutions are based in Istanbul but serve patients from across the country. Although all recruiting institutions were located in Istanbul, these centers serve individuals with SCI from different regions of Türkiye. Region of residence was available for 181 participants and showed representation from all seven geographical regions (Figure S1). Therefore, the sample provides geographic diversity, but it should not be interpreted as a population-based nationally representative sample. Because recruitment was institution-based, the sample may also have preferentially included individuals with greater access to specialist rehabilitation services or organized support networks.

Participants rated overall QoL using a single item (“How would you rate your quality of life?”) on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = very bad, 2 = bad, 3 = neither good nor bad, 4 = good, 5 = very good). The full distribution across all five categories is reported. Single-item global QoL measures are commonly used in SCI datasets when the aim is to capture an overall subjective appraisal rather than domain-specific QoL [7]. For comparative analyses and clinical interpretability, the 5-point overall QoL item was dichotomised into high QoL (responses 4–5: good/very good) versus low or non-high QoL (responses 1–3: very bad/bad/neither good nor bad). The full 5-category distribution was retained for descriptive reporting, and additional sensitivity analyses using ordinal and three-category QoL outcomes were performed to assess whether the findings were robust to alternative outcome specifications.

Furthermore, participants assessed their overall health using a single item: “Overall, what would you say about your health?” Response options were recorded on a 5-point Likert scale coded as 1 = Very good, 2 = Good, 3 = Fair, 4 = Bad, 5 = Very bad, where lower scores indicate better perceived health. This variable was treated as an ordinal measure and entered into the QoL analyses (including the multivariable logistic regression) per one-level worsening in self-rated health status.

Participants reported whether they experienced specific secondary health conditions during the previous four weeks (presence/absence). Severity, frequency and management of these conditions were not assessed in the module used for this analysis.

Pain intensity during the previous week was assessed using a 0–10 visual analogue scale (VAS), where 0 indicated “no pain” and 10 indicated “worst imaginable pain.” Clinically significant pain was defined as VAS ≥ 4.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0. Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages, were used to summarize sociodemographic characteristics, injury-related variables, secondary health conditions, and quality of life indicators. Independent samples t-tests were performed to compare continuous variables (e.g., age, income satisfaction, pain level) between participants reporting low and high QoL. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square tests. A secondary binary logistic regression explored independent associations with high QoL (dependent variable: high QoL = 1, low QoL = 0) in complete-case data; results are reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Variables included in the multivariable logistic regression model were selected a priori based on clinical relevance, previous SCI literature, and the structure of the InSCI dataset. Age, sex, time since injury, traumatic etiology, income category, and self-rated health were retained to represent key sociodemographic, injury-related, socioeconomic, and perceived health domains [1, 2]. Injury completeness was not included in the final multivariable model despite its clinical relevance because the primary model was deliberately restricted to a small number of variables to reduce overfitting in the complete-case sample. In addition, injury completeness was self-reported in the survey context and was not based on a standardized neurological examination at the time of analysis. Therefore, completeness was described in the descriptive analyses but not retained in the final adjusted model. The number of variables was kept limited to reduce the risk of model overfitting given the complete-case sample size. Employment status was not retained in the final multivariable model in order to preserve model parsimony in the complete-case sample and to reduce the risk of overfitting. In addition, employment was considered a contextual participation-related factor that may be downstream of perceived health and environmental barriers; therefore, it was described and interpreted as an important contextual variable rather than included in the final adjusted model. Secondary health conditions were also not entered simultaneously into the final multivariable model because the survey captured multiple conditions as binary presence/absence variables without severity or frequency grading. Including numerous potentially correlated secondary conditions in the complete-case model would have increased model complexity and the risk of overfitting, and might also have introduced conceptual overlap with self-rated health. Complete-case analysis was used because the multivariable model was exploratory and intended to provide transparent estimates based only on participants with observed data for all model variables. No imputation was performed because the assumptions required for reliable imputation could not be verified in this secondary cross-sectional dataset. To evaluate the potential influence of case exclusion, participants included in the complete-case model were compared with those excluded from the model on key observed demographic, injury-related, and QoL variables. Because the primary QoL outcome was dichotomized, sensitivity analyses were conducted using alternative QoL specifications. First, ordinal logistic regression was used with the original 5-level QoL item. Second, a three-category QoL model was used by grouping responses as low (1–2), neutral (3), and high (4–5). A significance level of p < 0.05 was used to determine statistical significance.

Results

A total of 475 individuals were assessed for eligibility, of whom 405 met the inclusion criteria and were enrolled in the study. The mean age was 43.95 years (SD = 13.48; n = 400; range: 17–94). Among participants with valid sex data, 286/405 were male (70.5%) and 119/405 were female (29.5%). In terms of relationship status, 191/403 were married (47.4%), 160/403 were single (39.7%), 26/403 were cohabiting (6.5%), 19/403 were separated (4.7%), and 7/403 were widowed (1.7%) (Table 1).

Region of residence was available for 181 participants. The sample included individuals from all seven geographical regions of Türkiye: Marmara 43/181 (23.8%), Central Anatolia 39/181 (21.5%), Southeastern Anatolia 34/181 (18.8%), Mediterranean 24/181 (13.3%), Black Sea 17/181 (9.4%), Aegean 12/181 (6.6%), and Eastern Anatolia 12/181 (6.6%), supporting geographic diversity among those with available regional data.

Educational background varied: 131/402 had primary education (32.6%), 102/402 had high school education (25.4%), and 46/402 had university degrees (11.4%). The mean duration of education was 8.79 years (SD = 4.49; n = 391), with a mean of 1.29 years of education attained after SCI (SD = 3.18; n = 359). Regarding income, 225/318 participants earned above the minimum wage (70.8%), while 93/318 were below the minimum wage (29.2%) (Table 1). The mean satisfaction with income was 3.56 on a 10-point scale.

Spinal cord injury characteristics

Participants had been living with SCI for a mean of 15.49 years (SD = 11.94; n = 386). Most participants reported paraplegia 311/399 (77.9%), while 82/399 reported tetraplegia (20.6%) and 6/399 reported being fully recovered (1.5%). Complete and incomplete injuries were nearly equally distributed among participants with classifiable completeness data: 192/388 (49.5%) and 196/388 (50.5%), respectively. The majority of participants with available etiology data had traumatic SCI, whereas a smaller proportion had non-traumatic SCI (Table 2). Percentages for lesion type and completeness may not sum to 100% due to the separate “fully recovered” category and missing responses.

Quality of life and self-rated health status

Overall QoL was rated as very bad by 25/396 (6.3%), bad by 53/396 (13.4%), neither good nor bad by 222/396 (56.1%), good by 80/396 (20.2%), and very good by 16/396 (4.0%). Thus, 78/396 rated QoL as bad/very bad (19.7%) and 96/396 rated QoL as good/very good (24.2%).

For self-rated health status, 17/399 rated their general health as very good (4.3%), 6/399 as good (1.5%), 118/399 as fair (29.6%), 169/399 as bad (42.4%), and 89/399 as very bad (22.3%).

Group differences in sociodemographic and clinical variables by QoL

Tables 3 and 4 show the bivariate comparisons and multivariable logistic regression results. Of the 405 enrolled participants, 251 were included in the complete-case multivariable regression model. The largest amount of missing data was observed for income category, followed by etiology and time since injury. Variable-specific missing values for all model variables are presented in Supplementary Table S1. To assess the potential impact of this reduction in sample size, included and excluded participants were compared on key observed characteristics. In the complete-case regression model (n = 251), worse self-rated health was independently associated with lower odds of high QoL (OR approximately 0.25 per one-level worsening; 95% CI approximately 0.15–0.42; p < 0.001) (Fig. 2). Age, sex, traumatic injury, time since injury, and income category were not independently associated with high QoL. These findings should be interpreted cautiously and primarily as hypothesis-generating.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses were performed to address missing data and outcome specification. Participants included in the complete-case multivariable model (n = 251) did not differ significantly from those excluded (n = 154) in age, sex, paraplegia/tetraplegia distribution, time since injury, traumatic etiology, or the proportion with high QoL (all p > 0.05). In the ordinal logistic regression model using the original 5-level QoL item, worse self-rated health remained significantly associated with lower odds of being in a higher QoL category (OR = 0.28 per one-level worsening; 95% CI: 0.20–0.39; p < 0.001). Similarly, in a three-category QoL model (low = 1–2, neutral = 3, high = 4–5), worse self-rated health was associated with lower odds of being in the neutral versus low category (RRR = 0.22, 95% CI 0.12–0.38; p < 0.001) and in the high versus low category (RRR = 0.10, 95% CI 0.05–0.19; p < 0.001), confirming a consistent direction and magnitude of effect across model specifications. These findings suggest that the complete-case sample was broadly comparable to the excluded group with respect to the key observed variables examined, although bias related to unmeasured or missing factors cannot be excluded.

Discussion

This study provides one of the first large standardized descriptions of community-dwelling individuals with SCI in Türkiye using the InSCI framework. Beyond describing national cohort characteristics, the analysis highlights the coexistence of neutral QoL ratings, a high burden of secondary health conditions, low employment, and a close association between perceived health and global QoL. These findings provide a service-relevant profile that may support long-term rehabilitation planning and cautious comparison with international InSCI data [5, 6].

Sociodemographic and clinical profile

The sample was predominantly male, middle-aged and living with long-standing traumatic SCI, consistent with international patterns. In our study, 70.5% were male with a mean age of 43.95 years, which is consistent with data reported by the InSCI survey across 22 countries and similar national cohorts (e.g., Switzerland and China) [8, 9].

Regarding education and income, 70.8% of participants reported earnings above the minimum wage, and the average education duration was 8.79 years. These figures appear lower than those reported in high-income settings such as Switzerland and Germany, indicating potential gaps in educational and social support for people with SCI in Türkiye [8].

Traffic accidents (33.5%) and high falls (18.9%) were the leading causes of traumatic SCI, followed by work-related accidents (17.5%). This pattern aligns with the nationwide epidemiological study by Karacan et al. [10], which also identified motor vehicle accidents and falls as the most frequent etiologies. Differences in proportions between studies—particularly a lower share of motor vehicle injuries and a higher share of occupational injuries in our sample—may reflect changes over time in urbanization, traffic exposure, and workplace conditions, underscoring the need for updated prevention strategies.

Secondary health conditions were highly prevalent. Clinically significant pain (VAS ≥ 4) and spasticity affected 67.3% and 61.9% of participants with valid data, respectively, broadly comparable to international InSCI reports [11]. In our study, bladder dysfunction was reported by 61.1% of individuals with SCI, highlighting the significant burden of urinary problems in this population. This prevalence aligns closely with previous findings from Türkiye, where Yıldız et al. [12] reported that 77.9% of individuals with SCI required intermittent catheterization for bladder management, and 63.2% used anticholinergic medications to control neurogenic bladder symptoms. In contrast, sexual dysfunction was reported less frequently (51.8%) than in international averages [11]. This may reflect cultural barriers to disclosure and limited routine sexual health counselling in clinical practice, as suggested by Turkish studies documenting high dysfunction rates on standardized instruments alongside low rates of patient education. Supporting this, Çelik et al. [13] reported that all women with SCI in their Turkish cohort exhibited sexual dysfunction yet the majority had never received any information or counseling regarding sexuality after their injury. This further highlights the persistent lack of sexual health discussions in clinical settings and the potential underreporting of sexual problems in populations where sexuality remains a sensitive or taboo topic.

In our study, pressure ulcers were reported by 34.7% of participants with valid data, postural hypotension by 26.9%, and autonomic dysreflexia by 18.6%. These figures are comparable to those reported in international InSCI data, where pressure ulcers, postural hypotension, and autonomic dysreflexia were also commonly reported across participating countries [11]. A study conducted in South Africa similarly found postural hypotension and autonomic dysreflexia rates at 26%, with pressure ulcers at 27% [14].

Clinically, the high prevalence of secondary health conditions in this cohort indicates that long-term SCI care in Türkiye should extend beyond neurological classification and mobility-focused rehabilitation. Pain, spasticity, bladder dysfunction, and bowel problems were among the most frequently reported problems and represent clinically actionable targets for follow-up care. These conditions may contribute to reduced participation, sleep disturbance, psychological distress, caregiver burden, recurrent healthcare use, and preventable complications such as urinary tract infections and pressure ulcers. Therefore, routine screening for secondary health conditions should be integrated into long-term rehabilitation services, with particular attention to pain characterization and management, individualized spasticity treatment, structured bladder and bowel programs, skin protection, and timely referral to urology, pain medicine, rehabilitation nursing, psychology, and social support services when needed. These findings support a multidisciplinary and proactive model of SCI follow-up in which secondary-condition management is treated as a core component of QoL-oriented rehabilitation rather than as an additional or optional aspect of care.

Employment after SCI in Türkiye

Only 13% of our participants with SCI were currently employed, although 56.1% had worked before injury. Türkiye’s broader labor indicators are also modest (employment 48.3% in 2023 and 49.5% in 2024; youth unemployment 17.4% and 16.3%, respectively), and in 2024, 27.2% of people aged 15–34 were NEET—contextualizing, in part, the low employment observed in our cohort [15]. Yet the post-SCI decline is substantial, reinforcing employment as a key determinant of well-being. Prior Turkish data similarly reported low employment (21%) and associated better outcomes with younger age at injury, higher education, longer time since injury, and participation in social/educational activities, underscoring the need for targeted vocational rehabilitation and supportive policies [16].

Quality of life and related factors

More than half of the participants rated their QoL as “neither good nor bad,” indicating a predominantly neutral perception of overall life quality. In the adjusted analyses, self-rated health showed the most consistent association with QoL. This finding is clinically plausible because perceived health may reflect the cumulative burden of secondary conditions, daily symptoms, functional limitations, and psychosocial adaptation after SCI. However, it should be interpreted cautiously. Both self-rated health and QoL were measured using single-item global subjective assessments, and the observed association may partly reflect shared subjective appraisal, common-method variance, and conceptual proximity rather than a fully independent explanatory relationship. Therefore, this finding should be considered an association between related subjective appraisals in a cross-sectional dataset, not evidence that self-rated health independently determines QoL [1, 2, 7].

The lack of independent associations for sociodemographic and injury-related variables should also be interpreted cautiously. QoL after SCI is multidimensional and may be shaped not only by injury characteristics, but also by adaptation, family support, environmental facilitators, employment opportunities, and access to rehabilitation. Longitudinal rehabilitation-based studies further suggest that life satisfaction and selected QoL domains may change during and after inpatient rehabilitation, supporting the view of QoL as a dynamic rather than fixed outcome [3, 4].

Employment and economic participation remain important contextual factors in SCI. International InSCI comparisons have shown interactions among employment, QoL, and self-rated health [2]. In Türkiye, the relationship between employment and QoL may be complex and may be influenced by perceived health and environmental factors [16]. Although employment was not entered into the final multivariable model, the very low current employment rate in our cohort suggests that vocational rehabilitation, disability-inclusive workplaces, and participation-oriented policies remain relevant priorities in Türkiye. Taken together, QoL-oriented care should address secondary condition management, perceived health, environmental barriers, and social participation, rather than focusing solely on injury characteristics .

Psychosocial and cultural context

Cultural context and psychosocial adaptation also shape QoL after SCI. Although pain was common in our sample (VAS mean 5.12), it was not significantly different between QoL groups, consistent with evidence that pain’s effect on QoL is often mediated by coping and psychological resilience [7]. Qualitative research further suggests that individuals who develop self-acceptance and effective coping can report higher QoL despite substantial impairment [17]. Together, these findings support a biopsychosocial interpretation of QoL, in which perceived health reflects not only symptom burden but also adaptive resources within a given cultural context. These findings are also consistent with studies emphasizing that social reintegration, participation, and psychosocial adaptation are important components of QoL after SCI [18].

Limitations

First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Accordingly, the associations observed in this study should be interpreted as correlational rather than causal. Second, both QoL and self-rated health were assessed using single-item subjective measures. Although this approach is consistent with the InSCI framework and allows broad survey implementation, these two constructs are conceptually related. Therefore, the strong association between self-rated health and QoL may partly reflect construct overlap, common-method variance, or shared subjective appraisal, and should not be interpreted as evidence of an independent causal or predictive effect. Third, the original QoL item was ordinal with five response categories, but the primary regression analysis used a dichotomized outcome. Although this categorization improved interpretability and was supplemented by ordinal and three-category sensitivity analyses, dichotomization may have reduced statistical power and led to some loss of information. Fourth, the multivariable regression model included 251 of the 405 participants because of missing data in one or more model variables. Although participants included in the complete-case model did not differ significantly from excluded participants in the key observed characteristics examined, the possibility of selection bias cannot be ruled out. In particular, if participants with missing income, etiology, time since injury, or perceived health data differed systematically in unmeasured ways, the adjusted associations may have been overestimated or underestimated. Therefore, the regression findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive population-level estimates. In addition, injury completeness was not included in the final multivariable model, although it is clinically relevant to functioning and QoL after SCI. This decision was made to preserve model parsimony in the complete-case sample and because completeness was self-reported rather than confirmed by standardized neurological examination. Nevertheless, the omission of injury completeness should be considered when interpreting the adjusted model. Fifth, recruitment was conducted through Istanbul-based institutions, although these institutions serve individuals from different regions of Türkiye. Therefore, the sample has geographic diversity but should not be considered population-based or fully nationally representative. Institution-based recruitment may have preferentially captured individuals who were more connected to specialist rehabilitation services or disability organizations, while underrepresenting those with limited access to such services. Sixth, mixed-mode survey administration was used without formal validation of response equivalence across modes. In addition, secondary health conditions were assessed only as present or absent, without severity grading, which limits clinical interpretation. Finally, the small subgroup reporting full recovery should be considered when interpreting lesion-related findings.

Conclusion

This study provides a standardized profile of community-dwelling individuals with SCI in Türkiye and highlights the substantial burden of secondary health conditions. Pain, spasticity, bladder dysfunction, and bowel problems were common and represent important targets for long-term multidisciplinary rehabilitation. Perceived overall health was closely associated with global QoL; however, this relationship should be interpreted cautiously because both measures were single-item subjective assessments. These findings support rehabilitation models that integrate secondary-condition management, participation-oriented care, and patient-reported outcomes into long-term SCI care and policy planning in Türkiye.
