Mayo Clinic classification of presacral tumors: evolution of classifications and systematic review of pathologies
Authors: William R G Perry, Scott R Kelley, Dovile Cerkauskaite, Maurish Fatima, Justin T Brady, Amit Merchea, John C Cheville, Rondell P Graham, Eric J Dozois
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPresacral tumours are rare, heterogeneous lesions. Historical classification systems for presacral tumours have provided a valuable framework, but were based on limited available data. This study aimed to develop a contemporary classification aligned with current tumour entities and clinical practice.MethodsA prospectively registered systematic review of surgically managed presacral tumours published from 1975 to 2025 was combined with a retrospective multisite institutional surgical pathology review from 1986 to 2025. Pathological descriptors were extracted, cross-referenced, harmonized to current terminology, and organized through multidisciplinary expert review to obtain a new classification.ResultsThe systematic review identified 5997 records, of which 203 studies met the inclusion criteria and contributed 181 pathological terms after generic and unclassifiable diagnoses were removed. Review of 3606 pathology reports identified 19 additional terms, bringing the total to 200 pathological terms. After harmonization to current terminology and exclusion of non-neoplastic processes, a contemporary classification was developed using biological descriptors: developmental versus acquired origin; tissue lineage (neurogenic, osseous/haematopoietic, soft tissue, or miscellaneous); and tumour behaviour (benign, locally recurrent without metastatic potential, locally recurrent with low metastatic potential, malignant non-sarcoma, or malignant sarcoma).ConclusionThe Mayo Clinic classification of presacral tumours updates historical systems by reflecting the broader, more modern spectrum of neoplasms and distinguishing lesions by lineage and biological behaviour. It may improve diagnostic clarity, communication, and selection of appropriate observation, multidisciplinary assessment, and treatment pathways.

Introduction

The presacral space is a potential space that can harbour a broad range of rare and heterogeneous tumours. Reports from referral centres suggest an incidence as low as 1 in 40 000 to 60 000 hospital admissions or approximately 0.6 to 6.3 cases annually, underscoring both the rarity of these lesions and the limited experience most surgeons will accumulate during practice.1–8 The presacral space is primarily composed of connective tissue, nerves, adipose tissue, and blood vessels derived from totipotent cells that differentiate into three germ cell layers (endoderm, ectoderm, mesoderm).

Historical classification systems for presacral tumours have provided a valuable framework, but they were developed in an era when substantially fewer lesions had been described. The original classification system described by Lovelady and Dockerty9 in 1949 divided presacral masses into congenital, neurogenic, osseous, and miscellaneous. Mayo et al.10 added an inflammatory category in 1954 and, in 1975, Uhlig and Johnson5 updated the system by adding further clarification regarding developmental cysts (epidermoid, dermoid, mucus-secreting, teratoma). Various papers have also added an ‘other’ category over the years11–13. The most commonly cited and used classification system of Uhlig and Johnson5 separates presacral tumours into congenital and acquired (inflammatory, neurogenic, osseous, miscellaneous), while grouping benign and malignant pathologies together.

There has been an increasing understanding of presacral tumours as more data have been published; however, the significant heterogeneity and variety of masses described has resulted in confusion regarding pathological classification. Indeed, the Uhlig and Johnson classification system5 does not clearly delineate all the biologically diverse tumour types currently described in the literature. Further, difficulty in accurately characterizing presacral tumours is compounded by the use of different inclusion criteria in various studies, varying pathological diagnoses for similar entities (for example, peripheral nerve sheath tumour, malignant schwannoma and neurofibrosarcoma), and treatment by several different surgical specialties.

Given the broad differential diagnosis and significant implications of misdiagnosis and mismanagement, the recognition of presacral pathology and an understanding of appropriate management are imperative for improving patient care and outcomes2,14–17. Further, understanding the various subtypes, disease behaviour, and malignant potential is essential for tailoring treatment18.

Therefore, this study set out to systematically review the literature, review unique case reports, and interrogate a large multisite single-institution pathology database to develop a contemporary classification system for presacral tumours.

Methods

Study design and registration

This work combined three complementary components: a prospectively registered systematic review of presacral pathologies; a retrospective multisite institutional surgical pathology review; and analysis of pathologies and the development of a contemporary classification. The systematic review was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251175312) and was structured in accordance with the PRISMA framework19.

Systematic review

A structured search of the PubMed, Web of Science, and Cochrane databases was performed using terms related to presacral, retrorectal, tailgut, and sacrococcygeal lesions in combination with terms including tumour, mass, lesion, solid, cyst, benign, and malignant (Table S1). The search encompassed studies published from 1 January 1975 through 1 December 2025. Records were screened to identify original studies reporting surgically managed presacral tumours with extractable pathology data. Records were excluded if they were duplicates, did not present presacral tumours, did not include pathology, were not published in English, or were reviews or small (< 10 patients) case report series. Records were screened by two authors (D.C., M.F.), with disagreements resolved by secondary review and consensus. The full text was reviewed by four authors (D.C., M.F., W.R.G.P., S.R.K.), and data were extracted using a standardized form that included the author, year, institution, study size, and reported pathology. Risk of bias and/or methodological quality were not assessed because the extraction focused only on pathology.

Retrospective review

After approval from the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board (24-004785), a retrospective analysis of presacral surgical pathology was undertaken at the Mayo Clinic from 1 January 1986 to 1 December 2025. A rapid automatic keyword extraction technique was used in Mayo Database Explorer and in a centralized pathology database to screen for unique ‘presacral’ or ‘retrorectal’ pathology not previously identified in the systematic review. Unique pathologies were cross-referenced with chart review to confirm presacral origin.

Classification

The resulting list of pathological descriptors was reviewed by two pathologists (R.P.G., J.C.C.) in collaboration with three colorectal surgeons (E.J.D., S.R.K., W.R.G.P.). Synonymous pathological names were rationalized to World Health Organization (WHO) standard terminology. Obsolete terminology and generic classifications were removed, and non-neoplastic presacral processes were excluded. Candidate entities were then organized using recurring biological descriptors. A contemporary classification system was then developed through an iterative process, building on historical classification systems.

Results

The systematic review identified 5997 records. After removal of 2064 duplicates, 3933 titles and abstracts were screened. Of these, 330 full-text articles underwent eligibility assessment, and 203 studies met the final inclusion criteria and presented presacral pathology (Fig. 1). Some 3606 patients’ pathology reports were identified on retrospective review of Mayo Clinic data. This review found an additional 19 terms not reported in the systematic review. Therefore, 200 pathological terms were ultimately identified (Table S4).

After expert review and terminology harmonization, 76 entities were defined within a new classification using recurring biological descriptors: developmental versus acquired, lineage (neurogenic, osseous/haematopoietic, soft tissue, or miscellaneous), and behaviour (benign, locally recurrent without metastatic potential, locally recurrent with low metastatic potential, malignant non-sarcoma, or malignant sarcoma). Discussion

Historical classifications of presacral tumours have been indispensable in the study and management of this rare disease entity. However, these classifications were developed from a now relatively small pool of then-known pathologies. The original framework of congenital, neurogenic, osseous, and miscellaneous tumours was established on the basis of Lovelady and Dockerty's 1949 Mayo Clinic experience of managing 127 women with extragenital pelvic tumours9. Uhlig and Johnson, from the Portland Surgical Center in Oregon, updated the system in 1975 by adding further clarification regarding developmental cysts (epidermoid, dermoid, mucus-secreting, teratoma)5. In their paper, Uhlig and Johnson stated that they had ‘followed the classification of these tumours and cysts as presented in 1949 by Lovelady and Dockerty: Congenital, inflammatory, neurogenic, osseous, miscellaneous’5. Lovelady and Dockerty9 never discussed an inflammatory category in their paper; instead, they included the term inflammatory under a miscellaneous category. The first notation in the literature on the addition of an inflammatory category was, in fact, by Charles W. Mayo (the son of Mayo Clinic co-founder Charles H. Mayo) in 195410. Other authors have proposed additional revisions, including eliminating the inflammatory category and simplifying into broader categories.108,214 However, Uhlig and Johnson’s 1975 classification5 has remained the most commonly used to date.

Across 203 systematically identified studies and a large multisite pathology review, the modern literature documents a much broader and more biologically diverse spectrum of presacral tumours than older systems were designed to accommodate. Equally important, the literature remains hampered by variable criteria and inconsistent nomenclature. Some reports include inflammatory or postoperative processes alongside true neoplastic processes, whereas others use historical labels or overlapping terminology for the same lesion. A classification intended for contemporary practice must therefore do more than catalogue names. It must also rationalize terminology and separate primary tumours from secondary presacral processes.

The proposed Mayo Clinic presacral tumour classification provides a contemporary system grounded in clinical practice with a focus on neoplastic processes. Accordingly, the system deliberately removes non-neoplastic processes. This is a shift from the historical inclusion of secondary abnormalities such as abscesses, hematomas, granulomatous reactions, fistulas, perforations, and postoperative changes11–13,137,148,185. Retaining such processes within the central classification obscures the biology of genuine presacral neoplasms and diminishes the taxonomy's utility for clinicians evaluating a newly diagnosed mass. These secondary abnormalities should simply be referred to by their primary non-neoplastic label rather than as a presacral tumour.

The classification also separates lesions by biological behaviour rather than simply labelling them benign or malignant. That distinction is clinically important because management of presacral tumours has evolved substantially. Although beyond the scope of this paper, it is important to note that the diagnosis and management of presacral tumours have evolved in recent years due to improved imaging modalities, a better understanding of tumour biology, adjuvant chemotherapy and radiation, and more deliberate surgical approaches215. Certain tumours may not need to be resected initially and can instead be treated with neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (for example, Ewing’s sarcoma, osteogenic sarcoma, neurofibrosarcoma). Large pelvic desmoids can be removed more easily after their size is reduced with radiation. Other lesions may be treated primarily with chemotherapy alone (lymphomas, gastrointestinal stromal tumours). Hormonal therapy has shown benefit in reducing the size of giant aggressive angiomyxomas, and immunotherapy is showing promise for treating advanced sacrococcygeal chordomas216,217. To decrease intraoperative bleeding, preoperative selective coil embolization of feeding vessels may be useful for some large vascular tumours, such as solitary fibrous tumours218. Some patients with asymptomatic benign disease may simply be observed92. It is the authors’ belief that a more succinct classification system will only further enhance understanding of the extensive differential presacral pathologies present and help guide physicians and surgeons in managing these rare tumours locally with a multidisciplinary team or via referral to expert centres.

A further advantage of the present framework is explicit recognition of sarcomatous disease. The historical literature increasingly includes a wide array of osseous and soft-tissue sarcomas in the presacral compartment, and these lesions often warrant referral patterns, staging considerations, and multidisciplinary input distinct from those for non-sarcomatous tumours. Similarly, the soft-tissue category has expanded over time and now includes lesions that were previously scattered across miscellaneous groupings. Consolidating these tumours into lineage-based categories improves usability while preserving clinical nuance.

The use of developmental rather than congenital terminology is also intentional. Benign cystic remnants and related lesions may be congenital in origin, but malignant lesions encountered in this compartment are not congenital malignancies in the clinical sense; rather, they arise from developmental remnants or undergo malignant transformation over time. This terminology better reflects biology while preserving continuity with the historical classification literature.

This study has several strengths. It integrates a prospectively registered systematic review with a large multisite institutional pathology review and multidisciplinary consensus on terminology harmonization. It also preserves the practical strengths of older classification systems while updating them to reflect contemporary pathology and management. The study nevertheless has important limitations. It is possible that the retrospective review of a single multisite institution introduced bias in pathology selection; however, this was likely suitably counterbalanced by the large systematic review. The underlying literature of the systematic review is heterogeneous and dominated by retrospective surgical series, an inherent limitation. It was also intentionally focused on large series to capture those with experience in the pathological diagnosis and management of presacral tumours. However, excluding case reports or small series may prevent the capture of exceedingly rare diagnoses. Indeed, the list of pathologies does not purport to be exhaustive and will likely continue to expand over the coming years.

Ultimately, as the diagnosis of presacral tumours increases, more surgeons will be asked to evaluate these patients. Although most will encounter a patient with a presacral tumour in their career, few will have the opportunity to treat a large volume of these complex lesions. To improve patient care, outcomes, and communication between physicians and patients, this inclusive and updated classification system is urgently needed to further advance the successful treatment of these enigmatic tumours. Such a system will help clinicians direct patients to appropriate specialists and treatment pathways, which may include observation, chemotherapy, radiation, and various surgical approaches.

Overall, there have been significant changes in the diagnosis, evaluation, and treatment of presacral tumours since the adoption of the original classification system described by Lovelady and Dockerty in 19499, and updated by Uhlig and Johnson in 19755. Given the broad differential diagnosis and significant implications of misdiagnosis and mismanagement, the recognition of presacral pathology and an understanding of appropriate management are imperative for improving patient care and outcomes. The contemporary Mayo Clinic presacral tumour classification aims to improve patient care and communication between physicians and patients.
