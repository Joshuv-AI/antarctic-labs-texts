The role of indigenisation: China-manufactured PFA devices provide an effective solution to AF ablation
Authors: Yan Yao
Published: 2026

The history of catheter ablation for atrial fibrillation (AF) is, in many ways, a history of energy. From the early days of direct-current shock to the refinement of radiofrequency thermal energy, and then to the cryo-balloon, each epoch has been defined by the modality through which we seek to electrically silence the pulmonary veins, empirically. We now stand at the threshold of another such epoch—pulsed field ablation (PFA)—and what is striking about this moment is not merely the strategy and physics of irreversible electroporation, but the efforts of its manufacture. For the first time in the history of cardiac electrophysiology, a disruptive technology is being developed, refined, and brought to market at scale not only in Minneapolis, Haifa, and Boston, but also in Shenzhen, Chengdu, and Shanghai. This is indigenisation, and it matters profoundly to the global electrophysiology community.

Innovation as a Chinese thought process

There is a persistent, if unspoken, assumption in Western medical discourse that innovation flows in one direction: from the laboratories of North America to the clinics of Europe and then the developing world. This assumption is overdue for correction. The development of PFA technology in China is not a story of imitation or reverse engineering; in many respects, it represents genuine divergent innovation.

By the end of May 2026, at least nine Chinese PFA systems have received approval by the National Medical Products Administration (NMPA). The first generation of Western PFA systems—Boston Scientific's FARAPULSE, Medtronic's PulseSelect, and Johnson & Johnson's VARIPULSE—have largely pursued a ‘One-shot’ or ‘wide-footprint’ philosophy, deploying basket-shaped or loop-shaped catheters designed to deliver circumferential energy in a single application. Chinese manufacturers have not merely copied Western designs; they have interrogated the clinical limitations of first-generation PFA and engineered around them. Although several Chinese firms including APT, JJET, Dinova EP, Insight Life, Microport, and Accupulse have developed loop or flower-shaped catheters, most of which provide integrated three-dimensional mapping systems, reflecting the strong influence of the ‘Total 3D EP Mode’ promoted by Fuwai Hospital since March 2011 in China's electrophysiology practice.

The nanosecond-PFA (ns-PFA) systems, for instance Pulsecare and LotosPFA, address the neuromuscular stimulation and air embolism risks associated with earlier microsecond systems, enabling conscious sedation rather than general anaesthesia—a feature of profound relevance to healthcare systems where anaesthesiologist availability is a bottleneck. The SCENA-AF multicenter trial of this system reported 100% acute pulmonary vein isolation success, an 88.3% one-year treatment success rate, and over 92% of procedures completed under conscious sedation without device-related serious adverse events. However, by shortening the pulse width greatly, these system at least doubled the output voltage, the potential risks or disadvantages need to be observed.

The Chinese innovation ecosystem operates with a distinct temporal logic. Where Western device development is often constrained by legacy regulatory frameworks, entrenched supply chains, and the inertia of existing market share, Chinese firms benefit from a regulatory environment—the NMPA ‘innovative medical device green channel'—that accelerates review for domestically developed breakthrough technologies. The result is that Chinese PFA products have, in several instances, reached the domestic market ahead of their Western counterparts, creating a competitive landscape unprecedented in the history of cardiac electrophysiology.

Technical distinctiveness of Chinese PFA devices

The technical specifications of Chinese PFA devices reveal a design philosophy attuned to specific clinical and economic constraints. APT Medical (a subsidiary of Mindray) has developed a PFA system integrating three functions—pressure sensing and contact indication via impedance measurement, magnetic-electrical dual-localization three-dimensional mapping, and pulsed field ablation—into a single platform. This is claimed to be the world's first system to fuse all three capabilities. The pressure-sensing feature provides real-time feedback on electrode-tissue coupling, enabling selective discharge based on contact quality, thereby enhancing lesion durability while reducing the risk of ineffective applications. Clinical trials indicated an average procedural treatment time of approximately 50 min, with 100% acute pulmonary vein isolation success and no device-related serious adverse events.

The significance of this integration cannot be overstated. In Western markets, electroanatomical mapping (e.g., CARTO, NavX) and ablation energy delivery are often separate platforms, requiring proprietary interfaces and licensing agreements. The APT HT-9000 and JJET LEAD systems integrate conventional and 3D mapping into one, reducing expenditure for hospitals, simplifying workflow, and lowering the barrier to adoption in electrophysiology laboratories. This is innovation born of necessity—in a healthcare system where cost-effectiveness is paramount, and where the diffusion of technology to secondary and tertiary hospitals outside major metropolitan centres is a policy priority.

While the JJET FocalPoint contact force catheter was the first approved PFA catheter for supraventricular tachycardia application and achieved satisfactory results, the APT AForcePlus targets atrial fibrillation ablation in the classic point-by-point mode but replaces radiofrequency power with pulsed field energy, eliminating the need for irrigation. Both catheters provide a very attractive feature: no general anaesthesia is required. Combined with a PentaRay-like mapping catheter, the AForcePlus PFA offers a new choice for refractory cases that have undergone multiple radiofrequency or cryoablation procedures. Our recently published study indicates that this catheter achieved significantly higher success rates than radiofrequency ablation for atypical atrial flutter following multiple prior procedures.

What distinguishes these devices is not merely their engineering, but their embeddedness in a clinical context that differs from the Western trial environment. Chinese PFA trials have been conducted across a spectrum of anaesthesia protocols—general, deep sedation, and local—reflecting the reality of Chinese operating theatre logistics. The devices are designed to function effectively under local anaesthesia plus sedation, a requirement that has driven innovations in pulse waveform design to minimise neuromuscular stimulation. This is not a secondary feature; it is a primary design constraint that has yielded genuine technical advancement with global applicability.

Clinical evidence and outcomes

The body of clinical evidence for Chinese PFA devices is growing rapidly, though it remains younger than that for Western systems. The FARAPULSE system, with its ADVENT randomised trial and MANIFEST-17K real-world registry exceeding 17,000 patients, currently holds the largest published evidence base. However, the gap is narrowing. The SCENA-AF trial for the NxPFA nanosecond system, the LEAD-PFA multicentre registry, and the Huitech PFA pivotal study are generating data that meet international regulatory standards.

Importantly, Chinese clinical trials are increasingly designed with global regulatory acceptance in mind. The NMPA's alignment with International Medical Device Regulators Forum (IMDRF) principles, and the growing practice of conducting parallel FDA, CE, and NMPA submissions, means that data generated in Chinese centres is increasingly transferable to Western regulatory dossiers. This is a critical evolution: it signals that Chinese clinical science is no longer provincial but is aspiring to, and achieving, global standards.

The real-world adoption data are equally instructive. APT Medical has reported over 13,100 PFA procedures by the end of May 2026. JJET has achieved 11,000 in the first six months this year. The learning curve for Chinese operators appears shallow, facilitated by the intuitive design of domestic devices and the familiarity of Chinese electrophysiologists with the underlying 3D mapping platforms. JJET reported over 2000 clinical cases with a one-year success rate of 76%, approaching international benchmarks. These volumes reflect not merely market size but genuine clinical confidence in domestically manufactured technology.

The business of indigenisation: from import substitution to global export

The economic case for PFA indigenisation extends beyond the walls of the catheterisation laboratory. China is the world's second-largest medical device market, yet it has historically been a net importer of high-value cardiovascular technology. The electrophysiology market, in particular, has been dominated by Johnson & Johnson (Biosense Webster), Abbott, Medtronic, and Boston Scientific. PFA represents an inflection point where this balance may shift.

The Chinese government's ‘Planning Outline for the National Medical and Health Service System’ explicitly mandates progressive increases in the procurement of domestically manufactured medical equipment. Provincial-level centralised procurement for electrophysiology consumables has already been implemented across multiple alliances, covering most of the national market. Under these frameworks, domestic manufacturers have achieved volume through competitive pricing, accelerating market penetration and reducing healthcare expenditure.

The cost advantage is structural. Chinese PFA manufacturers operate with lower research and development overheads, streamlined regulatory pathways for domestic approval, and manufacturing ecosystems in Shenzhen and Suzhou that offer world-class precision engineering at significantly lower unit costs. This is not ‘cheap’ manufacturing; it is efficient manufacturing. The consequence is that Chinese PFA catheters and generators can be priced at a fraction of Western equivalents while maintaining comparable technical specifications and, increasingly, comparable clinical outcomes.

Naturally, Chinese medical device firms are no longer content with domestic market capture. The APT PFA system has already launched in Indonesia and Central Asia while the Microport and JJET also on the way. The export of PFA technology from China to emerging markets carries particular significance. Nations in Global south face the same AF epidemic—driven by aging populations and increasing cardiovascular risk factor prevalence—but lack the healthcare budgets to absorb premium Western device pricing. Chinese PFA systems, offered at accessible price points with technical support and training packages, represent a viable pathway for technology transfer to these regions. This is not merely commerce; it is a form of global health equity.

Comparison with western devices: a new parity

The comparison between Chinese and Western PFA devices must move beyond the simplistic frame of ‘imitation versus innovation.’ The technical and clinical landscapes are now characterised by parity in some domains, and Chinese advantage in others.

In terms of acute efficacy, all major PFA systems achieve near-universal acute pulmonary vein isolation. The differentiator lies in durability, safety profile, and procedural efficiency. The PULSAR trial of the Globe system (a Western device) reported 95% durable pulmonary vein isolation at redo procedures and a 77.8% one-year primary effectiveness rate. The SCENA-AF trial of the Chinese NxPFA system reported 88.3% one-year success and 100% acute pulmonary vein isolation. These are not statistically comparable head-to-head, but they indicate that Chinese systems are operating within the same performance envelope.

Where Chinese devices may hold genuine advantage is in specific safety domains. The nanosecond-pulse technology of NxPFA substantially reduces intravascular gas formation and erythrocytic damage, addressing a theoretical concern with microsecond PFA systems. The integrated pressure-sensing and mapping of the APT system reduces the risk of ineffective applications and may lower the incidence of pulmonary vein reconnection. There is a new PRA catheter designed by JJET just finished their registration trial for the ventricular tachyarrhythmias specific, which is the first one in the world.

Conversely, Western systems currently lead in the depth of real-world evidence, the breadth of international regulatory approvals, and the integration with established electroanatomical mapping ecosystems. The FARAPULSE system, with its CE, FDA, and NMPA approvals, and its extensive registry data, remains the global benchmark for safety and efficacy documentation. The VARIPULSE system's integration with the CARTO 3 platform offers real-time catheter visualisation that Chinese systems are still developing. Yet these advantages are not permanent. The velocity of Chinese regulatory and clinical development suggests that the evidence gap will close within this decade.

A Clarion Call

The indigenisation of PFA technology in China is not an isolated commercial phenomenon. It is a harbinger of a broader reconfiguration of global medical technology. For decades, the narrative of cardiovascular innovation has been authored in Western academic medical centres and industrial laboratories. The emergence of Chinese PFA as a clinically viable, economically competitive, and technically distinctive alternative challenges this narrative fundamentally.

For the electrophysiology community, this should be welcomed, not resisted. Competition drives innovation. The presence of Chinese PFA in the global market will exert downward pressure on device pricing, expand access to AF ablation in resource-limited settings, and catalyse further technical refinement across all manufacturers. The patients who stand to benefit number in the tens of millions. For Chinese electrophysiologists and researchers, the transition from domestic adoption to global acceptance requires rigorous, transparent, and internationally audited clinical science. The data should be impeccable, the publications should be in peer-reviewed journals of the highest standard, and the regulatory submissions must meet the most stringent criteria of national authorities worldwide.

For Western manufacturers, the message is equally clear: the era of uncontested market dominance in electrophysiology is ending. The competitive landscape will now be defined by genuine technical differentiation, cost-efficiency, and clinical value—not by brand heritage or regulatory incumbency. PFA has already changed how we ablate. Chinese indigenisation is changing who ablates, where they ablate, and at what cost. This is not a threat to the established order; it is an invitation to a more equitable, more competitive, and ultimately more innovative future for the treatment of atrial fibrillation.
