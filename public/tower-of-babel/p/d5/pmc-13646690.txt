Directed Evolution of Plant‐Associated Bacteria Enhances Plant Holobiont Stress Tolerance
Authors: Mohammadhossein Ravanbakhsh, Wu Xiong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPlant stress responses are shaped by both the plant genome and its associated microbial communities, which together form the plant holobiont. Given the rapid adaptive potential of plant‐associated microbes, we hypothesised that directed evolution under selective pressure can accelerate the development of holobiont‐level stress tolerance. Directed evolution of the plant‐associated bacterium Bacillus subtilis MR21 produced three evolved strains (EV1–EV3) with distinct plant‐beneficial traits. These strains enhanced coriander growth under conditions of lead (Pb) toxicity and micronutrient deficiency. The evolved bacteria improved rhizosphere conditions by reducing Pb availability and increasing the availability of essential nutrients, including phosphorus, iron and zinc, thereby enhancing plant holobiont stress tolerance. In addition, rhizosphere detoxification and elevated production of organic acids, such as succinic and glutamic acid, contributed to improved soil chemical conditions. Together, these processes promoted a by‐product mutualism within the rhizosphere, in which detoxifying bacteria indirectly supported less beneficial members of the microbial community. We conclude that directed evolution of plant‐associated bacteria provides a natural and efficient strategy to enhance plant stress tolerance at the holobiont level, offering a complementary approach to conventional breeding and genetic engineering.Keywords: directed evolution, evolved bacteria, experimental evolution, heavy metal toxicity, plant holobiont, plant nutrition, plant‐associated bacteria

Introduction

Plant breeding plays a vital role in ensuring global food security by creating new varieties and supporting crop production worldwide. However, due to the time‐consuming nature of traditional plant breeding, these efforts are primarily focused on a relatively narrow range of major cash crops. At the same time, plant breeding requires more effort than ever to face increasing pressures related to disease, the phasing out of pesticides and climate change. Emerging breeding innovations face challenges related to regulatory frameworks and social acceptance in various parts of the world (Lassoued et al. 2018). As a result, there is a growing need for alternative strategies to support future agriculture in an ever‐changing environment.

There is a growing recognition that a plant's response to its environment extends beyond its own genetic background and involves intricate associations with various microorganisms, including bacteria, fungi and protists (Jacoby et al. 2017; Francioli et al. 2025; Kampouris et al. 2025). Plants and their associated microorganisms collectively shape the performance of the plant holobiont (Vandenkoornhuyse et al. 2015; Ravanbakhsh et al. 2018), highlighting the interdependence between plant evolution and the evolution of its associated microorganisms. Leveraging the diversity and potential of plant‐associated microorganisms to enhance plant tolerance at the holobiont level has emerged as a promising approach to creating enhanced plant stress tolerance. In our recent study, we demonstrated that modifications in the genes of microbes associated with plants can yield virtually identical plant phenotypes compared to modifications in plant genes (Ravanbakhsh et al. 2020). Therefore, by manipulating the plant genome or its associated microbes, plant phenotypes such as nutrient status can be enhanced (Jousset and Ravanbakhsh 2022). We proposed that an evolution‐based approach can be used to naturally evolve plant‐associated microbes and examine their impact on the associated plant.

The experimental evolution approach provides valuable insights into the capabilities and limitations of bacteria to survive and function in diverse environments (Lawrence et al. 2012). It serves as a powerful tool to create novel mutualistic phenotypes and predict potential interactions with plant hosts and other members of the microbiome (E. Li et al. 2021). We argue that the directed evolution of plant‐associated bacteria may help to enhance bacterial survival, improve their plant‐beneficial traits and increase their adaptation competence to colonise the plant rhizosphere.

The functionality of the bacterial population is contingent upon a range of abiotic and biotic factors in the rhizosphere area (Foster and Wenseleers 2006). Some microbial populations have evolved mutualistic interactions with plants, whereby microbes receive carbon and energy in exchange for a range of services, such as protection from pathogens or fixing essential nutrients. However, the coevolution of such microbial populations with their plant host is susceptible to the evolution of microbial cheaters who receive benefits from the plant without delivering services. As microbial functions that positively impact plant performance can be metabolically costly, microbial cheaters can gain a selective advantage over cooperators, which will lead to an increase in their relative abundance. In several plant‐microbe interactions in the context of rhizobia (Kiers et al. 2003), mycorrhizal fungi (Kiers et al. 2011) and wasps (Jandér and Herre 2010), it has been shown that plants can distinguish cheaters and sanction them by providing them with less C and energy. However, unlike these controlled environments with only one or a few genotypes, the rhizosphere presents a distinctive challenge due to the coexistence of diverse mixtures of beneficial and less beneficial bacterial strains in an open system with close proximity (Kennedy and de Luna 2005).

In this study, we established an experimental evolution platform to facilitate the evolution of Bacillus subtilis MR21, a strain known to be able to improve plant performance under stress conditions such as the presence of heavy metals (Ilyas et al. 2022). The bacterium serves as part of the bacterial library that was originally isolated from the rhizosphere of tomatoes in southern Iran. As plant‐microbe interactions are often influenced by specific plant species and environmental factors, evolving bacteria can enhance their adaptability to colonise more diverse plant species under specific environmental conditions. This adaptability may be particularly important for optimising their performance in various rhizospheres and under different environmental pressures. To investigate the dynamics of bacterial evolution and their interaction with the host plant, we employed a simplified holobiont system using Coriandrum sativum L., a common plant in the horticulture context. In this controlled in vivo system, we inoculated the coriander plants with either multiple single bacteria or a minimal consortium of evolved bacteria. We examined the impact of ancestor bacterium, evolved strains and their consortia on plants' ability to respond to abiotic stresses of heavy metals and micronutrient deficiency. Using a sterile in vivo system allows us to closely examine bacterial colonisation and interactions with the host plant under stress conditions. In such circumstances, plants actively modify the rhizosphere, the region surrounding the root system. These modifications involve the secretion of various compounds, including detoxifiers, such as low‐weight organic molecules, sugars and chelating compounds like phytochelatins (e.g., Fe‐chelators). These compounds alter the availability of toxic and essential elements by changing their chemical forms (Kelebemang et al. 2017). Changes in the chemistry of the plant rhizosphere play a crucial role in ensuring plant survival and growth under conditions of nutrient deficiency and toxicity. This research focuses specifically on examining the effects of evolved bacteria derived from the in vitro experimental evolution experiment on rhizosphere chemistry and plant tolerance. We utilised a controlled rhizosphere system to explore the potential of making a synthetic community among evolved bacteria in a context that is less competitive and more cooperative (as the whole community originated from one ancestor). Previous studies have shown that synthetic communities of beneficial bacteria, despite some benefits (i.e., more redundancy in identity), often struggle to promote plant growth due to interspecific competition (Jousset et al. 2011). In addition, our controlled system allows us to investigate the potential ability of the coriander plant to sanction less beneficial evolved bacteria resulting from experimental evolution. We hypothesise that (1) the directed evolution approach can favour the evolution of beneficial plant‐associated microbes, leading to the emergence of bacterial strains that can adapt to the new condition and are more beneficial to the plants than their ancestors, (2) the effectiveness of bacteria in protecting plants will increase by creating a minimal community out of multiple evolved bacteria and (3) the host plant can recognise the more beneficial evolved bacteria within a minimum community of bacteria (and in a close system) and give them priority (i.e., in resource allocation). We will later discuss the potential evolutionary consequences of having multiple evolved bacteria, including more beneficial and less beneficial bacteria, associated with coriander plants within a minimal community.

Material and Method

In Vitro Experiment

Bacteria Model Strain and Experimental Evolution

We chose B. subtilis MR21 as our model bacterium (Table S1). This strain was originally isolated from the rhizosphere of tomato plants and tested for the production of microbial metabolites reported to be important from a plant growth perspective (Penrose and Glick 2003). Here, we passed this bacterium through in vitro experimental evolution steps, primarily to improve the bacteria's fitness to grow in the coriander rhizosphere, and under conditions of chemical or nutritional stress. We later used the evolved bacteria in two in vivo greenhouse tests with coriander plants (C. sativum).

Bacterial strains were kept as frozen stocks at −80°C. Before each experiment, one single colony of bacteria was grown overnight on 30 g L−1 TSB or TSB supplemented with 100 μg mL−1 tetracycline. TSB (Tryptic soy broth) medium consisted of tryptone (pancreatic digest of casein) (17 g L−1), soytone (peptic digest of soybean) (3 g L−1), sodium chloride (5 g L−1), dipotassium phosphate (K2HPO4) (2.5 g L−1) and D(+) glucose (2.5 g L−1). Bacteria were harvested by centrifugation (6000 g, 10 min), washed three times with 10 mM MgSO4 and adjusted to a density of 108 cells mL−1 before the start of the experiment. These bacteria were considered the ‘Ancestral’ state (later referred to as ‘Anc’).

Twenty microliters of bacterial suspension were added to 160 µL of soil minimal medium (Table S2; DF minimal medium composition) in a 96‐well plate, and placed on the rotary shaker (125 rpm) at 25°C. After 48 h of growth, 20 µL of grown bacteria (Anc) were kept in each well, and 140 µL of the medium was replaced with the new medium. Three designed media were used in this study, which are described in detail in the Supporting Materials (Supporting Information S1: Supporting Text; Method). The media were designed to create evolutionary pressure (metal toxicity or nutrient deficiency pressure) on the Anc bacterium. The bacteria were grown on these media for 48 h one growth cycle. This 48‐h growth cycle was repeated for 56 generations, resulting in three different evolved bacteria (Figure 1a, where (1) evolved bacterium No. 1 (EV1), the control selection line, evolved under non‐stress conditions in exudate‐enriched medium, and served as the baseline evolutionary reference for comparison with stress‐adapted lines (EV2 and EV3), (2) evolved bacterium No. 2 (EV2) represents the future of the Anc bacterium grown under Pb toxicity and (3) evolved bacterium No. 3 (EV3) represents the future of the Anc bacterium grown under deficiency of essential elements. Throughout the experimental evolution process, strain identity and culture integrity were monitored by regular inspection of colony morphology after each evolutionary cycle. Cultures were streaked on solid medium and examined for signs of contamination or mixed microbial populations. A near full‐length fragment of the 16S rRNA gene of the ancestor and evolved strains (EV1–EV3) was amplified and sequenced by Sanger sequencing. Sequencing procedures and the corresponding FASTA sequences are provided in the Supporting Materials (Text S1, Table S3). Details of the media, root exudate collection and experimental evolution test are described in the Supporting Materials (Text S1). Three replications per medium were included. In the course of evolution (every 16 days represents four generations), the evolved bacteria compared to the Anc in terms of some previously proposed beneficial traits of plant‐associated bacteria (e.g., auxin, EPS, biofilm). The details of the method for analysing the bacteria's beneficial traits are described in the Supporting Materials (Text S2). The last generation was grown in SMM solidified agar medium (similar composition of liquid medium), and a single isolate from each replicate was picked randomly from the final population, re‐grow in the liquid medium and kept in −80 as a reference. Evolved bacteria were used separately and in a minimal consortium for the in vivo experiment, to evaluate their plant beneficial traits and colonisation rate of evolved bacteria, in single inoculation, and within the community in the plant rhizosphere.

Construction of Minimal Community Comprising Evolved Bacteria

We made minimal consortia from three evolved bacteria and compared the effectiveness of consortia to the ancestral bacterium and a mock treatment (as a baseline) in the in vivo experiment. We harvested the bacteria, resuspended them in 10 mM MgSO4, and adjusted the final population to 108 bacteria per ml (O.D. equal to 1). To create different consortia, we used an equal share of each involved bacterium. In single‐strain inoculation, 100% of a single bacterium with an O.D. of 1 was used as an inoculant. In double‐ and triple‐inoculation, 50% of each bacterium and 33.3% of each bacterium were used, and the final OD600 values were kept at 1. The consortia were designed to show the power of experimental evolution to create a synthetic minimal community (out of one strain) and compare the protectiveness of the consortia with that of the ancestor. Experimental evolution was anticipated to yield distinct bacterial phenotypes, creating a wider array of beneficial traits compared to individual strains. By integrating bacteria with complementary traits, such as toxicity protection and enhanced micronutrient solubility, into minimal consortia, we expected to substantially augment their potential for synergistic benefits. This synergy among bacterial strains within the minimal consortia was anticipated to surpass the individual beneficial capabilities observed in single strains. To facilitate CFU quantification of the bacterial strains in in vivo experiments, the evolved bacteria were transformed with plasmid pTB497.1 and pTB498.1. These plasmids harbour the GFP and mKATE2 gene (SHCHERBO et al. 2009) under the control of the constitutive promoter and a tetracycline resistance gene within the flanking regions of the amyE gene. This allowed CFU quantification by plating the bacterial suspension on a selected medium in an in vivo plant experiment.

To explore the colonisation rate of bacteria when they co‐inoculate with the bacteria beyond the B. subtilis background, we inoculated evolved bacterial EV2 within a minimal consortium (EV2‐M51‐K31) containing two bacterial strains from different backgrounds. Enterobacter cloacae M51 and Pseudomonas fluorescens K31 were selected as co‐inoculant. We aimed to explore whether the higher colonisation at the community level is species‐specific (specific to a community of B. subtilis species) or if other bacteria might undergo similar selection, potentially due to shared public goods like exudates. Both M51 and K31 bacterial strains are plant‐beneficial strains and were isolated from tomato plants. The bacteria were inoculated into young coriander plants either by themselves or later within a new minimal community of K31M51EV2, to investigate their colonisation rate compared to the evolved strain EV2 (the most beneficial bacteria in the in vivo experiment No. 2).

In Vivo Experiments

Effects of Pb on Coriander Plants (Experiment No. 1)

We designed a preliminary experiment to check the effects of 10 mM Pb on coriander plant biomass by examining plant enzymes, anti‐oxidants, and root exudates in a semi‐sterile hydroponic system (as described in the next section). We used these data as a baseline for the general effects of Pb on coriander growth, selected nutrients and plant enzymes. Later, we evaluated the positive and negative effects of evolved bacteria on these plant factors (e.g., plant growth, nutrient content, enzymes and root exudates).

Effects of Evolved Bacteria on Coriander Growth and Pb Tolerance (In Vivo Experiment 2)

Coriander plants (C. sativum) were grown in a hydroponic system with a half‐strength Hoagland solution (as a baseline, Table S4). The primary materials (substrate, containers, tubes) were autoclaved. The plant growth conditions (in a growth chamber) were not sterile. Eight‐day‐old plant seedlings were selected based on homogeneity in size and development stage and inoculated with one of seven bacterial treatments (either a single bacterium or a minimal community of bacteria). The bacterial treatments were: (1) evolved bacteria on the background of B. Subtills MR21, in a single inoculation (EV1, EV2 and EV3), or a combination (EV1EV2, EV1EV3, EV2EV3, EV1EV2EV3), (2) ancestral bacterium B. Subtills MR21 Anc) and (3) 10 mM Mg(SO4)2 as a benchmark (non‐inoculated treatment). To obtain a similar starting cell number, the ancestor and evolved bacterial strains were adjusted to an O.D of 1, which yielded an approximate cell number of 108 cells per millilitre upon inoculation. Fifty microliters of bacterial suspension were inoculated into the bottom of the coriander stem. After 8 days, plants were transferred to main pots, containing a mixture of fine sand and perlite (both pasteurised at 90°C for 45 min), saturated with a Hoagland nutrient solution (Kane et al. 2006), enriched with 10 mM Pb (from PbSO4) and placed in the walk‐in growth chamber (20°C, 70% relative humidity, 9 h photoperiod, 200 µmol m−2 s−1 photosynthetic photon flux density) for 3 weeks more, before they harvested on the day of 36. The plant biomass (shoot and root), Nutrient uptake and bacterial colonisation were measured. The concentration of selected nutrients in the plant tissues was measured using ICP (Thermo Scientific ICP 6300 Fischer Scientific, Breda, Netherlands) after dry ashing digestion (Kalra and Soil and Plant Analysis Council 1998).

The Potential Mechanism Underlying Coriander's Tolerance to Toxicity and Micro‐Nutrient Sufficiency When Colonised by Evolved Bacteria (In Vivo Experiments 3 and 4)

We used a hydroponic system to examine the mechanism of plant‐stress alleviation by evolved bacteria on coriander plants. Seeds were scarified in 12% H2SO4 for 15 s and rinsed three times with distilled water. Coriander seeds were sown on the surface (Turface MVP, IL, USA) substrates for 8 days. Eight‐day‐old plants with two leaves were selected based on homogeneity in shape and size. The plants were inoculated with the bacterial strains Anc, EV1 and EV2 (for in vivo experiment No. 3), and Anc, EV1 and EV3 for in vivo experiment No. 4 (as described in details in Text S3), transferred to 120 mL plastic tubes (with eight drainage system per pot, SecurTainer, Sigma‐Aldrich, Netherlands), filled with mixture of pasteurised (90°C) sands and perlite, and kept by a 5‐cm holder.

Harvest and Measurements in In Vivo Experiments

Fresh and DW Measurements

At the time of harvest, plants were extracted from the substrate, and the roots were gently shaken to dislodge any adhering substrate particles. The root materials were placed in 50 mL centrifuge tubes filled with 25 mL of MQ water. The mixture was shaken (30 min at 200 min− 1) and vortexed (30 s), after which a sample was taken as root extract and kept for further analysis (next section). Shoots were separated from the roots using a razor blade, and the plant material was harvested to measure biomass and concentrations of chlorophyll, proline, peroxidase (POD), superoxide dismutase (SOD), reduced glutathione (GSH) and ethylene following standard protocols. The plant analyses are described in detail in the Supporting Materials (Text S4).

Element Measurement Using ICP and Ion Chromatography

The root extracts were collected and aliquoted into three parts. The first part was kept in a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube at 4°C to recover the evolved bacteria from the rhizoplane and for enumeration (which is described in Text S5). Chemical parameters such as pH, EC and dissolved organic carbon (DOC) were measured immediately in the second sample. DOC and elements were measured by a TOC analyser (combustion analysis using TOC‐L, Shimadzu, Japan) and an ICP analyser (Thermo Scientific ICP 6300 Fischer Scientific, Breda, Netherlands). The third sample was filtered through a 0.2 µm filter syringe, concentrated three times (speed vac concentrator, Farmington, USA) and frozen with liquid nitrogen before analysis of soluble ions and Low molecular weight organic acids (LMWOAs) by ion chromatography (Dionex ICS‐3000 equipped with a Dionex IonPac AS11‐HC analytical column, Dionex Corp., Sunnyvale, CA).

Statistical Analysis and MINTEQA2 Modelling

We used principal coordinate analysis to compare the effects of different media and incubation time on bacterial traits (IAA, NO, EPS, Siderophore, Proline, Biofilm, P‐solubilisation capability) in the in vitro experiment (R, package POA) (Haffenden 2019). The last generations of evolved bacteria (EV1, EV2 and EV3) were compared with the ancestor bacterium (Anc) for their beneficial traits by one‐way ANOVAs.

We used one‐way ANOVA (with Tukey post hoc tests) to compare the effects of evolved bacterial strains (EV1, EV2 and EV3) and their richness on plant biomass, nutrient content and plant enzymes. The data analyses were performed by SPSS (V. 22) (IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA). We designed two independent experiments (in vivo No. 3 and No. 4) to understand the potential mechanism underlying coriander's tolerance to Pb toxicity (Experiment 3) and P and Zn deficiency (Experiment No. 4). The results of in vivo experiments No. 3 and 4 were analysed independently using one‐way ANOVA complemented with the Tukey post hoc test.

Plant physiology and signalling (e.g. SOD, POD) in the second in vivo test were evaluated by One‐way ANOVAs complemented with contrast analysis (evolved bacteria vs. ancestral bacteria). The geochemical equilibrium model MINTEQA2s was used to calculate the equilibria of organic and inorganic ions (including P, Zn, Fe and Pb) in the rhizosphere samples (visual MINTEQ. 3.0). This model calculates the distribution coefficient between adsorbed and dissolved phases by calculating the chemical reaction independent of the mechanism. We run this model to evaluate different forms of Pb, Zn, Fe and P in the rhizosphere solution. DOC, EC, pH, cations, anions and LMWOAs were used as MINTEQA2 model input. The chemical forms of Fe, Zn and Pb were extracted from the model as output.

Results

Impact of In Vitro Experimental Evolution on Bacterial Traits

We monitored the evolutionary trajectory of B. subtilis MR21 by assessing several bacterial traits, previously described as plant‐beneficial (Penrose and Glick 2003), on a biweekly basis. We considered the original bacterium B. subtilis MR21 as the ancestral (Anc) bacterial strain, which served as the starting point for our study. To obtain the future evolved bacterium (EV1), the Anc strain was cultured for 56 generations in a DF minimal medium (Table S2; DF minimal medium composition) supplemented with coriander plant exudates (Figure 1a). We later grew the Anc strain on minimal soil medium and plant exudates, and subjected it to specific evolutionary pressures (through directed evolution as described earlier in the method section), resulting in three evolved bacterial strains: EV1, EV2 and EV3. Partial 16S rRNA sequencing confirmed that EV1–EV3 belong to the same species as the ancestral strain, with no evidence of contamination at the taxonomic level. We then compared the bacterial traits and investigated their effects on coriander plants in an in vivo experiment.

We conducted a comparative analysis of the bacterial traits, examining their changes under different evolutionary conditions. Principal component analysis of six selected bacterial traits after 56 generations showed a significant change in bacterial traits in response to different growth media (evolutionary conditions) (Figure 1b) over the course of the experiment (Figure S1) (as measured at seven time points). For example, EV2 (evolved on soil minimal medium supplemented with Pb as an evolutionary pressure) exhibited significantly increased production of bacterial NO, exopolysaccharides (EPS) and biofilms compared to both the ancestral strain and its other evolved bacterial counterparts (Figure 1c; Table S5). Furthermore, exposure to different evolutionary conditions (e.g., a second medium containing non‐soluble nutrients such as Phosphorus and Zinc) resulted in a significant enhancement of the bacterium's ability to metabolise non‐soluble forms of Phosphorus (P), when compared to ancestral strains (Table S5). Interestingly, in an in vivo experiment (described in the subsequent section), coriander plants inoculated with EV3 demonstrated heightened uptake of P, Zn and Fe, in contrast to plants treated with the Anc bacterial treatment.

In Vivo Experiments

Experiment 1: Effects of Pb on Coriander Plants (Biomass, Nutrient and Root Exudates)

We conducted a preliminary experiment to examine the effects of 10 mM Pb treatment on coriander biomass, selected plant signalling components and root exudates. The results demonstrated significant reductions in shoot dry weight (DW) and root DW in the 10 mM Pb‐treated plants compared to the control (no‐Pb treatment) (Table S6 and Figure S2). The treatment with 10 mM Pb significantly enhanced various plant signaling components in coriander, including ethylene, catalase, SOD, POD and GSH (Table S6 and Figure S3). In contrast, the concentrations of root exudates such as tartaric acid, glutamic acid and succinic acid declined in the coriander plants treated with 10 mM Pb (Table S6 and Figure S4). These findings served as a benchmark for assessing the effects of evolved bacteria on plant biomass and stress tolerance (Experiment No. 2), as well as for elucidating the underlying mechanisms (Experiments Nos. 3 and 4). Overall, exposure to 10 mM Pb imposed strong physiological stress on coriander plants, as evidenced by reduced biomass, elevated stress‐related enzymes and suppressed root exudation. This confirms that the selected Pb concentration was sufficient to induce toxicity and provides a clear baseline for evaluating bacterial‐mediated stress mitigation in subsequent experiments.

Experiment 2: Effects of Evolved Bacteria (Single Inoculation or Within Minimal Community) on Coriander Growth and Pb Tolerance

Association with different bacterial strains altered coriander biomass and components of plant stress responses. Notably, inoculation with evolved strains EV2 and EV3 consistently resulted in greater shoot and root biomass than inoculation with either the ancestral strain or EV1, indicating that experimental evolution had enhanced plant‐beneficial functions. Evolved bacteria EV2 and EV3, derived from B. subtilis MR21, exhibited enhanced plant traits such as root DW and shoot DW compared to the ancestral bacterium (representing their past) and the evolved bacterium EV1 (representing an artificial future state of Anc) (Figure 2). In addition to the plant biomass, the concentrations of P, Zn and Fe increased with bacterial richness in the minimal community (Figure S5). Inoculating coriander plants with EV2, EV3 and their combination (EV2EV3) significantly increased plant biomass (Figure 2a,b) and the concentrations of P and Zn in the shoot (Table S6, Figure S5) compared to inoculation with EV1 and the ancestral bacterium. These results demonstrate that bacterial evolutionary history and community composition jointly influence plant growth and nutrient availability under Pb stress, with EV2 and EV3 contributing disproportionately to improved plant performance.

Colonisation Response of Evolved Bacteria on the Coriander Rhizoplane Alone and Within Different Minimal Communities in In Vivo Experiment No. 2

We assessed the colonisation rate of the evolved bacterial strains on the rhizoplane of coriander plants through single inoculation and compared it with the colonisation rate in minimal communities (Figure 2c). In the single inoculation scenario, EV2 demonstrated significantly higher colonisation on coriander plant roots (F 1,102 = 103.7, p < 0.001) compared to the other counterparts, including the ancestral bacterial strain and the other evolved bacterial strains (EV1 and EV3) (Figure 2d). This indicates that EV2 possesses enhanced rhizoplane colonisation ability, which may contribute to stronger plant protective effects observed in Experiment 2 (part 3.2.2).

The colonisation rates of bacterial strains varied among the different minimal communities (whether single, double, or minimal communities) (F 1,102 = 5.59, p < 0.05). Within each community, each evolved bacterial strain exhibited distinct colonisation rates. EV2EV3 exhibited the highest colonisation rate. Focusing on the evolved bacterium within the community, the highest colonisation rate for each bacterial strain was observed when this strain was co‐inoculated with EV2, regardless of whether it was in a double or triple minimal community (Figure 2d). The consistently higher colonisation of co‐inoculated strains in the presence of EV2 suggested that EV2 facilitates a more favourable rhizosphere environment, indirectly supporting the establishment of other bacterial members within the community.

To further explore colonisation dynamics beyond the similar bacterial background (B. subtilis), we selected two coriander‐associated bacteria with a completely different background, E. Cloacae M51 and P. fluorescens K31 (Table S7), and introduced them into the minimal community of K31M51EV2. Interestingly, both M51 and K31 exhibited a high colonisation rate when co‐colonised with EV2 (Figure S6). These findings suggested that the EV2 strain has the potential to improve the colonisation of other co‐inoculated bacterial strains (with different backgrounds) within a minimal community.

Experiment 3: The Potential Mechanism Underlying Coriander's Tolerance to Toxicity When Colonised by EV2

We conducted a separate experiment (experiment 3) to gain insights into the mechanism underlying coriander's tolerance to toxicity when inoculated with the evolved bacterial strain EV2. EV2 strain evolved on a Pb‐enriched medium as described earlier. EV2 was compared to the ancestral bacterium (Anc) and EV1, which was grown on coriander exudates and considered a descendant of the ancestral strain, B. subtilis MR21.

Plant Shoot and Root Biomass and Chlorophyll Content Under Pb Stress Conditions

Inoculation with evolved bacteria EV1 and EV2 resulted in increased coriander shoot biomass and chlorophyll content (Figure 3a–c) and reduced the Pb content (Figure 3d,e) in the coriander plants compared to the Anc bacterial treatment. One‐way ANOVA, complemented with contrast analysis, revealed that the EV2 bacterial strain significantly increased root biomass in coriander plants compared to both Anc and EV1 bacterial treatments. The increase in biomass and chlorophyll content, together with reduced Pb accumulation, indicates that EV2 not only limits Pb uptake but also preserves photosynthetic capacity under Pb stress.

Plant Signalling, Enzymatic Activity and Low Molecular Weight Organic Acids Under Pb Stress Conditions

We observed a significant increase in the levels of Pb‐responsive enzymes, such as catalase, POD, SOD and GSH, and regulators such as ethylene, under Pb treatment (Table S6 and Figure S3). Inoculation of the evolved bacterium EV2 had a significant impact on the plant signalling components in coriander plants under Pb‐treated conditions (Figure 3g). One‐way ANOVA complemented with contrast analysis revealed that the inoculation of coriander plants with the evolved bacterium EV2 led to a reduction in the levels of Pb‐responsive enzymes, such as catalase, POD and SOD, compared to the Anc and EV1 treatments (Figure 3g), and influenced the pattern of root exudates (Figure 3h). The reduced activity of Pb‐responsive antioxidant enzymes in EV2‐inoculated plants suggested a lower physiological stress status, indicating that EV2 effectively alleviated Pb‐induced oxidative stress rather than merely activating stress defence pathways. The concentration of succinic acid and tartaric acid significantly increased upon inoculation with the EV2 compared to inoculations with either EV1 or Anc (Figure 3h). The increased secretion of organic acids, particularly succinic and tartaric acid, suggested a potential role of EV2 in modifying rhizosphere chemistry, which may contribute to Pb immobilisation, reduced metal bioavailability and modification in colonisation rate by bacteria.

Pb Concentration in the Shoot and Roots and Its Chemistry in the Rhizosphere

Inoculation with EV2 significantly reduced the concentration of Pb in the shoot and roots of coriander plants (Figure 3d,e). This reduction in the plants’ Pb uptake was consistent with the alteration of chemical forms of Pb in the rhizosphere solution. The results obtained from the MINTEQA2 equilibrium model revealed that EV2 inoculation led to the conversion of highly available Pb (i.e., Pb2+ free ions) to less available forms such as organic matter association, precipitated carbonate (i.e., Pb(CO3)2), phosphate and glutamate, which were no longer accessible for the plant. These results provide a mechanistic explanation for the reduced Pb uptake observed in EV2‐inoculated plants, linking bacterial activity to rhizosphere detoxification and altered metal speciation.

Experiment 4: The Potential Mechanism Underlying Coriander's Tolerance to Micronutrient Deficiency When Colonised by Evolved Bacteria EV3

We conducted a separate experiment (Experiment 4) to gain insights into the mechanism underlying coriander's tolerance to nutrient deficiency (P and Zn) when inoculated with the evolved bacterial strain EV3 compared to the ancestral bacterium (Anc) and EV1. The EV3 bacterial strain was evolved initially on the medium with reduced availability of P and Zn. We tested whether adaptation of EV3 to nutrient‐limited conditions translates into improved plant nutrient availability and altered rhizosphere chemistry under micronutrient deficiency.

Plant Biomass, Plant Signalling, Enzymatic Activity and Exudates Under Reduced Nutrient Conditions

Inoculation of coriander plants with the evolved bacteria EV3 significantly increased root biomass and nutrient concentrations (P, Zn and Fe concentrations) and nutrient uptake when plants were grown under nutrient‐limiting conditions (Figure 4a–e). These results indicate that EV3 specifically enhances nutrient availability and plant growth under nutrient‐limiting conditions, rather than primarily alleviating oxidative stress. Measured components of plant signalling (such as SOD, POD and GSH) were not significantly influenced by bacterial inoculation (except for ethylene) (Figure 4f). The absence of significant changes in most antioxidant enzymes suggested that nutrient limitation did not induce strong oxidative stress, and that EV3‐mediated growth promotion occurred without activating classical stress‐response pathways. The concentration of ethylene increased in the EV3‐inoculated coriander plants compared to the Anc and EV1 inoculation treatments. Examination of low molecular weight organic acids (LMWOA) showed a significant change in levels of oxalic acid and glutamic acid (Figure 4g). The altered profile of low molecular weight organic acids, particularly oxalic and glutamic acid, suggested that EV3 may modulate rhizosphere chemistry to facilitate nutrient mobilisation.

Shoot Concentrations of P, Fe and Zn in Coriander Plants and Their Speciation in the Rhizosphere

Inoculation with the evolved bacterium EV3 led to a significant increase in the phosphorus (P), iron (Fe) and zinc (Zn) contents in the aboveground parts of the plant (Figure 4c–e). Specifically, the concentration of Fe and Zn increased from 123.2 to 195.4 µg/g and from 70.5 to 115.3 µg/g, respectively, in the shoot of plants associated with EV3 compared to the EV1 counterpart. These increases demonstrate that EV3 markedly improves the translocation of micronutrients to the shoot under nutrient‐deficient conditions. Although the total concentration of Fe and Zn in the rhizosphere solution was not significantly affected by our treatments (27 ± 3.4 mg · L−1 for Fe and 9.2 ± 1.7 mg · L−1 for Zn, with 5.5% and 3.2% difference between Bacterial treatments), the inoculation of evolved bacteria did impact the speciation of these nutrients within the rhizosphere, transforming them into more readily available forms. This indicates that EV3 did not increase the total nutrient pool in the rhizosphere but instead enhanced nutrient bioavailability through chemical transformation. For example, the concentrations of Fe2+ and Zn2+ increased significantly, by 12 and 10 times, in the rhizosphere of coriander plants inoculated with EV3 compared to plants inoculated with EV1 (Figure 4h,i). These more available forms (Fe2+ and Zn2+) stem from the transformation of less available forms, such as carbonate and phosphate complexed ions. The pronounced increase in Fe2+ and Zn2+ highlights a key mechanism by which EV3 improves plant nutrition, namely by converting poorly available nutrient forms into readily absorbable ionic species. Fe and Zn showed high affinity to citrate and tartrate forms in all three bacterial (Anc, EV1 and EV3) inoculations, and it was not affected by inoculation with different bacterial strains (Figure 4h,i). Together, these findings suggested that EV3 enhanced coriander tolerance to micronutrient deficiency by reshaping rhizosphere nutrient speciation rather than by increasing total nutrient concentrations.

Discussion

The responses of plants to the environment depend not only on their genome, but also on their association with a wide range of microorganisms, living inside and around plants (Vandenkoornhuyse et al. 2015; Ravanbakhsh et al. 2020). Given this intertwined co‐regulation of the plant stress tolerance and the potential of rapid evolution among microorganisms (S.‐J. Li et al. 2014), directed evolution approaches may hold the potential to fast‐forward plant stress tolerance at a fraction of the time necessary for breeding or genetic modification of plants.

In this study, we selected B. subtilis MR21 as a model bacterium and passed this strain through 56 cycles of experimental evolution to enhance its ability to survive and grow in the rhizosphere of coriander plants and under Pb‐contaminated conditions. Under these conditions, microorganisms (and the host plants) are exposed to the toxicity of heavy metals as well as deficiencies in the essential micro‐nutrients (Rizvi et al. 2020). This artificially imposed selective pressure led to the rise of bacterial strains that exhibited an overproduction of bacterial metabolites (i.e., EPS and biofilm) involved in enhancing bacterial stress tolerance (Figure 1c). Under these conditions, rhizosphere bacteria often modify their metabolite production to enhance their fitness. Previous studies have shown increased siderophore production in iron‐limiting environments (McHugh et al. 2003), elevated EPS levels under copper toxicity (Anzano et al. 2022) and heightened biosurfactant production in response to drought (Koksharova and Safronov 2022). The EPS and biofilm matrix, for example, can bind heavy metals and reduce their toxicity to bacterial cells (Zhang et al. 2020; Mathivanan et al. 2021).

Inoculation of the evolved bacteria, either as single bacterial inoculants (i.e., EV2 and EV3) or within a minimal bacterial community (e.g., EV2EV3), significantly enhanced plant stress tolerance (Figure 2), for example by increasing plant biomass (Figure 2a,b), chlorophyll content (EV2 inoculation) (Figure 3c) and the level of essential nutrients in plants either exposed to Pb toxicity (Figure S5) or under deficiency of micronutrients such as P and Zn (Figure 4). The enhanced beneficial metabolites in evolved plant‐associated bacteria, such as phosphate‐solubilising compounds, indole‐3‐acetic acid (IAA) (Wang et al. 2022) and EPS (Bhardwaj et al. 2022), have been demonstrated to have positive effects on plant biomass and heavy metal tolerance. In this study, we assessed the effects of evolved bacterial metabolites on element chemistry in the rhizosphere of the coriander plant. Inoculation of EV2 onto plants had a significant impact on the Pb detoxification, subsequently mitigating Pb toxicity in the rhizosphere (Figure 3f). EV2 inoculation led to a tenfold reduction in the presence of highly plant‐absorbable forms of Pb, such as Pb2+ and aqueous Pb ion forms (Figure 3i,j). This reduction translated into a significant decrease in Pb uptake by coriander plants, 66% less than those associated with the ancestral (Anc) bacterial strain (Figure 3d,e). In line with the reduction of Pb toxicity in the rhizosphere, inoculation of EV2 reduced the levels of stress‐responsive enzymes (i.e., Pb‐induced plant enzymes), such as catalase, POD and SOD, compared to the Anc and EV1 treatments (Figure 3g). These enzymes and regulators are important in scavenging reactive oxygen species induced by Pb toxicity (Verbruggen et al. 2009). The reduced levels of these regulators suggested that coriander plants associated with EV2 experienced lower levels of Pb‐induced stress.

The evolved bacterial EV3 strain promoted plant growth and improved the nutritional value (P, Zn and Fe uptake) of coriander plants (Figure 2; Figure 4c–e). EV3 inoculation transforms P and Zn from less‐available forms (e.g., carbonate forms) to highly available forms (e.g., Zn2+) (Figure 4h,i). This suggested that evolving on a nutrient‐minimal medium enhanced the bacterial ability to transform nutrients into more available forms via a phenomenon known as nutrient adaptation (Singh et al. 2022). This adaptation can lead to the production of extracellular enzymes (e.g., P solubilising enzyme) that convert complex element compounds to more available forms (Singh et al. 2022). More available nutrients can eventually benefit the host plant under limited nutrient conditions.

The evolved bacterial strain EV2 exhibited a significantly higher colonisation rate in single inoculation compared to the Anc (ancestral strain), EV1 (representing a potential future evolved bacterial strain) and the EV3 strains (Figure 2d). The inoculation of EV2 in the rhizosphere led to a less toxic environment. This less‐toxic rhizosphere promoted the secretion of organic acids like succinic acid, tartaric acid and glutamic acid by the plant roots (Figure 3h) (Bali et al. 2020). These exudates potentially serve as valuable resources for bacterial communities, as suggested by previous studies (Sharma et al. 2023). The positive correlation observed between the levels of these exudates (tartaric acid and succinic acid) and bacterial colonisation (Anc, EV1 and EV2) in our in vivo experiment No. 2 (Figure S7) provided evidence that these root exudates serve as nutritional resources for bacteria.

Our study showed that creating a synthetic minimal community of evolved bacteria from a single bacterium leads to significant improvements in plant growth, increasing both root and shoot DW (Figure 2a,b). Compared to single‐bacterium inoculation, bacterial communities promote better plant development by broadening functional traits and providing redundancy (Hu et al. 2016). Although synthetic communities often struggle to reliably enhance growth due to interspecies competition (Jousset et al. 2011), our approach, assembling minimal communities from evolved bacteria, offers a natural alternative. This method not only boosts plant Pb tolerance and nutrient availability (e.g., P and Zn) but also strengthens bacterial fitness and interactions in the rhizosphere (Figure 2d).

In this study, we developed a minimal holobiont model consisting of one plant and a community of evolved bacteria derived from a single strain. This reductionist approach allows us to examine plant stress tolerance within a controlled, minimal holobiont context comprising a few strains. While natural systems and full‐scale holobionts involve complex interspecies interactions (Berendsen et al. 2012; Leite et al. 2021), our simplified model offers valuable insights by enhancing the understanding of bacterial colonisation dynamics within a minimal community. First of all, instead of isolating new bacteria and navigating the safety and regulatory hurdles common in biotechnology, this approach allowed the generation of multiple evolved strains from an already‐known bacterium. This allowed for the creation of multiple communities of bacteria with enhanced traits and reduced internal competition. Secondly, the simplified holobiont model enables us to examine the relationship between root exudates and the fitness of evolved bacterial strains in the rhizosphere, specifically whether the plant can selectively favour more beneficial over less beneficial strains (strain‐level recruitment hypothesis). To investigate this, we tested whether the coriander plant could preferentially support the most beneficial evolved bacteria (EV2) within a minimal community through exudate overproduction. Based on the result and to explain this phenomenon (Figure 5), we propose two potential hypotheses. (1) In a ‘recruitment hypothesis’, the evolved bacteria (e.g., EV2) actively detoxify the rhizosphere, creating a healthier environment for the plant. This encourages the plant to secrete more exudates, which in turn select for these bacteria, leading to a mutually beneficial positive reinforcement. (2) Alternatively, the ‘by‐product mutualism’ hypothesis suggests that the bacteria (i.e., EV2) detoxify toxic elements in the rhizosphere, for their own benefit, helping plants as a side effect (Feng et al. 2022). Specific exudates, such as glutamic acid, tartaric acid and succinic acid (Figure 3h), were predominantly produced by the root system unaffected (or less affected) by Pb (Bali et al. 2020) due to conversion into non‐toxic forms (Agarwal et al. 2023). We further examined the community dynamics of evolved bacteria from these two perspectives. From a colonisation perspective, our results provide limited evidence for strain‐level discrimination between more beneficial (e.g., EV2) and less beneficial rhizosphere bacteria during co‐inoculation (Figure 2d). This was evident from the similar colonisation levels of ancestral and evolved bacteria (i.e., Anc, EV1, EV2) with the same Bacillus ancestral background when co‐inoculated in the rhizosphere (Figure 2d). Evolved bacteria from the same ancestor (i.e., bacterial kin) may support each other's colonisation via inclusive fitness effects (Hamilton 1964); However, our findings indicate that the beneficial effects of EV2 extend beyond kin selection, as its inoculation also promotes the colonisation of unrelated co‐inoculated microbes, such as E. cloacae M51 and P. fluorescens K31 (Figure S6). We therefore proposed that EV2 contributes to plant health and supports the surrounding microbial community by fostering a more favourable rhizosphere environment through byproduct mutualism. In this process, the plant supplies nutrients to rhizosphere bacteria via a ‘non‐discriminatory plant microbiome selection’ (Figure 5). While previous studies have shown that host sanctions can effectively control less beneficial symbionts, albeit within specific plant compartments and when few genotypes are present (Kiers et al. 2003, 2011; Jandér and Herre 2010), our findings highlight the unique challenges presented by the open rhizosphere environment and within the rhizosphere community. In the natural system, the rhizosphere is a noisy and non‐compartmentalised system with the coexistence of diverse mixtures of microorganisms in close physical proximity, which further complicates the operation of selection and sanction mechanisms (Denison et al. 2003; Chomicki et al. 2020; Xun et al. 2021). We propose a model of ‘non‐discriminatory bacterial community selection’ in which plants produce exudates to sustain a diverse microbial community, among them beneficial strains such as EV2. While direct selection at the strain level is unlikely, plants support the entire microbial community to enhance overall fitness, even if some less beneficial bacteria (e.g., EV1, M51, or K31) also gain. The benefits conferred by EV2 may promote increased exudate production, further supporting the coexistence of other bacterial strains within the community (Figure 2d) (Figure S6) (Figure 5). To what extent the EV2 service stays evolutionary stable in the long term depends on the costs associated with producing the detoxifying metabolites and their effect on the fitness of individual bacterial cells (i.e., EV2). If the costs to beneficial individuals (e.g., EV2 and their clonemates) outweigh the benefits (i.e. rhizosphere detoxification poses trade‐off costs), less beneficial bacteria (e.g., EV1) that do not pay those costs may become more dominant, regardless of the benefits that EV2 provides to the community. In such cases, the new beneficial evolved bacterium may not persist. In such a system, EV2 may lose its competitive advantage over time compared to its ancestors (or less beneficial counterparts, such as EV1), while still sharing the benefits with other community members. From a biotechnological perspective, this association will still be beneficial to protect exposed plants if invasion by cheaters is slow enough that the beneficial microbes remain dominant at least over the growth season to support the production of more nutritious and healthier plants.

Schematic representation of the mechanism by which evolved bacteria benefit the associated coriander plant. The ancestral bacterium lacks the ability to alter Pb toxicity in the rhizosphere (left grey square). Evolved bacteria (which emerged through directed evolution from the ancestral strain, Figure 1a) transform free bioavailable Pb into a non‐bioavailable form, primarily for their own benefit (Arrow 1). This detoxification process enhances rhizosphere health, benefiting the associated plant (Arrow 2) and facilitating the colonisation of both related (derived from the same ancestor, Figure 2d) and unrelated microbial species (from different backgrounds, Figure S6) through byproduct mutualism (arrow 3). Specific root exudates (Figure 3h) are predominantly produced by plants that are unaffected or less affected by Pb due to this detoxification process, reinforcing a non‐discriminatory positive feedback loop (Arrows 4 and 5). There is a positive correlation between the levels of exudates (tartaric acid and succinic acid) and bacterial colonisation (Figure S7). As a result, the entire microbial community thrives, supported by the plant's exudate overproduction, which sustains a cooperative rhizosphere. Plant stress tolerance is a combination of both plant and associated microbiome traits. Enhanced plant stress tolerance can be approached through both genetic engineering of the plant genome and manipulation of the associated microbiota. Our results provide proof of the concept of diversifying and enhancing the beneficial traits of plant‐associated bacteria, thereby achieving a new plant phenotype. The generality of this approach, context‐dependent effects and whether it is limited to the selection of traits that help individual bacteria survive in soil, an open and uncontrolled system (rather than a controlled system), is unknown. We focused on the phenotypic characterisation of B. subtilis and its evolved mutants to test whether directed experimental evolution of a plant‐associated B. subtilis strain results in stable, functionally relevant phenotypic changes that enhance plant holobiont stress tolerance (Poltak and Cooper 2011; Blake et al. 2021). Future work integrating whole‐genome sequencing and transcriptomics will help identify the genetic basis of the evolved phenotypes resulting from experimental evolution, observed here.

In this study, we focused on the directed evolution of plant‐associated bacteria, presenting a proof‐of‐concept model for breeding microorganisms, rather than the plant itself, to accelerate plant stress tolerance. While we recognise the complexity of the rhizosphere in a full holobiont within natural ecosystems, our work provides a foundational step toward community‐level holobiont evolution, offering a promising tool for enhancing plant breeding at the holobiont level.
