Concomitant acute pulmonary embolism in recent dengue virus infection in an endemic country: case series
Authors: Astrid Putri, Rizky Hendiperdana, Siti Untari, Sumardjo, Listyowati, Syuharul Qomar, Ihsan Nurcahyo, Nugroho Sigit, Flemming Javier Olsen, Vincent Ehreiser, Dmitry Duplyakov, Deepty Ranganathan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere is a substantial gap in the understanding of dengue fever pathogenesis and pathophysiology concerning haemostatic equilibrium alteration. While haemorrhagic manifestation is common in dengue infection, the alteration towards venous thromboembolism (VTE) event is also known despite being infrequently reported. The VTE spectrum is composed of deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE).Case summaryWe reported six cases in similar presentation of acute shortness of breath in the recent phase of dengue virus infection. All patients had increased serum D-dimer level with clinical criteria of pulmonary thromboembolism. Four of six patients had pulmonary thrombosis confirmation from pulmonary angiography computed tomography imaging. The patients were diagnosed with acute pulmonary thrombosis in the setting of recent dengue infection. Parenteral and oral anticoagulants were administered in all patients with favourable outcome in five patients but one patient died during admission.DiscussionHaemorrhagic manifestation concern made the PE complication in dengue infection patients often overlooked. Although PE is uncommonly described as a dengue complication, this may be an underlying mechanism in which well-known complication in dengue commonly described such as circulatory collapse. This has become a challenge for clinicians to broaden the differential diagnosis to include PE in the setting of complicated dengue infection. Introduction

A substantial gap exists in understanding the pathogenesis and pathophysiology regarding haemostatic equilibrium alteration in dengue fever (DENV-1–4). Although haemorrhagic manifestation is common in dengue infection, venous thromboembolism (VTE) is also common, despite being infrequently reported.1–5 The VTE spectrum comprises deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE).6 Given the haemorrhagic manifestation concern in dengue infection, PE is often overlooked. In the clinical setting of haemorrhagic diathesis, significant clinical challenges arise upon the decision to treat PE pharmacologically.4 Herein, we report a case series of PE complications in patients with recent dengue infection.

Case illustration

Patient 1

The patient was a 58-year-old man with shortness of breath and fever with a 3-day onset. His dyspnoea worsened on the day of admission. The vital signs were as follows: heart rate (HR), 113 beats/min; blood pressure (BP), 160/70 mmHg; respiratory rate (RR), 26 breaths/minute; and peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2), 63% initially but improved to 90% with non-rebreathing mask oxygen supplementation.

Electrocardiography (ECG) showed sinus tachycardia with right axis deviation and right ventricular (RV) strain pattern (Figure 1). Laboratory evaluation revealed haemoglobin of 16.7 g/dL (12–16 g/dL); thrombocyte, 177 × 103 cells/µL (140–392 × 103 cells/µL); leucocyte, 24.9 × 103 cells/µL (4–10 × 103 cells/µL); creatinine (Cr), 2.7 mg/dL (0.6–1.1 mg/dL); and urea (Ur), 167 mg/dL (10–50 mg/dL). Significant increases in liver transaminases were also noted: serum glutamic oxaloacetic transaminase (SGOT), 2820 U/L (<35 U/L); serum glutamic pyruvic transaminase (SGPT), 1598 U/L (<41 U/L); troponin I, 9.06 ng/mL (0.01–0.02 ng/mL); D-dimer, 7671 ng/mL (<500 ng/mL); and lactate, 3.2 mmol/L (0.5–2.22 mmol/L). He was also positive for anti-dengue IgM. Echocardiography showed right atrial and ventricular dilation with severe tricuspid regurgitation (TR), regurgitation velocity of 3.8 m/s, and D-shaped left ventricle with preserved RV and LV systolic function. A computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA) revealed a thrombus in the pulmonary artery (PA), with dilation of the main PA diameter (Figure 2).

Electrocardiography of Case 1 showed sinus tachycardia with right ventricular strain.

Computed tomography pulmonary angiography showing a thrombus at the pulmonary artery with dilation of the main pulmonary artery diameter (arrow).

In the intensive cardiac care unit, the patient experienced hypotension 65/34 mmHg, and vasoactive drugs were initiated along with unfractionated heparin (UFH) at 800 units/h. He also experienced type II respiratory failure (pCO2 93 mmHg) with altered mental status and was intubated. Subsequently, his pulmonary embolism severity index (PESI) was Class V, which indicates a high risk of in-hospital mortality.

On Day 4, his haemodynamics improved significantly, and treatments were continued with sildenafil 40 mg t.i.d., beraprost 20 mcg t.i.d., a broad-spectrum antibiotic, and methylprednisolone 62.5 mg given intravenously twice daily. Despite the mild gastrointestinal bleeding, warfarin 2 mg o.d. and UFH were continued. An intravenous proton pump inhibitor was administered, and blood transfusion was not needed. The patient was discharged on Day 6 after admission.

Patient 2

A 69-year-old woman presented with a 4-day fever, chest discomfort, and shortness of breath 1 day before admission. Her vital signs were as follows: HR, 94 beats/min; BP, 89/58 mmHg; SpO2, 98%; and RR, 26 breaths/minute. She also had an altered mental status. However, the physical examination was unremarkable. Electrocardiography showed sinus tachycardia with right-axis deviation. Chest X-ray imaging revealed a hyperlucent area in bilateral apical lobes (Westermark sign) (Figure 3). Wells’ score (4.5) indicated PE, with tachycardia, and PE was likely the first differential diagnosis. This patient had PESI Class V and a high risk of in-hospital mortality.

Chest X-ray imaging showing a hyperlucent area at bilateral apical lobes (Westermark sign).

The laboratory results showed positivity for IgM dengue antibody, as well as thrombocyte of 34 × 103 cells/µL (140–392 × 103 cells/µL); impaired renal function Cr, 1.8 mg/dL (0.6–1.1 mg/dL); Ur, 83 mg/dL (10–50 mg/dL); depletion of sodium and potassium, 124 mmol/L (135–148 mmol/L) and 2.9 mmol/L (3.5–5.3 mmol/L), respectively; increased Troponin I level, (6.82 ng/dL); and significantly increased D-dimer level, > 10 000 ng/mL (<500 ng/mL). Blood gas analysis revealed normal results. Echocardiography showed RV dilatation, positive McConnell’s sign, and preserved left heart function supporting the diagnosis of PE. Subcutaneous low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) enoxaparin 40 mg o.d. and warfarin 2 mg o.d. were administered. The patient was discharged on Day 5 of admission without any bleeding complications.

Patient 3

An 11-year-old girl presented with intense chest pain accompanied by shortness of breath for 2 days before admission. Her vital signs were as follows: HR, 110 beats/min; BP, 90/70 mmHg, SpO2, 86% initially but improved to 98% with non-rebreathing mask oxygen supplementation; and RR, 26 breaths/minute. Electrocardiography showed sinus tachycardia with right-axis deviation, RV strain pattern with T-wave inversion at V1–V3 leads, and persistent S-wave at V5 and V6 leads (Figure 4). Chest X-ray imaging revealed a normal cardiac silhouette with a Westermark sign. Electrocardiography also showed slight right atrial and RV dilation with preserved left ventricular (LV) and right systolic functions and normal valve functions. D-Dimer test showed >10 000 ng/mL (<500 ng/mL); SGOT and SGPT increased to 544 U/L (<31 U/L) and 477 U/L (<41 U/L), respectively; and thrombocyte count was 350 × 103 cells/µL (140–392 × 103 cells/µL). She was also positive for anti-dengue IgM.

Electrocardiography showing sinus tachycardia with right-axis deviation, right ventricular strain pattern with T-wave inversion at V1–V3 leads, and persistent S-wave at V5 and V6 leads.

The CTPA revealed bilateral PA thrombus, whereas the thrombus at the left PA was located at the main left PA trunk (Figure 5). Enoxaparin 1 mg/kg body weight was injected subcutaneously twice daily, and warfarin 2 mg o.d. was also administered. Other therapies, such as antibiotics and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug, were administered intravenously, as well as other supportive therapy for dengue infection. This patient did not have high-risk features such as cardiac arrest, intracerebral bleeding, and shock. However, the mortality rate is higher in children compared to adults.7 The patient was discharged on Day 8 with no bleeding complications.

Computed tomography pulmonary angiography in the axial view showing left main pulmonary artery thrombus (A) and thrombus at the right pulmonary artery (arrow) (B). Dilation of right atrium and right ventricle compared with the left heart (C). Left pulmonary artery thrombus (arrow) (D). CT, computed tomography; PA, pulmonary artery; RA, right atrium; RV, right ventricle.

Patient 4

A 51-year-old woman with dyspnoea and fever was admitted to the emergency department. Her symptoms occurred 2 days before the admission and were worsening rapidly. Her vital signs were as follows: HR, 126 beats/min; BP, 86/62 mmHg; SpO2, 89%; and RR, 28 breaths/min. Electrocardiography showed sinus tachycardia. Her PESI score was Class V. Laboratory results showed D-dimer > 10.000 ng/mL. Her SGOT and SGPT were 344 U/L (<31 U/L) and 100 U/L (<41 U/L), respectively. She was positive for IgG dengue and HIV positive.

A vasopressor drug was administered. Multiple CTPA thrombi were detected in both PAs (Figure 6), supporting high-risk in-hospital mortality. Enoxaparin 0.6 mg twice daily was administered immediately, accompanied by warfarin 2 mg o.d. Massive gastrointestinal bleeding occurred on Day 3. The haemoglobin level decreased from 8.6 to 6.4 g/dL. The thrombocyte count decreased from 76 × 103 to 49 × 103 cells/µL (140–392 × 103 cells/µL). The anticoagulant was stopped, and blood transfusions were administered. Unfortunately, the patient died on Day 6 of hospitalization.

Computed tomography pulmonary angiography revealed a thrombus in both pulmonary arteries. The left panel (axial view) shows a thrombus in the right pulmonary artery. The right panel shows a thrombus in the right pulmonary artery (arrow).

Patient 5

A 74-year-old woman presented to the cardiac outpatient with shortness of breath and lower extremity oedema. She was released from the hospital due to herpes zoster. Her vital signs were as follows: HR, 92 beats/min; BP, 154/84/70 mmHg; RR, 26 breaths/min, and SpO2, 98% room air.

Laboratory tests showed D-dimer > 10.000 ng/mL (<500 ng/mL), a thrombocyte count of 295 × 103 cells/µL (140–392 × 103 cells/µL), and IgM anti-dengue positivity. Electrocardiography showed sinus tachycardia. The CTPA revealed a long thrombus in the right and left PA (Figure 7). This patient was considered intermediate–low risk for in-hospital mortality, with PESI Class III and thrombus on CTPA. Enoxaparin 40 mg t.o.d. was administered, followed by warfarin 2 mg o.d. Supportive treatment, such as antiviral acyclovir, was given. The patient was discharged on Day 3 without bleeding complications.

Computed tomography pulmonary angiography showing a long thrombus in the right and left pulmonary arteries (arrow).

Patient 6

A 66-year-old woman presented with fever, cough, and lethargy for 3 days. On the day of admission, she experienced shortness of breath, and her condition deteriorated rapidly. The vital signs were as follows: BP, 140/80 mmHg; HR, 88 beats/min, RR 26 breaths/min; and SpO2, 89% initially but increased to 93% after oxygen supplementation. Physical examination revealed rales, and a systolic murmur was heard in the lower left sternal border and apex. Chest X-ray imaging revealed cardiomegaly and pulmonary oedema. The laboratory showed positivity for IgM dengue antibody; thrombocytopenia, 125 295 × 103 cells/µL (140–392 × 103 cells/µL); and increased troponin level, 0.03 ng/mL (0.01–0.02 ng/mL), and D-dimer, 705 ng/mL (<500 ng/mL), levels. Her PESI score was Class III. Thus, she underwent CTPA, which showed a thrombus in the right PA (Figure 8), indicating an intermediate-to-high in-hospital mortality risk.

Computed tomography pulmonary angiography showing a thrombus in the right pulmonary artery (arrow).

Enoxaparin 0.4 mg t.i.d. was administered, accompanied by warfarin 2 mg o.d. Given her history of heart failure, candesartan 16 mg twice a day, spironolactone 25 mg o.d., furosemide 5 mg/h, and bisoprolol, which was previously administered at 5 mg o.d. and decreased to 2.5 mg o.d. due to acute congestion, were added alongside her dengue treatment. The patient was discharged on Day 5 without bleeding complications. Electrocardiography 2 months after admission showed left atrial (left atrium volume index, 87.44 mL/m2) and LV (LV internal diameter at end diastole, 57 mm) dilatation, with an ejection fraction of 44% (biplane). Severe regurgitation of the mitral valve was noted, accompanied by mild TR and mild aortic regurgitation (Supplementary material online, Videos S1-S4).

Discussion

This case series reported the occurrence of acute PE in patients with recent dengue virus infection. Pulmonary embolism poses a high risk of morbidity and mortality if not properly treated. Pulmonary embolism is a serious spectrum of VTE in dengue infection and commonly occurs 7 days after the onset of fever when the acute phase of the illness has passed.1

Several studies have described an association between dengue and VTE. It is hypothesized that systemic inflammation in acute dengue infection will change the haemostatic equilibrium, leading to thrombosis via the increased synthesis of procoagulant factors.3,8 Other hypotheses, such as endothelial damage and capillary leakage theory, lead to hypercoagulability and thrombotic complications in dengue.5 Although patients with dengue are more susceptible to haemorrhagic diathesis, the potential thromboembolic events in dengue infection, though rare, must be considered, as they are based on dysregulated immune networks through increased levels of inflammatory cytokines, von Willebrand factor, plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1), loss of endothelium, and anti-thrombogenic protective factors.3–6,9

The interaction between the immune and coagulation systems that results in thrombus formation is the key mechanism underlying thrombosis in inflammation and infection, which results in thromboinflammation. Physiologically, endothelial cells naturally resist inflammation and thrombosis, and the change in this phenotype could be triggered by endotoxin or pathogen recognition. In the presented cases, dengue infection is hypothesized to stimulate this phenotype shift, culminating in thrombotic phenomena.6

Venous thromboembolism in patients with dengue can occur in all age groups, from children to the older population.1 Roy et al.10 reported a case of DVT in an 11-year-old boy with dengue, which was successfully managed with enoxaparin and warfarin. Although DVT has been reported in patients with dengue shock syndrome (DSS) and thrombocytopenia, this case raises a clinical dilemma concerning anticoagulant management.2,8 Concerning pulmonary thromboembolism complication, a previous study described a CTPA-confirmed case of PE 3 weeks after the initial diagnosis of dengue infection, which was successfully treated with fondaparinux.5 da Costa et al.4 reported five patients with dengue fever who experienced thrombotic events, whereas two of them experienced CTPA-confirmed pulmonary thromboembolism.

Although PE is uncommonly described as a dengue complication, this may be an underlying mechanism in which well-known dengue complications are commonly described, such as circulatory collapse, which overlaps with massive PE complications. The clinician commonly recognizes dengue complications in the spectrum of DSS and haemorrhagic phenomena.3,5

Objective findings such as ECG, D-dimer assay, troponin, and imaging studies (echocardiography and ultimately CTPA) are expected to reveal specific diagnosis possibilities.9 Hence, clinician vigilance and increased awareness and suspicion index of thrombotic complications of dengue are essential, particularly in endemic areas and hospital settings.1,4,9

Given that dengue infection is strongly associated with haemorrhagic complications,3 anticoagulant therapy administration in dengue is quite questionable and poses clinical dilemmas.1,5 In the report by da Costa et al.4 of PE in dengue fever and thrombocytopenia, LMWH was administered with good results. Currently, no guidelines have described a clear management pathway in this patient population.5 Thus, the decisions in our cases were based on previous case reports and experiences from other centres.9 The limitations in our case series are related to the absence of specific DEN serologic identification and the lack of CTPA in certain cases.

Conclusion

This case series included patients with dengue with or without thrombocytopenia, which might be complicated by acute PE. Anticoagulant therapy was still given according to the guidelines for PE management,11 with close monitoring for haemorrhagic complications.9
