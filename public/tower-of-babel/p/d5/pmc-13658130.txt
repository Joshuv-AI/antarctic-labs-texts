First exposure to SARS-CoV-2 shapes IgG subclass switching after mRNA vaccination
Authors: Carla Martin Pérez, Sílvia Ruiz-Rius, Anna Ramírez-Morros, Marta Vidal, Alfons Jiménez, Luis Izquierdo, Pere Santamaria, Josep Vidal-Alaball, James G. Beeson, Luis M. Molinos-Albert, Ruth Aguilar, Anna Ruiz-Comellas, Gemma Moncunill, Carlota Dobaño
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundmRNA vaccines against SARS-CoV-2 have proven highly effective in reducing severe disease and death, but repeated administration induces an unusual shift in IgG subclasses. In particular, increases in IgG2 and IgG4 raise questions about their regulation, persistence, and implications for protective immunity.MethodsTo investigate the longitudinal dynamics and factors influencing IgG subclasses and their impact on protective immunity, we analyzed the levels of IgG1–4 subclasses, C1q, and FcγR-binding IgG against antigens from multiple SARS-CoV-2 variants in a well-characterized cohort of healthcare workers in Spain.ResultsOur findings indicate that subclass responses are shaped by exposure history, with infection before vaccination limiting the extent of IgG2 and IgG4 class switching relative to infection after vaccination. IgG4 exhibits broader reactivity to Omicron variants than IgG1, and both IgG2 and IgG4 show slower decay of circulating antibody levels than IgG1. IgG4 levels correlate with FcγR- and C1q-binding antibodies only in participants with lower IgG1, suggesting competition between subclasses. During Omicron predominance, elevated IgG2 and IgG4 are linked to increased risk of breakthrough infection in vaccinees, whereas levels of IgG1 and FcγR- and C1q-binding antibodies are associated with protection.ConclusionsOur findings highlight marked differences in IgG subclass profiles according to first exposure history and suggest that IgG subclass distribution and persistence may influence antibody-mediated immunity following mRNA vaccination. Further studies are warranted to clarify the mechanistic and clinical implications of IgG subclass shift for long-term protection.

Introduction

The rapid deployment of COVID-19 vaccines substantially reduced the burden of severe disease and mortality worldwide. Among them, mRNA vaccines, including BNT162b2 and mRNA-1273, proved to be highly effective1, eliciting strong neutralizing antibodies, durable B- and T-cell memory2, and remain a critical component of booster strategies worldwide.

Repeated administration of SARS-CoV-2 mRNA vaccines has been associated with an unusual shift in IgG subclasses, notably the increase in spike (S)-specific IgG2 and IgG4 levels months after primary vaccination, and after subsequent booster doses3–10. This pattern is uncommon in the context of vaccination, as IgG4 responses are typically rare following most vaccines and are instead more often observed in settings of chronic antigen exposure11, allergy12, or tolerance13. This skewing toward non-cytophilic subclasses has not been observed after adenoviral vector-based COVID-19 vaccines4,14, inactivated vaccines14,15, or protein-based formulations7, underscoring the unique influence of the mRNA vaccine platform on IgG class switching. The biological basis of this phenomenon remains incompletely understood, but may relate to the strong and prolonged antigen expression induced by mRNA vaccines16.

We previously showed that elevated IgG4 levels after booster vaccination were associated with a higher risk of SARS-CoV-2 breakthrough infection, and that an increased ratio of non-cytophilic to cytophilic antibodies correlated with reduced functionality5. These findings suggest that expanding IgG2 and IgG4 may compete with IgG1 and IgG3 cytophilic subclasses, thereby compromising antiviral effector functions. Indeed, IgG1 and IgG3 efficiently engage activating Fcγ receptors (FcγRI, FcγRIIa/b, FcγRIIIa/b) and complement, enabling antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC), phagocytosis (ADCP), and complement activation17. In contrast, IgG2 and IgG4 have more limited effector potential18–20: IgG2, while capable of engaging FcγRIIa and mediating limited complement activation21, primarily targets polysaccharides and has weak antiviral effector activity18, while IgG4 is considered the least inflammatory subclass, with poor complement activation, low FcγR binding17, and the unique property of Fab-arm exchange22.

The determinants and durability of this subclass reprogramming associated with mRNA vaccination are only beginning to be defined. Dose number, exposure intervals23, and prior infection history4,6,23 have each been implicated, but the impact of type of exposure sequence (infection-first vs. vaccination-first) on subclass balance remains unclear. While total anti-S IgG half-life is ~150–200 days after infection or vaccination24–26, the persistence of individual subclasses is poorly characterized. Given their distinct regulation and function, differential half-lives could shape the long-term antibody landscape after repeated vaccination.

Here, we investigate the longitudinal evolution of IgG subclasses, FcγR- and C1q-binding antibodies, and their determinants, in a cohort of healthcare workers with different exposure histories to SARS-CoV-2 infection and mRNA vaccination. We examine how the sequence of first exposure (infection vs. vaccination) shapes subclass responses in the long term, assess the reactivity of IgG subclasses against Omicron subvariants, and model the antibody response half-lives of IgG1–4 after repeated vaccination. Finally, we analyze for how long these immunological features influenced the risk of SARS-CoV-2 breakthrough infection during periods of Omicron predominance.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This analysis was conducted within the framework of the CovidCatCentral cohort27,28, which followed primary healthcare workers (HCWs) from three counties in the province of Barcelona, Spain (Fig. 1). The cohort comprised two recruitment rounds. A first group was enrolled during March–April 2020, at the peak of the initial COVID-19 wave, and included 247 HCWs with laboratory-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection (positive rRT-PCR [real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction] and/or rapid diagnostic test [RDT]) and compatible symptoms. All eligible HCWs meeting these criteria were invited to participate. A second group consisted of SARS-CoV-2–naïve HCWs recruited in March–April 2021, shortly after completing their primary vaccination series (n = 200), matched to the infection-primed cohort for age, sex, professional category, and smoking status. Follow-up included nine cross-sectional visits, with venous blood sampling until May 2024. At baseline and at follow-up visits, demographic and clinical information was collected via telephone interviews and online questionnaires administered by trained physicians and nurses. Data included documented SARS-CoV-2 infections, COVID-19 symptoms, vaccination details (type, dates, and adverse effects), comorbidities, and smoking status.

For the present study, we selected a longitudinal subset of 93 HCWs from both recruitment rounds, prioritizing those who had received three vaccine doses by timepoint (T)9, along with an additional ten participants who had not received any SARS-CoV-2 vaccine doses up to T13. Between T9 and T13, among participants who had received three vaccine doses by T9, 29 received a fourth dose and eight received a fifth dose. The fourth dose corresponded predominantly to the Wuhan/BA.4/5 bivalent vaccine, although four participants received a monovalent XBB.1.5 vaccine as their fourth dose during the subsequent rollout (by T13); the fifth dose corresponded to the monovalent XBB.1.5 vaccine. To enhance statistical power for evaluating correlates of protection over the subsequent 1-year and 1.5-year periods, when Omicron BA.5, BQ.1, and XBB were the predominant variants, an additional 66 HCWs from the naïve and infected cohorts with three vaccine doses by T9 were included for analysis at T9 only. All breakthrough infections detected during follow-up were mild, preventing analysis of associations with disease severity. Baseline characteristics of participants included in the correlates of protection analyses are summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

Plasma samples analyzed corresponded to blood draws collected at the following visits: T0 (July–September 2020, baseline sample), T1 (September 2020), T2 (October 2020), T3 (November 2020), T4 (January–February 2021), T5 (March–April 2021), T7 (July 2021), T9 (May 2022), T10 (November–December 2022), T11 (May–June 2023), and T13 (May 2024). Symptomatic infections were identified through passive case detection, whereby participants underwent rRT-PCR and/or RDT testing following symptoms or known exposure. Asymptomatic infections were identified retrospectively via serology, as described previously28.

Quantification of IgG subclasses

IgG1–4 responses to the SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid (N) and full-length S proteins, as well as to the receptor-binding domain (RBD) from the Wuhan strain and variants of concern (VoC: Delta, BA.1.1, BA.2, BA.4/5, and BQ.1.1), were quantified as median fluorescence intensity (MFI) using a quantitative suspension array technology (qSAT) assay. Plasma samples were diluted 1:500 for IgG1 measurements and 1:100 for IgG2, IgG3, and IgG4, and incubated in 384-well plates with MagPlex-C beads (10,000 beads/μl; 1,500 beads/well) covalently coupled to the corresponding SARS-CoV-2 antigens. Incubations were carried out for 1 h at room temperature (RT) with shaking at 900 rpm, protected from light. Each plate included a serially diluted positive control (pooled plasma from 30 SARS-CoV-2–exposed and vaccinated donors), pre-pandemic negative controls (n = 129), and Luminex buffer blanks. Automated liquid handling was performed using Viaflo and Assist Plus Integra systems.

Following sample and beads incubation, plates were washed three times with PBS containing 0.05% Tween 20 (PBS-T) using a BioTek 405 TS washer. Bound antibodies were detected with subclass-specific mouse anti-human monoclonal IgG antibodies conjugated to phycoerythrin (PE), diluted to 1:250 for IgG1 and 1:50 for IgG2–4 (2 μg/ml, Southern Biotech: IgG1 Cat. No.: 9054-09, Clone name: HP6001, Lot No.: G2222 − WL82, IgG2 Cat. No.: 9070-09, Clone name: HP6002, Lot No.: I2911 − WG52, IgG3 HP6050 Cat. No.: 9210-09, Clone name: HP6050, Lot No.: F5821 − TA72B, IgG4 Cat. No.: 9200-09, Clone name: HP6025, Lot No.: G1322 − YL02), and incubated for 30 min at RT with shaking. After a second washing step, beads were resuspended in Luminex buffer, and fluorescence was measured on a Flexmap 3D® (xMAP, Luminex). Wuhan RBD and S proteins were expressed in CHO-S cells transduced with lentiviral vectors and purified via Strep-tag affinity chromatography (Santamaria Lab, IDIBAPS). RBD antigens from Delta, BA.1.1, BA.2, BA.4/5, BQ.1.1, and XBB variants were produced in-house in eukaryotic HEK293F expression systems.

Quantification of FcγR-binding antibodies

FcγR binding by N-, S-, and RBD-specific antibodies was also measured using qSAT. Plasma samples were diluted 1:200 and incubated for 2 h at RT with multiplexed beads coated with the relevant SARS-CoV-2 antigens (1,500 beads/well). After washing with PBS-T, bead–antibody complexes were incubated for 1 h at RT with biotinylated recombinant human FcγRs premixed with streptavidin–PE (1:250 dilution) in Luminex buffer. The panel included FcγRI (0.25 μg/μl), FcγRIIa-H131 (0.88 μg/μl), FcγRIIb (1.12 μg/μl), FcγRIIIa-V158 (0.92 μg/μl), and FcγRIIIb (0.92 μg/μl), all produced at the Duke Institute facility (USA) and used at a final concentration of 0.5 μg/ml. Plates were washed and resuspended in 100 μl of Luminex buffer before fluorescence acquisition on a Flexmap 3D®. Positive controls, negative controls, and blanks were included in all runs.

Quantification of C1q-binding antibodies

C1q-binding activity to N-, S-, and RBD-specific antibodies was determined using qSAT. Plasma samples diluted 1:200 were incubated for 30 min at RT with antigen-coupled beads (1,500 beads/well) in 384-well plates. After washing, bead–antibody complexes were incubated with purified human C1q (EMD Millipore Corp.) at a final concentration of 0.0015 μg/μl for 30 min at RT. Following another wash, beads were incubated with rabbit anti-human C1q IgG (1:250 dilution, generated in J.G. Beeson’s laboratory29,30) for 15 min, washed, and then incubated with PE-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:250, 4 µg/ml, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No.: P-2771MP, Lot No.: 2856103) for 15 min. Beads were washed again, resuspended in Luminex buffer, and read on a Flexmap 3D®. Each plate included serially diluted positive controls, pre-pandemic negative controls (n = 129), and blanks.

Statistics and reproducibility

IgG subclass and C1q-binding antibody levels were log10-transformed. Differences in antibody levels and proportions between the different exposure groups were measured by Wilcoxon Rank-Sum test adjusted by Benjamini-Hochberg to account for multiple testing. IgG subclass proportions were calculated for each sample by dividing the MFI of each subclass by the sum of MFIs across all IgG subclasses, providing a relative measure of subclass distribution. In linear regression models assessing the association between IgG subclasses and exposure groups, time since last exposure was modeled using natural splines (df = 3) to account for potential non-linear effects, while all other variables were included as linear terms.

To examine the relationship between FcγR-binding antibodies and IgG4 levels to RBD according to IgG1 levels, we fitted linear regression models of FcγR-binding antibody levels against log₁₀-transformed IgG4 levels at T9, T11, and T13, stratifying participants by IgG1 level (low/medium vs. high tertiles). Interaction terms between IgG4 and IgG1 as continuous variables were included, and significance was defined as p for interaction <0.05. Antibody response half-lives were estimated by fitting exponential decay curves using mixed-effects models with a random intercept for participant. For each analyte, the mean difference in half-life between IgG4 and IgG1 was calculated via nonparametric bootstrapping with 1000 iterations.

To investigate the effect of antibody levels on the risk of SARS-CoV-2 breakthrough infection among individuals with a confirmed infection date, we conducted a survival analysis using multivariable Cox regression modeling. Vaccinated individuals were considered at risk from T9 until the first reported episode of SARS-CoV-2 breakthrough infection, receipt of an additional vaccine dose (at which point they were censored), or the end of the follow-up period (T11 for 1-year follow-up period or T12 for 1.5-year follow-up period). Participants with asymptomatic infections (determined by serology) during the follow-up period were excluded from the Cox regression analysis, as the timing of those infections could not be ascertained. Models were adjusted by age, sex, comorbidities, smoking, number of infections, and time since last exposure. P-values lower than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. All tests were performed two-sided. Sample sizes for each analysis are indicated in the respective Figure legends. Statistical analysis was done in R version 4.2.2.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, was approved by the IRB Comitè Ètic d’Investigació Clínica (IRB Clinical Research Ethics Committee) IDIAP Jordi Gol (code 20/162-PCV), and written informed consent was obtained from all study participants before enrollment.

Levels of IgG1 to RBD Wuhan were the highest in hybrid immunity groups, with no significant difference between first-vaccinated and first-infected participants (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). For RBD XBB, differences at T9 (after first booster) were limited to higher levels in hybrid immunity versus vaccinated-only, and higher levels in first-infected versus infected-only at T9 and T13 (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b). IgG1 levels declined between 2 and 5 months after dose 2, particularly in participants without a prior infection, and then rose again from 5 months post–dose 2 to 5 months post–dose 3, with the largest rebound in this group (Supplementary Fig. 2b). IgG3 levels to RBD Wuhan were highest in vaccinated-only participants after primary vaccination, but declined after the first booster, and no significant subgroup differences were observed thereafter (Supplementary Fig. 2a, 2c). This pattern is unexpected, as IgG3 is typically associated with infection-driven inflammatory responses.

Analysis of clinical and demographic factors revealed that smoking was associated with higher IgG2 and IgG3 levels to RBD Wuhan at T9 (Fig. 2b), as well as higher IgG4 levels to RBD XBB at T9 (Supplementary Fig. 2b). No significant associations were observed for age, sex, or comorbidities in these responses.

Together, these results suggest that IgG2 and IgG4 induction after mRNA vaccination is attenuated in first-infected compared with first-vaccinated individuals, while hybrid immunity is associated with higher IgG1 levels.

First-vaccinated have lower IgG4 and IgG2 responses to N than first-infected and infected-only

In contrast to RBD, IgG4 responses to N were lowest in the first-vaccinated and vaccinated-only groups, and highest in the infected-only and first-infected groups (Supplementary Fig. 3), highlighting a stronger IgG4 induction to N when infection preceded vaccination. Consistent with natural infection driving strong IgG1 responses to N, IgG1 levels were markedly higher in participants with prior infection than in vaccinated-only individuals (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Male sex was associated with lower IgG1 levels to N at T13, smoking with lower IgG4 levels at T13, and comorbidities with higher IgG4 levels at T9 (Supplementary Fig. 3b). Age showed no significant associations.

Overall, the data suggest that natural infection is the main driver of IgG subclass responses to N, with first-infected individuals showing stronger IgG4 N-induction than first-vaccinated, likely reflecting more prolonged N exposure during primary infection.

IgG4 enhances FcγR binding when IgG1 SARS-CoV-2 levels are low

Given that the largest increase in IgG4 was observed after the 3rd dose, we examined its potential impact on the functional activity of plasma antibodies post-first booster dose. Thus, we investigated the association between IgG4 levels and FcγR- and C1q-binding antibody responses focusing on the last three study timepoints (T9, T11, and T13). FcγR- and C1q-binding measures serve as proxies of antibody effector function and do not directly assess functional activity. Because prior studies and our own data suggested an interaction between IgG4 and IgG1 levels, we stratified participants into two groups: low-to-medium IgG1 (first two tertiles) and high IgG1 (third tertile). This grouping was performed for visualization purposes only, after observing similar patterns across the lower tertiles. Importantly, the interaction between IgG4 and IgG1 was formally assessed using models with IgG1 as a continuous variable.

For both RBD- and S-specific antibodies, FcγRI, FcγRIIa, FcγRIIb, and C1q showed little to no association with IgG4 in participants with high IgG1 levels (Fig. 3, Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5). In contrast, in the low-to-medium IgG1 group, IgG4 was positively associated with FcγRI, FcγRIIa, FcγRIIb, and C1q at multiple timepoints (Fig. 3, Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5). For FcγRIIIa, we only detected a positive association in participants with low IgG1 levels at T11, consistent with the lower affinity of IgG4 for this receptor (Fig. 3, Supplementary Fig. 4). Omicron antibody recognition is higher for IgG4 than for IgG1

To understand whether different IgG subclasses vary in their ability to recognize emerging SARS-CoV-2 variants, we assessed IgG subclass -reactivity to the RBD of major VoCs. Breadth of antibody recognition is critical because viral evolution can reduce antibody binding, potentially diminishing protection. Subclass-specific differences in epitope binding, and affinity maturation may influence their sensitivity to antigenic drift. We compared IgG subclass binding to the RBD of Delta, BA.1.1, BA.2, BA.4/5, BQ.1.1, and XBB variants relative to binding to the ancestral Wuhan RBD after the first booster dose (Fig. 4, Supplementary Fig. 6). For all Omicron VoCs analyzed, IgG4, IgG2, and IgG3 reactivity was markedly higher than that of IgG1, often by more than 10-fold. Importantly, similar IgG subclass reactivity patterns were observed when analyses were stratified by first exposure type, with both first-infected hybrid and first-vaccinated hybrid groups showing higher IgG4 than IgG1 reactivity to Omicron RBDs (Supplementary Fig. 7). Although IgG2 and IgG3 showed higher reactivity to Omicron RBDs than IgG1, only a minority of participants had levels above the seropositivity cutoff, indicating that their apparent breadth reflects a small subset with detectable responses. These findings should be interpreted in the context of exposure history, as first-infected participants were primarily infected with pre-Omicron variants, whereas first-vaccinated individuals were more frequently infected during the Omicron period. IgG4 antibody response exhibits a longer half-life than IgG1

We next examined the longitudinal kinetics of antibody subclasses following the third vaccine dose (Fig. 5a). Across all antigens tested, IgG1 levels declined more rapidly than IgG4 levels. Exponential decay modeling estimated IgG1 half-lives at ~300–500 days, whereas IgG4 half-lives were substantially longer, typically ~900–1,200 days (Fig. 5b). To formally assess these differences, we calculated the mean difference in half-life between IgG4 and IgG1 for each subclass-antigen pair using nonparametric bootstrapping (1,000 iterations). Positive values, indicating longer IgG4 persistence, were observed for all antigens (Fig. 5c). Consistent with the slower predicted decay of IgG4 relative to IgG1, the proportion of IgG4 increased while IgG1 decreased between T9 and T11 in participants included in the decay analysis (Supplementary Fig. 9), supporting a shift in subclass composition over time. Regarding IgG2, half-lives were generally ~1,000 days, while IgG3 half-lives varied by antigen—~1,500 days for RBD, and ~800 days for full-length S (Supplementary Fig. 8). For IgG3 responses to S1 and S2, no consistent exponential decay could be reliably estimated, as trajectories varied markedly between individuals, leading to unstable half-life estimates (Supplementary Fig. 8). When IgG2–4 subclass levels were adjusted for IgG1 (Supplementary Fig. 10), the associations of IgG2 to S Wuhan and IgG4 to S, RBD Wuhan, and RBD BQ.1.1 with increased infection risk over the 1-year follow-up period remained evident, indicating the association is independent of IgG1 levels. In contrast, higher IgG1 levels to RBD Wuhan and higher C1q-binding antibody levels to S were associated with protection, as were FcγRIIIa- and FcγRIIIb-binding antibody levels to S, and FcγRIIIb-binding antibody levels to RBD (Fig. 6a). Over the extended 1.5-year follow-up period (T9–T12), the IgG4/IgG2-to-IgG1/IgG3 ratio to S, RBD Wuhan and RBD BQ.1.1 remained positively associated with infection risk (RBD HR[10-fold increase]=1.29, 95% CI = 1.03–1.63, p = 0.026), with IgG4 to S and RBD Wuhan also showing significant associations (RBD HR[10-fold increase]=1.49, 95% CI = 1.04–2.13, p = 0.026) (Fig. 6b). Together, these results indicate that elevated IgG4 and IgG2 levels to S antigens, and their higher ratio relative to IgG1 and IgG3, are associated with increased risk of SARS-CoV-2 infection, whereas IgG1, C1q-, and FcγR-binding antibody responses are protective.

Discussion

In this study, we show that exposure history profoundly shaped IgG subclass responses to SARS-CoV-2, with infection prior to vaccination restricting subsequent IgG2 and IgG4 class-switch recombination compared with infection after vaccination. We further found that IgG4 displayed broader apparent cross-reactivity to Omicron subvariants than IgG1, and that both IgG2 and IgG4 declined slower than IgG1 across post-vaccination timepoints. Importantly, these subclass patterns were associated with differences in infection risk during periods of Omicron predominance. Higher levels of IgG2 and IgG4 were linked to increased risk of breakthrough infection, whereas higher IgG1 and FcγR- or C1q-binding antibody levels were associated with lower risk. Together, these findings indicate that IgG subclass distribution and persistence may influence the quality of antibody-mediated immunity, highlighting the need to consider not only antibody magnitude but also subclass composition in the evaluation of vaccine-induced responses.

Individuals first infected and then vaccinated likely mounted an initial immune response dominated by IgG1-producing B cells. This is characteristic of the immune response to natural infection, which tends to elicit strong and transient responses with limited antigen persistence16,31. In contrast, individuals who were first vaccinated and later infected were initially exposed to a high and prolonged concentration of S via mRNA vaccination, which is known to promote broader class switching, including to IgG2 and IgG4, especially after repeated exposures3. Upon subsequent exposures, either by infection or additional vaccination, first-infected individuals would preferentially recall their IgG1 memory B cells. This rapid recall response might effectively control antigen levels early, thereby limiting the opportunity or need for further class switching (e.g., to IgG4). In contrast, first vaccinated individuals, would recall not only IgG1 but also pre-existing IgG4 memory B cells, contributing to a higher proportion of IgG4 responses over time. Similar trends have been reported in two prior studies, despite small sample sizes, showing lower IgG4 levels in previously infected individuals as compared to only vaccinated after three or five mRNA vaccine doses4,6. Our study extends these findings by providing longitudinal evidence in a larger cohort, allowing us to track within-individual IgG subclass dynamics across multiple timepoints, and to compare subgroups according to the sequence of infection and vaccination, which had not been addressed in prior cross-sectional studies. In contrast, another study reported an accelerated appearance of S-specific IgG4 after two doses in previously infected individuals as compared to vaccinated only individuals23; however, that analysis was conducted three weeks after the second dose with a limited sample size, whereas in our study, IgG4 responses assessed at two- and five-months post–dose 222.

IgG subclasses differ markedly in their ability to engage Fc receptors and activate downstream effector functions. Overall, our findings on IgG subclass functionality are consistent with prior reports showing that only when total IgG titers are low, IgG4 can contribute modestly to FcγRI- and FcγRIIa-mediated functions such as ADCP, given IgG4 moderate binding capacity to these receptors32. At high titers, however, competition with IgG1 may reduce IgG4-mediated engagement, although this was not directly tested in our study. In contrast, IgG4 does not meaningfully contribute to NK cell-mediated ADCC or complement activation due to its poor binding to FcγRIIIa and C1q23,32. The absence of a clear negative association of IgG4 with Fcγ receptors at high IgG1 levels in our study may indicate that FcγR were in excess relative to FcγR binding sites in the assay, such that the receptors became saturated and could bind both subclasses regardless of their differences in affinity, or that IgG4 binding, although weaker, still occurs in parallel. Notably, negative associations between S-specific IgG4 and FcγR- and C1q-binding antibodies in individuals with higher IgG1 levels were more pronounced than those observed for RBD-specific responses. This may reflect differences in antigen-specific immunodominance, whereby stronger RBD responses could lead to signal saturation at higher antibody levels, potentially masking negative associations. In addition, the positive association between IgG4 and C1q binding at low IgG1 levels may be driven indirectly by IgG2, which can bind C1q, and whose levels are correlated with IgG4 in our dataset. Such collinearity could produce an apparent association between IgG4 and C1q despite the negligible affinity of IgG4 for C1q-binding, and lack of complement-fixing capacity.

Antigenic drift in SARS-CoV-2, particularly in the Omicron lineage, has reduced the binding and neutralization capacity of antibodies induced by earlier strains33,34. However, we observed that IgG4 reactivity to Omicron RBDs was consistently broader than that of IgG1. One possible explanation is that a prolonged antigenic stimulation through repeated mRNA vaccination could favor the selection IgG4-switched high-affinity B-cell clones recognizing conserved epitopes between Wuhan and Omicron variants. In contrast, a large proportion of the remaining IgG1 pool may still target the highly mutable ACE2-binding region, which is heavily mutated in Omicron, thereby reducing its broader reactivity.

Our analysis of antibody kinetics following the third vaccine dose revealed markedly longer predicted half-lives for IgG4 compared with IgG1 across all antigens tested, with IgG4 typically persisting for 900–1,200 days versus ~300–500 days for IgG1. IgG2 half-lives were similarly long (~1,000 days), while IgG3 showed the greatest variability by antigen, ranging from ~800 days for full-length S to ~1,500 days for RBD. To our knowledge, subclass-specific half-lives against SARS-CoV-2 antigens have not been previously reported, and our estimates are substantially longer than total S-specific IgG half-lives reported in prior studies, which range from ~140 to 230 days after infection24,25,35,36 and ~90–150 days25,26 after mRNA vaccination. These discrepancies may partly reflect methodological differences: our mixed-effects exponential decay model estimates decay slopes over an extended follow-up, whereas many earlier studies derive half-lives from shorter post-exposure intervals that capture the initial rapid decline of antibodies from short-lived plasma cells. The slower decay phase we observed likely reflects the long-term maintenance of antibodies secreted by long-lived plasma cells in the bone marrow, which can persist independently of further antigen exposure37. The particularly long half-lives of IgG4 and IgG2 may be attributable to subclass-specific differences in FcRn-mediated recycling38, intrinsic catabolic rates, or the stability of plasma cell populations producing these antibodies. Further studies are warranted to confirm these subclass-specific differences which raise important questions about their role in long-term immunity and susceptibility to breakthrough infection.

Our protection analysis suggests that these long-lived subclasses may influence durable protection against SARS-CoV-2 infection. Over both the 1-year and 1.5-year follow-up periods, elevated IgG4 or IgG2 levels to specific antigens were risk factors, and a higher ratio of IgG4 and IgG2 relative to IgG1 and IgG3 was also associated with increased infection risk. In contrast, higher IgG1 to RBD Wuhan and increased FcγRIIIa-, FcγRIIIb-, and C1q-binding antibody levels to S were associated with protection. FcγRIIIa is an activating Fc receptor primarily expressed on natural killer cells, macrophages, and a subset of monocytes, where it mediates ADCC and phagocytosis. In contrast, FcγRIIIb is expressed on neutrophils, contributing to immune complex clearance, cell adhesion, and activation of effector functions in cooperation with FcγRIIa. These findings are consistent with our previous work in the same cohort5, where we also identified a positive association between high IgG4 levels and risk of breakthrough infection albeit in a shorter follow-up period, alongside negative correlations between IgG4 and neutralization, and Fc-effector functionality. IgG4 has limited capacity to engage FcγRIIIa/b or activate complement, which likely explains why higher IgG4 levels were not associated with protection in our cohort. Beyond Fc-mediated effector functions, emerging evidence suggests that IgG4 responses may also influence neutralization capacity. Recent studies have linked elevated IgG4 levels following repeated mRNA vaccination with reduced neutralization of emerging variants and increased susceptibility to breakthrough infection5,7,39. Potential mechanisms include delayed avidity maturation of IgG4 compared to IgG123, which may limit effective binding to evolving viral epitopes, as well as Fab-arm exchange22, a unique property of IgG4 that can reduce functional affinity by generating bispecific antibodies.Taken together, these data suggest that the unusually long half-life of IgG4, and to a lesser extent IgG2, may extend the persistence of antibody profiles associated with reduced Fc-dependent antiviral functionality and increased susceptibility to breakthrough infection.

This study has some limitations. First, our cohort was composed mainly of young adult women working in healthcare, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to the wider population and reduces the ability to evaluate sex-specific differences. Second, instead of directly quantifying complement deposition or Fc-mediated cellular effector functions, we relied on measurements of C1q- and FcγR-binding antibodies. Nonetheless, FcγR-binding assays are widely recognized as robust proxies for receptor engagement and correlate strongly with functional readouts such as antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity and phagocytosis40. Similarly, C1q binding has been shown to align with antibody-dependent complement deposition29,41. Although we observed that higher IgG4 levels following repeated mRNA vaccination were associated with an increased risk of breakthrough infection, this does not imply that mRNA vaccination itself increases infection risk. mRNA vaccines overall enhance multiple protective immune mechanisms, and remain highly effective in preventing severe disease1,2. Our findings instead suggest that the extent of IgG4 class switching may modulate the quality of humoral immunity within vaccinated individuals, such that the protective efficacy of mRNA vaccination may be reduced by a shift towards IgG4 responses. This warrants confirmation in other cohorts. The available sampling timepoints in our study were not optimized to capture the early decay phase, and our estimates therefore primarily reflect the longer-term decay component. In our study, individuals in the infected-only and first-infection groups were mainly infected in 2020–2021 with pre-Omicron variants, while those in the vaccinated-only and first-vaccinated groups were mostly infected post-vaccination, when Omicron predominated. These patterns reflect Spain’s vaccine rollout and epidemic waves and are therefore difficult to disentangle. Thus, it remains uncertain whether the same effects would have been observed if the first infection had involved a different variant, yet our findings represent the real-world sequence of exposures.

Together, our findings indicate that in addition to antibody magnitude, the quality of subclass responses may warrant consideration when evaluating mRNA vaccine formulations and booster strategies, particularly in individuals first vaccinated, in whom IgG2 and IgG4 class switching is accentuated. Beyond SARS-CoV-2, insights into subclass switching, persistence, and variant recognition may also inform future studies aimed at optimizing mRNA vaccine-induced immunity against other pathogens.
