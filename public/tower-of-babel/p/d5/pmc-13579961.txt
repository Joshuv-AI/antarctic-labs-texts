Immediate extubation post-surgery in cardiac patients: a retrospective single-center case series and literature review
Authors: Xiaodong Cai, Mintai Gao, Jiaying Chen, Rui Liu, Xuexian Tang, Dan Li, Ruiyu Li, Ganghua Yang, Huifen Zhou, Dandan Li, Xiangwen Zhao, Qingshi Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmediate extubation following cardiac surgery is rarely performed, largely due to concerns over hemodynamic instability, respiratory compromise, and potential postoperative complications. Most centers limit immediate extubation to low-risk patients, leaving its broader applicability less understood. This study utilized a standardized anesthetic procedure to implement immediate extubation in a diverse cardiac surgical population with varying risk profiles based on a single-center case series.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed the medical records of 34 cardiac surgery patients (congenital, valve, aortic, and coronary artery bypass grafting) from May 2024 to January 2025. A standardized, 10-step perioperative procedure was employed to facilitate immediate extubation. The effects of immediate extubation were assessed by extubation time, intensive care unit (ICU) length of stay, hospital length of stay, and postoperative complications.ResultsThis study included 34 patients (73.5% male, mean age 55.9 ± 16.1 years), predominantly New York Heart Association class II (55.9%) and American Society of Anesthesiologists class IV (52.9%). Most completed preoperative pulmonary training with nebulization, and 88.2% maintained negative fluid balance pre-surgery. Valve replacements comprised 67.6% of cases, followed by coronary artery bypass grafting (17.6%) and congenital cardiac defect repairs (11.8%). Immediate extubation was achieved in 97.1% of patients (33/34), with mean extubation time of 5 ± 4.7 min post-operation. Mean ICU stay was 4 ± 4.1 days and postoperative hospitalization 14 ± 5.9 days. Acute kidney injury represented the most common complication (33.3%), followed by delirium (18.2%) and 24-hour reintubation (9.1%). Most perioperative complications remained manageable within standard protocols.ConclusionImmediate extubation appears feasible in a carefully selected cohort of predominantly minimally invasive cardiac surgery patients managed under a standardised perioperative protocol, with an immediate extubation success rate of 97.1%. Larger, prospective, risk-stratified studies are needed before immediate extubation can be recommended as a broadly safe strategy across all cardiac surgical subgroups.Keywords: Immediate extubation, Cardiac surgery, Recovery, Fast-track anesthesia

Introduction

Patients undergoing cardiac surgery face a high risk for postoperative respiratory complications, acute kidney injury, and prolonged hospitalization [1]. In recent years, immediate extubation, removal of the tracheal tube in the operating room or within one hour after surgery, gained attention as a strategy to accelerate recovery and reduce the costs and risks associated with prolonged mechanical ventilation [2–4]. Compared to fast-track extubation (< 6 h postoperatively) or traditional delayed extubation (> 6 h), immediate extubation has been shown to decrease pulmonary complications, shorten intensive care unit (ICU) stays, lower healthcare costs, and reduce postoperative mortality [5, 6].

Although immediate extubation protocols have been adopted in many centers worldwide [7, 8], their application has been limited to only those cardiac patients exhibiting a lower risk postoperatively [9]. As a result, the broader application of immediate extubation and the ability to enhance the recovery of all patients’ post-surgery, irrespective of the risk severity, are restricted [10]. Notably, while promising data on immediate extubation exist worldwide, its execution within the Chinese healthcare settings is not only relatively novel but also comparatively advanced. This is mainly due to the differences in institutional culture, conventional perioperative workflows, and the availability of resources.

At our cardiac center, we have developed and successfully implemented a standardized, ten-step procedure to support immediate extubation (removal of the tracheal tube within 30 min after surgery) in the operating room for a wide range of cardiac surgical procedures, including congenital defect repair, aortic surgery, valve replacements, and coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG). To our knowledge, this presents one of the structured executions of immediate extubation across a range of cardiac procedures for patients with different risk profiles within a Chinese clinical setting. Unlike many cardiac centres that perform immediate extubation after surgery on patients with relatively mild conditions, the experience of our center focused on early extubation irrespective of the severity of the conditions and surgical conditions of the cardiac patients after the operation. In this report, we present our experience with immediate extubation in 34 cardiac surgery patients across diverse risk profiles.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective study reviewed medical records of 34 patients who underwent cardiac surgery at Xiaolan People’s Hospital of ZhongShan from May 2024 and January 2025. The study protocol, including the waiver of informed consent due to its non-interventional study using routinely collected data, was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Xiaolan People’s Hospital of ZhongShan (ZSXL-LL2025-089). Data collection adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Adult patients scheduled to undergo elective cardiac surgery were considered for inclusion. The exclusion criteria were: (1) aortic surgery; (2) New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class ≥ IV; (3) American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status ≥ V; (4) requirement for preoperative mechanical circulatory support or mechanical ventilation; (5) morbid obesity; (6) severe obstructive sleep apnea; (7) significant pulmonary disease; (8) long-term exposure of opioids, benzodiazepines, or other sedatives; (9) long-term corticosteroids exposure; (10) coagulopathy (international normalized ratio > 2.0); and (11) inability to communicate or comprehend study procedures; (12) patients with incomplete or lost data.

Anaesthesia and extubation procedure

We adopted a procedure that had 10 core steps designed to facilitate immediate extubation in patients undergoing cardiac surgery. The procedure is tailored to the characteristics of Chinese cardiac surgical practice, which includes a preference for high-dose opioid anesthesia due to its hemodynamic stability and mitigation of intraoperative stress responses. Below, we outline each step: (i) Preoperative respiratory preparation: Preoperatively, patients were engaged in incentive spirometer exercises, active preoperative active breathing cycle techniques, and active preoperative coughing training. Low-flow oxygen therapy was introduced as part of a prehabilitation protocol. These exercises not only improved baseline pulmonary function and encouraged postoperative cooperation, but also directly reduced the risk of postoperative pulmonary complications—a critical consideration in cardiac patients, many of whom have limited cardiopulmonary reserve [11]. (ii) Psychological preparation and expectation management: We place strong emphasis on psychological preparation. One-on-one preoperative counselling sessions were conducted to alleviate anxiety, reinforce comprehension of perioperative milestones, and increase compliance with commands during extubation [12]. In Chinese practice, where patients may have heightened concerns about awareness or unexpected events, cultivating patient willpower and setting realistic expectations is essential for postoperative success [13]. (iii) Induction of anesthesia with high-dose opioids: Upon admission to the surgical theater, routine monitoring of blood oxygen saturation and electrocardiogram was performed. Radial artery cannulation was used to monitor invasive arterial blood pressure. Before anesthesia, the Narcotrend anesthesia depth monitor and muscle relaxant monitor are connected to facilitate monitoring of anesthesia depth and muscle relaxation during anesthesia and surgery. Anesthesia induction involved ciprofol (0.5–1.0 mg/kg), cisatracurium (0.15 mg/kg) and high-dose fentanyl (20-30ug/kg). The patients were given sufficient opioids on time before the surgical incision started to avert surgical stress [14, 15]. In addition, administering the medication prior to incision was emphasized based on pharmacokinetic considerations. This approach aimed to ensure that the opioid reaches its peak effect during the critical phases of surgery, allowing its plasma concentration to sufficiently decline by the end of the procedure, thereby minimizing the risk of postoperative respiratory depression and delayed emergence due to insufficient drug metabolism time. (iv) Maintenance of anesthesia: Maintenance was achieved via continuous infusions of ciprofol (100–200 µg/kg/min), remifentanil (0.2–0.3 µg/kg/min), cisatracurium besylate (1.0–2.0 µg/kg/min), and intermittent sevoflurane. Bispectral Index values were kept between 40 and 60, ensuring adequate anesthesia depth while permitting rapid emergence [16]. (v) Early cessation of muscle relaxants: Muscle relaxants were stopped early (after prosthesis implantation in valve surgery) to facilitate timely recovery of neuromuscular function [17]. This enabled assessment of spontaneous movement and allowed quantitative train-of-four monitoring to ensure full recovery prior to extubation. (vi) Anesthesia recovery: approximately 30 min before the conclusion of the surgical procedure, adjustments were made to the maintenance doses of ciprofol and remifentanil based on the degree of surgical stimulation. Additionally, the infusion of cisatracurium besylate was ceased. When discontinuation of cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) support, ciprofol should be discontinued, and reliance should be shifted to maintaining a remifentanil infusion. Remifentanil’s unique pharmacokinetics allow for fine titration—the patient can emerge quickly without residual sedation, facilitating early assessment of neurological status [18–20]. (vii) Post-hemostatis adjustment: Remifentanil was gradually lowered to 0.1 µg/kg/min post-hemostasis, balancing analgesia with the need for fast emergence and hemodynamic stability. The aim was to maintain comfort while preparing for extubation. (viii) A low dose of naloxone (0.04 mg intravenous injection, max 0.1 mg) was administered at skin closure to reverse opioid-induced respiratory depression. In our practice, partial opioid reversal is critical—full antagonism leads to agitation, catecholamine surges, and secondary pain. By carefully titrating naloxone, we restore spontaneous breathing while preserving some analgesic effect, achieving a balance between airway safety and postoperative comfort [21]. (ix) Extubation criteria and procedure: Patients who promptly regained consciousness and fulfilled extubation criteria at the conclusion of the procedure underwent tracheal tube removal at the operating table. Hemodynamic criteria for tracheal tube removal: mean arterial pressure ≥ 65 mmHg with norepinephrine ≤ 0.05–0.1 µg/kg/min and no epinephrine administration; body temperature ≥ 35.5 °C; oxygen saturation ≥ 95%; arterial pH ≥ 7.30; urine output ≥ 0.5 mL/kg/h; train-of-four ratio ≥ 0.9 with quantitative neuromuscular monitoring and confirmed full reversal; adequate tidal volume and respiratory effort, and intact airway protective reflexes. Patients undergoing deep hypothermic circulatory arrest and CPB times more than 300 min were not considered for immediate extubation [14]. Following extubation, an oral airway was inserted to prevent tongue-induced airway obstruction owing to residual anesthetic effects. (x) Post-extubation care and analgesia: Patients received routine high-flow humidified O2 therapy. Postoperative pain management for all patients was achieved using a patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA) pump combined with local infiltration at the incision site. The PCIA regimen included oxycodone 20 mg, flurbiprofen axetil 250 mg, and tropisetron 10 mg, all diluted to 100 mL with 0.9% saline. The device was programmed with a background infusion rate of 2 mL/h, a bolus dose of 2 mL per demand, and a lockout interval of 15 min [22, 23].

Statistical analysis

The success rate of immediate extubation is defined as the proportion of patients who succeed removal the tracheal tube within 30 min after surgery. Reintubation rate refers to the proportion of re-establishing an endotracheal tube after initial immediate extubation. According to the KDIGO 2026 Clinical Practice Guideline, acute kidney injury (AKI) is defined as any of the following: an increase in serum creatinine by ≥ 0.3 mg/dL (≥ 26.5 µmol/L) within 48 h; or an increase in serum creatinine to ≥ 1.5 times baseline, which is known or presumed to have occurred within the prior 7 days; or a urine volume of < 0.5 mL/kg/h for 6 h; or positive kidney injury biomarkers indicating acute kidney damage [24].

Descriptive statistical techniques were used since this study adopted an exploratory approach. For the continuous variables, such as age of patients, and extubation time were reported as mean ± and standard deviation (SD). In addition, categorical variables including sex of patient and the type of surgical procedure was summarized using not only frequencies but also percentages. The analysis of data in this case series report was performed using SPSS 24.0.

Results

Patient demographics

A total of 34 patients were included in this study, with a predominance of males (73.5%) and a mean age of 55.9 ± 16.1 years. Nearly half of the patients (44.1%) were aged 61–80 years. The mean body mass index (BMI) was 23.8 ± 4.87 kg/m², indicating that most patients were of normal weight. The most common comorbidities were hypertension (44.1%) and diabetes (32.4%). Renal insufficiency was present in 38.2% of the cohort. Most patients were classified as NYHA Class II, reflecting moderate limitation of physical activity, and 52.9% were categorized as ASA Class IV (Table 1).

Preoperative management

The majority of individuals took part in organized preoperative pulmonary training to prepare for immediate extubation. Almost every patient performed incentive spirometer exercises, active breathing cycle techniques and coughing exercises, which were accomplished by 94.1% to 100% of patients. All patients received preoperative nebulization, and 76.5% received low-flow oxygen inhalation. Most patients (88.2%) maintained a negative fluid balance prior to surgery. Additionally, 29.4% received albumin infusions to optimize nutrition and physiological status. These interventions were aimed at improving respiratory function, reducing perioperative stress, and facilitating safe immediate extubation (Table 2).

Surgical and anesthetic characteristics

Valve replacement was the most common procedure (67.6%), followed by CABG (17.6%) and congenital cardiac defect repair (11.8%). Minimally invasive video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) was utilized in 76.5% of cases, whereas median sternotomy was performed in 23.5%. CPB was required in 82.4%, with mean bypass and aortic cross-clamp times of 172 ± 60.7 min and 100 ± 41.0 min, respectively.

Immediate extubation, removal of the tracheal tube within 30 min after surgery, was achieved in 97.1% patients (33/34), with the only failure occurring in a redo cardiac surgery patient. The mean duration of surgery and anesthesia were 291 ± 84.9 min and 412 ± 80.4 min, respectively. Anesthetic protocols included the use of fentanyl (1351 ± 406.9 µg), remifentanil (5448 ± 1768.7 µg), ciprofol (199 ± 106.3 mg), and cisatracurium (30 ± 8.2 mg), ensuring optimal sedation and muscle relaxation during the procedure (Table 3).

Management after immediate extubation

Immediate extubation was achieved promptly after surgery, with an average extubation time of 5 ± 7.4 min. After being quickly taken off mechanical ventilation most of the patients were oxygen saturation stable with a mean minimum oxygen saturation of 95.1% ± 2.0%. A total of 60.6% of the patients received an oropharyngeal airway to maintain airway patency in the early period after extubation. Additional respiratory support, including mask positive pressure, and non-invasive ventilation was required by none of patients. All patients received nebulization therapy after surgery as part of routine care. When indicated, high-flow nasal cannula therapy was given and this lasted an average of 23 ± 23.3 h. Monitoring fluid balance in the first days after surgery showed that most patients maintained a controlled negative fluid balance, which helps with the reduction of pulmonary congestion and stabilization of the respiratory function after surgery. The mean ICU stay was 4 ± 4.1 days, and the mean postoperative hospital stay was 14 ± 5.9 days (Table 4).

Postoperative complications

The most frequently observed complication was AKI (33.3%) and delirium (18.2%). A total of 11 patients developed AKI, of whom four had pre-existing kidney injury. Two patients had preoperative diagnosis of chronic kidney disease, and two cases of AKI were attributed to perioperative contrast-induced elevations in serum creatinine. One patient required renal replacement therapy (RRT) and died within 30 days. Three patients required reintubation within 24 h post-surgery. Of these, two cases were due to postoperative active thoracic hemorrhage, necessitating emergency re-exploration for hemostasis along with reintubation for anesthesia. The remaining case was due to the onset of low cardiac output syndrome, requiring reintubation for the initiation of extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) support therapy. Two patients (6.1%) died within 30-day. Most complications were managed effectively with standard postoperative care (Table 5).

Discussion

This single-center retrospective case series shares our institutional experience with immediate extubation in a diverse adult cardiac surgery population with varying risk profiles. Most immediate or early extubation apply stringent selection and are preferentially implemented in low‑risk cardiac surgery patients. Typical inclusion features reported in fast‑track series and Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) pathways include low preoperative risk scores (e.g., EuroSCORE II low‑risk), preserved cardiopulmonary function (NYHA class I–II, no or only mild COPD with acceptable spirometry), without severe comorbidities (e.g., hepatic and renal dysfunction, pulmonary diseases, diabetes), or good nutritional [4, 25, 26]. In contrast to the highly selected low-risk populations that predominate in the literature, our cohort was intentionally broader and included higher-risk features. In this cohort, nearly half of the patients (44.1%) were aged 61–80 years, with a high prevalence of comorbidities such as hypertension (44.1%), diabetes (32.4%), and renal insufficiency (38.2%). The percentage of ASA Class IV was 52.9% and NYHA Class III was 29.4%, illustrating moderate severity of illness.

A notable feature of our perioperative management strategy was the use of high-dose opioids. Although global practices are increasingly shifting toward opioid minimization in the context of ERAS [27], our study was conducted in an environment where high-dose opioids remain the standard in many Chinese cardiac centers [14], including our municipal hospital, which operates with limited technical capabilities and medical equipment. Our protocol leverages high-dose opioids to optimize intraoperative hemodynamic stability and reduce perioperative stress responses, particularly suited to the clinical realities in China, where many patients present with advanced cardiac disease and comorbidities and resources may be limited. The procedure is designed around pharmacological principles—allowing for peak effectiveness during periods of maximal surgical stress, followed by timely drug clearance to facilitate immediate extubation and recovery. Careful titration and monitoring are essential.

Despite this case‑mix, immediate extubation was achieved in 97.1% (33/34) of patients, with only one failure occurring in a patient undergoing redo cardiac surgery. Published success rates of immediate extubation vary widely (20%–94%), depending on surgery type, or inclusion criteria [28–31]. Notably, the high rate of successful immediate extubation in our study may be partially attributable to the predominance of minimally invasive procedures in this cohort, which are generally associated with reduced surgical trauma compared to conventional open surgery. However, this success rate of immediate extubation should be interpreted with caution given the retrospective, single-center design, small sample size, absence of a comparator, and the predominance of minimally invasive approaches, which may confound the observed success rate.

The mean extubation time of 5 ± 7.4 min postoperatively reflected efficient perioperative and anesthetic management, comparable to best practices reported internationally [32]. Our results further corroborate previous findings that immediate extubation can reduce ICU length of stay (mean 4 ± 4.1 days) and postoperative hospitalization (mean 14 ± 5.9 days) [33–35]. Among the 33 patients who were extubated in the operating room, the most frequently encountered complications were AKI (33.3%) and delirium (18.2%). The incidence of AKI in our study is consistent with previously reported rates, which range from 20 to 50.6% depending on the definition used and the complexity of the cardiac procedure [36, 37]. However, we observed a rate of postoperative RRT of 3.0%, which is at the upper end of the 2–5% range documented in the literature for cardiac surgery patients [38]. The requirement for RRT is known to increase with more complex surgeries and in patients with preoperative organ dysfunction. This relatively higher rate of RRT in our cohort may be partially attributed to our small sample size and the increased prevalence of pre-existing renal impairment—four of the eleven AKI cases had a history of kidney dysfunction prior to surgery. This underlying vulnerability may have contributed to the observed outcomes. Overall, our results underscore the importance of vigilant renal function assessment and management in cardiac surgical patients, particularly for those with pre-existing conditions. Postoperative delirium was observed in 18.2% of our patients, aligning with previously reported incidence rates after cardiac surgery, which vary widely (1–55%) depending on patient age, procedure duration, and perioperative factors such as cardiopulmonary bypass use, and length of mechanical ventilation [39, 40]. The reintubation rate was 9.1%, however, among them, no respiratory-related reintubations occurred. Previous studies reported reintubation rates of 2%–4%. In a retrospective CABG cohort with on-table extubation, reintubation was 2.6% versus 3.5% for early extubation, and larger institutional datasets report 1.5%–1.6% for isolated CABG and aortic valve replacement surgery [41, 42]. Our 24-hour all-cause reintubation rate after IE was 9.1%, driven exclusively by non-respiratory complications (bleeding re-exploration, ECMO), whereas respiratory-related reintubation was 0%. Our findings support the need for comprehensive perioperative management protocols to mitigate these common complications.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. The retrospective, single-center design inherently limits the generalizability of our results. Validation in larger, multicenter prospective trials is required to confirm applicability across diverse institutional settings. The relatively small cohort of 34 patients reduces statistical power to detect subtle associations between patient factors and outcomes, including differential recovery by surgical type or comorbidities. Additionally, absence of long-term follow-up precludes evaluation of sustained benefits or late complications. Despite broad inclusion criteria, selection bias remains possible, as patients deemed more stable intra- or preoperatively may have been preferentially selected for immediate extubation.

Conclusion

Immediate extubation appears feasible in a carefully selected cohort of predominantly minimally invasive cardiac surgery patients managed under a standardised perioperative protocol, with an immediate extubation success rate of 97%. However, given the small sample size, heterogeneous surgical population, and absence of preoperative risk stratification, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Larger, prospective, risk-stratified studies are needed before immediate extubation can be recommended as a broadly safe strategy across all cardiac surgical subgroups.
