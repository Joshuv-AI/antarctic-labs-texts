Old may still be gold: 45- to 55-year-old donors yield safe early outcomes in adult heart transplantation
Authors: Swaroop Bommareddi, Chen Chia Wang, Awab Ahmad, John Trahanas, Brian Lima, Kevin C McGann, Mark Petrovic, Stephen Devries, Joshua Lowman, Tarek Absi, Eric Quintana, Kelly Schlendorf, Matthew Bacchetta, Ashish S Shah, Aaron M Williams
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveAdvanced donor age is a common reason for donor heart offer turndown, especially in donation after circulatory death donors. With advancements in donor heart preservation and perioperative management strategies, the association between donor age and heart transplant outcomes in the current era is unclear.MethodsThis was a single-center study of all adult heart transplants from January 2020 to June 2025. The cohort was stratified by donors aged less than 45 years versus donors aged 45 years or more. A subgroup analysis compared donation after brain death donors aged 45 years or more and donation after circulatory death donors aged 45 years or more. Propensity score matching created balanced cohorts. Outcomes of interest included primary graft dysfunction, measures of early graft function and index hospitalization course, and up to 1-year survival.ResultsA total of 784 transplants were included in this study, of which 676 (86.2%) were in the standard donor group and 108 (13.8%) were in the old donor group. After 1:1 propensity matching, rates of primary graft dysfunction, cardiac indices and inotropic requirements, ventilation duration, and intensive care unit and hospital lengths of stay were comparable between the group. One-year mortality was also similar (10.2% donors aged <45 years vs 7.4% donors aged ≥45 years, P = .63). Within the donor cohort aged 45 years or more, 74 (68.5%) were donation after brain death and 34 (31.5%) were donation after circulatory death transplants. After propensity matching, outcomes were also comparable among older donation after brain death and donation after circulatory death donors.ConclusionsIn the modern era, adult heart transplantation using donors aged 45 years or more yields safe early outcomes. Outcomes between old donation after brain death and donation after circulatory death donors were also comparable, despite the historical hesitancy around old donation after circulatory death donors.Key Words: donor age, donation after circulatory death, extended criteria donors, heart transplant

Hearts from donors aged more than 45 years are safe to use in adult heart transplantation.

Central Message

Heart transplantation with donor allografts aged more than 45 years yield outcomes comparable to those of younger donors in both DCD or DBD.

Perspective

Adult heart transplantation using allografts from donors aged more than 45 years yields outcomes comparable to those of donors aged less than 45 years. Outcomes among old DCD donors were similar to those among old DBD donors. Thorough evaluation of older donors, such as obtaining heart catheterization and echocardiograms, may shift the focus away from refusing donor offers solely due to donor age.

Despite increasing adult heart transplantation volume over recent decades, the number of patients on the transplant waitlist continues to outpace organ availability.1 Unfortunately, advanced donor age remains a commonly cited reason for refusal of donor heart offers.2 This hesitation has been largely supported by various registry database studies highlighting decreased recipient survival after heart transplant when older donors have been used. This inflection point has been observed with donors between the ages of 40 and 50 years.3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 However, several single-center and multi-institutional studies have contrasting findings demonstrating that heart use in older donation after brain death (DBD) donors does not negatively impact recipient survival.9, 10, 11, 12 However, most of these studies were reported before the new revised organ allocation system in the United States in 201813 and in an era when donor heart preservation technologies and perioperative management strategies were not as advanced as they are today.

Another paradigm shift within heart transplantation not captured by previous studies has been the rising use of donation after circulatory death (DCD) heart donors. Historically, DCD donor hearts used tended to be younger than DBD donors because of possible concerns of older donor hearts not tolerating periods of warm ischemia during the DCD recovery process compared with younger DCD donor hearts.14 As such, some centers have even limited eligible DCD donors to be aged less than 50 years.14 Although DCD heart transplantation overall has yielded similar outcomes to DBD heart transplantation, the relationship between DCD heart donors and donor age remains less clear.15 This has likely been secondary to nonuse of these older DCD donors in the past, but given modern advancements in perioperative care, further study to evaluate the outcomes of these older DCD hearts remains crucial.

This study aims to provide the largest single-center experience using old donor hearts in the modern era of adult heart transplantation. We hypothesize that increased donor age would not be associated with poorer outcomes and no differences between older DBD and DCD donors would be observed.

Material and Methods

Study Population and Outcomes

All adult heart transplant recipients at a single institution from January 2020 to August 2025 were included. Cohorts were stratified based on donor age less than 45 years and donor age 45 years or more. Outcomes of interest include severe primary graft dysfunction (PGD), as defined by the International Society of Heart and Lung Transplantation consensus statement in 2014,16 30-day and 1-year survival, post–cardiopulmonary bypass left ventricular ejection fraction and right ventricular function, postoperative cardiac indices, postoperative vasoactive inotrope score (VIS), left ventricular ejection fraction at postoperative day 7, duration of mechanical ventilation, intensive care unit (ICU) length of stay, and hospital length of stay. VIS was calculated as dopamine (μg/kg/min) + dobutamine (μg/kg/min) + 100 ∗ epinephrine (μg/kg/min) + 100 ∗ norepinephrine (μg/kg/min) + 15 ∗ milrinone (μg/kg/min).

Various donor types, recovery strategies, preservation types, and types of static storage were included throughout the study period. Donor types included both DBD and DCD donors. All DBD donors were recovered in a similar fashion, whereas DCD recovery strategies included direct procurement and perfusion, thoracoabdominal normothermic regional perfusion, and rapid recovery with extended ultra-oxygenated preservation.17 Preservation types include static cold storage or machine perfusion. Machine perfusion includes normothermic machine perfusion (Transmedics Inc) and hypothermic machine perfusion (XVIVO Perfusion Inc), and modes of static storage included storage on ice, storage at 10 °C (Traferox Technologies Inc), storage at 4 to 8 °C (Paragonix Technologies), and storage at 10 °C using a commercially available cooler. A subgroup analysis was performed comparing outcomes between DBD and DCD transplants using donors aged 45 years or more.

Statistical Analyses

The patient population was stratified into a standard donor (donor age <45 years) and old donor (donor age ≥45 years) cohorts. Categorical variables are reported as number (percentage) and compared using Fisher exact test. Continuous variables are reported as median (quartile 1-3) and compared using Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Kaplan–Meier curves compared survival between the 2 groups, and differences in survival were compared by log-rank test. Patients in the old donor cohort were set as the matching cohort, and patients in the standard donor cohort were set as the control. A 1:1 propensity match using optimal matching method was performed to create balanced cohorts. Parameters included in propensity matching were procurement type, DCD recovery technique, preservation type, machine perfusion and static storage type, total ischemic time, predicted heart mass ratio, heart failure etiology, pretransplant left ventricular assist device support, pretransplant extracorporeal membrane oxygenation support, multiorgan transplant, and waitlist status. To supplement our analysis, univariate logistic regression was used to visualize the association between donor age as continuous variables and 1-year mortality. Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis was also used to evaluate the ability of donor age to predict 1-year mortality.

All analysis was conducted using R 4.3.3 (R Core Team). The Institutional Review Board or equivalent ethics committee of the Vanderbilt University Medical Center approved the study protocol and publication of data, under protocol #241909, approved December 30, 2025. Patient written consent for the publication of the study data was waived by the Institutional Review Board due to the retrospective chart review nature of the study.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

A total of 784 transplants were included in this study, of which 676 (86.2%) were in the standard donor group and 108 (13.8%) were in the old donor group (Figure 1). Among the old donor group, the median donor age was 48 years (IQR, 46-50; range 45-55). Donor sex distribution was similar between the groups, but donors were more likely to have hypertension in the old donor group (38.0% vs 15.2%, P < .001) (Table 1). Coronary artery vetting via cardiac catheterization (84.3% vs 29.7%, P < .001) was more likely to be performed in the old donor group. The proportion of DBD and DCD donors were similar between the groups, but DCD hearts in the old donor group were more frequently recovered using rapid recovery with extended ultra-oxygenated preservation (7.4% vs 3.7%, P = .025) and stored in a noncommercial 10 °C cooler when using static storage (52.8% vs 33.9%, P < .001). Total ischemic time was also longer in the old donor group (median 233 vs 222 minutes, P = .041).

Recipients were older in the old donor group (median 62 vs 55 years, P < .001), but recipient sex distribution, body mass index, rate of diabetes, and rate of hypertension were comparable. Rates of ischemic heart failure, congenital heart disease, prior sternotomy, and pretransplant mechanical circulatory support were also similar between the groups. Unmatched Cohort Outcomes

In the unmatched cohorts, rates of intraoperative severe right ventricle (RV) dysfunction and primary graft dysfunction were similar (Table E1). On ICU arrival, VIS was lower in the old donor group (median 14.2 vs 16.4, P = .007), but was similar at 24 hours and 72 hours post-transplant. ICU lengths of stay were longer in the old donor group (median 9 days vs 7, P = .033), but total hospital lengths of stay were similar between the groups. Mortalities at 30 days, 90 days, and 1 year were all comparable between the standard and old donor groups.

Univariate logistic regression found no association between donor age as a continuous variable and 1-year mortality (odds ratio, 1.01, 95% CI, 0.98-1.03, P = .55), and receiver operating characteristic curve analysis demonstrated poor discriminative ability of donor age on 1-year mortality (area under the curve, 0.53, 95% CI, 0.46-0.59) (Figure E1).

Propensity Matching and Outcomes

After propensity matching, there were 108 patients (50%) in the standard donor group and 108 patients (50%) in the old donor group (Table 2). The standardized mean differences for the propensity score and individual covariates showed improved balance after matching (Table E2). Between the matched cohorts, cardiac indices and VIS were similar immediately and up to 72 hours post-transplant. Rates of intraoperative severe RV dysfunction, PGD, ventilation duration, ICU and hospital lengths of stay, and mortality were also comparable between the groups (Figure 2).

Discussion

This study represents the largest and most comprehensive single-center series of adult heart transplant outcomes using hearts from old donors aged 45 years or more. Our main findings were (1) adult heart transplants performed using donors aged 45 years or more yielded short-term recipient outcomes similar to those that receive donor hearts that were less than 45 years of age; and (2) adult heart transplant recipient outcomes from old DCD donors were comparable to those from old DBD donors. Overall, these findings represent an important update to the current understanding of how donor age is associated with postoperative adult heart transplant outcomes and provides valuable insight to support inclusion of older donors, whether from DBD or DCD donors, in the adult heart transplant donor pool.

Our overall findings remain similar to those from other studies that also demonstrate no difference in mortality in adult heart transplant recipient outcomes when using younger versus older donors.9, 10, 11, 12 Data from prior single-center studies included adult heart transplant recipient outcomes approximately a decade ago, with the most recent patient cohort involving 2010-2019 by Chen and colleagues12 Our study reiterates these conclusions in the current era, fully encompassing changes to the field of adult heart transplantation, such as the 2018 allocation policy change and increasing use of DCD hearts, which has not been accounted for in any other single-center study. In recent years, Hong and colleagues18 examined all DCD heart transplants in the United States from 2019 to 2023 and found comparable 90-day survival between standard and advanced age (≥40 years) donors.

We recognize there is conflicting evidence from many large registry studies when compared with the findings of this study. However, a major flaw of these studies remains not taking into account transplant center volume and experience with DCD donors.3,4,6, 7, 8,19,20 There is a clear, established relationship between increasing heart transplant volume and improved outcomes.21,22 It is plausible that experienced centers are more adept at using extended criteria donors, such as old donors, compared with centers with lower volume. There are even some that posit that DCD heart transplantation should be considered to be regionalized because there are DCD heart transplant recipient outcomes that have been shown to be related to transplant center volume.23 Furthermore, there is generally more uniformity in donor heart recovery and preservation protocols within single-center studies compared with large registry studies where varying techniques have been known to confound outcomes. These factors could explain the discrepancy in findings between studies using large registry databases and single-center studies including our current study. Therefore, we believe that the focus should be shifted away from excluding donors based on age and toward identifying methods of evaluating and recovering older allografts that allow for their safe use with acceptable postoperative adult heart transplant recipient outcomes. Future studies should attempt to identify risk factors and specific donor-recipient characteristics predictive of poor outcomes among heart transplants with older donors.

We also highlight that the evaluation of older donors differs from that of younger donors. In this study, the majority of our old donors received an angiogram to assess the coronary arteries. In contrast, most of our young donors did not receive a coronary angiogram, and their coronary arteries were mainly assessed through computed tomography imaging. For the older donors we used, we tried to obtain as robust testing of the graft as possible, often including left and right heart catheterization and serial echocardiograms. In the event we could not obtain left heart catheterization, which has become more frequent in the case with DCD donors, we obtained a computed tomography scan with contrast to assess for the presence of coronary calcium to help properly stratify donor risk. We also take recipient age into account and prefer to match older donors to older recipients, but exceptions may occur due to recipient urgency status. Currently, we do not specifically consider recipient sex when matching an old donor to a waitlist candidate.

Our study also remains the first to compare outcomes between old DBD and DCD donors. This comparison has historically been impractical given the lack of using old DCD donors. However, our center does not have a DCD donor age limit. Although our propensity-matched subgroup analysis found no statistically significant differences in clinical outcomes, the 85.7% 1-year survival in the old DCD group compared with 94.1% in the old DBD group calls for caution and further investigation. Given the low sample size of the cohorts in this subgroup analysis, it is possible that larger studies may or may not yield statistically significant survival difference between old DCD and DBD donors. At present, these outcomes rest on the difference involving a single patient between groups. We also suspect that part of this difference is due to our institution being at the forefront of using new organ preservation technologies and devices in machine perfusion. The impact of the learning curve associated with new technologies and techniques will likely be normalized with larger powered series.

Limitations

This study has several limitations inherent to single-center retrospective studies. As a high-volume transplant center, our data may not be generalizable to all centers. However, we believe that our findings still importantly demonstrate that it is possible to safely use old donors, whether from DBD or DCD donors, in adult heart transplantation. This study also lacked long-term outcomes data, which may yield differences seen in past studies such as 3- and 5-year survivals.4,7 Last, we did not have follow-up data on rates of coronary artery vasculopathy, which has been shown to be higher in transplants using older donors.10,11

Conclusions

This study represents the largest, most comprehensive evaluation of post-heart transplant outcomes using old DBD and DCD donors. Our findings contrast previous large registry studies by demonstrating similar outcomes between donors aged 45 years or more and donors aged less than 45 years. We also found similar outcomes between old DBD and old DCD donors, but further validation with larger samples are needed. This study also provides practical takeaways when evaluating old donors, such as the use of left/right heart catheterization and serial echocardiograms to thoroughly assess heart function. Instead of excluding old donors from consideration, the heart transplant community should shift the focus toward identifying practices that can help facilitate the safe use of old donor allografts.
