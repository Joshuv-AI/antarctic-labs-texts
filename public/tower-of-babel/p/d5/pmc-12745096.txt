GLP-1 Receptor Agonists Initiation and Risk of Acute Pancreatitis and Pancreatic Cancer: A Real-World Comparative Study
Authors: Omar Faour, Moheb Boktor, Hanford Yau, Mustafa Kinaan, Ishak A Mansi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlucagon-like peptide 1 receptor agonist (GLP-1RA) medications are widely used in managing type 2 diabetes because of their cardio-renal-metabolic benefits. However, concerns persist regarding their potential association with acute pancreatitis (AP) and pancreatic cancer. This study’s objective was to examine the association of GLP-1RAs with the risk of AP and pancreatic cancer.MethodsThis retrospective propensity score-matched cohort study used Veterans Health Administration national data during fiscal years 2006 to 2021. Using a new-user active comparator design, we included veterans who initiated either GLP-1RA or dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitor (DPP-4i) medication, the latter as an active comparator. The primary outcomes were incident AP and pancreatic cancer. We excluded patients with a history of pancreatitis, pancreatic tumors, pancreatic congenital anomalies, and alcohol use. Secondary analysis included adjusting for confounders that may have been introduced during the follow-up period, such as gallbladder diseases, and post hoc analysis restricted analysis to people who had normal serum lipase during follow-up.ResultsWe matched 88,972 pairs of GLP-1RA and DPP-4i users on all characteristics. AP was diagnosed in 214 (0.24%) DPP-4i users versus 273 (0.31%) GLP-1RA users (OR 1.28; 95% CI, 1.07-1.53), and pancreatic cancer was diagnosed in 154 (0.17%) DPP-4i users versus 211 (0.24%) GLP-1RA users (OR 1.37; 95% CI, 1.11-1.69). Whereas the increased risk seems modest compared to their benefits as shown in the literature, vigilance is recommended, specifically, when GLP-1RAs are used for off label indications.Keywords: Acute pancreatitis, pancreatic cancer, GLP-1 receptor agonists

Introduction

Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) are widely used in the management of type 2 diabetes (T2D) due to their efficacy in improving glycemic control, weight loss, and cardiorenal benefits.1 However, some studies have raised concerns about the association between GLP-1RA use and pancreatic diseases. Real-world studies using the Food and Drug Administration Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) database, as well as post-marketing observational data, reported a higher risk of acute pancreatitis (AP) and pancreatic cancer in association with GLP-1RA use.2, 3, 4, 5, 6 Additionally, GLP-1RAs were noted to cause elevations in serum lipase and amylase levels, raising more concerns for such association.7,8

Yet not all studies found an association between GLP-1RA use and pancreatic diseases. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) did not show an increased incidence of AP in GLP-1RA users compared to placebo.9, 10, 11, 12 Meta-analyses of RCTs found no increased risk of pancreatic cancer.13, 14, 15, 16, 17 However, RCTs are commonly of short duration and smaller size, which may not detect slow-to-develop or uncommon adverse events. Yet, observational studies did not find significant differences in pancreatitis and pancreatic cancer rates between GLP-1RAs and comparators.18,19 Furthermore, patients with diabetes often have other risk factors for AP, including obesity, gallbladder diseases, and hypertriglyceridemia, that may increase pancreatitis risk.

Some of the proposed mechanisms linking GLP-1RAs to AP and pancreatic cancer include changes to the pancreatic tissue such as ductal hyperplasia, intraepithelial neoplasia, increased cell proliferation, and increased pancreatic mass, and that elevation of pancreatic enzyme levels (amylase, lipase) is related to pancreatic inflammation and hypertrophy.20,21 GLP-1RAs promote B-cell survival and proliferation, which is helpful in treating diabetes but could lead to reduced apoptosis in pancreatic ductal cells, which could potentially allow cancer cells to proliferate.20 On a molecular level, CAMP/protein kinase signaling, calcium channel dysregulation, and oxidative stress have also been implicated.22

Given the widespread use of GLP-1RAs, this study aimed to examine the association of GLP-1RA medications with risk of AP and pancreatic cancer in a large population who were followed longitudinally within the same health care system with similar access and standards of care. To limit confounding, we used dipeptidyl peptidase 4 inhibitors (DPP-4i) as an active comparator. We considered DPP-4i medications as an appropriate active comparator given that several meta-analyses of RCTs and observational studies have shown that DPP-4i are not known to be associated with our outcomes.23, 24, 25 Additionally, both GLP-1RA and DPP-4i were considered second-line therapy at time of the study, and both were introduced to medical practice at similar time, making them suitable comparators.

Methods

This is a retrospective cohort study using national data from the Veterans Health Administration (VHA) Corporate Data Warehouse (CDW), hosted in the VA Informatics and Computing Infrastructure (VINCI) ennvirnment. CDW is a national repository comprising several clinical and administrative systems according to published and validated protocols.26 CDW data are stored in a relational database to include administrative codes from both inpatient and outpatient encounters, pharmacy use records, and clinical records such as blood pressure, weight and height, laboratory investigations, and demographic records. The study was approved by the Orlando Veterans Affairs (VA) Health Care System Institutional Review Boards, which granted waivers for informed consent and HIPPA authorization because data were fully de-identified by VINCI staff before providing access to the investigators.

Study Population

We extracted data of patients who filled either GLP-1RA or DPP-4i prescriptions from fiscal years (FY) 2006 to the end of FY 2021 (October 1, 2005 to September 30, 2021). The study included all adults who had at least 1 inpatient or outpatient medical encounter, with at least 1 set of vital signs and 1 set of laboratory investigations before initiation of GLP-1RA or DPP-4i medications. The rationale for this requirement is to ensure that we will be able to describe their baseline characteristics. Cohort entry was defined as the date of the first inpatient or outpatient medical encounter. At the time of the study, the VA formulary restricted the use of these medications to patients with type 2 diabetes.27

Study Groups

We used an active comparator, a new-user design, to minimize confounding, as previously described.28 An active comparator design requires that the drug of interest to be compared with another drug of similar use but to not associated with the outcome of interest, instead of “non-use” of the drug of interest as a comprison.29 Use of an active comparator mitigates confounders and minimizes bias in observational studies, because “non-user” patients may have not received the drug of interest because they have milder disease or contraindications for further treatment. Hence, use of an active comparator increases similarity of measured characteristics between comparison groups, reduces the potential for unmeasured confounders, and improves the clinical relevance of the research question.29 The study groups included the following: (1) GLP-1RA users—patients who initiated GLP1-RA medications; and (2) active comparator group—patients who initiated DPP-4i medications. There were no prevalent users, because these medications were introduced in the United States after the date of study start.30,31

Study Intervals

Index date was defined as the date of initiation (first fill) of either GLP-1RA or DPP-4i medications. The baseline period, used to describe baseline characteristics of the study groups, included the 3-year period preceding the index date. The follow-up period used to examine outcomes started from the index date and continued until study end (September 30, 2021), death, or initiation of GLP-1RAs in the DPP-4i group, whichever came first.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

We required for inclusion that patients had a follow-up period of any duration after the index date (i.e., the index date was not the last day of the study). We excluded concomitant users of both GLP-1RAs and DPP-4i. We also excluded patients who had any of the following diseases during the baseline period: acute pancreatitis, pancreatic cancer, chronic pancreatitis, benign pancreatic tumors, or pancreatic congenital anomalies. We also excluded patients who were diagnosed, at any time during the study period, with cystic fibrosis or alcohol use disorder (Table S1 in supplementary online material).

Cohort Characterization and Propensity Score Matching

Patients’ baseline characteristics were extracted using validated definitions.35, 36, 37 We selected a priori 86 baseline characteristics to ensure comparability of the 2 comparison groups. In addition to demographic and social history, these characteristics may be grouped as diabetes-related conditions (disease categories reflecting diabetes severity, laboratory investigation, use of other anti-diabetes medications), pancreas-related conditions (pancreatic disease groups and lipase level), liver-related conditions (liver disease categories and laboratory investigation), health care utilization measures (to mitigate ascertainment bias), overall comorbidities (disease categories, laboratory investigations, medication use that may reflect comorbidities), and use of medications known to be associated with the highest risk of acute pancreatitis. We also calculated the VHA frailty index36 and the weighted Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), 38,39 given that comorbidity and frailty may influence the decision to pursue a definite diagnosis.

We created a propensity score (PS) using these characteristics, to match the comparison groups using a multivariable logistic regression to estimate the propensity score and perform nearest number matching with a caliper of 0.01 with no replacement.40,41 This caliper was found to reduce standardized differences (St Diff) after matching to <.1 and maximize study size. Further details of variable handling and propensity matching S-methods can be found in supplementary online methods.

Primary and Secondary Analysis

Primary analysis examined the incidence and risk of outcomes in the PS-matched cohort. We used 2 different statistical methods to examine the risk of outcomes:

1.Odds ratio (OR) of outcomes: we used logistic regression analysis to compare the odds of each outcome in the PS-matched cohort. We used a separate model for each outcome, in which the outcome was the dependent variable and the use of medications as the explanatory variable.

Odds ratio (OR) of outcomes: we used logistic regression analysis to compare the odds of each outcome in the PS-matched cohort. We used a separate model for each outcome, in which the outcome was the dependent variable and the use of medications as the explanatory variable.

2.Hazard ratio (HR) of outcomes: we used the Cox proportional hazard regression model to estimate the HR and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) of the primary outcome. Index date was the date of entry into the model, and date of first occurrence of the outcome was the event of interest. Entries were censored at the last date of the study period, death, or the date of GLP-1RAs initiation among DDP4i users who discontinued DDP4i and started GLP1-RAs.

Hazard ratio (HR) of outcomes: we used the Cox proportional hazard regression model to estimate the HR and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) of the primary outcome. Index date was the date of entry into the model, and date of first occurrence of the outcome was the event of interest. Entries were censored at the last date of the study period, death, or the date of GLP-1RAs initiation among DDP4i users who discontinued DDP4i and started GLP1-RAs.

Secondary analysis examined the primary outcomes in a series of analyses including whole cohort (all patients who fulfilled inclusion or exclusion criteria before PS-matching); PS-matched cohort adjusting for any confounders that may have been introduced during the follow-up period, such as gall bladder diseases or use of medications with the highest risk of acute pancreatitis during follow-up; and PS-matched cohort excluding patients who were diagnosed during the follow-up period with any gallbladder disease. In a secondary analysis, we included the propensity score as an explanatory variable in the regression model to adjust for confounders.

Lastly, we performed 2 post hoc analyses to examine if the increased risk of pancreatic cancer in association with GLP-1RA use was the result of ascertainment bias from imaging secondary to acute pancreatitis during follow-up or was prompted by elevated serum lipase associated with GLP-1RA use.7,8 In the first post hoc analysis, we examined the incidence and OR of pancreatic cancer excluding patients who were diagnosed with acute pancreatitis during follow-up. In the second post hoc analysis, we restricted the analysis to patients who had normal serum lipase during the follow-up period. In these analyses, we included the propensity score as an explanatory variable in the regression model to adjust for confounders.

Other Statistical Analyses

Statistical significance was defined as two-tailed P < .05. Statistical analyses were performed using STATA version 18. Missing individual laboratory data were transformed to categorical variables, based on commonly used definitions in clinical practice, and we ensured that the proportion of patients with missing values was similar in the 2 comparison groups (S-Methods in Supplementary online material). Overall, missing values constituted <0.01% of the available data. The study followed the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Results

The study population consisted of 137,918 people in the GLP-1RA group and 241,561 people in the DPP-4i group, before PS matching (Figure 1). The mean (SD) age of GLP-1RA users was 65 (10) years and for DPP-4i users 67 (11) years. The population was predominantly male, 92.2% (127,089) among GLP-1RA users versus 94.5% (228,196) among DPP-4i. Female patients were 7.9% (10,829) in the GLP-1RAs group and 5.5% (13,364) in the DPP-4i group.

Baseline Patient Characteristics After Propensity Score Matching

The PS-matched cohort included 88,972 patients in each group. There were no residual differences between the 2 groups after PS-matching (Table 1). The mean (SD) age was 66 (10) years in both groups. Both groups had similar proportions of men (92.6%) and women (7.4%). Most patients were White (74.5% of GLP-1RAs users versus 71.5% of DPP-4i users), with smaller proportions identifying as African American (16.0% versus 18.2%, respectively). The mean duration of follow-up was not different between GLP-1RA and DPP-4i users (923 days versus 917 days, respectively).

Secondary Analysis

Whole Cohort Analysis (Unmatched) showed AP incidence was 401 (0.29%) in GLP-1RAs users versus 556 (0.23%) in DPP-4i users (OR 1.31; 95% CI, 1.12-1.53; P = .001), and for pancreatic cancer, the incidence was 292 (0.21%) in GLP-1RA users versus 649 (0.27%) in DPP-4i users (OR 1.28; 95% CI, 1.09-1.51; P = .003). Adjusting for the presence of gallbladder disease or use of medications associated with higher risk of AP during follow-up showed results consistent with our primary analysis.

When subjects with gallbladder disease during follow-up were excluded, the incidence of AP and pancreatic cancer was higher in GLP-1RAs users compared to DPP-4i users (Table 2). Post hoc analysis showed results consistent with our primary and secondary analyses (Table 2).

Discussion

Our findings suggest a modest but statistically significant increased risk of AP and pancreatic cancer with GLP-1RAs when compared to DPP-4i. These associations persisted across multiple sensitivity analyses, including adjustments for events that may occur during follow-up and the exclusion of patients with gallbladder disease. We also hypothesized that the increased risk of pancreatic cancer may be the result of ascertainment bias related to aggressive imaging for patients who had acute pancreatitis during follow-up or for those with an asymptomatic increase in serum lipase known to occur among GLP-1RA users.7,8 Yet, our post hoc analysis restricting cohort to those who did not have acute pancreatitis during follow-up or those who had normal serum lipase during follow-up still showed higher OR of pancreatic cancer. This study adds to the existing body of literature by providing real-world evidence on the potential risks associated with GLP-1RAs in a large cohort.

This association aligns with previous studies; a matched case-control study of patients hospitalized for acute pancreatitis noted that current use of GLP-1RAs was associated with significantly increased odds of acute pancreatitis (adjusted OR 2.24; 95% CI, 1.36-3.68).6 Another retrospective cohort study comparing GLP-1RA users (613 semaglutide users and 4,144 liraglutide users) for weight loss with 654 bupropion-naltrexone users as an active comparator showed that GLP-1RAs were associated with an increased risk of pancreatitis (HR 9.09; 95% CI, 1.25-66.00).42 Pharmacovigilance studies using FAERS identified 3,073 pancreatic cancer reports potentially linked to GLP-1RAs, where the proportional reporting ratio was significantly higher for all GLP-1RA agents.22 It should be noted that whereas there is a dose-dependent relationship between GLP-1RAs and gastrointestinal side effects such as nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea, with higher doses and more potent agents associated with increased frequency and severity of these adverse events,43, 44, 45 there is no established dose-dependent relationship between GLP-1RAs and pancreatic diseases, to our knowledge.

Nevertheless, other studies did not reach the same conclusion; notably, a population-based cohort that compared GLP-1RA users to insulin users found no increased risk of pancreatic cancer (HR 0.50; 95% CI, 0.15-1.71).46 However, this study had a limited description of baseline characteristics, comorbidities, and common risk factors for pancreatic cancer. Another retrospective cohort study evaluated the risk of 13 obesity-associated cancers in GLP-1RA users compared with insulin or metformin users.47 There was a significantly reduced risk of pancreatic cancer (HR 0.41; 95% CI, 0.33-0.50) with GLP-1RA use compared to insulin47 but not when compared to metformin. However, the study did not include data about specific risk factors for pancreatic diseases, laboratory investigation, or actual body weight, thus increasing the chance for unmeasured confounders. Large randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials have not found major differences in pancreatitis rates.9, 10, 11, 12 However, RTCs are usually of smaller size and continue for a shorter period of time, hence they may not capture slower-to-develop adverse events.

While our study observed a slightly higher risk for AP and pancreatic cancer, the absolute number of cases was low. Using a previously published formula,48 the number needed to harm in association with GLP-1RA use, according to our study, is 1,157 for acute pancreatitis and 1,130 for pancreatic cancer. On the other hand, the number needed to treat to decrease a major cardiovascular event in GLP-1RA users with diabetes was noted in other studies to be 77 individuals,49 to decrease composite renal outcome was 85,50 and for all-cause death was 100.51 Hence, while the benefits seems to outweigh the risk in patients with diabetes, this may not be the case for the widespread use of GLP-1RA for other indications.

Strengths and Limitations

This study using approximately 180,000 matched patients is among the largest studies that examined the risk of AP and pancreatic cancer. We extensively matched comparison groups on a large host of characteristics that may confound outcomes to include actual body weight, laboratory investigations, several comorbidity scores, liver and biliary disease categories, pancreatic diseases, parameters that may estimate healthy-user bias, and health care utilization measures to mitigate ascertainment bias. Our results remained consistent across different types of analysis. We also considered that confounders may have been introduced during the follow-up period given that GLP-1RAs may cause biliary diseases. However, adjusting for the presence of biliary diseases or excluding patients with biliary diseases did not change our findings. This large real-world study provides insight to patients with diabetes who could have multiple comorbidities, contrary to the selectivity of randomized trials.

This study also has some limitations. This is a retrospective observational study, so residual confounding may still exist, and unmeasured confounders may still influence the results. For example, we had several missing laboratory data in the PS-matched cohort (approximately 0.5% of serum creatinine, 0.9% of platelets count, and 0.4% of serum triglycerides, among others). Whereas it is unlikely that these missing labs were differentially missing between the groups, we cannot ascertain this assumption. Our data also lacked information about lifestyle, exercise, diet, or genetic factors. We have attempted to use variables that may act as a proxy for these important confounders, such as BMI, frailty index, and several variables that may reflect healthy or unhealthy behaviors; still, uncertainty remains. Although our data included laboratory investigations and vital signs, we used administrative codes to identify most disease categories. Administrative codes may not accurately identify disease severity; therefore, we included the use of several medication classes related to different chronic diseases in our cohort formation as a proxy for disease severity. Although we used validated and commonly used administrative codes to identify our outcomes, the lack of imaging or pathology reports is another limitation in our study. The results should be interpreted cautiously given that the association of increased risk of AP and pancreatic cancer may be influenced by surveillance bias or diagnostic intensity. However, a post hoc analysis restricting analysis to people without pancreatitis during follow-up or those with normal serum lipase during follow-up resulted in findings similar to the primary analysis. Our population was predominantly men (approximately 92%) and majority White (approximately 73%), which may limit its generalizability. However, prior research has noted that men in the VA population have similar health characteristics to individuals with other insurance coverage, suggesting greater generalizability.52 We used DPP4i as active comparators; although most studies did not identify increased risk of acute pancreatitis in association with DPP4i,23, 24, 25 some studies noted that DPP4i may be associated with the risk of acute pancreatitis.53 Therefore, our results may have underestimated the risk of acute pancreatitis in association of GLP-1RA use.

This study contributes critical insights to the ongoing safety evaluation of GLP-1RAs, a drug class widely embraced for its cardio-renal-metabolic benefits. GLP-1RAs have transformed the management of type 2 diabetes and obesity, with strong evidence supporting their efficacy in glycemic control, weight reduction, and reduction in major adverse cardiovascular and renal events. However, their expanding use, particularly among individuals without diabetes and those with lower baseline cardiovascular or renal risk, underscores the importance of individual risk stratification. This study does not advocate for reduced use of GLP-1RAs across the board, as the absolute risk of pancreatic adverse events remains low, and the clinical benefits—especially in high-risk cardiometabolic populations—are substantial. Instead, it supports a more personalized approach to prescribing, particularly for patients with a personal or family history of pancreatic disease, or other predisposing risk factors such as gallstones, chronic pancreatitis, or heavy alcohol use. In these individuals, shared decision-making should include a discussion of both therapeutic benefits and potential pancreatic risks, even if rare. In addition, clinicians should educate patients about alarming symptoms and remain vigilant for early symptoms of pancreatitis until more definitive evidence becomes available.

This study also emphasizes the need for further investigation. In vitro studies focusing on the effects of GLP-1RA on pancreatic ductal cell proliferation, exocrine function, and inflammatory responses have provided causal pathways.20,21 Further prospective cohort studies and post hoc analyses of large clinical trials focused on high-risk subgroups could help refine risk prediction models and inform clinical guidelines. Longitudinal safety studies for the use of GLP-1RA in patients without diabetes—who are likely to have lower baseline cardiovascular or renal risk profiles—are also needed to ensure safer practice.

Conclusion

In this study, we found a small but significant increase in the risk of acute pancreatitis and pancreatic cancer associated with GLP-1RA compared with DPP-4i use. The low absolute risk should not overshadow the well-established benefits of GLP-1RAs in reducing cardiovascular and renal complications. Further studies are needed to examine the benefit–risk balance in people without diabetes at the lower risk spectrum of cardiovascular and renal diseases.
