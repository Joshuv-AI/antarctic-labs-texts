Dietary pantethine improved egg quality and lipid metabolism via bilateral cooperation of hepatic genes and intestinal microbiota in the laying hens
Authors: Xi Bai, Liangru Ma, Anjian Li, Taozong Guo, Hong Hu, Hongbin Pan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe present work investigated the effects of pantethine on egg quality, lipid parameters, lipid metabolism-related enzymes, hepatic gene expression, and gut microbiota in laying hens. A total of 144 46-week-old laying hens were randomly assigned to two treatments (6 replicates × 12 birds per group): a basic diet without pantethine (CK) and a basic diet supplemented with 200 mg/kg pantethine (TP). Dietary pantethine maintained productive performance and increased egg Haugh unit (P < 0.05). Serum and hepatic triglyceride contents were reduced in pantethine-treated laying hens (P < 0.05). Dietary pantethine elevated lipoprotein lipase and total lipase levels, while decreasing fatty acid synthase and acetyl-CoA carboxylase levels in the liver (P < 0.05). Principal coordinate analysis revealed that dietary pantethine altered the hepatic gene profiles (P < 0.05). Liver transcriptomic analysis identified 427 genes showing significant expression changes in hepatic tissues of the TP group compared with CK group, including 276 upregulated and 151 downregulated genes. KEGG analysis showed enrichment of these genes in the PPAR signaling pathway, involving PPARG, PCK1, PLIN1, and SCD. For intestinal microbiota ​, dietary pantethine significantly changed cecal microbiota structure. Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes were the dominant phyla, but pantethine significantly reduced Firmicutes abundance and the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio in the cecal microbiota (P < 0.05). Burkholderia, Veillonella, and Mycobacterium were positively associated with PPARG but negatively related to PCK1, SCD, and PLIN1. In conclusion, hepatic genes and gut bacteria interacted synergistically to improve egg Haugh unit and hepatic lipid metabolism in pantethine-treated laying hens.Keywords: Pantethin, Laying hens, Lipid metabolism, Liver gene profile, Cecum microbiome

Introduction

As a source of high-quality animal protein, eggs are nutritionally rich and serve as an important food component for maintaining human health (Réhault-Godbert and Guyot, 2019). With global economic expansion and living standards, the demand for eggs has increased rapidly, positioning the laying poultry industry as a crucial pillar of animal husbandry (Bist et al., 2024). However, laying hens are subjected to high-intensity production over long periods, resulting in heavy metabolic burden that renders them prone to diseases and reduces their productive performance (Gao et al., 2021). In modern intensive farming systems, lipid metabolic disorders are key nutritional and metabolic diseases limiting the efficient development of this industry (Hu et al., 2024a; Eck et al., 2023). Characterized by abnormal hepatic fat synthesis and excessive fat deposition in peripheral tissues, lipid metabolic disorders significantly impair the health and productivity of laying hens. Their occurrence results from the interactive effects of multiple factors, including nutritional supply, genetic characteristics, and environmental management.

The gut microbiota-liver axis is a key regulator of lipid metabolism in animals (Yakut, 2025). The liver is the primary site of lipid metabolism in poultry and underpins the ability of laying hens to sustain optimal productivity (Xiao et al., 2025). As a highly productive and intensively managed species, laying hens need to synthesize egg yolk, egg white, and other components to meet the high nutritional demands of the laying period. This drastically increases the metabolic burden on the liver, predisposing hens to impair hepatic metabolic function (Han et al., 2023). With advancing age, the capacity of the liver to transform and utilize nutrients gradually declines, leading to lipid metabolism disorders, reduced egg production rates, and diminished egg quality (Gu et al., 2021). Moreover, intestinal microorganisms play critical roles in regulating laying rate, feed conversion efficiency, and hepatic lipid metabolism (Xue et al., 2025). Therefore, identifying dietary feed additives capable of modulating gut microbiota and optimizing hepatic lipid metabolism represents a key strategy for overcoming these challenges in laying hens.

As a bioactive derivative of vitamin B5, pantethine serves as a critical regulator in lipid metabolic pathways (Chen et al., 2023a). It can decrease liver and abdominal fat deposition and improve metabolic disorders mediated by insulin resistance (Gijsel‐Bonnello et al., 2015). It has pharmacological effects and application potential in prevention of lipid metabolism-related diseases, including nonalcoholic fatty liver disease and atherosclerosis (Tóth et al., 2021). Although pantethine is used in the pharmaceutical, food, and health fields for its strong lipid-lowering ability, studies on its use in laying hens have been scarce. In the present study, we aimed to analyze the influences of pantethine on the productive performance, egg quality, and lipid metabolism. The liver transcriptomics and 16S RNA sequencing were also performed to clarify the possible regulatory mechanisms in the pantethine-treated laying hens. The present work may provide a scientific foundation for the application of pantethine in laying hen farming.

Materials and methods

Experimental design

This study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Yunnan Agricultural University (approval no. 202405010). In this experiment, the inclusion level of phloretin was set at 200 mg/kg, following Hsu et al. (1987). A total of 144 46-week-old Jinghong-2 laying hens (Yunling Guangda Yukou Poultry Industry Co., Ltd., Yunnan, China) with similar productive performance were randomly assigned to two treatments (6 replicates × 12 birds per group): a basal diet without pantethine (CK) and a basal diet supplemented with 200 mg/kg pantethine (TP). The basal diet (Table 1) was designed according to the Feeding Standard of Chickens (NY/T 33-2004). The amount of feed delivered at regular times each day was 112 g. The experimental design included a 7-day acclimatization phase, followed by a 10-week main experimental period. The laying hen subjects had unrestricted access to clean water. Management of this work was carried out in accordance with the Jinghong-2 Yukou Laying Hen Feeding and Management Manual.

Sample Collection

At the termination of the experimental period, 12 eggs (2 eggs/replicate) from each group were randomly collected for quality determination. Twelve laying hens (2 birds/replicate) from each group were randomly selected for slaughter. Blood was collected to isolate serum for assessing serum lipid metabolism. The liver and cecal digesta samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen immediately after collection, then stored at -80°C for subsequent analytical studies.

Laying performance measurement

Daily recordings of egg number and individual mass were conducted for laying hens across all replicates. The feed conversion efficiency and egg production rate for each group were quantified as described by Hu et al. (2024a).

Egg quality measurement

Yolk%, albumen height, and Haugh unit (HU) were measured using a Robotmation EMT-7300 (Tokyo, Japan). The digital caliper was used to detect the egg shell thickness, length and width. Egg shape index was quantified as the length/width ratio of eggs. Egg weight was measured using a 0.1 mg electronic balance (Sartorius, Göttingen, Germany).

Determination of serum and liver lipid indices

Serum and liver homogenate levels of total protein (BCA assay), total cholesterol (T-CHO), and triglyceride (TG) were determined by spectrophotometric analysis using commercially available assay kits (Jiancheng, Nanjing, China). Commercial kits (Comin, Suzhou, China) were used to measure low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C).

Determination of hepatic lipid metabolism-related enzymes

Lipoprotein lipase (LPL), lipase (LPS), hepatic lipase (HL), total lipase (TL), fatty acid synthase (FAS), and acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC) levels were measured using commercial kits (Jiancheng, Nanjing, China).

Liver transcriptomic analysis

Total RNA sample (1 birds/replicate) was isolated from liver tissue with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA). RNA quality was assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis, Nanodrop2000 (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA), and Agilent2100 (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). A transcriptome library was then constructed as described by Hu et al. (2024a) and sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA). Following the methodology of Hu et al. (2024a), data quality assessment, genome alignment, and transcript abundance analyses were performed. The DESeq2 software (version 1.36.0) was employed to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs; fold change > 2 and P < 0.05). Subsequently, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) annotations were obtained from the reference genome, and functional enrichment analysis was conducted using clusterProfiler 4.0.

Cecum microbiome sequencing

The cecal contents of chickens harbor a highly abundant and diverse microbial community with core functions in lipid metabolism, making them an ideal sample type for investigating the relationship between gut microbiota and hepatic lipid metabolism in chickens (Hu et al., 2024a; Liu et al., 2023). Therefore, cecal content was selected for further analysis. Microbial DNA sample (1 birds/replicate) was extracted using a commercial DNA extraction kit (Omega, Norcross, GA). To analyze bacterial community structure, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification was performed with universal primers 27F (5′-AGRGTTYGATYMTGGCTCAG-3′) and 1492R (5′-RGYTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′), targeting the V1–V9 region. PCR amplification and sequencing library preparation followed the methods of Hu et al. (2024a). Libraries were sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA) using a 250-bp paired-end strategy at BIOZERON Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Clean reads, species annotation, alpha and beta diversity indices, and differentially abundant microbes were analyzed according to Hu et al. (2024a). Paired-end reads were assembled, chimeric sequences were removed, and the remaining clean data were clustered into amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) using the DADA2 plugin in QIIME2. Singletons (ASVs with a count of 1) were excluded, and the remaining ASVs were taxonomically annotated using the SILVA database (Release 138).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of experimental data was performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test via SPSS16.0, and results were presented as mean ± SEM. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Correlation analyses were performed using Spearman’s method implemented in the psych package of R v4.4.2. The Spearman correlation coefficient (R) was used to assess the degree of monotonic association between two variables, with P < 0.05 indicating a significant correlation.

Liver transcriptomics analysis

Differences in hepatic gene profiles between the CK and TP groups were analyzed using principal coordinate analysis (PCoA; Fig. 1a). The six samples in each group clustered separately, and distinct hepatic gene expression patterns were observed between the two groups (P < 0.05). DEGs analysis identified 427 genes with significant expression changes in the TP group relative to CK group, including 276 upregulated and 151 downregulated genes (Fig. 1b). As shown in Fig. 1c, the upregulated genes in the TP group were mainly associated with signal transduction, whereas the downregulated genes were more evenly distributed and largely involved in general metabolism. KEGG enrichment analysis indicated that the PPAR signaling pathway, including PPARG, PCK1, PLIN1, and SCD, was a key pathway involved in regulating hepatic lipid metabolism in laying hens (Fig. 1d and 1e).

Effect of dietary pantethine on the cecal microbiome

No significant differences in alpha diversity were observed in the cecal microbiota of pantethine-treated laying hens (Fig. 2a). Conversely, dietary pantethine significantly changed the structure of the cecal microbiota (P < 0.05; Fig. 2b). The cecal microbiota of laying hens was dominated by the phyla Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes (Fig. 2c). Dietary pantethine significantly reduced Firmicutes abundance and Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio in the cecal microbiota (P < 0.05; Fig. 2d). At the genus level, dietary pantethine increased the relative abundance of Phocaeicola, Megamonas, and Megasphaera, while decreasing Ligilactobacillus, Barnesiella, and Butyricicoccus levels (P < 0.05; Fig. 2e). Pantethine treatment replaced the original mean selection process with heterogeneous selection (Fig. 2f). Random forest analysis identified 19 bacterial genera, including Limonspira, Burkholderia, and Veillonella, as key contributors to differences in intestinal microbiota between the CK and TP groups (Fig. 2e).

Correlation analysis of the hepatic lipid metabolism, PPAR pathway related genes, and cecal bacteria

Correlation analysis was conducted to evaluate inter-indicator relationships among egg quality, lipid metabolism indices, liver transcriptomic profiles, and different gut bacteria in pantethine-treated laying hens (Fig. 3). Following significant correlations, core biomarkers associated with hepatic lipid dysregulation were identified, encompassing seven hepatic lipid metabolic parameters, four liver-expressed genes, and nineteen gut microbiota taxa. As shown in the Fig.. 3a, a positive association was observed between PCK1 expression and S-TG levels as well as FAS activity; PLIN1 was positively correlated with S-HDL; SCD was positively related to L-TG, but inversely related to HL and TES; PPARG was inversely related to L-TG and ACC (P < 0.05). As shown in Fig. 3b, Burkholderia, Veillonella, and Mycobacterium were positively related to PPARG but inversely related to PCK1, SCD, and PLIN1. PPARG was positively correlated with the HU of the eggs (Fig. 3c). According to these functions and significant correlations, the underlying mechanism may be that dietary pantethine improved lipid metabolism through gut microbiota -liver axis (Fig. 4).

Discussion

In intensive large-scale farming systems, laying hens in the peak laying period remain in a continuous state of high production and are subjected to heavy physiological burdens (Gao et al., 2021). This condition compromises the body’s metabolic capacity, particularly lipid metabolism, thereby influencing both productive performance and egg quality (Eck et al., 2023). Pantethine, a vitamin-type compound, is widely used in medical research as a lipid-lowering product (Osono et al., 2000); however, its application in laying hens has not been much studied. Hsu et al. (1987) found that dietary 200 mg/kg pantethine showed no measurable impact on laying hen performance parameters, including final body weight, daily feed consumption, egg weight, and egg production. Consistent with these findings, dietary pantethine in the present study did not significantly affect the productive performance of laying hens. Egg quality, typically assessed through parameters such as eggshell thickness and strength, HU, and egg shape index, is a critical indicator in laying hen production (Wang et al., 2021). HU is widely used as a criterion for evaluating egg freshness and, to some extent, reflects the nutritional value of eggs. It is also important for laying hen farming, egg processing, and marketing (Rho and Cho, 2024). HU has been reported to be positively correlated with egg quality (Rho and Cho, 2024). In this study, pantethine supplementation increased HU, suggesting a role in improving egg quality. The underlying mechanism may be linked to hepatic lipid metabolism. Li et al. (2024) reported that feed additives can enhance egg quality (HU, albumen height, and yolk color) by improving hepatic lipid metabolism.

Lipid is primarily synthesized in the liver of poultry (Liu et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2024b). Normal lipid metabolism ensures the balance among the lipogenesis, lipid transportation, and lipolysis in the liver. However, during peak egg-laying period, the liver of laying hens undertakes substantial lipid synthesis and transport, making it prone to excessive fat deposition that compromises hepatic health (Eck et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2024). Therefore, maintaining a good hepatic lipid metabolism is crucial for preventing liver diseases and ensuring the health of laying hens. Pantethine is a well-tolerated drug that lowers the blood lipid and cholesterol levels (Abou-Hamdan et al., 2023). Chen et al. (2023a) found that oral pantethine reduced body weight, body fat, and cholesterol levels in mice. Pantethine not only effectively reduces TG levels but also exerts beneficial effects on T-CHO, HDL-C, and LDL-C (Chen et al., 2023a; Hsu et al., 1987). Adding 200 ppm/kg pantethine to a corn-soybean basal diet was reported to significantly decrease abdominal fat weight, hepatic TG levels, and plasma T-CHO and 17β-estradiol concentrations in laying hens (Hsu et al., 1987). In this study, pantethine supplementation in laying hen diets significantly reduced TG levels in the serum and liver, which is consistent with previous findings. Consequently, a diet supplemented with pantethine can reduce hepatic fat deposition in laying hens.

Hepatic lipid metabolism involves multiple key enzymes, including those responsible for fatty acid synthesis and lipid catabolism, which play vital roles in lipid synthesis, degradation, and transport (Heeren and Scheja, 2021). The principal lipogenic enzymes, ACC and FAS, catalyze the conversion of acetyl-CoA to malonyl-CoA (the initiation step) and subsequent carbon chain elongation (Gong et al., 2023). Lipid degradation depends on enzymes such as LPL, HL, and TL. LPL primarily hydrolyzes TGs in chylomicrons and very-low-density lipoproteins (VLDLs), whereas HL regulates HDL metabolism and hepatic lipid output balance (Hu et al., 2024b). Several studies have reported that pantethine significantly decreases ACC and FAS activity, thereby stimulating lipolysis and inhibiting lipogenesis in hepatocytes and liver tissue (Hsu et al., 1992; Angiari et al., 2024). We found that dietary 200 mg/kg pantethine significantly reduced fatty acid synthesis enzyme (ACC and FAS) levels and increased the fatty acid catabolic enzyme (LPS and TL) levels. This mechanism positively regulates hepatic lipid metabolism by inhibiting fat synthesis and activating fat decomposition, thereby reducing hepatic lipid deposition.

Transcriptome sequencing enables systematic investigation of gene functions, gene structures, and specific biological processes (Wan et al., 2011). In this study, 427 DEGs were identified between the CK and TP groups, indicating that dietary pantethine altered the hepatic transcriptional profile of laying hens. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment of the PPAR signaling pathway, a master regulator of hepatic lipid metabolism and energy balance (Chen et al., 2023b; Pawlak et al., 2015). In the liver, activation of the PPAR pathway stimulates the transcription of genes associated with lipid catabolism, thereby enhancing mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation (Chen et al., 2023b; Qiu et al., 2023). Notably, PPARG, a gene associated with fatty acid β-oxidation, was upregulated, whereas PCK1, PLIN1, and SCD, which are involved in fatty acid synthesis, were downregulated. Collectively, these findings suggest that pantethine supplementation regulates the hepatic PPAR signaling pathway, thereby enhancing lipid metabolism, reducing fat deposition, and supporting hepatic health.

The intestinal microbial community plays an indispensable role in poultry physiology through its extensive involvement in nutrient metabolism and immunological regulation (Huang et al., 2024). For PCoA analysis, CK and TP groups clustered separately in distinct directions with significant differences, indicating that pantethine supplementation affected the intestinal microbiota structure of laying hens. The dominant phyla in the gut of laying hens were Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, and Proteobacteria, with Firmicutes showing absolute predominance (Wang et al., 2023). Firmicutes mainly help hosts absorb energy, often leading to weight gain (Sun et al., 2023). However, the carbohydrate metabolism and sugar decomposition to produce volatile fatty acids are functions commonly associated with Bacteroidetes (Houtman et al., 2022). Compared to normal-weight patients, obese patients with nonalcoholic steatohepatitis exhibit reduced Bacteroidetes and increased Firmicutes levels (Zhu et al., 2013). The F/B ratio is pivotal for intestinal balance and correlates with adipose tissue deposition patterns (Zhao et al., 2022). Higher F/B ratios are often observed in obese individuals (Magne et al., 2020; Grigor’eva, 2020). The CK group exhibited significantly greater abundance of Firmicutes compared to the TP group, resulting in a substantially lower F/B ratio in the TP group. Furthermore, random forest and correlation analyses revealed that Burkholderia, Veillonella, and Mycobacterium were significantly associated with PPARG, PCK1, PLIN1, and SCD. Numerous studies have demonstrated a close interaction between the gut microbiota and the hepatic PPAR signaling pathway (Murakami et al., 2016; Hasan et al., 2019). The gut microbiota can influence the expression and activation of hepatic PPARG through microbial metabolites, thereby regulating hepatic lipid metabolism (Murakami et al., 2016; Hasan et al., 2019). Hu et al. (2024c) reported that dietary bile acids improved gut microbiota composition and hepatic PPARG activity, thereby reducing hepatic lipid deposition. It can be inferred that dietary pantethine may regulate the PPAR signaling pathway via modulation of the gut microbiota to improve hepatic lipid metabolism in laying hens.

Conclusions

In conclusion, dietary pantethine supplementation improved egg quality to some extent. The PPAR signaling pathway appears to be a key target mediating the metabolic regulatory actions of pantethine. Moreover, pantethine significantly modulated the intestinal microbial community of laying hens by reducing the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio, reshaping microbiota composition, and ultimately enhancing lipid metabolism. Together, these findings indicate that hepatic genes and gut bacteria act cooperatively to promote liver lipid metabolism in pantethine-treated laying hens. This study provides a theoretical foundation for the potential application of pantethine in commercial laying hen production systems.
