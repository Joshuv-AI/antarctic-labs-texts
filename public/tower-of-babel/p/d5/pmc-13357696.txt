Anti‐amyloid nanobody‐Fc fusion protein drives potent amyloid clearance through microglial recruitment
Authors: Zonghui Ding, Katelin A. Gibson, David G. Nascari, Lauren E. Tallant, Samantha M. Bouchal, Dennis W. Dickson, John D. Fryer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONAnti‐amyloid beta (Aβ) monoclonal antibodies are effective at lowering amyloid in Alzheimer's disease (AD). However, whether Fc‐mediated effector function is absolutely required for efficacy is not completely understood. This is important for optimizing therapeutic efficacy and mitigating side effects such as amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities (ARIA). Antibodies lacking Fc effector function, like single‐domain antibodies (nanobodies), offer a unique tool to dissect these mechanisms, as their small size facilitates blood–brain barrier (BBB) penetration and allows Fc‐mediated functions to be studied independently.METHODSWe immunized a llama with Aβ aggregates and constructed a phage display library to screen for aggregate‐specific nanobodies. Lead candidates were characterized by epitope mapping and binding affinity to amyloid plaques in both murine and human AD brain tissues. We further assessed their BBB permeability and evaluated their efficacy in clearing pre‐existing plaques in amyloid precursor protein (APP)/presenilin 1 (PS1) mice.RESULTSWe identified two lead nanobodies, 3A11 and 2D10, that bind distinct epitopes and specifically bind Aβ plaques in murine and human AD brain tissues. Following systemic administration, the monovalent, unmodified (Fc‐less) 2D10 nanobody, but not 3A11, successfully crossed the BBB and engaged amyloid plaques in APP/PS1 mice. However, despite robust target engagement, the Fc‐less 2D10 failed to recruit microglia or reduce plaque burden. In contrast, an engineered 2D10‐Fc fusion antibody potently cleared amyloid plaques, achieving a reduction in pathology comparable to aducanumab treatment. This efficacy was directly correlated with Fc‐mediated microglial recruitment and activation, demonstrating that the Fc domain is essential for phagocytic plaque removal.DISCUSSIONOur findings demonstrate that Fc effector function is indispensable for microglial‐mediated amyloid clearance in vivo. By clarifying this fundamental mechanism, this study provides a framework for the rational design of next‐generation immunotherapies. Furthermore, 2D10‐Fc represents a promising therapeutic candidate, combining the high‐affinity targeting of nanobodies with the effector power necessary for robust plaque clearance.

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the leading cause of age‐related dementia, affecting an estimated 7.2 million individuals in the United States and posing an escalating global public health challenge. 1 This devastating disease is pathologically characterized by the accumulation of extracellular amyloid beta (Aβ) in the form of parenchymal plaques and cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA), as well as intraneuronal neurofibrillary tangles of hyperphosphorylated tau. 2 These pathologies trigger downstream events including synaptic loss, chronic neuroinflammation, and progressive neurodegeneration, which are the primary drivers of clinical cognitive decline. 3 The amyloid hypothesis posits that Aβ accumulation is the critical initiating event in AD pathogenesis. 4 , 5 This paradigm recently reached a critical milestone with the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval of the first disease‐modifying therapies for AD: the anti‐Aβ monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) aducanumab, lecanemab, and donanemab. 6

While the clinical efficacy of these mAbs correlates with their capacity to clear amyloid plaques from the brain, optimizing next‐generation drugs requires resolving whether plaque clearance requires Fc‐mediated effector function by engaging microglial Fc receptors (FcγRs) to trigger phagocytosis. 7 Notably, evidence for non‐Fc‐mediated clearance mechanisms has been reported in several key studies. For instance, Das et al. observed equivalent Aβ reduction in FcγR‐sufficient versus FcγR‐knockout Tg2576 mice following active Aβ1‐42 immunization. 8 Similarly, Bacskai et al. demonstrated that F(ab’)2 fragments of the anti‐Aβ 3D6 antibody cleared approximately 50% of plaques in 20‐month‐old PDAPP mice, which is as effective as the full‐length 3D6 antibody. 9 Furthermore, Wang et al. and Cattepoel et al. independently found that anti‐Aβ single‐chain variable fragments (scFvs) reduced cerebral Aβ and improved cognition performance in APP/PS1 mice via systemic or intranasal delivery, despite lacking an Fc domain. 10 , 11 Critically, the Fc‐mediated pathway is also implicated in amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities with edema or hemorrhage (ARIA‐E and ARIA‐H, respectively), the primary adverse events associated with anti‐Aβ immunotherapy, likely originating from over‐activation of immune cells surrounding CAA. Furthermore, conventional IgGs exhibit poor blood–brain barrier (BBB) penetrance, with typically <0.5% of systemic doses reaching the central nervous system (CNS). 12 , 13 This inefficiency necessitates high, frequent dosing, increasing both treatment costs and the risk of systemic side effects.

Here, we utilized single‐domain antibodies (nanobodies or VHHs) to better understand these mechanisms. Derived from camelid heavy‐chain‐only antibodies, nanobodies are small (∼12 to 15 kDa), monovalent, and naturally lack an Fc domain. 14 Their high stability, solubility, and modularity, which are highlighted by the 2019 FDA approval of caplacizumab, make them an ideal tool for therapeutics or mechanistic studies. 15 Comparing a parent nanobody to its Fc‐fused derivative makes it possible to test the contribution of the Fc domain experimentally.

Previous work demonstrated that anti‐Aβ nanobodies could bind diverse Aβ conformations and deposits in human tissue and cross the BBB in vitro and in vivo. 16 , 17 , 18 However, a fundamental question remains: Can high‐affinity, brain‐penetrant nanobodies independently drive plaque clearance, or is an Fc domain necessary to recruit microglia and phagocytose plaques? Resolving this is essential for engineering immunotherapies that maximize amyloid clearance while minimizing the inflammatory triggers of ARIA.

In this study, we address this question using two lead anti‐Aβ nanobody clones, 3A11 and 2D10, that recognize distinct epitopes and robustly bind amyloid plaques in both murine models and human AD post mortem brain sections. We confirmed that our lead candidate, 2D10, engaged plaques in vivo following systemic administration. By performing a head‐to‐head efficacy study comparing monovalent 2D10, an engineered 2D10‐Fc fusion, and chimeric mouse aducanumab, we demonstrate that while the monovalent nanobody is sufficient for target engagement, the Fc domain is absolutely required for plaque clearance. Our findings clarify the necessity of Fc‐mediated microglial recruitment in AD immunotherapy and establish a versatile nanobody‐based platform for the rational design of future modular CNS biologics.

METHODS

Library construction and nanobody discovery

A llama was immunized with Aβ1‐42 aggregates to elicit an immune response. The generation of an immune phage display library and isolation of Aβ1‐42 aggregates binding variable heavy domain of a heavy chain‐only antibody (VHH) clones were performed using previously described methods. 19 , 20 Briefly, total RNA was extracted from peripheral blood lymphocytes and used to synthesize first‐strand complementary DNA (cDNA) using the SuperScript IV First‐Strand Synthesis System. The VHH repertoire was amplified from the cDNA using Q5 high‐fidelity DNA polymerase with specific primers (CALL001: 5′‐GTCCTGGCTGCTCTTCTACAAGG‐3′; and CALL002: 5′‐GGTACGTGCTGTTGAACTGTTCC‐3′). The amplicon derived from heavy‐chain‐only IgG was separated from conventional IgG using agarose gel electrophoresis, and the ∼700 bp band was isolated and purified using a QIAquick Gel Extraction kit. A secondary polymerase chain reaction was performed using primers containing SapI restriction sites (VHH‐Back‐SAPI: 5’‐CTTGGCTCTTCTGTGCAGCTGCAGGAGTCTGGRGGAGG‐3’; and VHH‐Forward‐SAPI: 5’‐TGATGCTCTTCCGCTGAGGAGACGGTGACCTGGGT‐3’) to facilitate cloning into the pMECS‐GG phagemid vector. 20 This vector, which incorporates C‐terminal HA and 6xHis tags, was kindly provided by Dr. Serge Muyldermans (Vrije Universiteit Brussel). The resulting VHH repertoire was digested with SapI, ligated into the pMECS‐GG phagemid vector, and transformed to construct a high‐diversity immune library. Phage particles were rescued using M13KO7 helper phage and purified through polyethylene glycol (20% PEG8000/2.5 M NaCl). To enrich for high‐affinity binders, three rounds of biopanning were performed against immobilized Aβ1‐42 aggregates. Following enrichment, VHH sequences were confirmed by Sanger sequencing (GeneWiz, NJ) using the MP57 primer (5’‐TTATGCTTCCGGCTCGTATG‐3’). Binding specificity was validated via enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) using monoclonal periplasmic extracts. Based on these binding profiles and subsequent epitope mapping, two lead candidates, 2D10 and 3A11, were selected for comprehensive characterization.

Expression and purification of single‐domain antibodies and aducanumab

For initial in vitro characterization, nanobodies were expressed in the periplasm of E. coli (strain WK6). Following induction with isopropyl‐β‐D‐thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG), cells were harvested, and the periplasmic fraction was extracted via osmotic shock. The nanobodies, which contain C‐terminal HA and 6xHis tags, were purified using immobilized metal affinity chromatography (IMAC). For in vivo studies, large‐scale expression of 2D10 and anti‐green fluorescent protein (GFP) (negative control) nanobodies with C‐terminal HA and His tags were produced using the TurboCHO protein expression service (GenScript Biotech, Piscataway, NJ), followed by Ni‐NTA affinity chromatography. The 2D10‐Fc fusion protein (using a mouse IgG2a Fc domain) was similarly produced via TurboCHO and purified using protein A magnetic beads. To serve as a positive control, the chimeric mouse aducanumab (comprising the variable regions of aducanumab on a mouse IgG2a backbone) was generated by Absolute Antibody Ltd (Cleveland, UK) through transient transfection of HEK293 cells followed by protein purification. All large‐scale protein preparations were validated for high purity (> 96%) via SDS‐PAGE and were confirmed to have low endotoxin levels (< 0.1 EU/mg) to ensure suitability for systemic administration in mice.

Epitope mapping and binding Assays

For direct ELISA, 96‐well microtiter plates (Maxisorp; Nunc) were coated with Aβ1‐42 aggregates (300 ng/well) in 1x ELISA coating buffer overnight at 4°C. Following blocking, individual nanobody clones (200 nM) were incubated for 1 h at room temperature. A non‐targeting irrelevant nanobody served as a negative control. Bound nanobodies were detected using a primary rabbit anti‐HA antibody (1:2000, Catalog No.: 3724, Cell Signaling Technology) followed by a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‐conjugated goat anti‐rabbit secondary antibody (1:5000, Catalog No.: 1706515, Bio‐Rad). After the final wash, the assay was developed using 1‐Step Ultra TMB‐ELISA Substrate Solutions (Catalog No.: 34028, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Absorbance was measured at 370 nm using an Agilent BioTek Cytation 5 Cell Imaging MultiMode microplate reader.

For epitope mapping via dot‐blotting, nitrocellulose membranes were spotted with 600 ng of various indicated Aβ fragments (Catalog Nos.: AS‐25365, AS‐62447, AS‐22823, AS‐61972, ANASPEC). Membranes were blocked with EveryBlot blocking buffer and incubated with 2 ug/mL of lead nanobodies (3A11 or 2D10). Signals were visualized using DyLight 680‐conjugated anti‐HA antibody (Catalog No.: 600‐444‐384, ROCKLAND) and imaged on a LI‐COR Odyssey Fc system.

Histopathology and immunofluorescence (IF) of mouse brain sections

For immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis, formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) mouse brain sections (10 um) were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded series of ethanol. Heat‐induced antigen retrieval was performed using a citric acid‐based antigen unmasking solution (Catalog No.: H‐3300‐250, Vector Laboratories). To quench endogenous peroxidase activity, the sections were incubated with BLOXALL endogenous peroxidase and alkaline phosphatase blocking solution (Catalog No.: SP‐6000, Vector Laboratories). The sections were blocked with normal goat serum and incubated overnight at 4°C with the lead nanobodies (2D10 or 3A11, 100 ng/mL) or PBS, which served as a negative no‐VHH control. Following primary incubation, sections were incubated with a rabbit anti‐HA antibody (to target the C‐terminal HA tag), followed by a goat anti‐rabbit HRP‐polymer (Catalog No.: MP‐7451, Vector Laboratories). After three washes in Tris‐buffered saline solution containing 1% Tween‐20 (TBS‐T), the signal was developed using a DAB substrate kit (Catalog No.: SK‐4100, Vector Laboratories). Sections were counterstained with hematoxylin and mounted using Cytoseal 60. Images were acquired using a Keyence BZ‐X800 microscope with 4× and 20× objectives.

For IF, coronal frozen sections (50 um) from amyloid precursor protein (APP)/presenilin 1 (PS1) mice were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton‐X in PBS for 30 min and blocked with 2% milk in PBS‐X for 2 h. The sections were then co‐stained overnight at 4°C with Alexa Fluor 488‐conjugated MOAB‐2 (1:500, Catalog No.: NBP2‐13075AF488, Novus Biologicals) or the lead nanobodies (2D10 or 3A11, 2 ug/mL). Bound nanobodies were visualized through a secondary incubation with an Alexa Fluor 555‐conjugated mouse anti‐HA antibody (1:250, Catalog No.: 26183‐A555, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The sections were mounted with VECTASHIELD antifade mounting medium (Vector Laboratories). Images were acquired using a Keyence BZ‐X800 microscope with a 20× objective.

IHC analysis of human AD brain tissues

To evaluate the translational potential of the lead nanobodies in human pathology, FFPE serial sections (10 um) were obtained from the brain bank at the Mayo Clinic's Department of Neuroscience in Florida. This tissue originated from a 74‐year‐old female AD patient characterized by advanced pathology (Braak stage VI, Thal phase 5), an APOE ε3/ε4 genotype, and severe (3+) CAA. Sections were deparaffinized, subjected to heat‐induced antigen retrieval, and treated to quench endogenous peroxidase activity as described above. The sections were blocked with normal goat or horse serum and incubated overnight at 4°C with the lead nanobodies (2D10 or 3A11, 100 ng/mL) or chimeric mouse aducanumab (10 ng/mL), which served as a positive clinical‐stage control. Following primary incubation, nanobody‐labeled sections were incubated with a rabbit anti‐HA antibody, followed by a goat anti‐rabbit HRP polymer. The section labeled with the chimeric mouse aducanumab was detected using a horse anti‐mouse HRP polymer (Catalog No.: MP‐7422, Vector Laboratories). After washing three times in TBS‐T, the signal was developed using a DAB substrate kit. Sections were counterstained with hematoxylin and mounted using Cytoseal 60. Representative images of parenchymal plaques and CAA were acquired using a Keyence BZ‐X800 microscope with a 4× objective.

Animals

APPswe/PS1dE9 (APP/PS1) transgenic mice of both sexes were housed in a temperature‐ and humidity‐controlled animal facility at Mayo Clinic Arizona under a 12 h light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Animal Case and Use Committee (IACUC).

In vivo target engagement assay

Nine‐month‐old APP/PS1 mice (N 2/group) received daily intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections of 2D10 (40 mg/kg) or PBS for four consecutive days. A separate APP/PS1 mouse received daily i.p. injections of 3A11 (40 mg/kg) for three consecutive days. Five hours after the final dose, mice were deeply anesthetized with Euthasol and intracardially perfused with ice‐cold PBS containing 1 mM EDTA. Brains were harvested and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at 4°C for 48 h, followed by cryoprotection in a 30% sucrose in PBS solution until they sank. Coronal frozen sections (50 um) were co‐stained with Alexa Fluor 488‐conjugated MOAB‐2 to identify amyloid plaques and either an Alexa Fluor 555‐conjugated mouse anti‐HA antibody or a Cy3‐conjugated goat anti‐alpaca VHH antibody (1:250, Catalog No.: 128‐165‐230, Jackson ImmunoResearch) to detect the HA‐tagged nanobodies bound to the pathology. Images were acquired using the Keyence BZ‐X800 microscope with 4× and 20× objectives.

In vivo efficacy study

Fifteen‐month‐old APP/PS1 mice (male and female) on a pure C57BL/6J background were randomly assigned to one of four treatment groups: monovalent 2D10 (N = 17), 2D10‐Fc (N = 16), mouse chimeric aducanumab (positive control, N = 16), or anti‐GFP nanobody (negative control, N = 10). The study team was blinded to the treatment groups. Mice received weekly i.p. injections (40 mg/kg) for a duration of 6 weeks. One week after the final dose, brains were collected following the perfusion and fixation procedures and subjected to the Congo red derivative X34 staining and immunostaining as described above. Amyloid burden was quantified as the percentage area occupied by MAOB‐2 (total Aβ) in both the cortex and hippocampus. Microglial recruitment and activation were assessed via LGALS3 immunoreactivity. The following antibodies were used: Alexa Fluor 750‐conjugated mouse anti‐human Aβ (1:500, NBP2‐13075AF750; Novus), goat anti‐mouse LGALS3 (1:300, AF1197, R&D Systems), rabbit anti‐IBA1 antibody (1:500, Catalog No.: 019‐19741, FUJIFILM), Alexa Fluor 488‐conjugated donkey anti‐goat antibody (1:750, Catalog No.: 705‐545‐003, Jackson ImmunoResearch), and Cy3‐conjugated donkey anti‐rabbit antibody (1:500, Catalog No.: 711‐165‐152, Jackson ImmunoResearch). Whole‐slide digital images were acquired using a Zeiss Axioscan 7 slide scanner with a 10× objective.

Plaque and microglia analysis

Quantitative analysis of immunofluorescent samples from the efficacy study was performed in QuPath version 0.6.0. Cortex and hippocampus were annotated in a blinded manner looking at the DAPI channel only. Within these annotations, plaques were thresholded on both the X34 and MOAB2 channels and created as objects. For intensity analysis (LGALS3), the plaque objects were created using combined X34 and MOAB2 signal and expanded by 10 µm, and the mean fluorescent intensity of the desired channel was measured within the plaque object. All quantitative image analysis was performed by an investigator (D.G.N.) blinded to the treatment groups to ensure unbiased assessment of plaque burden and microglial activation.

Statistical analyses

Statistical significance was determined using GraphPad Prism version 10. For multiple comparisons, we used a one‐way ANOVA followed by a Holm‐Sidak post hoc test. All data are represented as mean ± SEM. P values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001).

RESULTS

Identification of Aβ1‐42 aggregate‐specific nanobodies

To generate nanobodies (VHHs) targeting pathogenic Aβ species, we immunized a llama with Aβ1‐42 aggregates, leveraging the camelid immune system's ability to produce heavy‐chain‐only antibodies. Following immunization, peripheral blood lymphocyte RNA was isolated, and the VHH repertoire was cloned into the pMECS‐GG phage‐display vector to construct a high‐diversity immune library (Figure 1A). After three rounds of biopanning against immobilized Aβ1‐42 aggregates, we isolated several unique clones.

The VHH repertoire was cloned into the pMECS‐GG phagemid vector to construct a high‐diversity phage‐display library. Three rounds of panning were performed against immobilized Aβ1‐42 aggregates, and specificity of soluble nanobodies was validated via ELISA assays. (B) Validation of candidates via direct ELISA. Based on preliminary binding results, we selected two potential lead candidates, 3A11 and 2D10, for further characterization. Direct enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs) confirmed that both clones exhibited high‐affinity binding to Aβ1‐42 aggregates (Figure 1B). Epitope mapping via dot‐blotting with overlapping Aβ fragments revealed that 3A11 and 2D10 recognized distinct, non‐overlapping C‐terminal sequences: GAIIGLM (residues 29‐35) and VGGVVIA (residues 36–42), respectively (Figure 1C). Targeting these C‐terminal epitopes might be therapeutically advantageous, as it avoids the “secondary nucleation” effects associated with N‐terminal antibodies, which can promote the formation of more neurotoxic Aβ oligomers. 21

Histopathological characterization in murine and human AD brains

We next assessed the ability of 3A11 and 2D10 nanobodies to recognize pathological amyloid plaques in situ. IHC analysis of brain sections from 12‐month‐old APP/PS1 mice revealed robust labeling of both dense‐core and diffuse plaques in the cortex and hippocampus (Figure 2A). To confirm specificity, we performed dual‐label immunofluorescence with MOAB‐2, a pan‐Aβ antibody. Both nanobodies showed colocalization with MOAB‐2‐positive plaques, confirming their high specificity for amyloid plaques in this model (Figure 2B).

To evaluate translational potential, we examined human AD post mortem tissue. IHC analysis showed that both 3A11 and 2D10 robustly labeled parenchymal plaques and CAA, exhibiting a staining profile remarkably similar to the anti‐amyloid monoclonal chimeric mouse aducanumab (hereafter referred to as “aducanumab”) that has the humanized antibody's variable region but with a “murinized” mouse IgG2a Fc domain (Figure 3).

In vivo target engagement and BBB penetration of 2D10

We next assessed whether our lead nanobodies could spontaneously cross the BBB and engage amyloid plaques in vivo. To evaluate target engagement, 9‐month‐old APP/PS1 mice received daily i.p. injections of either 40 mg/kg 2D10 or a PBS vehicle control for four consecutive days (Figure 4A). Post mortem immunofluorescence analyses revealed robust 2D10 immunoreactivity associated with amyloid plaques in the treated group, confirming successful BBB penetration and specific target engagement by 2D10 (Figure 4B). In contrast, 3A11 showed negligible engagement of cortical plaques (Figure S1). Consequently, 2D10 was prioritized over 3A11 for subsequent studies evaluating the efficacy of plaque clearance in the APP/PS1 model.

Fc‐Effector function is essential for plaque clearance

To investigate whether Fc‐mediated microglial recruitment was essential for plaque clearance, we conducted a 6‐week efficacy study using 15‐month‐old APP/PS1 mice. Animals received weekly i.p. injections with 40 mg/kg of monovalent 2D10, 2D10‐Fc fusion (fused to a mouse IgG2a Fc domain), aducanumab (positive control), or anti‐GFP nanobody (negative control) (Figure 5A). Quantitative analysis revealed a clear mechanistic distinction for the monovalent and Fc‐fused nanobodies. Consistent with our previous observations, 22 aducanumab significantly reduced the total amyloid burden in both the cortex and hippocampus. Despite its demonstrated ability to penetrate the BBB and engage pathology, monovalent 2D10 failed to reduce amyloid plaque burden in either the cortex or the hippocampus (Figure 5B). In contrast, 2D10‐Fc exhibited potent therapeutic efficacy, reducing total amyloid load (MOAB‐2 positive) to levels comparable to those achieved by aducanumab. This disparity between two molecules sharing an identical paratope demonstrates that the Fc domain is indispensable for amyloid clearance in vivo. Treatment with 2D10‐Fc or aducanumab, but not monovalent 2D10, resulted in significant changes in microglial recruitment and activation (LGALS3) in the cortex and hippocampus, indicating effective recruitment and activation of microglia around amyloid plaques (Figure 6A,B). These findings indicate that the presence of an Fc domain is required to effectively recruit and activate microglia around amyloid plaques to drive therapeutic clearance.

DISCUSSION

The clinical validation of anti‐Aβ monoclonal antibodies has intensified the need to resolve their precise mechanism of action, specifically the relative importance of Fab‐mediated neutralization versus Fc‐mediated effector function. Our finding that the monovalent 2D10 nanobody failed to reduce plaque burden despite robust target engagement contrasts with several previous reports suggesting that Aβ clearance can occur independently of Fc‐mediated pathways. For instance, active immunization in FcγR‐knockout mice has been reported to yield Aβ reduction equivalent to that of FcγR‐sufficient controls, 8 and F(ab’)2 fragments of the 3D6 antibody were shown to clear approximately 50% of plaques in PDAPP mice. 9 Furthermore, studies utilizing scFvs demonstrated that these Fc‐less constructs could reduce cerebral Aβ and improve cognitive performance. 10 , 11 Additionally, previous studies suggested that removing the Fc domain eliminated plaque clearance, 23 and the structural differences between fragmented IgGs, scFvs, and full‐length antibodies have often confounded definitive conclusions. In this study, we utilized the unique modularity of single‐domain antibodies (nanobodies) to isolate the Fc domain as a single experimental variable. Our results provide direct evidence that while high‐affinity target engagement is achievable with monovalent nanobodies, therapeutic plaque clearance is fundamentally dependent on the presence of a functional Fc domain to recruit microglial phagocytic machinery.

Importantly, the ability of our nanobodies to recognize both parenchymal and vascular amyloid (CAA) in human AD tissue mirrors the binding profile of aducanumab. 16 , 24 This suggests that the C‐terminal epitopes recognized by these nanobodies are highly accessible within the complex tissue environment of the AD brain, positioning them as versatile tools for both diagnostic imaging and therapy. In addition, the targeting of the C‐terminus (residues 36‐42) differentiates 2D10 from clinical mAbs like aducanumab and lecanemab, which target the N‐terminus. 25 , 26 Furthermore, C‐terminal targeting potentially avoids the “secondary nucleation” effects associated with N‐terminal antibodies, which can inadvertently catalyze the formation of neurotoxic oligomers.

A critical challenge in CNS drug development is the highly selective and restrictive nature of the BBB. Although the small size of nanobodies (∼12 to 15 kDa) is theoretically advantageous, their rapid renal clearance and short serum half‐life (typically <30 min) often limit brain bioavailability. 17 , 18 We observed that our basic nanobody, 2D10 (pI = 9.5), successfully crossed the BBB and engaged plaques following systemic administration, which is consistent with reports that a high isoelectric point facilitates spontaneous BBB penetration. 27 However, our efficacy study revealed a critical distinction where target engagement by the monovalent 2D10 did not translate into plaque reduction or altered microglia activation.

In our study, the primary difference between the 2D10 and 2D10‐Fc cohorts was the recruitment and activation of microglia (LGALS3+) around the plaques. This indicates that the clearance observed with 2D10‐Fc is driven by microglia‐mediated phagocytosis rather than a passive “peripheral sink” or simple neutralization effect. This observation is also in line with the very recent evidence that the efficacy of anti‐Aβ antibody lecanemab relies on the antibody's Fc fragment, which activates microglia to effectively clear amyloid plaques, whereas either Fc‐silenced lecanemab or microglia deficiency had this effect. 28

The necessity of the Fc domain for clearance presents a “double‐edged sword” in AD immunotherapy. The same FcγR‐mediated activation required for plaque removal is also the likely primary driver of ARIA, particularly when antibodies engage amyloid in the cerebral vasculature. Our findings suggest that future development should focus on tuning the Fc domain rather than eliminating it. For example, leveraging antibody transport vehicles, such as TfR‐binding platforms, could enhance brain delivery while allowing for the engineering of “effector‐silent” or “effector‐tuned” Fc domains that maximize parenchymal plaque clearance while minimizing vascular inflammation and ARIA risk. 13 However, the clinical translation of these engineered formats also requires a broader understanding of their impact on the disease's overall progression.

The principle established here, that Fc‐mediated recruitment is non‐redundant for clearing insoluble protein aggregates, likely extends to other proteinopathies, including those involving tau or α‐synuclein. Our work provides a generalized blueprint for the rational design of CNS biologics: utilizing the nanobody's small size and high specificity for epitope targeting, then layering an engineered Fc domain to provide the necessary “clearance machinery.”

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that the Fc domain is indispensable for amyloid clearance in vivo. By establishing a high‐affinity nanobody‐Fc platform, we provide a foundation for next‐generation modular immunotherapies that can be rationally engineered for superior BBB penetration, targeted effector function, and an improved safety profile for the treatment of AD.
