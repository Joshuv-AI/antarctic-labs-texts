Barriers and facilitators to implementing treatment for opioid use disorder in community hospitals
Authors: Riley Shearer, Hildi Hagedorn, Honora Englander, Tracy Siegler, Roxanne Kibben, Adetayo Fawole, Alisa Patten, Amy Fitzpatrick, JoAn Laes, Jasmine Fernando, Noa Appleton, Emily Oot, Hope Titus, Noa Krawczyk, Zoe Weinstein, Jennifer McNeely, Paulette Baukol, Udi Ghitza, Dave Gustafson, Gavin Bart, Angela Bazzi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction:Methadone and buprenorphine are effective treatment for opioid use disorder (OUD), yet they are vastly under-utilized across US hospitals. To inform a national trial assessing the effectiveness of implementation strategies to increase adoption of an inpatient hospital-based opioid treatment (HBOT) model (NCT04921787), we explored barriers and facilitators to expanding medication for opioid use disorder (MOUD) within community hospitals across the United States.Methods:From November 2021 to March 2022, we used purposeful and snowball sampling to identify and interview participants involved in inpatient care of patients with OUD from twelve community hospitals. We conducted semi-structured interviews on providers’ experiences and perspectives on current treatment approaches as well as potential influences on MOUD expansion in their hospitals. We used thematic analysis to identify key barriers and facilitators that could impact implementation of an HBOT model, and organized these findings based on the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR).Results:From qualitative interviews with 57 participants (30 physicians, 7 pharmacists, 6 nurses, and 14 professionals involved in the care of patients with OUD), we identified key barriers and facilitators mapped to CFIR’s internal and outer settings. The most salient inner setting domains included tension for change and relative priority, compatibility, available resources, organizational culture, access to knowledge and information, relational connections and communications, and information technology infrastructure. Outer setting domains included policies and laws, financing, and partnerships and connections.Conclusions:Identifying potential barriers and facilitators can inform hospital-specific strategies to support implementation of HBOT. Implementation strategies that address barriers such as staff availability, knowledge, and attitudes may support increased HBOT adoption. On a broader scale, national policy changes such as increased financing and public reporting of quality metrics would address other barriers we identified and may also encourage hospitals to adopt HBOT models.Keywords: Hospital addiction services, Consolidated framework for implementation research, Medication for opioid use disorder, Implementation science

Introduction

The U.S. overdose crisis continues to be driven by opioids, which were involved in over 84,000 of the 111,000 overdose deaths in 2022 (Ahmad et al., 2023). Medications for opioid use disorder (MOUD), including buprenorphine and methadone, are effective treatments for reducing mortality risk (Ma et al., 2019; Wakeman et al., 2020). However, only 18.3 % of people aged 12 and older with an opioid use disorder received MOUD in the past year (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, 2023). Inpatient hospital admissions among individuals with OUD are common (Gryczynski et al., 2016), and represent an opportunity to engage patients in MOUD treatment (Larochelle et al., 2019; Liebschutz et al., 2014). However, initiation of MOUD in hospital settings remains rare (Priest, Lovejoy, et al., 2020). To increase the proportion of patients engaged in OUD treatment, efforts are needed to improve identification of OUD and initiation of MOUD during inpatient hospitalizations (Williams et al., 2019).

Hospital-based opioid treatment (HBOT) models are one approach wherein generalists deliver OUD care, including MOUD initiation, as part of their standard practice (Englander et al., 2022). However, numerous barriers to the adoption of MOUD treatment in hospital settings have been identified. For example, hospital-based general medicine clinicians may lack knowledge and comfort initiating and managing MOUD treatment (Jakubowski et al., 2023). Additionally, regulatory restrictions on MOUD provision, lack of inpatient treatment resources (e.g., addiction medicine consultation services, social workers), and limited external infrastructure (e.g., community treatment options) have been cited as primary barriers to initiating MOUD in hospitals (Reed et al., 2022).

Targeted implementation strategies may support hospitals overcoming barriers to adopting an HBOT model. To inform a national trial assessing the effectiveness of high-compared to low-intensity implementation strategies to increase HBOT adoption (NCT04921787), (Bart et al., 2024) we used pre-implementation interviews to qualitatively explore barriers and facilitators to expanding MOUD in community hospitals across the United States. Using the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR), an implementation determinant framework (Damschroder et al., 2022), we describe barriers and facilitators both internal and external to hospital organizations. These findings can inform implementation strategies for increasing the adoption of MOUD treatment in a diverse array of hospitals in which individuals with OUD may present.

Materials and methods

Study design and sample

From November 2021 to March 2022, we conducted preimplementation qualitative interviews with key representatives from 12 hospitals. Overall hospital eligibility for the trial included not already having established addiction medicine services (e.g., an HBOT program or addition consultation service), being willing to support expansion of an HBOT program, and located in one of four states (Oregon, Minnesota, Massachusetts, and New York). Within participating hospitals, we used purposive sampling to identify individuals interested in supporting the HBOT implementation (Johnson, 1990), including potential HBOT “champions” referred from hospital leadership. We also used snowball sampling by asking enrolled participants to suggest other individuals representing a range of disciplines involved in the care of patients with OUD including medical and surgical providers (e.g., physicians, physician assistants (PAs), nurse practitioners (NPs)), nurses, social workers, case managers, peer recovery specialists, addiction counselors, and substance use navigators who might have insights into HBOT implementation barriers and facilitators (Biernacki & Waldorf, 1981). From each hospital we interviewed 3–7 participants. Participants provided verbal informed consent and were offered $50 compensation. The Advarra, Inc., institutional review board reviewed and approved the study protocol (Trial registration: NCT04921787).

Data collection

As part of the overall trial, pre-implementation interviews were designed to explore hospitals’ current approaches to treating patients with OUD (including whether MOUD was currently offered) and elicit participants’ perspectives on barriers and facilitators to implementing HBOT. To guide the development of open-ended questions in our semi-structured interview guide, we drew from selected CFIR inner setting constructs and expertise across our investigator team (Appendix A). The primary aim of the interviews was to understand hospital-specific barriers and facilitators to tailor the practice facilitation implementation strategies to each hospital. An experienced, lead qualitative investigator provided qualitative interviewing training and supervision to interviewers (graduate-level research staff) who conducted interviews with participants. Research staff conducted interviews virtually (e.g., via Zoom or Webex videoconferencing) or by phone, over approximately 45–60 min with audio-recording and professional transcription. Interviewers reviewed transcripts for accuracy and to confirm de-identification following a structured protocol (McLellan et al., 2003).

Data analysis

We employed a collaborative codebook development process involving the lead qualitative investigator, interviewers, and a graduate-level research assistant (DeCuir-Gunby et al., 2011; MacQueen et al., 1998). Drawing on key interview guide questions, notes on emergent topics from interviewers, and transcribed data, we generated a preliminary codebook with draft codes, sub-codes, and definitions. In addition to inner setting constructs, several findings related to CFIR’s outer setting constructs emerged in the interviews and were included in the preliminary codebook. Research team members independently tested these preliminary codes on a set of three transcript excerpts and then met to compare code application, discuss discrepancies, and modify the codebook for application to another set of transcripts. Through six additional rounds of this process, we continued making refinements until reaching consensus on a final codebook. Seven team members then used NVivo (QSR International Pty Ltd., release 1.7.1, 2022) to apply final codes independently to transcripts. The team met regularly throughout the coding process to resolve coding discrepancies (through a consensus approach), monitor progress, and discuss emergent findings and preliminary conclusions (Ryan & Bernard, 2003). We doubled coded 55 % of the transcripts and the lead analyst qualitatively assessed alignment of the team. Following coding, we used the framework method to systematically chart (i.e., summarize) coded data at the participant level, write higher-level memos interpreting data within each code, and extract exemplar quotes (Gale et al., 2013). In-depth, thematic analysis then involved synthesizing coded data and memos most relevant to key CFIR constructs (Corbin & Strauss, 2008; Patton, 2014). Findings are described in the sections below using illustrative, anonymized quotes.

Results

Sample characteristics and overview of qualitative findings

We interviewed a total of 30 physicians, seven pharmacists, seven social workers, six nurses, three advanced practice clinicians (nurse practitioners and physician assistants), three addiction counselors and substance use navigators, and one peer recovery specialist. Of the 57 total participants, 19 physicians, six pharmacists, five nurses, one advance practice clinician, and one addiction counselor were also responsible for administrative, managerial, or quality improvement work. Inner setting barriers and facilitators

Participants described many aspects of the hospital inpatient setting that they expected would impact attempts to expand MOUD prescribing. In addition to how OUD was currently treated in their inpatient setting, participants described staff knowledge and culture, communication practices, available resources, and competing priorities as all potentially impacting implementation of an HBOT model. We identified facilitators and barriers relating to the following CFIR constructs for inner setting characteristics explored in detail below: tension for change and relative priority, compatibility, available resources, organizational culture, access to knowledge and information, relational connections and communications, and information technology infrastructure (Damschroder et al., 2022) (Table 1).

Tension for change & relative priority

Participants expressed concerns about the negative impacts of the overdose crisis on their patients and surrounding communities, noting common beliefs among hospital staff that something needed to change to address patients’ OUD better. For example, a pharmacist explained: “The [staff] see the same people over and over for the same issue and would love to have something to offer them. I think they’re motivated to do that [initiate MOUD in the hospital].” A lack of standardized protocols for caring for patients with OUD, along with negative beliefs and actions among staff (see Section 3.2.4 Culture), frequently led to poor patient outcomes (e.g., patient-directed discharge) which in turn also contributed to feelings of burnout among staff. Furthermore, without established MOUD protocols or policies, treatment of patients’ OUD was often placed on “the back burner,” leading participants to worry that they were not adequately caring for hospitalized patients with OUD. Although most participants described a desire to initiate MOUD in the hospital, one pharmacist did not think they needed to change MOUD prescribing practices, highlighting how, in their hospital, “we’re really focused on the acute” and would rather defer MOUD initiation and management to outpatient providers.

Participants identified past quality improvement projects (e.g., reducing opioid analgesic and increasing naloxone prescribing) that signaled their hospitals’ ability to prioritize increased inpatient MOUD prescribing. However, competing priorities were also identified, including staffing shortages (see Section 3.2.3), patient length of stay, and readmission rates, and other quality metrics. As one participant explained, “We just haven’t prioritized this patient population. We’re driven by Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, by our measured metrics, and [MOUD] is not [one of] those things that drive up our reimbursement.”

Compatibility

Across hospitals, participants viewed HBOT as generally fitting within their existing workflows and practices which could facilitate broader adoption. Although MOUD treatment was rarely initiated in their inpatient settings, participants often described continuing patients’ existing MOUD during hospital stays, as a hospitalist reflected: “I have certainly seen their treatment continued, but never started.” Participants also had expertise with specific aspects of MOUD treatment such as using the Clinical Opioid Withdrawal Scale (COWS) to monitor patient symptoms. Other participants emphasized that providers routinely used medications with more complicated titration protocols (e.g., insulin) and higher risk profiles (e.g., full agonist opioid analgesics) and so they should be well equipped to learn MOUD protocols and manage the relatively minor risks of initiating it in the hospital. Pharmacists were identified as an important resource to support providers who were learning MOUD initiation protocols.

In addition to existing workflows, facilitators supporting HBOT adoption were recently implemented emergency department (ED)-based initiatives to increase MOUD initiation in some hospitals. Inpatient providers were more confident they could increase MOUD initiations after observing success in the ED. Participants were encouraged that they would be able to overcome the barriers and begin initiating MOUD in the inpatient setting after observing success in another area of their hospital. Furthermore, they described ED programs as providing “a good foundation” upon which to build more widespread adoption throughout the hospital. For example, they would be able to leverage recently established partnerships with community treatment providers to discharge patients recently initiated on MOUD.

Available resources

Participants identified staff shortages and increased workloads as the biggest barriers to expanding MOUD prescribing in their hospitals. Across job functions (e.g., physicians, nurses, and social workers), participants felt hospital staff were “being asked to do a great deal with less and less providers.” These sentiments likely contributed to staff burnout and created concerns for the feasibility of adopting an HBOT model in the absence of substantial support. As one physician worried: “It just goes onto a long list of things that are supposed to be done, but in reality, there’s way too much to [do and] there’s no way to do a hundred percent of what’s requested for every patient.”

To meaningfully increase MOUD prescribing, participants felt that providers needed incentives and dedicated time to learn new clinical skills. As one physician explained: “If you just ask [providers] to do something on their free time, it’s going to be a lot slower, and less percentage of uptake, than if we provide structured ways to do this within the scope of their jobs.”

Culture

Participants described a culture at their hospitals which included stigmatizing behavior and beliefs among some hospital staff as a barrier to adopting HBOT. One participant acknowledged that some patients’ behaviors were characterized as “drug seeking” (e.g., requesting opioid agonist pain medication) reinforcing providers’ reluctance to prescribe MOUD. Additionally, a nurse practitioner reflected that some coworkers felt that patients with OUD “did this to [themselves]” and that their treatment created “an unnecessary headache for the healthcare system.” Although these views were not commonly endorsed among our interview participants and were not described as universal among the staff of their hospitals, stigma frequently affected patient care. A physician explained, “There’s a tremendous amount of paranoia here...when people have OUD and they have pain, there’s the rolling of the eyes…and then people sign out AMA (against medical advice).” At some hospitals stigma contributed to a hospital culture where patients’ pain from withdrawal was not recognized or treated. Both untreated withdrawal and experiencing stigma from providers led patients to leave the hospital early before their medical treatment was completed, which often resulted in readmission. Additionally, some hospital staff described “a high level of burnout” among their peers. In addition to discomfort working with patients with OUD, the perceived lack of effective inpatient treatment options negatively impacted hospital culture.

Across hospitals, participants also described a culture of mistrust of patients with substance use disorders as a barrier to improving care of patients with OUD. For example, when asked, participants felt that hospital policies for involving hospital security or searching patients and rooms were typically unclear and inconsistently applied. Some participants did not know whether their hospitals had formal policies regarding room searches but noted that patients with a history of SUD were often subjected to such searches if staff had any suspicion of substance use in the hospital. Some participants recognized the stigmatizing nature of these searches, particularly when involving security, which could lead to patients leaving the hospital early. As one social worker described: “We are not super trauma informed in those situations, and it often ends up in a large security presence in their rooms, and often, the patients will leave after that.”

Access to knowledge & information

A barrier to broader HBOT adoption was limited experience with buprenorphine induction protocols and mitigating the risks of precipitated withdrawal among providers. Across hospitals, participants described a need for increased education and guidance on prescribing MOUD. Participants also shared clinical staff (physicians, nurses, pharmacists, etc.) did not have experience talking with patients about OUD and available treatment options.

Participants identified a range of existing educational opportunities which could be leveraged to facilitate increased HBOT adoption. Some described existing educational programs within their hospital systems (e.g., grand rounds) that could be used to spread information among the broader staff. Participants also called for increased mentorship and continued feedback, which they felt would increase MOUD prescribing.

Communications & relational connections

The degree of multidisciplinary communication and inter-role collaboration varied between hospitals. Some community hospitals relied on temporarily contracted physicians which was viewed as a barrier to HBOT adoption. Participants felt it would be more difficult to build relationships with and effect practice changes for these providers because they were not engaged in training initiatives, educational meetings, or hospital-wide efforts to adopt new care models. Another participant felt the variability between individual provider approaches to treating patients was a potential barrier to adopting HBOT: “There are approximately 50 of us [hospitalists], and everybody kind of handles things their own way.”

In the absence of a dedicated addiction consult service, which some participants felt could help facilitate coordination around OUD treatment, clinicians commonly relied on psychiatry and social work to help manage substance use treatment. One physician described how a dedicated specialist could help facilitate increased MOUD prescribing: “We need a specialist to reassure people. Over time, with experience, you’ll be like, ‘I’ve done this before; I can do it.’” Participants at teaching hospitals also identified medical residents as potentially facilitating increased MOUD prescribing as they tended to integrate new evidence into their practice. This could encourage attending physicians to adopt new practices, as a physician explained, “Residents are a huge tool. If they hear of something they love trying it, [and] now it’s on the attending to really dig deep and try that.”

Structural characteristics: IT infrastructure

Participants shared that information technology (IT) infrastructure, within the electronic health record, could facilitate HBOT adoption and support treatment of patients with OUD. For example, some participants already conducted chart reviews and consulted the prescription drug monitoring program to identify patients with OUD. Participants also felt that streamlined processes for ordering MOUD would help facilitate implementing HBOT. As a pharmacy clinical coordinator recommended, “Order sets [aggregated collection of clinical orders for a given scenario] would be very helpful. If it’s easy for the provider to prescribe it, they’re going to use it; if they have to figure it out on their own, it [won’t] get done.” Despite the potential benefits of existing IT, one participant described how their current system had limited data access which may be a barrier to monitoring and assessing progress expanding MOUD prescribing.

Outer setting barriers and facilitators

Participants also highlighted important external factors that might impact attempts to implement HBOT. Participants frequently discussed the broader substance use treatment landscape and their connections, or lack thereof, with community providers. They also described factors such as limited financing or restrictive laws, which may affect implementation. We identified facilitators and barriers relating to the following CFIR constructs for outer setting characteristics: policies and laws, financing, and partnerships and connections (Table 1).

Policies & laws

Although the requirement was removed at the end of 2022, a Drug Enforcement Agency DATA-Waiver (i.e., X-Waiver) was historically necessary to prescribe buprenorphine during the hospital discharge process when our interviews were conducted. Some participants described past efforts to get hospitalists waivered but noted that MOUD initiations had not substantially increased in the absence of broader practice changes. One potential barrier is non-waivered providers not continuing prescribing buprenorphine and/or linking patients to community treatment during the discharge process. As one social worker explained, “When that [waivered] provider is done and the new provider comes on, those conversations and monitoring of [buprenorphine] that [were] started may not be there. I’ve seen people started on [MOUD] with zero linkage to outpatient care.” In some community hospitals, a few waivered providers were responsible for seeing all patients prescribed MOUD during discharge, as a physician noted: “I come in frequently, do meetings, and write prescriptions for patients I’ve never met.” Existing expectations that only a few providers would manage all MOUD in the hospital was viewed as a potential barrier to hospital wide adoption of an HBOT model.

In addition to legal barriers to prescribing buprenorphine, confusion regarding who could provide methadone for OUD treatment may be a barrier to expanding treatment with MOUD. Across hospitals, some participants did not know that they could order and dose methadone during a hospitalization (e.g., one social worker said, “Of course we can’t do methadone!”). Other participants were unaware of who could manage methadone in the community, expressing some confusion about where to refer patients post-discharge, which increased hesitancy to offer it to inpatients.

Financing

Participants recognized that a lack of financial incentives for hospitals, and limited patient insurance coverage, could be barriers to expanding treatment initiation and retention. Hospitals lacked robust financing for addiction care and participants felt that hospital reimbursement incentives and penalties did not encourage inpatient MOUD initiation. While participants described hospital leadership as generally supportive of expanding OUD treatment within their hospitals, some expressed concerns that metrics with clearer financial implications would inevitably be prioritized. While some participants hypothesized that MOUD prescribing could be better framed as cost-saving (i.e., rather than revenue generating), others were skeptical that hospital administrators would agree because “cost savings [are] theoretical.” Ultimately, as one nurse practitioner summarized, hospital leadership was most interested in quantifiable metrics such as “length of stay, readmission rates, and throughput” that had direct “return on investment.” They continued, “I think that patient outcomes are important, but what I see paid most attention to is the other things.”

Partnerships & connections

Participants described their limited confidence in ensuring patients’ continued treatment post-discharge as one of the biggest barriers to initiating MOUD. Participants felt that community treatment options were inaccessible or required long wait times that would impede engagement. The perceived accessibility of continued buprenorphine or methadone treatment post-discharge varied considerably across communities and geographic regions. For example, in one state [Oregon], participants often encountered barriers linking patients to methadone (i. e., opioid treatment programs) and so preferred office-based buprenorphine treatment, whereas in other states [New York and Massachusetts] this was less of a barrier, as one participant noted methadone required only waiting “a couple of days, but Suboxone [buprenorphine] takes awfully long [7–10 days].” In some hospitals, case managers and social workers had extensive relationships with local treatment providers, but their knowledge was rarely institutionalized and processes for linking patients to these services were largely informal. Without close relationships and established referral processes with local MOUD options, some participants felt that referring patients to clinics that accepted walk in appointment could be an initial facilitator to HBOT adoption in the hospital.

Participants described a few models linking patients to community treatment as potential facilitators of expanding HBOT. Hospitals had strong existing relationships with community clinics that could continue discharged patients on buprenorphine, which supported MOUD initiation in hospitals. One hospital had a dedicated referral line linking hospital providers with outpatient treatment programs offering “inreach” services to their hospitalized patients. (Englander et al., 2022) Other participants noted that a subset of hospitalized patients could be seen by residents or faculty who also work part time in clinics where they could continue seeing and managing patients initiated on MOUD in the hospital.

Discussion

This qualitative study examined the barriers and facilitators to implementing a hospital based opioid treatment (HBOT) model in community hospitals. Drawing on perspectives from geographically dispersed professionals involved in inpatient care (e.g., physicians, pharmacists, nurses, social workers), we identified facilitators and barriers related to the CFIR constructs of inner and outer setting. Across sites, participants were motivated to improve the treatment of patients with OUD in their hospitals. They described the effects of the overdose crisis on their communities, negative attitudes and behavior from some staff members, and suboptimal care for patients with OUD as motivation to improve treatment in the hospital setting.

Despite motivation to improve treatment of OUD in the hospital, participants acknowledged barriers to implementing HBOT. Many participants reported a lack of knowledge as a key barrier at baseline. In line with previous research, participants also described challenges including negative views and behaviors towards patients with OUD (Bearnot et al., 2019; Hall et al., 2021; Hoover et al., 2022; Horner et al., 2019; Paroz et al., 2023), difficulty treating pain, and communicating with patients with OUD (Horner et al., 2019; Paroz et al., 2023; Reed et al., 2022). To increase widespread adoption of MOUD prescribing, hospital clinicians may need to develop experience and comfort explicitly discussing and addressing substance use. Participants also identified barriers such as competing priorities, staffing shortages, and increased workloads that could limit widespread implementation of HBOT. Despite recognizing MOUD’s efficacy and wanting to improve patient care, providers felt they would not have the time to learn new protocols and adopt MOUD prescribing unless it was prioritized over other responsibilities.

The availability of, and hospitals’ relationship with, community substance use treatment providers may also impact adoption of HBOT. Consistent with previous research, some participants were concerned that difficulty linking patients with ongoing treatment could be a barrier to adoption of MOUD prescribing in the inpatient setting (Blevins et al., 2018; Reed et al., 2022). Limited partnerships and connections between inpatient and outpatient providers may reduce hospital clinician adoption of MOUD prescribing because they are not confident patients will be able to continue treatment. Conversely, participants from some hospitals described existing partnerships with addiction clinics that they viewed as an important facilitator for further increasing MOUD prescribing in the hospital.

Participants also emphasized external barriers which may not be directly addressable by individuals or healthcare systems. For example, participants noted that existing financing structures did not incentivize hospital addiction care (Priest, Englander, & McCarty, 2020; Priest & McCarty, 2019). Increasing reimbursement for OUD treatment could incentivize hospital leadership to provide more resources and staff time to appropriately care for patients (Englander & Davis, 2022). Similarly, in this study participants felt hospital leadership were more concerned with length of stay or other quality metrics such as the rate of hospital acquired infections. Public reporting on hospitals’ OUD-specific quality metrics, such as the proportion of patients receiving MOUD, could encourage hospitals to focus on improving addiction care (Englander & Davis, 2022). Although participants reported personal motivation and a tension for change within their hospitals, without broader financing and policy changes it may be challenging to overcome barriers such as staffing shortages and competing priorities.

Implementation strategies to increase HBOT adoptions

Based on participant interviews, authors’ experience implementing addiction treatment in hospitals, and implementation science and substance use treatment literature we identified several implementation strategies to help hospitals leverage identified facilitators and overcome barriers to HBOT implementation. For example, targeted implementation approaches could help overcome the frequently cited barrier of lack of knowledge. In an implementation trial of Veterans Health Affairs clinics, external facilitation was associated with MOUD prescribing increased in facilities where a lack of knowledge was reported as a barrier pre-implementation (Gustavson et al., 2021). Participants in our study identified existing hospital-based educational opportunities such as grand rounds, which could be leveraged to disseminate knowledge on treatment with MOUD. In addition to formal didactic education, participants also felt that mentorship could support HBOT adoption. Clinical champions, individuals dedicated to spearheading an implementation and mentoring their peers, is one strategy to promote behavior change (Morena et al., 2022). Although the effectiveness of clinical champions has not been widely studied (Santos et al., 2022), they have been successful used in substance use treatment settings to overcome internal and external barriers (Wood et al., 2020). Additionally, evidence from a “train the trainer” implementation approach in Veterans Affairs Clinics suggests that peer led education and mentorship can increase MOUD prescribing (Hawkins et al., 2021).

In addition to a lack of knowledge, participants routinely stated that competing priorities and high workloads were a barrier to adopting HBOT. However, participants highlighted IT infrastructure in the electronic health record as a potential facilitator to support MOUD prescribing. For example, helping hospitals build order sets (collection of orders (e.g., medications, laboratory testing, clinical monitoring) commonly placed together for specific clinical scenarios) could increase provider adoption by reducing their workload and nudging them to prescribe MOUD as the default options. While their impact on MOUD prescribing remains unknown, order sets in the electronic health record have been shown to effect physician behavior and increase uptake of evidence based medicine in other fields of medicine (Last et al., 2021).

Limitations

This qualitative study has several limitations. First, as we recruited from community hospitals enrolled in an ongoing implementation trial in specific geographic locations, our findings may not be generalizable across the entire United States, to a wider range of hospitals or communities, or to institutions not yet contemplating improving addiction treatment that would have been ineligible for or uninterested in the trial. Second, we sampled hospital representatives likely to be personally involved in HBOT implementation efforts who may have higher levels of interest in and knowledge of MOUD prescribing; future studies should include a broader range of key informants, including individuals with expertise that is more limited to specific departments or those who view MOUD prescribing more negatively than our participants. Third, we did not verify participants’ descriptions of their hospitals’ approaches or formal policies or include any assessment of patient outcomes, which could shed additional light on some of the findings identified here. Future research should integrate quantitative measures of MOUD prescribing and patient outcomes to assess how implementation strategies impact adoption of an HBOT model. Additionally, hospital-level barriers and facilitators identified here should be considered in analysis as potential moderators impacting the effectiveness of implementation strategies.

Conclusion

Hospitalizations are an important opportunity to engage patients in substance use treatment, but MOUD initiation has not yet been widely adopted in hospital settings. In this qualitative study on community hospitals’ readiness to implement HBOT, participants identified multiple facilitators within the inner setting such as a tension for change, experiences in the emergency department, and existing information technology resources. Participants also identified inner and outer setting barriers including limited staff availability, lack of comfort working with patients with OUD, competing priorities, limited financing for hospital addiction care. In addition to hospital-specific implementation strategies, state and national policy changes such as easing regulations on MOUD, increasing reimbursement for OUD treatment and publicly reporting quality metrics may support increased adoption of hospital based opioid treatment.
