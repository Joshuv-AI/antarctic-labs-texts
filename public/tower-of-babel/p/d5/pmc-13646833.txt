Blood Eosinophil Count, Disease Severity, and Exacerbation Risk in Korean Patients with Bronchiectasis: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Sun-Hyung Kim, Dong Eun Kye, Geun-Hyeong Kim, Jiyoul Yang, Jun Yeun Cho, Ki Man Lee, Seung Park, Eun Jin Kwon, Kyung Sik Yi, Hyun Lee, Bumhee Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground Peripheral blood eosinophil counts have been associated with bronchiectasis outcomes, but findings remain inconsistent, and their role in acute exacerbations among Korean patients is unclear.Methods This study retrospectively evaluated 491 patients with bronchiectasis at a tertiary hospital in Korea between 2014 and 2021. Using a negative binomial regression model adjusted for follow-up duration, we evaluated the association between peripheral eosinophil counts and acute exacerbations in Korean patients with bronchiectasis.Results Among the 491 patients, 190 (38.7%) were categorized into the lower eosinophilic group, 221 (45.0%) into the normal eosinophilic group, and 80 (16.3%) into the higher eosinophilic group. No significant differences were observed across eosinophilic groups in terms of hospitalization within 1 year, bronchiectasis severity index scores, or forced expiratory volume in 1 second (F), age (A), presence of chronic colonization by Pseudomonas aeruginosa (C), radiological extension (number of pulmonary lobes affected) (E), dyspnea (D) (FACED) scores (all p>0.05). In the parsimonious negative binomial regression analysis with false discovery rate (FDR) correction, oral corticosteroid use (incidence rate ratio [IRR], 2.26; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.46 to 3.52; FDR p=0.001), older age (IRR, 1.03; 95% CI, 1.01 to 1.04; FDR p=0.002), and lower forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1) % predicted (IRR, 0.98; 95% CI, 0.97 to 0.99; FDR p<0.001) were identified as independent risk factors for acute exacerbations.Conclusion There was no significant association between peripheral blood eosinophil counts and either acute exacerbations or disease severity in Korean patients with bronchiectasis. In contrast, older age, lower lung function (FEV1 % predicted), and the use of oral corticosteroids were identified as independent risk factors for acute exacerbations. These findings underscore the importance of considering region-specific clinical and epidemiological factors in the management of bronchiectasis.Keywords: Bronchiectasis, Eosinophil Count, Acute Exacerbation, Risk Factors

Introduction

Bronchiectasis is a heterogeneous chronic airway disease, and identifying clinically relevant phenotypes may improve risk stratification and management [1,2]. Among emerging biomarkers, peripheral blood eosinophil count has recently attracted attention as a potential indicator of eosinophilic airway inflammation in bronchiectasis [1,3]. While neutrophilic inflammation has long been recognized as a major feature of bronchiectasis, the role of eosinophils in disease expression and outcomes remains less well defined [4,5].

However, the clinical relevance of eosinophils in bronchiectasis remains uncertain. Previous studies have reported inconsistent findings regarding the association between peripheral blood eosinophil count and bronchiectasis outcomes. A European multicohort study found that higher eosinophil counts were associated with a shorter time to exacerbation [6], whereas a Spanish registry study reported a U-shaped relationship between eosinophil count and the risk of exacerbation or disease severity [3]. In addition, an analysis from the European Bronchiectasis Registry suggested that elevated blood eosinophils may identify a subgroup of patients with reduced exacerbation frequency during inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) treatment [7]. A prospective cohort study also reported associations between eosinophilic bronchiectasis, more frequent exacerbations, and greater disease severity [8]. These findings suggest that eosinophil-based phenotyping may be clinically relevant, but its prognostic and therapeutic implications in bronchiectasis remain incompletely understood.

This question may be particularly important in Asian populations, where the etiologic and clinical profiles of bronchiectasis may differ from those observed in Western cohorts [9,10]. In Korea, post-infectious bronchiectasis, including tuberculosis-related structural lung disease, remains relatively common, and chronic airway infection may contribute substantially to disease burden and exacerbation risk [11]. Such differences raise the possibility that the clinical implications of peripheral eosinophil count may not be directly generalizable across populations. Nevertheless, data regarding the relevance of peripheral eosinophil count in Asian patients with bronchiectasis remain limited.

Therefore, this study aimed to assess the clinical relevance of peripheral blood eosinophil count in Korean patients with bronchiectasis by examining its association with disease severity, exacerbation frequency, and hospitalization. We also explored other clinical factors associated with future exacerbations in this cohort.

Materials and Methods

1. Study population

We retrospectively reviewed the medical records of patients who had visited the Department of Pulmonary and Critical Care Medicine at Chungbuk National University Hospital, a 793-bed tertiary referral university hospital in Cheongju, Republic of Korea, between January 2014 and August 2021, and had an International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) diagnosis code of J47. Bronchiectasis was confirmed by chest computed tomography (CT) review before final inclusion. Because our institution is a tertiary referral center, the study population consisted of patients who were clinically evaluated or treated for bronchiectasis in routine practice. Coexisting airway diseases such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) or asthma were not considered exclusion criteria if bronchiectasis was confirmed on chest CT and the patient was clinically managed for bronchiectasis. Among 1,369 screened patients, those who did not undergo pulmonary function testing (n=863) or blood eosinophil measurement (n=15) were excluded. A total of 491 patients were included in the final analysis. We classified the patients with bronchiectasis into three groups according to their eosinophil counts (Figure 1). Additionally, higher eosinophilic patients were classified into two groups based on the use of corticosteroids (ICS or oral corticosteroids) or not: those using corticosteroids (n=24) and those not using corticosteroids (n=56).

Flow chart of the study population.

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chungbuk National University Hospital (IRB No. 2024-09-010). Patient data were anonymized and de-identified before analysis. As a result, the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the study's retrospective design.

2. Clinical data

Baseline demographic characteristics included age, sex, body mass index (BMI, weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared), and smoking status (never, current-, or ex-smoker). Dyspnea was evaluated using the modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) scale [12,13]. The bronchiectasis severity index (BSI) and the forced expiratory volume in 1 second (F), age (A), presence of chronic colonization by Pseudomonas aeruginosa (C), radiological extension (number of pulmonary lobes affected) (E), dyspnea (D) (FACED) score were used to assess bronchiectasis severity, as previously described [13,14]. The radiological severity of bronchiectasis was measured using the modified Reiff score [13]. Chest CT was used to evaluate the radiological extent of bronchiectasis in all participants. The number of lobes involved (the lingula was considered to be a separate lobe) and degree of dilatation (tubular: 1, varicose: 2, and cystic: 3) were determined by a radiologist (Kyung Sik Yi). Acute exacerbation of bronchiectasis was defined as the worsening of three or more major symptoms lasting ≥48 hours, resulting in a change in treatment, and included cases where the patient visited the emergency room or was hospitalized due to worsening symptoms. The major symptoms include cough, sputum volume/viscosity, sputum suppuration, dyspnea, exercise ability, fatigue, malaise, and hemoptysis [15]. “Previous acute exacerbations” were defined as events occurring within the 12 months prior to baseline, whereas “acute exacerbations” referred to events occurring during the follow-up period after baseline.

3. Laboratory tests

White blood cell (WBC) count, neutrophil count, and eosinophil count were measured during clinically stable conditions. For patients who had been hospitalized because of acute exacerbation, laboratory values obtained during hospitalization were not used; instead, values measured after discharge during a stable state were used. Absolute neutrophil count was calculated by multiplying the WBC count by the neutrophil percentage, and absolute eosinophil count was calculated by multiplying the WBC count by the eosinophil percentage. Based on laboratory eosinophil counts, patients were classified into the lower eosinophil group (≤100 cells/μL), the normal eosinophil group (>100 to ≤300 cells/μL), or the higher eosinophil group (>300 cells/μL). The categorization was based on cutoffs used in previous studies to ensure clinical relevance and comparability with previous European and airway disease research [3,6,16-18].

4. Pulmonary function tests

Pre-bronchodilator and post-bronchodilator spirometry were performed according to American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society criteria [19]. The absolute values of forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1) and forced vital capacity (FVC) were recorded, and the percentage of predicted values for FEV1 and FVC were calculated using an automatic calculator with a reference equation obtained from a representative Korean sample [20].

5. Microbiology

Spontaneous sputum or lower respiratory tract specimens (bronchoalveolar lavage) were obtained from all patients whenever possible. The specimens were subjected to microbiological analyses according to standard methods [21]. Conventional semi-qualitative bacterial cultures were performed. All samples underwent initial Gram staining prior to sputum culture, if the Murray and Washington criteria were met [22]. Nontuberculous mycobacterial-pulmonary disease was diagnosed using the clinical and microbiological criteria provided by the American Thoracic Society and Infectious Diseases Society of America [23].

6. Statistical analysis

Data were presented as the median and interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables and frequency (percentage) for categorical variables. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Depending on the distribution, comparisons between two groups were performed using either the Student’s t-test (for normally distributed variables) or the Mann–Whitney U test (for non-normally distributed variables). For comparisons among the three eosinophilic groups, p-values were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or Welch’s ANOVA was used as appropriate, depending on the assumption of equal variances. To identify factors influencing the frequency of acute exacerbations, a negative binomial regression model was fitted with the log-transformed follow-up duration as an offset variable. Variables included in the parsimonious model were adjusted for age, BMI, history of tuberculosis, P. aeruginosa colonization, FEV1 % predicted, eosinophil group, and oral corticosteroid use. To account for multiple comparisons, p-values in the multivariable model were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction. All tests were two-sided, with p-values <0.05 considered to be statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using the R statistical software version 4.1.2 (R Core Team 2021, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

1. Baseline characteristics

Baseline characteristics of the 491 patients with bronchiectasis are shown in Table 1. Among them, 190 patients (38.7%) were categorized into the lower eosinophilic group, 221 (45.0%) into the normal eosinophilic group, and 80 (16.3%) into the higher eosinophilic group. The lower eosinophilic group had the highest proportion of never-smokers (66.8%, p=0.023), the lowest absolute values of FVC (2.4 L [IQR, 1.9 to 3.0], p=0.015) and FEV1 (1.5 L [IQR, 1.1 to 2.0], p=0.016), the highest neutrophil count (4,920/μL [IQR, 3,245 to 7,114], p=0.002), the highest proportion of patients using oral corticosteroids (11.1%, p=0.043) among the three groups. There were no significant between-group differences in sex, age, BMI, mMRC score, FACED score, BSI score, the number of patients with acute exacerbation, number of acute exacerbations per year, modified Reiff score, percentage of predicted values for FEV1 and FVC, FEV1/FVC ratio, microbiological findings, WBC count, comorbidities, inhaler use, macrolide use, or follow-up duration (all p>0.05).

2. Comparison of steroid use in the high eosinophil group

Among the 80 patients in the high eosinophilic group, 24 used corticosteroids, whereas 56 did not (Table 2). Compared with the non-steroid group, steroid users had a significantly higher mMRC dyspnea score (median, 1.5 [IQR, 1 to 2] vs. 1.0 [IQR, 0 to 2], p=0.016), and significantly lower median FEV1 (1.3 L vs. 2.0 L, p=0.013), percent predicted FEV1 (59% vs. 77%, p=0.005), and FEV1/FVC ratio (61% vs. 73%, p=0.002). Steroid users also had higher rates of COPD (75.0% vs. 42.9%, p=0.009), asthma (66.7% vs. 5.4%, p=0.001), and long-acting β2-agonist inhaler use (95.8% vs. 23.2%, p=0.001). No significant differences were found in other baseline characteristics, radiologic scores, microbiological findings, or follow-up duration (all p>0.05).

5. Risk factors for acute exacerbation

Negative binomial regression analysis with FDR correction (Figure 2) identified oral corticosteroid use (incidence rate ratio [IRR], 2.26; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.46 to 3.52; FDR p=0.001), older age (IRR, 1.03; 95% CI, 1.01 to 1.04; FDR p=0.002), and lower FEV1 % predicted (IRR, 0.98; 95% CI, 0.97 to 0.99; FDR p<0.001) as independent risk factors for acute exacerbations. Eosinophil count, history of tuberculosis, and P. aeruginosa colonization were not significantly associated with exacerbation risk.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is the first to evaluate the clinical relevance of peripheral blood eosinophil counts in Korean patients with bronchiectasis. In this retrospective cohort of 491 patients, the eosinophil count was not significantly associated with major clinical outcomes, including disease severity, exacerbation frequency, and hospitalization. In contrast, older age, lower lung function (FEV1 % predicted), and oral corticosteroid use were independently associated with exacerbation frequency.

Previous studies from European cohorts have suggested a U-shaped relationship between eosinophil count and bronchiectasis severity and have shown that ICS may reduce exacerbations in eosinophilic patients [3,7]. However, our findings diverge from these results. We found no evidence that eosinophil counts—either high or low—correlated with worse clinical outcomes in our Korean population. One possible explanation is the distinctive epidemiologic and clinical features of bronchiectasis in Korea, where post-infectious etiologies, such as sequelae from pulmonary tuberculosis, are more prevalent than in Western countries [9,24]. In addition, patterns of airway microbiota, patient immune profiles, and responses to pharmacologic interventions may differ significantly by ethnicity and healthcare context [24]. These findings underscore the importance of region-specific phenotyping, as factors such as prior tuberculosis infection and local microbial colonization patterns are particularly relevant in the Korean context [9,24,25]. These factors may influence not only the baseline eosinophil distribution but also its functional role in the airway immune environment. In European populations, the use of ICS in patients with eosinophilic bronchiectasis has been reported to reduce the number of acute exacerbations and the severity of the disease [3,26,27].

However, the results of this study showed that steroid use in Korean patients with eosinophilic bronchiectasis did not significantly protect against acute exacerbations. This finding aligns with the previously discussed characteristics of bronchiectasis in the Korean population.

Our study demonstrated that, in Korean patients with bronchiectasis, older age, lower FEV1 % predicted, and the use of oral corticosteroids were significant risk factors for the frequency of acute exacerbations. Although Korea has a relatively high prevalence of tuberculosis and post-infectious etiologies are a leading cause of bronchiectasis, our rigorous multivariable analysis, which adjusted for follow-up duration and multiple testing, revealed that historical infection itself (e.g., previous tuberculosis) and P. aeruginosa colonization lost their independent statistical significance. Instead, lower FEV1 % predicted emerged as a highly significant predictor. Chronic inflammation caused by tuberculosis results in structural damage to the bronchi, which directly manifests as a decline in FEV1. Therefore, objective structural and functional impairment (FEV1) and biological aging appear to be more direct and proximal determinants of exacerbation risk than the initial infectious etiology. This epidemiologic pattern represents a distinct pathophysiologic profile that differs from the mechanisms predominantly observed in Western cohorts.

Persistent airway colonization by pathogenic organisms like P. aeruginosa is a well-established factor in disease progression. However, in our cohort, its impact on exacerbation frequency was less significant than the severity of baseline airflow obstruction (FEV1) and age. Importantly, our study also found that the use of oral corticosteroids was associated with an increased risk of acute exacerbations. As potent immunosuppressive agents, systemic corticosteroids may impair host defense mechanisms, facilitate microbial colonization, or trigger dysregulated immune responses within the airways, potentially leading to worse clinical outcomes [28]. Notably, unlike prior studies that focused on ICS, our findings specifically implicate oral corticosteroids. This suggests a potential difference in pharmacologic effect or susceptibility among Korean patients and highlights the need to consider the unique immunopathological characteristics of bronchiectasis in this population when developing treatment strategies.

Our study has several limitations. First, although previous studies on bronchiectasis have included about 1,000 patients, our sample size of 491 may not have been sufficient to fully represent the broader population of Korean patients with bronchiectasis [3,6]. Second, this study was conducted at a single tertiary referral center, which may have introduce selection bias, as patients with more severe disease or complex comorbidities are more likely to be referred to such institutions. As a result, the findings may not be fully generalizable to patients in primary or community care settings. Third, due to the retrospective nature of the study, certain variables—such as symptom fluctuation, medication adherence, and detailed environmental exposures— could not be systematically assessed. Additionally, the temporal relationship between corticosteroid use and exacerbation events could not be clearly established, limiting our ability to draw causal inferences. Despite these limitations, our study remains meaningful in that it is the first to explore risk factors associated with acute exacerbations of bronchiectasis in a well-characterized Korean cohort. Given the known heterogeneity of bronchiectasis across populations, our findings underscore the need for region-specific evidence to guide clinical decision-making and future research. Fourth, because overlap of bronchiectasis with COPD or asthma was common in our cohort, underlying heterogeneity in airway inflammation may have influenced the observed relationship between eosinophil counts and exacerbations. However, subgroup analyses stratified by overlap phenotype were limited by the relatively small sample size within each subgroup, particularly in the high eosinophil group, making robust inference difficult. Future studies with larger cohorts are needed to clarify whether the clinical role of eosinophils differs according to overlap phenotype.

In conclusion, this study found no significant association between peripheral blood eosinophil counts and acute exacerbations or disease severity in Korean patients with bronchiectasis. Furthermore, the use of corticosteroids, including oral formulations, did not confer a protective benefit in patients with elevated eosinophil levels. Instead, older age, lower lung function (FEV1 % predicted), and the use of oral corticosteroids were identified as independent risk factors for acute exacerbations. These findings underscore the importance of considering region-specific clinical and epidemiological factors when managing bronchiectasis, particularly in Asian populations. Further large-scale, prospective studies are warranted to validate these results and to inform personalized treatment strategies tailored to the unique characteristics of Korean patients with bronchiectasis.
