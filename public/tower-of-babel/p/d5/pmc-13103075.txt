Smart technologies for enhancing greenhouse production management under climate and economic challenges
Authors: Ali Akbar Barati, Mohammadali Zandi Nezami, Hossein Shabanali Fami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe increase in climate fluctuations and their effects has led farmers to favor controlled environments, including greenhouses. However, neglecting good management practices can not only result in the waste of production resources but also increase production costs and their negative impacts. Using smart technologies can accelerate good greenhouse management practices (GGMP). The main objective of the present study was to examine how the use of smart technologies affects GGMP, with emphasis on climatic and economic factors. A composite index (GGMP Index) was developed to evaluate GGMP, and path analysis was used for data analysis. The sample consisted of 141 agricultural greenhouse units. Based on the findings of this study, the GGMP index dropped from 7.2 to 4.5 (out of 10) from the pre-production to the post-production stages. The study revealed that currently, only about 30% of greenhouses have a satisfactory level of good management. This is concerning, as using smart technologies can optimize agricultural operations for food production in greenhouses and highlights the impact of variables such as climatic and economic conditions, regulations and standards, and the technical characteristics of greenhouses, especially in regions with harsh or unpredictable climatic conditions such as extreme temperatures, droughts, or excessive rainfall.Keywords: Climate-smart agriculture, Smart farming, Good agricultural practices, Crop product managementSubject terms: Climate sciences, Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences

Introduction

Today, climate change and its resulting impacts, primarily caused by industrialization and urbanization1, have led to extensive human interventions in natural cycles. Over the past decades, the unsustainable and excessive exploitation of natural resources and non-renewable energy sources to meet the increasing demands driven by population growth and the rising need for food, goods, and services, has become one of humanity’s fundamental challenges2,3. For example, the world’s population will reach around 10 billion by the year 2050, requiring approximately 56% more food production than the total food produced in 2010 to meet the food needs of this population. Climate change refers to long-term changes in temperature, precipitation patterns, and other atmospheric conditions4,5 lead to extreme weather events, floods, droughts, and wildfires6. This issue poses a threat not only to human life but also to the entire planet. One of the most significant effects of these changes has been on the agricultural sector7–9 including crop and livestock production. For instance, rising temperatures, altered precipitation patterns, increased frequency of extreme weather events, rising CO2 emission, shifts in growing seasons, soil degradation10,11, water crisis12, etc. are some key climate changes that could affect agriculture13 through posh stress on crops, reducing yields and productivity, reduce soil moisture or wash away soil nutrients, expand the range and lifecycle of pests and diseases, higher pesticide uses and associated environmental risks, etc. Of course, it should not be overlooked that agriculture and climate conditions have a two-way relationship10,14,15. This means that both influence each other and are affected by one another. Therefore, the sustainable management of each can lead to the sustainability of the other. Given agriculture’s crucial role in ensuring food security16, climate change has become a serious threat to this security. As a response, humanity has turned to innovative agricultural methods17, such as producing crops in controlled environments like greenhouses, which are less affected by climatic parameters. Consequently, in recent years, the area under greenhouse cultivation has been expanding in Iran18 and globally19. In Iran, the cultivated area of greenhouse crops doubled between 2002 and 2012, increasing from 3380 ha to 6630 ha20. By the end of 2019, this area had expanded further to 15,678 ha. Additionally, due to the country’s dry and semi-arid climate, along with the benefits of greenhouse farming, the construction of approximately 50,000 hectares of greenhouses is included in Iran’s Ministry of Agriculture 2025 development plan18. Therefore, it seems that the expansion of greenhouse cultivation, both globally and in Iran, will continue and accelerate in the coming years. Although the development of greenhouse farming can help achieve food security and reduce the risks associated with agricultural production, poor and traditional management of greenhouses can lead to harmful environmental impacts, which in turn may cause economic and social consequences. Therefore, improving the traditional greenhouse management system and updating the technologies used in them is a necessity. In this context, alongside the development of smart technologies such as the Internet of Things (IoT), sensors, drones, and others, greenhouse operators have begun adopting these technologies to optimize management and increase production efficiency. However, critical questions arise: Can the use of smart technologies truly lead to better greenhouse management? Furthermore, what factors influence the implementation of smart technologies in greenhouses? This study aims to address these two key questions.

Conceptualization of the research model and hypotheses

Conceptualization of the research model

It should be noted that in this study, concept of optimal greenhouse management refers to performing all necessary operations (Good Greenhouse Management Practices or GGMP) that have been identified as essential by experts in order to achieve better performance. In general, the optimal management of greenhouses is influenced by various factors. Some of these factors are external to the units, while others are internal. External factors, although beyond the control of the greenhouse managers, can still affect their managerial performance21. The most significant of these include climatic conditions and changes20,22,23, economic parameters24,25(especially macroeconomics), and the presence of standards and regulations26,27. Other factors, although internal (such as the technical characteristics of the greenhouses or their microeconomic conditions), are themselves heavily influenced by external or environmental variables and parameters28.

For example, climate changes in recent decades have led to the expansion of cultivation in controlled environments19,26. As a result, depending on the extent of these changes and their impact on agricultural production, different methods and technologies are required. Therefore, climate change can not only directly affect the management of these units but also indirectly influence greenhouse management by determining the technical characteristics and types of technologies needed to manage these effects. Moreover, the level and type of technologies used in agricultural greenhouses29,30, as an internal variable, are themselves influenced by the economic conditions of these units and the broader macroeconomic environment. The economic conditions of society, along with the development of various smart technologies, can lead to the formulation of laws and regulations that directly and indirectly affect the management of greenhouse units.

Additionally, the technical characteristics of greenhouses are influenced not only by climatic conditions but also by existing laws, standards, and regulations26. Based on this, the model presented in Fig. 1 was developed as the conceptual framework for the study. More variables could have been added to this model, but to avoid excessive complexity, the focus of this study at this stage was limited to examining the impact of climatic variables, economic conditions, technical characteristics, ‘laws, regulations, and standards,’ and, finally, smart technologies on optimizing greenhouse management. However, in future studies, other variables, such as individual characteristics or cultural and social factors20, can also be examined.

Hypotheses

By clarifying the relationships of the variables in the model, the hypotheses of the research were developed as follows:

H1. Climatic conditions and their changes affect the optimization of greenhouse management (GGMP) both directly and indirectly.

H2. Economic variables influence the optimization of greenhouse management by impacting the use of smart technologies as well as the establishment of various standards and regulations.

H3. The use of smart technologies in greenhouses affects the optimization of greenhouse management (GGMP) both directly and indirectly.

H4. The establishment of various regulations and standards impacts the optimization of greenhouse management both (GGMP) directly and indirectly.

H5. The technical features of greenhouses, which are influenced by regulations and standards in this field as well as climatic conditions, affect the optimization of greenhouse management (GGMP).

Materials and methods

The study area

This study was an applied, non-experimental research employing field methods to collect the necessary data. The study area was Tehran province located in the northern part of Iran (Fig. 2) and lies at the center of the country and shares borders with provinces such as Alborz, Markazi, Qom, Semnan, and Mazandaran. According to the latest official census of Iran (2016), the population of Tehran province was more than 13 million people. Tehran province has a diverse climate due to its varied geography. The overall climate can be classified as dry to semi-dry, with the province facing low annual rainfall and significant temperature variations between day and night. This area has around 187,000 hectares of agricultural land. This includes both irrigated and rain-fed fields, with a portion of this area dedicated to greenhouse farming, which is increasingly popular due to the province’s climate. The main agricultural products of the province include vegetables, ornamental plants and flowers, and fruits (including pomegranates, grapes, and apples). Greenhouse farming plays a significant role in agricultural production due to its ability to mitigate harsh climatic conditions.

Location of the study area (This map was created by the authors using ARC-GIS software by ARC-Map version 10.5.).

The population targeted for this research included all active greenhouses in Tehran province, totaling 2,574 units. According to statistics published by the Ministry of Agriculture Jihad in 2023, greenhouses in Iran cover approximately 24,000 hectares, with Tehran province accounting for the majority (around 5,000 hectares). Currently, these greenhouses annually produce over one million tons of green vegetables and 93 million stems of flowers and ornamental plants, positioning Tehran as one of Iran’s leading provinces in greenhouse area and production. The crops typically cultivated in these greenhouses include tomatoes, cucumbers, bell peppers, lettuce, leafy greens, herbs, strawberries, melons, flowers, and ornamental plants due to the controlled environment they provide.

The variables were chosen to represent the key external and internal factors influencing greenhouse management performance. a) Climatic Conditions (CC): This construct was included to account for the direct and indirect pressure that environmental factors exert on agricultural production. It measures the extent to which managers consider local climate (e.g., temperature extremes, rainfall patterns) when making critical decisions about greenhouse design, equipment selection, and crop choice. This variable is crucial because climate variability is a primary driver for adopting controlled-environment agriculture.

b) Economic Variables (EV): This construct captures the macroeconomic and microeconomic context in which greenhouses operate. EV was included because financial constraints and incentives fundamentally influence a manager’s capacity to invest in technology and adhere to standards, thereby affecting overall management quality.

c) Technical Features (TF): This internal construct evaluates the suitability and efficiency of the greenhouse’s physical infrastructure and systems (e.g., structural design, water/energy efficiency). It was included as a mediating variable to understand how external factors translate into the physical capabilities of the production unit.

d) Standards and Regulations (SR): This construct measures adherence to formal rules and best practices in greenhouse construction and production. SR was included because regulatory frameworks are key mechanisms for promoting sustainable and efficient management practices, and their implementation is often tied to economic capacity.

e) Smart Technology (ST): This construct quantifies the adoption level of technologies such as sensors, automation, and control systems. ST is positioned as a central solution for optimizing management in the model, as it directly enables precision control over the production environment. Its adoption is hypothesized to be driven by economic capacity and climatic challenges.

f) Optimization of Greenhouse Management (OGM): OGM is the outcome variable, representing the performance of Good Greenhouse Management Practices (GGMP). It is a composite index derived from practices across pre-production, production, and post-production stages. This holistic measure was chosen to provide a comprehensive assessment of management quality. In this study the term of optimization refers to the improvement of management practices, which is operationalized as the latent outcome variable (OGM).

Sampling and data gathering

This study employed path analysis using the process algorithm in SmartPLS software v.4 to estimate the proposed conceptual model. It was because SmartPLS can include the variables of a path model as single-item constructs. Cohen’s method was used for estimating the sample size. Given the number of observed variables (21) and latent variables (6) in the model (Fig. 3), along with an anticipated effect size of 0.1, a desired probability level of 0.05, and statistical power set at 0.8, which is appropriate for social science32, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 136, but in this study, 141 greenhouse managers were interviewed, ensuring adequate data for robust analysis. The research tools consisted of a researcher-designed questionnaire, and data were collected through face-to-face interviews during the summer of 2022.

Data analysis

For data analysis, this study employed a path analysis method by SmartPLS due to the exploratory nature of the proposed model and the sample size limitations33. The PLS-SEM algorithm employs an iterative procedure that minimizes the residual variances of the endogenous constructs. For the proposed model, the estimation process converged rapidly, achieving the stopping criterion (a minimum change in the outer weights of 10⁻⁷) within a few iterations, which indicates stable and reliable model estimation. The final path coefficients (Fig. 4) represent the outcome of this optimization process. It should be noted that the PLS-SEM algorithm iteratively finds an optimal solution by minimizing the residual variances of the endogenous constructs.

Direct path coefficients and P values (enclosed in parentheses) of the model.

In addition, SmartPLS can include the variables of a path model as single-item constructs, and for variables with multiple indicators, identical weights are assigned to obtain the construct scores. In principle, only the structural relationships between the constructs are modeled. This type of model is often employed when one or more variables mediate the relationship between two other variables34. Therefore, this method provided the best fit for the proposed model in this research (Fig. 3). The objective of this model is to predict and explain the variance in the management optimization construct, rather than to present a classical numerical optimization algorithm for resource allocation.

The following steps and processes were undertaken to analyze the model:

1. Transforming the conceptual model into a measurement (outer) model and structural (inner) model: This required identifying latent variables (constructs), observed variables (indicators), and the relationships between them (Fig. 3). In this regard, at first, relevant indicators (observed variables) for the six constructs (latent variables) of the conceptual model (Fig. 1) were identified based on a literature review and semi-structured interviews with subject matter experts. The measurement model included 21 observed variables (Fig. 3; Table 1). However, some of these observed variables, such as climatic conditions and economic conditions, were themselves derived from the average of several other observed variables (Table 1) (total 48 observed variables).

2. Data collection tools and protocol: This study utilized a questionnaire consisting of three main sections: (a) Individual characteristics of the greenhouse manager and technical features of the greenhouses, (b) Optimal greenhouse management indicators at the pre-, during, and post-production stages (comprising a total of 23 indicators), and (c) The status of variables affecting the optimization of greenhouse management. Some of these variables were measured using a 5-point Likert scale, while others were based on ratio or interval scales. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews during the summer of 2022. The interviews were conducted on-site at the greenhouse units whenever possible, allowing for visual verification of some technical features. The average duration of each interview was approximately 45 min.

3. Data preprocessing and cleaning: Following data collection, a multi-step preprocessing procedure was implemented to ensure data quality and readiness for analysis: (a) Data entry and verification: The collected data were entered into a digital spreadsheet and were cross-checked against the original questionnaires by two separate individuals to minimize data entry errors; (b) Handling of missing values: The dataset was screened for missing values and cases with missing values were excluded from the final analysis; (c) Data screening: The data were screened to assess the normality of the distribution for the indicators using skewness and kurtosis statistics. However, since PLS-SEM does not require strict normality assumptions and is robust to deviations, the data were deemed suitable for the chosen analysis method.

4. Model estimation: This stage involves creating a database and estimating the model. After reviewing the collected data to ensure accuracy and correct any potential errors, the proposed model was implemented and estimated.

5. Evaluating the model fit: At this stage, the inner and outer model fit indicators are investigated and evaluated. If the indices meet the required adequacy threshold, the results can then be interpreted.

6. Presenting and interpreting the results and conclusion: It involves examining the research hypotheses or addressing the research questions based on the outputs of the final model. The results are then analyzed to derive conclusions, offering insights and recommendations related to the study’s objectives.

Model validation

The validation of any structural models must be evaluated before interpreting the results. Unlike structural equation models, where validation includes an assessment of both the measurement and structural models33, the validation of a path analysis model, similar to regression models, focuses on (a) assessing the model fit using R-squared, f-squared, and P-value (T-statistic) as quality criteria, and (b) evaluating the statistical significance of direct and indirect paths based on path coefficients and P-values (T-statistic) for the hypothesized paths in the structural model. It’s important to note that in this approach (path analysis in PROCESS algorithm in SmartPLS), the weights of the observed variables for each construct are considered equal35.

Regarding the quality criteria (Table 2), since the R-squared values for most of the independent constructs exceed 0.3, one construct shows a value greater than 0.1, and all f-squared values are greater than zero, indicating that the model is valid. Additionally, the P-values are below 0.05, confirming the statistical significance of the model. Furthermore, since the P-values for all path coefficients are below 0.05, all hypothesized paths are significant, confirming that the fitted model is valid and interpretable (Fig. 4).

Results and discussions

This section presents the study’s findings and discusses their implications in the context of existing literature. The results not only quantify the status of GGMP but also elucidate the complex relationships between external drivers, technological adoption, and management outcomes.

Descriptive results

The results regarding the age of the respondents (greenhouse managers) showed an average age of approximately 38 years (with a standard deviation of 10.4 years), with the youngest being 20 and the oldest 62. Regarding education, only about 40% had academic qualifications. In terms of agricultural and greenhouse work experience, the average agricultural experience was 14.4 years (with a standard deviation of 12.6 years), while the average work experience in the greenhouse was 12.3 years (with a standard deviation of about 9 years).

In terms of the general characteristics of the studied greenhouses, the results showed that the average greenhouse area was 5,668 square meters, with the largest being 38,000 square meters and the smallest 300 square meters. Ownership of 91.5% of the greenhouses was private, while the remainder were rented or state-owned. The average number of permanent workers was 3.8, and the average number of seasonal workers was 4.2. Over 53% of the greenhouses did not provide any training to workers.

Regarding structural characteristics, more than 71% of the greenhouses featured a multi-span curved design with relatively high ceilings, while the others were single-span tunnels or domed structures. Approximately 92% had galvanized frames, while the rest had wooden frames. About 90% of the greenhouses used a single-layer plastic covering. In terms of heating, 66% of the greenhouses used heaters, while 27% used heating stoves. For cooling, over 85% employed mechanical systems such as fans and pads. Around 65% of the greenhouses lacked automation, and 25% had low to moderate levels of automation. There were no fully mechanized greenhouses.

Regarding crop types, the main products cultivated were cucumbers, peppers, cut flowers, ornamental plants, and potted plants, using seeds, seedlings, cuttings, and saplings. Soil-based cultivation was practiced by 77% of greenhouse owners, while 23% used hydroponics. Over 75% of the greenhouses relied exclusively on chemical methods for pest and disease control, while the rest used integrated pest management.

In terms of marketing management, the average distance to the market was over 18 km. About 15% of the greenhouses had storage facilities, 55% had invoicing systems, and 81% had product packaging systems. Approximately 90% of greenhouse owners targeted domestic markets. More than 52% sold their products through intermediaries, 39% through direct sales (personal stores or market stalls), and around 4% used a marketing team. Regarding environmental management, only 29% of greenhouse owners adhered to product safety and hygiene standards.

The current status of optimal management in the studied greenhouses

Based on the results (Table 3; Fig. 5), there is a decline in the quality of good greenhouse management practices as one moves from the pre-production phase to the production and post-production phases. The score for the pre-production phase is approximately 7.2 out of 10, while the scores for the production and post-production phases are around 6.3 and 4.5, respectively. It appears that greenhouse managers focus more on pre-production practices than on production and post-production. This is concerning, as the latter two phases, especially post-production, play a significant role in generating profit and income. Part of this lack of attention is due to the limited time managers have to focus adequately on all aspects of the production process. Without a doubt, the use of smart technologies can greatly assist in managing greenhouse production and post-production processes. In this context, some scholars36–38 consider smart farming as a managerial concept that, through the use of modern technologies and computational paradigms, can lead to both quantitative and qualitative improvements in crop products, agricultural productivity, and the potential for diminished environmental effect. Moreover, enabling risk management, and consequently increasing the profitability of production are other benefits of using smart technologies for managing agricultural production39.

Therefore, improving greenhouse management systems is an undeniable necessity, which is also aligned with achieving many global sustainable development goals41. Given that agricultural production is directly related to the consumption of limited resources such as water, energy, land, capital, and other production inputs, proper management of agricultural production systems is of great importance and should be prioritized. In this regard, some studies42,43 emphasize the need to improve production management systems in the agricultural sector to reduce its environmental impacts and ensure the sustainable use of natural resources.

The direct and indirect pathways to management optimization

Table (5) provides a breakdown of the direct and indirect influences of the variables in the developed model (Fig. 4). Our path analysis provides nuanced insights into the drivers of OGM. Crucially, the model confirms that smart technology (ST) adoption exerts the strongest direct influence on OGM (β = 0.373, p< 0.05). This underscores the transformative potential of technologies like IoT sensors and automation systems in enabling precise, data-driven decision-making for greenhouse managers. This finding aligns with the work of Binaei, et al18., who identified technology adoption as a key lever for improving resource efficiency. They noted that using smart technology is an effective way to enhance the performance of food production in a greenhouse, and its lack is a major challenge that significantly affects water and energy usage in food production within greenhouses. However, our study adds a critical layer: the effect of ST is not isolated.

Perhaps the most significant finding is the total effect of climatic conditions (CC), which is the highest among all variables (β = 0.508). While its direct effect (β = 0.291) is smaller than that of ST, its substantial indirect effect (β = 0.217) reveals a more complex story. This means that climate acts as a fundamental contextual driver; it not only directly pressures management decisions but also indirectly shapes OGM by necessitating specific technical features and encouraging the adoption of smart technologies suited to local environmental challenges. For instance, regions with high temperature volatility might see greater investment in smart climate control systems, as noted by Baudoin, et al26.. Therefore, while ST is a powerful direct tool, the initial impetus for its adoption and the design of the entire production system are often a response to climatic pressures. Several studies44–47 have demonstrated the impact of climate change on the management performance of various agricultural production systems, including greenhouses.

Furthermore, economic variables emerged as having the greatest indirect impact on OGM (β = 0.289), despite having no significant direct effect. This suggests that favorable economic conditions do not automatically translate into better management. Instead, they function as a critical enabler, facilitating the adoption of ST and compliance with SR. This indirect role highlights a key policy implication: financial interventions (e.g., subsidies, loans) may be most effective when they are channeled towards supporting technology acquisition and adherence to best practices, rather than expecting economic improvement alone to solve management deficits. This resonates with the conclusions of Pereira48, who demonstrated the long-term economic and environmental benefits of investing in smart agricultural technologies.

It seems that while favorable microeconomic conditions (at the greenhouse level) and macroeconomic conditions (at the societal level) may not directly lead to better greenhouse management, they will indirectly create the groundwork for improving their management. The indirect impact of EV occurs both by enabling the adoption of smart technologies (β = 0.400) and by creating the foundation for the development and adherence to various regulations and standards (β = 0.555) for greenhouse production and management. Economic variables and conditions can significantly influence factors such as investment in smart technology, energy costs, and operational efficiency. Improved economic conditions can provide the funding necessary for good agricultural practices and the adoption of innovations, which in turn can lead to better management performance in greenhouses. Conversely, poor economic conditions can limit resources and result in inefficient management practices. Therefore, EVs are essential for good greenhouse practices management, as they greatly impact managers’ decisions and capabilities. For instance, higher income levels and improved access to resources allow managers to adopt better technologies and sustainable practices, which in turn boost productivity and optimize operations through the use of smart technologies. Ultimately, good greenhouse practices management aims to maximize yields and reduce production costs. Pereira, et al48. indicated that smart farms can reduce energy costs by 83.2% and save approximately 5,527 kg CO2over 8 years. Moreover, according to Rehman, et al49. using smart technologies in agriculture is addresses both operational and environmental challenges.

Considering climatic conditions leads to an improvement in the technical characteristics of the greenhouse (β = 0.229), which in turn improves greenhouse management (β = 0.250). Therefore, while cultivation in controlled environments such as greenhouses reduces the impact of climate change and fluctuations on crop production by either influencing the technical specifications needed for greenhouse construction or by imposing additional technical and technological requirements to enhance greenhouse performance. Baudoin, et al26. believe that local climate and greenhouse growing conditions affect both the cost and quality of production in greenhouses. The level of investment in technology, whether simple or advanced greenhouses and equipment, as well as management, depends primarily on the local climate.

In addition, EV facilitates compliance with SR (β = 0.555), and adherence to SR not only directly leads to better greenhouse management but also indirectly helps improve technical features of the greenhouses (β = 0.232), thereby contributing to improved management of these production units. Therefore, in this study, in addition to the CC and EV, SR and TF were identified as two important intermediary variables in improving the management of greenhouse units (Fig. 4; Table 4). Hypotheses testing

In this study, five hypotheses were examined regarding the relationships between the variables under investigation. The following section presents the results related to the testing of these hypotheses.

H1. Climatic conditions and their changes affect the optimization of greenhouse management both directly and indirectly. The study’s findings support this hypothesis. As shown in Fig. 4 and Table 4, the variable ‘CC’ significantly influenced the variable ‘OGM’ directly and indirectly.

H2. Economic variables influence the optimization of greenhouse management by impacting the use of smart technologies as well as the establishment of various standards and regulations. The study’s findings support this hypothesis. Based on Fig. 4 and Table 4, the variable ‘EV’ significantly influenced ‘OGM’ through its direct impact on ‘ST’ and ‘SR’.

H3. The use of smart technologies in greenhouses affects the optimization of greenhouse management both directly and indirectly. The study’s findings support this hypothesis. As indicated in Fig. 4 and Table 4, ‘ST’ significantly influenced ‘OGM’ directly and indirectly through its impact on ‘SR’.

H4. The establishment of various regulations and standards impacts the optimization of greenhouse management both directly and indirectly. The study’s findings (Fig. 4 and Table 4) support this hypothesis. ‘SR’ significantly influenced ‘OGM’ directly. It also affects ‘OGM’ indirectly through its significant impact on ‘TF’.

H5. The technical features of greenhouses, which are influenced by regulations and standards in this field as well as climatic conditions, affect the optimization of greenhouse management. The study’s findings support this hypothesis. As shown in Fig. 4 and Table 4, ‘TF’ significantly influenced the variable ‘OGM’ in a direct manner.

Scalability and adaptability of the findings

The conceptual model and findings of this study offer a framework that is fundamentally scalable and adaptable to diverse environmental and socioeconomic contexts, which is a core strength of the research. The identified pathways, where economic variables enable technology adoption, and climatic conditions dictate technical requirements, represent universal mechanisms that are likely to hold true across different regions. For instance, the critical role of economic variables as a key enabler (H2) suggests that financial support mechanisms will be essential for scaling smart technology adoption, whether in semi-arid regions or in humid tropical climates. However, the specific manifestations of these relationships will be context-dependent. The smart technology package suitable for a region facing extreme heat (e.g., focusing on advanced cooling and water-saving systems) will differ from one optimized for a region with low light intensity. Similarly, optimal technical features (TF) are inherently defined by local climatic pressures (CC). Therefore, while the structural model itself is scalable, its successful application requires localized adaptation of the indicators, such as selecting context-appropriate technologies and regulations to address region-specific environmental challenges. Future research should quantitatively test this model in different agro-ecological zones to validate its generalizability and create a taxonomy of adaptations.

Study limitations and future research

Despite the valuable insights, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional nature of the data limits our ability to infer causal relationships definitively; a longitudinal design would be beneficial to observe how management practices evolve over time. Second, the focus on greenhouse units in Tehran province, while illustrative, may affect the generalizability of the findings to other regions with different climatic, economic, and cultural contexts. Third, the model did not include potential influential variables such as the psychological or behavioral characteristics of managers (e.g., risk aversion, innovativeness), which could significantly affect technology adoption and management quality. Future research could address these limitations by employing longitudinal data, expanding the geographical scope, and incorporating behavioral factors to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the dynamics influencing greenhouse management optimization.

Conclusions

In recent years, agricultural production and food security, have posed a serious challenge in arid and semi-arid regions such as Iran, because of the increasing extent and intensity of climate change and its impacts. To address this challenge and ensure food security, one of the main government’s strategies and policies is the development of cultivation in controlled environments such as greenhouses. Where the management of environmental variables is more easily and the risk of production is lower and the yields can higher. However, if these production units are not optimally managed, not only will production not increase, but inefficient resource use may lead to severe environmental impacts, exacerbating climate change. The use of smart technologies is one solution for optimizing greenhouse management. These technologies play a significant role in improving greenhouse management by increasing resources productivity and reducing environmental impacts. They assist greenhouses managers to make more informed decisions and contribute to better management of pre-production, production, and post-production processes. Based on the findings of this study, only about 30% of greenhouses have a satisfactory management index. A significant portion of this issue is linked to the post-production phase, suggesting that managers tend to overlook the importance of post-production processes. Therefore, to improve greenhouse management, managers, planners, and policymakers should prioritize post-production management practices, as this phase currently shows the weakest performance. Nevertheless, economic and climatic factors significantly influence the adoption and implementation of smart technologies in greenhouses. Although in the long term, smart technologies can lead to economic savings and improved management processes by optimizing resource use, reducing labor costs, and increasing yields, the high initial costs of smart technologies is a barrier for many greenhouse owners. Government subsidies, financial incentives, and provide access to credit can play a key role in improving the use of these technologies for small scale farmers.

Climatic factors also heighten the need for smart technologies. In regions with harsh or unpredictable climatic conditions, using smart technologies in greenhouses, enable managers to precise control on climate factors such as extreme temperatures, droughts, or excessive rainfall. These technologies help managers to mitigate the effects of adverse climatic conditions and ensure sustainable production by controlling many variables include temperature, humidity, and water use. In regions like Iran, the use of smart technologies can help maintain productivity and reduce climate-related risks for food production. The findings of this study demonstrated how smart technologies can optimize agricultural good operations for food production in greenhouses and highlighted the impact of variables such as climatic and economic conditions, regulations and standards, and the technical characteristics of greenhouses.
