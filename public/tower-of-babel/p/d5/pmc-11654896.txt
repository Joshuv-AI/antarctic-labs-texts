Robotic‐assisted enucleation for renal masses: A comparison of on‐clamp and off‐clamp approach
Authors: Goran Rac, Christopher James, Jeffrey L Ellis, Gregory J Barton, Robert H Blackwell, Gopal N Gupta
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIn surgically excising renal masses, studies have demonstrated that tumor enucleation is an effective option. However, there is limited literature comparing off‐clamp to on‐clamp tumor enucleation.Materials and MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed the charts of 189 patients who underwent robotic‐assisted laparoscopic partial nephrectomy via tumor enucleation by a single surgeon from March 2012 and April 2022. Patients were stratified based on use of renal hilar clamping intraoperatively. Surgical, oncologic, and renal functional outcomes were captured. Variables were analyzed and compared between the two groups using Student's T‐tests and Chi‐square tests.ResultsOf 189 procedures analyzed, 124 were performed on‐clamp and 65 were performed off‐clamp. There were no differences in patient demographics or average length of follow‐up. There were no differences in estimated blood loss, complications, or hospital length of stay. Recurrence rates were similar for the two groups. The absolute difference in estimated glomerular filtration rate change between the two groups at time of first follow‐up was not significant (p = 0.25).ConclusionsThere is no significant difference in perioperative outcomes such as surgical time, blood loss, or complications between the two groups. Furthermore, there was no significant difference in postoperative kidney function between the two techniques.Keywords: hilar clamping, partial nephrectomy, renal mass, tumor enucleation

INTRODUCTION

The American Cancer Society predicts 81 800 new cases of kidney cancer in 2023, with nearly 15 000 deaths expected. 1 The incidence of kidney cancer has been steadily increasing over the past two decades at a rate of ~2% per year. 2 Many of these renal masses undergo surgical extirpation as the accepted curative therapy. 3 Nephron‐sparing surgery through partial nephrectomy (PN) has been shown to have equivalent oncologic efficacy as radical nephrectomy in clinical T1a and T1b tumors with the added benefit of superior renal function preservation. 4 Thus, as minimally invasive surgery has progressed, robotic‐assisted laparoscopic PN (RAPN) has become the preferred technique for extirpation of small renal masses.

The goals of PN are to safely excise the tumor while preserving as much renal function as possible. Dong et al. suggested that the two mechanisms affecting renal function after PN are loss of nephrons and incomplete or failed recovery of nephrons due to ischemia. 5 Historically, PN required the removal of roughly 1 cm of normal renal parenchyma to obtain negative surgical margins. This often has required hilar clamping for vascular control and visualization with resultant warm ischemia in the minimally invasive setting. While it is realized now that only a minimal margin of normal tissue is necessary for satisfactory oncologic outcomes, enucleation of small renal masses has increased in popularity. 6 Tumor enucleation relies on utilizing a surgical plane between the tumor and its pseudocapsule, which is present in the majority of renal masses. Enucleation has been proven to not only be equivalent in oncologic outcomes to standard margin PN, but Dong and colleagues have shown that enucleation is superior in perioperative outcomes and renal function preservation. 7 , 8

It is well documented that renal ischemia contributes to a decline in renal function after PN. 4 , 5 As such, urologists emphasize efficacious tumor excision following hilar clamping to minimize warm ischemia time, and ultimately damage to normal nephrons. There have been a number of techniques described to minimize warm ischemia time including selective renal parenchymal clamping, laser supported minimally invasive PN, and sequential preplaced suture renorrhaphy, but being relatively new techniques their validation in the literature is lacking. 9 , 10 , 11 We hypothesize that there is no significant difference between renal tumor enucleation without hilar clamping (“off‐clamp”) compared to performing it with hilar clamping (“on‐clamp”) with regard to perioperative outcomes, disease recurrence, and postoperative renal function. Our objective is to retrospectively compare perioperative, oncologic, and renal functional outcomes between presence and absence of hilar clamping with robotic tumor enucleation.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Data collection and cohort criteria

The hospital records for 189 patients undergoing RAPN utilizing the institution's specific enucleation technique between March 2012 and April 2022 were retrospectively reviewed in an institutional review board approved database. Exclusion criteria included patients who underwent conversion to standard margin PN, radical nephrectomy and/or to an open surgical procedure. Further, any patients for whom preoperative creatinine data were not available were excluded, as preoperative GFR could not be calculated.

Perioperative outcomes

Preoperative imaging was studied to calculate location, size, and RENAL nephrometry score of the renal mass. Operative parameters were collected. Surgical specimens were evaluated by pathologists specializing in renal pathology to determine their size, histopathology, International Society of Urological Pathologists (ISUP) grade, surgical margin status, and pathological stage.

Patients requiring readmission were defined as any patient who was readmitted due to a surgical complication within 1 month of surgery. One‐month perioperative complications were graded using the modified Clavien‐Dindo classification system and divided into minor (Grade 1−2) and major (Grade 3+) complications.

Evaluation of renal function

Renal function was measured by the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). This was assessed preoperatively, on postoperative Day 1, the day of discharge, at an early follow‐up (defined as within 0−3 months of surgery), intermediate follow‐up (defined as within 6−9 months of surgery), and at 1 year follow‐up (defined as within 11−13 months of surgery). Additionally, any patient with laboratory data beyond the 1 year follow‐up was also evaluated for renal function at this last known follow‐up. We calculated change in renal function by comparing the patient's eGFR at these time points to their preoperative eGFR.

Follow‐up

Patient survival was assessed by reviewing hospital medical records (both at our institution and at outside facilities, when available) and cause of death was determined. Local or metastatic recurrence of renal cancer was assessed by reviewing the patient's last laboratory or radiographic data available.

Statistical analysis

Bivariate analyses of variables between the on‐clamp and off‐clamp enucleation cohorts were performed by utilizing Student's T tests and Chi‐square tests, as appropriate. An α of 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed with SAS version 9.4.

RESULTS

Of the 189 patients undergoing enucleation, hilar clamping was used in 124 (65.6%), while hilar clamping was not used in the remaining 65 (34.4%). Patient demographics and preoperative characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Age, race, gender, rates of prior abdominal surgery, BMI, tumor laterality, and preoperative renal function were similar between cohorts. The mean length of follow‐up for the overall cohort was 42.0 months. Patients undergoing hilar clamping were followed for a mean of 34.3 months, and those not undergoing hilar clamping were followed for a mean of 56.7 months (p < 0.001).

Operative factors and perioperative outcomes are summarized in Table 3. The majority of procedures were performed via retroperitoneal compared to transperitoneal approach (72.5% vs. 27.5%). A greater proportion of the procedures in the on‐clamp cohort were performed via the retroperitoneal approach compared to that of the off‐clamp cohort (79.0% vs. 60.0%, p < 0.01). There was no significant difference in mean surgical time in the on‐clamp group compared to the off‐clamp group (146.8 vs. 156.7 min, p = 0.16). Patients in the on‐clamp cohort had a mean warm ischemia time of 15.8 min (standard deviation 8.0 min). There was no significant difference in estimated blood loss (EBL) between the two groups (mean EBL of 76.1 mL vs. 109.0 mL for on‐clamp and off‐clamp, p = 0.37). There were no intraoperative complications in either cohort. Additionally, there was no difference in the rates of postoperative complications (12.0% vs. 12.3% for on‐clamp and off‐clamp, p = 0.39). The only major complications occurred in the on‐clamp group. These included postoperative hemorrhage in one patient, hydronephrosis necessitating temporary ureteral stent placement in one patient, and death within 30 days of surgery in one patient. There was no significant difference in hospital length of stay between the two groups (mean 1.3 days vs. 1.5 days for on‐clamp and off‐clamp, p = 0.25). There was no significant difference in disease recurrence, with two recurrences in each group (1.6% vs. 3.1% for on‐clamp and off‐clamp, p = 0.51). There were a total of five deaths, four of which were in the on‐clamp group with one in the off‐clamp group. Only one death in the on‐clamp group was determined to be related to renal cell carcinoma.

The renal functional outcomes are summarized in Table 4. The overall trend in eGFR for each cohort is displayed in Figure 1. A significant increase in eGFR was noted in both groups on postoperative Day 1 with an increase of 4.3 mL/min/1.73 m2 for on‐clamp (p < 0.001) and 7.7 mL/min/1.73 m2 for off‐clamp (p < 0.01). Similarly, there was a significant increase in eGFR noted in both groups at time of discharge with an increase of 4.9 mL/min/1.73m2 for on‐clamp (p < 0.001) and 10.3 mL/min/1.73m2 for off‐clamp (p < 0.001). However, there was no significant eGFR change appreciated in either group at the 1−3 month, 6−9 month at 12 month time points. The on‐clamp group did have a significant decrease in eGFR compared to baseline at time of last eGFR (−3.0 mL/min/1.73 m2; p = 0.033), while the off‐clamp did not have any significant change (+2.1 mL/min/1.73 m2; p = 0.62). When comparing the absolute difference in eGFR between the on‐clamp and off‐clamp cohorts, the only significant difference occurred at time of discharge at which point the increase in eGFR for the off‐clamp group was greater than that for the on‐clamp group (+10.3 vs. +4.9 mL/min/1.73 m2, respectively; p < 0.01).

DISCUSSION

In our investigation of on‐ and off‐clamp approaches to RAPN utilizing tumor enucleation, there were no significant differences between the approaches in the vast majority of functional and oncologic outcomes. A few notable exceptions include smaller tumor size, greater observed increase in eGFR at time of discharge, and shorter follow up data in the off‐clamp group. In our cohort, off‐clamp tumor enucleation RAPN was more likely to be performed in a transperitoneal fashion, whereas on‐clamp enucleation RAPN was more likely to be performed retroperitoneally. Notable variables without significant differences between the cohorts include operative time, EBL, complication rate (across all Clavien‐Dindo complication grades), length of hospital stay, and disease recurrence. There was also no difference in eGFR change at almost all timepoints including postoperative day #1, 1−3 month follow up, 6−9 month follow up, 12 month follow up, and time of most recent follow up. Overall, our results show that off‐clamp tumor enucleation can be performed with similar functional and oncologic outcomes to on‐clamp tumor enucleation.

A recent small series looked at perioperative outcomes for patients undergoing robotic tumor enucleation performed off‐clamp with a single layer medullary renorrhaphy and had zero Clavien‐Dindo grade 3 or higher complications. 12 While this is a small series with no on‐clamp group for comparison, this demonstrates the immediate safety profile of the procedure.

To our knowledge, there exists one other study comparing perioperative outcomes between on‐clamp and off‐clamp renal tumor enucleation. In 2018, Mari and colleagues investigated functional outcomes between 120 patients who underwent on‐clamp endoscopic robotic‐assisted simple enucleations (ERASE) to 120 patients who underwent off‐clamp ERASE. 13 Their results also demonstrate a statistically significant lower decrease in eGFR in the off‐clamp group compared to the on‐clamp group at time of discharge and at 30‐day follow up. This difference lost statistical significance at 6 month follow up and beyond. While our studies show similar results with regard to renal function changes between the two clamping techniques, Mari and colleagues did not concurrently evaluate oncologic outcomes. With more information regarding similarities in both the oncologic and functional outcomes between the two techniques provided by our study, the urologist can best choose an enucleation clamping approach based on patient and tumor characteristics.

There was no difference in EBL between the two groups in our cohort. Although hilar clamping is thought to allow for better visualization of the operative field due to decreased blood loss, our results demonstrate a negligible difference in EBL, regardless of whether hilar clamping is utilized. When performing enucleation, the plane of dissection is relatively avascular and any vessels are identified and controlled as they are encountered. As such, hemostasis and adequate visualization may be easier to maintain.

In analyzing complication rates between the on‐ and off‐clamp enucleations, no significant differences were observed. Further, of the complications that did occur, the vast majority (87.0%) were minor complications. This appears to be consistent with existing urologic literature regarding on‐ and off‐clamp enucleation. Satkunasivam and colleagues compared off‐clamp minimal margin PN with superselective hilar clamping. They also found no difference in complication rates between the two techniques. 14 Azhar and associates, in a precursor study to Satkunavisam, also found no difference in complication rates between superselective clamping and no clamping. 15

Preserving renal function postoperatively is one of the main goals of PN. There was a significant eGFR change for both groups at postoperative Day 1 and at time of discharge with both groups experiencing an increase in eGFR at those timepoints. The increase for the off‐clamp group was significantly greater than that for the on‐clamp group at time of discharge. On longer follow‐up, this difference was no longer appreciable. When assessing the last‐available eGFR values, the on‐clamp group did have a statistically significant decrease in eGFR. However, the clinical significance of the 3.0 mL/min decrease in eGFR is equivocal. This may be a function of the longer period of follow‐up in the on‐clamp cohort. Additionally, the absolute difference in eGFR change between the two cohorts was not significant. Our results demonstrated that limited warm ischemia in the setting of enucleation does not influence long‐term eGFR and does not appear to be correlated with postoperative renal function. We propose that a short application of warm ischemia does not affect renal function postoperatively to a significant degree, and instead, focus should be placed on minimizing normal renal tissue loss during the surgery. Although our study did not compare enucleation to sharp excision, Blackwell and colleagues demonstrated that enucleation was superior to sharp excision when performing PN in preserving renal function as measured by postoperative eGFR. 16

With regard to oncologic outcomes associated with PN, a 2019 study by Bertolo and colleagues investigating 5‐year outcomes associated with standard RAPN for clinical T1 renal tumors found a 3.6% local recurrence rate. 17 These oncologic RAPN outcomes utilizing the tumor enucleation technique were further studied by Minervini and associates, who quoted a similar 3.6% local recurrence rate. 18 Considering our similar recurrence rate (3.1%), off‐clamp tumor enucleation displays oncologically sound outcomes. This is likely due in part to the presence of an identifiable pseudocapsule present for most renal have that can be utilized to prevent positive margins. 15

Limitations of the present study include the retrospective design and that all PN cases were performed by a single surgeon. Given the nonrandomized nature of the study, there is potential for selection bias. The tumors in the off‐clamp cohort were noted to have a significantly smaller radiographic size; however, the overall complexity of the tumors as quantified by RENAL nephrometry score did not significantly differ between the two groups. The length of follow‐up for the on‐clamp group was significantly longer than that for the off‐clamp group, though each group was followed for a mean of greater than 30 months. This shorter follow up period is likely a product of the relatively recent adoption of the off‐clamp technique compared to the conventional on‐clamp technique.

The decision to exclude patients who underwent conversion to standard margin PN, radical nephrectomy and/or to an open surgical procedure also warrants discussion on the potential introduction of selection bias. Seven patients within the initial cohort experienced such a conversion. Two of these patients underwent conversion to robotic radical nephrectomy, while five patients underwent conversion to a standard margin PN. Of those patients that were converted to a standard margin PN, four cases remained robotic, while one case was converted to an open approach. The majority of these cases were converted due to unanticipated tumor complexity and adverse tumor characteristics, with only one case undergoing conversion due to bleeding before mass resection. As our primary goal was to evaluate perioperative outcomes associated with the enucleation technique, the decision was made to exclude these patients rather than include on an intention to treat basis, as their presence in the analysis would confound data.

CONCLUSION

There was no significant difference in perioperative outcome measured, nor in postoperative kidney function between on‐clamp and off‐clamp tumor enucleation. As such, we recommend that each surgeon make individualized decisions on hilar clamping based on patient and tumor characteristics.
