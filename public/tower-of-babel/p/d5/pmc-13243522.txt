Artificial sweeteners and risk of cardiometabolic outcomes and mortality: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Minseo Kim, Sunny Kumar, Gerald Chi, Jane J Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDArtificial sweeteners are widely used as sugar substitutes to reduce sugar intake and mitigate health risks. However, their potential long-term health effects remain uncertain as existing studies have reported inconsistent findings.OBJECTIVEThis systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to examine the association between artificial sweetener intake and the risk of cardiometabolic outcomes and mortality.METHODSStudies were identified through comprehensive searches of PubMed and Embase up to October 8, 2025. Pooled hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) comparing the highest and lowest artificial sweetener consumption categories were calculated using a random-effects model. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic. The methodological quality of the included studies was evaluated using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale.RESULTSIn total, 3,625,613 participants from 37 studies were included. Higher artificial sweetener intake was associated with an increased risk of type 2 diabetes (HR, 1.26; 95% CI, 1.14–1.40), hypertension (HR, 1.13; 95% CI, 1.09–1.18), heart failure (HR, 1.26; 95% CI, 1.14–1.39), stroke (HR, 1.12; 95% CI, 1.05–1.19), coronary artery disease (HR, 1.11; 95% CI, 1.00–1.23), cardiovascular mortality (HR, 1.18; 95% CI, 1.03–1.35), and all-cause mortality (HR, 1.09; 95% CI, 1.01–1.17). The association with atrial fibrillation was not significant (HR, 1.13; 95% CI, 0.94–1.37). Significant heterogeneity was observed for all endpoints except for stroke and heart failure.CONCLUSIONSThis meta-analysis demonstrates that a higher intake of artificial sweeteners is associated with an increased risk of several cardiometabolic outcomes and mortality. These findings underscore the need for cautious consumption and further research into their cardiometabolic effects.Trial RegistrationInternational Prospective Registration of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) Identifier: CRD420251020488

INTRODUCTION

The human preference for sweetness is an innate biological trait, with evidence indicating that newborns are predisposed to sweet-tasting substances [1]. An inherent preference for sweet flavors has contributed to the global increase in sugar consumption, a trend further amplified by the broad availability of sugar-sweetened beverages and ultra-processed foods [23]. Excessive sugar consumption has been associated with a growing burden of chronic diseases such as type 2 diabetes [4] and cardiovascular disease [56], both of which are major contributors to global cardiometabolic morbidity and mortality [456]. These trends have increased public and scientific awareness and prompted extensive public health efforts to reduce added sugar intake.

In response, artificial sweeteners, also referred to as non-nutritive, low-, or zero-calorie sweeteners, have gained popularity as sugar substitutes by providing high-intensity sweetness with minimal or no calorie content [78]. These compounds are commonly used in beverages, processed foods, and pharmaceutical formulations [8]. For instance, sucralose is approximately 600 times sweeter than sucrose; saccharin is 300 to 400 times sweeter; aspartame and acesulfame potassium (acesulfame-K) are approximately 200 times sweeter; and cyclamate is approximately 30 times sweeter [9]. Sucralose and aspartame are currently the most widely used artificial sweeteners, with aspartame alone found in more than 6,000 food products worldwide [10]. An analysis of United States dietary data reported that adult consumption of artificial sweeteners increased from 18.7% in 1999–2000 to 24.4% in 2009–2012, primarily driven by increased intake of diet beverages [11]. Moreover, a recent 14-yr cohort study of over 36,000 Australian adults found that the habitual consumption of just one can of artificially sweetened soft drink per day was associated with a 38% higher risk of developing type 2 diabetes, a relationship that persisted even after adjusting for body weight, suggesting potential metabolic effects beyond adiposity [12]. Despite their widespread use, the safety and metabolic effects of artificial sweeteners remain controversial. In July 2023, the International Agency for Research on Cancer classified aspartame as possibly carcinogenic to humans, whereas the Food and Drug Administration stated that it is safe for consumption within established limits [13]. These regulatory inconsistencies have heightened public and scientific interest in the potential health implications of artificial sweeteners.

Artificial sweeteners are widely marketed as sugar alternatives for individuals with metabolic disorders, such as obesity and type 2 diabetes, based on their lack of caloric contribution and minimal glycemic impact [1415]. They are commonly included in sugar-free food products and promoted for weight management and blood glucose control. Recent data from the NutriNet-Santé cohort showed that individuals with a higher body mass index (BMI) and pre-existing metabolic conditions were more likely to consume artificial sweeteners, especially in the form of diet beverages and sugar-free food products [16]. However, growing evidence suggests that prolonged and excessive intake of artificial sweeteners may be associated with impaired metabolic health, contributing to insulin resistance, adiposity, and increased cardiovascular risk [1718]. Recent studies have also indicated that artificial sweeteners may alter metabolic pathways, disrupt glucose homeostasis, and adversely affect cardiovascular function, especially in individuals with pre-existing metabolic disorders [19202122].

Given the global burden of cardiometabolic diseases and the increasing use of artificial sweeteners, further comprehensive evaluation of their health associations is warranted. Several systematic reviews and meta-analyses have examined the relationship between artificial sweetener consumption and selected metabolic and cardiovascular outcomes [23242526]. Nevertheless, differences in scope, outcome definitions, and analytical approaches underscore the need for a more comprehensive evaluation that incorporates a wider range of cardiometabolic endpoints and potential sources of heterogeneity. In particular, earlier reviews often focused on individual outcomes [2324] or specific populations [25] and have rarely examined multiple cardiometabolic endpoints within a unified analytical framework [232526]. Furthermore, few studies have investigated sex-related differences or incorporated exploratory analyses to contextualize broader research trends in this field [25]. Collectively, these gaps underscore the need for an updated and comprehensive synthesis of the available evidence. Therefore, this study aimed to conduct a systematic review and meta-analysis to examine the association among artificial sweetener intake, cardiometabolic outcomes, and mortality.

METHODS

This systematic review and meta-analysis adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines [27]. The protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Registration of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; Registration ID: CRD420251020488), accessible at https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/prospero/.

Literature search and selection criteria

A comprehensive literature search was performed using PubMed and Embase from their inception until October 8, 2025. For PubMed, the search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH), which are standardized terms used for indexing in biomedical databases, with relevant keywords to capture studies assessing the association between artificial sweetener consumption, cardiometabolic outcomes, and mortality. The exposure term “sweetening agents” (MeSH Unique ID: D013549) was combined with MeSH terms for the outcomes, including “diabetes mellitus, type 2,” “hypertension,” “atrial fibrillation,” “heart failure,” “stroke,” “coronary artery disease,” “cardiovascular diseases,” and “mortality.” For Embase, the strategy combined the exposure term ‘sweetening agent’ with outcome-related terms including ‘non insulin dependent diabetes mellitus,’ ‘hypertension,’ ‘atrial fibrillation,’ ‘heart failure,’ ‘stroke,’ ‘coronary heart disease,’ ‘mortality,’ and ‘cardiovascular disease.’ Boolean operators (‘AND’ and ‘OR’) were applied to refine the search (Supplementary Table 1) [28].

Studies were included if they met the following criteria: 1) original, peer-reviewed research articles, letters, or brief reports; 2) published in English with full text available; 3) designed to investigate the association between artificial sweetener intake and specified cardiometabolic outcomes and mortality; 4) conducted among populations at risk for cardiometabolic outcomes, type 2 diabetes, or mortality; and 5) relevant to the outcomes of interest.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) review articles, editorials, commentaries, case reports, or nonhuman studies, 2) studies that did not provide sufficient data for effect size estimation, 3) studies that focused solely on natural sweeteners without evaluating artificial sweeteners, and 4) studies with inadequate exposure or outcome definitions.

Three investigators screened and selected the studies based on predefined eligibility criteria. Two investigators independently selected relevant studies during the initial screening process. Any discrepancies in the selection process were resolved through discussions and consultation with a third investigator. In case of multiple publications originating from the same cohort, the study with the most comprehensive data or the most recent publication was included.

Data extraction

The following data were extracted from the studies included in the final analysis: study title (including the first author and year of publication), country, study design, study population or database origin, types of artificial sweeteners, participant demographics and characteristics (sample size, case count, mean age or age range, and sex proportion), study period (mean follow-up years), artificial sweetener intake unit, measurement of artificial sweetener consumption (lowest vs. highest category), hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs, and endpoints. Two investigators independently extracted and tabulated data. Any discrepancies in data extraction or entry were resolved by a third or fourth investigator.

Quality assessment

The risk of bias and methodological quality of each included study were independently assessed by 2 investigators using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), which evaluates 3 domains: selection of study groups (maximum 4 stars), comparability of groups (maximum 2 stars), and ascertainment of exposure or outcome (maximum 3 stars), with a maximum total score of 9 stars. Higher scores indicate a lower risk of bias. Quality assessments were performed independently by 2 investigators, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third independent investigator to reach a consensus.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Stata BE version 19.5 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). The primary effect measures were expressed as pooled HRs with corresponding 95% CIs, comparing the highest and lowest categories of artificial sweetener intake, with the lowest category generally representing no or minimal intake. Pooled estimates were computed using the inverse-variance estimation method with a restricted maximum likelihood approach using random effects models. Heterogeneity across studies was assessed using Cochran’s Q statistic and quantified using the Higgins I2 index, with substantial heterogeneity defined as I2 > 50%. Further meta-regression analyses were conducted to explore the potential sources of heterogeneity. Study-level demographic and clinical characteristics were individually incorporated, including age (mean age), sex (proportion of women), race (proportion of White participants), and BMI (mean BMI).

In addition, key terms were analyzed using MeSH terms extracted from the included studies, and a network map was generated using VOSviewer (version 1.6.20; Leiden University, Leiden, Netherlands) to visualize and explore their relationships and thematic structure. This was undertaken to characterize the thematic focus of the included studies within the meta-analysis, provide an overview of the research landscape, and help interpret how the evidence on artificial sweeteners, cardiometabolic outcomes, and mortality was distributed. The minimum occurrence threshold for inclusion in the analysis was set to 3 instances to ensure adequate representation. The occurrences reflected how frequently a MeSH term appeared across the included studies, indicating its prominence. Links represent the number of times a term co-occurred with other terms within the same study, and each link was assigned a strength score based on its frequency. The total link strength is defined as the sum of the co-occurrence strength scores between a term and all other terms, indicating its overall connectivity within the network. Based on these metrics, related terms were automatically grouped into clusters using VOSviewer, where terms within the same cluster co-occurred more frequently than those in other clusters. The resulting network map visualizes the nodes representing MeSH terms, with the node size indicating the frequency of occurrence. Thicker and denser lines between nodes represent stronger co-occurrence relationships. Clusters were distinguished by color-highlighted thematic groupings, allowing for interpretation of the structural organization of research topics across the included studies.

RESULTS

Identification of relevant studies

Fig. 1 illustrates the study selection process. A systematic database search identified 669 records (PubMed: 440, Embase: 225, citation references: 4). After removing 46 duplicate records through automated screening, and 28 additional duplicates through manual review, 591 records were retained for further evaluation. Of these, one record was irretrievable, leaving 590 records for eligibility assessment. After screening, 557 studies were excluded for the following reasons: irrelevant study design (n = 225), irrelevant intervention (n = 172), irrelevant population (n = 24), irrelevant outcomes (n = 55), and non-original study design (n = 81). All 4 records identified through the citation search met the inclusion criteria and were retained. Quality assessment

All the included studies were assessed for methodological quality using the NOS. The average NOS score was 7.7 (range, 6–9), indicating a high level of study quality (Table 2). Among the 37 studies, 35 (94.6%) received a score of 7 or higher and were classified as high quality, while 2 studies (5.4%) received a score of 6 and were considered to be of moderate quality. No studies were rated as low-quality (≤ 3 points). All studies received full stars for the comparability domain, the selection domain item selection of the non-exposed cohort, and the outcome domain item adequacy of follow-up of cohorts, whereas variability was primarily observed in the items representativeness of the exposed cohort, and ascertainment of exposure. These results suggested that the overall methodological quality of the included studies was high, supporting the credibility of the findings of this meta-analysis.

Pooled analysis of all cardiometabolic outcomes

Pooled analysis demonstrated significant associations between high artificial sweetener intake and all evaluated cardiometabolic outcomes and mortality, except for atrial fibrillation (Fig. 2). The pooled HRs for each outcome were as follows: type 2 diabetes (HR, 1.26; 95% CI, 1.14–1.40; I2 = 86.6%) (Fig. 2A), hypertension (HR, 1.13; 95% CI, 1.09–1.18; I2 = 66.7%) (Fig. 2B), atrial fibrillation (HR, 1.13; 95% CI, 0.94–1.37; I2 = 86.7%) (Fig. 2C), heart failure (HR, 1.26; 95% CI, 1.14–1.39; I2 = 0.01%) (Fig. 2D), stroke (HR, 1.12; 95% CI, 1.05–1.19; I2 = 6.2%) (Fig. 2E), coronary artery disease (HR, 1.11; 95% CI, 1.00–1.23; I2 = 69.4%) (Fig. 2F), cardiovascular mortality (HR, 1.18; 95% CI, 1.03–1.35; I2 = 65.6%) (Fig. 2G), and all-cause mortality (HR, 1.09; 95% CI, 1.01–1.17; I2 = 77.9%) (Fig. 2H). Significant heterogeneity was noted for all outcomes except for stroke and heart failure.

Meta-regression analyses examining study-level characteristics

The results of the meta-regression analyses examining study-level characteristics are presented in Supplementary Table 2. For type 2 diabetes, a higher proportion of women and White participants were associated with lower risk estimates (women: HR, 0.621; 95% CI, 0.406–0.951; P = 0.028; White participants: HR, 0.996; 95% CI, 0.992–0.999; P = 0.011). For atrial fibrillation, a higher proportion of women was associated with lower risk estimates (HR, 0.560; 95% CI, 0.412–0.761; P < 0.001). For coronary artery disease, older mean age (HR, 1.019; 95% CI, 1.009–1.030; P < 0.001), a higher proportion of women (HR, 1.235; 95% CI, 1.040–1.466; P = 0.016), and higher mean BMI (HR, 1.084; 95% CI, 1.000–1.175; P = 0.049) were associated with higher risk estimates. For cardiovascular mortality, a high mean BMI was associated with lower risk estimates (HR, 0.921; 95% CI, 0.870–0.975; P = 0.005). Higher mean BMI was associated with lower risk estimates for all-cause mortality (HR, 0.962; 95% CI, 0.937–0.987; P = 0.003). No association was observed between hypertension and stroke. A meta-regression analysis for heart failure could not be conducted because of the insufficient number of included studies.

Mapping of MeSH terms and their relationships

Network analysis of the MeSH terms extracted from the 37 included studies revealed several distinct clusters reflecting the major thematic domains in the existing literature. The minimum occurrence threshold was set at 3, resulting in 40 MeSH terms for analysis (Supplementary Table 3). The frequency of occurrence ranged from 3 to 36, the number of links from 17 to 39, and the total link strength from 28 to 384. In this analysis, occurrence refers to the number of times a given MeSH term appeared across the included studies, links indicate the number of unique terms that co-occurred with that term within individual records, and total link strength represents the summed co-occurrence strength between a given term and all other terms.

The resulting network map (Supplementary Fig. 1) revealed 4 distinct color-coded clusters, each representing a coherent thematic domain. Cluster 1 (red) comprised terms related to dietary factors, survey methodology, and cardiovascular risk indicators, whereas cluster 2 (green) included demographic descriptors, beverage consumption patterns (e.g., fruit and vegetable juices), and major cardiometabolic endpoints. Cluster 3 (blue) was centered on cohort study design elements and hypertension-related themes, and cluster 4 (yellow) encompassed topics related to older populations, diabetes, and beverage-related metabolic risk. At the core of the network, large nodes such as “humans,” “female,” “sweetening agents,” “middle aged,” and “adult” demonstrated high frequencies and strong connectivity, reflecting their centrality in the literature. In contrast, smaller peripheral nodes (e.g., nutrition surveys and fruit and vegetable juices) represented relatively infrequent terms with weaker connections, suggesting niche or emerging research areas.

DISCUSSION

This systematic review and meta-analysis provides critical insights into the relationship between artificial sweetener consumption and cardiometabolic health outcomes. Our key findings are 3-fold. First, by synthesizing evidence from 37 observational studies encompassing over 3.6 million participants, which represents a substantially larger dataset than in previous meta-analyses on this topic, we found that a high intake of artificial sweeteners was significantly associated with an increased risk of type 2 diabetes, hypertension, heart failure, stroke, coronary artery disease, cardiovascular mortality, and all-cause mortality. Atrial fibrillation was the only outcome showing no significant association. The expanded dataset and inclusion of a broader set of cardiometabolic endpoints allowed for a more comprehensive and contemporary understanding of the diverse health implications of artificial sweetener consumption. Second, meta-regression analyses indicated that study-level characteristics, including age, sex, race, and BMI, were associated with differences in the reported associations between artificial sweetener intake and cardiometabolic outcomes across studies. These findings underscore the importance of considering population characteristics when interpreting the cardiometabolic effects of artificial sweetener consumption. Third, the network map revealed that research on artificial sweeteners and cardiometabolic outcomes is structured around 4 major thematic domains: dietary exposure, population characteristics, study methodologies, and clinical endpoints. Central terms such as “human,” “female,” “sweetening agents,” and “middle aged” emerged as the most prominent, reflecting their dominant role in the existing literature. Although exploratory, this analysis provides contextual insights into the evolution of the research themes and methodological approaches. Overall, our findings build on previous reviews by drawing on a larger and more diverse body of evidence, covering a wider range of cardiometabolic outcomes and exploring potential sources of heterogeneity. Taken together, these results provide an updated and comprehensive perspective on the association between artificial sweetener consumption, cardiometabolic health outcomes, and mortality.

The findings of this study align with those of previous research, suggesting that artificial sweetener consumption may be associated with adverse cardiometabolic outcomes. Several studies have reported that individuals with higher artificial sweetener intake exhibit increased risks of obesity, metabolic syndrome, and cardiovascular diseases [66676869]. Additionally, recent studies have indicated that artificial sweeteners may disrupt glucose homeostasis and insulin sensitivity, potentially increasing the risk of cardiometabolic diseases, including type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular events [7071]. Our study strengthens these observations by incorporating a more extensive dataset and systematically assessing multiple cardiometabolic endpoints. In particular, several of the outcomes included in our analysis, such as hypertension and type 2 diabetes, have been less commonly synthesized together with major cardiovascular outcomes in the published literature. This study contributes to presenting these associations in a more integrated manner. However, the observed associations are not universally supported by the literature. Some randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have reported neutral or even beneficial effects of artificial sweeteners on body weight and glycemic control when used as a sugar substitute in controlled dietary settings [1572]. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in the study design, duration of follow-up, and participant characteristics. Unlike observational studies, RCTs typically involve short intervention periods that may not reflect the long-term effects of habitual artificial sweetener consumption. Furthermore, confounding by indication remains a concern in observational research as individuals consuming artificial sweeteners may already have metabolic risk factors that predispose them to adverse outcomes. Across the included studies, most analyses were adjusted for key cardiometabolic confounders such as age, sex, BMI, smoking status, alcohol intake, physical activity, and total energy intake, as summarized in the adjusted covariate column of Table 1. However, the selection and extent of covariate adjustments varied among studies. In addition, several other factors may have contributed to the substantial heterogeneity, including differences in exposure assessment, outcome ascertainment, baseline health status of the study populations, and geographic variation across the study regions. These methodological and population differences may have partly contributed to the between-study heterogeneity and should be considered when interpreting the pooled estimates.

Several potential mechanisms may explain the observed associations between artificial sweetener intake, adverse cardiometabolic outcomes, and mortality. One hypothesis is that artificial sweeteners may alter gut microbiota composition, leading to metabolic dysregulation and increased insulin resistance [7374]. Emerging evidence suggests that artificial sweetener consumption induces dysbiosis by promoting the growth of bacteria associated with glucose intolerance, thereby exacerbating metabolic disturbances [737475]. Another possible mechanism involves the disruption of metabolic signaling pathways. Artificial sweeteners may activate sweet taste receptors in the gastrointestinal tract, triggering insulin secretion even in the absence of caloric intake [76]. This paradoxical response may contribute to compensatory hyperinsulinemia and impaired glucose metabolism over time [7677]. Additionally, artificial sweeteners have been implicated in promoting food cravings and increased energy intake, which may indirectly elevate the risk of obesity and cardiometabolic diseases [7879]. Some artificial sweeteners have been shown to exert direct cardiovascular effects. For instance, sucralose and aspartame have been linked to endothelial dysfunction and increased arterial stiffness, which are critical precursors of hypertension and atherosclerosis [4180]. Experimental studies suggest that chronic artificial sweetener exposure may also enhance oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, further contributing to the pathogenesis of cardiometabolic disorders [4581]. Moreover, emerging evidence suggests that the metabolic and cardiovascular effects of artificial sweeteners differ depending on the compounds. For example, saccharin has been reported to alter the gut microbiota composition and induce glucose intolerance in experimental models [7382], whereas sucralose has been associated with altered glycemic and insulin responses during glucose tolerance tests [76]. Taken together, these mechanistic pathways provide biological plausibility for the observed associations among artificial sweetener consumption, increased cardiometabolic risk and type 2 diabetes.

This study has several strengths. First, we utilized the most up-to-date and globally representative large-scale dataset to comprehensively analyze multiple cardiometabolic outcomes. The number of included studies and participants in this study was larger than those in earlier studies, which helped improve the precision of the pooled estimates. Second, we applied rigorous inclusion criteria and systematic quality assessment, ensuring that only methodologically robust studies contributed to the pooled estimates and minimizing bias. Third, we incorporated an exploratory MeSH term network analysis to visually map thematic patterns across studies, offering additional insights into research trends and methodological structures within the field. Fourth, subgroup meta-regression analyses by sex were conducted to explore the potential explanatory factors for between-study heterogeneity. This analytical component has rarely been incorporated into earlier reviews and provides an additional context for interpreting the pooled results.

Despite these strengths, certain limitations should be acknowledged. One important limitation of this meta-analysis is the inclusion of multiple observational study designs, including cohort, case-cohort, and cross-sectional studies. These designs differ in their methodological structure, particularly in terms of temporality and potential sources of bias. While longitudinal cohort studies allow the assessment of incident outcomes over time, cross-sectional studies cannot establish temporal relationships between exposure and outcomes. Pooling the effect estimates across heterogeneous study designs may introduce methodological variability and limit causal interpretation. Although meta-regression using study design as a study-level variable was considered to explore potential sources of heterogeneity, it could not be performed because the majority of the included studies were cohort studies, and only a small number represented other designs. This limited variation in the study design prevented meaningful statistical assessments of design-related heterogeneity. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution. As all the studies included in this review were observational in design, the inherent nature of these studies precludes the establishment of causality. Although heart failure was significantly associated with artificial sweetener intake, a sex-specific meta-regression could not be conducted because of the limited number of available studies. Consequently, potential sex differences could not be evaluated and warrant further investigation in large-scale studies. Additionally, the classification of artificial sweetener consumption varied across studies, with some relying on self-reported dietary assessments subject to recall bias and others failing to distinguish between specific types of artificial sweeteners at the time of data collection. The observed heterogeneity in the effect sizes underscores the complexity of this research area and emphasizes the need for standardized exposure definitions and more granular data on artificial sweetener types and consumption patterns in future observational studies. Future studies could explore the differential effects of various artificial sweeteners on cardiometabolic health and mortality.

Considering the widespread use of artificial sweeteners in modern diets, our findings have important public health implications. Although artificial sweeteners are often considered healthier alternatives to sugar, their potential metabolic and cardiovascular risks warrant careful consideration. Public health policies may incorporate evidence-based recommendations regarding artificial sweetener consumption, particularly for individuals at high risk of cardiometabolic events. Further research is needed to refine the dietary guidelines and determine safe intake thresholds for artificial sweeteners. Our meta-analysis suggests that the adverse impact of artificial sweetener consumption may be more pronounced among women, particularly for coronary artery disease and cardiovascular mortality. The stronger association in studies with a higher proportion of women participants indicates the potential role of sex-specific biological factors, such as hormonal regulation, reproductive characteristics, and metabolic responses. This underscores the importance of conducting dedicated research focusing on cardiometabolic health in women.

In conclusion, this systematic review and meta-analysis provides evidence that high artificial sweetener consumption is associated with an increased risk of adverse cardiometabolic outcomes and mortality. These findings underscore the need for continued research to elucidate the long-term health effects of artificial sweeteners and to guide clinical practice and dietary recommendations regarding their use in food products. Until more definitive evidence is available, it is prudent to regard the promotion of artificial sweeteners as risk-free sugar substitutes with caution.
