Rare coexistence of isolated celiac artery dissection and superior mesenteric artery dissection: diagnosis, treatment, follow-up outcomes of a single patient and clinical implications
Authors: Sida Liu, Xinxin Xu, Zhaoyu Zhang, Kai Yang, Weiqiang Chen, Wenfeng Cao, Shuqiang Dong, Yu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSpontaneous isolated celiac artery dissection (ICAD) coexisting with spontaneous isolated superior mesenteric artery dissection (ISMAD) is exceedingly rare, and the underlying etiology, natural history, and optimal management strategy remain poorly understood.Case presentationWe report a 58-year-old man in whom contrast-enhanced computed tomography angiography (CTA) disclosed ICAD (Sakamoto type I) coexisting with ISMAD (Sakamoto type IIIa). Conservative management resulted in complete symptom resolution. Follow-up CTA at one month demonstrated stable dissections without adverse events. A systematic review of the literature was undertaken to evaluate the impact of classification systems, timing of intervention, antithrombotic strategies, and radiologic surveillance protocols on clinical outcomes.ConclusionsThese data indicate that meticulous conservative therapy coupled with rigorous imaging follow-up can yield favorable results in this rare clinical entity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13019-026-04590-z.

Background

Arterial dissection (AD) typically originates from an intimal tear induced by pulsatile shear stress, permitting blood to penetrate the tunica media and thereby generating a true lumen and a false lumen with resultant compression of the former. While non-aortic arterial dissections most frequently involve the renal and carotid arteries, spontaneous isolated visceral artery dissection (SIVAD) is a rare clinical entity; concomitant involvement of both the celiac artery (CA) and superior mesenteric artery (SMA) is exceedingly uncommon [1, 2]. The etiologic risk factors and pathologic features of this condition remain incompletely characterized [3]. Notably, Asian populations exhibit a higher incidence; however, contributing factors such as genetic susceptibility and regional dietary variations have yet to be validated by large-scale cohort studies [4, 5].

Isolated celiac artery dissection (ICAD) and isolated superior mesenteric artery dissection (ISMAD) exhibit highly similar clinical presentations, typically characterized by abdominal pain, back radiation, nausea, and vomiting [6]. Although patients with ICAD may experience more severe symptoms compared to those with ISMAD, the ultimate assessment of distal tissue ischemia, hypoxia, infarction, or necrosis relies critically on the evaluation of distal blood flow perfusion status resulting from the arterial dissection [7]. Case reports on this condition demonstrate considerable variability in patient prognosis, primarily attributed to individual differences in the degree of stenosis affecting the dissected celiac artery and superior mesenteric artery [8]. Early assessment of stenosis severity in these vessels is therefore of critical clinical importance. Multiple imaging modalities are available for evaluating abdominal vasculature, including color Doppler ultrasound, aortic contrast-enhanced CT angiography (CTA), magnetic resonance angiography, and digital subtraction angiography. Among these, aortic CTA has become the primary diagnostic tool for ICAD and ISMAD. CTA enables precise, patient-specific evaluation of true lumen compression, distal organ perfusion, and the presence of ischemia-related hypoperfusion or infarction in the liver, spleen, and gastric wall. This comprehensive assessment facilitates timely decision-making regarding conservative management, endovascular intervention, or emergency surgery, thereby optimizing patient outcomes.

Case presentation

Clinical presentation in the emergency department

The public disclosure of the following clinical data and information has been approved by the patient. A 58-year-old male was admitted for evaluation of upper abdominal pain persisting for three days. He was 174 cm in height, weighed 72 kg, with a body surface area of 1.83 m² and a BMI of 23.78. Three days prior to admission, he consumed alcohol and ingested unclean food, after which he experienced sudden-onset upper abdominal pain. Upon emergency presentation, he reported persistent distending pain localized to the epigastric and periumbilical regions, radiating to the back, with a Numerical Rating Scale pain score of 7/10, described as severe and poorly tolerated. Throughout the illness, he denied nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, melena, hematochezia, abdominal distension, or fever. Flatus was present, but no bowel movement had occurred since symptom onset. His medical history included hypertension diagnosed three years earlier, with documented peak blood pressure of 170/120 mmHg; however, he had discontinued antihypertensive therapy without medical consultation six months prior to admission. He denied known hyperlipidemia, atherosclerosis, vasculitis, or prior abdominal surgery. There was no clinical evidence of connective tissue disorders such as Marfan syndrome or Ehlers-Danlos syndrome. He had a 30-year history of both smoking and alcohol use. Upon admission, four-extremity blood pressure was measured (left upper extremity: 144/98 mmHg; right upper extremity: 149/101 mmHg; left ankle: 165/95 mmHg; right ankle: 163/89 mmHg), confirming hypertension. No significant upper-to-lower extremity blood pressure gradient was observed, and there were no clinical signs of lower extremity ischemia. These findings preliminarily exclude abdominal aortic dissection as the etiology of the patient’s abdominal pain. Bedside physical examination demonstrated tenderness in the upper abdomen, with no rebound tenderness or muscular rigidity. No abdominal masses or pulsatile abnormalities were palpated, and bowel sounds were normoactive on auscultation. Abdominal ultrasound, transthoracic echocardiography, and CTA were subsequently performed to exclude contraindications to analgesic use. Following confirmation of safety, subcutaneous administration of 5 mg morphine hydrochloride resulted in effective pain relief. Aortic CTA findings (Fig. 1, Supplementary Video.1) revealed no significant abnormalities in the thoracic or abdominal aorta. The abdominal aortic diameter was measured at 27.1 mm. A localized dissection was identified at the proximal CA—Sakamoto type I (Fig. 1A), extending over approximately 18.5 mm. At the site of dissection, focal luminal dilatation was observed, with a maximal diameter of 12.2 mm. Intimal tears were evident at both the proximal and distal ends of the false lumen, and the false lumen demonstrated homogeneous contrast enhancement, consistent with patent flow. In addition, mild luminal dilation was noted in the SMA—Sakamoto type IIIa (Fig. 1B), measuring 1.47 cm in diameter. Within the SMA lumen, an eccentric region of hypoenhancement was present, suggestive of intramural thrombus within the false lumen. The true lumen was markedly compressed, with approximately 84.13% stenosis. Notably, a localized ulcer-like projection was observed within the thrombosed segment of the false lumen at the proximal SMA, with a depth-to-width ratio of 0.56—indicative of a penetrating atherosclerotic ulcer morphology. Distal to this lesion, the SMA lumen remained patent with preserved downstream flow.

Hospitalization and inpatient management

Following admission to the ward, the patient was initiated on strict fasting and fluid restriction with continuous gastrointestinal decompression. Parenteral hyperalimentation, prophylaxis for stress-related mucosal injury, and comprehensive fluid management were implemented. Serial laboratory tests (Supplementary Figure) were performed to evaluate potential complications, including concurrent infection, hepatic or renal dysfunction, coagulopathy, and electrolyte imbalances, and targeted interventions were applied to maintain internal homeostasis based on the results. Gastric aspirates remained consistently negative for occult blood throughout monitoring, and serum lipase levels showed no evidence of acute pancreatitis or pancreatic necrosis. Serial CTA was planed to monitor the compression of the true lumen in the CA and SMA, distal perfusion status, and morphological progression of the false lumen. The decision to escalate to more aggressive intervention will be based on a comprehensive assessment of these imaging findings, combined with clinical manifestations such as worsening abdominal pain or distension.

On day 7 of fasting and fluid restriction, repeat aortic CTA (Fig. 2) revealed stable dissection of the celiac artery without significant progression, maintained via a communicating intimal tear with the true lumen. In the superior mesenteric artery, localized contrast enhancement was observed within the false lumen (approximately 1.49 cm in length), surrounded by persistent thrombus; the local arterial diameter remained unchanged at 1.47 cm, with no notable luminal expansion. Despite compression of the true lumen, antegrade blood flow was preserved and unobstructed.

The patient was subsequently initiated on a trial of oral hydration, followed by a stepwise transition to intermittent clear liquids and then semi-solid diets. After three days of close observation without recurrence of abdominal pain or distension, normal dietary intake was gradually resumed. Anticoagulation therapy with subcutaneous low-molecular-weight heparin calcium was administered throughout this phase to mitigate the risk of thrombotic occlusion at the site of true lumen stenosis and to prevent lower extremity deep vein thrombosis. Blood pressure was carefully controlled using irbesartan tablets, with antihypertensive dosage titrated according to serial blood pressure measurements. Systolic blood pressure was strictly maintained below 130 mmHg to reduce the risk of disease progression of ICAD and ISMAD. Notably, on the morning of hospital day 7, the patient developed fever. Repeat complete blood count and inflammatory markers indicated a bacterial infection (Supplementary Fig. B). Fever in this patient was considered more likely attributable to central line-associated bloodstream infection or hypostatic pneumonia, rather than absorption fever or bacterial translocation secondary to gastrointestinal ischemia. The latter was deemed less likely because it typically presents with an earlier onset and more severe clinical manifestations. The central venous catheter used for parenteral nutrition was promptly replaced, and intravenous cefuroxime sodium (1.5 g every 8 h) was initiated. Following four days of targeted antimicrobial therapy, laboratory parameters normalized, prompting the discontinuation of antibiotics. Furthermore, the absence of any changes in abdominal symptoms or evidence of occult blood in the stool effectively excluded gastrointestinal ischemia.

Condition at discharge

The patient successfully resumed a normal diet during hospitalization and was closely monitored for 7 consecutive days. Throughout this period, no significant abdominal pain or distension occurred, and bowel function remained intact with regular flatus and defecation. Vital signs—including body temperature, blood pressure, and heart rate—were consistently within normal limits, and abdominal physical examination revealed no abnormal findings. Repeat aortic CTA (Fig. 3) demonstrated stable imaging findings compared to the prior study 7 days earlier, with no evidence of ischemia in the celiac artery or distal branches of the superior mesenteric artery. Furthermore, the region of contrast enhancement within the false lumen of the superior mesenteric artery showed no appreciable change in size. Following comprehensive clinical and radiological evaluation, the patient was deemed eligible for discharge. Anticoagulation therapy was de-escalated to monotherapy with enteric-coated aspirin for antiplatelet treatment, while the previous antihypertensive regimen was maintained without modification.

Post-discharge follow-up

One month after discharge, the patient returned to the outpatient clinic as scheduled for follow-up evaluation. No significant abdominal symptoms were reported during the post-discharge period, and dietary intake remained normal. Abdominal aortic CTA was performed (Fig. 4, Supplementary Video. 2), revealing stable true and false lumens in the celiac artery, with no notable morphological changes. In the proximal superior mesenteric artery, contrast enhancement within the false lumen was markedly reduced, and there was no evidence of false lumen dilation or aneurysm formation. The local arterial diameter was remeasured at 1.40 cm, indicating a reduction compared to prior imaging. Based on these clinical and radiological findings, a structured follow-up plan was established, including regular assessment of medication adherence, monitoring of vital signs—particularly blood pressure control—and documentation of any changes in abdominal symptoms. Long-term follow-up was recommended, with instructions for the patient to seek prompt medical attention if recurrent abdominal distension or mild abdominal pain occurred. For patients with a stable clinical course, outpatient serial aortic CTA was scheduled at 3 and 6 months post-discharge, with subsequent intervals extended to annually upon confirmation of sustained stability. This imaging surveillance strategy was designed to dynamically assess the true lumen patency of the CA and SMA, as well as the compression status of the false lumen, thereby facilitating the timely identification of potential indications for surgical intervention.

Discussion

SIVAD typically refers to isolated dissection of the celiac artery or superior mesenteric artery in the absence of aortic dissection. This condition is clinically rare; however, with advances in imaging technology—particularly the widespread use of CTA—its detection rate has steadily increased [9]. The predominant patient population consists of middle-aged men, who account for approximately 80% of cases, and these individuals frequently present with comorbid hypertension and a history of smoking [6, 10]. The clinical manifestations of this condition are predominantly characterized by acute or chronic upper abdominal pain, frequently radiating to the back, and may be associated with nausea, vomiting, or anorexia. Approximately 50% of cases are diagnosed incidentally during imaging examinations [2]. A meta-analysis reported that the incidence of SMAD was higher than that of ICAD, with SMAD accounting for approximately 60% (95% CI: 50%–71%) and ICAD for about 37% (95% CI: 27%–46%) [6]. It is noteworthy that simultaneous involvement of both the celiac trunk and the superior mesenteric artery is exceedingly rare. In such cases, the risk of mesenteric infarction is significantly elevated, likely due to inadequate compensation by collateral circulation—such as the pancreaticoduodenal arteries—for the dual impairment of blood supply. The exact etiology of SIVAD remains incompletely understood. Accumulating evidence indicates that its pathogenesis is closely associated with atherosclerosis, medial degeneration of the vessel wall, and abnormal mechanical stress. Park et al. proposed that the SMA exhibits relatively greater mobility at the inferior border of the pancreas, creating a biomechanical disparity with the adjacent fixed segment. This discrepancy may predispose the vessel to abnormal shear stress, ultimately leading to arterial dissection [11]. The patient described in our report presents with established risk factors such as male gender, hypertension, and a history of smoking, all of which have been corroborated by existing studies. Typically, the CA arises from the aorta at a right angle, whereas the SMA originates from the distal aorta at a 45° angle. Research has demonstrated that an angle between the SMA and the distal aorta exceeding 70° is associated with a higher likelihood of developing ISMAD [12]. We hypothesize that an increased angle between the branch artery and the distal aorta increases hemodynamic shear stress at the branch artery ostium, leading to intimal injury and even rupture, which may represent one of the common pathogenic mechanisms underlying ISMAD [13]. In contrast, the etiology of ICAD may be more complex. In this particular case (Fig. 5), the CA originated from the distal aorta at an angle of 55.89° rather than the typical 90°, exhibiting angular variation with a 35.22° right-anterior deviation. Furthermore, another angulation was present 1.52 cm from the celiac artery ostium (angle between proximal and distal segments: 127.23°), where the development of arterial dissection may be related to altered hemodynamic shear stress. And the SMA and distal aorta was 67.11°—values that are approaching, yet still below, the threshold reported in the literature. Given this borderline anatomical configuration, in conjunction with established risk factors and the potential ethnic susceptibility within Asian populations, the SIVAD in this patient is likely multifactorial in etiology. The acute onset was presumably precipitated by a transient hypertensive episode secondary to dietary indiscretion three days prior to presentation.

The Sakamoto classification system is widely used in the imaging evaluation of ISMAD. In type I (both true and false lumens patent, with identifiable entry and re-entry sites) and type II (the false lumen is blind-ended, lacking a re-entry point), arterial blood flow is present within the false lumen. In contrast, types III and IV are characterized by thrombosed false lumens with no perfusion: type III involves partial thrombosis with ulcer-like protrusions—classified as IIIa when limited to the proximal segment and IIIb when involving both proximal and distal segments—while type IV features complete thrombosis of the false lumen without ulcer-like protrusions, resulting in absent arterial blood flow. Some scholars have further proposed type V (aneurysmal dissection with distal stenosis) and type VI (occlusion of the celiac artery or superior mesenteric artery), offering more refined guidance for improved disease characterization and therapeutic decision-making [14]. As the lesion anatomical features of ICAD are quite similar to those of ISMAD, some studies have recommended extending the Sakamoto classification system to the classification research of SIVAD [15]. We also contend that applying this classification method to SIVAD may facilitate prognostic prediction for various aortic branch dissections. Emerging evidence has demonstrated a significant association between SIVAD classification and clinical prognosis. A study analyzing patient characteristics and outcomes revealed that Sakamoto type IV is significantly more likely to undergo vascular remodeling (OR = 11.26, p = 0.039), whereas type II shows minimal potential for remodeling (OR = 0, p = 0.05). This difference may be attributed to the greater structural stability of the false lumen in type IV dissections [10]. It is noteworthy that ICAD and ISMAD exhibit significant differences in anatomical characteristics. Specifically, ISMAD lesions tend to be substantially longer in length, whereas ICAD is more frequently associated with Sakamoto type II morphology and carries a higher risk of late-phase progression [6, 10]. Therefore, precise classification and assessment of SIVAD are essential for optimizing long-term prognostic evaluation and guiding clinical decision-making.

Currently, no treatment guidelines are available that are based on high-level evidence. However, multiple studies indicate that therapeutic strategies should be individualized according to symptom severity, dissection morphology, and the presence of complications such as intestinal ischemia or aneurysm formation. Conservative management of SIVAD, comprising diet resistance, parenteral nutrition, pain control, and antihypertensive therapy, is generally indicated for hemodynamically stable patients (Type I, IIa, IIIa, and IVa) [15, 16]. Conversely, early intervention—including open surgery, hybrid revascularization, and endovascular stenting—is often reserved for patients with recurrent symptoms, poor visceral perfusion (Type IIb, IIIb, and IVb), or aneurysm formation (Type V) [15]. Ultimately, the choice of treatment strategy is largely influenced by the treating physician’s clinical judgment and experience [14]. Early fasting and fluid restriction are clinically necessary, as they reduce the functional burden on the digestive system during sudden reductions in blood supply, thereby facilitating the transition from acute hypoxic-ischemic injury to a more tolerable physiological state. While most evidence supports that initial conservative management benefits the majority of patients, and a meta-analysis has shown that asymptomatic individuals undergoing conservative treatment do not face an increased risk of requiring subsequent intervention, emerging data still indicate that patients with persistent symptoms following conservative therapy are more likely to require endovascular intervention [7, 10, 13, 14]. Regarding the timing of surgical intervention in SIVAD, current evidence supports intervention only for patients with persistently worsening symptoms, signs of end-organ ischemia, or risk of aneurysm rupture. Furthermore, in patients with Sakamoto type II disease, active intervention may be warranted in the presence of severe true lumen stenosis or documented enlargement of the false lumen aneurysm (> 2 cm) [6]. The dissection itself often presents with prominent abdominal pain, which may complicate the diagnosis of abdominal organ ischemia. Therefore, meticulous interpretation of CTA findings is essential for assessing vascular patency and identifying indirect signs of impaired visceral perfusion, such as bowel wall thickening. Furthermore, serial monitoring of laboratory markers—including white blood cell count, liver and renal function tests, and serum amylase levels—can provide valuable evidence to guide decisions regarding the need for active intervention. Moreover, a lower true lumen residual ratio a greater extent of dissection involvement, and an increased arterial angulation are recognized as potential predictors of conservative treatment failure and subsequent requirement for interventional therapy [17]. Therefore, based on the serial CTA findings, this case was diagnosed as ICAD (Sakamoto type I) combined with an ISMAD (Sakamoto type IIIa). In light of previous studies demonstrating the favorable morphological characteristics of SIVAD, a conservative management strategy was adopted. This approach aligns with the therapeutic principles of optimizing treatment efficacy while minimizing intervention-related morbidity.

In recent years, 3D printing technology, with its core strengths of precise patient-specific anatomical replication and controllable fabrication, has exhibited substantial application potential in the precision diagnosis and treatment of aortic dissection and visceral artery dissections involving the SMA and celiac trunk [18]. Its clinical value is reflected not only in that radiological reconstruction and solid modeling can be performed based on CTA imaging data, which enables 1:1 accurate replication of the true and false lumen morphology, intimal tear distribution, and extent of branch vessel involvement in individual patients; when combined with hemodynamic analysis, this technique can quantitatively assess the risk of aortic rupture, retrograde dissection, and disease progression, overcome the limitations of conventional two-dimensional imaging in conveying spatial anatomical information, and provide objective radiological and biomechanical evidence for accurate risk stratification of dissection and individualized optimization of preoperative intervention strategies, but also in the promising application prospects of patient-specific endovascular stent grafts fabricated via 3D printing [19]. Such customized stent grafts can achieve optimal anatomical matching with the individual morphological features of the aorta and visceral branch arteries, and their mechanical properties can be precisely tuned through material and structural design to match the biomechanical characteristics of native vessels, which is expected to further mitigate the risk of retrograde dissection induced by stent-vessel stiffness mismatch in future clinical applications; meanwhile, the stents can be functionalized with bioactive components to promote the post-implantation endothelialization process, thereby ameliorating long-term stability deficits including stent graft malapposition and reduced patency caused by impaired endothelialization, and ultimately realizing precise and safe endovascular management of visceral artery dissection [20].

The choice between an antiplatelet-based and anticoagulation-based treatment strategy remains uncertain. Although two cohort studies have shown that antithrombotic therapy confers no significant benefit in clinical or morphological outcomes for ISMAD, no randomized controlled trials have directly compared the short-term and long-term efficacy of these approaches. Therefore, this issue warrants further investigation and critical discussion [14]. In this case, recanalization of the thrombosed false lumen during treatment may be associated with early anticoagulation using low-molecular-weight heparin. Prior to thrombus stabilization, processes such as organization or recanalization may reduce intraluminal pressure within the compressed true lumen while improving distal perfusion. However, this phenomenon could potentially compromise the structural integrity of the local vessel wall. Furthermore, in the presence of residual flow within the false lumen, anticoagulation may more effectively maintain luminal patency, although the bleeding risk must be carefully balanced. Upon discharge, antiplatelet therapy was initiated. Once the false lumen thrombus has stabilized, antiplatelet agents can effectively prevent platelet adhesion and aggregation at sites of true lumen compression, thereby preserving long-term true lumen patency. Nevertheless, ongoing follow-up remains essential to monitor for late complications such as arterial stenosis or embolism.

Long-term, regular CTA surveillance with dynamic comparison of imaging data is essential for patients presenting with ICAD, ISMAD, or concurrent involvement. We recommend dynamic imaging assessments at the time of onset and subsequently every 7 days during the acute phase. Should clinical symptoms change—such as the sudden onset of abdominal pain, distension, nausea, or vomiting—prompt imaging should be performed to clarify the diagnosis. This approach facilitates a rapid and comprehensive evaluation of the entire aorta and its branches, enabling the early detection of localized arterial dilation and the assessment of long-term perfusion in the affected vascular territories. Furthermore, for patients with a stable clinical course, serial aortic CTA at 1 and 6 months post-discharge is warranted. If continuous monitoring demonstrates no significant progression of SIVAD during this period, the follow-up interval may be appropriately extended to annually. Furthermore, studies have demonstrated that transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) is a safe and feasible modality for evaluating the upper abdominal aortic segment, proximal celiac artery, and superior mesenteric artery. This imaging approach may offer particular advantages for patients with severe contrast allergies or renal insufficiency, in whom repeated CTA is contraindicated or carries elevated risk [21]. In the last few years, technological developments in the medical field have been rapid and are continuously evolving. One of the most revolutionary breakthroughs was the introduction of the Internet of Things (IoT) concept within medical practice [22]. The IoT enables the construction of a perioperative real-time monitoring system through the implantation of multimodal sensing devices within the body. Implantable arterial pressure sensors utilized for postoperative follow-up of endovascular repair in aneurysms or dissections offer the advantage of real-time monitoring of sac pressure changes compared to CTA, and may become an important follow-up modality for significantly reducing delayed aortic rupture in the future.

Conclusion

Spontaneous isolated dissection of the CA and SMA is a rare but clinically significant vascular condition. Management should be individualized according to symptom severity, anatomical classification, and risk of complications. The majority of patients achieve favorable outcomes with conservative medical therapy; however, long-term follow-up remains essential for early detection of late complications. Future large-scale prospective studies are warranted to better define optimal antithrombotic strategies, intervention thresholds, and the influence of racial differences on disease progression and natural history.
