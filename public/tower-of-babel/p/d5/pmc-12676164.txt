How Soon Is Too Soon? Triaging Inpatient Transthoracic Echocardiogram Orders to Reduce Turnaround Times

Transthoracic echocardiograms (TTEs) are a common noninvasive cardiac imaging modality that provides information about cardiac structure and function. At our tertiary care center, there were a total of 7,439 inpatient TTEs ordered in 2023, a substantial increase from the 5,203 TTEs ordered in 2018 (Table 1). The increasing burden of inpatient TTE orders over the years has led to delays in performing urgently indicated studies, affected scanning workflow, overall turnaround time, and length of hospital stay.

Project Rationale

As our medical center gradually expanded to accommodate more beds over the years, there has also been an accompanying rise in both annual total ED visits and number of inpatient admissions. These trends were met with increasing numbers of total annual TTE orders. In addition, the gradual increase in cardiovascular disease prevalence over the years may also have contributed to the observed increases in total echo study orders. It is reported that approximately 10% to 15% of noninvasive cardiac imaging is ordered for rarely appropriate indications.1 However, even among studies ordered for appropriate indications, there still remains the issue of ordering studies with the inappropriate priority level.

At our institution, there are 3 main TTE priority levels: “STAT” TTEs are urgently indicated and require verbal authorization from a cardiology fellow or attending; “pending discharge” (PD) TTEs are intended for patients who will be eligible for discharge as soon as their TTE is reviewed; and “routine” is used for all other inpatient TTE requests. The PD TTE category was created with the intention to facilitate inpatient throughput as our hospital system grew and echo volumes rose. It was intended to allow for further prioritization among non-STAT TTE orders and to help facilitate patients whose discharge and follow-up planning depended on the findings of the echo studies. PD TTEs may be particularly useful for obtaining specific diagnoses, such as for new heart failure or as part of syncope workup when the clinical exam and history are consistent with cardiogenic causes.

Close examination of PD TTE orders revealed that some of these were being ordered inappropriately, such as PD TTEs ordered for intubated patients in the intensive care unit, as part of preoperative management, and for patients better suited for routine TTEs. It was theorized that providers were inappropriately utilizing the PD TTE priority in an attempt to bypass the inpatient TTE queue and prioritize their orders over TTEs ordered as routine, while avoiding the authorization required from cardiology to order a STAT TTE. The growing proportion of PD TTE orders over routine led to further increases in turnaround times to acquire routine TTEs and delayed downstream patient care management including discharge.

Project Description

Our goal was to reduce the proportion of PD TTEs being inappropriately ordered and to decrease the turnaround time to acquire routine TTEs. The turnaround time to acquire TTEs was defined as the number of hours between the time of TTE order and the time the images are obtained.

Starting in September 2023, we reviewed the monthly number of PD and routine TTE orders, in addition to the mean turnaround time to complete scanning. A multidisciplinary team meeting was held with the key stakeholders including cardiology fellow representatives, the medical director of our echocardiography laboratory, the cardiac sonographer managers, inpatient service physician leaders, and clinical application specialists.

A previous study showed that an educational intervention on appropriate use criteria for TTEs may have the potential to reduce the number of rarely appropriate TTEs being ordered by practitioners.2 In hopes of obtaining a similar result, on January 1, 2024, we initiated the first intervention consisting of an educational flyer distributed throughout hospital workrooms, to reinforce appropriate criteria for ordering PD TTEs (Figure 1). In addition, the leadership of key departments from which large volumes of TTE orders are generated was notified and instructed to distribute the flyer and its information. Data from January 2024 till May 2024 were analyzed to assess the results of our first intervention.

Educational Flyer

Printouts were distributed across the hospital and included appropriateness criteria for ordering pending discharge echos. ED = emergency department.

After reconvening with the multidisciplinary team, our second intervention, which featured an electronic medical record (EMR) system hard stop was implemented in June 1, 2024 (Figure 2). This EMR change prevented PD TTE orders from being signed without a documented estimated discharge date (EDD) in the EMR, a parameter that is discussed and updated daily after each medical team's case manager rounds. Post-EMR intervention data was collected from June 2024 till the end of October 2024 (phase 1 post intervention), and for an additional 5 months till March 2025 (phase 2 post intervention).

Project Deliverables

Throughout the various time periods featured in the project, data were analyzed for several key parameters using the nonparametric Kruskal-Wallis test applied to mean turnaround time to acquire PD TTEs, mean turnaround time to acquire routine TTEs, and proportion of PD TTEs from total TTE orders. These results were compared before and after each intervention period to assess improved outcomes.

Project Outcome

Starting from September to December 2023, preintervention data showed, on average, 27.25% of all inpatient TTEs were ordered as PD priority (Figure 3). During this preintervention period, the mean turnaround time to acquire routine TTEs and PD TTEs were 27.79 hours and 7.81 hours, respectively (Figure 4). After the first intervention initiated on January 2024, which combined the educational flyer distribution and notification of department leadership, there was an unanticipated increase in the proportion of PD TTEs to 31.4% and an increase in mean turnaround time to acquire both routine (21.63% increase to 33.8 hours) and PD TTEs (25.18% increase to 9.77 hours).

The second intervention implementing the EMR system hard stop in June 2024 (Figure 3) led to statistically significant decreases in the proportion of PD TTEs (31.4% to 17%), and a decrease in turnaround time to acquire both routine (17.03 hours from 33.8 hours) and PD TTEs (7.052 hours from 9.77 hours).

Data was collected for an additional 5 months in the final phase of the study (phase 2 post intervention) and compared to the preceding phase. The proportion of PD TTEs ordered remained consistent (17% vs 17.2%), and the mean turnaround time to acquire routine and PD TTEs decreased by an additional 10.37% and 2.84%, respectively. Comparing the pre-EMR intervention months with the phase 2 postintervention months revealed a statistically significant further decrease in turnaround time to acquire PD TTEs (9.8 hours vs 6.8 hours).

Discussion

Increased PD TTE volumes have contributed to the increased turnaround time to acquire routine TTEs at our tertiary care center as they redirected limited sonographer time and resources. The educational intervention failed to accomplish our goal of reducing the proportion of PD TTE orders and reducing the mean image acquisition time for routine TTEs, the priority class which constitutes the majority of inpatient TTE orders.

Analysis of our data collected in the final phase (phase 2 post EMR intervention) of the study showed minimal differences from the post-EMR intervention period, but similar improvements when compared to the preintervention period. These results support continued improvements in TTE image-acquisition workflow with a lasting impact in the improvements seen after the implementation of the EMR hard stop in January 1, 2025.

Working as a multidisciplinary team was essential in addition to the development of an EMR hard stop to reduce PD TTE orders and improve turnaround time for overall TTE completion. The success of our intervention was influenced by daily assessment of EDD, which encouraged discussion between providers, case managers, and their teams regarding patient discharge dates. Anticipated EDDs are agreed upon dates that are formed after daily case manager rounding in which discussions are held between physician teams and their case managers. In this way, the case manager served as an independent party to help provide more accurate EDDs with which sonographers are able to work with. In addition, having an accurate EDD allowed sonographers to better prioritize and triage among PD TTE orders by scanning those who are closest to their EDD.

Future directions include further examination of potential downstream impact by these interventions on the length of hospital stay and improvement on appropriate use of TTE priority orders.

Conclusions

An EMR-based intervention requiring a documented EDD prior to ordering a PD TTE significantly reduced both the proportion of PD TTEs ordered and the turnaround time to acquire routine and PD TTE studies. EMR intervention was more effective than our educational intervention at accomplishing these outcomes. EMR-based interventions to improve inpatient triaging may be an effective strategy for reducing turnaround times for acquiring inpatient imaging studies.
