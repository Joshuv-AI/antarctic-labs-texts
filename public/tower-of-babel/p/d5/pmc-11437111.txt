Declustering characteristics of the North China Plain seismic belt and its effect on probabilistic seismic hazard analysis
Authors: Jinmeng Bi, Cheng Song, Fuyang Cao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The accurate and impartial identification of background seismic activity is an important foundation for earthquake model construction and probabilistic seismic hazard analysis (PSHA). We use the Gardner and Knopoff, Reasenberg, nearest-neighbor and stochastic declustering methods to decluster in the North China Plain seismic belt, analyzing the effect of declustering. We optimize the input parameters of the algorithms through Kolmogorov-Smirnov Poisson distribution tests (e.g., the clustering threshold of the nearest-neighbor method is set to 0.6). Four algorithms demonstrate good declustering effects, removing the impact of the strong seismicity in Tangshan while retaining similar trends to all events. The occurrence of major earthquake events can significantly impact the declustering characteristics in terms of time, space and magnitude. There is a difference in the overall hazard between the four declustering algorithms and the Fifth Generation Seismic Ground Motion Parameters Zonation Map of China. The difference in seismic hazard curves is mainly influenced by the annual average occurrence rate (background rates) and the Gutenberg-Richter b value, with the effect of the b value being more pronounced. The analysis of the effect of algorithms and their impact on PSHA can provide reference for earthquake risk assessment, engineering seismic design and disaster research.

Introduction

Probabilistic seismic hazard analysis (PSHA) is commonly based on the approach described by Cornell1, which assumes earthquake occurrence times to be representable by a stationary Poisson process. The purpose of declustering is to eliminate correlation factors between seismic events, so that the occurrence of earthquakes to satisfy the characteristics of a stationary and independent Poisson model2. While background earthquakes are typically related to long-term tectonic phenomena, triggering events are mainly attributed to stress changes caused by previous events3. The choice of declustering algorithms significantly impacts regional activity parameters and PSHA4. How we can effectively identify background earthquakes that reflect the trend of seismic activity is crucial for analyzing the evolution of seismicity characteristics before strong earthquakes5,6, inverting the background stress field source mechanism7,8, and estimating seismic hazard9–13.

Currently, we considered four of the most popular declustering methods: the Gardner and Knopoff method14, the Reasenberg method15, the nearest-neighbor method5,16, and the stochastic declustering method17 based on the epidemic-type aftershock sequence (ETAS) model18,19. The Gardner and Knopoff method is widely applied extensively in seismic hazard models in regions and countries such as France20, Turkey21 and Europe22. However, due to all events within the time and space window after a strong earthquake will be deleted, the declustering ratio is too high in the short term23. The Reasenberg method considers stress redistribution and the Omori law to determine the spatio-temporal interactions between earthquakes. It has been applied in seismic hazard studies in Ecuador24 and Afghanistan25. Zaliapin and Ben-Zion5 introduce an algorithm for declustering earthquake catalogs based on the nearest-neighbor analysis of seismicity, which discriminates between background and clustered events by random thinning that removes events according to a space‐varying threshold. The stochastic declustering method17 is the probability-based declustering method based on the ETAS model. It goes beyond simply classifying each earthquake as either a background event or a triggering event and provides the probability of each event being a background event. This algorithm avoids the subjectivity associated with the selection of initial parameters and has become increasingly popular26–28.

The seismic activity parameter b value describes the proportional distribution characteristics of large and small earthquakes, but it can vary with time, region, depth and stress state29. It is also significantly influenced by declustering methods30–33. The background seismic activity parameter b value is the long-term cumulative result over many years, which causes some differences between the b values of background events and cluster events. For example, Gulia and Wiemer34 found that the average b value in aftershock sequences is higher than background events by 20%, while in the North-South seismic belt of China, the b value of background events is higher than aftershock events35. Mizrahi et al.13 studied the impact of declustering on b value in California since 1980 and found that declustering reduced the b value by 30% compared to an undeclustered catalog. Pastoressa et al.6 applied the stochastic declustering method to the central region of Italy, and the study found that the lower the clustering degree, the higher the b value. Moreover, Mizrahi et al.13 found that the effect of declustering on b value is more significant in synthetic earthquake catalogs because all declustering algorithms are based on the assumption of isotropic spatial distribution of aftershocks, which is very different from the actual situation, more small events can be removed from the synthetic earthquake catalogs.

The Gutenberg-Richter relationship is used to extrapolate the rate of smaller, more commonly observed earthquakes to larger and more damaging ones. The Gutenberg-Richter b value plays a crucial role in PSHA because it influences the probability distribution of earthquake magnitudes for each seismic source. A higher b value means a greater number of small earthquakes relative to large ones, which can affect the overall seismic hazard assessment. In PSHA, the b value is used to estimate the frequency of earthquakes of different magnitudes. This, in turn, affects the calculation of the probability of exceeding various levels of ground shaking at the site. Accurate estimation of the b value is essential for reliable PSHA results, which can lead to both overestimation and underestimation of hazard. Declustering may lead to significant underestimation of the true seismic hazard. Eroglu Azak et al.12 found the impact of declustering on Istanbul’s risk assessment that declustering resulted in a change of up to 20% in peak ground acceleration (PGA). It is also necessary that the seismicity rates be corrected for compensating the rate reduction due to declustering, Marzocchi and Taroni11 propose a methodology that can be used in PSHA to avoid this potential bias. The ROBERE (Regionally Optimized Background Earthquake Rates from ETAS) method36 estimates b values from the non-declustered catalog and uses the declustered catalogue for the spatial model. Moreover, van Stiphout et al.37 found that the choice of declustering method has a major effect on seismicity rate change estimations.

The PSHA of the North China Plain seismic belt, which has undergone five updates in 1957, 1977, 1990, 2001 and 2015, respectively. The space-time window method was employed for declustering in these updates, it usually causes a large area of events within the space-time window to be removed after a strong earthquake, resulting in a higher number of deleted aftershocks23,38. At present, we are preparing for the sixth update, and the new generation of PSHA urgently needs to perform more reliable data declustering analysis to obtain reliable seismicity parameters.

In this article, we combined K-S Poisson test to optimize the initial parameters of the declustering algorithm, analyzed the declustering effect of each algorithm, and obtained the change characteristics of the declustering ratio of the North China Plain seismic belt. We have analyzed the effect of seismic activity parameters and PSHA, which can better serve the sixth generation zoning map of China, practical earthquake disaster prevention and relief work.

Structure background and data

The North China Plain seismic belt is a seismically active region in the eastern Chinese mainland. It is one of the main areas of strong seismicity in North China, located in the North China craton area. The subsurface contains multiple geological structural units intersecting, developing a series of new seismic belts. The North China Plain seismic belt experiences frequent crustal movements, with plates squeezing and moving against each other, causing deformation and stress accumulation. When this stress reaches a certain level, it triggers earthquakes. The combination of these factors leads to a relatively concentrated seismic activity. The North China Plain seismic belt has experienced several strong earthquakes, such as the 1679 Sanhe-Pinggu M8.0 earthquake, the 1830 Cixian M7.5 earthquake, the 1966 southeastern Ningjin M7.2 earthquake, and the 1976 Tangshan M7.8 earthquake. These frequent strong seismic activities have caused huge casualties and economic losses.

To systematically investigate the effect of declustering in the North China Plain seismic belt and its effect on the PSHA, we utilized the earthquake catalog “National Unified Formal Catalog” provided by the China Earthquake Administration from January 1, 1970 to December 31, 2023. During this period, there are 2510 earthquakes with M ≥ 3.0 recorded in the North China Plain seismic belt, including 1359 earthquakes of M3.0 ~ 3.9, 1038 earthquakes of M4.0 ~ 4.9, 106 earthquakes of M5.0 ~ 5.9, and 7 earthquakes of M ≥ 6.0. The largest earthquake was the Tangshan M7.8 earthquake on July 28, 1976. Figure 1a shows the distribution of epicenters. The distribution of significant earthquakes mainly concentrated in the northern of the North China Plain seismic belt (Tangshan seismic zone). To better quantify the concentration of spatial earthquake numbers, Fig. 1b provides the spatial distribution of earthquake numbers, showing some spatial differences. From a spatial perspective, the seismic activity is mainly influenced by the strong earthquakes such as the 1976 Tangshan M7.8 earthquake, the 1981 Xingtai M6.1 earthquake, and the 1983 Heze M6.2 earthquake, leading to relatively strong seismic activity within certain regional ranges.

Method

The declustering results of the earthquake catalog are primarily used for constructing long-term earthquake forecasting models and PSHA. A key assumption in conducting these research works is that the occurrence of earthquake events follows a Poisson distribution in time, and a significance level of α = 0.05 is used to determine whether it conforms to a Poisson distribution by Kolmogorov-Smirnov (K-S) test. Furthermore, in order to preserve as many events as possible, removing less data can better represent the actual seismic productivity. Therefore, in the application of declustering algorithms, various optimization techniques were employed to ensure compliance with the Poisson distribution while retaining a maximum number of seismic events. The declustering process typically includes earthquake catalog data preparation, setting criteria for different algorithm background events, identification of background events (parameter setting and optimization), removal of cluster events, and iterative calculations.

Gardner and Knopoff

Gardner and Knopoff14 grouped earthquakes based on the spatio-temporal distance. For each cluster, the event with the highest magnitude is designated as the mainshock, and events within the space-time window of the mainshock are removed. For each event M, if subsequent events occur within specific time interval T(M) and distance interval L(M), they are identified as aftershocks. This method is reset based on the magnitude of the largest earthquake in the sequence, and usually does not distinguish between direct and indirect aftershocks. This method is a simple and widely used declustering algorithm. However, because all background events within the calculation window are assumed to be aftershocks, it may lead to the omission of some background events.

Reasenberg

Reasenberg15 developed an interaction zone, specified by spatio-temporal distances, centered at each event. The largest event is considered as the mainshock, while other events are regarded as aftershocks. Specifically, if event A is the mainshock for event B, and event B is the mainshock for event C, then events A, B, and C are all considered to belong to the same cluster, with the largest earthquake in the cluster being designated as the mainshock and the smaller earthquakes classified as foreshocks and aftershocks. Nearest-neighbor

The nearest-neighbor method5,16 is a commonly used research method for studying the spatio-temporal relationships of seismic activities. It infers the clustering of seismic events by calculating the distances between each event and its nearest neighbor, and analyzes the evolutionary patterns of seismic activities. Zaliapin et al.46 developed a method for partitioning earthquakes using the nearest neighbor approach based on the spatio-temporal distances between events proposed by Baiesi and Paczuski47, which was further developed in subsequent works5,16. The proximity of earthquake j to previous earthquake i is asymmetric in time and is defined as46,47.6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda (t,x,y) = \mu (x,y) + \sum\limits_{{i:t_{i} < t}} {\kappa (m_{i} )g(t - t_{i} )f(x - x_{i} ,y - y_{i} |m_{i} )}$$\end{document}Here, ηij is the spatio-temporal distance between events i and j, tij is the event intercurrence time, which is positive if event j occurred after event i; rij ≥ 0 is the spatial distance between the earthquake hypocenters (or epicenters); d is the fractal dimension of the hypocenters (or epicenters); and w is the parameter that introduces exponential weight of the earlier event i by its magnitude; M is the magnitude of event i. We identify seismic events by calculating the spatio-temporal distance, finding the nearest neighbor for each event that occurred before them, and generating a bimodal distribution of nearest neighbor distance5.

The cluster threshold α0 directly controls the number of background events in a declustered catalog, and it basically does not affect the relative spatial intensity of background events, it only affects the overall background intensity5. In practice, this algorithm only requires three input parameters (d, w, α0, where α0 is the clustering threshold, typically chosen close to zero for clustering purposes), with two parameters (d and w) having no significant impact on the output declustered catalog5. On the other hand, the nearest-neighbor method is most sensitive to the cluster threshold α0, ensuring stability of relative background intensities at different locations and filling in numerous gaps near mainshocks. The determination of threshold parameters can be based on the total number of background events (e.g., “retain 50% of the catalog”) or test results (e.g., maximum number of background events not rejected by Poisson test), but this process involves subjective judgment without universal rules, depending on the actual seismic catalog and specific issues at hand. We have tested the clustering threshold to meet the conditions of maximizing the number of background events and fitting a Poisson distribution, providing the post-clustering background number based on the threshold values, as shown in Table 2. The P values of various thresholds were calculated using K-S Poisson test, with a clustering threshold of 0.6 selected as the input parameter for subsequent declustering analysis.

Stochastic declustering

Zhuang et al.17 proposed a stochastic declustering method based on the space-time ETAS model18,19, which assumes that each event is an independent event and gives the background event probability, without distinguishing between foreshocks, mainshocks, and aftershocks. In the application process of an ETAS-based stochastic declustering method, the algorithm uses maximum likelihood estimation to estimate 8 parameters, providing the best-fitting parameters converged to the input catalog, eliminating the subjectivity of parameter setting. However, the algorithm is highly computationally complex, and requires abundant computing resources and time for simulation and analysis. The stochastic declustering method assigns the first independent earthquake (not necessarily the mainshock) in the cluster to the background17, so that the largest events generated in the declustering catalog could have very small background probabilities and will be removed as cluster events, which may lead to bias in the occurrence probability of large earthquake events. In addition, since the stochastic declustering method obtains the probability of each event as a background event, there is a significant variability in possible background catalogs. Analysis of declustering results

To accurately analyze the declustering effects of four algorithms, we conducted declustering processing using the “optimal” parameters for the Reasenberg, nearest-neighbor and stochastic declustering algorithms, as well as the default window of Gardner and Knopoff algorithm14, in the North China Plain seismic belt. Due to significant strong seismic activities, earthquakes exhibit certain space-time clustering characteristics. Figure 2 shows the changing features of declustering results for seismic events with M ≥ 3.0 in the North China Plain seismic belt. From the trend of cumulative seismic events over time (Fig. 2a), it is evident that the clustering of seismic events in the North China Plain seismic belt was primarily influenced by the 1976 Tangshan M7.8 earthquake sequence. All four declustering algorithms mitigated the clustering events during the active period of Tangshan aftershocks, demonstrating a relatively stable trend. The declustering ratios for the Gardner and Knopoff, Reasenberg, nearest-neighbor and stochastic declustering methods were 72.95%, 53.67%, 64.10% and 65.22%, respectively. Among the four declustering algorithms, the Reasenberg algorithm tends to remove a lower proportion, the Gardner and Knopoff algorithm typically removes a higher proportion, while the other two algorithms show certain variability across different regions4,33,49,50.

Seismicity declustering characteristics in time series. The variation of cumulative earthquake number (a), earthquake frequency (b) over time. The color of the solid line represents different declustering algorithms.

Figure 2b illustrates the changing characteristics of annual event frequencies over time, to describe the declustering effects for each time period. It is observed that the number of seismic events significantly decreased during active periods after declustering, revealing high declustering ratios during these periods. Overall, the declustering results exhibit some similarities in changing trends with the total events. However, the unique characteristics of each algorithm lead to differences in declustering effects during different time periods. For example, the Gardner and Knopoff method results in fewer background seismic events due to a higher declustering proportion, thus showing less correlation with the overall event trends and demonstrating a relatively small changing trend. Each time period shows certain similarities, with the 1976 Tangshan M7.8 earthquake sequence causing a significantly increase in the total number. However, the declustering algorithm removed short-term aftershock events after significant earthquake, resulting in a relatively stable trend for background events. Around 1980, there was another increase in earthquake numbers, primarily due to the restoration of monitoring capability in the Tangshan area to pre-earthquake levels (with a significant increase in recorded minor seismic events) and enhanced background activity in the Xingtai region (located in the central part of the North China Plain seismic belt), leading to a relatively high level of background events. Therefore, the occurrence of significant events can lead to a significantly increase in the total earthquake number, but except for the Reasenberg algorithm, the other three algorithms have essentially removed the impact of significant events.

The four declustering algorithms are based on different assumptions and definitions, which may start with identifying clusters and then selecting a single event from each cluster as background (and remove all other events as clustered). Due to the algorithms having different conventions for background events, the two most popular choices are the first event or the largest event. For instance, the stochastic declustering method considers the first event within a cluster (which is not necessarily the mainshock) as the background event, whereas other algorithms regard the largest event in each cluster (i.e., the mainshock) as the background seismic event. This leads to variability in the declustering results. Figure 3 presents the spatial declustering results of the four algorithms, which exhibit significant consistency. Clustered events are notably influenced by strong seismic events, such as the 1976 Tangshan M7.8 earthquake, the 1981 Xingtai M6.1 earthquake, and the 1983 Heze M6.2 earthquake, which displays distinct regional characteristics.

To quantify the variation characteristics of declustering ratios for each period, we categorized into decades and conducted statistical analysis. As shown in Fig. 4a, during the period from 1970 to 1979, the declustering ratios of the four declustering algorithms were relatively high, with an average of 83.64%. In the period from 1980 to 1989, there was a significant difference in declustering ratios among the four algorithms, with the nearest-neighbor and stochastic declustering methods being more similar (58.41%, 55.04%), the Gardner and Knopoff method having a higher proportion (74.69%), and the Reasenberg method relatively lower (41.24%). After 1990, the declustering ratios of the Gardner and Knopoff, nearest-neighbor and stochastic declustering methods fluctuated around 40%, while the ratio of the Reasenberg method remained below 20% after 2000. Overall, the four declustering algorithms show a certain downward trend over time. The declustering ratio declined rapidly before 2000, mainly due to the impact of aftershocks of the Tangshan M7.8 earthquake on July 28, 1976. The earthquake occurrence ratio in the Tangshan area after 1997 has had little association with the Tangshan M7.8 earthquake51, and the declustering ratio has tended to stabilize thereafter, indicating that the North China Plain seismic belt has been less affected by strong earthquakes since then. Therefore, the occurrence of major events, particularly those with a high number of aftershocks, can significantly impact the temporal declustering characteristics of the sequence, leading to a significant increase in the declustering ratio in the early post-earthquake period.

Effects of declustering on seismic hazard

Effects of declustering on the seismicity parameter

The seismicity parameter b value is one of the important parameters in earthquake activity modeling and seismic hazard assessment. The Gutenberg-Richter (G-R) relation53, which describes the magnitude frequency relationship, can be well used to forecast the number of earthquake events. It can be expressed as:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\text{l}\text{g}N=a-bM$$\end{document}where M and N denote the earthquake magnitude and the cumulative number of earthquakes exceeding M, respectively. The a value represents the overall seismicity level. The b value has been proven to be negatively correlated with stress in the Earth’s crust54,55, making indirectly reflect the physical processes of earthquake generation. The significant variations in the seismic activity parameter b value, along with its relationship with the physical properties of earthquake ruptures (such as source depth), tectonic environment and stress evolution characteristics, make it an important parameter for determining the occurrence of earthquakes56,57.

The seismic activity parameter b value using maximum likelihood estimation58:12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b=\frac{{{{\log }_{10}}(e)}}{{[\bar {M} - ({M_c} - \Delta {M_{bin}}/2)]}}$$\end{document}Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\bar {M}$$\end{document}is the average magnitude of M ≥ Mc, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta {M_{bin}}$$\end{document}is the magnitude sliding width of the earthquake catalog, generally \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta {M_{bin}}=0.1$$\end{document}

The uncertainty estimation of the b value can be obtained by the following equation59:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta b=2.30{b^2}\sqrt {\sum\limits_{{i=1}}^{n} {{{({M_i} - \bar {M})}^2}} /n(n - 1)}$$\end{document}

To further explore the effect of declustering on the b value, we used the maximum likelihood method to calculate the b values of background events using the Gardner and Knopoff, Reasenberg, nearest-neighbor and stochastic declustering methods. As shown in Fig. 5, the results were 0.71 ± 0.02, 0.95 ± 0.01, 0.76 ± 0.02 and 0.86 ± 0.02, respectively. In addition, compared to the b value (0.63 ± 0.01) of the entire catalog, the declustering b value is relatively large, which is related to the short-term incompleteness of the early monitoring capability after the 1976 Tangshan M7.8 earthquake (The monitoring capability was at M4.0 during the early post-earthquake period, leading to the omission of seismic events between M3.0-3.9, which reduced the proportion of small earthquakes, resulting in a relatively smaller b value for the entire catalog).

To further investigate the impact of short-term incompleteness after mainshock, Table 6 presents different the Gutenberg-Richter b value calculation results under different completeness magnitudes and time periods. We found that the b values obtained by the four declustering algorithms under different conditions varied little, while the choice of completeness magnitude and time period had a greater impact on the calculation results of the entire catalog. When we remove the influence of short-term incompleteness, the b value of the entire catalog is 0.87 ± 0.01, by calculating using earthquake events with M ≥ 4.0, which is comparable to the results calculated since 1980.

The differences in declustering methods for each magnitude bin resulted in changes in the proportion of large earthquakes, leading to varying degrees of impact on the b value. The Gardner and Knopoff, and nearest-neighbor methods tend to remove a relatively high proportion of small earthquakes, leading to an increased ratio of large to small earthquakes, which results in a relatively smaller b value. The Reasenberg method tends to remove a relatively high proportion of large earthquakes, causing a decrease in the ratio of large to small earthquakes, which results in a relatively larger b value. The stochastic declustering method maintains a relatively stable proportion of both large and small earthquakes being deleted, and the b value is situated among those other declustering algorithms. In addition, we calculated the a values in the G-R relationship for each declustering algorithm, and the a values were 4.92, 5.75, 5.29 and 5.20, respectively, which were negatively correlated with the declustering ratios (72.95%, 53.67%, 64.10%, 65.22%). This also indicates that a high declustering ratio (removing too much cluster events) will lead to a decrease in the overall seismic activity level. Therefore, the selection of algorithms is an important factor affecting seismic activity parameters.

Effects of declustering on PSHA

The PSHA are an important foundation for guiding building design codes, seismic design, and other disaster reduction engineering practices, and may be the most effective way to ensure earthquake safety at present1. The basic idea of PSHA is to first establish a probabilistic model of regional seismic activity, calculate the probability of earthquake occurrence, and then, based on a specific seismic ground motion prediction model, assess the probability distribution characteristics of the seismic ground motion levels produced by regional seismic activity at specific sites, thereby determining probabilistic seismic hazard curves. By combining the probabilistic distribution of ground motion predictions, the seismic hazard results at specific sites can be comprehensively obtained60.

Considering the diverse spatial and temporal patterns of seismicity in different regions of China, a modified version of PSHA called Chinese PSHA (CPSHA) was developed61. This method has been applied in the compilation of seismic zonation map, including FGSGMPZMC. The FGSGMPZMC primarily utilized instrumentally recorded seismic catalogs and historical destructive earthquake records. The time span of the seismic catalogs used was relatively extensive, and manual declustering was predominantly employed in the historical destructive earthquake catalogs in FGSGMPZMC. While in the instrumental catalog, the space-time window method has been employed. In terms of time, the window settings were referenced according to the Gardner and Knopoff14, while for spatial parameters, the parameters of the Chinese mainland have been adopted (with two coefficients being 0.50 and − 1.78, respectively62). The seismic zonation map represents long-term seismic hazard levels in the future and can serve as a basis for seismic design of buildings52. In our study, we refer to the potential seismic source zone delineation results as used in FGSGMPZMC52,63.

According to the assumptions of the CPSHA model, the magnitude distribution satisfies the truncated Gutenberg-Richter relationship, which allows the magnitude range to be discretized into Nm magnitude bins, mj represents the central value of the j-th magnitude bin, and the magnitude distribution P(mj) for the mj bin can be obtained as follows:14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P({m_j})=\frac{{2\exp [ - \beta ({m_j} - {m_0})]}}{{1 - \exp [ - \beta ({m_{uz}} - {m_0})]}} \cdot sh(\frac{\beta }{2}\Delta m)$$\end{document}Where, β = bln10, m0 is the lower magnitude limit, and muz is the upper magnitude limit, where “sh” represents the hyperbolic sine function. The number of earthquake occurrences of M ≥ m0 satisfies a Poisson distribution with an annual average earthquake occurrence rate v0, thus:15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{P}}({\text{n}})=\frac{{{{({v_0})}^n}\exp ( - {v_0})}}{{n!}}$$\end{document}

The probability of earthquakes in the mj magnitude bin occurring in the i-th potential seismic source zone within the study area is denoted as fi, mj. The area of the i-th potential seismic source zone is Ai, therefore:16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{P}}({(x,y)_i}|{m_j})={f_{i,mj}} \cdot \frac{1}{{{A_i}}}$$\end{document}

The declustering does not alter the inherent characteristics of the study area, including seismicity parameters such as the upper magnitude limit (muz) and the lower magnitude limit (m0). However, declustering does change the proportional relationship between large and small earthquakes in the catalog, as well as the number of earthquakes, which can lead to changes in seismic activity parameters such as the b value, the annual average occurrence rate (v0), and the spatial distribution function (fi, mj). These changes can significantly affect the results of PSHA.

Taking the Tianjin and Zhengzhou as examples, Fig. 6a and b show the seismic hazard curves obtained from four different declustering algorithms and FGSGMPZMC. It is found that the hazard levels calculated based on various declustering algorithms are in descending order from low to high as follows: the Reasenberg, stochastic declustering, Gardner and Knopoff, and nearest-neighbor methods. We obtained the peak ground acceleration (PGA) and response spectra at both sites under 5% probability of exceedance in 50 years by utilizing PSHA, as shown in Fig. 6c and d. The spectra acceleration exhibits a similar trend. We found that there are certain differences between the results of declustering calculations and FGSGMPZMC, with smaller differences between the Gardner and Knopoff and nearest-neighbor methods, and relatively larger differences between the Reasenberg and stochastic declustering methods. However, there are also certain differences at each site, for Tianjin, the hazard levels calculated based on the Reasenberg and stochastic declustering methods are lower than the results calculated by the FGSGMPZMC when PGA is small, showing more consistency in the hazard curve at low PGA compared to other algorithms. Nonetheless, when PGA is large, the hazard levels calculated by the stochastic declustering method are higher than the Reasenberg method but lower than other declustering algorithms. The Gardner and Knopoff method and nearest neighbor method calculate slightly lower hazard levels at low PGA compared to the FGSGMPZMC, with the Gardner and Knopoff method showing lower hazard levels than the nearest neighbor method. Conversely, at high PGA, the Gardner and Knopoff method yields hazard levels slightly higher than those calculated by the the FGSGMPZMC, showing more consistency in the seismic hazard results and higher hazard levels compared to other calculation methods. The results for Zhengzhou are consistent with those of Tianjin, except for some differences in the hazard levels between the Gardner and Knopoff method and nearest neighbor method compared to the the FGSGMPZMC. In larger PGA scenarios, there are certain differences in the points of the annual exceedance probability for Zhengzhou.

From the calculation results of the two sites, there is a certain difference in the overall hazard between the four declustering algorithms and FGSGMPZMC. Although the declustering reduces the annual average earthquake occurrence rate (background rates), the Gardner and Knopoff, nearest-neighbor methods reduce the G-R b values (Gardner and Knopoff: 0.71, nearest-neighbor: 0.76, FGSGMPZMC: 0.86), while the Reasenberg and stochastic declustering methods increase the b values (Reasenberg: 0.95, stochastic declustering: 0.86). The hazard of the Gardner and Knopoff, nearest-neighbor methods is higher than FGSGMPZMC, while the other two algorithms is lower than FGSGMPZMC. The hazard derived from the stochastic declustering method is lower than FGSGMPZMC, that is, when the b value is the same, the earthquake hazard also depends on the background occurrence rate, the higher the background occurrence rate, the greater the probability of large earthquakes. The differences in the earthquake hazard curves are primarily influenced by the annual average earthquake occurrence rate (background rates) and the seismicity parameter b value. Since the same b value had a similar impact on the results in the two different sites of the North China Plain seismic belt, and the influence of other factors was relatively minor, hence the effect of the b value on the site hazard is more pronounced.

Conclusion and discussion

The accurate and objective identification of background events and triggering events is the basis for constructing region seismicity models and PSHA. We optimized the initial parameters of the four clustering algorithms, and tested the declustering effect of the North China Plain seismic belt. We analyzed the impact of declustering on the b value and PSHA, and obtained the following conclusions.

Due to the influence of the Tangshan strong earthquake sequence, the declustering ratio for events with M ≥ 4.0 significantly increased. The declustering algorithms also caused differences in different earthquake sizes, resulting in varying degrees of impact on the b values. The Gardner and Knopoff and nearest-neighbor methods yielded smaller b values, while the Reasenberg method resulted in larger b values. Therefore, the choice of algorithm is an important factor affecting seismic activity parameters.

Different declustering algorithms have a certain influence on PSHA. Taking Tianjin and Zhengzhou as examples, the hazard levels calculated based on the Gardner and Knopoff and nearest neighbor methods declustering were close to each other and higher than the FGSGMPZMC hazard level. The hazard level calculated by the stochastic declustering method was higher than that of the Reasenberg method and lower than the FGSGMPZMC hazard level. The difference in seismic hazard curves is mainly influenced by the annual average earthquake occurrence rate (background rates) and the G-R relation b value, with the effect of the seismicity parameter b value being more pronounced.

The clustering masks the statistical characteristics of earthquakes as independent events, causing the spatio-temporal distribution of seismicity to deviate from its normal state, making it difficult to conduct an objective analysis of seismicity17. Because of the lack of a single and accurate mathematical description of the foreshock/aftershock process, it is difficult to obtain the actual categorization of background and clustered seismic events. It is also challenging to verify whether the declustering results can reflect the background activity or which one better reflects the nature of real seismic activity. Due to the non uniqueness of the seismic declustering results, we have not yet made an absolute judgment on the quality of declustering. Currently, we can only choose to verify the declustering effect by following Poisson distribution based on experience, actual needs, and other factors. However, as more and more studies have raised doubts about the Poisson characteristics of declustered data, and have suggested rethinking the quality indicators for declustering effects64, identifying the real characteristics of background seismic activity remains a future focus. We should be cautious when interpreting or relying on the analysis of the declustering results, as these results may not accurately reflect the exact properties of the foreshocks, mainshocks, and aftershocks.

The occurrence of major earthquake events can significantly impact the declustering characteristics in terms of time, space and magnitude. In subsequent regional declustering studies, it is crucial to fully consider the effect of major events in three aspects. The differences in region seismicity characteristics, algorithm parameters, completeness magnitude, and tectonic settings all have varying degrees of impact on declustering, resulting in certain variability in the declustering b value. The declustered catalogs often significantly lower b value compared to full catalogs30–32,49). When the raw catalog is significantly influenced by strong earthquake sequences, and due to the impact of short-term incompleteness post-earthquake, the b value of the declustered events may be greater than the raw catalog. However, in most cases, the declustering results of different algorithms will exhibit some degree of difference from the raw catalog. Some algorithms may produce declustering b value that are larger than the raw catalog, while others may yield results that are smaller, the declustering b value by the Reasenberg algorithm is typically greater than the Gardner and Knopoff algorithm4,13,22,36,50.

Perry and Bendick50 investigate the results of four declustering algorithms in four different tectonic settings: the Northern Rocky Mountains (excluding Yellowstone), Yellowstone, Southern California, and Japan, which found that there are certain differences in the performance of algorithms across different tectonic settings. Due to differences in the selection of completeness magnitude and the duration of the catalog used will also affect the seismicity parameters52. The setting of the completeness magnitude may sever the triggering relationships between seismic events below the completeness magnitude and other events, leading to a “missing link” phenomenon65. Therefore, setting a reasonable completeness magnitude is also one of the important foundations for declustering and calculating seismicity parameters. This paper only focuses on the study of cluster removal in the North China Plain seismic belt. The optimized initial parameters and declustering effect are only applicable to this region. The study of other regions also needs to select the appropriate declustering algorithm in combination with the specific tectonic environment and seismic activity characteristics.

The declustering results not only provide a better understanding of the spatio-temporal patterns of seismic activity for earthquake risk assessment, but also can be combined with faults to more accurately investigate the faults activity and the mechanisms of earthquake occurrence. Anderson and Zaliapin66 take into account the contribution of faults, and in regions where faults dominate the hazard, the method of thinning has little effect. It is possible to construct earthquake probability occurrence models under different declustering algorithms, such as Poisson models or time-dependent models, which are the basis for conducting PSHA. The declustering results play a crucial role in improving the accuracy and reliability of PSHA, providing a more precise reflection of the seismic risk in specific sites, which is used to guide building codes, urban planning. We only considered the impact of seismicity parameters on PSHA. Furthermore, it should be combined with physical models to explore the physical mechanisms of seismic activity. For example, by analyzing the distribution characteristics of seismic event groups and the stress state inside the Earth, the mechanism and physical processes behind earthquake occurrence can be inferred, and the dynamic evolution process of seismic activity can be understood, providing scientific basis for disaster prevention and emergency management.
