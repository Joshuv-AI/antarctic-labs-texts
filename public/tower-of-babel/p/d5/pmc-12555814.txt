Unlocking New Therapeutic Strategies in Oligometastatic Prostate Cancer with Radioligand and Metastasis-directed Radiation Therapy
Authors: Salvatore Cozzi, Camille Roukoz, Thomas Zilli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Take Home MessageFor oligometastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer, the combination of radioligand therapy and stereotactic body radiotherapy offers potential for better tumor control and low toxicity. Future research should define optimal patient selection and treatment sequencing to establish the role of this combined strategy in personalized management of prostate cancer.

Over the years, nuclear medicine imaging techniques have become increasingly valuable because of their ability to improve the accuracy of staging and restaging of prostate cancer. More recently, in addition to their diagnostic applications, radiopharmaceuticals emitting α or β particles have emerged as promising therapeutic agents in the treatment of prostate cancer [1] via delivery of targeted radiation to cancer cells while minimizing irradiation to healthy tissues. Thus, nuclear medicine now plays a central role in diagnostic and therapeutic management of prostate cancer [2].

While stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) continues to play a major role for local ablative treatment of oligometastatic prostate cancer, the integration of systemic therapies to address microscopic disease spread remains essential for improving patient outcomes.

Dual therapies combining SBRT and radioligand therapy (RLT) with radiopharmaceuticals such as radium-223 (Ra-223) and 177Lu-labeled prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA) ligands, both of which have been largely validated in castration-resistant prostate cancer, offer a unique opportunity to improve both local and distant disease control while potentially avoiding the systemic adverse effects associated with androgen deprivation. This approach may be particularly relevant in metachronous oligometastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer (omHSPC), in which the disease burden is limited and preservation of quality of life remains a key consideration.

As summarized in Table 1, three phase 2 trials [[3], [4], [5], [6]] investigated the potential benefit of adding six cycles of Ra-223 to metastasis-directed therapy (MDT) via SBRT in patients with omHSPC. In the single-arm RadSABR trial, which involved 20 patients with androgen deprivation therapy (ADT)-naïve bone-only omHPSC, 55% (n = 11) of patients experienced a prostate-specific antigen (PSA) decline of >50%, and 25% (n = 5) a decline of >90%. Two patients achieved an undetectable PSA level that lasted for 36 mo in one patient and 51 mo in the other patient without additional therapy. Notably, 50% of patients remained free from ADT at 15 mo, and 40% at 24 mo. Similarly, in the single-arm SHARP trial, median progression-free survival (PFS) reached 34.3 mo in the group with de novo omHSPC, and 17.7 mo in the group with metachronous omHSPC. Unfortunately, in the randomized phase 2 RAVENS trial (bone-only disease in 78% of the omHPSC group), no PFS benefit was observed for addition of Ra-223 to SBRT in comparison to SBRT alone [3]. The inclusion of patients with metastases in sites other than bone may explain in part the lack of benefit observed. In all trials, the combination treatment was well tolerated, with no grade 4 or 5 toxicities reported. Adverse events reported for the combined approach include skeletal-related events, lymphopenia, and a single case of neuropathy. Given the ability of combined 177Lu-PSMA + SBRT to simultaneously target nodal and bone lesions, this treatment strategy appears to be highly promising and is currently under evaluation in multiple ongoing clinical trials (Table 2). In patients with omHSPC with up to five lesions, the BULLSEYE trial (NCT04443062) [7] has demonstrated promising outcomes in terms of PSA responses and PFS for administration of two (+2) cycles of 177Lu-PSMA-617 in comparison to deferred ADT or watchful waiting. These findings suggest additional potential for combining systemic treatment of microscopic disease with 177Lu-PSMA and local ablative therapies. While we await mature data from ongoing clinical trials, particularly those investigating 177Lu-PSMA, several important considerations should be kept in mind.

Radiobiological principles provide a strong rationale for combining external beam radiotherapy (EBRT) with RLT. EBRT predominantly induces double-strand breaks in DNA via high-dose focal irradiation, whereas RLT delivers continuous low-dose radiation directly to tumor cells via targeted radionuclide emission. This complementary pattern of DNA damage may enhance cumulative cytotoxicity and decrease the efficiency of repair mechanisms, thereby improving tumor control. In addition, radiation modulates the tumor microenvironment by altering the vasculature, oxygenation, and immune cell infiltration. These changes can sensitize tumor cells to subsequent radiation exposure and may improve immune-mediated tumor recognition, which is the rationale for the hypothesis that there is a synergistic interaction between EBRT and RLT.

From a physical perspective, the two modalities differ fundamentally in their mode of delivery and spatial selectivity. SBRT is a localized treatment that delivers ablative radiation doses to a precisely defined tumor volume, whereas PSMA-targeted RLT is a systemic approach that allows treatment of both visible and microscopic disseminated disease. Therefore, a combination of RLT and SBRT has the radiobiological potential to deliver higher equivalent doses to macroscopically visible tumors [8] and address occult microscopic disease while limiting the dose exposure for healthy tissues. As observed in preclinical models, combined treatments have shown better tumor control and improvements in survival in comparison to EBRT or RLT alone [9]. The spectrum of organs at risk also differs between the modalities. With SBRT, off-target effects are largely determined by the anatomic proximity of critical organs to the irradiation field, while RLT-associated toxicities are typically of low grade and primarily arise in organs with physiological PSMA expression and radioligand uptake, such as the salivary glands, kidneys, and bone marrow.

In the context of this combined treatment modality, the optimal timing and sequencing of RLT and SBRT remain aspects for further investigation. It has been shown that EBRT delivery before RLT enhances the in vivo tumor-absorbed dose in comparison to RLT alone via upregulation of PSMA expression on the surface of prostate cancer cells [9]. Concomitant delivery of RLT and EBRT can maximize tumor uptake of the radioligand by increasing flow and vessel permeability [10], even if clinical implementation in practice remains challenging. Conversely, RLT administration before EBRT can potentially reduce the gross tumor burden without delaying treatment of systemic disease. However, there are not yet any data for direct comparison of upfront EBRT + RLT with a strategy based on sequencing at the time of progression. Although sequencing may compromise the additive radiobiological and physical effects of these two modalities, prospective studies are required to establish whether early treatment intensification provides superior outcomes over sequential approaches. Furthermore, additional combinations with hormonal therapies or use of α- or Auger-emitting theranostics could possibly be used to improve outcomes for patients with omHSPC.

Improvements in dosimetry and radiobiological dose estimation remain key challenges given the distinct dose distributions and absorption patterns of RLT and SBRT. Addressing these complexities is essential for optimizing RLT dosing schedules as well as SBRT dose and fractionation schemes, ultimately aiming to enhance treatment efficacy while minimizing toxicity.

Patient selection and treatment monitoring remain critical challenges for this therapeutic approach. The integration of next-generation imaging with emerging prognostic biomarkers will be essential in identifying the most suitable candidates for this dual-therapy strategy. Moreover, country-specific regulatory frameworks and reimbursement policies are additional hurdles that must be addressed to facilitate broader clinical implementation in the near future. Last but not least, well-designed phase 2/3 randomized trials focusing on robust clinical endpoints, such as time to CRPC and overall survival, are awaited to clarify the long-term efficacy and role of this combined treatment.

In conclusion, combination of EBRT with RLT for patients with omHPSC is conceptually promising: it is expected that spatial cooperation, enhanced tumor responses, and mitigation of toxicity could potentially improve the therapeutic ratio in comparison to MDT strategies that combine androgen suppression and SBRT.

However, key challenges remain, including optimal patient selection, refinement of dosimetry, treatment sequencing, and integration of advanced imaging and biomarker-based monitoring. Results from ongoing clinical trials, along with future preclinical and clinical research, will be essential in clarifying the role of this combined approach and supporting a more personalized and effective treatment strategy for patients with omHSPC.
