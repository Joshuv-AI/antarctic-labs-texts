Single-bubble sonoluminescence at two adjacent antinodes in a standing wave acoustic field
Authors: Xueping Wang, Jinfu Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study employed both experimental observations and numerical simulations to investigate the formation mechanism of single-bubble sonoluminescence (SBSL) light pulses at two adjacent antinodes within a rectangular acoustic resonator. We recorded the light pulses emitted by SBSL at each antinode alongside the corresponding temporal evolution of the acoustic pressure. We calculated and simulated the temporal evolution of the bubble wall, number density of various internal particles, and radiated light pulses and spectra by integrating fluid dynamics equations, the Keller–Miksis equation, and spectroscopic theory. The results showed that the light pulse amplitude emitted by the bubble at the antinode with higher driving acoustic pressure was higher, which concurred with the experimental observations. This suggests that local acoustic pressure significantly influences sonoluminescence characteristics. In addition, the trends in the physical quantities, e.g., temperature, pressure, ionization degree, and energy spectrum, inside the bubbles at both antinodes matched those observed in SBSL within spherical acoustic fields, which underscores the theoretical and experimental importance of clarifying the sonoluminescence radiation mechanism. These findings will enable the optimization of sonochemical reactor designs and spatial control of acoustic cavitation processes in industrial applications.Keywords: Acoustic field distribution, Antinode, Sonoluminescence, Light pulse

Introduction

Single-bubble sonoluminescence (SBSL) refers to the phenomenon in which a stable bubble, confined within a standing-wave acoustic field under specific conditions, emits an extremely brief and highly intense light pulse during its violent collapse [1], [2], [3]. Current SBSL research primarily focuses on symmetrical acoustic cavities, especially spherical [4], [5] or cylindrical [6], [7] configurations. These geometries ensure a uniform acoustic field distribution and typically feature only a single stable bubble core point, thereby facilitating systematic control over bubble dynamics and luminescence behavior. In such idealized settings, comprehensive experimental and simulation-based investigations have been conducted on the characteristics of light pulses, spectral properties, and dynamic behaviors associated with SBSL. However, the acoustic field often presents multiple antinodes and non-uniformities in more complex or practical applications, enabling bubbles to reside at various extreme-pressure points where the local acoustic environments exhibit significant spatial variations. Certain studies have explored cavitation behavior in complex acoustic fields. Matula et al. [8] validated a dynamic model of bubbles stably levitated at antinodes within a rectangular cell. Hatanka et al. [9], [10], Lee et al. [11], and Thoemann et al. [12] both theoretically and experimentally demonstrated the spatial correlation between the multi-bubble sonoluminescence (MBSL) intensity and local acoustic pressure maxima in rectangular reactors. Yasui et al. [13] visualized the standing-wave field in a rectangular cell using sonochemistry. Vian et al. [14] experimentally imaged and simulated the acoustic field and MBSL distribution in a cylindrical cavity. Furthermore, several researchers [15], [16], [17], [18], [19] have conducted numerical studies on acoustic field distributions in rectangular cavities, and Knox et al. [20] optimized sonoluminescence efficiency by modifying cavity structures and dimensions. Nonetheless, most studies have focused on MBSL or the simulation of the acoustic fields themselves, with limited research addressing SBSL characteristics at different antinodes within the same standing-wave field.

In this study, we established a stable acoustic field with two antinodes within a rectangular acoustic resonator, generating SBSL at each antinode. We then utilized an identical photomultiplier tube (PMT) system to capture the sonoluminescence signals emitted by the bubbles at both locations. A hydrophone was used to measure the local sound pressure amplitudes at these two positions. Leveraging the experimental data, we integrated gas dynamic equations, Keller–Miksis equations, and spectral formulas to compute and simulate the temporal evolution of the bubble wall, as well as the temperature, pressure, number density of various particles, ionization degree, light pulses, and energy spectra of the emitted radiation. This methodology enabled us to investigate the radiation mechanisms of sonoluminescence, providing new experimental insights and theoretical support for studying sonoluminescence in complex ultrasonic environments.

Experiment

Experimental setup and methods

The experimental system primarily consisted of three components: a sonoluminescence system, an optical signal acquisition system, and an acoustic field detection system. The sonoluminescence system included an acoustic resonator, a signal generator, a power amplifier, and an adjustable inductor coil. The optical signal acquisition system comprised a PMT and power amplifier, whereas the acoustic field detection system consisted of a hydrophone and power amplifier. An oscilloscope displayed the voltages of both the optical and acoustic signals. In addition, a camera captured sonoluminescence images of the two antinodes, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

Experimental setup diagram.

The experimental acoustic resonator comprised a quartz glass bottle measuring 5 cm × 5 cm × 20 cm, filled with approximately 10 cm of deaerated and deionized distilled water. A sinusoidal ultrasonic signal generated by a signal generator (Keysight 33 600 A) was amplified by an power amplifier (Aigtek ATA-2021H) and then transmitted through a matching inductive coil to a piezoelectric ceramic transducer (PZT) located at the base of the resonator. This setup provided a stable ultrasonic standing-wave field within the resonator. Two bubbles were introduced at the antinodes of the liquid, where acoustic radiation forces stably suspended each bubble at its respective position. By appropriately adjusting the acoustic pressure and frequency, the bubbles emitted light, resulting in sonoluminescence at both antinodes. Sonoluminescence images were captured using a Nikon D7500 digital camera, as shown in Fig. 2(a). Extended exposure times were required owing to the low intensity of the sonoluminescence signal.

(a) Sonoluminescence images of two adjacent antinodes; (b) Time-varying light signal from bubble 1; (c) Time-varying light signal from bubble 2. Experiment conducted in deionized distilled water with f= 27.312 kHz, H= 10 cm, and A=3.1Vpp.

A PMT (HAMAMATSU, H10720-20) was employed to collect the light intensity signals from the two sonoluminescent bubbles. Initially, the three-dimensional (3D) motion platform was adjusted to align the PMT with each bubble at its respective acoustic antinode, facilitating sequential signal acquisition. The detected light intensity signals were displayed and recorded in real time using a high-performance digital storage oscilloscope (SIGLENT SDS2074X Plus), as illustrated in Figs. 2(b) and 2(c). All measurements were performed in a dark room to minimize experimental errors. During signal acquisition, the horizontal distance between the PMT and resonator was maintained constant, and both the resonator and PMT were securely covered with four layers of light-blocking fabric to eliminate ambient light interference effectively. In addition, an RHCA-7 hydrophone was used to measure the acoustic pressure within the ultrasonic field, which was captured and stored in real time using the same oscilloscope.

Analysis and results

Theoretical model

We performed numerical calculations based on the experimental data, incorporating the bubble wall dynamic equation, fluid mechanics equations, and a spectral radiation formula, to investigate the radiation mechanism of sonoluminescence near two adjacent antinodes. These calculations enabled the determination of key physical quantities, including the light pulse characteristics of SBSL at the two antinodes, as well as the temperature, pressure, and degree of ionization of the bubble.

To illustrate that the interaction between two adjacent bubbles has a relatively minor effect on the pulsation and radial movement of the bubbles, we used the single and double bubble models with no translation [21], [22] and with translation [23], [24]to calculate the radial radius and center translation of the two bubbles, as well as their interaction force (secondary Bjerknes force). The calculation results showed that the interaction between the two bubbles had almost no effect on the radial movement of the bubbles. In addition, the interaction between the two bubbles has a relatively small effect on the size of the center displacement of the bubbles, which was approximately 1/1000 of the radial movement magnitude. This indicates that, under the acoustic field conditions of present study, the interaction between bubbles at two adjacent antinodes has almost no effect on the radial and translational movements of the bubbles, and the bubbles are driven by the primary Bjerknes force. Therefore, in this study, the light pulses and spectra of the sonoluminescence were numerically calculated using the SBSL model [21], [25], [26] .

In the stable phase of SBSL, the argon rectification hypothesis [28] proposes that, following numerous acoustic cycles, active gases (such as N2 and O2) inside the bubble are converted into soluble substances through chemical reactions and dissolve into the surrounding liquid, while chemically inert argon (Ar) becomes progressively enriched within the bubble. Simultaneously, an asymmetry exists in the water vapor transport during bubble pulsation: a significant influx occurs during the expansion phase, whereas incomplete condensation and expulsion take place during the extremely short collapse phase. Therefore, in this study, we simplified the gas composition of the bubble to two constituents: an inert gas (Ar) and water vapor (H2O).

Analytical results

Numerical simulations were performed based on experimental observations of two argon bubbles positioned at the antinodes, each with an initial radius of 4.5 μm and initial wall velocity of 0 m/s, to examine the sonoluminescence characteristics of bubbles located at distinct antinodes within an ultrasonic field. The experimental medium was deionized water, with a driving frequency of 27.312 kHz and an ambient temperature of 30∘C. The driving acoustic pressure amplitudes at the locations of bubbles 1 and 2 were 1.205 atm and 1.198 atm, respectively. During bubble compression, the internal gas underwent adiabatic heating, resulting in the emission of light known as sonoluminescence. We simulated the SBSL radiation processes of the two bubbles utilizing Eqs. (3)–(9).

Fig. 5(a) depicts the total radiation power generated by each flash of bubble 1 and the evolution of its radius over time. We identified six characteristic time points (A-F) on the curve for further analysis of the radiation energy spectrum [Fig. 5(b)], pressure distribution within the bubble [Fig. 5(c)], and temperature distribution within the bubble [Fig. 5(d)]. The simulation results indicated that the total intensity increased gradually over time as the radiation energy spectrum accumulated. The radiation power intensified consistently from points A to E, accompanied by a corresponding increase in the intensity of the continuous background. Notably, the OH spectral peak within the 0.30–0.36 μm wavelength range and Ar+ spectral peak around 0.40 μm progressively diminished until they vanished from the spectrum [Fig. 5(b)].

Concurrently, as observed in Figs. 5(c) and (d), the temperature and pressure inside bubble 1 steadily increased during its gradual compression, reaching a minimum radius of 0.747744 μm at time point E. At this juncture, the radiation power peaked at1.6293×10−5W, with the temperature and pressure attaining 7424.2 K and 2.03×109 Pa, respectively. Subsequently, the bubble rebounded and expanded as the temperature and pressure decreased. In addition, the simulations revealed that the temperature was highest at the bubble center and dropped sharply near the wall, creating a significant gradient. In contrast, the pressure distribution remained relatively uniform throughout the bubble. This uniformity resulted from the high thermal conductivity of the gas and limited compressibility of the liquid, allowing pressure to equilibrate rapidly within the bubble.

To investigate the material structure inside the SBSL bubble further, we calculated the spatial distributions of various particle number densities within bubble 1 at different times. Fig. 6 illustrates these spatial distributions from times A to F, as depicted in Fig. 5(a). The figure demonstrates that the Ar atoms [Fig. 6(a)] maintained a high number density throughout the entire cycle, serving as the stable background gas inside the bubble. In contrast, the H2O molecules underwent gradual chemical decomposition and ionization under high temperature and pressure, generating various active particles. At various time points, all particles except for the Ar atoms exhibited the maximum number density at the bubble center, where the temperature peaked, and sharply declined near the bubble wall. This spatial distribution aligned closely with the internal temperature profile of the bubble. The elevated central temperature promoted ionization and dissociation reactions, resulting in a significant accumulation of active species. Furthermore, the particle number density within the bubble was positively correlated with the bubble size, temperature, and pressure. Specifically, when bubble 1 attained radiation peak E, its center temperature reached a maximum of 7424.2 K and the ionized electron number density was approximately 1.1613×1025m−3. The high electron density signified substantial ionization, which consumed considerable thermal energy. Consequently, the temperature rise was suppressed, limiting further increases. As the bubble expanded and its temperature decreased, the electron number density rapidly declined, demonstrating a strong positive correlation with temperature fluctuations. This ionization-induced energy consumption mechanism, coupled with its negative feedback on the temperature elevation, is a key factor in the relatively short duration of SBSL. Specifically, elevated temperatures trigger ionization, which consumes energy and inhibits further temperature increases. Consequently, the ensuing temperature decrease led to a pronounced decline in the electron number density, making the maintenance of the luminescence process challenging.

Discussion and conclusions

A comparative analysis of the light pulse characteristics of SBSL at two antinodes in an ultrasonic standing-wave field was conducted using experimental measurements and numerical simulations. The simulation results showed that the FWHM of the light pulses emitted by bubbles 1 and 2 were 0.36 ns and 0.38 ns, respectively, compared with the experimentally measured FWHM values of 3.50 ns and 3.45 ns. The theoretical values are approximately an order of magnitude lower than the experimental values. This discrepancy aligns with the pulse widths of 0.24 ns (pure water) and 4.35 ns (95% sulfuric acid) reported in Ref. [34], suggesting that the pulse width is significantly influenced by both the medium and the measurement system. The observed pulse broadening is primarily attributed to the bandwidth limitations of the experimental setup. Despite the use of a high-speed PMT and high-sampling-rate oscilloscope, the inherent nanosecond-scale response time of the oscilloscope restricts its ability to resolve the theoretical sub-nanosecond pulse structure fully, resulting in pronounced temporal broadening of the recorded optical signal.

In terms of radiation power, the experiments demonstrated that the amplitude of the sound pressure at the position of bubble 1 (approximately 1.205 atm) was greater than that of bubble 2 (approximately 1.198 atm), and the maximum power of sonoluminescence of bubble 1 (approximately 6.95×10−6 W) was greater than that of bubble 2 (approximately 5.51×10−6 W). The theoretical calculation results also indicated that the maximum power of bubble 1 (approximately 1.6293×10−5 W) was greater than that of bubble 2 (approximately 1.0392×10−5 W). This suggests that the larger the pressure amplitude, the greater the radiation power of the sonoluminescence. To clarify this relationship, we theoretically calculated the maximum radiation power of a bubble driven by six different sound pressure amplitudes (see Fig. 10). The results show that the maximum radiation power of the bubble increased with an increase in the sound pressure amplitude, although not linearly.

Maximum radiated power of the bubble under different driving sound pressures.

It should be noted that the maximum power of the optical pulses measured at the two antinodes was approximately half of the theoretical value. This discrepancy may be owing to variations in the photon collection efficiency. In the experiment, the PMT detected only a fraction of the photons emitted by the luminescent bubbles within a limited solid angle, rather than capturing the entire photon flux radiated over the full 4π solid angle. Although the measurement distance remained constant, potential bias in the calibration of the collection efficiency may have led to the measured optical power values being lower than the actual total radiation power. In addition, the experiment lacked direct, in situ measurements of the dynamic radii of the bubbles. In the theoretical model, we assumed that the dynamic behavior of the bubbles was solely governed by the prevailing acoustic field conditions, utilizing classical theories for calculations. However, the collapse dynamics of the bubbles directly influenced the temperature and pressure of the internal gas, thereby affecting the width and intensity of the light pulse. The lack of direct constraints from these critical data likely accounts for the quantitative discrepancies between the theoretical predictions and experimental results.

In summary, this research systematically investigated the SBSL characteristics of double antinodes within a rectangular acoustic resonator through experimental observations and numerical simulations. The experimental results revealed a minor variation in the local acoustic pressure between the two adjacent antinodes. Consequently, bubble 1, situated in the region of higher acoustic pressure, exhibited greater luminescence intensity, leading to elevated temperature, pressure, ionization degree, and radiation power values. This observation aligns with the experimental finding that bubble 1 exhibited higher luminescence intensity. However, the radiation mechanisms of SBSL at both antinodes remained identical, involving the ionization luminescence of gas under high-temperature and high-pressure conditions. The simulation results of the acoustic field within the resonator closely matched the experimental data. This consistency not only confirms the non-uniform distribution of the acoustic field in the rectangular resonator, but also validates the reliability of the experimental platform for investigating the spatial effects of SBSL. Furthermore, it provides novel insights and methodological approaches for future studies on sonoluminescence in complex acoustic field environments.
