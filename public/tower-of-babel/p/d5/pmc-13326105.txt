Metabolic healthy obesity: A window for early cardiometabolic prevention
Authors: Zhiqi Yao, Michael J. Blaha, Khurram Nasir
Published: 2026

Obesity is often viewed through the lens of cardiometabolic dysfunction; however, not all individuals with obesity exhibit metabolic abnormalities such as hypertension, diabetes, or dyslipidemia. This observation gave rise to the concept of metabolically healthy obesity (MHO) [1]. As a substantial proportion of individuals with obesity fall into this category, understanding the long-term health risks associated with MHO is important for informing both prevention strategies and clinical management.

In this issue, Ananda, Ray, and colleagues provide new insights using data from the UK Biobank [2]. In a prospective cohort of 157,159 participants free of cardiovascular disease and followed for a median of 12.9 years, the authors examined the joint associations of obesity and metabolic health with several downstream health outcomes and mortality. Using a relatively strict definition of metabolic health which requires the absence of hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia, their study shows that compared with individuals with normal body mass index (BMI) and no metabolic abnormalities, those with MHO had higher risks of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, heart failure, metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease, and all-cause mortality (hazard ratios 1.36–2.37 in men and 1.27–4.44 in women). As expected, their study shows that when metabolic abnormalities coexist with obesity, risk escalates substantially (e.g., for cardiovascular outcomes, risk was approximately doubled compared with individuals classified as MHO). The patterns were generally consistent across outcomes. ESRD was associated with a 5.42-fold increased risk in men only.

Taken together, these findings reinforce an important message: MHO should not be considered a risk-free state. Even in the absence of clinically recognized metabolic disease, excess adiposity appears to confer measurable long-term cardiometabolic risk. At the same time, these observations warrant careful interpretation, reflecting the inherent conceptual challenges in defining and capturing MHO that are not unique to the present study.

Metabolic abnormalities frequently arise as downstream consequences of excess adiposity [3]. As such, classifying individuals by metabolic status may not fully disentangle the biological effects of adiposity from its metabolic manifestations. This challenge is further complicated by the threshold-based definitions commonly used to identify metabolic abnormalities, which rely on laboratory cutoffs or medication use and may fail to capture early or subclinical metabolic dysfunction. In this light, the MHO assessment may represent less a distinct obesity-only biological state than a cross-sectional snapshot along a dynamic metabolic trajectory. Longitudinal studies reinforce this view, showing that metabolic health among individuals with obesity is often unstable, with a substantial proportion transitioning between metabolically healthy and unhealthy states over time [4,5]. Viewed this way, MHO defined at a single time point likely represents a heterogeneous phenotype, capturing both individuals with stable metabolic profiles and others who may simply be at an earlier stage of cardiometabolic deterioration.

The conceptual challenges surrounding MHO pose a practical dilemma. Ideally, we hope to identify individuals with obesity who will remain metabolically healthier over the long term. Such a truly stable MHO phenotype would be scientifically intriguing and could offer insights into biological mechanisms that buffer the adverse metabolic consequences of excess adiposity. In practice, however, distinguishing such individuals is difficult without long-term follow-up and close surveillance. From a clinical standpoint, deferring intervention until metabolic abnormalities emerge effectively means waiting for disease to develop before acting – an approach that runs counter to the principles of modern preventive medicine. Yet the study by Ananda and Ray indicates that even at a single assessment, and in the absence of overt metabolic disease, obesity itself is associated with elevated risks for several major health outcomes [2]. These observations reinforce an important clinical message: MHO should not be dismissed from risk assessment or dismissed from management strategies. Rather than providing reassurance alone, identifying individuals with MHO may represent a critical window for early intervention to reduce the risk of future long-term adverse health outcomes.

Recently, the Lancet Diabetes & Endocrinology Commission on the definition and diagnosis of clinical obesity introduced the concept of preclinical obesity, referring to individuals with excess adiposity who have not yet developed clinical conditions or functional limitations attributable to obesity [6]. This category was intended to be broader than the traditional concept of MHO, extending beyond metabolic dysfunction alone and representing an earlier or relatively healthier stage within the spectrum of obesity [7]. Within this framework, management emphasizes monitoring, risk-factor surveillance, and lifestyle guidance, with more intensive interventions generally reserved for individuals who develop clinical complications (i.e. clinical obesity). While this approach acknowledges the heterogeneity of obesity and aims to avoid overtreatment, it may also inadvertently convey no need for intervention among individuals with preclinical obesity at a given time point, a group that accounted for more than one-quarter of participants in our prior analysis of the All of Us cohort [8]. As suggested by the present study, the absence of clinically recognized metabolic abnormalities does not necessarily indicate the absence of cardiometabolic and mortality risk. If metabolic disturbances represent downstream manifestations of prolonged adiposity, delaying intervention until such thresholds are crossed likely allows a substantial period of risk exposure to accumulate.

Lifestyle interventions, including dietary modification, increased physical activity, and weight reduction, remain the cornerstone of obesity management [9]. At the same time, the therapeutic landscape is rapidly evolving with the emergence of newer-generation anti-obesity medications (AOMs), which have increasingly demonstrated benefits that extend beyond weight reduction to broader cardiometabolic outcomes. However, most randomized trials of obesity therapies have not specifically examined MHO or preclinical obesity as a distinct phenotype. Trial eligibility has typically been based on BMI and the presence of obesity-related comorbidities, and analyses rarely distinguish between metabolically healthy and metabolically unhealthy individuals. As a result, individuals with MHO are often underrepresented or grouped with higher-risk populations, leaving uncertainty about the potential benefits of early pharmacologic intervention. In the context of accumulative evidence that MHO is not benign, if excess adiposity itself represents an early driver of cardiometabolic risk, intervening before overt metabolic dysfunction develops may offer an important opportunity for prevention. Future randomized trials will therefore be important to determine whether early treatment, especially with AOMs, can favorably modify cardiometabolic trajectories among individuals with MHO. Such studies will also need to consider appropriate endpoints, which may extend beyond short-term metabolic improvements to include longer-term cardiometabolic and kidney outcomes and disease progression. Clarifying these questions will be critical for informing clinical guidelines, payer coverage decisions, and broader public policy.

Looking ahead, in addition to the need for randomized clinical trials, several important questions remain. Longitudinal studies are needed to better characterize the natural history of metabolically healthy obesity, including patterns of transition between metabolic states and the determinants that drive such trajectories. Equally important will be efforts to identify biological features that distinguish individuals with truly stable metabolic profiles from those more likely to progress toward metabolic dysfunction. Approaches may include genetic susceptibility profiling, detailed metabolic phenotyping, imaging-based assessments of ectopic fat distribution, and early biomarkers reflecting subclinical cardiometabolic stress.
