Genome Evolution in Plant Pathogenic Bacteria
Authors: Kylie S Weis, Amanpreet Kaur, Palash Ghosh, Neha Potnis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBacterial plant pathogens have ravaged crops since the dawn of agriculture and continue to pose a serious threat today. Bacteria and their plant hosts have co-evolved in an evolutionary arms race, with artificial selection due to agriculture tipping the scale in favor of the pathogen. This review gives an overview of plant pathogenic bacterial diversity, showing that pathogenicity has independently evolved numerous times, and that there is not one unifying trait determining plant pathogenicity. Instead, these bacteria represent repeated, independent evolutionary transitions driven by life in complex ecological networks that include plant hosts, insect vectors, microbial competitors, and highly heterogeneous abiotic environments. Their genomes reflect this interplay through a dynamic balance of architecture and flux. These structural features, along with highly variable pangenomes, capture the balance between genome stability and flux imposed by ecological constraints and epidemiological dynamics. Horizontal gene transfer via conjugative plasmids, prophages, integrative and conjugative elements, transposons, and in some lineages, natural competence, remains the major source of adaptive novelty, enabling rapid remodeling of virulence repertoires, metabolic capabilities, and antibiotic or heavy metal resistance genes. These changes create distinct selective landscapes. Agricultural practices such as chemical use, host resistance deployment, or seed trade, can drive recurrent bottlenecks, expansions, and admixture events that leave strong genomic signatures in pathogens. Finally, this review explores the genomic differences enabling the divergence of lifestyles, while also acknowledging knowledge gaps and future directions of research on the evolution of bacterial plant pathogens.

Bacterial plant pathogens can devastate the production of crops used for food, fiber, or fuel. New emerging pathogens capable of causing epidemics due to host range expansion, acquired pathogenicity, overcoming host resistance, or antibiotic resistance, is an ever-present threat in today's world of agriculture and global trade. Understanding the drivers and mechanisms of evolution of these traits, especially pathogenicity, is important for informing predictive disease models, creating new disease resistant plant cultivars, developing biological control methods, and even informing public policy for surveillance and quarantine guidelines. Additionally, much of the information gained about bacterial plant pathogen evolution may also be translatable and informative of pathogens causing infectious disease in humans.

Introduction

In 1878, Burrill published the first paper showing bacteria as the causative agent of plant disease. Since then, bacteria have been found to make up 18% of emerging infectious disease reports in crops from 1996 to 2002 (Anderson et al. 2004) and a major contributor to crop losses. Bacterial leaf blight caused an estimated yield loss of 17% to 31% in rice in India in the late 1990s and with the introduction of citrus greening disease in Florida in 2005, citrus production decreased by 74% by 2019 (Rajarajeswari and Muralidharan 2006; Singerman and Rogers 2020). Bacterial plant pathogens not only affect all staple crops (maize, rice, wheat, and potato), but also many other economically important crops including but not limited to Solanaceae (tomato, pepper), Cucurbits (squash, melon), Fabaceae (green bean, soybean, peanut), Brassicaceae (broccoli, cabbage), Rosaceae (apple, peach), Apiaceae (carrot, celery), grape, banana, pecan, walnut, cassava, and even ornamental flowers and grasses.

While some pathogens are opportunistic generalists, many are specialized and infect only one plant family or species. This provides evidence of co-evolution between plants and their bacterial pathogens. In fact, bacteria are in an arms race with their plant hosts. As plants evolve to sense a bacterial invader and launch an immune response, bacteria then evolve mechanisms to evade detection and dampen immunity, and the plant must evolve once again and the cycle continues. This is seen particularly in H.H. Flor's gene for gene theory where a particular virulence gene in the pathogen corresponds to a resistance gene in the host (Flor 1971). However, the advent of agriculture has disrupted this historic cycle, with artificial selection by humans instead of natural selection for pathogen resistance, increasing the emergence, prevalence, and severity of plant diseases. Early investigations into the evolution of plant pathogenic bacteria relied on multi-locus sequence typing (MLST), which used the sequences of only a handful of housekeeping genes to infer strain level diversity, phylogeny, and estimate recombination/mutation rates (Scally et al. 2005). While transformative for its time, MLST captured only a narrow slice of genomic diversity and provided limited resolution on the mechanisms shaping pathogen evolution. The field shifted dramatically with the sequencing of the first complete bacterial plant pathogen genome, Xylella fastidiosa, in 2000, and subsequent improvements in technology and the rise of sequencing accessibility and affordability produced thousands of assemblies across major pathogenic genera (651 assemblies for Erwinia, 1,642 assemblies for Ralstonia, and 6,447 assemblies for Xanthomonas) (NCBI accessed 5-19-26). This sequencing effort allowed comparative genomics, identification of mobile genetic elements (MGEs), secretion systems and effector proteins, and genome-scale reconstruction of the evolutionary history of plant pathogenic bacteria. Although rare, due to a number of limitations, there have been a few sequencing studies of historical herbarium specimens to study the evolution of pathogens over time. Such studies can reveal key information on diversification, host range expansion or when the emergence of new lineages or new geographic introductions occurred, as well as finding rates of evolution (Donegan et al. 2025). However, more commonly seen are studies of extant populations and culture collections, which can date back decades to also provide temporal context, used with comparative genomics and population genomic studies. Population genomics aligns genomes to a reference and uses single nucleotide polymorphisms to construct a phylogenetic tree to track vertical genomic changes and estimate evolutionary rate. Horizontal gene transfer (HGT) and recombination events, found using GC content differences or markers of MGEs, are also usually noted along with differences in the accessory portion of the pangenomes, to find correlations between acquisition of new traits and host range expansion or increases in virulence (Straub et al. 2021). Additionally, shotgun metagenomics has been emerging as a powerful approach, not only for pathogen surveillance and detection but also for understanding disease outbreaks through real-time longitudinal sampling and machine-learning-based modeling approaches. Metagenomic studies have revealed the emergence of novel mutations in response to host resistance, allelic fluctuations across growing seasons under shifting environmental conditions, and HGT driven by microbial interactions, thus providing a robust framework for tracking pathogen genome evolution in real-time (Bhandari et al. 2025; Kaur et al. 2025a).

The genomic signatures of plant pathogenic bacteria are selected for by both environment and host and refined for fitness advantages and even multifunctionality in some cases. However, there is no universal set of genes essential for survival in planta found amongst plant pathogenic bacteria, with fitness and pathogenicity determinants varied even within genera. Plant bacterial pathogens encompass an exceptionally diverse set of lifestyles, including necrotrophic, biotrophic, and hemibiotrophic strategies, vascular and nonvascular colonization, and dissemination through seeds, insect vectors, or rain. Their broad taxonomic distribution, intermixed with nonpathogenic relatives, and lacking one unifying trait imparting pathogenicity, underscores that plant pathogenicity has evolved repeatedly and independently through acquisition, loss, and reconfiguration of modular genetic traits.

Genome evolution in plant pathogenic bacteria is shaped by interconnected ecological interactions and evolutionary processes that collectively influence adaptation, host specificity, lifestyle transitions, and pathogenicity. A conceptual overview of the evolutionary and ecological forces shaping genome evolution in plant pathogenic bacteria. This figure summarizes the dynamic interactions between the core genome, accessory genome, and selective pressures discussed in this review that collectively drive genomic diversification and adaptation in plant pathogenic bacteria. Multiple mechanisms of genomic change (top middle of the figure), including horizontal gene transfer (HGT), recombination, duplication, pseudogenization, and random mutation, contribute to genome plasticity leading to acquisition or loss of adaptive traits. HGT occurs through conjugation, transduction, and transformation, and is frequently mediated by MGEs such as plasmids, prophages, integrative and conjugative elements (ICEs), and transposons. These processes are further modulated by bacterial defense and regulatory systems, including CRISPR-Cas and restriction-modification (R-M) systems, which influence gene flow and genomic stability. These genome-level changes are subsequently shaped by reciprocal selective pressures imposed by plant hosts, insect vectors, and neighboring microbial communities, resulting in bidirectional coevolutionary interactions that influence pathogen fitness, adaptation, and survival (left of the figure). Host-associated selective pressures include plant immunity pathways, such as pattern-triggered immunity (PTI) and effector-triggered immunity (ETI), as well as plant host genetic diversity, and tissue variability. Interactions with host-associated competing microbes and bacteriophages can also impose selective pressures through bacteriocins, type IV and type VI secretion systems, while simultaneously contributing to environmental genetic reservoirs that facilitate the acquisition of adaptive traits. Ecological filtering imposed by UV exposure, temperature fluctuations, water availability, nutritional shifts during survival in soil or water, and agricultural practices such as copper sulfate, antibiotics, and pesticide applications further contribute to genome plasticity, adaptation and evolutionary diversification in plant pathogenic bacteria (see right side of the figure). Collectively, these interconnected ecological and evolutionary processes drive genome dynamics that ultimately shape the fitness, lifestyle, host range, and infection strategies of plant pathogenic bacteria.

Building on this conceptual framework, this review covers 30 genera, 100 species, and 171 strains of plant pathogenic bacteria (Fig. 2) and showcases not only their diversity, but also how their genomic architectures shape both ecological versatility and pathogenicity. Furthermore, we examine how evolutionary processes such as conjugation, transduction, transformation, and mobilome flux shape pangenomes, effector repertoires, and metabolic capabilities, thereby driving diversification. We also highlight cases of adaptation to specific selection pressures uniquely experienced by bacterial plant pathogens. Lastly, we discuss how these mechanisms underlie the evolutionary divergence of infection strategies and life histories, all emerging from the interplay between genome dynamics and ecological context.

Genomic Architecture Itself is an Adaptive Trait Shaped by Lifestyle and Niche Specialization

Genome Size and Lifestyle

Plant pathogenic bacteria exhibit diverse, dynamic genome architectures, from single circular chromosomes to multipartite (chromid- or megaplasmid-bearing), as well as reduced genomes, reflecting tradeoffs among growth, virulence, and ecology (Fig. 3). Many lineages depart from the single circular chromosome paradigm by partitioning essential and adaptive functions across a chromosome and a secondary replicon (megaplasmids or chromids). In Burkholderiaceae, for example chromids replicate like plasmids, yet carry chromosome-like gene content, clarifying how such replicons become indispensable for cell viability and niche adaptation (diCenzo et al. 2019).

Ralstonia solanacearum species complex (RSSC) has a bipartite genome (∼3.7Mb chromosome and a megaplasmid of ∼2Mb, >5000 genes). The megaplasmid differs from a chromosome in its origin of replication and gene content, being enriched for lifestyle and virulence determinants (e.g. hrp/hrc, and a broad effector arsenal), with signatures of horizontal acquisition and a mosaic structure (Salanoubat et al. 2002). Agrobacterium tumefaciens C58 exemplifies architectural diversity (one circular and one linear chromosome and two plasmids). The linear chromosome likely arose via homologous recombination-mediated chromosome-chromid fusion (Wood et al. 2001). Engineering C58 into a single fused replicon (linear or circular) increased growth and competitiveness, while native bipartite architecture favored higher virulence gene expression and transient plant transformation efficiency, suggesting that chromosome number and geometry alone can have an influence on fitness and virulence (Aliu et al. 2025).

Beyond chromosomal count, structural rearrangements can shape genome architecture. Large chromosome inversions have been identified in Erwinia amylovora, likely arising via homologous recombination, often involving rRNA operons. These generate multiple inversion types (Yang et al. 2023), yet maintain near-balanced replichores, implying selection to preserve ori/ter symmetry and replication efficiency. Such rearrangements near the origin of replication can be considered as adaptive repositioning through gene dosage effects (Martinez-Gutierrez et al. 2026).

A survey of plasmids by Urriza et al. (2025) revealed key insights on the distributions of plasmids in Gammaproteobacteria. Plasmids are nearly ubiquitous across plant pathogenic bacteria, with averages per strain of 1.63 in Actinomycetes, 1.88 in Alphaproteobacteria, 1.94 in Betaproteobacteria, 1.79 in Gammaproteobacteria, and 2.11 in Mollicutes. Most plasmids (>90%) are <200 kb, with exceptions in Agrobacterium, Pantoea, and Ralstonia. Some genera such as Brenneria, Leifsonia, and Liberibacter contain few or no plasmids. Mobility and cargo vary by lineages. Most plasmids in Pseudomonadales and Lysobacterales are predicted to be mobile, whereas Enterobacterales plasmids are less so. Insertion sequence content in plasmids also differs (14.7% in Pseudomonadales vs. 4.9% to 8.0% in Enterobacterales). Cargo patterns mirror ecology, with type III secreted effectors (T3Es) popular in Pseudomonas and Xanthomonas and hydrogen peroxide resistance prevalent in Pantoea (Urriza et al. 2025).

Virulence and other host-interaction traits often reside on large, low-copy plasmids. Examples include A. tumefaciens Ti plasmids, or pPATH plasmids in Pantoea agglomerans carrying T3SS, effectors and phytohormone biosynthesis. A large Pantoea plasmid is shown to be domesticated and considered a chromid, due to presence of core genes. Its replication is coordinated with cell division. Over evolutionary time, this plasmid is considered to be chromodized, converging in composition and gene content toward essentiality (Picazo et al. 2024). Persistence of such large low-copy plasmids depends on partitioning systems, toxin-antitoxin modules for postsegregational killing, and regulatory cross-talk with chromosomes (Effe et al. 2025). At the opposite end of the spectrum, some pathogens such as Xanthomonas albilineans and Xylella fastidiosa exhibit convergent reductive evolution, coupled with niche restriction, reflecting adaptation to nutrient-poor xylem and vector/host constraints, while also retaining lineage-specific novel genes such as the toxin albicidin, and abundant prophage remnants, or pseudogenes (Pieretti et al. 2015; Kaur et al. 2025b). Another striking example of rapid genome decay is Erwinia tracheiphila, a host-restricted cucurbit pathogen. E. tracheiphila has experienced massive pseudogenization with ∼20% of coding sequences inactivated, along with proliferation of MGEs, and large rearrangements yielding a mosaic genome. Despite these drastic changes in genome, the species is genetically monomorphic, suggesting a recent population bottleneck and rapid niche emergence (Shapiro et al. 2018). In Candidatus Liberibacter spp. with its host-restricted, phloem-limited lifestyle, compact genomes (∼1.2Mb) reflect extensive loss of metabolic and regulatory genes and long-term adaptation to plant phloem and psyllid vectors. This streamlining is accompanied by erosion of biosynthetic pathways. In Ca. L. solanacearum (CLso), the lysine pathway enzyme, dihydrodipicolinate synthase, has genome reduction- and drift-driven structural and kinetic alterations, that has led to accumulation of deleterious mutations and destabilized protein structure, which is compensated for through increased chaperone expression and stabilized oligomeric state (Gilkes et al. 2024). Despite widespread gene loss, Ca. L. asiaticus (CLas) maintains a highly connected protein-protein interaction network, suggesting that functional multitasking and network plasticity can help offset informational deficits that accompany reductive evolution (Carter et al. 2023).

Amongst the representative genomes in Fig. 2, phylogenetically informed statistics revealed evolutionary linkage between larger genome sizes and the presence of T3SS or T6SS with phylogenetic signals (α) of 1.25 and 2.05 respectively. Our analysis (Fig. 2 and https://github.com/Potnislab/Genome_Evolution_review/tree/main/Scripts/genome_phylogenetic_correlation_stat/GBE_correlation2_labels.R), although not statistically significant, suggests that larger genomes tend to be associated with dicot-infecting pathogens (BH adjusted P-value = 0.25), and consistent with observations from previous studies that smaller genomes are associated with being insect vectored as many insect-vectored pathogens are obligates or fastidious and use their host for metabolism, allowing for genomic reduction (Bendix and Lewis 2018).

Pangenome Architecture Mirrors Ecological Breadth

Plant pathogenic bacteria display striking variations in their pangenome architecture, reflecting differences in ecological niches, host associations, and evolutionary dynamics. The pangenome, the sum of core genes shared by all strains and accessory genes variably distributed, serves as an indicator of adaptive potential. Pangenomes according to the ProPan database are shown by the pie charts in Fig. 3. In open pangenomes, novel genes continue to accrue at high rate, and the accessory genome often exceeds the core genome. In closed pangenomes, gene discovery plateaus, leaving a proportionally larger and more stable core. Ecological exposure to diverse communities, the intensity of HGT, and the prevalence of homologous recombination jointly determine where a lineage falls on this continuum of genome fluidity (Dewar et al. 2024). Pseudomonas syringae, Xanthomonas, RSSC, Pectobacterium, Burkholderia, and Agrobacterium, possess open, expansive pangenomes. These species often inhabit diverse environmental reservoirs beyond the host itself, encountering dense microbial communities and abundant MGEs. As a result, accessory genomes are highly dynamic, frequently remodeling ecologically relevant loci important for within-host and outside-host fitness. In contrast, plant pathogens with more specialized lifestyles tend toward closed or partially closed pangenomes. Highly structured, low-competition habitats promote genomic stability and reduction. Strictly phloem or xylem limited, vector-dependent or host-restricted taxa, such as Candidatus Liberibacter, Leifsonia, Rathayibacter, some Dickeya species, and some Clavibacter lineages, show limited accessory gene turnover, reduced mobilome context, and strong genome streamlining. Even genera with moderate openness display nested patterns of variability. For example, the X. fastidiosa species level pangenome is moderately open. However, individual subspecies exhibit a far more closed dynamics due to ecological confinement and repeated transmission bottlenecks (Vanhove et al. 2019).

Horizontal Gene Transfer is a Primary Engine of Functional Diversification and Evolution of New Traits

HGT is a major force shaping diversification, ecological adaptation, and virulence in plant pathogenic bacteria. Below, we outline the major mechanisms that facilitate HGT, and how each contributes to pathogen evolution, as well as the genomic systems that act to constrain or regulate genetic exchange.

Conjugation

Arguably one of the most common methods for the introduction of new genetic material in bacteria is via conjugation, or the movement of plasmids between compatible cells by the type 4 secretion system (T4SS) sex pili or potentially through outer membrane vesicles (Dell’Annunziata et al. 2021). Plasmids circulating in plant-associated habitats can be narrow-host range confined to a genus or broad-host range plasmids that readily move across taxa. High cell density and biofilm promoting environments such as the phyllosphere, rhizosphere, xylem vessels, or insect guts promote conjugation. Because plasmid maintenance can be costly, conjugation is tightly regulated through quorum sensing, stress responses, and plant-derived cues, with regulators like TraR or Trb integrating the signals (Zatyka and Thomas 1998). Although large-conjugative plasmids impose metabolic burdens, they persist via toxin-antitoxin systems, partitioning modules, and strong selection for adaptive traits associated as cargo genes such as those encoding heavy metal resistance, antibiotic resistance, or virulence. Conjugation occurs not only among pathogenic strains but also between pathogens and commensals, allowing nonpathogenic reservoirs to serve as sources of mobilizable adaptive traits.

Bacteriophages (Transduction)

Beyond serving as vectors of HGT via generalized and specialized transduction, phages are pervasive across major plant pathogenic genera, with the only exception being phytoplasmas (Varani et al. 2013) and repeatedly shape genome architecture. Prophage cargo frequently includes virulence genes, T3Es, cell-wall degrading enzymes (CWDEs), PAAR proteins, ZOT-like toxins, and various stress response factors, making them a major source of accessory functions (Czajkowski 2019). Lineage-specific prophage profiles, such as those in the RSSC, often mirror population structure and coevolutionary history (Greenrod et al. 2022). Phage influence extends beyond cargo. Prophage integration/excision generates recombination hotspots and mosaic genomic islands, along with dynamic T3E movement, e.g. P. syringae hopAR1 transfer on leaf surfaces (Hulin et al. 2023). Phage induction is environmentally contingent with SOS-like cues, and other stresses associated with the plant environment, driving turnover, nutrient flux, and time-shifted host-phage co-evolution in natural communities (Dewald-Wang et al. 2022).

Plant pathogens also host filamentous (Inoviridae) phages, which do not cause host cell lysis but establish an association with the host and produce and release phage particles from dividing cells that can alter adhesion, biofilm, and virulence depending on their state (free, prophage, or superinfective) (Ahmad et al. 2014). Phage ancestry underpins contractile machineries repurposed by bacteria that can reshape pathogen ecology, as discussed in a later section.

Natural Competence (Transformation)

Natural competence enables uptake of extracellular DNA via type IV pili, DNA-binding proteins (FimT, ComEA), and RecA-dependent recombination machinery (Merfa et al. 2023). Though unevenly distributed, competence plays a major evolutionary role in some plant pathogenic bacteria. In RSSC, natural competence is widespread across phylotypes. Strains can take up both plasmids and genomic DNA, with single events replacing chromosomal segments of 30 to 90 kb, and reshaping virulence and effector content (Coupat et al. 2008). While widespread natural competence and intersubspecific recombination have been confirmed in many X. fastidiosa lineages, recent comprehensive analyses reveal that this trait is highly variable and completely absent in several subspecies (Liu et al. 2025). Competence is energetically costly, and therefore tightly regulated. It is induced by environmental cues (nutrient limitation, stress, or host defense response), cell-cell signaling and surface/biofilm growth that promotes pilus activity.

Mobile Genetic Elements

Integrative and Conjugative Elements

Integrative and conjugative elements (ICEs) function as plasmid–phage hybrids, that integrate at specific chromosomal site (typically tRNAs), excise, and transfer horizontally via T4SS-mediated conjugation. Across 300 surveyed genomes, 78 ICEs were identified, with P. syringae carrying the largest number (33), while Xanthomonas oryzae, X. axonopodis, and E. amylovora lacked detectable ICEs (de Assis et al. 2022). ICEs vary widely in size (40 to 161 kb) and share a conserved mobility module (int, oriT, traI, ssb), enabling excision, DNA processing, transfer, and reintegration. In combination with phages and Tn3-family transposases, they could have multiple motility modes, as seen with X. perforans copper island (Bibi et al. 2023), while the PPHGI island in P. syringae pv. phaseolicola can also move via natural transformation (Lovell et al. 2009).

Functionally, ICEs act as major vehicles of adaptive cargo, T3Es, CWDEs, heavy-metal resistance, and other ecological fitness genes. ICEs often exhibit atypical GC content and may encode entry-exclusion genes (eex, traG), preventing redundant uptake and avoiding unnecessary fitness costs. ICEs contribute significantly to intraspecies variability. In the kiwifruit pathogen P. syringae pv. actinidiae (Psa), ICEs exhibit high mobility and mosaicism, with documented translocations, movement of native ICEs, and formation of recombinant chimeric ICEs. Most copper-resistant Psa isolates carry multiple unique, variably positioned ICEs, and even near-clonal epidemic lineages showed ICE-driven genomic instability. ICEs transfer rapidly in vitro (within 30 min), and at far higher frequencies in planta (Colombi et al. 2017). Highly mosaic ICEs in X. arboricola (95-kb) and P. syringae PG2b (90 and 125 kb) further highlight cross-kingdom and interspecies gene flow (Newberry et al. 2019). Another well-characterized island from Pseudomonas, PPHGI-1 (106 kb), carrying avrPphB/hopAR1 effector, undergoes excision during host hypersensitive response (HR), entering an episomal state that suppresses effector expression (Fig. 4), potentially enabling the pathogen to transiently evade host surveillance (Neale et al. 2018). The lineage-specific patterns in ICE presence suggest that differences in ICE entry-control mechanisms may govern whether species readily acquire or exclude ICEs.

Adaptive genomic responses in plant pathogenic bacteria to environmental, chemical, host, and microbial selection pressures. Examples shown in the figure highlight how specific selection pressures drive genomic changes that lead to adaptive outcomes in plant pathogenic bacteria. Under chemical selection, copper sprays select for acquisition of conjugative plasmid containing an ICE with copLAB copper resistance. The ICE can be seen chromosomally integrated in multiple strains of X. perforans (Bibi et al. 2023). Streptomycin pressure selects both for horizontally acquired resistance determinants, such as a mobile integron containing aadA1, which modifies streptomycin in X. oryzae (Xu et al. 2013), and for a single missense mutation changing the ribosomal protein target of streptomycin leading to resistance, in C. michiganensis (Lyu et al. 2019). Host-driven selection pressures have led to diversification, truncation, or loss of avirulence genes to evade effector-triggered immunity. In response to Bs2 deployment, independent emergence of multiple avrBs2 mutant alleles has occurred. A 5 bp insertion causing truncation of the AvrBs2 protein in X. vesicatoria evades ETI but loses virulence (Swords et al. 1996). A single point mutation leading to an amino acid substitution in avrBs2 also evades plant recognition but without loss of virulence (Gassmann et al. 2000). Excision of an ICE carrying avrPphB from the chromosome leads to decreased gene expression and evasion of ETI by P. syringae pv. phaseolicola (Godfrey et al. 2011). Pattern-triggered immunity can select for MAMP diversification. X. euvesicatoria has acquired multiple amino acid substitutions in flg22 allowing for evasion of PTI but at the cost of reduced motility (Malvino et al. 2022). In response to microbial competition, tailocins have evolved from prophages in the genome, such as Pseudovirus P2 in Dickeya dadantii, to become antimicrobial tailocins (Borowicz et al. 2023). Modification of T4 prophage genes, and the addition of genes, has led to development of the type VI secretion system for microbial competition (Gallegos-Monterrosa and Coulthurst 2021). The time scale represents the time for an organism to acquire the adaptation, not for it to become prevalent in the population. For HGT or a point mutation this can occur in a single generation, while evolution of a secretion system may take thousands of generations.

Transposable Elements

Transposable elements (TEs), primarily insertion sequences (ISs) and composite transposons, are abundant drivers of genome plasticity and evolution. A survey of 270 complete genomes identified 35,692 ISs and 71 transposons, with host specialization strongly predicting TE distributions (Fernandes et al. 2024). In P. syringae pv. phaseolicola 1448A, 17 IS types plus miniature inverted repeat transposable elements (MITEs) were detected, with strong enrichment on a virulence plasmid (TEs comprising 25.8% of a 132 kb virulence plasmid vs. 2.8% of the chromosome) (Bardaji et al. 2011).

IS are the simplest TEs (<2.5 kb), that carry only a transposase and short imperfect terminal inverted repeat sequences at their extremities. IS3 and IS5 are especially widespread in plant pathogenic bacterial genomes. These elements disrupt coding sequences or regulatory elements, often inactivating avirulence genes, and reshaping effector repertoires. In X. oryzae, IS-mediated disruption of avr genes and IS-driven deletions of pathogenicity regions contribute to resistance breakdown and strain diversification (Salzberg et al. 2008). E. tracheiphila shows extreme IS proliferation, contributing to extensive pseudogenization with large chromosomal rearrangements (Shapiro et al. 2016). This expansion likely accelerated genome decay by increasing mutation rates and inactivating genes, while simultaneously enhancing genome plasticity. Despite this decay, key virulence determinants have been retained, whereas genes unnecessary outside the host–vector cycle have been preferentially lost. This pattern underscores how IS-mediated genome reduction can function as an adaptive process that reinforces host specialization rather than simply reflecting degenerative evolution. Interestingly, not all reduced genomes show high abundance of IS elements, as seen with X. fastidiosa.

Transposons (Tns), on the other hand, are larger and more complex. They share similar transposition mechanism as ISs, being characterized by two inverted repeated sequences. Tns carry passenger or cargo genes, coding for pathogenicity, resistance to antibiotics, or heavy metals. Domesticated Tns encode products of interest to the host cell and perform new functions. Tns can regulate small noncoding regulatory RNAs and may target plant cell transcriptional activators. TnXax1 is a Tn3-family transposon with a canonical architecture that includes a transposase (tnpA), two site-specific recombination genes with 193 bp resolution sites containing two 14-bp palindromes and short inverted repeats that flank the element and generate 5-bp target site duplications upon insertion, making it as a functional replicative transposon. In Xanthomonas citri and related xanthomonads, homologous TnXax1-like variants shuffle T3E loads, and transcription activator-like effector (TALE) diversity may arise via fork-slippage during replicative Tn3-family transposition, helping to rapidly expand effector repertoire and refine host manipulation strategies (Ferreira et al. 2015). Tn3-family transposons, first identified for their role in the spread of antibiotic resistance, are also observed in R. solanacearum, Pectobacterium, and Dickeya. Their mobility is tightly regulated. The large DDE/D-family transposase TnpA performs a replicative copy-paste transposition, with target DNA guiding precise insertion and enforcing target immunity (Shkumatov et al. 2022). In P. syringae pv. tomato DC3000, an “effector transposon” carrying a T3E HopX1/AvrPphE generates target site duplication of 5 bp and contributes to plasmid structural variation among DC3000 derivatives (Landgraf et al. 2006). In P. syringae pv. syringae populations from mango, a chromosomally located large Tn7-like transposon carrying putative copper/arsenic resistance genes underlies a copper “hyper-resistant” phenotype (Aprile et al. 2021). Together, Tns not only generate mutations, but also rewire virulence repertoires, redistribute adaptive genes, and catalyze genome rearrangements that facilitate host adaptation, immune evasion, and persistence in managed environments.

Recombination

Recombination is a fundamental genetic process through which bacteria exchange DNA, resulting in the incorporation of homologous or nonhomologous genetic sequences into the genome. Together with HGT, recombination reshapes population structure, virulence loci, and host range. Recent pangenome and lifestyle-focused studies further show gene flow between co-occurring commensal, or weakly pathogenic, and pathogenic lineages, clarifying how nonpathogenic reservoirs can contribute adaptive alleles to epidemic strains (Cai et al. 2011; Merda et al. 2016). In P. syringae, recombination disproportionately affects ecologically significant loci and can exceed point mutation as a source of diversity in some lineages (Dillon et al. 2019). In P. syringae pv. phaseolicola, prophage-associated recombination promotes T3E (hopAR1) mobility and contributes to host shifts (Hulin et al. 2023). Transposon-associated rearrangements and plasmid-chromosome exchanges further amplify restructuring (Bardaji et al. 2011). In Agrobacterium, recombination intersects with multipartite genome structure and plasmid ecology to generate new pathogenic lineages. While homologous recombination declines with sequence divergence, the drop-off near species boundaries is modest, implying ample room for gene flow (Costechareyre et al. 2009). Pathogen emergence frequently proceeds via plasmid acquisition and recombination. Nonpathogenic Agrobacterium biovar 1 strains become hairy root pathogens by acquiring Ri plasmids (Bosmans et al. 2015). Despite their modularity, Ti/Ri plasmids exhibit unexpected structural conservation and global transmission, with evolution constrained by conserved backbones even as accessory cargo reshuffles via recombination (Weisberg et al. 2023). Cointegration between Ti plasmids and broad-host range plasmids (e.g. RP4) illustrates one route to recombination-mediated plasmid innovation and mobility (Slater et al. 2009). These dynamics explain how virulence loci can recombine into nononcogenic plasmids to seed novel oncogenic replicons and why plasmid exchange repeatedly underlies emergent agrobacterial diseases. Similar recombination-driven diversification occurs in Xanthomonas, where genome-wide exchange drives race shifts, TALE diversification, and host range expansion (Newberry et al. 2019). X. fastidiosa exemplifies a naturally competent, recombination-rich vascular pathogen in which homologous recombination operates over both ancient and contemporary timescales. Both inter- and intra-subspecific recombination events are common, with 2 to 10 kb regions (and >10 kb in some clades), resulting in ongoing gene flow with recombination over mutation ratios >2 and recombination hotspots in loci linked to regulation, colonization, and host evasion patterns that help explain rapid emergence in newly mixed global trade networks (Vanhove et al. 2019).

Gene Duplication and Neofunctionalization

Gene duplication, although less frequent in bacteria than in eukaryotes, contributes meaningfully to genome evolution, particularly in lineages with large genomes and active MGEs. Duplicated genes arise through unequal recombination, transposon-mediated duplication, or replicative transposition (Birchler and Yang 2022), and can diverge under relaxed purified selection to acquire new or specialized functions, i.e. neofunctionalization. In plant pathogenic bacteria, duplication-driven neofunctionalization is evident in gene families involved in virulence, competition, nutrient acquisition mechanisms, or response to abiotic stress. TALEs in Xanthomonas, Rip proteins in Ralstonia, and Hop families in P. syringae often contain multiple paralogs that evolved through duplication followed by divergence in secretion signals, host targets, and immune suppressive functions. The modular and repetitive structure of TALEs facilitates unequal recombination and slippage events that lead to repeat number variation, creating new DNA-binding specificities and allowing rapid neofunctionalization toward novel host susceptibility genes or bypassing deployed resistance alleles (Teper et al. 2023). Soft rot pathogens harbor expanded, functionally specialized CWDEs, offering them the ability to degrade heterogeneous plant polysaccharides, via substrate-specific paralogs (Fig. 2). Similar patterns occur beyond host manipulation. Duplicated antimicrobial toxins, transporters, or enzymes diversify to support competition and nutrient acquisition. For example, expansion of TonB-dependent receptors in Xanthomonas enables uptake and transport of different sugars, iron, or vitamins (Blanvillain et al. 2007), and diversification of tailocin receptor-binding proteins expands killing specificity (Fautt et al. 2025).

Defense Systems (CRISPR-Cas, Restriction-Modification Systems) Evolve Under Pressure From MGEs and Shape Lineage Boundaries

CRISPR-Cas

Clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats, or CRISPR, regions of DNA composed of shortdirect repeats (21 to 47 bp) and similarly sized nonrepetitive spacer sequences, work together with CRISPR-associated (cas) genes to protect the cell against viruses and foreign interference. They operate through an RNAi-like mechanism to recognize and degrade foreign complementary DNA or RNA (Koonin et al. 2017). By restricting acquisition of foreign DNA, CRISPR-Cas systems influence bacterial evolution by modulating HGT. CRISPR spacer repertoires frequently match phages and plasmids, carrying virulence cargo, consistent with lineage-specific selection against reacquisition of particular MGEs and enabling reconstruction of phage and plasmid circulation. Emerging global resources such as SpacerDB now allow spacer mining and target linkage (https://spacers.jgi.doe.gov). Across lineages, shifts in CRISPR activity or its loss often coincide with major ecological transitions. In X. campestris, loss of the CRISPR-Cas locus increased genome plasticity, including acquisition of new T3Es, and MGEs, and is associated with the transition from mesophyll- to vascular-pathogenic lineages (Paauw et al. 2025). CRISPR-Cas is heterogeneously distributed and frequently attenuated in other plant pathogens. In RSSC, CRISPR-Cas loci are rare and often inactive, with other antiviral systems (Restriction-Modification, abortive infection) dominating (Motoche-Monar et al. 2024). Some type I CRISPR systems also regulate endogenous targets. Self-targeting spaces can modulate expression of prophages or MGEs (Wimmer and Beisel 2019), while MGE-encoded anti-CRISPR (Acr) proteins can suppress CRISPR activity, e.g. X. albilineans Acrs blocking Cas3-mediated degradation (Hong et al. 2018). These dynamics underscore a key evolutionary tradeoff, i.e. strong CRISPR protection limits plasmid/phage invasion, but also restricts access to adaptive traits, and loss of CRISPR can catalyze bursts of gene acquisition that facilitate host shifts, or resistance to chemicals. Thus, CRISPR's gatekeeping of HGT must be balanced against adaptive needs in dynamic environments.

Restriction-modification Systems

Restriction-modification (R-M) systems distinguish self from nonself DNA through methylation via methyltransferases and cleavage of unmethylated foreign DNA by restriction endonucleases (Tock and Dryden 2005). They act as major barriers to HGT and thereby influence lineage diversification. In X. fastidiosa, Type I R-M systems strongly regulate natural competence. Comparative genomics across 129 strains revealed extensive recombination among specificity subunit (hsdS) domains, generating diverse target specificities and shaping intraspecies lineage structure (O’Leary and Burbank 2023). In Pectobacterium carotovorum, the R-M system uses its methyltransferases by epigenetically repressing type IV restriction endonuclease genes to avoid self-DNA degradation (Birkholz et al. 2022). The R-M system is also in R. solanacearum and is upregulated during phage exposure (Wang et al. 2024). Pathogens with reduced genomes, such as CLas, retain functional R-M genes, including those within prophage regions (Zheng et al. 2018), suggesting that R-M systems continue to play a role in preventing foreign DNA entry despite genome streamlining.

Selection Pressures Unique to Agricultural Systems, Such as Widespread Chemical Use or Deployment of Disease Resistance Genes, Leave Strong Genomic Signatures

Chemical and Antibiotic Resistance

Chemical pesticides and antibiotics have driven widespread resistance in plant pathogenic bacteria since their use in the 1960s. Copper resistance (CuR) first reported in X. campestris pv. vesicatoria in the 1980s, is largely mediated by self-transmissible plasmids, of varying sizes (200 to 300 kb) (Bender et al. 1990), encoding copLAB operons. Although these systems can be costly in copper-free environments, global surveys reveal diverse cop alleles shaped by local copper-use histories (Behlau et al. 2017). Copper resistance can also arise by recruiting and rearranging multiple efflux determinants (copG, plasmid variants of cusCBA, and chromosomal variants of czcCBA). In X. perforans (Xp), chromosomally integrated CuR genomic islands, closely related to Stenotrophomonas acidaminiphila genes, now predominate, suggesting recurrent HGT from nonpathogenic reservoirs (Bibi et al. 2023). Some Xp strains harbor both plasmid and chromosome-encoded CuR, which elevates tolerance (Fig. 4). Other examples of copper resistance HGT via ICE and transposon have been discussed in the other sections of this review.

Beyond copper, the evolution of resistance to agricultural antibiotics, including quinolones, streptomycin, kasugamycin, and gentamicin, has been extensively reviewed (Sundin and Wang 2018). This review details how resistance in plant pathogenic bacteria predominantly arises through horizontally acquired determinants such as strAB and aadA, as well as point mutations in target genes, such as rpsL and gyrA (Fig. 4). More recent studies have documented additional resistance mechanisms, including integron-associated aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes, transposon-borne strAB in E. amylovora, reduced drug uptake via loss of opp/dpp permeases, and efflux-mediated resistance (e.g. tetC). Importantly, experimental evolution work continues to show that resistance often incurs fitness tradeoffs, such as reduced virulence, growth or motility, highlighting the ecological costs that shape resistance dynamics in natural pathogen populations.

Plant Immunity and the Coevolutionary Response

Plant immunity imposes one of the strongest and more persistent selection pressures on plant-associated bacteria, and this pressure leaves deep signatures in pathogen genomes. The interplay between pattern-triggered immunity (PTI) and effector-triggered immunity (ETI) continually reshapes pathogen genomes through point mutations, recombination, gene loss, and the diversification of secretion system effectors.

PTI-driven Evolution

PTI is initiated when cell surface pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) recognize conserved microbe-associated molecular patterns, such as flagellin peptide flg22, triggering oxidative bursts, cell-wall reinforcement, and salicylic acid (SA)-mediated defenses (Chinchilla et al. 2006). Because PTI targets highly conserved structures, bacterial genomes frequently evolve microbe-associated molecular pattern (MAMP) variants that minimize recognition while balancing essential physiological roles, as seen with flg22 variants (Fig. 4). Genomes may also encode mixed immunogenic and nonimmunogenic MAMP variants, as seen with cold shock protein, where the nonimmunogenic forms block perception of immunogenic ones and suppress plant immune responses (Stevens et al. 2024). Alongside epitope modification, pathogens evolve suppressors of hormone-mediated immunity. Some inhibit SA biosynthesis (T3E HopI1) and others mimic plant hormones (coronatine-mimicking jasmonic acid; RaxX mimicking PSY) (Ronald and Joe 2018). These adaptations collectively reshape the pathogen genome, altering surface structures, regulatory circuits, and effector repertoires, to reduce immune visibility, while maintaining core biological functions.

ETI-driven Evolution

ETI exerts an even sharper selective filter. When plant R proteins detect pathogen effectors (Avr proteins), they trigger a HR to restrict pathogen spread (Flor 1971). Pathogen genomes can evolve rapidly to escape recognition while preserving effector functions essential for fitness. Classic mechanisms for evolution include single-amino acid substitution in P. syringae pv. tomato, T3Es avrPto and avrPtoB (syn. hopAB2) that reduces mRNA levels and diminishes recognition by tomato Pto (Kunkeaw et al. 2010). Mutations in avrPphE, and allelic diversity in avrD avoid recognition by R2 gene in bean and Rpg4 in soybean, respectively (Keith et al. 1997; Stevens et al. 1998). Structural variation generated by recombination or MGEs can also allow the pathogen to escape host recognition, as seen with the alteration of the structure of T3E, avrXa7, bypassing the rice xa7 resistance gene (Yang et al. 2005). Insertion of MGEs such as MITEs or IS elements can inactivate avirulence genes, as seen with avrA in R. solanacearum (Robertson et al. 2004) and avrBs1 in Xanthomonas (Kearney and Staskawicz 1990). Complete effector loss also occurs, such as the loss of avrPphF and avrPphB, located on pathogenicity islands, under bean R gene selection pressure (Rivas et al. 2005). Beyond single genes, entire effector families such as the P. syringae HopZ family diversify through a combination of pathoadaptation and HGT, producing divergent alleles that alter how hosts recognize the pathogen (Ma et al. 2006). TALEs represent a dramatic case of immune-driven effector evolution. While terminal regions are conserved, the central repeats diversify quickly through expansion, recombination, transposons, and repeat-variable di-residue substitution, creating new DNA-binding specificities (Erkes et al. 2017). R gene deployment, such as xa13, selects for PthXo1 variants with new RVDs and targets (Mishra et al. 2025), and even single repeat changes can shift host specificity, as in TAL14 variants (Cohn et al. 2016). Many X. oryzae pv. oryzae strains also carry atypical TALE variants called interfering TALEs (iTALEs or truncTALEs), that suppress Xa1-mediated recognition in rice (Ji et al. 2020). Plasmid recombination, including diverse pthA variants in X. citri, further accelerates TALE innovation. These rapidly evolving loci exemplify how genome structure promotes adaptation to host immunity. T3E diversification is widespread across genera, although the specific host-driven selection pressures are not always known. Many T3Es lack recognizable homologs, suggesting frequent phage-mediated acquisition. Their modular structure enables terminal reassortment, a recombination-driven process that frequently generates chimeric and truncated variants, and drives rapid expansion of effector diversity (Stavrinides et al. 2008).

Beyond the Host Selection Pressure: Environmental Factors and Persistence Outside the Host Shape Pathogen Evolution

The environment acts as a powerful driver of pathogen evolution, at times, acting independently and in some cases, complementing and interacting with host-mediated selection pressures. Plant-associated bacteria inhabit highly variable environments, such as leaf surfaces, soil, and water, where temperature, radiation, nutrient availability, and chemical stressors fluctuate rapidly. These conditions select for genomic traits that enhance persistence, stress tolerance, and survival outside the host, often through gene gain, duplication, allelic diversification, or regulatory control. P. syringae illustrates this through ice nucleation genes (ina genes), which both promote frost damage to facilitate host entry and protect the bacterium from intracellular freezing (Lindow 1983). On the phyllosphere, exposure to temperature fluctuations, solar radiation, ultraviolet (UV) light, and oxidative stress, favors protective traits such as xanthomonadin pigments in Xanthomonas, and plasmid-borne rulAB UV-tolerance genes in P. syringae. Under resource limitation, many bacteria arrest growth but maintain viability, preventing irreversible damage. The soil-borne pathogen R. solanacearum can enter a viable but nonculturable (VBNC) state, upregulating stress-associated genes (omp, oxyR, rpoS, and dps) and persist until favorable conditions return (van Elsas et al. 2000).

Morris et al. (2009) proposed a new paradigm that asserts that nonhost habitats and community interactions such as predation, competition, and abiotic stress, can preselect suites of traits, such as stress tolerance, secretion systems, and efflux systems, that later manifest as pathogenic success in plants. This view urges us to expand beyond an agrocentric lens by treating environmental ecology as a driver of plant pathogen evolution. Morris et al. used a cross-kingdom analogy to explain evolution of virulence. Escherichia coli O157:H7 evolved resistance to amoebal predation incidentally enabled survival in macrophages, because amebae and macrophages share convergent phagocytic constraints. In effect, selection in a nonhost reservoir preadapts pathogen for virulence in a different host context, suggesting existence of dual-use traits in pathogens. Thus, selective pressures from biotic and abiotic stressors outside the host may favor the evolution of adaptive traits that simultaneously enhance persistence outside the host and act as virulence determinants within plants. Exopolysaccharides (EPS) may be one such example, it protects the pathogen both inside and outside the host by contributing to adhesion and biofilm formation, water retention, nutrient sequestration, signaling, ROS resistance, and protection from microbial antagonism, while also being essential for virulence in xylem colonizing pathogens (Killiny et al. 2013).

Beyond promoting persistence outside the host, environmental variability experienced during host colonization can also reshape pathogen evolution, by altering both host immune dynamics, and by affecting the pathogen's strategies for surviving episodic stress. Field studies demonstrate that X. perforans populations exhibit seasonal allelic fluctuations in response to shifting climatic conditions, leading to the coexistence of diverse strains within a single growing season. This standing genetic diversity likely provides adaptive flexibility in response to unpredictable environmental fluctuations (Bhandari et al. 2025). Likewise, exposure to elevated ozone levels drives rapid, transient mutational bursts within a single growing season, independent of plant resistance, thereby accelerating diversification (Kaur et al. 2025a). Thus, pathogen evolution must be reframed within a broader ecological and environmental context that governs pathogen life history, and defines diverse habitats in which bacteria persist, diversify, and exchange genes. Such an expanded approach will require relating patterns of mobilome flux to exposure histories in environmental reservoirs; tracking MGE circulation in microbial communities and pathogen populations to understand how these elements seed virulence and stress adaptative traits; quantifying how abiotic fluctuations influence mutation and recombination rates and alter pathogenicity traits or host range; and integrating microbial competition into the framework of evolution of virulence by identifying dual-use traits.

Microbial Competition is an Underappreciated Major Force of Genome Evolution

Competitive interactions between microbes on leaf surfaces, in soil, or within insect vectors can be as influential as plant immunity in shaping effector diversification and accessory genome turnover. Under pathogen attack, plants recruit protective microbiota that suppress the pathogen in addition to serving as a microbial legacy for future plant generations (Pereira et al. 2023). Network-based studies further show that pathogen invasions can alter microbiome structure and interactions while maintaining the same overall functional output (Bhandari et al. 2023). There is an ongoing war among microbes and pathogens for survival, niche space, nutrients, and other limited resources. From the pathogen's perspective, this competition often involves production of antimicrobial weapons, including toxins, bacteriolytic enzymes, phages, metabolic derivatives, antibiotics, and bacteriocins, that together impose strong selection pressure on cell surface receptors, immunity cassettes, and MGEs (Riley 1998). Indeed, recombination hotspots in Korean field isolates of R. solanacearum phylotype I were enriched for predicted contact-dependent inhibition loci, indicating that antagonistic interactions among co-occurring strains actively remodel genomic regions linked to interbacterial warfare (Prokchorchik et al. 2020).

Bacteriocins

Bacteriocins, narrow-spectrum antimicrobial peptides, found in nearly 99% of bacterial species, can be plasmid-encoded or chromosomally integrated and are produced at different stages of bacterial growth under a wide range of environmental conditions (Daw and Falkiner 1996). They fall broadly into three types: ribosomally synthesized and post-translationally modified peptides (RiPP family; <10 kDa), large unmodified proteins, and phage-tail-like bacteriocins (known as tailocins). Many plant pathogenic bacteria produce bacteriocins, including Xanthomonas, Pseudomonas, Erwinia, and Clavibacter. Their activity is often strain-specific, as seen with glycinecin A from X. campestris pv. glycines, which has two precursor proteins (GlyA, GlyB) that mature into toxin subunits that affect cell membrane permeability (Pham et al. 2004). Bacteriocin-mediated competition can shape pathogen dynamics, exemplified by X. perforans, where BCN-A/B/C conferred a fitness advantage that promoted displacement of X. euvesicatoria in tomato fields (Hert et al. 2005). In Pseudomonas, bacteriocin-mediated fitness benefits are strongest when producers are initially rare, and effectiveness can be modulated by virulence background (e.g. T3SS) (Ehau-Taumaunu and Hockett 2022). Tailocins are phage-derived contractile nanomachines that lack capsids and typically target closely related strains via LPS receptors (Fig. 4). Their core structural genes are conserved, while receptor-binding modules diversify rapidly via HGT and recombination (Baltrus et al. 2019). Tailocin production is triggered by stress responses such as SOS pathway through exogenous stressors (UV, oxidants, chemicals) or presence of other competitor strains. Tailocin diversity reflects strong spatial, temporal, and population-level selection pressures (Backman et al. 2024). Coexisting Pseudomonas lineages often maintain stable, lineage-specific tailocin variants, consistent with balancing selection. Host range is largely determined by tail fibers, and fiber swapping can shift specificity. Cross-genus activity also occurs, as seen with dickeyocins against multiple pseudomonads (Borowicz et al. 2026). Interestingly, rhizospheric pseudomonads can produce two types of tailocins from a single lysis cassette, enabling both interspecies and intraspecies competition (Dorosky et al. 2017). Producer strains shield themselves from tailocin-mediated killing via LPS modifications, absence of O-specific antigen receptors, or thick LPS. Beyond classical resistance, sensitive cells can survive tailocin attacks via a persistence-like physiological state, especially in the stationary phase, allowing long-term coexistence without acquiring costly resistance (Kandel et al. 2020; Carim et al. 2021). At the genomic level, selection concentrates on receptor tuning (LPS) and immunity cassettes rather than obligate resistance alleles (Backman et al. 2024).

Streptomyces genomes can carry 8 to 83 biosynthetic gene clusters encoding diverse antimicrobials that drive competition in the soil. Exposure to antimicrobials promotes HGT, allowing ICE-associated BGCs to disseminate through conjugative plasmids (Norte et al. 2025).

Contact-dependent Mechanisms

Bacteria also engage in contact-dependent killing via T4SS and T6SS. The T4SS-mediated killing has been demonstrated in Pseudomonas putida, X. citri, and the opportunistic human pathogen Stenotrophomonas maltophilia. In bacterial genomes, prophages have led to the development of contractile tail structures, such as extracellular contractile injection systems, which include tailocins, and cell-wall-anchored T6SSs which also shows evidence of evolutionary origin from bacteriophages (Fig. 4) (Gallegos-Monterrosa and Coulthurst 2021). Microbial competition may select for the T6SS, which uses a contractile mechanism to inject effectors, including those that inhibit both bacteria and fungi, into competitor, or prey cells (Yan et al. 2025). Amongst the representative genomes chosen for this review (Fig. 2), a phylogenetically informed logistic regression revealed that if a pathovar has T6SS, it is 6.5 times more likely to be seedborne (BH adjusted P-value 0.013), with almost no phylogenetic signal (α= 89.4). In our analysis, 108 out of 167 of the representative bacterial plant pathogens (64.7%) and 14 out of 30 genera possess an intact T6SS. This demonstrates that the T6SS is relatively common among bacterial plant pathogens. Some Pseudomonas and Xanthomonas genomes were found to have T6SS components vgrG and hcp but lacked other core machinery genes, suggesting either HGT of these components or a possible loss of T6SS. RSSC genomes encode highly diverse T6SS effector-immunity pairs, and show lineage-specific distributions shaped by plant-associated selective pressures. A recent pangenome atlas revealed 1,066 auxiliary T6SS toxin clusters across 99 genomes, encompassing 25 effector types with broad substrate specificities, and horizontally acquired genetic neighborhoods, highlighting pangenome fluidity that underpins effector-immunity diversification in RSSC. Despite this diversity, some nonsoilborne Ralstonia lineages, including banana-infecting strains, have convergently lost their T6SSs, suggesting that selective pressures in certain environmental contexts may disfavor maintaining this system (Aoun et al. 2024). While T6SS is a relatively large system, capable of spanning the length of the cell, recent studies have revealed that the T6SS has little to no fitness cost for the cell (Zhang et al. 2024). Although, having a T6SS may be disadvantageous in certain environments where it may act as an unintended pore to let in harmful substances (Gupta et al. 2021), and it may be detrimental for the bacterial population by inciting attacks from another bacteria's T6SS, since some bacteria only activate their T6SS against competitors that also have a T6SS (Basler et al. 2013).

However, due to its prevalence, the T6SS is likely advantageous for the bacteria that possess it, especially in bacteria that live epiphytically or in the rhizosphere where microbial competitors are commonly encountered (Liyanapathiranage et al. 2022). In addition to providing an advantage in microbial competition on the leaf surface, the T6SS can also play a role in HGT by lysing bacterial cells and freeing DNA for uptake via natural competence so that the pathogen can acquire new traits (Thomas et al. 2017). In Xanthomonas translucens, the T6SS and T4SS have overlapping roles in microbial competition and are found to be mutually exclusive with strains possessing only one system or the other. Interestingly though, many of the T4SS had experienced pseudogenization and loss of microbial antagonism, suggesting a potential shift in advantage to T6SS for microbial competition, possibly due to the fitness cost of T4SS (Peduzzi et al. 2025).

The Evolution of Divergent Lifestyles and Infection Strategies is Driven by Modular Traits that are Continually Restructured Through MGE Flux

Commensal vs. Pathogenic Lifestyle

Plant-associated bacteria span a continuum from commensal, weakly pathogenic to aggressively pathogenic, and transitions along this continuum are common and mechanistically diverse. Not only are bacterial plant pathogens phylogenetically diverse, but they also have diverse mechanisms of causing disease, suggesting that pathogenicity to plants has independently evolved multiple times. Lifestyle is an emergent property of modular traits, such as secretion systems, effector repertoires, metabolic capacities, toxins, and regulatory networks that are frequently gained or lost through HGT or decay under niche specialization. A majority of the members of Streptomyces are saprophytic soil dwellers that are nonpathogenic to plants. A handful of species that are pathogenic have acquired a pathogenicity island for thaxtomin production. Interestingly, S. acidiscabies and S. ipomoeae, which are not closely related among Streptomyces, both have this island, suggesting that it was acquired through HGT and that pathogenicity independently evolved (Huguet-Tapia et al. 2016). Another example of acquisition of pathogenicity through HGT is seen in P. agglomerans on onion, with the plasmid pBC1C carrying Allium thiosulfinate resistance and the pantaphos toxin conferring pathogenicity to commensal strains (Shin et al. 2025).

Type III secretion system (T3SS) and its effector repertoires remain central to many Gram-negative plant pathogens, yet their distribution across plant-associated bacteria cautions against using T3SS as a strict lifestyle delimitator. T3SS injectisome originated through repurposing of the flagellum and subsequently diversified into multiple families, with extensive HGT shaping its current distribution. In Xanthomonas, T3SS presence/absence is decoupled from lifestyle at the genus scale. Some commensals lack a functional Hrp2 T3SS cluster, whereas others retain T3SS regulators or partial clusters (Pena Michelle et al. 2024). Conversely, pathogens tend to accumulate large T3E repertoires through stepwise acquisitions and recombination (Jacobs et al. 2015). Similar observation in P. syringae have been made in surveys of agricultural and nonagricultural environments, where some P. syringae isolates from nonagricultural environments lack the canonical T3SS, yet can persist in plant tissues or cause disease, highlighting complex evolutionary trajectories involving acquisition and loss of the T3SS (Diallo et al. 2012). Yet, large combinatorial deletion experiments in P. syringae suggest that pathogenicity often maps more strongly to the effector repertoire than to the secretion system itself (Chakravarthy et al. 2018). Commensal-to-pathogen transition by plasmid acquisition is documented in the genus Pantoea. Pathogenicity in pathovars gypsophilae and betae depends on presence of a large pathogenicity plasmid (pPATH) that encodes the Hrp T3SS, its associated effectors, and additional virulence factors such as auxin and cytokinin biosynthesis genes, along with four IS element families. The structure of pPATH, including gene remnants from other pathogens, suggests recent acquisition of a complete virulence system, potentially transforming commensal strains into pathogens in a small number of evolutionary steps (Manulis and Barash 2003).

In Xanthomonas, some strains have Hrp regulators (HrpG and HrpX) without a T3SS, supporting a stepwise evolutionary model in which regulatory control and T2SS-exported enzymes precede full T3SS injectisomes, with later acquisition of T3SS/T3SE amplifying virulence potential (Jacobs et al. 2015). Indeed, commensal and weakly pathogenic strains of Xanthomonas occupy distinct genomic clusters from virulent pathogens, with lifestyle best predicted by carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes) (Pena Michelle et al. 2024). Commensals and weak pathogens carry different CAZyme repertoires and show evidence of T2SS-linked strategies compared to pathogens. In planta gnotobiotic studies further reveal that opportunistic Xanthomonas can drive leaf-microbiome dysbiosis via T2SS-secreted CAZymes, shifting from commensal to opportunistic pathogenic lifestyle when host immunity is compromised (Pfeilmeier et al. 2024).

Host and Tissue Specificity

Host specificity in plant pathogenic bacteria is an emergent, multigenic property shaped by interplay between pathogen virulence factors and the host's immune and physiological landscape. Identifying factors determining host specificity remains an open and complex question. Early work in characterizing host specificity determinants focused on the contribution of individual T3SEs as determinants of host range, and several examples support this view as indicated in the previous section. Subsequent comparative and functional studies revealed that host specificity generally emerges from the combined action of effector repertoires, rather than single genes. This led to the repertoire-for-repertoire hypothesis, which proposes that interactions between suites of core and variable effectors and corresponding host immune components and not phylogenetic relatedness of strains predict pathovar identity and host range, indicating that host-driven immune selection sculpts effectoromes (Hajri et al. 2009). Such hypothesis was noted in case of Xanthomonas, Ralstonia and P. syringae. Recent studies, however, have expanded this view. Genome-wide association studies in X. perforans and X. euvesicatoria demonstrate involvement of factors beyond T3Es, involving TonB-dependent receptors, and additional loci likely influencing nutrient uptake and metabolic compatibility (Newberry et al. 2023). In addition, MAMPs, LPS, transcriptional regulators, CWDEs, and chemotactic sensors are also indicated to act as ecological filters that determine whether bacteria can access and colonize host tissues, thus, acting as host-range determinants (Jacques et al. 2016). Variation in PRRs and MAMP epitopes impacts the strength of PTI and shapes which effector sets are favored by selection, showing host influence on genomic variation.

Tissue specificity in plant pathogenic bacteria reflects adaptations that enable colonization of distinct niches, such as vascular or nonvascular tissues, each presenting unique structural, chemical, and immune environments. Specific enzymes can dictate infection niches; for instance, the CWDE CbsA (cellobiosidase) acts as a phenotypic switch determining vascular pathogenicity in xanthomonads (Gluck-Thaler et al. 2020). Gain of cbsA enables apoplast-colonizing X. translucens to invade the vasculature, whereas its loss shifts vascular pathogens toward nonvascular colonization. Experimental evolution in C. michiganensis demonstrated that tissue specificity can be genetically tractable. Repeated passaging in vascular vs. nonvascular tissue enhanced pathogen fitness within each tissue and selected for phenotypically divergent traits that included altered EPS production, surface attachment, and transcriptional regulation (Verma et al. 2025). Additionally, for pathogens that have adapted to insect vectors for dissemination, their host range and tissue specificity has become dictated by their vector behavior (Perilla-Henao and Casteel 2016).

Insect-Vectored Transmission

Several high-impact phytobacteria rely on persistent, circulative, and often propagative transmission in specialized hemipteran vectors. These associations require lineage-specific genetic toolkits that enable acquisition and retention within the insect host. Although the broad transmission mode is similar, the molecular interactions and ecological dynamics vary substantially among pathogens.

X. fastidiosa represents a model of highly specialized foregut colonization. Successful colonization of the sharpshooter/spittlebug foregut depends on a suite of adhesins (HxfA/HxfB, FimA), type I/IV pili, and diffusible signaling factor (DSF) regulation of biofilm formation (Killiny and Almeida 2014). Mutants in EPS biosynthesis (gumH/D) or adhesins show reduced attachment or retention and poor transmission. DSF quorum signaling tunes a balance between biofilm adhesiveness (vector retention) and within-plant movement and virulence, as indicated by rpfF mutants defective in DSF synthesis and vector transmissibility, underscoring signaling-driven tradeoffs at the insect-plant interface (Newman et al. 2004). Real-time imaging and SEM suggest that vector behavior dynamically disrupt foregut biofilms, making acquisition a stochastic, behavior-constrained process, not merely a bacterial trait (Backus and Shugart 2024).

In contrast, CLas is phloem-limited and transmitted by psyllids in a persistent mode, with long latent periods and weeks-long transmission competence after nymphal acquisition, consistent with circulative propagative biology within vector. Vector competence maps onto pathogen gene expression. During acquisition by the vector, CLas shows high transcription of pilus and core metabolism, while flagellar genes are preferentially expressed in psyllids, but not in planta, suggesting context-dependent motility/adhesion gene expression that facilitate vector colonization. CLas is also equipped with a peroxiredoxin enzyme that weakens nitric-oxide driven systemic acquired resistance signaling, thereby helping CLas to be acquired and transmitted multiple times by highly reproductive psyllids during the short citrus flush period (Jain et al. 2022). Other Liberibacter species (CLso) engage with psyllid immune pathways, such as autophagy, in haplotype specific ways that influence bacterial titers and transmission efficiency (Sarkar et al. 2023).

While hemipteran vectors dominate phytobacterial transmission, other insects can contribute through semi-persistent or transitive dissemination. Beetles, bees, and other pollinators often visit a broader range of host plants, expanding opportunities for cross-species transmission. Conversely, some phytobacteria have evolved dedicated molecular pathways for interaction with their insect vectors. For example, Pantoea stewartii have developed another separate and distinct T3SS (PSI-2) from the one used to colonize the plant host (PSI-1) specifically to mediate interactions with their beetle insect vectors (Correa et al. 2012). Pantoea species possess up to three distinct T3SSs, PSI-1 (HrcI), PSI-2 (SPI-1), and PSI-3 (SPI-2), which exhibit multiple variants and complex evolutionary trajectories. Evidence suggests that the PSI-1 system was vertically inherited from a common ancestor of enterobacterial plant pathogens following divergence from the Erwinia-Pantoea lineage, whereas the PSI-3 system was likely acquired horizontally from other Enterobacteriaceae. Phylogenetic analyses further indicate that some T3SSs were retained from ancestral plant pathogenic lineages, while others were independently acquired, potentially contributing to adaptation to insect vectors or hosts (Kirzinger et al. 2015).

Seed Transmission

Bacteria reach seeds through vascular, floral, fruit, or postharvest pathways. Not all plant pathogenic bacteria can be seed-transmitted, but the ones that are seed-transmitted are also phylogenetically distant, suggesting that seed transmissibility reflects convergent evolution rather than a single genetic trait. The first necessary step is to reach the seed, therefore most seedborne bacteria are motile, although, nonmotile bacteria, such as Rathayibacter can rely on vectors such as seed gall nematodes (Murray et al. 2017). Flower-to-seed transmission can be permissive, especially in bean, as incompatible pathogens, nonpathogens, and T3SS knockouts can reach seeds via flower inoculation (Darsonval et al. 2008). Transcriptomic analyses of X. citri pv. fuscans on bean show early upregulation of T3SS genes and CAZymes during seed filling, but not maturation, suggesting early host-interaction followed by a stealth phase (Darrasse et al. 2026).

Seed-transmitted bacteria must also be desiccation tolerant and be able to enter dormancy or a viable but nonculturable (VBNC) state (Gerna et al. 2026), which support persistence through dry storage and resuscitation at germination. However, desiccation tolerance is not the only trait required for survival on seeds. X. vesicatoria, which externally colonizes seeds, remained viable on nonhost seeds for only 30 d, while it remained viable on tomato seed for at least 100 d and at least 5 months on pepper seeds (Bashan et al. 1982). This implies that interactions between the seed and the pathogen must take place for long-term survival on seeds. Furthermore, logistic regression analysis of the reference genomes (Fig. 2) revealed that bacteria that cause disease on a diverse range of plant hosts are 83.5% less likely to be seedborne (BH adjusted P-value 0.095), highlighting the importance of pathogen host specificity for seed transmissibility.

Bacteria located inside the embryo have more protection from the environment and can remain viable for extended periods of time, such as 34 years as seen in Acidovorax citrulli in watermelon seeds (Dutta et al. 2016). This provides seedborne bacterial pathogens the advantage of disseminating long distances, even globally through the seed trade, and can therefore potentially lead to the introduction of new genetic material to existing populations. Lastly, and most importantly, for bacteria to be seed transmitted it must have seed to seedling transmission and infect the next generation. Not all seedborne microbes are also seed transmitted, colonizing the seedlings from seeds creates a bottleneck, with only 43 taxa found on bean seedlings from 223 taxa found on the seeds (Chesneau et al. 2022). Causes for this bottleneck are still not understood, although microbial competition may play a role, the plant may not be a determining factor as both compatible, incompatible, and null-interaction bacteria have been found to transmit from seed to seedling.

Lifestyles: Biotrophs, Necrotrophic and Hemibiotrophic Modes

Although the biotroph-hemibiotroph-necrotroph continuum was developed largely from fungal pathosystems based on microscopic observations, this terminology has been used in the literature for plant pathogenic bacteria even though it does not translate well. Gram-negative plant pathogenic bacteria remain extracellular throughout infection, making it impossible to classify them by the same criteria used for fungal lifestyles. As a result, bacterial pathogens are inconsistently labeled as biotrophic, necrotrophic, or hemibiotrophic in the literature. Biotrophs obtain nutrients from living cells, whereas necrotrophs derive nutrients by killing host tissue. Hemibiotrophs combine features of both lifestyles, by initially colonizing and feeding on living cells, then transitioning to a necrotrophic phase in which they kill host cells to acquire nutrients. The ability of plant pathogenic bacteria to suppress or avoid immunity (stealth mode) vs. tissue destruction (brute-force mode) and metabolic dependence vs. versatility, jointly predict their trophic levels (Arnold and Preston 2019). These frameworks and association of fundamentally different and often antagonistic SA- and jasmonic acid/ethylene-mediated defenses with biotrophs and necrotrophs, respectively, has motivated efforts to classify bacterial pathogens with the same terminology. However, it has been argued that defining these lifestyles based on host defenses is not straightforward and might lead to confusion as exemplified by Dickeya dadantii, which elicits effective JA-associated defenses during its maceration phase, yet can also be blocked by host cell death early in infection. Additionally, many soft rot pathogens possess an initially biotrophic asymptomatic phase, which may be highly environmentally dependent, making it hard to classify among these trophic levels (Kraepiel and Barny 2016).

Biotrophic pathogens maintain host viability through stealth and metabolic dependence. Obligate or near-obligate biotrophs (e.g. Liberibacter, phytoplasmas, X. fastidiosa) often exhibit reduced genomes, streamlined central metabolism, and enrichment of transporters to import host-derived nutrients, minimizing autonomous biosynthesis while retaining uptake capacities. Lacking a classical T3SS, they carry low CAZyme burden (Fig. 2) and rely instead on immune suppression and adhesion/biofilm systems that permit long-term residence within the host or within vector foreguts without provoking extensive damage. Exception to the reduced genomes in biotrophs is Agrobacterium, which is a true biotroph, because it induces the plant tumor development producing metabolites that it can catabolize and proliferates. Xanthomonads and pseudomonads are described as hemibiotrophs that initiate infection with effector-rich T3SS programs that suppress PTI/ETI allowing stealthy mode of infection, followed by upshifts in CWDEs, toxins, EPS, and other factors that drive necrosis and systemic spread (Arnold and Preston 2019). The transition from biotrophic to necrotrophic phase correlates with redox-reprograming, ROS, and upregulation of CWDEs, reinforcing a genetic control for switching infection phases. Soft rot enterobacterales such as Pectobacterium and Dickeya are described as necrotrophic pathogens. They exhibit broad, high-copy CAZyme repertoires (pectinases, cellulases, and other polysaccharide lyases) that drive maceration and saprophytic growth on dead tissues. Amongst the reference genomes (Fig. 2), T2SS CAZyme repertoire and nutritional lifestyle have a significant relationship (BH adjusted P-value = 0.021) with necrotrophs having a predicted 57.7 more CAZymes than biotrophs. Necrotrophic virulence, with the brute-force mode of pathogenesis, is often dose dependent (enzyme/toxin load) and less constrained by effector-R gene specificity. However, these soft rot pathogens can also be found in latent asymptomatic infections. In Pectobacterium atrosepticum, quorum sensing acts as a global regulator, where brute-force programing, i.e. induction of CWDEs that macerate tissue, is coordinated with stealth programing (Liu et al. 2008). In contrast to Dickeya and Pectobacterium, Pantoea exhibits phosphonate toxin-dependent necrotrophy.

Regulatory architectures such as c-di-GMP signaling networks and two-component regulatory systems (TCS) play a central role in mediating lifestyle transition, such as in E. amylovora, where c-di-GMP functions as a master regulator balancing epiphytic survival with invasive necrotrophic growth. Indeed, Enterobacterales display diversification of GGDEF/EAL domain proteins and TCS that collectively coordinate EPS production, motility, biofilm, and virulence gene expression. This regulatory rewiring illustrates how lifestyle shifts in plant pathogenic bacteria often arise from evolution of signaling networks rather than solely from acquisition or loss of structural virulence genes (Niroula et al. 2026).

Future Directions

In this review, we summarized current knowledge on the evolution of genes and genomes in plant pathogenic bacteria with regard to how selection pressures induce genomic changes, how these changes affect genome architecture, and how together this contributes to a diversity of pathogen lifestyles (Fig. 5). Building on this foundation, several outstanding questions remain that will guide this field. Research focus needs to shift from gene-centric descriptions to broader frameworks that explain the drivers, constraints, and contexts that shape genomic changes across pathogens. A key gap is our limited understanding of how environmental conditions interact with bacterial genomes. Climatic shifts are hypothesized to influence mutation rates, recombination, MGE activity, and population bottlenecks, yet the mechanisms linking these stressors to genome evolution in plant pathogenic bacteria remain unclear, partly because of the complexity of plant-pathogen-microbiome-environment interactions. Understanding how these external pressures interact with plant defenses, plant-associated microbial communities, and the mobilomes will be essential for predicting when and how pathogen genomes diversify, stabilize, or undergo rapid adaptive change. Another major frontier will be to understand MGE persistence within new hosts, epistatic constraints, and ecological factors that would ensure MGE compatibility and shape pathogen virulence. We also lack a clear understanding of the mechanistic basis of host adaptation such as how combinations of effectors, metabolic traits, and stress response systems define whether a pathogen has a broad-host-range or is specialized onto certain hosts, or which molecular switches enable transition from epiphytic to apoplastic, or determine vascular vs. nonvascular colonization, or grant the ability to be seed transmitted. Recent advances on the importance of microbial communities in plant health also warrant our attention to address pathogen genome evolution within the broader ecology of the plant microbiome, where microbial competitors or mutualists can act as powerful filters that either constrain or facilitate MGE acquisition, host adaptation, and pathogen diversification. Lastly, we need to integrate evolutionary frameworks that consider the life of a pathogen outside the host i.e. beyond cropping systems, to fully capture its influence in shaping emergence of dual traits of virulence and persistence that collectively shape the pathogen genome. Emerging technologies such as long-read sequencing, pangenomics, single-cell and spatial transcriptomics, lineage-resolved metagenomics, and in planta barcoded transposon sequencing, allow for capturing fine-scale genetic heterogeneity that shapes pathogen population. These technologies when combined with ecological and epidemiological data will enable developing predictive models of pathogen evolution. Such models which consider pangenome structure, environmental context, and regulatory networks of pathogenesis, will help in predicting disease emergence, durability of host resistance, and identify factors that would constrain pathogen genome fluidity. Thus, these integrative and predictive frameworks will lay the foundations of how plant pathogenic bacteria evolve and adapt across hosts and environments.
