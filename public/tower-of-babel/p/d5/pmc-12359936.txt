Weathering the virtual storm: state boredom and coping strategies of novice and experienced Iranian EFL teachers in online learning
Authors: Masoomeh Estaji, Seyedeh Azadeh Sadr
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEmotions are an integral part of the classrooms, where learners and teachers constantly interact. Boredom, a negative academic emotion, adversely affects learning processes and achievement. As a result of its importance and essential role in EFL contexts, this area of inquiry has been the focus of academic research and critical examination. However, few studies (if any) have focused on EFL teachers’ coping strategies to ward off state boredom in online learning, considering their experience level. To fill this gap, the current study investigated the coping strategies of 237 novice and experienced Iranian EFL teachers with different experiences in online teaching contexts through the Coping with Boredom Scale (CBS) Questionnaire [1]. Besides, the authors delivered an open-ended questionnaire to 30 EFL teachers and conducted a semi-structured interview with 16 EFL teachers from the same sample. The results of the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U-Tests analysis indicated that novice and experienced teachers significantly differed in boredom coping strategies (p = .002) in that experienced teachers utilized a more comprehensive range of coping strategies to deal with their state boredom. The results of MAXQDA also evidenced that although there were some similarities in the utilized strategies across both groups, experienced teachers’ strategies were affected by their teaching experience, leading them to apply cognitive-avoidance strategies, like altering lesson plans and content, more frequently than novice teachers. The study presents practical implications for EFL teachers and researchers, stressing the role of teachers’ experiences in coping with boredom.Keywords: Coping strategies, EFL teachers, Experienced teachers, Novice teachers, Online teaching, State boredom, Teaching experience

Introduction

The presence of emotions in education has been well documented by researchers since the 1990s, focusing on the roles of positive and negative emotions in the classroom [2]. These emotional experiences are also substantial in helping individuals to develop their perception of the learning process [3, 4]. Although positive emotions have been widely explored in foreign language learning contexts [5, 6], it is vital not to overlook negative emotions, as they can hinder students’ learning process and engagement, impeding teachers’ efficacy [7, 8]. Consequently, the investigation of arrays of strategies to mitigate negative emotions in learning contexts deserves consideration.

In 2020, due to the spread of the COVID-19 pandemic, online education has spread throughout the world. Nevertheless, this new educational context in Iran continued beyond the pandemic as schools and universities were forced to close during autumn and winter because of severe air pollution. Additionally, some educational centers, such as English language institutes, run virtual classes besides their in-person classrooms even after the termination of the pandemic. This new teaching environment entails different challenges for teachers, including technological competency in navigating digital platforms effectively and managing academic emotions in online learning environments [9–11].

Boredom is a complex negative emotion whose interaction with various factors has made it impossible to establish a single definition. However, the role of the situation and environment is a common thread in all existing definitions. State boredom (situational boredom), the primary focus of this study, is one of the most prevalent negative emotions in educational settings, hindering learning [e.g., 12, 13, 14]. Given the new online setting, English as a Foreign Language (EFL) teachers may be more prone to experience state boredom, influencing their enthusiasm, autonomy, commitment, and rapport with students [15]. As a result, they need to possess the necessary ways of coping with the adverse effects of boredom in an online learning setting. Previous studies have been mostly concerned about boredom coping strategies among students [e.g., 16, 1, 17], and researchers have overlooked the teachers’ coping strategies, giving them far too little attention [18–21]. Furthermore, to date, little evidence has been found associating teaching experience and how to cope with boredom in an EFL online learning.

This study’s findings enrich current research and reaffirm the control-value theory’s (CVT) applicability to achievement emotions. They prove the relationship between years of teaching experience and competency to manage and regulate negative feelings in online learning environments. Furthermore, this study demonstrates the practical utility of the (CBS) questionnaire for teachers in online learning settings. Recognizing the significance of boredom and developing strategies to mitigate its impact can be highly beneficial for EFL teachers, assisting them in identifying and employing strategies that effectively minimize their boredom and ensuring that their teaching quality remains uninfluenced. The findings of this study can guide teacher trainers in creating training programs that emphasize the significance of emotions in online learning and equip teachers with strategies to mitigate negative emotions. Moreover, the results may inspire materials developers to create specialized, engaging, and interactive resources to tackle the unique challenges of online learning environments. Considering this background and the lack of literature regarding boredom, specifically state boredom in the online learning, this study aimed to investigate the state boredom coping strategies of novice and experienced EFL teachers in online learning.

Literature review

Academic boredom: definitions and typologies

Emotions are prevalent in the classroom because students and teachers frequently experience positive and negative emotions. In the emerging field of academic emotions research, researchers recognize emotions as critically crucial for shaping students’ learning, developmental trajectories, psychological health, and teachers’ productivity in educational institutions [22]. Academic emotions are defined as emotions experienced by individuals in educational settings that directly correspond with learning behaviors, classroom activities, and achievement outcomes, and its domain would include students’ achievement emotions experienced in an educational context and emotions relating to instruction or the study process [23]. However, academic emotions are considered relatively “unexplored” [24, p. 289].

It is now well established from various studies that teachers’ emotions contribute positively and negatively to teachers’ efficacy and students’ performance in the learning environment. Students notice their teachers’ emotional experiences [25]; consequently, teachers’ motivational stimulation depends on their emotional experiences during teaching. Indeed, classroom situations and events are the origin of teachers’ emotions being positive or negative [26]. In sum, how teachers manage, regulate, mitigate, and sometimes even repress their emotions in the instructional context has significant implications for their interpersonal relationships with students and affects the teacher-related outcome [27].

Boredom, a pervasive negative academic emotion in educational contexts, has been consistently associated with detrimental impacts on students’ learning and performance, resulting in a growing body of research focusing on understanding the implications of boredom and investigating strategies to reduce its effects within the academic learning environment [22]. Reviewing the existing literature on academic boredom reveals that most of the studies in L2 learning have only focused on students’ boredom [e.g., 28, 29, 17, 30] but rarely on teachers. Recently, Wang [30] investigated the causes and solutions of boredom among 256 EFL Chinese teachers in an online setting during the COVID-19 pandemic. The result illuminated that the online mode of delivery is more boring than the face-to-face mode. In a similar study, Pawlak et al. [20] explored boredom among 34 teachers and 256 students from universities across Iran through maximum variation sampling to examine which class mode (online or face-to-face classes) was more boring. Most teachers and students argued that online learning were more boring than in-person classroom.

However, boredom is a complex, fluctuating system interacting with other complex dynamic systems in L2 learning [31], including teachers, students, methods, topics, activities, materials, teaching, and learning environment. Consequently, understanding the teachers’ emotions and regulations is essential for a quality performance. Regardless of the causes, teachers’ emotions and the strong influence on their professional well-being, job satisfaction, burnout, and self-efficacy have been long identified [32–34]. Nevertheless, the empirical studies of boredom and coping strategies among teachers are scant.

Different scholars have also attempted to classify the complex construct of boredom into distinct subtypes. Todman [35] distinguished between situation-independent boredom and situation-dependent boredom. In another classification, researchers identified boredom as either a “trait” or a “state,” distinguishing between its enduring nature in individuals and its temporary occurrence in specific situations. boredom was classified as a “trait” or “state” [36]. Trait Boredom is one’s tendency to become bored. More precisely, this type seems to be an inherent part of one’s personality, meaning one tends to be permanently disengaged and/or disinterested. State boredom is a transient, context-dependent, and situation-specific attribute that results from an individual’s perception of their immediate learning environment as impoverished and thus not sufficiently stimulating [37, 38].

To date, researchers have overlooked state boredom, leaving its impact underexplored. Recently, Solhi [39] examined 186 undergraduate students from a Turkish university majoring in English language teaching. The findings revealed that the male participants experienced higher levels of state boredom than the females. Reviewing the literature demonstrates that most studies focus on trait boredom exploration, or nearly all studies have observed trait and state boredom simultaneously [e.g., 14]. However, state boredom and the problems caused by this construct have received little attention. Consequently, this study only considered state boredom and EFL teachers’ coping strategies to eliminate this situational boredom in online learning.

Theories and models of boredom

Various theories and models of boredom have been proposed in the literature. Russel [40] proposed the two-dimensional model, which is known as the Circumplex Model of Affect. This model demonstrates that affective dimensions are interrelated. According to Russel [40], boredom increases when low-arousal tasks induce unpleasant emotional experiences. Next, Hill and Perkins [41] proposed the forced-effort model, conceptualizing boredom as being forced to invest mental effort in a frustrating and monotonous task. The idea of this model derives from teacher-directed activities where students have little control over their learning. The understimulation model by Larson and Richard [42] suggests that boredom emerges in any situation portrayed by repetition and lack of challenge. Studies showed that low and high arousal could make an individual bored.

In 2006, Pekrun [43] developed the Control-Value Theory (CVT) of achievement emotions to explain the effects of boredom on academic context. This theory emphasizes that control and value assessments directly shape achievement-related emotions [44–46]. Li and Dewaele [46] argued that the CVT is closely tied to the educational context and has applicability in SLA research. This theory asserts that subjective control and value appraisals are essential for triggering achievement emotions. As applied in the current study, the model postulated above, defines “Appraisal of Control” as the extent to which an individual believes they can undertake tasks relevant to achievement or anticipate certain outcomes. In contrast, “Appraisal of Value” refers to the perceived importance, relevance, and meaningfulness of actions and their outcomes [43].

Online L2 learning and boredom coping strategies

In recent years, due to the pandemic of COVID-19, and in response to the global health crisis, traditional face-to-face instruction has transitioned to virtual learning environments. Initially, many assumed that new-emerging settings would be a temporary measure during the pandemic. However, online education has now become an established and enduring facet of academic contexts, particularly in language learning settings. The shift to online platforms has allowed various learners to extend educational access, integrating multimedia into lessons more extensively than traditional in-person learning. However, the transition to virtual learning has changed the interaction of teachers and students, making engagement more difficult. Teachers often find it challenging to hold students’ attention and keep them motivated without physical presence. Accordingly, screen fatigue and repetitive content delivery stimulate negative stressors such as boredom in the online classroom [47]. Previous research has found boredom as a destructive factor that has detrimental impacts on the L2 learning process, teacher effectiveness, and student engagement [48, 49].

The experience level of teachers is another factor that significantly influences their ability to cope with feelings of boredom; novice teachers may lack the breadth of strategies necessary to mitigate this emotion, whereas experienced teachers may exploit a more diverse array of coping strategies, which not only help them manage their boredom but also enhance the overall learning experience for students [50]. This reality necessitates a profound analysis of the factors leading to the accomplishment or failure of online learning, as well as strategies to ward off the detrimental impact of the negative emotions that have arisen.

Given the prevalence and undeniable effects of boredom in academic settings, researchers have explored the different types and effectiveness of diverse strategies students and teachers use to cope with the negative feeling of boredom. Coping is an ongoing behavioral, cognitive, and emotional process people use to manage circumstances threatening feelings of stability. Its purpose is to avoid, minimize, master, or manage life’s situations in such a way as to decrease any potential negative impact or outcome [51].

In this study, the concept of coping strategies complies with the four-factor model of Nett et al. [1], which has categorized boredom coping strategies into four groups: cognitive-approach, behavioral-approach, cognitive- avoidance, and behavioral-avoidance. Teachers remind themselves of the importance of a course, concentrate on the lessons, and change their perception of the boring situation, which are cognitive-approach strategies. While behavioral-approach strategies refer to changing the boring situation, for instance, students tend to adopt behavioral-approach strategies like looking for a more exciting task or activities during lessons when they experience boredom. However, cognitive-avoidance and behavioral-avoidance strategies involve mental or behavioral activities that disconnect from the current situation. That is, students who utilize cognitive-avoidance strategies think or study something irrelevant during lessons. In contrast, those who adopt behavioral-avoidance strategies distract themselves by interacting with their classmates [1, 19, 48].

Some studies have employed the four-factor model of Nett et al. [1] in their research, such as Eren [50], who examined 467 prospective teachers’ boredom coping strategies. Results suggested that prospective teachers may significantly decrease their levels of boredom not only by adopting cognitive-approach strategies but also by increasing their emotional engagement during the lessons. Another relevant study would be Dumančić [18], who explored how 38 Croatian EFL teachers overcame boredom in their classes. The qualitative data analysis of boredom among Croatian primary and secondary school English teachers suggested that the dominant theme was introducing different tasks or activities, known as the behavioral approach.

Despite the significance of EFL teachers’ emotional states in online learning, very little is known about how they cope with the negative emotion of boredom. To address this gap in the literature, the present study endeavored to examine the coping strategies employed by Iranian novice and experienced EFL teachers in online learning. Additionally, the study aimed to determine if there were notable differences in the boredom coping strategies employed between the two groups. More particularly, this research sought to answer the following questions:

To what extent, if any, do the Iranian novice and experienced EFL teachers differ in their coping strategies in online learning environments?

To what extent, if any, do the Iranian novice and experienced EFL teachers differ in their coping strategies in online learning environments?

What coping strategies do novice and experienced Iranian EFL teachers suggest for reducing the experience of boredom in online learning environments?

Method

To address each research question, the authors employed a sequential explanatory mixed-methods approach [36] to investigate the strategies utilized by novice and experienced EFL teachers in coping with state boredom within the context of online EFL learning environments. Using both quantitative and qualitative design (mixed methods) helps the researcher to make better inferences from the study results [52]. In a mixed-methods sequential explanatory design, quantitative components will investigate the problem, and the qualitative data will support the previously established claims [53, 54]. This approach is employed when the research objective complements the quantitative results with those qualitative; therefore, “the qualitative data is used in the subsequent interpretation and clarification of the results from the quantitative data analysis” [54, 55, p.196].

Participants and research setting

This study involved 237 Iranian EFL teachers, hailing from 33 cities across Iran, who were either currently enrolled in or had completed their education from 18 state and 43 private universities. The participants represented diverse educational backgrounds, including undergraduate student teachers and those holding Bachelor’s, Master’s, or Doctoral degrees in Teaching English as a Foreign Language (TEFL) or English Literature. They were employed at various educational institutions, including universities and private English teaching centers. The study involved non-native English teachers working either full-time or part-time, with a range of experience in online teaching. To gather data, the authors initially contacted 312 English teachers through Skype, Telegram, and WhatsApp to confirm their experience with the online environment. Upon receiving 263 confirmations, the authors distributed a Google Forms questionnaire to the participants, requesting them to respond to all questions thoughtfully. To ensure the participants’ consent, the first section of the questionnaire requested the participants to check a box and confirm their agreement to participate in the survey. Notably, the authors categorized the participants into groups with high online teaching experience (more than five years) and low online teaching experience (less than two years). Within three months, they collected a total of 237 completed questionnaires from an initial pool of 263 participants. It is worth mentioning that the authors selected the participants through non-random convenience sampling. While this sampling method may not guarantee representation of the entire target population, the authors decided to employ it due to the participants’ availability, accessibility, and willingness to partake in the study, aligning with the definition provided by Mackey and Gass [55].

To gather qualitative data for the study, the authors primarily performed a random selection of 30 responses from the open-ended questionnaires completed by both groups of teachers. Finally, based on various levels of experience demonstrated through the teacher demographic questionnaire, 16 teachers (novice N = 8 and experienced N = 8) were selected through purposive sampling, and they provided consent to partake in the interview phase of the study. Mackey and Gass [55] assert that Purposive sampling is a non-random sampling method in which the researcher selects participants based on specific criteria or their knowledge about the sample to collect relevant data. For this study, the interviewees were chosen based on their teaching experience, academic qualifications, and willingness to cooperate further. The participants ticked a box for their involvement in the interview, indicating willingness to participate in subsequent stages of the investigation.

Instruments

Coping with boredom scale (CBS)

–Most previous studies have used self-report measurements to investigate students’ and teachers’ strategies to cope with boredom [e.g., 56–58]. However, self-reporting is restricted to processes accessible to memory and consciousness, subjected to various biases. This method typically assesses only verbally describable content and lacks the temporal resolution required to capture real-time learning. Moreover, responses are under the respondent’s control and their psychological activation [58]. Consequently, the authors selected the Coping with Boredom Scale (CBS) developed by Nett et al. [1], to assess novice and experienced teachers’ boredom coping strategies in online learning. The scale comprises four factors and analyses the boredom dimensions: cognitive approach, behavioral approach, cognitive avoidance, and behavioral avoidance. Each factor consists of five items that assess situational boredom and, in total, includes 20 items. It is worth noting that, in this study, after ensuring the scale validity, the authors reduced the number of items to 18.

Moreover, The original scale was designed to investigate coping with boredom in math courses, and each item began with “When I am bored in mathematics class.” However, since this study focused on online English classes, “When I am bored in my online English learning” was substituted to address boredom coping strategies in online EFL learning. The scale comprises four factors: Cognitive approach (e.g., When I am bored in my online English classes, I make myself aware of the importance of my course instruction), behavioral approach (e.g., When I am bored in my online English classes, I suggest students a variety of tasks), cognitive avoidance (e.g., When I am bored in my online English classes, I think about my lesson plan or course content), and behavioral avoidance (e.g., When I am bored in my online English classes, I distract myself by interacting with my students). Nett et al. [1] ran a confirmatory factor analysis on data from 976 German students, supporting the four-factor model mentioned above. Besides, Nett et al. [59] and Tze et al. [60] reported the internal consistency reliabilities of cognitive approach (0.91), behavioral-approach (0.83), cognitive-avoidance (0.83), and behavioral avoidance (0.92).

In this study, the authors re-ensured validity and gained composite reliability (CR) values above 0.7 for the questionnaire and its subscales. Since the authors made no major modification in the scale items of Coping with Boredom in this study, they only ran a CFA to ensure that the scale would fit the context of the study. For all items, the unstandardized estimated loadings were significant. However, items 18 and 19 had standardized loadings below 0.5, which, according to Kline [61], endangered the convergent validity of the questionnaire. Therefore, the authors excluded the items from the questionnaire, leaving the final version with 18 items. The results showed the CFA model had acceptable to excellent goodness of fit, and all variables had composite reliability (CR) values above 0.7, confirming acceptable reliability. Moreover, the square root of AVE (the bold values in the table) was above the inter-correlations of the factors, indicating discriminant validity, according to Fornell and Larcker [62]. Based on the results of CFA, the scale of coping with boredom was reduced to 18 items, and its convergent/discriminant validity was ensured.

Open-Ended questionnaire

In alignment with the research objectives and assessing the relevant literature, the authors developed an open-ended questionnaire including three questions. This design aimed to gather diverse responses from participants, encouraging them to remember and share their experiences on their encounters with boredom and elicit the strategies they employed to cope with this negative emotion in online learning environments (Appendix A). The authors developed this open-ended questionnaire, and its content validity was measured through three experts holding Ph.D. in Teaching English as a Foreign Language (TEFL) who checked the items regarding their language clarity and content relevance. The experts primarily validated the content relevance of the content of the items for the present study. However, they proposed revisions to enhance the clarity and transparency of the language used in the items. In response to the experts’ feedback, the authors modified the language of some items and sought confirmation from the experts regarding these changes. Finally, the appropriateness of the items was assessed, leading to the completion of the research instruments for the beginning of the qualitative data collection phase.

Semi-Structured interview

In addition, to have further information on Iranian EFL teachers’ attitudes toward strategies to reduce boredom in online learning and to examine the significant difference between novice and experienced teachers, the authors interviewed 16 consenting EFL teachers. Interviews provide valuable insights into participants’ experiences, enhancing the methodological accuracy of a study, particularly when direct observation is not feasible [63]. Likewise, these interviews can enrich authors’ understanding of the subject, offering a more profound comprehension of the topic under investigation. To thoroughly understand teachers’ varied strategies, the selection of participants was based on their teaching experience. The interview commenced with prefabricated, structured questions focusing on boredom, particularly teachers’ strategies in online learning environments (Appendix B).

The authors formulated these questions, drawing on the relevant literature and the study’s objectives centered on the experience of boredom in online learning (state boredom) and the strategies teachers use to address this issue. However, the participants had enough freedom to expound their ideas and present other relevant points during the interview. Since all participants were EFL teachers, the authors decided to interview in English. They held online interviews and audio-taped for the participants’ convenience that lasted 15 min on average. To test the content validity of the study’s interview questions, three experts holding a PhD. in Teaching English as a Foreign Language (TEFL) checked the items of these two instruments regarding their language clarity and content relevance. They made modifications to the language of the items and their appropriateness to enhance their transparency.

Data collection procedure

To fulfill the study’s requirements, the authors utilized three instruments to collect the data. To ensure the efficiency and reliability of the instruments, they pre-tested the initial version that concluded 18 questions and piloted it with 30 respondents who closely resembled the target participants in terms of gender, age, major, experience, and academic degree. Then three university professors double-checked the content and language appropriacy of the questions and omitted two confusing and redundant items from the final package.

In the main phase of the study, the authors individually contacted EFL teachers via direct messaging in Telegram, Skype, and WhatsApp to confirm teachers’ experience in online learning. Following this, the authors used Google Forms to distribute a questionnaire to a pool of 263 EFL teachers with diverse experience levels in online learning environments. They collected 237 responses from the target participants over three months, which served as the basis for the subsequent data analysis. After the authors had completed the quantitative phase, they divided the participants into two groups: Novice and experienced. They randomly selected 30 responses from the open-ended questionnaires to analyze in the qualitative phase of the study.

In the final phase of the study, based on the consent and availability of the participants to attend the interview, the authors conducted semi-structured interviews with 16 EFL teachers (8 novices and 8 experienced) via WhatsApp and Skype, initiating the interviews with a couple of prefabricated questions. The interview questions revolved around different perceptions of teachers’ boredom and coping strategies in online learning and took 15 min on average. For privacy concerns, the authors turned off cameras during interviews. After completing all the interviews, the authors transcribed the audio files, preparing them for analysis.

Data analysis

To analyze the data collected through the (CBS) questionnaire, exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) were employed. Due to some data not following a normal distribution, the Mann-Whitney U-test, a non-parametric statistical method, was utilized to determine the levels of statistical significance. Regarding the qualitative data after the administration of the interviews and transcription of the data, the authors analyzed the collected responses from the open-ended questionnaire and semi-structured interview through MAXQDA and categorized the emerging themes based on their frequency. The authors pursued inter-coder reliability to ensure consistency in the raters’ coding. To check the credibility of the analytical process, they randomly chose 20% of the generated codes to be recoded by a second coder. More particularly, in this study, a total of 188 codes were created, and 35 codes were sent to the second coder. As a result of coding, the second coder disagreed with the first coder about three codes. Therefore, inter-coder agreement obtained in this study was 97%. To resolve the disagreements, the two coders discussed and applied the needed modifications and, subsequently, finalized the qualitative data process. Next, they created the open codes in terms of pertinent axial codes/subthemes and grouped the subthemes under higher-order general themes.

To uphold the standards of scholarly work while completing the current study, several measures were implemented during the analysis to enhance the principle of trustworthiness. Likewise, to maintain credibility and dependability, the authors requested the interviewees to review and validate the extracted themes and interpretations. While there were some minor discrepancies, they provided positive feedback regarding the accuracy of the interpretations. Finally, the authors prepared a detailed and complete report of the finalized data analysis process and its interpretation. Next, they extracted the themes from the codes. They triangulated the data collected from both the quantitative and qualitative phases to capture a vivid picture of how Iranian EFL teachers utilized and suggested coping strategies to ward off state boredom in online learning. Finally, the authors employed MAXQDA’s content and thematic analysis to further refine the qualitative data, and utilized MAXMAP properties to generate visual representations of the findings. Regarding researcher positionality, during the semi-structured interviews, the authors adopted the role of “outsiders in collaboration with insiders” [64, p. 31]]. As such, our primary responsibilities were to present questions and request additional clarification when necessary, effectively positioning ourselves as data collectors and analysts.

Qualitative results

The authors deployed the four-factor model to explore how teachers coped with boredom and developed an appropriate strategy [1]. According to this conceptualization, four categories of coping strategies are identified by two dimensions. The first dimension includes approach and avoidance strategies, with approach strategies centered on directly tackling and solving the problem, while avoidance strategies involve coping by avoiding the issue altogether. The second dimension differentiates between cognitive and behavioral strategies associated with coping.

Coping strategies for boredom in online environment suggested by novice and experienced Iranian EFL teachers

The second research question explored arrays of coping strategies that novice and experienced Iranian EFL teachers suggested to cope with boredom when teaching online. Based on the information presented in Table 4; Fig. 2, qualitative data analysis related to the second research question revealed 29 categories and 89 codes for the coping strategies employed by novice teachers to address boredom during online learning. Based on the coping strategies identified for novice Iranian EFL teachers dealing with boredom in online environments, four broad themes have been extracted to represent these strategies. The first theme, Engagement and Interaction, represents strategies to intensify student involvement, such as adding fun and games to activities and encouraging participation. The second theme, Adaptation and Innovation in Teaching, stresses the importance of modifying teaching methods and materials, including using audiovisual resources and finding new activities to maintain interest. The third theme, Self-Care and Time Management, underscores personal well-being and effective time management strategies, such as setting breaks and incorporating physical exercises before the class. Lastly, the fourth theme, Professional Responsibility and Reflection, revolves around the reflective practices and responsibilities teachers uphold, including pursuing lesson plans and considering the significance of their profession in dealing with boredom.

Discussion

This study’s quantitative and qualitative results provide a comprehensive understanding of the coping strategies utilized by Iranian novice and experienced EFL teachers in online classrooms, with each method complementing the other in significant ways. The quantitative data, derived from the Coping with Boredom Scale (CBS) Questionnaire, highlights the significant differences between novice and experienced teachers in their use of coping strategies (U = 3424, Z = 3.06, p = .002), with experienced teachers employing a more diverse and effective range of strategies to manage state boredom. The analysis indicates that in all sub-scales of coping with boredom (Cognitive-approach, Behavioral-approach, Cognitive-avoidance, Behavioral-avoidance), except for the cognitive-approach, the experienced group has higher ranks than the novices. The Z value shows that the difference in cognitive-avoidance (Z = 3.99) is the highest, and the behavioral-approach (Z = 2.24) is the lowest.

The differences identified between novice and experienced teachers resonate with Pekrun’s [43] research regarding regulating emotions in academic settings. This research indicates that experienced teachers use a wider range of coping strategies, allowing them to better navigate challenges like boredom. The novice teachers’ underdeveloped emotional regulation skills may limit their range of coping strategies, a case reported by Tze et al. [60], who assert that insufficient training can lead to emotional strain, ultimately impacting teacher retention and student outcomes.

In line with CVT theory, the highest Mean is associated with cognitive-approach strategies (e.g., teachers reminding themselves of the importance of a course, concentrating on lessons, making an effort to focus, paying attention). This finding confirms that both groups value the lessons and courses, supporting previous research that claims such cognitive-approach strategies are the most effective for reducing boredom in learning [16, 1]. The second-highest mean is associated with behavioral-approach strategies (e.g., changing tasks or topics), whereas cognitive and behavioral avoidance showed the lowest mean. The significant difference in using cognitive-avoidance strategies (e.g., revising or adjusting the lesson plan and reviewing course content suggests that novice teachers have less control over altering content and lesson plans, resulting in a greater sense of boredom than their more experienced counterparts. Experienced teachers have many years of teaching experience and know learning contexts. They know how to use different kinds of strategies to solve a wide range of teaching and learning problems. Therefore, they can better modify and revise their lesson plans and change or replace the course content and designed activities for each class session. Following the same reasoning, it is reasonable to explain why experienced teachers utilized cognitive-avoidance strategies more than novice teachers.

The findings also align with Eren [50] and Nett et al. [1, 59], who claimed that cognitive and behavioral-approach strategies are the most effective strategies in reducing boredom among prospective teachers and students. This finding is important because both groups of teachers are familiar with the best cognitive strategies to ward off boredom in online learning settings. The reason might be the nature of online learning, in which teachers should concentrate on each moment of their class to keep their students engaged.

The results partially align with Dumančić [18], who found behavioral-approach strategies to be the most salient ones among EFL teachers. (e.g., introducing different activities/tasks and engaging students). The difference between the two studies may be due to dissimilarities between face-to-face and online learning environments. In face-to-face learning, the teacher can carry out movement and the physical act of moving around (behavioral approach), and this cannot be applied when the interaction is online.

The qualitative insights gathered from open-ended questionnaires and semi-structured interviews provided a deeper understanding of the differences in teachers’ coping strategies, highlighting the limitations of numerical data in fully capturing these nuances. These findings highlight the emotional experience of teachers. For instance, novice teachers often show a narrower range of coping strategies, and their reflections reveal feelings of uncertainty and stress that aren’t fully captured by quantitative data. This suggests that while the numerical differences reflect varying levels of coping effectiveness, teachers’ real experiences and insights reveal how these strategies are formed and shaped in a teaching environment. The qualitative findings illuminate how coping strategies are deeply influenced by factors such as teaching experience, context, and emotional perception. This not only enhances the understanding of the specific strategies employed by teachers but also reinforces the study’s objectives, illustrating the intricate ways in which teaching experiences and emotional factors shape teachers’ approaches to manage the classroom challenges.

In this study, the qualitative results demonstrated that 89 codes for novice teachers and 99 codes for experienced were created. “Adding fun and games to activities” had the most frequency among novice teachers, and “Adding variety/novelty to tasks, activities, and content” was the most used strategy of experienced teachers to ward off the negative feeling of boredom in online learning. However, some codes like “encouraging students’ engagement and participation,” “setting time for a break,” and “using audiovisual materials” were common across both groups.

Experienced teachers often described more complex approaches, such as altering lesson plans and incorporating interactive activities. They conceive problems insightfully and are equipped with greater instructional knowledge, thinking, and actions, all of which stem from their accumulated knowledge and skills. As a result, they can better interpret classroom events and react to situations to ward off negative feelings and control the situation to positively affect their teaching and students’ learning. Many experienced teachers reflected on the importance of proactively engaging students by employing diverse strategies not only to seek to maintain students’ interest but also to mitigate negative emotional experience that supports effective learning. This approach aligns with the research goal of exploring how emotional management in the classroom can enhance teaching effectiveness, especially in the challenging context of online learning. In contrast, novice teachers tended to rely on simpler techniques, like sticking to routines. They are beginning their careers, and it takes time to recognize, assess, make decisions, and respond quickly to events. The insights gained emphasize the importance of considering emotional dynamics in educational settings, contributing to a more profound understanding of how teachers can effectively manage boredom in online learning environments. These findings offer a holistic view of how teaching experiences shape not just day-to-day practices but the long-term growth and adaptation of teachers within the constantly changing landscape of education.

Previous research findings [e.g., 46, 47] assert that state boredom is a transient feeling due to over-challenging or under-challenging situations, tasks, or activities have been confirmed by this study’s outcomes, in that most participants utilized behavioral-approach strategies such as changing the task or adding novelty to the activities to decrease their boredom. This is consistent with Pawlak et al. [20], who consider “increasing students’ engagement” and “task adjustment” (behavioral approach) as the most utilized strategies by teachers to overcome boredom in online classrooms and partially in line with Derakhshan et al. [66] who emphasize the adverse effects of boredom on students’ engagement and claimed that bored students adopt specific behavioral approaches such as using alternative activities and tasks to overcome their boredom.

The findings of this study are particularly significant in the Iranian context, where the shift to online education was intensified by the COVID-19 pandemic and ongoing issues like air pollution, leading to extended school closures. By bringing attention to the unique challenges Iranian EFL teachers face, this research underscores the need for professional development programs that provide teachers with effective coping strategies tailored to the complexities of online teaching in Iran. Such programs would help teachers navigate their environments more successfully and enhance their teaching effectiveness.

Conclusion and implications

These results showed that the experienced teachers used several strategies to alleviate state boredom in online learning. This may be attributed to their experience; they stand to benefit from a rich inventory of instructional knowledge, skills, and actions in handling classroom events and counteracting negative emotions. On the other hand, novice teachers utilized a narrower range of strategies to mitigate boredom in online learning environments. This suggests that, despite their knowledge and competence in English, novice teachers require further experience to effectively manage their emotions and employ diverse strategies and approaches in response to classroom situations. The results illuminated that although there were some similarities between the two groups of teachers in using the cognitive-approach (e.g., I make myself aware of the importance of my course instruction) and behavioral-approach (e.g., I suggest to students a variety of tasks), there was a significant difference between novices and experienced teachers in using cognitive-avoidance strategies (e.g., revising or modifying the lesson plan, revisiting the course content). It underscored the ability of experienced teachers to effectively utilize diverse knowledge derived from their schemata to adapt and revise their lesson plans. They are competent enough to modify or replace course content and activities during each class session.

The results of this study may have insightful theoretical and pedagogical implications for teacher educators, EFL teachers, and researchers. Since few studies have investigated teachers’ strategies to cope with boredom in online learning, this research provided detailed insight into how factors like teachers’ experiences play a role in teachers’ coping strategies and adds insights to the available literature. The authors adapted the CBS questionnaire employed in this study considering the teachers and online English learning. Hence, they consolidated the scale’s reliability, validity, precision, and obtained satisfactory results, confirming the practicality of CBS in examining teachers’ coping strategies in an online context. This framework can help researchers and teachers better understand how coping strategies in educational settings differ regarding teaching experience, particularly in online learning where traditional methods may not be applicable. The findings suggest that teaching experience is critical in how teachers manage negative emotions, such as boredom. This can lead to a re-evaluation of existing theories about teacher’s emotional regulation, especially in the context of online teaching. The study highlights the necessity of considering contextual factors in emotional experiences and coping strategies. Since online teaching environments present unique challenges compared to traditional classrooms, teaching and learning theories must evolve to account for the specific dynamics of online interaction and its psychological impacts on teachers.

Pedagogically, EFL teachers can use the findings of this study to confront the state boredom through appropriate coping strategies and minimize the detrimental effects of this negative feeling during their teaching; more specifically, novice teachers can be inspired to take part in teacher training courses or expose themselves to the experts’ thinking, teaching, and knowledge. Additionally, this study can benefit teacher trainers, materials developers, and syllabus designers. Teacher trainers and training course designers can also gain fresh insights into designing such training courses, conferences, and workshops, where teachers receive training on using various coping strategies, with a focus on addressing their negative emotions in online learning. Lastly, materials developers and course designers can create more engaging, motivational, and interactive content that is better suited to online learning environments.

However, this study, like any other research, had some limitations that need to be considered. Firstly, the generalization of the findings could be constrained since all the participants were Iranian EFL teachers. Secondly, this study solely explored EFL teachers’ coping strategies for boredom; hence, it would be advantageous to carry out comparative boredom research of teachers and students in the same learning environment. Thirdly, about the qualitative research questions, the data gathered from the participants might have been subjected to bias. This issue was likely an inevitable matter in any study involving the perspectives of different individuals. Future studies could incorporate cross-cultural perspectives by including online teachers from diverse countries, allowing for a broader understanding of the subject. Moreover, interventions for both groups of novice and experienced teachers for improving the approaches of boredom management would bring more valuable data. Conducting a comparative investigation of these groups in both online and in-person EFL learning contexts would enrich the existing literature and contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of teachers’ adaptive strategies in diverse educational environments. Another direction for the literature investigation would be to expand the investigation of demographic factors influencing teachers’ use of coping strategies in online learning contexts, including gender, age, and personality. Lastly, a replication study utilizing solely qualitative data collection methods like teacher self-reports, narrative frames, or classroom recordings would offer deeper insights into teachers’ experiences and perceptions regarding managing boredom in online learning environments.
