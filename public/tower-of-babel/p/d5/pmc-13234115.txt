Biofilms reshape soil-water dynamics with enhanced vapor adsorption and hysteresis reversal under dry conditions
Authors: Huan Hu, Chong Chen, Yili Lu, Robert Horton, Tusheng Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBacterial biofilms are crucial for microbial survival during drought by modifying soil-water dynamics. Their influence on water vapor transport in dry soils remains unexplored. This study systematically investigates biofilm-mediated changes in soil water vapor sorption (SWVS) dynamics across a wide matric potential range (−400 MPa to −10 MPa). Using soils of varying textures inoculated with wild-type and mutant strains of Pseudomonas putida and Bacillus subtilis, we characterized SWVS isotherms and concomitant changes in soil and biofilm properties. Key findings reveal that active cells and biofilms persist in dry soils (water activity aw < 0.93), altering SWVS behavior through two distinct mechanisms: (1) Enhanced SWVS capacity (10–49% increase across soils) resulting from biofilm hydration through swelling and extensive water retention within extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) matrices; (2) Hysteresis reversal at critical water activity values (aw < 0.6), where biofilm-dominated adsorption exceeds particle surface desorption. Mechanistic analysis demonstrates that EPS components influence SWVS through functional groups (-COOH and -OH) with strong water-binding affinity, EPS swelling, and suppression of cation/surface hydration, which physically obscures localized mineral surfaces. A conceptual framework for vapor dynamics in biofilm-colonized soils is proposed, and such results address a gap in understanding biological controls on soil hydrology under drought.Subject terms: Biogeochemistry, Environmental sciences, Hydrology

Introduction

As key architects of the soil water environment, bacterial biofilms profoundly influence soil water retention, distribution, and availability through their hydrologic processes at microscopic interfaces1. In soil environments, more than 99% of bacteria live in biofilms, multicellular clusters that attach to solid surfaces and interfaces2. During biofilm formation, bacterial cells produce extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) to facilitate surface adhesion and maintain their structural integrity3. EPS is a porous and hydrated matrix composed of polysaccharides, proteins, ions, extracellular DNA, and other substances4. It is widely known that biofilms and their EPS components manifest relatively large porosity and water-holding capacity5–7. Or et al.8 reported that EPS can hold up to 20 times its dry weight in water and retain soil water through bonding mechanisms, including electrostatic forces and hydrogen bonds. The development of biofilms is considered as a survival strategy that enhances microbial resilience during periods of drought9.

The presence of bacterial biofilms or EPS alters soil water retention patterns, primarily in moderately to relatively wet conditions (i.e., matric potential ψ > −103 kPa), where soil water retention is predominantly governed by capillary and gravitational forces7,10,11. Within this moisture range, the addition of EPS-analogous compounds or the formation of bacterial biofilms in soils has been found to significantly increase soil water-holding capacity, decrease saturated hydraulic conductivity, and reduce cumulative evaporation during drying4,7,12. EPS components facilitate the formation of soil aggregates and influence the spatial distribution of soil pore sizes, resulting in intra-aggregate spaces that can retain water11,13–16.

At low matric potential levels (ψ ≤ −10 MPa), soil water is mostly bound to particle surfaces in the form of water films, and yet the exact ψ threshold for forming water films depends strongly on soil textural properties and pore geometry. Under these conditions, microbial activity, mobility, and survival are severely inhibited and primarily limited by decreases in water film thickness, which is crucial for cell responses to desiccation tolerance during the dehydration phase17. Field observations show that EPS-rich biological soil crusts prolong hydration and foster dew adsorption18–20. This indicates that under dry conditions, water movement occurs primarily via vapor-phase diffusion and adsorption onto soil particle surfaces21. Thus, it is still desired to study the patterns of water retained in the form of vapor at very low ψ values in biofilm or EPS-soil systems.

Due to limitations of measurement techniques, dry soil studies have been constrained to a few matric potential values. It is common to use desiccators with saturated saline solutions or dew-point potentiometers to determine matric potential at ψ < −100 MPa. By this means, Chenu10 and Volk et al.11 found no significant alterations in water retention at −100 MPa for either mineral-EPS analog mixtures or incubated soil samples. The recently developed technique using a vapor adsorption analyzer (VSA), enables the measurement of soil water vapor sorption (SWVS) isotherms, i.e., the relationship between relative vapor pressure (water activity, aw) and water content (θ) simultaneously across a range of aw values, at a given temperature along an adsorption or desorption path22,23. The SWVS isotherm patterns can reflect the amount and status of water retained in the soil under very dry conditions.

For porous systems like dry soils (ψ from −400 MPa to −10 MPa), soil particles adsorb water molecules through (cation and surface) hydration during the monolayer adsorption phase24. Once a monolayer of water forms, the adsorbed water molecules serve as nucleation sites for multilayer adsorption, in which subsequent water molecules are incorporated via hydrogen bonding, ultimately forming structured layers of liquid water25. At the end of adsorption, soil water vapor condensation occurs as aw continues to increase. Furthermore, hysteresis is observed when adsorption and desorption isotherms diverge due to the history of changes in water activity of water vapor24. Thus, SWVS can be affected by a range of soil properties, including clay mineral type and content, cation-exchange capacity, organic-matter content, specific surface area, and hydrophilicity22,26,27. Moreover, in field soils, microbial activity is highly dynamic and can modify soil water properties through EPS production, biosurfactant release, and cell attachment on soil particle surfaces, thereby altering contact angle, viscosity, wettability, and pore characteristics, ultimately influencing water retention and SWVS28,29.

As with biofilms and EPS, the sorption behavior of polymer-based sorbents, along with their hydration and dehydration properties, has been widely studied30–34. Hydrophilic polymers show a distinctive SWVS mechanism, driven by polymer swelling, monolayer or multilayer adsorption involving bound and free water, and the occurrence of absorption/adsorption hysteresis33,34. Likewise, EPS exhibits both hydrophilic and hydrophobic properties, which can significantly alter soil wettability and dewatering characteristics35.

Despite mechanistic clues provided by polymer and soil-wettability studies, thus far, no experiment has fully determined whether living biofilms measurably change SWVS or hysteresis patterns in the vapor-dominated range. We address this gap by coupling high-resolution SWVS measurements with quantitative biofilm imaging across five soils that differ in texture, surface charge, and organic-matter content. Using both wild-type and EPS-deficient mutants of Pseudomonas putida (P. putida) and Bacillus subtilis (B. subtilis), we isolate the specific contribution of the EPS matrix to SWVS. Specifically, we ask: (i) Do bacterial biofilms alter the quantity and trajectory of water vapor sorbed by dry soils? (ii) Do they modify adsorption-desorption hysteresis, thereby influencing water availability during wet-dry processes? Answering these questions in the affirmative will establish biofilms as autonomous regulators of soil hydrology and provide mechanistic parameters for next-generation models of vapor fluxes, microbial habitat connectivity, and contaminant mobility in arid ecosystems.

Results

Biofilm morphology from scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis

Figure 1 shows the surface conditions of soil particles in bacteria-free and bacteria-inoculated soils under dry conditions (~−250 MPa). SEM images confirmed the absence of bacterial cells or biofilm structures on sterilized soil samples (Fig. 1a–e), validating the efficacy of the sterilization protocol. In contrast, soil samples inoculated with B. subtilis or P. putida exhibited biofilm layers (about 1–2 μm in thickness) that uniformly coated particle surfaces across all soil textures for the observation area (Fig. 1f–o; scale bar: 10 µm). These biofilms appeared as continuous, dehydrated matrices, potentially altering the surface morphology of soil particles. We also observed distinct differences in bacterial cell integrity between the two species: P. putida biofilms exhibited shriveled and aggregated cells (Fig. 1f–j), whereas B. subtilis biofilms retained rod-shaped structures with minimal distortion (Fig. 1k–o). Although biofilm coverage on particle surfaces was pervasive in the observation area, it should be noted that with limited water and nutrient availability, bacterial cells are likely to concentrate in soil pores or water-film hotspots, resulting in patchy and localized spatial distributions and consequently leading to a heterogeneous distribution of EPS36,37.

Biofilm structure and composition of dry soils

The spatial distribution and composition of biofilms of air-dry soils were characterized using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM). Three-dimensional-rendered images revealed distinct differences in EPS distribution between bacteria-free and bacteria-inoculated soils under dry conditions (Fig. 2 and Figs. S1–S3). Soils inoculated with B. subtilis or P. putida exhibited dense networks of polysaccharides and proteins, as indicated by the numerous colored spots in Fig. 2f–o, while the control soils showed minimal EPS signals (Fig. 2a–e). Additionally, quantitative assessment of EPS thickness from CLSM images showed that bacteria-inoculated soils exhibited thicker EPS layers coating soil particle surfaces than the bacteria-free soils (Fig. S4a–S4c). EPS thickness in bacteria-free soils was nearly negligible (~0.1 μm), whereas P. putida- and B. subtilis-inoculated soils exhibited EPS thicknesses of approximately 1.5 μm and 4.0 μm, respectively.

Independent chemical determinations of EPS components, performed for each sample using the cation-exchange resin method, revealed substantially higher concentrations of EPS-polysaccharides and EPS-proteins in bacteria-inoculated soils than in controls (Fig. 3). For instance, in loamy sand (Soil 1), B. subtilis-inoculated samples contained 393 µg g−1 dry soil EPS—14% higher than P. putida (346 µg g−1 dry soil) and 28% higher than control samples (308 µg g−1 dry soil). The polysaccharide and protein contents of B. subtilis biofilms were significantly higher than those of P. putida biofilms. Comparably, CLSM-derived volumes indicated that polysaccharides dominated EPS matrices, with obviously higher polysaccharide and protein volumes of B. subtilis biofilms than those of P. putida biofilms (Fig. S4d–S4f).

Bacterial viability and physicochemical properties of dry soils

Under dry conditions, CLSM images revealed significant differences in cell viability among the three treatments (Fig. 4 and Figs. S5–S7). In sterilized controls, no live cells were detected (Fig. 4a–e), confirming the absence of microbial activity. In contrast, both P. putida and B. subtilis-inoculated soils maintained high levels of cell viability after air-drying, with live-cell proportions ranging from 86–94% and 61–83%, respectively (Fig. 4f–o). Plate count assays further confirmed substantial bacteria survival, with viable cell numbers on the order of ~106–107 cells g−1 dry soil, but at reduced levels relative to the initial inoculation state (~107 cells g−1 dry soil). No significant interspecies differences in culturable cell numbers were detected (Table 1), indicating that both biofilm-forming bacteria retained comparably high functional viability at low ψ values. Viability disparities between species observed by CLSM might stem from physiological responses rather than population declines.

Table 1 summarizes the basic physicochemical properties of soils with and without bacteria following air-drying. Compared with bacteria-free soils, bacteria-inoculated soils did not have significantly different bulk soil properties, including soil organic carbon (SOC), total nitrogen (TN), and cation-exchange capacity (CEC). SWVS isotherms with or without wild-type bacteria

Figure S8 shows the typical SWVS isotherms, which describe the relationship between θ and aw. These isotherms exhibit a clear separation between the adsorption and desorption branches, resulting in pronounced hysteresis loops that reflect the irreversibility of water vapor uptake and release on soil particle surfaces. The degree of hysteresis is quantified using local hysteresis (Hw) and average hysteresis (Ha) values, where Hw represents the difference in θ between adsorption and desorption at a given aw, and Ha denotes an integrated hysteresis metric obtained by averaging Hw over a specified aw interval (Fig. S8). The maximum water vapor adsorption capacity (θmax) is indicated by the adsorption branch at the highest aw. In this study, θmax was reached at aw = 0.93.

Figure 5 and S9 present the SWVS isotherms and the corresponding Hw values for soil samples with and without bacteria. All isotherms exhibited high reproducibility, with mean standard deviations (MSD) of 0.0001–0.0042 g g−1 across triplicate measurements. In the bacteria-free soils, clear hysteresis was consistently observed across the entire aw range (Fig. 5a–e). For instance, in loamy sand (Fig. 5a), the adsorption branch began at aw of 0.05 (θ about 0 g g−1) and gradually increased to θ = 0.035 g g−1 at aw 0.93, while the desorption branch followed a higher θ trajectory at equivalent aw values, yielding positive Hw across the entire range. This hysteresis pattern was consistent across all biofilm-free controls (Fig. 5a–e), confirming the inherent irreversibility of water vapor sorption in the soils.

The θmax varied significantly with soil texture due to differences in clay and SOC contents. At aw = 0.93, θmax ranged from 0.035 g g−1 in loamy sand (Soil 1) to 0.143 g g−1 in clay loam (Soil 5), a 4.1-fold increase attributable to the higher CEC and hygroscopic content of Soil 5 (Table 1). Notably, the hysteresis magnitude scaled with θmax, suggesting that soils with greater sorption capacity retained more water vapor during desorption. These baseline results demonstrate how soil physicochemical properties govern SWVS behavior in the absence of biofilms, providing context for evaluating biofilm-mediated effects.

The most noticeable phenomenon is that the existence of bacteria significantly altered both the shape of SWVS isotherms and hysteresis patterns in dry soils (Fig. 5f–o). The differences between P. putida and B. subtilis SWVS isotherms were minor, as both induced distinct changes compared to controls. In soils with low SOC and clay contents (Soils 1–3), adsorption-θ (blue circles) exceeded desorption-θ (red circles) at low aw (< 0.6), with the two curves converging at aw values > 0.6 (Fig. 5f–h, k–m). This reversal indicates enhanced water vapor adsorption over desorption below a critical aw threshold (~ 0.6), above which hysteresis diminished. For soils with high SOC (Soil 4) or clay content (Soil 5), this threshold shifted to aw values of 0.5-0.6, followed by conventional hysteresis patterns at aw = 0.6-0.9 (Fig. 5i–j, n–o). Consequently, Hw values exhibited a triphasic trend: (1) rapid decline in aw range of 0.05–0.2, (2) gradual increase in aw range of 0.2–0.8, and (3) decrease to 0 at aw of 0.8 (Fig. 5f–o). Ha values for bacteria-inoculated soils (−0.12 g 100 g−1) contrasted sharply with controls (0.60 g 100 g−1), underscoring a bacteria-induced hysteresis reversal. Besides, the adsorption curve showed a bimodal trend: (1) substantial increases at low aw (< 0.6) and (2) minimum changes at high aw (> 0.6). This divergence suggests bacteria preferentially modify low-aw water retention mechanisms. Overall, bacteria reversed adsorption-desorption hysteresis at low aw (< 0.5–0.6) across all soil textures. For coarse soils (Soils 1–3), hysteresis vanished above the threshold, while finer-textured soils (Soils 4–5) exhibited weakened but persistent hysteresis. These findings demonstrate that bacteria fundamentally reprogram soil-water vapor interactions under dehydration conditions.

We compared the sorption capacities across the full aw range: desorption-θ was consistently lower in bacteria-inoculated soils, falling by 22%, 8%, 5%, 11%, and 12% for Soils 1–5, respectively, whereas the corresponding adsorption-θ rose by 27%, 24%, 49%, 10%, and 10%, respectively (Fig. S9). Water content changes (WCC) were then examined within discrete aw windows, as indicated by the lowercase numbers (Fig. 6). During adsorption, bacterial inoculation raised WCC0.05-0.2 but lowered WCC0.2-0.6. For Soil 5, as an example, these shifts were +39.5% and −23.0%, respectively (Fig. 6a, b). Thus, inoculated soils captured more water as aw rose from 0.05 to 0.2, yet less as aw climbed from 0.2 to 0.6. During desorption, this pattern reversed: WCC0.05-0.2 increased while WCC0.6-0.93 decreased (Fig. 6d, f), indicating greater initial water loss (aw changing from 0.93 to 0.6), but reduced release at low aw (from 0.2 to 0.05). The results of θ at transitional aw corroborated these trends (Fig. S10). Under adsorption, θ was significantly higher for inoculated soils only at aw = 0.2; under desorption, it was significantly lower at aw = 0.2 and 0.6. No bacterial effect emerged at aw = 0.93, confirming that inoculation modified θ at intermediate water activities without altering θmax.

SWVS isotherms: wild-type versus biofilm-deficient mutant

To further examine a key factor potentially affecting SWVS, for comparison, we performed independent SWVS measurements using a biofilm-deficient mutant strain. At the first step, the cell viability of the mutant and wild-type strains was compared and found to be comparable, with no significant differences (Fig. S11, Table S1), indicating that the mutation did not affect basic bacterial physiological activity. As expected, the biofilm-forming ability of the mutant strain was significantly lower than that of the wild type (Fig. 7a, b), demonstrating its inability to synthesize EPS and form biofilms. SEM observations further showed that mutant cells were predominantly found as isolated, dispersed individuals on soil particle surfaces, lacking EPS-mediated cell aggregation structures (Fig. 7c, d). Consistently, the contents of EPS-polysaccharides/proteins in soils inoculated with the mutant strain did not differ significantly from those in no bacteria-inoculated soils (Fig. 7e, f). Together, these results confirm that the mutant strain was unable to form biofilms effectively in soil.

Based on the above measured results, SWVS behavior was further examined in two soils inoculated with the mutant strain. Compared with the bacteria-free soils, no differences were observed in the shape of SWVS isotherms or in hysteresis features for soils treated with mutants (Fig. 7g–l). Besides, WCC values at transitional points showed no significant differences between treatments (Figs. S12–S13). These results indicated that biofilm-deficient mutants are incapable of EPS production and show no alterations in SWVS isotherms. Thus, in the absence of EPS-mediated biofilms, the bacteria exerted no detectable effect on SWVS, underscoring that the previously observed shifts in SWVS are driven specifically by biofilm/EPS presence in dry soils.

Discussion

We quantified bacterial viability and biofilm-associated EPS in dry soils. Our results demonstrate that even under dry conditions (approximately −250 MPa, after 4 days of air-drying at room temperature), soils inoculated with either the wild-type or the EPS-deficient mutant retained relatively high levels of bacterial activity (~106–107 cells g−1 dry soil; Table 1 and S1). Although both strains exhibited comparable levels of overall activity, they displayed pronounced differences in spatial organization and attachment patterns on soil particle surfaces. Multiscale microscopic observations revealed extensive biofilm formation and associated EPS structures in soils inoculated with the wild type (Figs. 1–3). In contrast, mutant strains were predominantly isolated and dispersed individual cells, lacking EPS-mediated cell aggregation and biofilm structures (Fig. 7c–d). This pronounced morphological contrast highlights the critical role of EPS production in determining cellular spatial organization and structural integrity of biofilms. Roberson and Firestone38 used SEM to show that the amount of EPS on soil particle surfaces increased substantially as ψ fell from −0.025 MPa to −1 MPa, forming thick layers that partially embedded bacterial cells, thereby reflecting a structural response of biofilms to changing water conditions. These EPS and biofilm structures may function as effective water-retention barriers, helping to buffer cells from external drying and thereby supporting microbial survival and basal physiological functions under drought conditions.

Furthermore, studies indicate that microorganisms can preserve cellular integrity, sustain basic metabolism, and grow slowly even when ψ values drop to several tens of MPa below zero35,39. Such drought tolerance often involves physiological adaptations to water stress, such as osmotic regulation, EPS production, and biofilm formation. EPS can create highly water-retentive microenvironments around cells, buffering the direct effects of external drying and preserving microscale liquid-water continuity38,40. In this study, soil hygroscopic θ measured at the end of the drying period was significantly higher in wild-type-inoculated soils than in controls, whereas no significant differences were observed between mutant-inoculated soils and controls (Table 1 and S1). Notably, SEM imaging revealed pronounced cell shrinkage and surface collapse in P. putida under dry conditions, whereas B. subtilis largely retained its rod-shaped morphology (Fig. 1f–o). In contrast, CLSM live/dead staining indicated a relatively higher proportion of viable P. putida cells (Fig. 4), revealing a notable mismatch between cell morphology and viability. This discrepancy likely reflects methodological differences: SEM sample preparation (including gold sputtering and high vacuum) can induce additional cell deformation, whereas CLSM staining primarily assesses membrane integrity and more directly reflects instantaneous physiological activity41–43.

A direct comparison between soils inoculated with EPS-deficient mutant and wild-type strains reveals that biofilm-bearing soils exhibit distinct water vapor sorption behavior attributable to EPS. This observation aligns with earlier work showing that EPS enhances water retention in drying soils. For EPS-soil mixtures, Rosenzweig et al. 4 reported that EPS significantly increased θ of sandy soils at a given matric head above −1 MPa. Similarly, Chenu10 demonstrated that polysaccharides-clay or -sand interactions substantially boost soil water retention during drying, with the studied water potential extending to approximately −100 MPa. In contrast, the present results cover a much drier range (−10 to −400 MPa), indicating that bacterial biofilms alter SWVS isotherms even under extremely dry conditions, a factor often overlooked in such studies.

In biofilm-free soils, SWVS is jointly regulated by clay type and content, SOC, and CEC. However, bulk soil properties, including SOC, TN, and CEC, did not differ significantly between bacteria-free and bacteria-inoculated soils, whereas bacterial inoculation significantly increased hygroscopic water content (Table 1 and S1). Notably, both B. subtilis and P. putida biofilms produced similar effects, suggesting that SWVS modification is a generic biofilm trait rather than species-specific. The key finding is that biofilms fundamentally shift SWVS processes, as seen in changed water-retention capacity and hysteresis-reversal patterns. Specifically, biofilm-treated soils show diminished or even reversed hysteresis at low water activity (aw < 0.6) and suppressed desorption capacity.

We also found that the biofilm hydration effect depends on texture, i.e., water retention was more obviously enhanced in coarse-textured soils (Soils 1–2) than in fine-textured soils (Soils 3–5), where intrinsic solid properties (clay content, mineral type, and SOC content) remain the dominant SWVS determinants under the tested inoculation level (~ 107 cells g−1 dry soil).

Hysteresis is a common feature of the adsorption-desorption cycle in SWVS. In bacteria-free soils, the isotherm is governed primarily by interactions of water vapor with mineral soil surfaces, especially clay particles. Water vapor adsorption occurs through three sequential mechanisms: (1) monolayer adsorption (aw < 0.2), where water binds directly to surface sites; (2) multilayer adsorption (0.2 ≤ aw ≤ 0.6) as additional water layers assemble; and (3) capillary condensation (aw > 0.6) in mesopores44. Sorption hysteresis, where desorption θ exceeds adsorption θ at the same aw, arises from two competing hydration processes. Particle-surface hydration is fully reversible, but interlayer cation hydration shows significant path dependence due to the energy barrier associated with cation-water complex rearrangement24. Note that the previously reported aw thresholds are primarily derived from clay-dominated systems without biofilms. Here, for the bacteria-soil system, their aw thresholds for SWVS may shift in coarse-textured soils due to differences in pore-size distribution and mineral surface properties as well the complicated interactions between biofilms and soil minerals.

In biofilm-free soils, SWVS hysteresis results from several physical mechanisms. Asymmetric hydration of exchangeable cations in layered clay minerals during adsorption and desorption leads to path-dependent changes in interlayer spacing and bound water states, separating the sorption isotherms24. Pore-scale capillary effects provide another major source of hysteresis: the critical water activities for condensation and evaporation differ among pore sizes, producing distinct water distributions during adsorption and desorption45. This mechanistic framework explains our observations in control soils (Fig. 5a–e), where hysteresis magnitude correlates strongly with CEC (R2 = 0.98)—confirming that interlayer cation hydration dominates the hysteretic behavior across all five soil types.

In biofilm-colonized soils, the observed patterns reflect a shift in adsorption mechanisms driven by biofilm hydration, rather than relying solely on cation hydration or particle-surface interactions on bare soil particles. This process predominates at low water activities for two key reasons. First, biofilms rapidly adsorb substantial water vapor through their extracellular polymeric matrix, leading to swelling. It is well established that biofilms and their EPS components (polysaccharides and proteins) interact with water via hydrogen bonding and hygroscopic swelling3,10. EPS contains a high density of polar functional groups (e.g., hydroxyl, carboxyl, and amine groups) that actively bind water vapor even at low matric potentials (aw < ~0.2)46. These hydrophilic moieties create a hydration shell that dominates vapor adsorption under dry conditions3. Polysaccharides show pronounced hygroscopicity due to abundant -OH groups, which form structured hydration shells through hydrogen bonding8,40. The biofilm hydration effect weakens as aw increases, consistent with saturation of high-affinity EPS binding sites. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy further corroborates these findings (Fig. S14). Compared with bacteria-free soils, bacteria-inoculated soils exhibited stronger infrared absorption signals associated with multiple water-binding and hydrophilic functional groups, including hydroxyl (-OH), carboxyl (-COOH), as well as polysaccharide- and phosphate-related C-O, C-O-C, and C-O-P functional groups. This indicates that biofilm formation substantially increased the relative contribution of hydrophilic functional groups, such as hydroxyl, carboxyl, and phosphate groups, within the soil matrix, potentially increasing the binding sites for water vapor. Although biofilm presence amplified signal intensities (especially in B. subtilis-colonized soils compared to P. putida), it did not alter the fundamental functional-group composition of soils, as peak shapes and locations remained consistent across treatments (Fig. S14 and Table S2). Second, as aw increases, the biofilm hydration effect diminishes, and cation hydration and surface hydration of soil solids become increasingly important.

Certain hydrophilic polymer sorbents exhibited a type II adsorption isotherm featuring a distinct pseudo-plateau region at low aw values, resulting from polymer particle swelling and the monolayer/multilayer sorption pattern of the polymer itself34. The adsorption isotherms we observed in bacteria-soil systems similarly show a comparable pseudo-plateau (convex patterns) at low water activities on coarse soils, indicating that biofilm matrices swell during adsorption, thereby increasing their water-accessible volume. This swelling‑based modification offers a plausible explanation for the characteristic hysteresis reversals seen in SWVS isotherms at low water activity (aw < 0.6). Beyond swelling, biofilm coatings can alter soil particle surface properties in localized ‘hot spot’ regions, modifying wettability and contact angles by creating heterogeneous hydrophobic/hydrophilic domains28,29.

Water potential represents the free energy per unit volume (or mole) of water in a system. From an energy perspective, the observed hysteresis shift can be linked to changes in free energy (i.e., water potential change) within the biofilm-soil system along the two sorption pathways. Previous studies report that the free-energy changes associated with surface hydration range from 12.5 to 52.3 kJ mol−1, while multilayer adsorption exhibits a lower value of approximately 10.8 kJ mol−1 47–50. For comparison, hydrogen-bond energy between water molecules typically lies between 10 and 40 kJ mol−1 47. Based on these values, we infer that biofilm-induced free-energy changes exceed the ranges cited above at aw values < 0.6.

At higher water activities (aw > 0.6), capillary condensation became dominant, with capillarity-driven free-energy as low as 0.126–2.53 kJ mol−150. Within this range, biofilm-hydration-induced free energy shifts aligned closely with capillary-driven changes, as evidenced by the near-overlap of the respective isotherms. Furthermore, compared to the substantial free-energy changes associated with ionic hydration on soil particle surfaces (250–1500 kJ mol−1)51, the forces within EPS were relatively weak (generally < 40 kJ mol−1). Consequently, water vapor desorbed more readily from biofilms than from soil particle surfaces. This difference was reflected in the 12% reduction in average desorption capacity observed in biofilm-treated soils (Fig. S9). Our findings indicated that biofilm matrices facilitate both rapid water uptake (adsorption) and release (desorption) in dry soil environments.

Here, we propose a unifying framework for biofilm hydration effects on SWVS. Biofilm hydration constitutes an additional, biologically driven pathway in soil-water interactions that becomes dominant at low ψ. By reconfiguring both adsorption kinetics and retention capacity, biofilms act as “biological sponges” that reset the SWVS signature of dry soils through molecular-scale water-EPS coupling (Fig. 8). In biofilm-free soils, SWVS proceeds through three canonical stages: (i) monolayer adsorption, (ii) multilayer adsorption, and (iii) capillary condensation. Where bacteria colonize in ‘hot spot’ regions, EPS coatings re-engineer surface energetics and replace this sequence with the following regime:

Unifying framework illustrating biofilm-mediated modulation of soil water vapor sorption (SWVS).

Top: Comparative SWVS isotherms for biofilm-coated versus biofilm-free soils; Bottom: Corresponding hydration mechanisms that generate the observed divergence.

(1) EPS-adsorption domain (aw < 0.2). Concurrent monolayer adsorption and biofilm hydration generate an extra adsorption reservoir, producing a pseudo-plateau in the isotherms and raising water content above the abiotic baseline. Hysteresis starts to reverse in this domain.

(2) Transition domain (0.2 ≤ aw ≤ 0.6). Biofilm hydration approaches saturation and water films thicken. The governing mechanism migrates from surface adsorption toward pore-scale capillary condensation. Hysteresis is still inverted because EPS desorption requires less energy than mineral-surface desorption.

(3) Capillary domain (aw > 0.6). Capillary condensation dominates, and the adsorption and desorption branches converge. Hysteresis collapses in coarse-textured soils or is strongly attenuated in fine-textured or SOC-rich soils.

The magnitude of these effects is soil-specific and scales with (i) bacterial biomass, (ii) EPS-mineral contact that modulates local wettability/hydrophobicity, and (iii) the spatial association between biofilm and clay surfaces—an interaction that disrupts both particle hydration and interlayer cation hydration, two key SWVS mechanisms in clay minerals. Given the limited existing research on biofilm sorption hysteresis, the conceptual model provides a provisional sketch and highlights the need for further investigation of the underlying mechanisms, considering the factors listed above, to establish a theoretical model.

This study presents a systematic investigation of the effects of bacterial biofilms on SWVS behavior under dry conditions. We show that living biofilms re-engineer SWVS even at ψ values that approach the hydration limit of bacteria. Two mechanisms, acting sequentially with increasing aw, explain the shift. First, in the low-aw range (0.05–0.6), biofilm hydration predominates due to the high water-holding capacity of EPS and the strong swelling of polymer networks, resulting in reversed hysteresis patterns relative to abiotic mineral surfaces. Second, there is a transition threshold at aw ≈ 0.6, below which biofilm hydration controls water retention through polymer-water interactions, while capillary forces progressively overshadow biofilm effects at higher aw values. These changes introduce an enhanced water retention pathway and create distinct hydrodynamic signatures in sorption isotherms in bacteria-soil systems. Even trace biofilms can override clay interlayer hydration, demonstrating that biological (not mineral) surfaces can set the dry-end water retention curve.

The biofilm-mediated alterations in SWVS dynamics revealed in this study have profound implications for environmental management and agricultural sustainability, particularly in water-limited ecosystems. The enhanced water vapor adsorption capacity and hysteresis reversal at low water activities suggest that biofilms can function as biological water reservoirs during prolonged drought periods. This microbial water-harvesting mechanism could significantly impact soil moisture management strategies in arid and semi-arid regions by enhancing soil water capture from vapor related sources such as dew, fog, and humid air, which are critical water inputs in moisture-limited environments. The observed hysteresis reversal is particularly significant, as it indicates that biofilm-colonized soils may retain more plant-accessible water during initial drying phases while enhancing vapor capture during rewetting events that effectively widen the hydration window for biological activity in soils. These findings establish a quantitative foundation for bacteria-controlled SWVS processes, although current data are limited by soil texture variability, bacterial diversity, and population sizes. The study emphasizes the necessity of developing a comprehensive mechanistic theory, incorporating biofilm hydration into soil hydraulic models, and enhancing the predictive understanding of microbial influence on water availability in arid soils. Future research can focus on (i) parameterizing species-specific EPS sorption coefficients, (ii) defining critical biofilm hydration thresholds, and (iii) coupling hydration dynamics to microbial activity feedbacks for bulk- or field-scale predictions.

Methods

Soil samples

To isolate biofilm-specific effects on SWVS, we selected five natural soils with textures ranging from loamy sand (8% clay) to clay loam (30% clay), and SOC contents ranging from 1.5 to 16.0 g kg−1 (Table 2). This selection provides a controlled comparison of textural and organic matter influences while focusing on biofilm-mediated processes. Soil samples were first air-dried at room temperature, with plant residues removed, and then sieved through a < 2 mm mesh to ensure sample homogeneity. Subsequently, indigenous microorganisms were eliminated using steam sterilization. Specifically, the samples were autoclaved at 121 °C and 103 kPa for 30 min, and the sterilization was repeated after a 24 h interval (double autoclaving) to minimize the possibility of microbial survival. Sterilized soils were equilibrated at room temperature (25 °C) for 24 h prior to bacteria inoculation. Basic soil properties, including particle size distributions, SOC contents, and nutrient concentrations, are provided in Table 2 and Table S3 (Supplementary Material).

Bacterial strains

P. putida and B. subtilis are well-documented as plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria that enhance drought tolerance and soil water retention52–54. We selected standard strains of P. putida and B. subtilis (Shanghai Biological Resources Conservation Center) for their robust biofilm-forming capabilities. To elucidate biofilm-specific effects on SWVS, biofilm-deficient mutant strains, namely P. putida Δeps-ΔtasA and B. subtilis Δeps-ΔtasA, were additionally employed. These mutant strains were also obtained from the Shanghai Biological Resources Conservation Center. The eps genes are involved in extracellular polysaccharide synthesis and secretion55, whereas tasA encodes a key extracellular matrix protein essential for biofilm assembly and structural stability56. Deletion of these genes markedly impairs biofilm formation.

All strains, including both wild-type and mutant strains, were propagated and prepared using the same procedure. Strains were activated on Luria-Bertani (LB) agar plates (Fig. S15 shows a representative image of wild-type strains), transferred to LB liquid medium, and incubated at 180 rpm, 28 °C for 48 h. The bacterial suspension was then centrifuged (4000 rpm, 10 min) to remove LB medium, and pellets were washed twice with sterile distilled water to remove residual nutrients. Washed bacterial cells were resuspended in sterile distilled water to a final concentration of 1.0 × 108 cells mL−1, confirmed via plate counting. These suspensions were used for subsequent soil inoculation experiments. To verify the viability and biofilm-deficient phenotype of the mutant strains, growth curves and biofilm formation assays were performed in parallel with the corresponding wild-type strains.

For growth curve analysis, each strain was inoculated into 5 mL LB liquid medium and incubated at 37°C with shaking at 180 rpm for 12–16 h to achieve activation. Activated cultures were then transferred (1%, v/v) into fresh LB medium and grown to an early exponential phase (OD600 ≈ 0.6), serving as seed cultures. Seed cultures were inoculated (1%, v/v) into 96-well microplates containing 100 μL of fresh LB medium per well. Each strain was analyzed with three biological replicates, and wells containing only LB medium were included as blanks. Microplates were incubated at 37 °C, and optical density at 600 nm (OD600) was measured at 0.5 h intervals using a microplate reader.

Biofilm formation was quantified using a crystal violet staining assay. Cultures at mid-exponential phase were adjusted to OD600 = 0.5 and inoculated (1%, v/v) into 96-well microplates containing 200 μL LB medium per well. Plates were incubated statically at 28 °C for 24 h. After incubation, planktonic cells were removed, and wells were gently rinsed twice with sterile water. Biofilms were stained with 1% (w/v) crystal violet for 15 min, washed to remove excess stain, and destained with 200 μL absolute ethanol for 5 min. Absorbance was measured at 595 nm (OD595) using a microplate reader. Three biological replicates were performed for each strain.

Sample preparation

To assess biofilm effects on SWVS patterns during adsorption/desorption processes, we prepared soil samples with and without biofilms using inoculated bacterial cells capable of producing EPS5,7. For biofilm samples, 100 g of soil (spread in sterile Petri dishes) was inoculated with 10 mL of bacterial suspension (1.0 × 108 cells mL−1), achieving a concentration of 1 × 107 cells g−1 dry soil. The suspension and soil were mixed thoroughly with a sterile stirrer. The water content at the end of inoculation was about 0.10 g g−1. Control samples received equal volumes of sterile water. All samples were incubated under ventilated conditions (30 °C, dark, 3 days). During incubation, sterile water was added daily based on mass changes to compensate for evaporation losses, and the soil was gently stirred with a sterile rod to ensure a more uniform distribution of bacterial cells and associated biofilms (EPS) within the soil matrix. After incubation, the samples were air-dried at 25 °C for approximately 4 days, at which point the ψ was approximately −250 MPa, and were subsequently subjected to SWVS measurements.

Soil water vapor sorption isotherms and local hysteresis

SWVS isotherms were measured using an AquaLab Water Vapor Sorption Analyzer (METER Group, USA) in Dynamic Dewpoint Isotherm (DDI) mode57. Air-dried samples (~3.0 g) were analyzed within 24 h of preparation at 25 ± 0.2 °C across aw (i.e., the ratio of the vapor pressure associated with water in soil to the vapor pressure of pure water at the same temperature) range of 0.05–0.93 (ψ from −400 to −10 MPa), with 0.02 aw resolution. By maintaining precise control of vapor pressure, the DDI system provided the resolution needed to distinguish biofilm-altered hygroscopic water retention from water retention in untreated control samples. Three replicates per sample were measured.

Based on established conceptual models of soil water vapor sorption, the SWVS process can be broadly divided into three regimes: monolayer sorption (aw < ~ 0.2), multilayer sorption (~0.2 < aw < ~0.6), and capillary condensation (aw > ~0.6)44. Accordingly, the measured sorption data were partitioned into three corresponding aw intervals (0.05–0.2, 0.2–0.6, and 0.6–0.93). WCC, defined as the difference in water content between two aw levels, were calculated within each interval and denoted as WCC0.05-0.2, WCC0.2-0.6, and WCC0.6-0.93, respectively.

To further quantify the SWVS isotherms, the relationship between soil water content and its corresponding aw was obtained at ψ values below −10 MPa. Hw was calculated as the difference in water content between the desorption and adsorption branches58. Ha was computed as the mean of 39 Hw values spanning aw = 0.1–0.859. Calculations are illustrated in Figure S8 (Supplementary Material).

Biofilm visualization and cell viability assessment

Biofilm characterization was performed alongside SWVS measurements using aliquots from the same batch. Soil particles were visualized via scanning electron microscope (SEM, SU3500, Hitachi, Japan; 50 nm resolution) after gold-sputtering. To assess biofilm viability under dehydrated conditions, we used CLSM combined with live/dead staining to quantify bacterial cell survival and EPS distribution on soil particle surfaces. This approach provided in situ visualization and quantitative analysis of biofilm colonization under low ψ (air-dried) conditions. For viability assessment, biofilms were stained with LIVE/DEAD® BacLightTM (SYTO 9-propidium iodide; Thermo Fisher) and imaged using CLSM (Leica STELLARIS 5, Germany; 40 × water immersion lens). SYTO 9 and PI were excited at 488 nm and 561 nm, respectively, with emissions collected at 500–550 nm (SYTO 9) and 570–620 nm (PI). Five random fields per sample were analyzed in ImageJ (v1.53): fluorescence channels were segmented, converted to area, and used to calculate live/dead cell ratios. Colony-forming units (CFUs) were quantified by plating serial dilutions on LB agar (28 °C, 48 h).

Determination of EPS components and contents

To assess biofilm-induced changes in soil surface properties, we analyzed EPS components via staining and chemical assays. Soil samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (1 h, 4 °C) and stained with Calcofluor White (β-polysaccharides; 405 nm/435 nm), FITC (proteins; 488 nm/500–550 nm), and TRITC-ConA (α-polysaccharides; 561 nm/570–620 nm). Stained samples were imaged in 3D CLSM (Leica STELLARIS 5, Germany; 63 × oil immersion lens; 185 × 185 × 30 μm scan range). Fluorescent channels were threshold-segmented in ImageJ (v1.53) and converted to 3D volumes. Additionally, we estimated the average thickness of the EPS on the soil particle surface based on the 3D fluorescent volume. The specific method involved dividing the threshold fluorescent volume by the area of the scanning plane (185 × 185 μm2), providing a rough estimate of the thickness at the observed scale. Due to significant unevenness in soil surface morphology and particle size, this method serves only as a relative indicator for comparing biofilm coverage across different treatments, rather than an absolute thickness measurement.

For EPS quantification, EPS was extracted using cation-exchange resin60. Polysaccharides were measured via the sulfuric acid-phenol method (glucose standard)61, and proteins via the BCA assay. Three replicates per sample were analyzed. It should be noted that the EPS volume quantified by CLSM represents only fluorescently labeled, spatially continuous biofilm structures located on or immediately adjacent to soil particle surfaces within the effective imaging depth. During image analysis, EPS was distinguished from mineral particles based on fluorescence intensity, and the volume of soil particles was excluded from the calculation. In contrast, the CER method quantifies the total extractable EPS at the bulk soil scale60. Therefore, CLSM- and CER-derived EPS metrics are used to compare relative differences and trends in EPS abundance among treatments rather than for direct quantitative equivalence.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy measurement

To assess biofilm-induced changes in soil functional groups, FTIR spectra were acquired for the same samples used for SWVS measurements. Samples were homogenized with KBr (1:100 ratio), pressed into pellets, and analyzed using a Nicolet IS10 spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Spectra were collected at 25 °C (64 scans, 4 cm−1 resolution, 400–4000 cm−1), with background subtraction using pure KBr. Post-processing included baseline correction and normalization to the 1030 cm−1 Si-O band.

Measuring soil physicochemical properties

To distinguish biofilm-mediated effects from inherent soil properties, physicochemical analyses were performed on post-treatment aliquots. SOC and TN contents were quantified via an elemental analyzer (Flash EA1112, Thermo Electron Corporation, US). CEC was determined using the ammonium acetate method (pH = 7)62. ψ value of each air-dry soil sample was measured by the dewpoint method using a WP4C potentiometer (Decagon Devices, Pullman, WA). Following the ψ measurements, the samples were oven-dried (105 °C, until mass stability < 1% over 24 h) to verify gravimetric water content. All measurements included three replicates per treatment.

Statistical analysis

To identify factors affecting SWVS and associated hysteresis, we performed one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) (IBM SPSS Statistics 25) on soil properties comparing biofilm-treated and control groups. The differences in soil properties with and without biofilm treatments were tested at a 95% significance level.
