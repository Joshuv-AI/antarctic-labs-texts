REAC-based bioelectrical neuromodulation as a conservative strategy in canine cranial cruciate ligament rupture: a translational pilot study
Authors: Eraldo Sanna Passino, Sabrina Caggiu, Ghulam Fatima, Alessandro Castagna, Vania Fontani, Alberto Maria Crovace, Salvatore Rinaldi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCranial cruciate ligament (CCL) rupture is one of the most common orthopedic disorders in dogs and is associated with pain, joint instability, lameness, and progressive osteoarthritis. Although surgical stabilization is widely used, conservative management remains relevant in selected cases, particularly in smaller dogs or when surgery is declined or contraindicated. Radio Electric Asymmetric Conveyer (REAC) technology is a non-invasive therapeutic platform designed to modulate endogenous bioelectrical activity, with a proposed role in neuromotor regulation and tissue repair. This study was designed as a translational, hypothesis-generating pilot investigation to assess whether a standardized REAC-based treatment protocol could produce a coherent and durable clinical signal in dogs with naturally occurring CCL rupture. Sixteen client-owned dogs with mono- or bilateral CCL rupture were enrolled and stratified by body weight into two groups: ≤15 kg and > 15 kg. All dogs received a standardized protocol including Veterinary Neuro Postural Optimization (VNPO), Veterinary Neuro Psycho Physical Optimization (VNPPO), and Reparative Tissue Optimization (TO-RPR). Clinical outcomes were assessed by standardized orthopedic examination at baseline, within 15 days after treatment completion, and at 2- and 12-month follow-up. All dogs completed treatment and follow-up. In dogs weighing ≤ 15 kg, complete functional recovery was observed in all cases. In dogs weighing > 15 kg, most showed either complete recovery or clinically meaningful improvement, while one dog was classified as a non-responder and referred for surgical evaluation. Clinical improvements observed in responder dogs remained stable at 12 months. These findings do not constitute definitive evidence of efficacy, but suggest the presence of a coherent and durable clinical signal supporting further investigation of REAC-based treatment as a conservative or adjunctive strategy in canine CCL disease. Future controlled studies incorporating objective biomechanical, imaging, and biological outcome measures are warranted.Keywords: Cranial cruciate ligament, Dog, REAC, Neuromodulation, Reparative medicine, Conservative treatment

Introduction

The cranial cruciate ligament (CCL) is a fundamental structure stabilizing the canine stifle joint by preventing cranial displacement of the tibia, internal rotation, and hyperextension. Damage to the CCL typically results in joint instability, inflammation, lameness, and eventually degenerative joint disease [1–3]. Chronic instability leads to altered biomechanics and compensatory mechanisms that further impair limb function and quality of life.

Historically, the first surgical CCL repair was reported in humans by Hey Groves in 1917. In veterinary medicine, Paatsama’s pioneering work in the 1950s introduced the drawer test, the concept of partial ruptures, and some of the earliest repair techniques in dogs. Since then, various procedures, such as TPLO (Tibial Plateau Leveling Osteotomy) and TTA (Tibial Tuberosity Advancement), have been developed and are commonly used today [1–3].

Nevertheless, conservative management remains a relevant option in small dogs or when surgical intervention is declined or contraindicated. Factors such as age, physical condition, and body weight significantly influence prognosis and therapeutic decisions [8]. Conservative strategies are traditionally limited to rest, leash-controlled activity, and weight control, but newer approaches involving neuromodulation and reparative medicine are gaining interest [1–3].

However, increasing experimental and clinical evidence indicates that CCL disease is not merely a mechanical failure of a ligament, but a complex degenerative and inflammatory process involving altered neuromuscular control, chronic synovitis, and dysregulation of tissue repair mechanisms [6, 8, 18, 19, 22]. Similar to human anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) disease, canine CCL rupture occurs in the context of progressive biological and biomechanical deterioration rather than as an isolated traumatic event.

In this framework, spontaneous canine CCL rupture represents a naturally occurring large-animal model of human ACL disease, sharing key features such as chronic degeneration, inflammation, neuromuscular dysfunction, and progressive osteoarthritis [7, 16]. This makes the dog an especially relevant translational model for exploring non-surgical and biologically oriented therapeutic strategies.

REAC (Radio Electric Asymmetric Conveyer) technology represents one such approach. It is a patented, non-invasive, painless, contact-based therapeutic platform in which an Asymmetric Conveyor Probe (ACP) selectively channels very low-intensity, asymmetrically conveyed radio electric fields into the body. These fields interact only with cell populations or neural networks where alterations of endogenous bioelectrical activity (EBA) are present, progressively restoring correct membrane polarity and bioelectrical homeostasis.

Endogenous bioelectrical activity is a fundamental regulator of cell polarity, tissue organization, inflammation, and regeneration. Following injury, chronic mechanical overload, or persistent stress, disruption of bioelectrical gradients contributes to loss of coordinated cellular behavior, impaired tissue repair, and pathological remodeling. REAC technology is designed to selectively restore these altered bioelectrical patterns, thereby promoting both neuromotor reorganization and local reparative responses.

REAC treatments have shown efficacy in human medicine for ligament injuries, knee osteoarthritis, neuromusculoskeletal disorders, and neuropsychiatric conditions [5, 11–16, 23–25, 27, 28]. These human data provide a translational biological rationale for exploring REAC in veterinary orthopedics, particularly in spontaneous disease models that closely mirror human pathology.

Importantly, the present study was not conceived as a definitive therapeutic validation trial, nor as a comparison with surgical techniques. Instead, it was designed as a translational, hypothesis-generating, observational pilot study aimed at determining whether modulation of endogenous bioelectrical activity by REAC produces a coherent and durable clinical signal in dogs with naturally occurring CCL rupture.

Within this context, the primary objective was to assess feasibility, safety, and the consistency of functional recovery over time, in order to inform the design of future controlled clinical trials.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This observational study was conducted between December 2012 and December 2016 at the University Veterinary Teaching Hospital of Sassari. The enrolled population consisted of 16 privately owned dogs, all presenting with mono- or bilateral CCL rupture confirmed by clinical examination and radiographic imaging. The cohort included a variety of breeds (mixed breed, Siberian Husky, Labrador Retriever, Setter, Poodle, Beagle, Maremma Sheepdog) with ages ranging from 2 to 13 years. All dogs presented with grade III or IV lameness according to a standard clinical grading system described by Cook et al. (2010), accompanied by positive drawer and tibial compression tests. Dogs with systemic disease, previous CCL repair, or concurrent orthopedic conditions were excluded.

This study was conceived as a pilot, hypothesis-generating, translational observational investigation. In the absence of prior veterinary data for a formal power calculation, the sample size was selected according to methodological recommendations for exploratory pilot studies, whose goal is to estimate feasibility, variability, and effect direction rather than to provide confirmatory statistical inference.

Grouping criteria

To assess the potential influence of body weight on therapeutic response, subjects were stratified into:

Subjects were stratified into Group A (dogs ≤ 15 kg, n = 7) and Group B (dogs > 15 kg, n = 9).

This stratification reflects the well-established role of body mass as a biomechanical and prognostic modifier in canine CCL disease.

Treatment protocols

The REAC B.E.N.E. device (ASMED, Florence, Italy) was employed for all treatments. Each dog received the following protocols:

Neuro Postural Optimization (VNPO): A single session with the Asymmetric Conveyor Probe (ACP) applied to the left auricular antitragus to initiate neuro-psychomotor realignment.

Neuro Psycho Physical Optimization (VNPPO): The protocol provided for 18 applications divided into 6 sessions, carried out on different days (3 applications per session, spaced 1 h apart). This protocol is aimed at modulating subcortical regulatory circuits related to stress, motor control, and neuroplasticity. The ACP was placed on the cervico-brachial region.

Reparative Tissue Optimization (TO-RPR): Local application on the affected stifle joint using a laminar ACP for eight 30-minute sessions (total: 4 h) to promote cellular repair, reduce inflammation, and support joint tissue remodeling.

All REAC protocols are delivered using pre-set, operator-independent parameters to ensure reproducibility and to prevent variability related to manual adjustment. Treatments are non-invasive, painless, contact-based, and do not induce perceptible electrical or thermal sensations.

Standard of care during treatment

Throughout the protocol, no pharmacological treatments, anesthesia, or sedation were administered. Owners were instructed to maintain controlled leash walking, avoid running and jumping, and follow a dietary plan to optimize the dog’s body condition by maintaining an ideal weight and muscle tone.

“Optimization of body condition” refers to maintaining or achieving a Body Condition Score within the ideal range through controlled feeding and activity, in order to minimize excessive mechanical loading on the affected joint and to support neuromuscular recovery.

Outcome assessment

Dogs were evaluated clinically at baseline, within 15 days after treatment completion, and at 2- and 12-month follow-ups.

All evaluations were performed by experienced board-certified veterinary surgeons who were not involved in treatment delivery and were blinded to expected outcomes.

Clinical outcome was assessed using standardized orthopedic examination, including lameness grading, joint stability testing, pain on manipulation, weight-bearing during gait, and functional use of the limb during normal activity. Outcomes were classified as Normal (complete resolution), Improved (minor residual signs without functional limitation), or Non-responder (persistent clinically relevant lameness).

Objective instrumental assessments such as force plate analysis or MRI were not included in this pilot study by design. The primary goal was to evaluate clinically meaningful functional recovery and durability in a real-world veterinary setting, while minimizing animal stress and unnecessary procedures. This approach is consistent with exploratory translational studies intended to generate feasibility and efficacy signals to guide subsequent controlled trials.

Results

Among the 16 enrolled dogs, all completed the REAC treatment cycle and follow-up evaluations. In Group A (dogs ≤ 15 kg, n = 7), clinical outcomes were highly favorable. All dogs showed full recovery of joint function within the first month after treatment. No signs of lameness or pain were observed at the 2-month and 12-month follow-ups. Functional classification at 2 months revealed that 100% of dogs were rated as “Normal,” indicating complete resolution (Table 1).

Discussion

Cranial cruciate ligament (CCL) rupture is one of the most common orthopedic disorders in dogs and is associated with pain, joint instability, progressive osteoarthritis, and functional impairment. Although surgical stabilization remains the standard option in many cases, conservative management continues to play a role in selected patients, particularly in smaller dogs, older animals, or when surgery is declined or not feasible [3, 4, 6, 14, 20, 21]. Within this context, the present study was designed to explore whether a standardized REAC-based treatment protocol could generate a coherent clinical signal deserving further investigation, rather than to establish definitive therapeutic efficacy.

The main finding of this pilot study is that clinically meaningful recovery was observed in most treated dogs, with complete recovery in all dogs weighing ≤ 15 kg and partial or complete improvement in most dogs weighing > 15 kg. An additional relevant observation is that these outcomes remained stable during 12-month follow-up in responder animals. These findings are noteworthy because they suggest the presence of a durable functional signal in a spontaneous large-animal orthopedic condition. At the same time, the present results must be interpreted with caution, given the observational design, the absence of a control group, and the limited sample size. In this sense, the current study should be interpreted not as proof of efficacy, but as evidence of signal.

When interpreting these findings, the possibility of spontaneous clinical improvement cannot be overlooked. Especially in lighter dogs, periarticular fibrosis and adaptive compensation may lead over time to a degree of apparent stabilization. These factors represent a legitimate alternative explanation for the observed outcomes and should be carefully considered [26, 29]. However, in the present cohort, the consistency of the favorable outcomes, their relatively early clinical appearance, and their persistence at 12 months in responder dogs suggest that the observed trajectory may not be fully explained by passive spontaneous stabilization alone. Rather than excluding spontaneous recovery mechanisms, our interpretation is that REAC treatment may have influenced the quality or timing of functional recovery in a subset of animals. This remains a hypothesis that requires formal testing in controlled trials.

The more heterogeneous response observed in dogs weighing > 15 kg is also clinically relevant. Body weight is a well-recognized prognostic modifier in canine cranial cruciate disease, because greater mechanical load is associated with more demanding joint biomechanics and often with more advanced degenerative changes [1, 9, 10, 17, 22, 29]. The fact that one dog in the heavier group did not respond and required referral for surgery further supports an important point: the present findings do not support REAC treatment as a replacement for surgical stabilization in all cases. Rather, they suggest that REAC-based treatment may deserve consideration as a conservative or adjunctive option in selected patients, especially when surgery is not immediately pursued or when a less invasive approach is initially preferred.

Another important limitation concerns the absence of objective instrumental assessments such as force plate analysis, kinematic evaluation, advanced imaging, or arthroscopy. Such measures would have substantially strengthened the evidentiary value of the study. Their absence should therefore be considered a limitation from a confirmatory perspective. Nonetheless, this choice was deliberate at the pilot stage. The study was conducted in client-owned animals in a real-world clinical setting, and the primary goal was to detect a clinically meaningful and durable functional signal while minimizing additional stress, invasiveness, sedation, and owner-related procedural burden. For this reason, standardized orthopedic clinical assessment performed by experienced evaluators blinded to expected outcomes was selected as the principal outcome approach. Future studies should incorporate objective biomechanical, structural, and imaging-based endpoints.

No direct mechanistic or biological measurements were obtained in the treated dogs, and this should be considered when interpreting the findings. The biological rationale of REAC discussed here is based on previous experimental and translational literature and should therefore be viewed only as an interpretive framework, rather than as something demonstrated by the present data. In this context, REAC may be regarded as a putative modulator of the biological conditions underlying recovery, but the current study does not directly demonstrate the mechanisms involved in canine ligament disease. Its value lies instead in the observation of a clinically coherent pattern that may justify deeper mechanistic investigation in future studies [5, 12, 16, 28].

This distinction is particularly important because the contribution of the present work does not lie in proving how REAC works, but in showing that the observed clinical pattern was sufficiently coherent, durable, and safe to justify more rigorous study. No adverse effects were observed, all dogs completed treatment and follow-up, and the protocol proved feasible in routine clinical practice without anesthesia, sedation, or pharmacological support. These aspects are relevant for translational development, especially in veterinary settings where conservative treatment options are limited or inconsistently effective.

The small sample size, observational nature, and lack of a control group, together with the absence of instrumental and biological assessments, limit the ability of the present study to support causal inference or comparative efficacy claims. However, these findings do allow cautious hypothesis generation. In this sense, the study provides a useful preliminary basis for the design of future prospective controlled trials incorporating objective measures such as force plate analysis, imaging endpoints, validated pain and lameness scores, and stratification by body weight, chronicity, and meniscal status.

Overall, the present findings support the view that REAC-based treatment deserves further investigation in canine cranial cruciate ligament disease, not because efficacy has already been demonstrated, but because a clinically relevant and durable signal was observed in a spontaneous translational model under routine veterinary conditions. At this stage, this appears to be the most appropriate interpretation of the present data.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this observational translational pilot study identified a coherent and durable clinical signal associated with a standardized REAC-based treatment protocol in dogs with cranial cruciate ligament rupture. These findings do not constitute definitive evidence of efficacy, nor do they support replacement of surgical stabilization when surgery is indicated. Rather, they suggest that REAC-based treatment may deserve further investigation as a conservative or adjunctive strategy in selected canine patients. The stability of clinical improvement observed in responder dogs over 12 months supports the relevance of this signal, while the limitations of the study design clearly indicate the need for cautious interpretation. Future prospective controlled studies incorporating objective biomechanical, imaging, and biological outcome measures will be necessary to define the true clinical value and indications of this approach.
