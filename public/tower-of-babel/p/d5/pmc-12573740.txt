Brain‐Wide Patterns of CRF Receptor 1 Expression in the Transgenic CRFR1‐Cre‐2a‐tdTom Rat
Authors: Douglas S Engelke, Lierni Ugartemendia, Jonathan Tao, Shivakumar Rajamanikam, Fabricio H Do Monte, Nicholas J Justice
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe ability to manipulate neurons that express corticotropin‐releasing factor (CRF) or its receptors has led to many new discoveries about how CRF signaling modulates stress‐related behaviors in mice. However, similar advances in rats have been limited by the lack of reporter lines targeting CRF receptors, restricting access to behavioral paradigms that are more suitable for, or specific to, rats. Here, we provide an extensive neuroanatomical characterization of a recently generated CRFR1‐Cre rat. We show that Cre and tdTomato are expressed in a pattern that closely aligns with previously reported CRFR1 expression patterns, as determined by in situ hybridization in rats and by genetic labeling in CRFR1 transgenic mice. We detail expression patterns at multiple rostrocaudal levels to validate the use of this transgenic rat in multiple neural circuits that signal via CRF activation of CRFR1. Furthermore, we demonstrate that expression of Cre recombinase, combined with viral vector delivery, can be used to trace CRFR1‐expressing neurons and their axonal terminals in target regions. This study is intended to serve as a resource for examining the expression pattern of CRFR1 and as a validation of the CRFR1‐Cre rat for reliable use with Cre‐dependent viral approaches to manipulate and/or record the activity of CRFR1‐expressing neurons in distinct brain regions. We expect that the availability of the CRFR1‐Cre rat, along with an increasing number of transgenic rat lines, will further expand our understanding of how CRF transmission mediates stress responses by leveraging the behavioral strengths of the rat as a model system.Keywords: corticotropin‐releasing factor, paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus, paraventricular nucleus of the thalamus, stress, transgenic rat

Introduction

When faced with real or perceived threats, animals, including humans, exhibit increased activity in stress‐responsive neural circuits, leading to a series of physiological changes that help them cope with potential danger (Tovote et al. 2015; Deussing and Chen 2018; Daviu and Bains 2021). These adaptations include an elevation in the sympathetic tone, increases in endocrine stress hormones, and a collection of behavioral modifications that vary according to the intensity of the stressor and the species involved (Johnson et al. 1992; Charmandari et al. 2005). During periods of stress, pre‐autonomic neurons in hypothalamic and brainstem regions become active, stimulating cells within the rostroventral portion of the medulla (Hirooka et al. 1997; Koba et al. 2018; Nakamura and Morrison 2022). These cells send signals to preganglionic sympathetic neurons located in the intermediolateral cell column of the spinal cord, which in turn activate chromaffin cells in the medulla of the adrenal gland, prompting the release of catecholamines into the bloodstream (Shahar and Palkovits 2007; Norcliffe‐Kaufmann 2022). In parallel, endocrine stress responses mediated by the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis are initiated following the activation of paraventricular hypothalamic nucleus (PVH) neurons, which release the neuropeptide corticotropin‐releasing factor (CRF) at the median eminence (Vale et al. 1981). CRF then transits via the portal system to the anterior pituitary, where it triggers the release of adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) by pituitary corticotropes. Elevated ACTH levels in the blood stimulate the cortex of the adrenal gland to increase circulating levels of corticosteroids within minutes of encountering a stressor. Acting through nuclear receptors that cause changes in cellular transcription, corticosteroids are responsible for many of the sustained alterations in physiological and behavioral functions that promote survival in threatening contexts (S. M. Smith and Vale 2006).

Exposure to stressors also prompts the release of CRF in numerous brain regions, thereby eliciting stereotypic stress‐evoked behavioral responses (Heinrichs et al. 1995; Füzesi et al. 2016; Daviu et al. 2020). In addition to the PVH, CRF is expressed at high concentrations within the paraventricular thalamic nucleus (PVT), central amygdalar nucleus (CeA), bed nucleus of the terminal stria (BST), accumbens nucleus (ACB), periaqueductal gray (PAG), and a few small nuclei located in the pons and medulla (Itoga et al. 2019; Peng et al. 2017; Pomrenze et al. 2015). Such interconnected groups of brain structures are coordinately activated by stressors and together execute many of the physiological, cognitive, and behavioral changes often referred to as “the stress response” (Deussing and Chen 2018). Because CRF is a central signal that mediates or influences many features of the stress response, identifying brain structures that contain CRF or express one of its two known receptors, CRF receptor 1 (CRFR1) (R. Chen et al. 1993; M. H. Perrin et al. 1993) or CRF receptor 2 (CRFR2) (Kishimoto et al. 1995; Lovenberg et al. 1995; M. Perrin et al. 1995), has proven valuable for elucidating the neural circuits that regulate behavioral alterations under stressful conditions (Dedic et al. 2018; Deussing and Chen 2018). CRFR1 is expressed broadly in the central nervous system by subpopulations of cells in many regions spanning the rostrocaudal extent of the brain (Potter et al. 1994; Van Pett et al. 2000; Justice et al. 2008). In contrast, CRFR2 is expressed at moderate levels in the central nervous system, with high levels of expression observed in peripheral tissues including the heart, skeletal muscle, and pancreas, where CRFR2 is the main binding site for the CRF‐related peptides urocortin 1, 2, and 3 (Van Pett et al. 2000; Bale and Vale 2004; Deussing and Chen 2018).

The recent generation of transgenic mouse models for CRFR1 has increased our understanding of the breadth of actions mediated by CRF, particularly in contexts of stress. Directed mutations of Crf or Crfr1 genes in mice have enabled the study of phenotypes resulting from loss of CRF signaling, which has helped to establish a role for CRF and CRFR1 in the regulation of HPA axis activity in response to stress (Muglia et al. 1995; G. W. Smith et al. 1998). Furthermore, the introduction of transgenic mice carrying reporters of CRFR1 expression has allowed the direct visualization of CRFR1‐expressing neurons in vivo, in slice preparations, and in immunohistochemically labeled tissue (Justice et al. 2008; Kühne et al. 2012; M. A. Herman et al. 2013; Hunt et al. 2018). Such transgenic models have improved our ability to study neurons that express CRFR1 at high cellular resolution, given the continued lack of antibodies that selectively recognize CRFR1 protein. Moreover, the development of transgenic CRFR1‐Cre (Sanford et al. 2017; Hunt et al. 2018; Jiang et al. 2018) and CRF‐Cre (Taniguchi et al. 2011) mice has enabled the use of new tools, including optogenetics, chemogenetics, cell ablation, and neuronal activity monitoring, to study CRFR1‐expressing neurons, which have helped to identify new roles for CRF in the regulation of behavioral and physiological responses to stress.

Although transgenic mouse models have facilitated our comprehension of the functional contribution of the CRF system to distinct physiological functions, rats are widely used as animal models for a number of research studies in which the CRF system plays a major role including: (i) memory and cognition, (ii) drug self‐administration, (iii) metabolic disorders, (iv) social interaction and acoustic communication, and (v) cardiovascular diseases such as stroke and hypertension (Iannaccone and Jacob 2009; Homberg et al. 2017). In the last decade, transgenic rat models that target CRF neurons or genes involved in stress responses have started to emerge in the field (Pomrenze et al. 2015; Scheimann et al. 2019). Recently, a transgenic CRFR1‐Cre‐tdTomato rat strain was developed that expresses both Cre‐recombinase (Cre) and the td‐Tomato reporter (tdTom) in CRFR1‐positive neurons (Weera et al. 2022), and demonstrated the functional activity of Cre‐recombinase and the expression of tdTomato specifically in CRFR1 neurons, with a special emphasis on the central amygdalar nucleus. Here, we provide a more comprehensive description of the distribution of the CRFR1‐Cre‐tdTom transgene throughout the brain and compare it with past in situ hybridization (ISH) studies describing the patterns of CRFR1 expression in the rat brain. Using Cre‐dependent anterograde tracing, we further validate the effectiveness of the CRFR1‐Cre‐tdTom rat model in expressing virally delivered transgenes to CRFR1‐positive neurons, thereby confirming the reliability of this rat model for conducting experiments aimed at investigating the anatomy, neuronal properties, and functional role of CRF signaling in rats.

Materials and Methods

Animals

To investigate the distribution of CRFR1‐Cre‐tdTom transgene across the brain, we used three male and three female CRFR1‐Cre‐2A‐Tom BAC transgenic rats, similar to those validated and described in a previous study (Weera et al. 2022).

Immunohistochemistry

To survey Cre/tdTom in the rat brain, we anesthetized animals with sodium pentobarbital (450 mg/kg), perfused them transcardially with 100 mL of saline (0.9%) followed by 500 mL of 4% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4), and transferred their brains to 30% sucrose in 0.1 M phosphate buffer and incubated at 4°C for 48 h. Brains were then frozen (−23°C), and coronal sections (40‐µm thick) were obtained using a cryostat (CM 1860, Leica). To generate permanent labeling of tdTomato protein, we stained the sections using immunohistochemistry. Brain slices were incubated in 2% normal goat serum in 0.4% Triton X‐100 in PBS containing biotinylated rabbit anti‐red fluorescent protein antibody (1:1000, Abcam, Cat# ab34771, RRID: AB_777699) at 4°C for 48 h. Sections were washed in PBS and incubated in biotinylated goat anti‐rabbit IgG antibody (1:200, Vector Laboratories, Cat# BA‐1000, RRID:AB_2313606) for 2 h, followed by incubation in an ABC peroxidase kit (Vectastain, Vector Labs, Cat# PK‐6100, RRID:AB_2336819) for 90 min and DAB‐Ni solution (Vector Labs, Cat# SK‐4100) until the desired intensity of staining was reached (∼10 min). Sections were washed in PBS, mounted on gelatin‐coated slides, dehydrated, and cover‐slipped with mounting medium (DPX, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 44581) for subsequent examination of transgenic expression of the CRFR1 reporter. For immunofluorescent labeling, sections were incubated in biotinylated goat anti‐tdTom (1:1000, Rockland, Cat #200‐101‐379, RRID:AB_2744552), along with rabbit anti‐CRF (1:2000, Vale lab #rc69, RRID:AB_853064), then incubated with anti‐rabbit‐Alexafluor488 (Jackson Immuno, 711‐546‐152, 1:1000, RRID:AB_2340619) and anti‐Goat‐Alexafluor555 (Invitrogen, A‐21432, 1:1000, RRID:AB_141788) for 2 h at room temperature, before washing and mounting.

ISH

All solutions were prepared with DEPC‐treated water, and all tools and surfaces were wiped with RNAzap, followed by DEPC‐treated water. Adult CRFR1:Cre (tdTomato) rats (three males and three females) were deeply anesthetized with Avertin before being transcardially perfused with PBS, followed by 4% PFA in PBS. Brains were removed, fixed in 4% PFA at 4°C overnight, equilibrated in 30% sucrose, sectioned into six series of sections (30 µM, coronal sections) on a frozen sliding microtome (SM 2000R, Leica), and stored in PBS at 4°C. Next, brain slices were mounted onto glass slides, dried, and processed through ISH using an RNAscope Multiplex Fluorescent kit v2 (ACDbio, Newark, CA) following the manufacturer's protocol.

Microscopy and Image Analysis

We used a slide scanner (Biotek Cytation 5) to image DAB‐stained sections (120 µm apart) throughout the entire rostrocaudal extent of the brain. For immunofluorescence, images were captured using a confocal microscope (model TCS SP5, Leica) and processed with Fiji ImageJ. For immunofluorescence studies, z‐stacks of ROIs (with six to eight optical sections; 2.5 µm/section; pinhole: 1 airy unit; line average: 2; resolution: 1024 × 1024 pixels) were captured at 20x, 40x, and 60x, then maximum‐projected and adjusted for brightness and contrast in Fiji ImageJ. For quantification of expression, each area was identified based on landmarks using the rat atlas (Swanson 2018) in two independent male animals. For quantification of ISH, tdTomato‐positive cells were outlined before determining whether they contained at least five puncta labeling CRFR1 mRNA.

Results

Brain‐Wide Analyses of the Transgenic Expression of Cre/Tom in CRFR1‐Cre‐2A‐tdTom Rats

To document the expression patterns of CRFR1 in the CRF1R‐Cre/tdTom rat, we selected a standardized set of brain regions located in sections that span the rostrocaudal extent of the brain and quantified tdTom‐positive cells in each brain area. The presence and degree of tdTomato expression were then compared with the expression patterns of Crfr1 mRNA reported in previous studies that used ISH in the rat brain (Potter et al. 1994; Van Pett et al. 2000). We found a remarkable similarity in the brain regions that express CRFR1, with only a few areas showing some inconsistencies in the levels of CRFR1 expression across the brain, as shown in Table 1. In addition, we observed that the expression patterns of CRFR1 in the CRFR1‐Cre‐2A‐tdTom rat closely resemble the pattern of Crfr1 mRNA in mice (Van Pett et al. 2000) as well as in the two CRFR1 transgenic reporter mice we have previously generated using similar BAC transgenic strategies (CRFR1‐GFP, Justice et al. 2008; CRFR1‐Cre, Jiang et al. 2018). In addition, we performed ISH for Crfr1 in CRFR1‐Cre‐2A‐tdTom brain slices and found that >80% of tdTomato‐positive cells colocalized with Crfr1 mRNA (Figure S1). This experiment, along with the similarity of the expression pattern to previously reported CRFR1 expression patterns in mice and rats, strongly suggests that the CRFR1‐Cre‐2A‐tdTom rat accurately labels CRFR1‐expressing neurons. In the following sections, we describe patterns of tdTom/Cre expression at different rostrocaudal levels of the rat brain by focusing mostly on brain regions that contain nuclei known for having important roles in mediating the stress response or for being affected by stress.

Prefrontal Cortical and Forebrain Regions (Swanson Atlas Sections 5–12)

At the rostral extent of the brain (Figure 1A), including medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) regions known for mediating the expression and extinction of conditioned stress‐evoked behaviors (Marek et al. 2019; Wellman and Moench 2019; de Oliveira Alvares and Do‐Monte 2021; Plas et al. 2024), we observe abundant tdTom labeling indicative of CRFR1 expression restricted to layers 2 and 3 of the dorsal anterior cingulate area (ACAd) and dorsal portion of the prelimbic area (PL) (Figure 1B,C), whereas in the more ventral portion of PL and in the adjacent infralimbic area (IL), CRFR1 expression is also detected in layer 5 (Figure 1C,D). In contrast, layer 6 neurons, distinguished by innervating the midline thalamic region (Vertes 2002; Xiao et al. 2009), display very sparse labeling along the entire dorsoventral extent of the mPFC. In the lateral aspect of the prefrontal cortex, significant labeling is observed in the transition between the lateral and the ventrolateral orbital areas (Figure 1A; ORBvl), which have been implicated in behavioral flexibility, goal‐directed responses, and task‐related cognitive maps (Bradfield and Hart 2020; Panayi et al. 2021; Klein‐Flügge et al. 2022), all functions largely affected during stress.

The labeling distribution of CRFR1 in these prefrontal regions is very similar to the pattern of CRFR1 expression in the CRFR1‐GFP mouse, as well as that detected by ISH of Crfr1 mRNA (Van Pett et al. 2000; Justice et al. 2008). Although the mPFC is not stratified into six distinct layers like the neocortex, layer‐specific expression of CRFR1 is better defined in dorsal regions of the mPFC (ACA and dorsal PL) compared to ventral regions of the mPFC (IL). In addition, expression of CRFR1 is high in layer 2 of the piriform area (PIR), as well as in the adjacent, less stratified endopiriform nucleus dorsal part (Figure 1A; EPd), which is consistent with previously reported patterns of CRFR1 expression in these regions in rats and mice. Both the piriform and the endopiriform areas are primarily involved in processing and integrating olfactory information (Sugai et al. 2012; Bekkers and Suzuki 2013), and previous studies have shown that stress‐induced functional and structural changes in these regions may contribute to impaired odor perception and olfactory memory formation (M. A. Smith et al. 1992; Bekkers and Suzuki 2013; Leem et al. 2014; Okuda et al. 2025). Collectively, our results demonstrate that CRFR1 expression reported by tdTomato in the CRFR1‐Cre‐2A‐tdTom rat matches known expression patterns for CRFR1 in stress‐associated areas of the anterior portions of the forebrain.

Other Neocortical Regions (Swanson Atlas Sections 13–18)

The neocortex begins to develop all six cortical layers at the level of the anterior commissure (Figure 2A). In the CRFR1‐Cre‐2A‐tdTom rat, we see a pattern of CRFR1 expression that precisely matches previously reported expression of CRFR1 in both the rat and the mouse (Van Pett et al. 2000; Justice et al. 2008). We observe abundant expression in both layers 2 and 3 of the neocortex. Although layers 2 and 3 are typically combined when cortical expression is reported, CRFR1 expression reveals a lower cell density in layer 2, which increases in layer 3, clearly separating the two layers (Figure 2B). In contrast to layer 2 neurons that make short‐range connections with nearby cortical areas, layer 3 neurons send long‐range corticocortical projections that are critical for both ipsilateral and contralateral communication (Tau and Peterson 2010).

Perhaps the most well‐defined cortical feature of CRFR1 expression is the very‐high expression levels in layer 4 (Potter et al. 1994; Van Pett et al. 2000). Identical to previous reports, we observe that nearly every cell in layer 4 expresses CRFR1, in contrast to layer 5, where very few neurons express CRFR1 (Figure 2B). The density of expression has moderate levels in the dorsal aspects of layer 6 (layer 6A), decreasing more ventrally within layer 6 (layer 6B) (Figure 2A,B). Additionally, the piriform cortex and the ventral olfactory tracts display copious CRFR1 expression (Figure 2C), which is nearly perfectly aligned with previously reported expression patterns for CRFR1 in both the rat and the mouse (Van Pett et al. 2000; Justice et al. 2008). It is worth noting that the reported expression of CRFR1 in the CRFR1‐Cre rat is not uniform throughout the neocortex at this or other caudal brain levels. Instead, cortical expression patterns vary in well‐defined anatomically and functionally distinct regions. For example, expression appears high in regions of the motor cortex (MOp), slightly decreases in somatosensory regions (SSp and SSs), and intensifies again in regions of the cortex that receive visceral information (VISC; Figure 2A). The level of anatomic resolution afforded by the CRFR1‐Cre‐2A‐tdTom rat allows for this and other subtleties of the CRFR1 expression pattern to be identified and examined.

The neocortex is very important for sensory processing, motor control, and higher cognitive functions. Layer 4 neurons are primarily responsible for receiving sensory thalamic inputs, processing them locally, and relaying the signals to other cortical layers, including layers 2 and 3 (Viaene et al. 2011). Thus, the large concentration of CRFR1 in these layers may explain why increased CRF transmission during stress significantly impacts neocortical functions.

Lateral Septal Nucleus, Bed Nucleus of the Terminal Stria, and Preoptic Hypothalamic Areas (Swanson Atlas Sections 14–22)

CRF has been shown to signal with neurons localized in the basal forebrain, including the lateral septal nucleus (LS) and the BST, as well as with neurons in anterior aspects of the hypothalamus, such as the medial and lateral preoptic areas (MPO and LPO). Both the LS and the BST regions are highly connected with broader stress circuits, with the LS receiving projections from the PVH and the ventral BST projecting to the PVH and adjacent hypothalamic regions (Jiang et al. 2018; Xu et al. 2019, Xu et al. 2023). The LS and BST are also highly interconnected with amygdalar circuits that are rich in CRF peptide (Walker et al. 2009). In turn, the MPO receives dense projections from magnocellular oxytocin‐ and vasopressin‐expressing neurons in the PVH and has been shown to mediate the expression of stress responses during socially and sexually charged contexts (Pedersen et al. 1994; Bosch et al. 2010; Okabe et al. 2017; Tsuneoka et al. 2022). In the CRFR1‐Cre‐2A‐tdTom rat, we observe extensive expression of tdTomato in all three regions (Figure 2A). In the BST (Figure 2D), the oval subnucleus of the BST (BSTov) contains the highest levels of CRF neuropeptide (Figure 2E–G). Interestingly, the BSTov lacks abundant cellular CRFR1 expression, suggesting that CRF may signal to CRFR1‐positive fibers that penetrate this BST subnucleus, as previously suggested in mice (Justice et al. 2008). In contrast, adjacent dorsal BST subnuclei that surround the BSTov, including the medial and the mediolateral subdivisions, display abundant expression of CRFR1 in both cells and within fibers. Ventral aspects of the BST below the anterior commissure display low expression of CRFR1 (Figure 2A), whereas medial regions display higher expression compared to lateral regions (Figure 2D). This distinction separates contiguous higher expression of CRFR1 within the anteromedial area of the BST (BSTam), with decreased expression in the fusiform subnucleus of the BST (BSTfu) located at the lateral edge of the larger BST cluster of subnuclei.

Medial to the ventral BST, expression is moderate in the MPO. Within the MPO, expression is highest in dorsal regions and declines ventrally, where the MPO is bordered by the third ventricle and meets the anteroventral periventricular nucleus. Expression of CRFR1 is low in the LPO. Notably, regions that lie both lateral and ventral to the LPO, along the ventral surface of the brain, including the olfactory tubercles, nucleus of the diagonal band, and striatal fundus, display low to moderate CRFR1 expression (Figure 2A).

Forebrain Regions at the Level of the Paraventricular Nucleus of the Hypothalamus (Swanson Atlas Sections 23–27)

At the level of the PVH, CRFR1 labeling is weak in the PVH, but expression becomes stronger in its ventral aspect and in the surrounding anterior and ventral hypothalamic regions, particularly in the subparaventricular zone (Figure 3A). Although the expression levels of CRFR1 are low, CRFR1‐labeled neurons in the PVH (Figure 3B,C) are in close proximity to CRF‐expressing cells within the same region (Figure 3D,E). In mice, PVH CRFR1‐positive neurons are GABAergic and have been shown to provide inhibitory feedback to neighboring CRF neurons, thereby limiting hyperactivity of the HPA axis (Ramot et al. 2017; Jiang et al. 2018). The observation that CRFR1‐labeled neurons are much less prominent in the rat PVH suggests that these cells might be primarily localized in neighboring hypothalamic regions, similar to the more segregated magnocellular and parvocellular neurons found in the rat PVH compared to the mouse PVH (Sawchenko et al. 1984). While this species difference is notable, the CRFR1 expression patterns in rats and mice broadly align at this rostrocaudal level. In the thalamus (TH) at the level of the PVH, prominent expression is visible in the PVT, anterodorsal nucleus, and nucleus reuniens. Lower densities of CRFR1‐positive cells are present in the reticular thalamic nucleus (RT) and anteromedial thalamus, with much lower expression levels in the lateral portions of the anterior thalamic complex. In striatal regions (STR), expression is sparse in the caudoputamen and absent in the substantia innominata and neighboring globus pallidus, a notable difference from CRFR1‐expression patterns in mice (Van Pett et al. 2000; Justice et al. 2008; Sztainberg et al. 2011; Hunt et al. 2018). At this anterior extent of the amygdala (Amyg), prominent expression is present in nuclei of the central and medial amygdala. In regions of the cortex, expression is sparse along the midline in the retrosplenial area (RSP), then increases through the somatomotor (MO), somatosensory (SS), and visceral (VISC) cortices. Notably, expression is highest in cortical layer 4, moderately high in layers 2/3, and sparser in layers 5 and 6. In the piriform area (PIR), expression is highest in layer 2 and decreases significantly in layer 3.

Forebrain Areas at the Rostrocaudal Levels of the Amygdala (Swanson Atlas Sections 23–31)

In sections that contain the main subnuclei of the amygdala, cortical CRFR1 expression is strongest in layer 4, followed by moderately dense expression in layers 2 and 3, and weak expression in layers 5 and 6 (Figure 4A), similar to more anterior cortical regions (Figures 2 and 3). In the hippocampus, labeled neurons are observed in CA1, CA3, and the dentate gyrus, although the level of expression is low. When images using the native fluorescence of tdTom are examined, expression appears to be much higher in the hippocampus, with most neurons in CA1 expressing CRFR1 (Figure S2). In the thalamus at this rostrocaudal level of the brain, many CRFR1‐positive neurons are visualized within the medial and ventral thalamic regions, as well as in the RT. Expression levels in the RT, which have been reported to be higher than in other surrounding thalamic regions in rats and mice, are not as dense in the CRFR1‐Cre rat, although there is still a substantial number of labeled cells in the RT (Figure 4A).

The most posterior of these amygdalar brain levels contains the caudal part of the caudoputamen, also known as the tail of the striatum, which is located dorsal to the lateral amygdala. This caudal juxta‐amygdalar striatal region exhibits a high density of CRFR1‐positive neurons in the CRFR1‐Cre rat (Figure 4A), in contrast to the more anterior regions of the striatum, where the number of labeled cells is relatively low. The caudal tail of the striatum is characterized by the convergence of corticostriatal, thalamostriatal, and nigrostriatal projections, which transmit multi‐sensory information essential for associative learning and goal‐oriented behaviors (Valjent and Gangarossa 2021). Despite the large number of CRFR1‐positive neurons in this region, it remains unknown how stress and CRF transmission affect the functionality of the caudal tail of the striatum.

In the posterior hypothalamus, high densities of CRFR1‐positive neurons are visible in the dorsomedial hypothalamus (DMH; Figure 4B), a region involved in the regulation of autonomic and neuroendocrine responses to stress (Brasil et al. 2019). In contrast, the ventromedial hypothalamus contains very few labeled neurons but displays dense labeling of synaptic fibers, suggesting that many CRFR1‐positive neurons innervate the VMH. Previous studies have shown that distinct subregions of the VMH play an important role in energy balance, reproductive processes, and defensive behaviors, all functions largely impaired during stress (Wang et al. 2015; Chang and Gean 2019; Nasanbuyan et al. 2024). In the arcuate nucleus, CRFR1 expression is scarce, but a moderate population of labeled neurons is observed in the medial tuberal nucleus (Figure 4B). Together, expression of CRFR1 reported by tdTomato is largely consistent with previously reported expression patterns in rats and mice at this rostrocaudal level (Van Pett et al. 2000; Justice et al. 2008).

Distinct Amygdalar Subnuclei (Swanson Atlas Sections 23–33)

The amygdala plays a crucial role in the integration and execution of the stress response (J. P. Herman and Cullinan 1997). CRF has been shown to signal in different amygdalar subnuclei, influencing neuronal activity and, consequently, stress and fear responses (Tovote et al. 2016; Sanford et al. 2017; Demaestri et al. 2024). In the CRFR1‐Cre rat, we have previously reported a misalignment between CRF peptide localization and CRFR1‐Cre expression in the central amygdalar nucleus (CeA; Weera et al. 2022), similar to previous reports of CRFR1 expression patterns observed using ISH for Crfr1 mRNA and transgenic reporter expression in the mouse (Van Pett et al. 2000; Justice et al. 2008). Accordingly, we observed a lack of CRFR1 expression in the lateral portion of the CeA (CeAl, Figure 4C), where CRF peptide labeling is highest, but only a few CRFR1‐positive neurons are visible (Justice et al. 2008). In contrast, the neighboring medial portion of the CeA (CeAm) contains many CRFR1‐positive neurons. The CeAm, as well as other nearby amygdalar subregions with abundant CRFR1‐positive neurons, send axonal and dendritic projections to the CeAl, where these cells respond to CRF release (Justice et al. 2008). In addition, the CeAl receives strong projections from the parabrachial nucleus and other hindbrain nuclei that contain many CRFR1‐positive neurons. Projections from CRFR1‐expressing neurons located in nuclei relatively distant from the CeA, such as the parabrachial nucleus, as well as nearby structures such as the CeAm, potentially express CRFR1 in their axonal terminals such that release of CRF in the CeAl can selectively modulate presynaptic activity onto CeA neurons in response to stress, as previously proposed in mice (Justice et al. 2008).

A significant number of CRFR1‐positive neurons are observed in distinct amygdalar subnuclei, including the lateral (LA), basolateral (BLA), basomedial (BMA), and medial (MEA), with the MEA containing the highest and strongest levels of expression (Figure 4C). Although CRFR1 expression has not been observed in the BLA/BMA subregions of the mouse CRFR1 reporter lines, ISH and other lines of evidence suggest that a subset of BLA neurons express CRFR1 and are responsive to the neuropeptide CRF (Van Pett et al. 2000; Roozendaal et al. 2002; Gehlert et al. 2005; Aukema et al. 2024). Interestingly, we also observed very little staining of intercalated cells in the amygdala (IA), in contrast to substantial staining of IA being present in the CRFR1‐GFP mouse (Justice et al. 2008). The presence or absence of CRFR1‐positive cells in the BLA or IA, respectively, may represent species differences between mice and rats, or it may reflect the strengths and limitations of different transgenic reporters of CRFR1 expression.

Thalamic Nuclei Including the Paraventricular Nucleus of the Thalamus (Swanson Atlas Sections 23–33)

The paraventricular nucleus of the thalamus (PVT) is an important region in the regulation of stress, fear, food seeking, feeding, nociception, and decision‐making processes (Do Monte et al. 2016; Millan et al. 2017; McGinty and Otis 2020; Kooiker et al. 2021; Penzo and Gao 2021; Kirouac 2025). Previous rodent studies have shown that a significant fraction of PVT neurons express CRF (Peng et al. 2017; Itoga et al. 2019; Engelke et al. 2021), and the PVT is one of the main sources of CRF‐expressing afferents to the ACB (Itoga et al. 2019), a critical region in the regulation of motivated behaviors (Klawonn and Malenka 2018). A prior report in mice demonstrated that the PVT also contains a significant subpopulation of CRFR1‐expressing neurons, and these cells do not overlap with CRF‐expressing neurons in the same region (Kooiker et al. 2023). Consistent with these findings, in the CRFR1‐Cre rat, we observe that CRFR1‐expressing neurons across the rostrocaudal aspect of the PVT (Figure 5A,B) lack CRF labeling but are in close contact with CRF‐positive fibers (Figure 5C–F). Using a Cre‐dependent viral reporter (AAV‐DIO‐GFP, Figure 5G), we detect significant colocalization between PVT CRFR1‐expressing neurons and the viral reporter GFP (Figure 5H–M), confirming the selectivity of the viral labeling. Dense axonal projections from CRFR1‐expressing PVT neurons are visualized mostly in the prefrontal cortex, ACB, and the RT (Figure 5N–P).

Other thalamic nuclei at the level of the PVT with high expression of CRFR1 include the laterodorsal, central medial, ventromedial, RT, and reuniens nuclei. Moderate CRFR1 labeling is observed within the epithalamus, particularly in the lateral nucleus of the habenula. At the more caudal aspect of the thalamus, including the lateral and medial geniculate nuclei regions, there is extensive expression in the superior colliculus. There is very little labeling in the ventral portion of the medial geniculate nucleus, but numerous positive cells are present in its dorsal aspect. In the lateral geniculate nucleus, there are scarce labeled neurons in the dorsal nucleus, but more abundant positive neurons in its ventral part. Together, the expression of the CRFR1 reporter in the thalamus is largely in agreement with previous mouse studies using transgenic reporters or ISH techniques in rats (Van Pett et al. 2000; Justice et al. 2008).

Midbrain Regions at the Levels of the Superior Colliculus (Swanson Atlas Sections 38–45)

CRF has been demonstrated to serve important functions in key autonomic nuclei located in the brainstem (Lehnert et al. 1998; M. W. Lewis et al. 2002; De Castro e Silva et al. 2006). Particularly in the midbrain subdivision of the brainstem at the levels of the superior colliculus (SC), we observe dense CRFR1 expression in the interpeduncular nucleus (IPN, Figure 6A), a region implicated in mood regulation and stress‐induced anxiety‐like behaviors in rodents (Zhao‐Shea et al. 2015; Sherafat et al. 2020). High to moderate expression is visualized in the substantia nigra compact part (SNc), with a lower number of CRFR1‐labeled cells in the substantia nigra reticular part (SNr) and the ventral tegmental nucleus (VTA). Consistent with our observation, previous studies in both rats and mice have shown significant CRFR1 expression in SNc and VTA neurons (Van Pett et al. 2000; Justice et al. 2008). CRFR1‐related signaling in these midbrain regions enhances the activity of dopaminergic neurons (Wanat et al. 2008), and knocking out CRFR1 in these cells reduces stress‐induced dopamine release in prefrontal cortex areas and promotes anxiety‐like behaviors, suggesting that CRFR1 signaling in midbrain dopaminergic neurons has an anxiolytic effect (Refojo et al. 2011).

At the level of the PAG, CRFR1 expression is higher in the postsubiculum (POST) and the superior colliculus (SC), with a smaller number of labeled neurons in the dorsolateral and ventrolateral columns of the PAG (Figure 6B), two regions involved in the expression of threat‐induced active versus passive defensive responses, respectively (Reis et al. 2023). Notably, SC neurons involved in threat detection send excitatory projections to the PAG to mediate the initiation of threat‐evoked defensive responses (Evans et al. 2018), but how the CRFergic neurotransmission modulates this pathway to regulate the expression of defensive behaviors remains unexplored.

Pontine Regions at the Levels of the Locus Ceruleus (Swanson Atlas Sections 34–58)

In the pons at the level of the locus ceruleus (LC), expression is high in the laterodorsal tegmental nucleus (LDT), parabrachial nucleus (PB), and principal sensory nucleus of the trigeminal nerve (Figure 7A). The LDT is part of the reticular activating system, which plays a critical role in regulating attention and arousal states (Brudzynski 2014), particularly during stress‐related situations (Fernandez et al. 2018; Romero‐Leguizamón and Kohlmeier 2020; Shabani et al. 2023). The high expression of CRFR1 in the LDT of CRFR1‐Cre rats is consistent with previous studies using transgenic mice or ISH in rats, which have shown dense CRFR1 labeling in LDT neurons (Van Pett et al. 2000; Justice et al. 2008). The LDT also contains a significant number of neurons that express the neuropeptide CRF (Swanson et al. 1983; Y. Chen et al. 2015; Kono et al. 2017), and these cells are activated to promote stress‐induced anxiety responses in rodents (Tang et al. 2021). However, it remains unknown whether CRFR1‐positive neurons in the LDT are modulated by the local release of CRF.

The LC is a critical region in the regulation of stress‐induced arousal responses (Ross and Van Bockstaele 2020; Slavova et al. 2024). LC neurons are identified by their rich expression of tyrosine hydroxylase, a rate‐limiting enzyme in the synthesis of catecholamines including noradrenaline and dopamine (Manger and Eschenko 2021). In mice, TH‐expressing neurons in the LC are largely devoid of CRFR1 but are surrounded by CRFR1‐positive neurons in neighboring pontine regions including the parabrachial nucleus (PB) (Justice et al. 2008), although CRFR1 expression has been reported by TH+ LC neurons in other studies (cf. Reyes et al. 2006), and CRF has been shown to modulate the excitability and function of LC neurons (Valentino et al. 1983; Butler et al. 1990; Lehnert et al. 1998; Lejeune and Millan 2003). In the CRFR1‐Cre rat, CRFR1 labeling is mostly absent in the LC with significantly higher expression in PB, particularly in its medial and lateral divisions (Figure 7B,C). PB neurons are directly innervated by inputs from CRF‐expressing cells in the CeA, and activation of this pathway promotes analgesia during chronic pain states (O'Neill and Meszaros 2020).

Medulla Oblongata Regions at the Level of the Nucleus of Solitary Tract (Swanson Atlas Sections 58–72)

Neurons expressing high levels of the CRFR1 reporter are present in distinct nuclei containing motor and sensory neurons that exit the brain via cranial nerves. For instance, expression is high in the spinal nucleus of the trigeminal nerve (SPV, Figure 8A,B), which contains neurons that innervate the face via the fifth cranial nerve, and moderate in the spinal vestibular nucleus (SPIV) and the spinal nucleus of the trigeminal nerve interpolar part (SPVI, Figure 8C,D). Medial regions of the medulla oblongata are peppered with neurons positive for CRFR1 reporter expression, with only slight variations between different reticular areas.

Among distinct medullary regions, the nucleus of the solitary tract (NTS) has been the most thoroughly characterized structure in mediating the influences of stress on autonomic tone (Pace and Myers 2023). The NTS projects to many nuclei that contain abundant CRF neurons and serves as an entry point for stress‐relevant visceral information to reach the broader stress‐activated central autonomic circuit (J. P. Herman 2018). In the CRFR1‐Cre rat, expression of CRFR1 is abundant in the NTS, especially in its central zone (Figure 8B,C). High CRFR1 expression is also observed in the inferior olivary complex (Figure 8B), a relay station between the spinal cord and the cerebellum (Paul and Das 2025), and in the ambiguus nucleus dorsal division (Figure 8B), which innervates somatic muscles of the pharynx, larynx, and soft palate involved in swallowing and phonation, and contributes to parasympathetic cardiac inhibition via the vagus nerve (Petko and Tadi 2025).

Discussion

We have thoroughly described the expression patterns of CRFR1 across the brain of the CRFR1‐Cre‐2A‐tdTom‐Cre rat and compared these patterns with previous literature on CRFR1 expression in both rats and mice. Our brain‐wide survey demonstrated that Cre/tdTom transgene expression closely mirrors previously reported expression patterns of CRFR1 in rats (Potter et al. 1994; Van Pett et al. 2000), aligning with mice studies in neuroanatomical distribution except for minor interspecies variations in expression intensity (Van Pett et al. 2000; Justice et al. 2008), which suggests subtle species‐specific differences in CRFR1 distribution. We further showed that tdTomato expression is readily detectable without immunohistochemical staining and that transgenic Cre efficiently drives Cre‐dependent viral expression of fluorescent reporters, enabling precise circuit mapping. We employed this strategy to map projection targets of CRFR1‐expressing PVT neurons; however, this approach can be used to express any exogenous viral probe selectively in CRFR1‐positive neurons, thereby allowing for functional interrogation of CRF signaling using innovative tools in vivo, particularly in behavioral assays that require rats as the model organism. Our findings solidify the reliability of the CRFR1‐Cre‐2A‐tdTom rat as a robust tool for probing CRF signaling pathways, including their anatomical organization, neuronal characteristics, and functional roles.

We observed transgenic expression of tdTomato and Cre recombinase in the CRFR1‐Cre‐2A‐tdTom‐Cre rat in a pattern that is highly reminiscent of previously reported expression patterns in mouse and rat (Van Pett et al. 2000; Justice et al. 2008). This includes very high levels of expression in layer 4 of the cortex, in the pontine gray, and in granule cells of the cerebellum. Other regions of the cortex, basal forebrain, midbrain, and hindbrain all align very well to reported expression patterns for CRFR1. This suggests that the CRFR1‐Cre rat contains most of the key elements of the Crfr1 promoter/enhancers that specify the expression pattern. However, there are a few differences between reported CRFR1 expression patterns that are notable. For example, in the basal ganglia, CRFR1 reporter expression is almost absent in the striatum and globus pallidus, regions that display high levels of expression in the CRFR1‐GFP mouse and by ISH quantification in the rat (Van Pett et al. 2000; Justice et al. 2008), consistent with previous studies from our group and others describing direct effects of CRF in the globus pallidus and striatum (Sztainberg et al. 2011; Hunt et al. 2018; Lemos et al. 2019). In the amygdala, we observed expression in the lateral and basolateral nuclei that are underrepresented in the CRFR1‐GFP mouse but positive by ISH in the rat (Van Pett et al. 2000; Justice et al. 2008). However, we observed much higher levels of expression in the medial nucleus of the amygdala, where the effects of CRF have been much less explored. Differences between transgenic reporters and mRNA expression patterns may originate from a number of sources. First, the cellular resolution of tdTomato immunofluorescence is much greater than the cellular resolution provided by radioactive ISH, such that expression can appear weaker when detected by radioactivity. There can also be certain patterns that are emphasized by reporter expression, potentially due to the genomic location of the BAC transgene, as we have seen in other reporter lines that we have generated (Justice et al. 2008; Jiang et al. 2018). Nonetheless, the significant alignment between tdTom/Cre reporter expression and previously reported expression patterns in both rat and mouse gives us confidence that the CRFR1‐Cre‐2A‐tdTom‐Cre rat delivers accurate expression of tdTomato and Cre in CRFR1‐positive cells in most brain regions.

When CRF was first isolated in the 1980s, the rat was the most commonly used model organism to study physiological changes evoked by stress (Deussing and Chen 2018). Most of these studies utilized gain‐of‐function experiments involving exogenous CRF administration to demonstrate that CRF signaling is sufficient to drive endocrine, autonomic, and behavioral changes reminiscent of the stress responses (Britton et al. 1982; Sutton et al. 1982). Rats were the primary model for CRF research until the late 1990s, when advances in mouse genetics allowed for selective mutation of genes to study the result of loss‐of‐function phenotypes. Since then, the family of CRF‐related receptors and neuropeptides has grown to include two receptors (CRFR1 and CRFR2) and three additional neuropeptides with high sequence similarity to CRF that also signal to CRFR1 and/or CRFR2, termed urocortins 1, 2, and 3 (Vaughan et al. 1995; K. Lewis et al. 2001; Reyes et al. 2001). Each of the genes encoding receptors for both CRF and CRF‐like neuropeptedes were mutated individually, and in some cases in combination, to determine how the lack of CRF signaling alters normal physiological function and stress‐evoked changes in both physiology and behavior in the context of stress (Muglia et al. 1995; G. W. Smith et al. 1998; Timpl et al. 1998; Contarino et al. 1999; Kishimoto et al. 2000; Bale et al. 2002; A. Chen et al. 2006).

Mouse genetics on CRF, CRFR1, and related CRF‐family signaling components confirmed that CRF is required for stress‐responsive activation of the HPA axis; both Crf and Crfr1 mutant mice do not display elevated circulating corticosteroid levels in response to stress (Muglia et al. 1995; G. W. Smith et al. 1998). One notable phenotype of Crfr1 mutant female mice is their inability to reproduce viable offspring, which is attributed to a requirement for HPA axis–mediated elevations in corticosteroid levels during embryonic development of the lungs. Despite the lack of an HPA axis stress response or CRF signaling centrally in response to stress, Crfr1 mutant animals surprisingly lack strong behavioral phenotypes, although they display mildly reduced anxiety‐related behavior when tested in a standard battery of behavioral assays used to measure anxiety/depression‐like behaviors in mice (e.g., the elevated plus maze, open field, and forced swim test).

Gain‐of‐function experiments in rats have demonstrated that centrally delivered CRF has dramatic effects on the expression of stress‐evoked behaviors (Britton et al. 1982; Sutton et al. 1982). These findings were further supported by studies in mice using neuron‐selective mutation of Crfr1 (using Cam2KA‐Cre and a floxed Crfr1 allele), which showed reduced stress‐evoked behaviors, while the HPA axis response to stress remained unchanged (Sutton et al. 1982; Müller et al. 2003). More comprehensive experiments in which Crfr1 was selectively mutated in many distinct classes of cells (e.g., glutamatergic, GABAergic, or dopaminergic neurons) revealed more nuanced roles for CRF signaling within different neural circuitry (Refojo et al. 2011; Sztainberg et al. 2011). For instance, loss‐of‐function in CRF signaling within glutamatergic neurons has anxiolytic effects, whereas the same manipulation within dopaminergic neurons has an anxiogenic effect in rodents (Refojo et al. 2011). Increased anxiety‐related behavior was also observed when CRFR1 expression was abrogated within the globus pallidus (Sztainberg et al. 2011), supporting the idea that CRF signaling can either enhance or attenuate the effects of stress, thereby increasing or decreasing the expression of anxiety‐related behaviors, respectively.

The rapid development of powerful genetically encoded tools for manipulating neural activity in specific cell populations while monitoring behavior in freely moving animals has advanced our understanding of how CRF signaling modulates anxiety and stress‐related responses. The development of CRFR1 reporter mice revealed high‐resolution mapping of CRF receptor expression across brain regions involved in stress responses (Justice et al. 2008; Kühne et al. 2012). More recently, CRFR1‐Cre mice, which enable targeted manipulation of CRFR1‐positive neurons through localized viral delivery, have begun to uncover the role of CRF signaling in previously untested stress‐related circuits (Sanford et al. 2017; Hunt et al. 2018; Jiang et al. 2018). The generation and neuroanatomical validation of the CRFR1‐Cre2A‐tdTom rat now allow experiments on stress‐related circuits previously identified in mice, while taking advantage of the rat's more complex behavioral repertoire and experimental paradigms (Homberg et al. 2017; Iannaccone and Jacob 2009). Collectively, our findings validating precise transgenic expression in CRFR1 neurons demonstrate the utility of the CRFR1‐Cre rat model for studies involving the investigation of CRF signaling pathways, circuit‐specific manipulations, and species‐specific behavioral assays.

In the Cortex (A,D,G), PVN (B,E,H) and TRN (C,F,I) we observed that greater than 80% of tdTom positive cells (red), were overlaid by clusters of green puncta, indicating a high degree of coexpression of tdTom and Crfr1 in the CRFR1‐Cre‐2A‐Tom rat. Scale bar = 50um.

Supplemental Figure 2. Fluorescent images of CRFR1‐Cre‐2a‐Tom rat brain sections. In the prefrontal cortex (A), the hippocampus (B), Amygdala (C), ventral midbrain (D), dorsal midbrain (E), and hindbrain (F), tdTomato neurons are visible as red fluorescent in a pattern that matches immunohistochemical staining for tdTom protein. Scale bar = 500um.
