MonoPER study: Effectiveness and tolerability of PERampanel in monotherapy in real‐world after 12 months of treatment
Authors: Alejandro Fernández‐Cabrera, Paula Santamaría‐Montero, Jesús García de‐Soto, Robustiano Pego‐Reigosa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the effectiveness, tolerability, and retention of PER as monotherapy in adults with focal or idiopathic generalized epilepsy over a 12‐month period in routine clinical practice.MethodsIn this single‐center, ambispective, noninterventional observational study, 74 patients (age 18–90 years) initiated PER monotherapy—either as first‐line treatment (n = 21) or conversion from another antiseizure medication (n = 53)—and were followed for 12 months. Demographic and clinical variables, prior antiseizure regimens, indication for PER initiation (suboptimal seizure control, adverse effects, or sleep improvement), PER dosage, seizure outcomes (seizure freedom and ≥ 50% reduction), and adverse events (AEs) were recorded at 6‐ and 12‐month visits. Retention rates were calculated, and safety was assessed by AE reports and discontinuations.ResultsThe mean PER dose was 7.22 ± 1.71 mg/day, with 85.1% of patients remaining on monotherapy at 12 months (first‐line: 90.5%; conversion: 83.0%). Overall, 70.3% of patients achieved seizure freedom at 12 months (first‐line: 81.0%; conversion: 66.0%), and 79.7% were ≥ 50% responders (first‐line: 85.7%; conversion: 75.5%). In patients initiated for sleep improvement (n = 24), hypnotic medications were withdrawn in 87.5%, and 79% reported better sleep. AEs were reported in 46.2% of patients at 12 months, most commonly somnolence (28.4%), dizziness (18.9%), and irritability (10.8%). Severe AEs leading to discontinuation occurred in 6.7% of cases.SignificanceIn a real‐world cohort, PER monotherapy demonstrated high rates of seizure freedom and retention, with a manageable safety profile. These findings support PER as an effective and tolerable monotherapy option for adult patients with focal or idiopathic generalized epilepsy, corroborating similar observations in recent Epilepsia Open studies of antiseizure monotherapy.Plain Language SummaryPerampanel (PER) is approved as an add‐on treatment for seizures, but information on its use alone (monotherapy) is scarce. INTRODUCTION

Epilepsy is a chronic neurological disease with an estimated global prevalence of 50 000 000 people, accounting for approximately 1% of the global burden of disease.

After initiation of an effective antiseizure drug, up to 70% of patients remain seizure‐free in the long term, of which 70% achieve this state with a single drug. 1 Polytherapy modestly increases efficacy in seizure control over monotherapy at the cost of a significant increase in adverse effects; so these schemes should be used rationally. 2

The advantages of using antiseizure medication (ASM) in monotherapy have been extensively detailed in numerous publications, highlighting the greater ease of optimizing drug doses, simpler posology, reduced treatment costs, lower risk of adverse effects and drug interactions, and, consequently, greater tolerance and adherence. 3 All this makes this treatment regimen the preferred option of clinicians in cases that allow its use, generally achieving adequate control of the disease and a marked improvement in the quality of life of patients.

Perampanel (PER) is a selective non‐competitive antagonist of the ionotropic AMPA glutamate receptor, the main excitatory neurotransmitter of the central nervous system. Its pharmacokinetic characteristics include high plasma protein binding (95%) and hepatic metabolism mediated by CYP3A, with a half‐life of 105 h when administered in the absence of inhibitors of this cytochrome. At present, a single daily oral formulation is available.

This drug has been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) as adjuvant treatment of generalized tonic–clonic seizures in patients older than 12 years and of focal seizures with or without evolution to generalized tonic–clonic seizures in patients older than 4 years; in the latter case, it can also be used in monotherapy. FDA approval of its use in monotherapy was made by extrapolating data from adjuvant PER trials, in the absence of randomized controlled trials in these conditions. However, in Europe, the European Medicines Agency (EMA) requires studies demonstrating the non‐inferiority of this drug in monotherapy for its approval in this regimen, so that for the time being, its use is contemplated only as adjuvant treatment in focal seizures in patients older than 4 years and in generalized tonic–clonic seizures in patients older than 7 years. 3 , 4

Despite the evolution of drug trials in recent years, there is currently insufficient information available on the use of the latest generation of ASM in monotherapy, including PER. Of particular relevance in this regard are the “real‐world” studies which, being outside the framework of randomized controlled clinical trials, provide a source of efficacy and tolerability data that can support the clinician in his daily practice. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8

On the other hand, there are new articles on the effect of PER on bone health, as well as on its efficacy and tolerability in other types of epilepsy such as Dravet syndrome. 9 , 10

The aim of this work was to analyze the clinical characteristics of patients with focal or idiopathic generalized epilepsy treated with PER in monotherapy in the context of real world use, as well as the effectiveness and safety of the drug.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

A single‐center, observational, ambispective, non‐interventional study was conducted to determine the safety and effectiveness of PER in monotherapy. The study protocol was approved by the ethics committee of Santiago de Compostela—Lugo with study number 2024/419. Informed consent was obtained from each patient or their legal representative for data collection and analysis. The study followed the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology) guidelines. 11

Data collection

For each case, demographic variables were collected (age and sex), type of epilepsy (focal or idiopathic generalized), cause of epilepsy when known, ASM prior to starting PER, reason for the change of ASM (suboptimal seizure control, adverse events or necessity of improve nocturnal rest), dose of PER used, treatment efficacy (seizure freedom, 50% responders), treatment efficacy considering the reason for starting, adverse events, and whether this treatment was maintained in subsequent follow‐up visits.

Regarding the reason for changing ASM, “suboptimal seizure control” was considered when, in the opinion of the attending neurologist, the epilepsy was not adequately controlled with the previous ASM. “Necessity of improve nocturnal rest” was considered when PER was added in order to improve nocturnal rest in patients with poor sleep quality affecting their quality of life or patients where the attending neurologist considered a seizure because of lack of sleep, given the effect of PER on sleep. PER was chosen as add‐on and finally conversion to monotherapy in order to improve sleep as well as improving epilepsy control. Adverse events (AE) were considered self‐explanatory when it was necessary to change the ASM because of an AE.

Study participants

The patients were recruited from the neurology clinic of the Lucus Augusti University Hospital.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) age over 18 and under 90 years; (2) diagnosis of epilepsy, regardless of type; (3) patients who started PER as monotherapy, either as first line or as a “conversion therapy”; and (4) signature of informed consent according to the study protocol either by the patient or by his or her representatives. Exclusion criteria included: (1) inability to give informed consent; (2) non‐reliable information on previous seizures or adverse effects of drugs due to the characteristics of the clinical history or of the patients themselves.

Data analysis and objectives

The data analysis included all patients who met the inclusion criteria and initiated treatment with PER as monotherapy, either as initial monotherapy or after conversion, and who had at least one efficacy measurement after initiation of treatment. Patients were evaluated 6 months and 1 year after starting treatment with PER, following the center's usual clinical care protocol. Data are given for the total population and for two subpopulations: (1) patients who received first‐line treatment with PER, that is, who had not previously received treatment with any ASM; and (2) patients who converted to PER monotherapy, that is, patients who were previously taking an ASM and were switched to PER monotherapy. The statistical analysis was performed using the two previously described groups. Patients who started another treatment with an additional ASM during the observation period were considered to have discontinued or abandoned PER treatment for the statistical study.

The primary endpoint of effectiveness was the percentage of seizure‐free patients 1 year after initiation of PER. Secondary effectiveness endpoints were the percentage of patients with a 50% response rate. The rate of retention of PER monotherapy at 1 year and patients with worsening seizures were also assessed.

Safety was assessed by patient reports of AEs during the follow‐up period. The severity of these AEs and whether drug discontinuation occurred for this reason were also assessed.

Missing values were not considered for the analysis, except in cases of discontinuation, where the last observation method was used for the treatment of missing response data. For the comparison of qualitative variables, the chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test was used. Quantitative variables were compared by Student's t‐test or Mann–Whitney's test if they were not normally distributed. Related quantitative variables were analyzed using Wilcoxon's test. Statistical analyses were performed with IBM SPSS Statistics 25. The threshold for statistical significance was 5% (p < 0.05).

RESULTS

A total of 74 patients were enrolled in the study, including 53 who converted to monotherapy and 21 who started monotherapy directly. The reasons for switching to monotherapy were ineffectiveness of previous ASM (35.1%), necessity of improving nocturnal rest and AE (32.4% in both cases). The majority of patients had focal epilepsy (75.7%), with this percentage being even higher in the starting monotherapy subgroup (85.7%). The most common etiology of epilepsy was structural and vascular (both 18.9%) followed by “unknown” (16.2%). Demographic and clinical characteristics of the patients can be seen in Table 1.

The mean dose used was similar in both groups, with a mean of 7.08 ± 1.67 in the initiation group and 7.31 ± 1.91 in the conversion group (p = 0.779). The most frequently used dose was 6 mg (n = 36) followed closely by 8 mg (n = 31); only six patients had the maximum dose of 12 mg. The retention of the drug can be seen in Figure 1. The percentage of retention was 90.5% and 85.1% at 6 months and 1 year, respectively. In the initiation group, the retention at one year was 90.5%, and in the monotherapy conversion group, it was 83%; this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.579). A total of 11 patients discontinued the drug: two in the initiation group and nine in the conversion group (p = 0.494).

The effectiveness of the treatment was evaluated both in terms of seizure freedom (seizure‐free, 50% responder) and in a subanalysis of the efficacy in the case that the treatment was given for “necessity of improve nocturnal rest,” evaluating in the latter whether concomitant medication used by the patients to fall asleep (such as benzodiazepines) was reduced. In the general group, seizure freedom at 12 months was achieved in 70.3% of the patients (52/74), being 81% (n = 17) in the initiation group and 66% (n = 35) in the group of conversion to monotherapy (p = 0.266). Of the patients in the conversion group who were seizure‐free at the 12‐month visit (n = 35), 15 (42.9%) were previously seizure‐free. They were 50% responders in all seizure types at 1 year 79.7% (n = 59) of patients, being 85.7% (n = 18) in the baseline group and 75.5% (n = 40) in the conversion to monotherapy group (Figure 2). A worsening of seizures was reported in a total of 6.8% (n = 5) of patients, 4.7% (n = 1) in the monotherapy group and 7.5% (n = 4) in the conversion group (p = 0.999). Subanalysis of effectiveness by epilepsy subtypes and by epilepsy etiology was not performed because of insufficient patient numbers.

Seizure responsiveness after 12 months.

Regarding the type of epilepsy, a total of 18 patients with IGE were included: 3 in the initiation group and 15 in the conversion to monotherapy subgroup. Of these patients, 14 became seizure‐free at the 12‐month visit, including 100% of those in the initiation group. Statistical significance was not reached; likely due to the small number of patients with IGE (p = 0.398).

When the drug was used to improve epilepsy and nocturnal rest, 87.5% of the patients (n = 21) were able to discontinue drugs prescribed as hypnotics. Of this subgroup, 79% (n = 19) reported an improvement in nocturnal rest; there were two patients who were able to withdraw drugs to fall asleep but still reported no improvement in nocturnal rest. A mean of 1.02 ± 0.41 sleep‐inducing drugs was successfully stopped in this subgroup of patients.

The most frequently used previous ASM in the monotherapy conversion group was levetiracetam (n = 22, 41.5%), followed by lacosamide (n = 12, 22.6%) and lamotrigine (n = 8, 15.1%). No differences were found in the results according to previous ASM (p = 0.987).

The drug was generally well tolerated, with 38.9% of patients reporting AEs at 6 months and 46.2% at 12 months. Most of the adverse effects were rated as “mild” or “moderate.” The most frequent adverse effects were drowsiness (28.4%), dizziness (18.9%), and irritability (10.8%). There were no notable differences between the two groups in the reporting of AEs except for drowsiness, which was much more marked in the baseline monotherapy versus conversion group (52.4% vs. 18.9%) (p ≤ 0.05). DISCUSSION

In this analysis in a real‐world setting with a cohort of patients and a follow‐up of at least 1 year, the efficacy and safety of PER in monotherapy have been demonstrated. One‐year seizure freedom rates were consistently above 70% in the entire population treated with PER in monotherapy and reached approximately 80% in those receiving PER as first‐line treatment. These results are similar to seizure freedom results in other “new generation” ASM in monotherapy. 7 , 12

We are not aware of many other studies of PER in monotherapy that allow an exact comparison with the results of our study. An extension analysis of the FREEDOM study examined the efficacy and safety of perampanel in focal seizures at 12 months; however, this study differs in the doses and characteristics of the patients from ours and also differs in the methodology; however, this study also showed a lower response than ours, with responses of around 67.4%, although, as mentioned above, the doses were lower than in our study. 13 Another study also investigated the efficacy of PER in monotherapy for 12 months, with a design similar to ours, although with lower mean doses than those in our study, with response rates slightly lower, being 52% of patients seizure‐free and 61% of responders at 50%. 14 This may be because their doses were lower than ours. The retention rate was lower than ours, 63% at 1 year versus 85.1%, and may be because their study had younger patients.

A study from 2020 already evaluated the efficacy of PER in monotherapy, with a design similar to ours, although it was retrospective and not prospective. They enrolled a total of 98 patients, with a mean age similar to ours (49.6 vs 50.6) and had retention rates at 12 months comparable to ours (80.9% vs 85.1%). The 50% responder and seizure freedom rate was slightly lower than our study at 12 months, with results of 41.5% and 52.8% versus 70.3% and 79.8%. The differences could be due to the dose, which was lower. 8

With response rates closer to ours, a study of PER in monotherapy in children reported results similar to ours, with 82.6% responders, 15 although patient characteristics, again, were not comparable.

Caution should be exercised, however, in interpreting these data since many real‐life studies do not report the number of previous seizures or were seizure‐free at treatment initiation, as it happens in our series, where approximately 50% of the monotherapy conversion group were previously seizure‐free. Our data also confirm the persistence of the response to PER maintained over time. We have not found any study in the literature that evaluates the reduction of other sleep medications such as benzodiazepines when PER is introduced. The worsening of seizures is similar to that reported with other ASMs in the literature.

Regarding adverse effects, most were mild or moderate. The number of patients who needed to abandon the drug due to AEs was similar to those reported in general by other ASMs. 16 All AEs reported were similar to those reported by other ASMs, except for drowsiness, which was significantly higher in our study than what is generally reported by ASMs. However, no patient had to stop the drug because of this AE, and most considered it “mild” and allowing them to go on with life as usual. Our study reports perhaps a greater likelihood of drowsiness than in similar studies, possibly because we used higher doses on average than in those reports. 8 , 13 , 15

The apparent noticeable difference in drowsiness between our two study groups may probably be due to the fact that in many cases the reason for the switch to PER in the conversion group was precisely to improve nighttime rest, so it is logical that less drowsiness was reported in that group. Likewise, in our series, the age of the patients, especially in the monotherapy conversion group, is slightly older than in other similar real‐life studies, which may lead to a higher proportion of dizziness or memory impairment; this would also likely be the reason for the apparent difference between our two groups regarding this specific adverse effect. 8 , 14

LIMITATIONS

This study has some limitations due to its observational design, with no control group and no “blinded” group, so the data should be interpreted with caution. In addition, the sample size is small and an underrepresentation of AE cannot be ruled out. Additionally, this study was not designed to assess differences between groups; therefore, our groups have non‐comparable characteristics.

Nevertheless, it is still a study with a long follow‐up time and in real life. Studies with a larger number of patients, multicenter, and even with a control group are needed to improve the evidence.

CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, this study supports the efficacy and safety in monotherapy of PER in real‐life epilepsy patients, including those directly initiating PER in monotherapy and elderly patients. PER may have a good profile in certain patients and could allow a reduction of other drugs.
