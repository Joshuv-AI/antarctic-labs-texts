Successful gene therapy for transfusion-dependent α-thalassemia: a case report∗
Authors: Xiaolin Yin, Linxia Qian, Guoyi Dong, Yue Chen, Yali Zhou, Beibei Yang, Guiping Liao, Haigang Sun, Honglian Guo, Xinru Zeng, Huilin Zou, Ting Ye, Jiajun He, Wenwen Yao, Wenjie Ouyang, Xinhua Zhang, Chao Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•First-in-human lentiviral gene therapy (HGI-002) achieves durable transfusion freedom and good safety for ≥24 months in an HbH–Constant Spring patient.•Modest gene correction (33.3% marrow chimerism) sustains adequate Hb, supporting reduced-intensity conditioning in α-thalassemia.

Introduction

α-Thalassemia is one of the most common inherited Hb (hemoglobin) disorders worldwide, affecting ∼5% of the global population.1 It results from deletional or nondeletional mutations in the α-globin gene cluster on chromosome 16p13.3, which impair α-globin chain synthesis.2, 3, 4 The imbalance between α- and non–α-globin chains leads to the formation of abnormal tetramers, such as HbH (β4) in adults and Hb Bart (γ4) in fetuses.1 These tetramers exhibit abnormally high oxygen affinity and contribute to ineffective erythropoiesis, chronic hemolysis, and anemia.5

Clinically, α-thalassemia encompasses a wide spectrum of severity. Silent carriers (1 gene affected) and α-thalassemia trait (2 genes affected) are typically asymptomatic, whereas HbH disease (3 affected genes) presents with variable anemia, and Hb Bart hydrops fetalis (4 affected genes) is usually fatal in utero. HbH disease is the most common clinically significant form, traditionally considered mild to moderate, but disease severity differs between genotypes.5 Deletional HbH is generally milder, with most patients not requiring transfusions, whereas nondeletional HbH (particularly Hb Constant Spring [Hb-CS]) is more severe, often presenting earlier, with increased hemolysis and higher rates of transfusion dependence.6, 7, 8, 9 HbH-CS, the most prevalent nondeletional variant in Southeast Asia and increasingly reported in North America, represents a high-risk subgroup and a priority population for curative interventions.5,10,11

For patients with transfusion-dependent HbH, treatment options remain limited. Chronic red blood cell (RBC) transfusions alleviate anemia but lead to progressive iron overload, necessitating long-term chelation therapy with associated toxicities and adherence challenges.12 Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation offers the only established cure but is restricted by donor availability and carries significant risks, including graft-versus-host disease and transplant-related mortality.

Gene therapy has emerged as a promising curative strategy for hemoglobinopathies.13,14 Lentiviral gene addition and CRISPR/Cas9-based editing have recently achieved regulatory approval in β-thalassemia and sickle cell disease.15,16 In contrast, gene therapy for α-thalassemia remains in early clinical development,17 with no approved products to date. Lentiviral vector–mediated α-globin gene addition has demonstrated the ability to restore balanced Hb synthesis in disease models, supporting its potential clinical application.18,19

Here, we report, to our knowledge, the first-in-human clinical application of lentiviral α-globin gene therapy in a patient with transfusion-dependent HbH-CS disease. Autologous CD34+ hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) were collected and genetically modified ex vivo using a lentiviral vector (Lenti-HBA) to restore α-globin expression. The final product was named HGI-002 injection, which was then reinfused after busulfan conditioning. The patient achieved long-term hematologic recovery and transfusion freedom, providing early clinical evidence of the feasibility and therapeutic potential for this strategy.

Methods

Study design

This single-arm, open-label phase 1 study (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05851105) was conducted to assess the safety and efficacy of gene therapy (HGI-002 injection) for transfusion-dependent α-thalassemia. The protocol was approved by the local ethics committee of The 923rd Hospital of the Joint Logistics Support Force of the People’s Liberation Army (Nanning, China), and all procedures were performed in adherence with the Declaration of Helsinki. Before enrollment, we obtained written informed consent from the patient and her legal guardian. The patient underwent comprehensive screening for eligibility (inclusion and exclusion criteria are summarized in supplemental Table 1) and was followed up for 24 months after HGI-002 infusion. The data analysis was performed by BGI Hemogen Therapeutic, and all authors had access to the primary clinical data.

Treatment process

This study included screening, mobilization and apheresis, HGI-002 manufacturing, myeloablative conditioning, HGI-002 infusion, and a 24-month follow-up (supplemental Figure 1). HGI-002 was administered as a single IV infusion, with detailed procedures provided in the supplemental Methods. Plerixafor and recombinant human granulocyte colony-stimulating factor were given to the patient, followed by 3 days of cell apheresis after confirming the patient’s eligibility (supplemental Figure 2). A small number of CD34+ cells (2 × 106 CD34+ cells per kg) were cryopreserved as backup, whereas the remaining portion was used to manufacture HGI-002. After HGI-002 passed quality control, the patient was admitted to the transplant unit for myeloablative conditioning (supplemental Figure 3), followed by HGI-002 infusion. We monitored the patient’s complete blood count daily to evaluate the engraftment of HSPCs during her hospitalization. When she met the neutrophil and platelet engraftment criteria and her clinical status became stable, she was discharged from the transplant unit.

Outcome assessment

The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the safety of infusing HGI-002 after myeloablative conditioning. Safety assessments included (1) neutrophil and platelet engraftment, (2) incidence and severity of adverse events (AEs), graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, (3) transplant-related mortality or disability within 100 days after infusion, (4) detection of replication-competent lentivirus (RCL), and (5) clonal dominance assessed by viral integration sites analysis.

The secondary objective focused on evaluating therapeutic efficacy, assessed by (1) changes in total Hb and Hb subtype composition; (2) reduction in transfusion requirements; (3) α/β-globin chain balance; (4) persistence of vector copy number (VCN) in the blood and bone marrow; and (5) long-term hematopoietic recovery, immune reconstitution, and iron status.

Results

Patient characteristics and product manufacturing

The girl, aged 14 years, was enrolled in this study on 13 February 2023. She was diagnosed with HbH-CS disease (genotype:--SEA/αCSα, identified via gap–polymerase chain reaction and reverse dot blot hybridization) at the age of 8 years. In her early childhood, her anemia was mild, but it gradually worsened over time, and she eventually became transfusion dependent. She needed to receive ∼114 mL/kg per year of leukocyte-depleted RBCs every month before enrollment. Her screening Hb level was 107 g/L (after RBC transfusion), with HbH constituting ∼7% of her total Hb. Mild iron overload was observed (serum ferritin, 856.6 ng/mL), but there was no cardiac involvement and only mild hepatic deposition. She had hepatosplenomegaly, which stemmed from her underlying condition. No viral infection or autoimmune disease was detected, and her organ function test results were normal. Successful hematopoietic reconstitution

The patient achieved successful hematopoietic reconstitution after HGI-002 infusion, which is the key primary end point of this study. Neutrophil engraftment (defined as an absolute neutrophil count of ≥0.5 × 109/L for 3 consecutive days) was observed on day 19 after infusion (Figure 1A). Platelet engraftment (defined as a platelet count of ≥20 × 109/L for 7 consecutive days without transfusion support) occurred earlier than we had expected, on day 17 (Figure 1B). During hospitalization, the patient received only 3 RBC transfusions, with the last unit given on day 17. After that, her Hb levels increased steadily, exceeding the transfusion freedom threshold (>90 g/L) on day 25 and remaining stable until her discharge on day 28 (Figure 1C). Safety

We documented 28 AEs throughout the study, as detailed in Table 2. One grade 4 AE (hypokalemia) occurred during the HSPC mobilization phase and was resolved with IV potassium supplementation. Three mild AEs (rash, diarrhea, and headache) occurred between mobilization and preconditioning, all resolving spontaneously. Most AEs (15/28 [53.6%]) occurred during the hospitalization period after conditioning, and these were primarily associated with busulfan toxicity. Among these, 3 grade ≥3 AEs presented as transient myelosuppression, reflected by decreased white blood cell, neutrophil, and platelet counts. During the posttransplant follow-up, 9 AEs were reported. These included persistent cytopenias as well as mild symptoms such as pyrexia, localized and generalized pruritus, and menstrual irregularities. Although most of these events were considered attributable to the conditioning regimen, 2 delayed AEs (tenosynovitis and upper respiratory tract infection) occurring >6 months after infusion were deemed unrelated to the study treatment. All AEs resolved either spontaneously or with standard supportive care, except for menstrual irregularities, which were still under active clinical management at the time of reporting.

There were 2 severe AEs recorded during the follow-up. The first was a pulmonary infection that occurred 21 months after infusion, which required hospitalization but resolved completely. It was judged unrelated to gene therapy. The second was ovarian failure, which was diagnosed 12 months after infusion and linked to busulfan exposure. Healthy ovarian function had not yet returned to normal at the time of reporting.

Importantly, none of the AEs or severe AEs were attributable to HGI-002 infusion. All events were managed in accordance with the institution’s standard protocols and resolved successfully, except for the anticipated busulfan-related effects on the reproductive system (menstrual irregularities and ovarian failure). The patient had sustained multilineage hematopoietic reconstitution by 24 months after HGI-002 infusion. Subpopulations of lymphocytes, particularly B cells, CD8+ T cells, and natural killer cells, were maintained within the normal reference ranges (Figure 2A-B). HGI-002 product consists of HSPCs transduced with the Lenti-HBA lentiviral vector, as illustrated in supplemental Figure 4. Insertional mutagenesis and RCL are 2 potential risks associated with lentiviral vectors. We implemented a rigorous monitoring strategy. Throughout the patient’s 24-month follow-up, RCL was not detected at any point (data not shown). Integration site analysis revealed that no integration sites contributed to >10% of total integrations in the patient’s peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) or bone marrow mononuclear cells (BMMCs), with no evidence of clonal dominance during the 24-month follow-up period (Figure 2E). Additionally, bone marrow aspirate smears at 24 months after infusion showed normal hematopoiesis and no signs of hyperplasia or marrow failure (Figure 2F).

Efficacy

Throughout the entire 24-month follow-up period, the patient only received 2 RBC infusions, at month 6 and month 9 (a pulmonary infection was linked to the Hb decline at month 9). The patient’s Hb levels increased after month 9 and essentially stabilized at 90 g/L by month 15, during which time she continued to be transfusion-free (Figure 3A). Hb component analysis showed that HbA persistently constituted >85% of total Hb, indicating that the transduced cells effectively restored HbA synthesis and played a central role in maintaining long-term transfusion freedom status (Figure 3B). Additionally, the elevated α/β-globin ratio confirmed that the functional expression of the introduced α-globin gene compensated for the patient’s innate α-globin deficiency (Figure 3C). VCN analysis confirmed the persistence of transgenic integration, with VCN levels of ∼0.1 per cell in BMMCs and PBMCs at month 24. Although iron chelation therapy had been completely discontinued for 2 years, the progression of liver iron accumulation was minimal, only from mild to borderline moderate (liver magnetic resonance imaging T2∗: 3.81 ms at baseline to 2.6 ms at month 24). This rate of progression was notably slower than that typical in untreated patients. Cardiac iron overload was absent across all follow-up time points (cardiac magnetic resonance imaging T2∗ of >20 ms at all time points; Figure 3E), accompanied by a significant reduction in serum ferritin (from 856.6 ng/mL at baseline to 569.1 ng/mL at month 24; Figure 3F). Longitudinal anthropometric data confirmed preservation of normal growth and development after conditioning (Figure 3G).

Discussion

This case report describes, to our knowledge, the first-in-human clinical application of lentiviral vector–mediated α-globin gene therapy for transfusion-dependent α-thalassemia. The patient, aged 14 years, with HbH-CS disease (--SEA/αCSα), became free of transfusions within 4 weeks after receiving the HGI-002 injection, with durable hematologic correction maintained over 24 months.

The data provide proof of concept that reexpression of α-globin can effectively counteract the deleterious effects of the CS point mutation, which has long been recognized as a driver of instability and severity in HbH disease.5 The early and sustained rise in Hb, together with HbA consistently exceeding 85% of total Hb, reflects robust functional expression of the introduced α-globin gene. A further notable outcome was the maintenance of Hb concentrations at ∼90 g/L in the patient, accompanied by preserved growth and development, as well as a reduction in serum ferritin levels. These clinical experiences are consistent with previous observations that sustaining a baseline Hb level of 9 to 10 g/dL improves the long-term outcomes in patients with transfusion-dependent thalassemia,20 underscoring the clinical significance of even moderate Hb restoration in the HbH population. Importantly, these effects were achieved at relatively low VCN (∼0.1 per cell in PBMCs), whereas transfusion-free β-thalassemia gene therapy is generally associated with PBMC VCN values exceeding ∼0.75.21 This suggests that HbH-CS, and potentially other HbH variants, may benefit from gene therapy even when engraftment and chimerism levels are modest. Therefore, the use of reduced-intensity or even nonmyeloablative conditioning regimens in future α-thalassemia gene therapy may provide a more favorable risk–benefit balance for patients with HbH22, 23, 24 while still enabling sufficient levels of gene-corrected hematopoiesis to achieve transfusion freedom. In parallel, ongoing advances in vector design, transduction efficiency, and manufacturing processes are expected to further enhance the efficacy and outcomes of the gene therapy for α-thalassemia.25

Safety outcomes were favorable. Most of the observed toxicities were attributable to busulfan conditioning, including transient cytopenias, menstrual irregularity, and ovarian failure. Immune reconstitution was achieved, and no AEs were directly attributed to the gene therapy product. Moreover, there was no evidence of RCL, clonal dominance, or insertional mutagenesis.

Although the outcomes are encouraging, this report only includes 1 patient. To confirm long-term safety, durability of transgene expression, and overall clinical benefit, more comprehensive studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up are necessary.

In conclusion, this case provides preliminary evidence of the therapeutic benefit of lentiviral α-globin gene therapy: HGI-002 can help patients with transfusion-dependent HbH-CS achieve favorable safety outcomes and sustained transfusion freedom. Interestingly, even modest gene correction efficacy (33.3% transduction efficiency of BMMCs) and low VCN (∼0.1 in BMMCs and PBMCs) can sustain Hb levels of >90 g/L, supporting the investigation of reduced-intensity conditioning approaches in α-thalassemia gene therapy. For patients without suitable hematopoietic stem cell transplantation donors, this therapy may be an effective treatment option. The aforementioned results strongly support expanding the scale of clinical trials to further validate the long-term safety, efficacy, and persistence of this therapy in diverse patient populations with HbH, despite the inherent limitations of single-case studies.
