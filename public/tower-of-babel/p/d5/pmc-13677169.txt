Effects of laminin-treated membrane culture inserts on the growth of bovine oocyte–granulosa cell complexes collected from early antral follicles
Authors: Yuji HIRAO, Satoshi AKAGI
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Growing bovine oocytes represent a potential source of embryos, but this has not been adopted in practice. Although several offspring were produced following a two-week culture period, further optimization of the culture system is required. In vivo, the outermost granulosa cell layer is in direct contact with the basement membrane (BM); however, this interaction is absent in cultured oocyte–granulosa cell complexes (OGCs). Since the BM is known to have regulatory roles, this study examined the effects of BM components on oocyte growth by treating membrane inserts with laminin, a major BM constituent, or with the laminin-containing protein mixture Matrigel®. OGCs containing growing oocytes (90–106 μm in diameter) were cultured from Day 0 to Day 14 on plain membranes or on membranes with spots of Matrigel or laminin. A modified minimum essential medium α supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum, 4% polyvinylpyrrolidone (average molecular weight, 360,000), steroid hormones, and 3 mM hypoxanthine was used. Granulosa cell migration was more pronounced in the treated spots, particularly in the Matrigel-treated group where migration extended across the entire treated area. Oocyte volume increased to 147% in the control group, 158% in the Matrigel group, and 160% in the laminin group, with Day 1 values normalized to 100%. Oocytes cultured on laminin-treated membranes were significantly larger than those in the control group (P < 0.05). Following in vitro fertilization, approximately 5% of the oocytes in each group developed to the blastocyst stage. Overall, the interaction between granulosa cells and laminin-treated membranes positively influenced the growth of bovine oocytes in vitro.

In modern cattle production, immature oocytes currently used for in vitro embryo production are retrieved from the ovary at full size and with meiotic competence [1, 2]. Growing oocytes, most of which degenerate before completing growth in vivo, represent a potential alternative source of embryos, but are not used in practice. To date, viable bovine offspring have been produced from in vitro–grown (IVG) oocytes, following a two-week culture period that is limited to the latter half of the oocyte growth phase [3,4,5,6]. During this period, oocytes increase in diameter from about 100 µm—corresponding to approximately 50% of their maximum volume—to 120–125 µm. When cultured as oocyte–granulosa cell complexes (OGCs), a subset of these oocytes acquires competence for meiotic maturation, fertilization, and subsequent embryonic development [4]. Nevertheless, most IVG oocytes grow slower than their in vivo counterparts [7], indicating that existing culture systems remain suboptimal.

Among the components of oocyte growth culture systems, the selection and modification of culture substrates to which OGCs attach and proliferate have not been adequately examined. Membrane inserts, which are commonly used in two-dimensional cell and tissue cultures, offer advantages by allowing nutrients and oxygen to be supplied from below while facilitating the removal of metabolic waste. When cultured on membrane inserts, OGCs gradually form dome-like structures of granulosa cells, with the oocyte positioned along the inner wall [8], partially mimicking the intrafollicular environment. However, in vivo, the outermost layer of the granulosa cells is in direct contact with the basement membrane (BM), a specialized extracellular matrix (ECM), whereas this interaction is absent in the in vitro OGC system.

The BM has a sheet-like structure composed primarily of laminin and collagen IV, with networks of these core components stabilized by additional ECM proteins, including nidogen, perlecan, and agrin [9, 10]. Although the BM was previously regarded mainly as a structural scaffold that segregated polarized cells from the underlying mesenchyme, it is now increasingly recognized as a dynamic regulator of tissue development and maintenance through mechanical and chemical signaling [11]. Signal transduction is mediated by matrix-bound integrins, dystroglycans, and receptor kinases, whose activities are modulated by the mechanical properties of the matrix [12]. The lack or insufficiency of these regulatory interactions in OGC cultures may partly account for the suboptimal growth of oocytes in vitro. Therefore, we treated the membrane inserts with laminin or with the laminin-containing ECM protein mixture Matrigel®, both derived from mouse Engelbreth–Holm–Swarm (EHS) sarcoma and examined their effects on the growth of bovine oocytes and OGCs.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals

Unless otherwise specified, all the chemicals were purchased from Nacalai Tesque (Kyoto, Japan). Concentrations of liquid supplements in the culture medium are expressed as volume/volume percentages.

Rearrangement of this equation yields y = 0.199x / (131 − x), where y represents follicular diameter (mm) and x denotes oocyte diameter (μm). Treatments of culture membrane inserts with basement membrane components

Culture was performed using Millicell® inserts (30-mm diameter, 0.4-μm pore size; product no. PICMORG50; EMD Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). As experimental treatments, a Matrigel® matrix solution (Corning® no. 354234; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and a laminin solution (0.5 mg/ml, derived from mouse EHS tumor; product no. 120-05751; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan) were used for spot coating on the membrane surface. Before use, the Matrigel matrix solution was diluted twofold in phosphate-buffered saline supplemented with 0.4% bovine serum albumin (PBS–BSA). The laminin solution was diluted in PBS–BSA to final concentrations of 0.025, 0.10, or 0.25 mg/ml, depending on the experimental condition. The series of experiments for comparison with Matrigel was carried out using a laminin concentration of 0.25 mg/ml. Aliquots (3 μl) of either the Matrigel or laminin solution were applied to the designated spots, each forming a circular region of approximately 13 mm2, and were allowed to dry. The outlines of all the spots, including the control regions, were lightly premarked with a flame-sterilized pencil tip to facilitate positional identification by adjusting the reflector of a stereomicroscope. Nine spots (three control, three Matrigel, and three laminin) were arranged on each insert, as shown in Fig. 1C, with representative examples presented in Figs. 1D–1F. An enlarged view of the nine-spot layout is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. Millicell® inserts were placed in Petri dishes (Falcon® no. 351007; Becton Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). Culture medium was added at volumes of 0.7 ml to the upper (membrane) compartment and 3.8 ml to the lower (dish) compartment. Two or three OGCs were placed in each spot, ensuring that the number of OGCs was as equal as possible across the three experimental groups.

In vitro growth of oocytes

Culture was initiated on Day 0 and continued through Day 14. Images of oocytes covered by granulosa cells were captured on Days 1 and 14, using a stereo dissecting microscope (M205C; Leica Microsystems Inc., Deerfield, IL, USA) and a digital camera (MC170 HD; Leica Microsystems Inc.) at 160 × magnification. The image was displayed on a computer monitor, resulting in a total magnification of 1,000 × for analysis. Given that magnification ratio, 400 μm on a stage micrometer was the optimal length to use as a reference (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Oocyte radius was calculated as one-fourth of the sum of the diameters perpendicular intersecting oocytes, rounded to the nearest 0.1 μm, and was used to calculate oocyte volume (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Based on morphology and oocyte diameter measured on Day 1, oocytes outside the target diameter range (90–106 µm) or showing degenerative features were excluded from subsequent examinations. Images of OGCs were captured using the same equipment used for oocytes, but at 7.8 × magnification, on Days 1 and 14, and when necessary, also on Day 7.

Oocyte growth was supported using a modified MEMα (32571101; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Logan, UT, USA). The medium was supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS; HyCloneTM no. SH30070.03; Thermo Fisher Scientific), 40 mg/ml polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP; K-90; product no. 28354-04), 0.08 mg/ml kanamycin sulfate, 10 mM HEPES, 50 ng/ml androstenedione (Sigma-Aldrich), 50 ng/ml estradiol 17β (Sigma-Aldrich), 10 ng/ml activin A (human recombinant; product no. 014-23961; FUJIFILM Wako), and 3 mM hypoxanthine (Sigma-Aldrich). The final serine concentration was adjusted to 1 mM by adding 0.76 mM serine to the basal medium.

Cultures were maintained under an atmosphere of 5% O2, 5% CO2, and 90% N2 for the first 3 days, after which the gas phase was changed to 5% CO2 in air from Day 3 to Day 14 [5, 14]. Half of the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium on Days 3, 6, 9, and 12. At the end of the 14-day culture period, OGCs were dissociated using a fine pipette to retrieve oocytes. Oocytes were considered viable if they showed no signs of degeneration, such as cytoplasmic deterioration or membrane disruption, and if cumulus cells covered at least half of the oocyte surface. All cultures were maintained at 38.5°C in incubators.

In vitro maturation of oocytes

Oocyte maturation was induced using a two-step protocol consisting of an initial three-hour phase followed by a 21-h phase. In the first step, oocytes were cultured in the same medium used for oocyte growth, supplemented with 1 mM dibutyryl cAMP (product no. D0260; Sigma-Aldrich), 50 ng/ml epidermal growth factor (EGF; human recombinant; product no. 01-102, Merck Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA), and 5 IU human chorionic gonadotropin (Gonatrophin®; Aska Pharmaceutical Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). For the second step, oocytes were transferred to a modified MEMα with a composition similar to that used for oocyte growth, except that PVP, steroid hormones, activin, and hypoxanthine, were omitted. This maturation medium was supplemented with 0.1 IU/ml FSH (Follistim®; Merck Serono, Rahway, NJ, USA), 1 ng/ml EGF, and 10 ng/ml neurotensin (product no. N6383; Sigma-Aldrich) [15] to promote oocyte maturation.

In vitro fertilization and embryo culture

Frozen semen straws were thawed at 38°C and washed twice by centrifugation in a modified BO solution (IVF100; Research Institute for the Functional Peptides Corporation, Yamagata, Japan). Before insemination, mature oocytes were washed three times in BO solution and transferred to 50-μl aliquots, to which sperm suspension was added to achieve a final volume of 100 µl and a sperm concentration of 5 × 106 cells/ml. Oocytes and sperm were co-incubated for 6 h under 5% CO2 in air. Following insemination, the oocytes were completely denuded by gentle aspiration using a small-bore pipette.

Presumptive zygotes were then cultured in BO-IVC medium (IVF Bioscience, Cornwall, UK) using well-of-the-well (WOW) culture dishes (Embryo Grid; Astec Corporation, Fukuoka, Japan). The central well of each dish contained 125 μl of BO-IVC medium overlaid with 5 ml of paraffin oil. Embryonic development to the blastocyst stage was assessed 8 days after in vitro fertilization (IVF).

Measurement of the areas occupied by migrating granulosa cells

The extent of granulosa cell migration outside the granulosa cell domes on Day 14 was assessed using images displayed on a computer monitor at 320 × final magnification. The outlines of the domes and granulosa cell-occupied areas were traced manually using ImageJ (version 1.51m9; NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA). For each spot, the area of the dome(s) was subtracted from the entire area covered by granulosa cells, and the resulting value was divided by the number of live oocytes recovered after 14 days of culture to compare the areas on a per-oocyte basis.

Data presentation and statistical analysis

Percentage data are presented as mean values derived from independent experimental replicates, with variability between experiments expressed as the standard error of the mean (SEM). Variability in oocyte volume increase is reported as SEM for each experimental group rather than across experiments. Percentage data (0–100% range) were subjected to arcsine transformation before statistical analysis. Comparisons between two groups were conducted using Welch’s t-test. Multiple comparisons of means were performed primarily using Tukey’s honestly significant difference [HSD] test, except for the assessment of the concentration-dependent effects of the laminin solution, for which Dunnett’s test was used, with the 0 mg/ml group as the control. All statistical analyses were performed using EZR (version 1.68; Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Tochigi, Japan) [16], which is a graphical user interface for R (version 4.5.2; The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that the interactions between granulosa cells and the culture substrate influence bovine oocyte growth, even when the oocyte is separated from the substrate by multiple granulosa cell layers. The control and treatment groups were cultured together on the same membrane insert to minimize the variability arising from global changes in the culture medium caused by cellular activity, although localized microenvironmental effects cannot be excluded. Furthermore, within the scope of this experiment, the density of OGCs per spot did not affect oocyte growth. In contrast to laminin treatment, in which granulosa cell migration was largely restricted to the vicinity of granulosa cell domes, Matrigel treatment promoted granulosa cell proliferation and migration across the entire treated area (Figs. 1E and 2H). Matrigel may be sufficiently potent to induce granulosa cell adhesion and proliferation independently of oocyte-derived regulation, whereas laminin may act in a manner that depends more strongly on the granulosa cell responses influenced by oocytes. Alternatively, ECM components in Matrigel may be more robustly stabilized and persist for longer than laminin alone.

Commercially available Matrigel® matrix is extracted from mouse EHS sarcoma and consists of approximately 60% laminin, 30% collagen IV, and 8% entactin, entactin contributing to the structural organization of laminin and collagen IV networks, according to the manufacturer’s frequently asked questions (FAQ) pages [17], which was accessed on January 17, 2026. The laminin used in this study was also derived from a mouse EHS tumor and corresponds to laminin-111 (α1β1γ1), the first laminin isoform to be identified [18] among the 16 laminin isoforms currently known in mammals [19]. Accordingly, the present comparison should not be interpreted as a direct opposition of laminin and Matrigel. In addition to its cooperative role with laminin, collagen IV alone supports normal cell attachment, as demonstrated in studies using anchorage-dependent cell lines [20]. Moreover, Matrigel matrix contains heparan sulfate proteoglycans as well as growth factors, including transforming growth factor and insulin-like growth factor [17]. Therefore, the extensive granulosa cell migration observed exclusively in the Matrigel-treated membranes is likely attributable to the combined effects of collagen IV, growth factors, or both.

In this study, both the Matrigel matrix and the laminin solution were applied to membrane inserts and allowed to dry. Although several methods exist for utilizing ECM components, some of which require avoiding dehydration, a spot-coating approach was adopted in this study. Purified laminins are uniquely capable of self-polymerization and of assembling into sheet-like ECM [21, 22]. A potential concern was that drying the membrane might obscure concentration-dependent effects. However, laminin exhibited a concentration-dependent activity: a concentration of 0.25 mg/ml promoted granulosa cell migration from the onset of culture, whereas this effect was attenuated at 0.1 mg/ml or lower.

Although dome formation occurred in all three experimental groups, oocytes cultured on control spots showed reduced growth. As the maximum volume increase ratios were comparable between the control and treatment groups, Matrigel and laminin treatments likely did not further enhance the growth of oocytes that were already progressing normally. Rather, the control group likely contained a higher proportion of oocytes whose growth was impeded during culture. This difference may be attributed to subtle variations in dome structure. In the control condition, the combination of a plain membrane and culture medium containing FBS—specifically fibronectin and vitronectin present in FBS—allowed cell adhesion but did not adequately flatten the three-dimensional structure of the OGCs (Fig. 2A). This configuration differs from that observed on standard plastic culture dishes, where granulosa cells typically spread extensively, as observed in Matrigel- and laminin-treated membranes. Over time, the OGCs in the control group adopted a hollow hemisphere structure, resembling an “igloo without an entrance,” which may be conceptualized as a spheroid attached to the membrane at its base. Studies of invitro tumor models have shown that large spheroids are prone to hypoxia in their central regions [23, 24]. Although the domes observed here contained large fluid-filled cavities, their outer diameters exceeded 500 μm, a size at which hypoxia becomes a concern in tumor spheroids [23]. Our previous study [14] showed that continuous hypoxic conditions over two-weeks in vitro suppress bovine oocyte growth; therefore, similar hypoxic conditions may have arisen within some domes in the control group. In contrast, granulosa cells on Matrigel- or laminin-treated membranes spread initially and then gradually bulged upward through multilayering to form domes, suggesting that these structures are more accurately described as layered cellular assemblies than true spheroids.

Laminin, together with other ECM components, regulates cell proliferation and migration through integrins expressed on the surface of contacting cells [25]. However, in the present study, multiple layers of granulosa cells intervened between the oocyte and ECM-treated substrate. The true nature of the signals by which Matrigel or laminin treatment exerted effects across these multiple granulosa cell layers remains unclear. In a similar study, Munakata et al. [26] examined the effect of substrate stiffness on growing porcine oocytes using polyacrylamide gels and demonstrated a positive influence on oocyte diameter, developmental competence, and granulosa cell proliferation. Transcriptomic analysis of granulosa cells in that study revealed upregulation of genes associated with mechanosensing.

In terms of embryonic development, neither Matrigel nor laminin treatment conferred an advantage over the control. Given the complexity of the processes spanning oocyte maturation, fertilization, and early embryonic development, potential differences in the developmental competence of IVG oocytes may not have been fully manifested under the conditions tested. Alternatively, as discussed above, these treatments may not further enhance the growth or competence of oocytes that are already progressing normally, and oocytes capable of supporting embryonic development may represent a subset of inherently high-quality oocytes. Further optimization of culture conditions for oocyte maturation, in vitro fertilization, and embryo development will therefore be required, along with strategies to enhance oocyte growth and competence across a broader oocyte population through the use of appropriate culture substrates.
