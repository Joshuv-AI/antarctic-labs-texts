Bridging heterogeneous and homogeneous catalysis in carbon dioxide valorization
Authors: Sergio Posada-Pérez, Anna Vidal-López, Aleix Comas-Vives, Albert Poater
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The valorization of carbon dioxide (CO2) into fuels and chemicals represents both a scientific challenge and an opportunity for sustainable energy transition. This review bridges heterogeneous and homogeneous approaches, highlighting how molecular precision and materials robustness can converge to address the intrinsic stability of CO2 and the complexity of its multi-electron transformations. Advances in single-atom catalysts, MXenes, and carbon nitrides demonstrate how structural control at the atomic scale can enhance activity and selectivity, while homogeneous complexes continue to provide mechanistic insights and tunable active sites. In parallel, machine learning (ML) is emerging as a transformative tool to accelerate catalyst discovery, identify descriptors, and guide rational design; however, its effectiveness depends critically on realistic datasets and experimental validation. We argue that future progress will rely on integrative strategies: combining computation, experiment, and automation within materials acceleration platforms. By adopting this integrative vision, CO2 can evolve from an environmental burden to a versatile feedstock for next-generation sustainable fuels and chemicals.

The Greenhouse Effect and CO₂

The link between atmospheric carbon dioxide (CO2) and its severe consequences for Earth is one of the most pressing issues in modern environmental science. As a key greenhouse gas, CO2 plays a fundamental role in global climate regulation, and in recent years, scientific evidence has directly associated it with global warming and climate change1. Additionally, increased atmospheric CO2 has been shown to influence climate patterns, contribute to more frequent extreme weather events, and pose challenges to ecosystems2–4.

Future projections suggest that if CO2 emissions continue at the current rate, global temperatures could rise by 3–4 °C by the end of the century. Climate models also indicate that rising temperatures will bring more extreme weather, sea level rise, and disruptions to agricultural systems5. Efforts to mitigate the impact of increasing CO2 levels are crucial. The 2024 Climate Summit (COP) confirms that global warming has already exceeded 1.5 °C6 and highlights the need for government action to curb further temperature increases. Achieving climate goals will require substantial reductions in CO2 emissions7, attainable through a shift toward renewable energy sources, improved energy efficiency, and the deployment of carbon capture and storage (CCS) technologies. However, large-scale adoption of CCS remains a costly and technological challenge. CCS technologies refer to processes designed at different scales to capture CO2 originating from combustion and gasification systems5. In this way, CCS is a potential tool to continue using fossil fuel-based energy sources while preventing CO2 emissions into the atmosphere6. However, CCS alone is no longer sufficient, and the concept has evolved into carbon capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS), which treats CO2 as a raw material to produce other high-value chemicals for industry6. The focus on utilization in CCUS represents an important step forward in developing sustainable energy solutions.

The Molecule

CO2 is a stable molecule, with a standard enthalpy of formation (ΔHf) of –393.5 kJ·mol⁻¹. However, it can be reactive at specific sites provided by catalytic systems, and activation is key to its subsequent chemical transformation8. CO2’s reactivity is related to structural changes such as bending of the O–C–O angle (from 180°), elongating a C–O bond, and charge polarization across the C and O atoms9. Bending the neutral molecule from its linear equilibrium geometry modifies the shape and energy of its molecular orbitals. The C–O bond lengths remain nearly constant as the angle decreases (to ~157°)10. The 2πu orbital (LUMO) energy drops, increasing the molecule’s electrophilicity (Fig. 1)9. When isolated and bent, CO2 carries a positive Mulliken charge on carbon ( + 0.368 e) and negative charges on oxygen (–0.184 e)11. Electron transfer to CO2 generates the radical anion (CO2•⁻), a process facilitated by the lowered LUMO12. The equilibrium geometry of CO2•⁻ has an O–C–O angle of ~135°, and its SOMO (singly occupied molecular orbital) corresponds in energy to the LUMO of neutral, bent CO29. The C–O bonds lengthen by ~0.08 Å, clearly indicating bond weakening9. This species is a key intermediate in many CO2 transformation reactions12. In this state, carbon becomes nucleophilic (–0.133 e)11 and can promote dissociation of one C–O bond, forming adsorbed CO and O atoms. Formate (HCOO-) is one of the most common intermediates in both catalytic regimes. Its anionic and/or radical forms are thought to form when a hydride or hydrogen atom attacks the carbon of CO213,14. Formate anion formation stabilizes the HOMO relative to the SOMO of CO2•⁻9. In contrast, the formate radical exhibits different orbital characteristics and low-energy LUMO/SOMO, making it highly electrophilic.

Reactivity and valorization of CO2: the catalysts

The thermodynamic stability of CO2 makes its transformation a chief challenge: each desirable product requires the concerted transfer of multiple electrons and protons. Using CO2 as a feedstock, various chemicals can be obtained, such as syngas, carbon monoxide, methanol, methane, hydrocarbons (alkanes and aromatics), alcohols, and carbamates via catalytic processes (thermocatalysis15, electrocatalysis16, or photocatalysis17) under favorable conditions. The value-added products and the catalytic strategies needed to obtain them depend on whether the catalyst operates in a molecular (homogeneous) or solid (heterogeneous) environment. This section summarizes the main valorization routes and examines how homogeneous and heterogeneous catalysts prioritize different products, highlighting opportunities and limitations for each (illustrated in Fig. 2a–d). Whether operating in homogeneous or heterogeneous regimes, CO2 hydrogenation proceeds through distinct intermediates depending on the reaction pathway. While formate is a key intermediate in many catalytic processes19, methanol formation can also occur via CO as a key intermediate, particularly in electrochemical systems, but also in thermally promoted ones20–22. According to the methanol economy concept introduced by Olah23, methanol could play an indispensable role in the future, and direct CO2 hydrogenation to methanol is one of the most promising regenerative routes.

A clear distinction should be made between thermal and electrocatalytic CO2 conversion, as the driving forces and reaction environments differ fundamentally and directly impact product selectivity. In thermocatalysis, temperature and pressure control the chemical potential of CO2 and H2 (effectively determining surface coverages), and product distributions are largely governed by thermodynamic equilibria and surface residence times. As a result, thermal CO2 hydrogenation commonly yields CO via the reverse water–gas shift reaction, as well as methanol, methane, or longer hydrocarbons through Fischer–Tropsch–type pathways, particularly under high-pressure conditions. In contrast, electrocatalytic CO2 reduction is driven by an applied potential that controls the chemical potential of proton–electron pairs, enabling proton-coupled electron transfer steps at the solid–liquid interface and lifting thermodynamic equilibrium constraints. Consequently, electrocatalysis typically favors products such as CO or formate at low overpotentials, while multicarbon products (e.g., ethylene or ethanol) can be accessed on copper-based catalysts at more negative potentials. Notably, even identical catalytic materials, such as Cu or Ni-based systems, may yield methanol or hydrocarbons under thermal conditions but predominantly produce CO or C2 products under electrochemical operation. These differences arise from distinct activation modes, intermediate stabilization, and competition with hydrogen evolution, highlighting the need to assess catalytic performance within the appropriate energetic and mechanistic framework24.

From the perspective of homogeneous catalysis, significant advances have been reported in CO2 hydrogenation, but notable challenges still remain. Early molecular systems exhibited low yields, poor selectivity, and needed high operating temperatures, providing little benefit over heterogeneous processes25. Furthermore, these molecular catalysts suffer from limited durability and recyclability: many degrade under sustained electrolysis and separating them from products is difficult. In practice, they rarely venture beyond C1 products; few systems produce methanol or C2+ species, and scaling up homogeneous catalysts is hampered by the cost of ligands, the scarcity of certain metals, and challenges in continuous operation26.

Heterogeneous systems, in contrast, are widely used in industry. The chemical nature of CO2 implies that its activation and conversion on catalytic surfaces involve bending and electron/hydrogen transfer steps. On solid surfaces, the CO2 activation by bending the linear O–C–O molecule is enabled by transferring electrons or reacting with hydrogen atoms that weaken its C = O bonds; this typically requires elevated temperatures and pressures. For instance, state-of-the-art Cu/ZnO/Al2O3 methanol-synthesis catalysts operate at 220–300 °C and several megapascal H2/CO2 mixtures, yet even under these harsh conditions, typical conversions remain moderate ( ~ 5–20%) and methanol selectivity seldom exceeds 60–70%. Only when pressures are raised to ≈36 MPa do conversions approach ≈95% and selectivity ≈98%, albeit at a significant energy cost27–29. In2O3-based systems also illustrate the trade-off: pure In2O3 yields ≈7% CO2 conversion and 40% methanol selectivity at 330 °C/5 MPa, whereas adding Pd increases conversion above 20% at 300 °C but only to 99% selectivity when ZrO2 is co-supported; even then, conversion drops to ≈5%. In electrochemical activation, CO2 is reduced by electron transfer, forming reactive radical species30. The electrochemical reduction of CO2 (CO2RR, see Fig. 2c, d) has received considerable attention, especially when coupled with electricity from renewable sources31. Thus, CO2RR is a promising approach for sustainable energy storage and conversion via low-temperature redox cycles. However, current catalysts remain inefficient, with slow reaction rates and poor selectivity toward reduced carbon products. Thermodynamically, the reduction potentials for major intermediates are within ±0.2 V of the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER)32, making HER a major side reaction. High CO2 activation barriers and multi-electron transfer further limit performance33–35.

Fixation of homogeneous catalysts

The traditional divide between homogeneous and heterogeneous catalysis reflects a trade-off between molecular precision and practical robustness, although the categorization may be subtle. A compelling example is the elucidation of the CO2 fixation mechanism via functionalization of epoxides into cyclic carbonates using NbCl536, where two units of this catalyst were mandatory, and which led to testing the findings in a more productive heterogeneous system37. This confirmed the occurrence of dual-metal catalysis, as the observed differences in catalytic efficiency were attributed to an unprecedented cooperative effect between two neighboring Nb centers on the surface. In contrast, simply modifying the niobium-based catalyst by substituting chloride ligands with ethyl groups eliminated dual-metal catalysis. This study demonstrated that dual metal catalysis is a rare and valuable phenomenon in chemical reactivity, a “golden nugget” not easily achieved.

Focusing on CO2 fixation and conversion, metal–salen complexes are among the most effective for catalyzing the transformation of CO2 and epoxides into cyclic carbonates. These tetradentate ligands form stable complexes with various metals38, often with octahedral geometry (Fig. 3a). Their chirality, ligand environment, and metal oxidation state allow for asymmetric induction and reaction control. Substituents on the salicyl part (e.g., electron-donating groups like –OMe) can tune electronic and steric effects. Figure 3a outlines five main synthesis routes. These complexes are useful in asymmetric catalysis, materials chemistry, and organic synthesis, offering tailored performance through ligand modification and coordination geometry. Particularly, they succeed in the selective cycloaddition of CO2 to epoxides to form cyclic carbonates, a valuable transformation that does not involve CO2 reduction. If the epoxides are derived from renewable resources, the process becomes sustainable. No universal catalyst exists for all types of epoxides, and thus, catalyst design often requires adjustments of the metal center, ligand structure, or initiator based on the substrate. Many metal-based and metal-free catalysts have been reported for this transformation39. The resulting cyclic carbonates have broad applications, such as green solvents, electrolytes for Li-ion batteries40, or intermediates in fine chemicals and polymer synthesis41. Due to their economic and industrial value, more efficient and cost-effective synthetic methods are highly desired. Mechanistically (Fig. 3b), these catalysts often feature Lewis acidic sites that coordinate with the epoxide oxygen, activating the ring for nucleophilic attack. This is followed by CO2 insertion and intramolecular ring closure to form the final product42. Reaction conditions are generally mild, typically operating at 25 °C–100 °C and 1–20 bar of CO243. There is growing interest in low-cost, environmentally friendly alternatives operating under mild conditions, including organocatalysts, dendrimers, quaternary ammonium/phosphonium salts44,45, and metal–salen complexes46. An exciting example of these dual roles of homogeneous-heterogeneous catalysts is the mixture between inexpensive alkali and alkaline-earth metal halides and immobilized pyridines in resins. These salts alone are poor catalysts under ambient pressure, but when paired with highly nucleophilic aminopyridine moieties, a cooperative “two-site” mechanism emerges: the pyridine activates and opens the epoxide ring, while the metal halide promotes CO2 insertion and ring closure47. Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations revealed very low energy barriers for ring-opening and closure when both components are present, rationalizing the high activity of the dual system48. Regarding pure homogeneous systems, a new generation can perform in liquid phase at 80 °C–150 °C with high selectivity, since well-designed ligands suppress side reactions and allow detailed mechanistic control. Transition-metal complexes epitomize molecular precision. Their ligands create well-defined coordination spheres that can be tuned electronically; as a result, many achieve high Faradaic efficiencies for CO or formate at low overpotentials49. Some systems achieve impressive efficiency: an Fe⁰ porphyrin complex converts CO2 to CO with nearly 100% Faradaic yield and high turnover frequency50, while a copper complex can reversibly interconvert CO2 and CO, though different complexes trade off between reduction and oxidation rates and depend strongly on ligand environment and solvent51. Nevertheless, most homogeneous catalysts struggle to hydrogenate beyond formate; high-hydricity metal hydrides and external mediators (alcohols or amines) are needed to liberate formate and complete methanol formation. Even the benchmark Ru-MACHO catalyst attains high conversion at modest temperatures, yet it depends on amine additives and the high cost of Ru52.

The bridge: Heterogenizing homogeneous catalysts

Homogeneous catalysts offer atomically defined active sites and tunable selectivity, but their separation and recycling remain problematic. Heterogeneous catalysts, in contrast, provide stability, reusability, and industrial scalability, yet at the cost of poorly defined active sites and limited control over reactivity. Single-atom catalysts (SACs) have recently emerged as a unifying platform, bringing the best of both worlds: isolated, molecular-like sites anchored on solid supports (Fig. 4). SACs can involve diverse metals and supports, creating a bridge between homogeneous and heterogeneous catalysis53–56. Importantly, SACs should not be viewed as a single, well-defined class of materials, but rather as part of a broader continuum that spans from extended surfaces and supported nanoparticles to fully isolated, molecularly defined active sites. In this regime, the nuclearity of the active site progressively decreases, while site isolation and coordination specificity increase. In this way, SACs “heterogenize” isolated active sites, trying to mimic the selectivity of the homogeneous catalysts and “homogenize” heterogeneous catalysts by transforming diffuse surfaces and supported nano-islands into ensembles of well-defined coordination environments that offer exceptional performance, cost reduction, high selectivity, and stability57–59. Their uniform active sites and molecular-like behavior make them ideal for computational modeling and understanding structure–activity relationships. From a conceptual and almost utopian perspective, SACs embody the unattainable ideal of catalysis. Yet this duality is not without its flip side: the properties that make SACs so appealing also pose inherent challenges that limit their expected, almost ‘perfect’, performance. SACs face significant experimental challenges, including the difficulty of achieving controlled synthesis with uniform atomic dispersion (particularly at high metal loadings), maintaining the stability of isolated atoms against aggregation, and the requirement of advanced characterization techniques, such as high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (STEM) and X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAFS) to confirm their mononuclear nature and coordination environment62. Beyond these synthetic and analytical barriers, SACs also suffer from intrinsic catalytic limitations: their typically low metal loading restricts overall activity, their oxidic electronic states can hinder reactions requiring metallic sites, and the absence of adjacent active centers limits their applicability in processes that demand cooperative ensembles. Moreover, even small fractions of coexisting nanoparticles may compromise stability and mechanistic interpretation63–65. Nevertheless, recent works have emphasized that SACs rarely operate in complete isolation: in many cases, clusters or nanoparticles act synergistically with single atoms, for example, by facilitating spillover or bond activation, so that mixed SAC–NP systems can deliver enhanced performance compared to atomically dispersed species alone66. Actually, hydrogen spillover is also relevant in SAC alloys. For example, isolated Pd atoms embedded in a Cu matrix can efficiently dissociate H2, generating reactive H* species that migrate onto neighboring Cu sites67, thereby enabling or enhancing subsequent hydrogenation reactions on the otherwise less active Cu surface.

Clearly, these considerations highlight that our understanding of SACs is still incomplete and that many fundamental questions remain open. It is increasingly evident that there will be no universal rule dictating how single atoms behave, as their performance is intrinsically dependent on the reaction conditions, the properties of the support, and the identity of the metal itself. Further advances in synthesis and characterization are needed to define active sites better and optimize conditions68,69. Furthermore, they require an efficient support to stabilize isolated metal atoms and maximize atomic utilization, preventing aggregation70. In this regard, two-dimensional (2D) materials offer clear advantages compared to conventional three-dimensional (3D) supports71. Due to their ultrathin laminar structure, 2D materials exhibit a high specific surface area and many anchoring sites, enabling a greater dispersion of metal atoms as isolated sites without clustering72. Moreover, the exposed surfaces of 2D materials ensure that virtually all active atoms are accessible to reactants, unlike 3D supports, where many atoms remain buried or inaccessible in the bulk.

MXenes (see Fig. 4) are among the most promising materials to anchor SACs (2D transition-metal carbides/nitrides), which are considered ideal supports because they possess a large surface area in comparison to 3D transition-metal carbides and metal oxides73–75, Furthermore, they have anchored functional groups (-O, -OH, -F, commonly denoted as T in the stoichiometric formula) that originate from the etching process and enable uniform anchoring of isolated metal atoms75. This allows the formation of strong metal–support covalent interactions, preventing aggregation and enabling precisely defined catalytic sites. Such characteristics directly address the central challenge in CO2 electroreduction: steering complex multi-electron pathways with selectivity.

Beyond their role as ideal supports for isolated atoms, MXenes have demonstrated intrinsic activity toward CO2 capture and conversion. Un-terminated MXenes (without functional groups at the exposed metal layers) are oxophilic and can bind and activate CO276. Morales-Salvador et al. demonstrated that surface Tx terminations (–O, –OH, –F) on MXenes strongly influence CO2 adsorption strength and selectivity, confirming their promise as tunable sorbents for CO2 capture and utilization77, while experiments have reported substantial CO2 uptake on Ti3C2Tx/V2CTx78. More recent calculations and simulations under realistic conditions confirm that Mo2C/Ti3C2 frameworks bind and convert CO2 effectively (often to CO), and map how electrolyte/operando aspects control activity and selectivity79. Critically, surface chemistry governs performance: mixed –O/–OH/–F terminations and –OH proton-donor roles shift limiting potentials. They can make Ti3C2 competitive for C1 products under suitable pH and potential80, and are identified as a central lever in state-of-the-art CO2RR modeling on MXenes81.

Nevertheless, while these studies establish MXenes as promising intrinsic catalysts, their performance remains constrained by HER competition and the lack of atomically precise sites. This realization has naturally driven the field toward the next stage: employing MXenes as ideal hosts for SACs, where vacancies and functional groups can immobilize isolated atoms, endowing the material with stability and molecular-level precision. The decisive breakthrough that positioned MXenes as platforms for single-atom catalysis came with the work of Zhao et al., who reported the confinement of Pt atoms within vacancy sites of Ti3C2Tₓ82. This seminal study demonstrated that surface vacancies and terminations in MXenes can strongly anchor isolated atoms, preventing agglomeration and establishing atomically dispersed active centers capable of engaging in CO2 functionalization. Building upon this concept, Zhao et al. showed that single Cu atoms can be enriched selectively on the Mo2CTx phase by migration from SiO2 under reductive pretreatment. This strategy produced catalysts containing two distinct types of Cu sites, the Cu/Mo2CTx and Cu/SiO2. The Cu/Mo2CTx interface, with Lewis acidic Cu+ sites, is responsible for the observed increased methanol formation rate and selectivity (Fig. 5a, b), a conclusion further supported by DFT calculations28,83 (Fig. 5c). Cu single atoms are also key catalysts, in this case coordinated with hollow O sites on Ti3C2Oₓ to promote CO-to-C2 coupling86. The catalyst delivered an outstanding 98% selectivity toward C2 products, including an unprecedented 71% selectivity for C2H4. Both experimental data and DFT calculations confirmed that O-coordinated Cu sites remain stable under electrolysis and facilitate CO conversion by promoting the *CO–CHO intermediate. This work highlighted that MXenes stabilize SACs and actively tune their electronic states to drive multi-carbon pathways. Theoretical calculations showed that, in addition to Cu species, other transition metals also positively enhance activity87. Chromium anchored on Ti2CO2 presented higher selectivity for CO2RR than for hydrogen evolution, and the use of Co on Ti3C2Tx shows good performance due to the easy coordination process of the metal88. Furthermore, high-throughput DFT screening predicts that Ru, Pd, and other metals anchored on O-terminated MXenes can exhibit exceptionally low limiting potentials for CO or CH3OH formation, pointing to the tunability of these platforms89.

Finally, recent descriptor-based analyses confirm that both metal and support electronic features for metal SACs supported on Mo2COx and Ti2COx account for their promoted CO2 cleavage to CO* and O* energetics (Fig. 5d-e); i.e., CO2 adsorption energy, transition-state, and the reaction energy90. For electrocatalytic reactions, the challenge is greater; one needs to account for a realistic modeling of electrolyte and operando environments, which can drastically alter predicted trends compared to vacuum81. Computational studies are essential to reveal how MXene surface terminations govern the activity of SACs. However, current methods still struggle to capture the true complexity of electrocatalysis under operando conditions91.

Beyond MXenes, carbon nitrides are among the rising star supports of SACs, in which graphitic C3N4 stands out among them. It is formed from heptazine units linked by tertiary amines (Fig. 4), where carbon atoms adopt sp2 hybridization, forming delocalized π-bonds, while nitrogen atoms donate lone pairs, promoting charge mobility and metal ion capture. Corrugation from electronic repulsion promotes active site formation92. These carbon matrices provide N-rich frameworks that strongly coordinate metal atoms, confining them as stable isolated sites61,93. This nitrogen-rich character makes these materials a unique 2D support for SACs, capable of stabilizing single atoms while influencing their catalytic behavior through electronic interactions94. The stability of SACs on carbon nitride frameworks depends critically on how strongly the metal binds to the support. Density-functional-theory-based Pourbaix analyses of M@N4-graphene systems (M = Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, Ru, Rh, Pd, Ag, Ir, Pt, Au) show that adsorbed H, O and OH species can block or even restructure the active sites, and that stability increases across the periods but drops sharply for coinage metals such as Cu, Ag and Au; in this model Mn and Co dissolve under acidic conditions, whereas Fe remains stable across all pH94. When the same analysis is extended to other supports—including N-doped graphene, C3N4, and covalent organic frameworks—the heptazine units of C3N4 anchor single Cr, Mn, Fe, or Co atoms too weakly: these atoms either dissolve or form hydroxides under electrochemical conditions, whereas analogous centers on covalent organic frameworks remain stable over a wide range of potentials and pH95.

Having established the thermodynamic stability of SACs on nitrogen-rich carbon supports, we now turn to their catalytic activity. The CO2 electroreduction has been exhaustively studied on dispersed metal–nitrogen–carbon (M–N–C) catalysts. The work of Li et al. studied M-N-C matrices using Mn-, Fe-, Co-, Ni-, and Cu as a single atom site. Electrochemical tests and DFT simulations revealed the effect of a single metal charge on the electrocatalytic performance. The selectivity towards CO or H2 was governed by the number of d-antibonding electrons, observing CO as the primary product with Fe and Ni, while CoNC predominantly favored H296. DFT calculations were employed to investigate CO2 adsorption and hydrogenation to formic acid using Fe, Co, Ni, and Cu SACs on g-C3N4. The CO2 adsorption energies are relatively weak ( − 0.16 to −0.40 eV), although the CO2 hydrogenation to formate (HCOO*) was predicted to occur, followed by hydrogenation to formic acid, being the rate-determining step dependent on the metal center97. However, insights into HER competitiveness were not provided. Another computational screening evaluated a transition metal single-atom on g-C3N4 for CO2RR. Only four systems (Ni-, Rh-, Os-, and Ir) were identified as promising catalysts. Rh and Ni were highlighted as particularly active for CO2 reduction to CH4, with CO and HCOO* protonation as the potential-determining steps, respectively98.

Cometto et al. synthesized Cu single atom embedded on C3N4, showing H2 and formic acid as the main products for CO2RR99, as illustrated on the cyclic voltammetry in Fig. 6a, b. Gas-phase DFT simulations (without solvent) align well with these findings, as although the production of CO is exergonic (Fig. 6c), the strong binding energy between CO and Cu1 prevents its desorption and boosts the production of HCOOH (Fig. 6d)100. Moreover, the moderate overpotential needed for the HER makes this catalyst non-selective for CO2RR, in alignment with the experimental results (Fig. 6e). Importantly, this study exemplifies both the usefulness and the limitations of relying solely on H* binding energies as descriptors. As highlighted in recent perspectives on computational electrochemistry93, such simplified descriptors neglect critical factors such as explicit charge transfer, electrolyte effects, and interfacial fluctuations. Yet, despite these well-known shortcomings, the model correctly predicted that H2 and HCOOH would dominate over CO as reduction products. However, the inability to capture electrolyte-dependent selectivity trends underlines the need to integrate more realistic interfacial and solvation effects into future computational schemes. Nevertheless, as the HER/CO2RR was predicted well, it motivated subsequent studies extending the analysis to the entire first row of transition metals, where only Ni exhibited a destabilization (repulsion) of H* adsorption (Fig. 6f). Even in this case, the overpotential required to drive methanol formation was found to be equivalent to that of HER, meaning that selectivity toward CO2RR remained unattainable101. The performance of Au1 was also investigated, predicting that CO2 can be reduced to CH4. However, once again, the proton binding energy emerges as a critical parameter. In related work, Feng and collaborators developed a Mn single-atom catalyst with Mn–N3 coordination embedded in C3N4 supported on carbon nanotubes, showing an efficient CO2 electroreduction. The catalyst achieves a Faradaic efficiency for CO of 98.8% at an overpotential of 0.44 V in aqueous electrolyte. DFT calculations revealed that the Mn–N3 site facilitates CO2 adsorption and lowers the free energy barrier for *COOH formation. The catalyst also demonstrated excellent stability over long-term electrolysis102. Finally, Varela and coworkers evaluated the CO2RR employing carbon nitride matrices derived from polyaniline and carbon black, doped with Fe and/or Mn. The catalysts exhibit high selectivity toward CO and syngas, with faradaic efficiencies up to ~80% at −0.5 V vs. RHE. Product analysis confirmed CO and H2 as the dominant products, with only trace amounts of formate detected. Mechanistic evidence indicates that nitrogen atoms serve as primary active sites for CO formation, while Fe centers, due to stronger CO adsorption, enable further hydrogenation to CH4 at more negative potentials. Mn, in contrast, binds CO weakly, favoring its desorption103.

Regarding photocatalytic applications, Fu and coworkers used DFT simulations to predict that single-atom Co incorporation on g-C3N4 dramatically lowers the activation barrier for CO2 hydrogenation from 1.63 eV on pristine g-C3N4 to 0.44 eV. Experimental synthesis of these Co-C3N4 via thermal copolymerization confirmed atomically dispersed Co coordinated with two-coordinated N atoms. The photocatalytic tests showed that 1% Co-C3N4 achieves a CO yield of 94.9 μmol g⁻¹ h⁻¹, nearly 380 times higher than pristine g-C3N4 (0.25 μmol g⁻¹ h⁻¹), with stable performance over extended operation104. Another DFT study presents Cu single-atom supported on nitrogen-rich g-C3N5 (Cu@g-C3N5) as a promising photocatalyst for CO2 conversion. The Cu–N diatomic active sites govern CO2 activation and facilitate C–C coupling105. The entire reaction pathway from CO* intermediates to C2H4 proceeds with downhill free energy changes, accounting for the high selectivity toward ethylene. The low-valence Cu center effectively alters the electronic structure of g-C3N5, stabilizing CO2 adsorption and enhancing hydrogenation activity. In contrast to Cu@g-C3N4, the g-C3N5-based system enables the stable formation of the COCO* intermediate, a critical step for multi-carbon products, thus providing a rational strategy for the selective production of C2+ species. Furthermore, DFT evaluated single Pd and Pt atoms supported on g-C3N4 as photocatalysts for CO2 conversion. The isolated metal atoms strongly interact with pyridinic N atoms in g-C3N4, remain in low oxidation states, and act as active sites, while g-C3N4 supplies hydrogen through the hydrogen evolution reaction. Reaction pathway analysis shows that Pd/g-C3N4 favors HCOOH while Pt/g-C3N4 preferentially reduces CO2 to CH4106.

Carbon nitride and broader M-N-C frameworks demonstrate how the precision of molecular catalysts can be merged with the robustness of solids: they anchor isolated metal atoms on N-rich scaffolds, tune electronic structure and lower activation barriers, leading to impressive Faradaic efficiencies for CO2 reduction to CO or formate and, in select cases, to methanol or light hydrocarbons. Yet, these materials are not a panacea. Their stability hinges on the strength of the metal–support bond—coinage metals like Cu or Ag readily dissolve or form hydroxides, and HER often (almost always) competes directly with CO2 reduction. Multi-carbon products remain rare, and simplified descriptors (such as proton adsorption energies) can mislead predictions of selectivity. In this context, the Bagger plots107 have emerged as a widely adopted approach to rationalize activity and selectivity trends by correlating hydrogen adsorption energetics with HER and CO2RR performance. These plots provide valuable intuitive insight and have guided catalyst screening across a broad range of materials. Nevertheless, their predictive power is inherently limited when addressing multi-electron, multi-intermediate pathways and complex interfacial effects, particularly for the formation of C2+ products.

Thus, while carbon nitrides and M-N-C catalysts represent a major step toward bridging the homogeneous and heterogeneous paradigms, realizing their full potential will require new strategies, dual-site architectures, electronically conductive supports, and operando modeling that simultaneously stabilize single atoms, suppress side reactions and drive multi-electron and multi-carbon transformations.

Machine learning for CO₂ valorization: progress, pitfalls, and future directions

Machine learning (ML) has rapidly entered the field of CO2 valorization108,109 as a tool to complement calculations and accelerate the discovery of catalytic materials110. Across both heterogeneous and homogeneous systems, ML has been used to mine large DFT-calculated datasets50,111, extract correlations between electronic descriptors and activity, and screen candidate catalysts at scales that would be otherwise impossible. For instance, several studies on SACs show how intrinsic descriptors such as electronegativity, valence electron counts, atomic radii, and coordination environments can rationalize product selectivity and overpotential, while supervised models (gradient boosting, random forests) identify promising SAC–support combinations112–114 Key descriptors determining CO2 activation on transition metal-based SACs on MXenes based on ML regression were also determined (vide supra). Similarly, the introduction of adsorption energy distributions has been proposed as a more realistic fingerprint of catalytic behavior across facets and alloys, enabling unsupervised learning to reveal both known Cu/Zn systems and new candidates, such as ZnRh or ZnPt3 for methanol synthesis115.

Another important branch is the development of ML interatomic potentials (MLIPs), which provide near-quantum accuracy at far lower computational cost. Reviews highlight the ability of ML approaches to capture adsorption, diffusion, and reaction dynamics on metal and oxide surfaces116, demonstrating their effectiveness for modeling complex surface processes with near ab initio accuracy. In this context, recently developed frameworks based on closed-form transfer learning of pretrained neural network descriptors represent an important advance. These approaches reuse descriptors that have already learned fundamental chemical environments and systematically adapt them to new materials or reaction conditions without retraining the entire model from scratch. As a result, training times can be reduced from weeks to minutes while largely preserving predictive accuracy117. This strategy significantly improves computational efficiency and enables rapid deployment of ML models across related catalytic systems, facilitating large-scale, high-throughput simulations of surface reactivity. These tools are beginning to bridge the gap between limited DFT datasets and realistic, dynamical catalyst simulations.

ML is also impacting homogeneous catalysis. In Ru-based CO2 hydrogenation, for example, ML uncovers hidden relationships between ligand design and catalytic turnover frequencies, guiding the rational development of complexes for formic acid and methanol production118, while transfer-learning frameworks allow pretrained neural network potentials to be fine-tuned efficiently to new chemical environments. Broader perspectives stress that AI in homogeneous catalysis goes beyond ML algorithms, including curated datasets, generative models for inverse design, and even robotic experimentation119. Together, these approaches open the door to automated exploration of vast chemical spaces that were previously intractable by trial-and-error. Screening catalysts via ML remains challenging due to difficulties in standardizing reaction conditions. For example, Ortiz-García et al. presented a detailed theoretical and data-driven investigation into the cycloaddition of CO2 to a diverse set of epoxides catalyzed by ascorbic acid combined with halide ions120. The study identified the rate-determining step (rds) as the final ring-closing step to form the cyclic carbonate. It revealed that the steric hindrance introduced by substituents on the epoxide substrate significantly affects the reaction energy barriers, especially at the Rate-Determining Step (RDS). Epoxides with bulkier or multiple substituents exhibited higher kinetic costs, while smaller and electron-withdrawing groups, such as fluorine, reduced the barriers.

Furthermore, the effect of different halide nucleophiles (I⁻, Br⁻, Cl⁻, F⁻) and counterions (K⁺, TBA⁺) were evaluated, showing the importance of solvation and ion-pair interactions. Although more than 20 epoxides were systematically compared, the actual set of candidates remained relatively limited, and in every case the evaluation required explicit transition state calculations. This highlights the enormous computational cost of generating high-quality datasets suitable for training ML potentials. However, the study also demonstrates that DFT calculations are not necessary to generate descriptors like buried volume (%VBur) and electronic parameters employing the AQME121 workflow and the ROBERT code122 for ML statistical analysis. The correlation with activation barriers achieves a strong predictive performance. These results offered valuable insights for designing efficient, sustainable catalysts and epoxide substrates for CO2 utilization.

Beyond reactivity, ML coupled with DFT enables the large-scale exploration of MXenes, highlighting how defect engineering, surface functionalization, and single-atom decoration can be rationally tuned for catalysis123. At a systems level, the concept of materials acceleration platforms (MAPs) brings ML together with automation, robotics, and closed-loop experimentation. These platforms have been proposed for heterogeneous CO2 photo(thermal)catalysis, an underexplored but promising route to solar fuels, where MAPs could integrate descriptor-based modeling, high-throughput synthesis, and AI-driven optimization across catalyst and reactor scales124. The vision is that by embedding ML in autonomous cycles of design–synthesis–testing–analysis, discovery can be accelerated while simultaneously generating mechanistic insights.

However, despite this impressive progress, essential caveats remain. Current ML workflows overwhelmingly rely on thousands of DFT-calculated adsorption energies, charges, or electronic descriptors mined by statistical algorithms to extract “key” features. But what is the actual validity of these datasets, and how realistic are the models they describe?

For electrocatalytic reactions, this concern is particularly severe. Although increasing attention is now being devoted to the role of electrolyte effects—most notably the identity and concentration of alkali metal cations—many DFT studies still neglect explicit solvent, electrolyte structure, and applied potential. It is now well established that metal cations can drastically alter CO2 activation (e.g., without a metal cation, the reaction does not take place in a pure 1 mM H2SO4 electrolyte using Au electrodes)125. Ignoring these effects, therefore, casts serious doubt on whether commonly used DFT descriptors faithfully represent real electrocatalytic behavior under operando conditions. DFT studies neglect explicit solvent, electrolyte, and applied potential, raising doubts about whether the resulting descriptors reflect real electrocatalytic behavior126,127. Without systematic experimental validation, these ML-derived descriptors risk remaining mere correlations, published because “including ML” is fashionable, rather than advancing mechanistic understanding.

Electrocatalysis illustrates this tension clearly. While binding energy scaling relations can be modeled and even used to train MLIPs, the representation of electrochemical environments remains rudimentary: implicit vs explicit solvent, the double layer’s role, and the electrolyte’s identity all strongly affect kinetics but are often ignored. In principle, all calculations should be performed consistently and realistically, ideally including explicit solvent, applied potential, and both anions and cations128. While building such datasets remains computationally demanding and resource-intensive, it is increasingly accessible, and several groups are now actively pursuing this direction129, notably through the development of ML interatomic potentials that can cheaply reproduce this complexity. The resulting predictions may inherit the same limitations if these potentials are trained on data derived from approximate models designed to cut computational cost. In other words, we risk chasing efficiency at the expense of physical reliability. This raises the question of whether dedicating vast resources to training increasingly sophisticated ML frameworks on incomplete or unrealistic models truly advances the field. In this light, ML should be viewed not as a shortcut to publishable correlations, but as an enabling layer of integration: bridging first-principles simulations, curated datasets, and experimental data. When implemented rigorously, with robust databases, interpretable descriptors, and feedback mechanisms, ML can accelerate the valorization of CO₂ from molecular to process scale. But until such integration is routine, caution is needed: ML risks becoming a decorative add-on rather than a genuine driver of innovation without grounding in realistic models and experiments.

Conclusions

The valorization of CO2 into fuels and chemicals has evolved into one of the most dynamic areas in catalysis, where the boundaries between homogeneous and heterogeneous approaches are increasingly blurred. Advances in molecular design, the rise of SACs, and the exploration of novel supports have opened new opportunities for steering reactivity, selectivity, and stability. Yet, it remains challenging: CO2 is intrinsically stable, multielectron pathways are difficult to control, and competition with hydrogen evolution often undermines efficiency. Bridging molecular precision with materials robustness will be essential to unlock scalable solutions. From a broader perspective, three main directions emerge.

First, rational design of hybrid catalytic platforms, such as SACs on conductive 2D supports, offers a unique route to combine the well-defined active sites of homogeneous systems with the durability of solids. However, this promise is contingent on overcoming synthetic and analytical bottlenecks, particularly the stabilization of isolated atoms at industrially relevant loadings and the ability to monitor them operando.

Second, the integration of ML is reshaping how catalysts are discovered and optimized. ML can accelerate descriptor identification, screen vast chemical spaces, and enable data-driven design strategies across homogeneous and heterogeneous systems. Yet, as discussed, its true potential will only be realized if models are trained on realistic datasets and continuously validated against experimental results. The emergence of materials acceleration platforms that embed ML within closed-loop synthesis, characterization, and testing cycles represents a transformative direction for shortening discovery timelines and guiding the field toward interpretable, scalable solutions.

Third, operando and multiscale approaches are needed to connect molecular-level insights with macroscopic performance. Realistic modeling of electrochemical interfaces, integration of spectroscopy and microscopy under reaction conditions, and multi-scale simulations that bridge quantum chemistry with reactor-level phenomena will be key to translating catalytic advances into practical processes.

In conclusion, the field of CO2 valorization is entering a new phase: one where integration between homogeneous and heterogeneous paradigms, computation and experiment, and between human intuition and machine intelligence, will define progress. Future breakthroughs will not arise from isolated advances, but from synergies: catalysts designed with atomistic precision yet manufactured at scale, databases that merge high-throughput computation with reliable experimental data, and platforms where automation and ML accelerate iteration while preserving physical interpretability. By embracing this integrative vision, catalysis can move closer to transforming CO2 from a liability into a sustainable feedstock for the chemicals and fuels of tomorrow.
