Introduction and importance

Chondrosarcomas are malignant cartilaginous neoplasms with various morphological features and clinical behavior. They account for about 20 % of all primary malignant tumors of the bone and are the second most common primary bone tumor. It may arise as a novel primary tumor or secondary from a preexisting benign cartilaginous neoplasm such as osteochondroma or enchondroma [1]. Primary or conventional chondrosarcoma accounts for 85–90 % of chondrosarcomas. It is subdivided into central, periosteal, and peripheral subgroups, while the secondary variants include clear cell chondrosarcoma, mesenchymal chondrosarcoma, and dedifferentiated chondrosarcoma [2]. The majority of patients are older than 50 years of age at diagnosis, with a slight male predominance [3]. Usually arise in the pelvis or long bones [4,5]. Spinal involvement is a rare occurrence, accounts for <10 % of all chondrosarcomas [6]. Thoracic spine is the most common involved part of spine [4,5,7,8]. The thoracic spine is the most frequent localization accounting for 60 % of spinal CSs, followed by the cervical and lumbar region. Unlike most other malignant spinal tumors, the lesions may arise in the vertebral body (5 %), the posterior elements (40 %), or both (45 %), since there are three growth centers in each vertebra from which the tumor originates.

The most common symptom in chondrosarcoma is a localized back pain with or without signs of nerve root compression, spinal cord compression, or history of increasing back swelling. The radiological features of chondrosarcomas vary significantly depending upon the histologic grade. CT-scan usually reveals a lytic, destructive, lesion of varying density, and CT is helpful in defining the tumor location and characterizing the tumor growth. MRI is used to estimate the extent of soft tissue invasion. The following criteria favor a diagnosis of chondrosarcoma: deep endosteal scalloping (>2/3 of cortical thickness), cortical disruption, periosteal reaction, soft tissue mass, and intense radionuclide uptake [9].

Chondrosarcomas are typically resistant to known protocols of radiotherapy and chemotherapy; therefore, surgical removal is essential, and the outcome is based on the margins achieved. Several surgical approaches for achieving en bloc resection have been mentioned in previous studies. En bloc resection has been proven as an effective treatment for aggressive benign, primary malignant, and solitary metastatic tumors involving lumbar and thoracic spine. Both one-stage surgery (using only a posterior or anterior approach) and two-stage surgery (using a combination of posterior and anterior approaches) have been mentioned. However, en bloc resection for large size tumor invading multilevel vertebrae, especially in upper thoracic spine is more complicated and has a higher complication rate compared with single level resection. Satisfied tumor local control can be expected in patients with adequate surgical margin [10]. The incidence is rare and only reported in several case reports [2]. There are limited recommendations for such conditions.

In this study, we report a case of a patient who admitted to Hospital with signs and symptoms of thoracal foraminal stenosis due to mass effect of primary chondrosarcoma arising from the the 5th thoracal vertebrae. The patient underwent surgical resection. The ethical commission of our university approved this case report. The patient provided written informed consent. This case report was in line with SCARE 2023 criteria [30].

The presentation of case

A 36-year-old male was referred to our hospital with back pain radiating to the legs. The complain was accompanied by weakness of both legs, especially the left leg, and numbness from the chest below since a month prior to admission. The patient had a routine check-up at our spine clinic with complaints of back pain and a history of a lump on the back in the past three years. In the beginning, lump in the size of a marble that getting bigger to the size of tennis ball. There was no history of prolonged fever, night sweat, history of consuming tuberculosis medication. No history of loss of appetite, no history of weight loss, and no history of trauma. There was no history of the same complaint related to the disease in his family. The patient had undergone a biopsy procedure 23 days prior to admission with the result of chondrosarcoma grade I at thoracal region vertebrae. Due to the complaints, the patient then referred to our hospital for further management.

On back region examination, there was a deformity, gibbus, and sutured wound, shiny skin, and solitaire lump, with no venectation. Associated with midline tenderness at the level of T5 vertebrae (Fig. 1). There was reduction of sensation from bellow nipple to the lower extremity with movements range of the spine was normal. On neurological examination, the upper extremities showed normal motoric strength (5/5), while there was a decrease of motoric strength in the lower extremities (3/4). Areas below the level of T4 were hypoesthesia. Physiological reflexes of knee patellar reflex and ankle pes reflex were normal and there was Babinski pathological reflex. The patient's vegetative functions were normal. According to The American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) Impairment Scale, these results were classified into Score C.Fig. 1Presentation of patients. Initial radiographs were obtained and showed expansile sclerotic lesion at right posterior aspect of 5th to 6th costae which suggests primary bone tumor (Fig. 2). The laboratory results were within normal limits. The thoracolumbal MRI scan of the lesion revealed heterogenous mass with irregular border containing necrotic tissue arising from T5–6 vertebrae. The mass was 12.99 × 7.63 × 13.10 cm in size, expanding to cutis and subcutis layers, destructing the spinous process, transversus process, costovertebral junction, and posterior costae of T5–6. The mass was infiltrating the intradural and intramedullary tissue at the level of T4–5 vertebrae causing spinal canal stenosis, obliterating the flavum, supraspinous, and interspinous ligaments. The spinalis thoracis m., illiocostalis thoracis m., illiocostalis lumborum m., rotares m., and bilateral semispinalis thoracis m. were also obliterated. The mass entered right and left posterior aspect of thorax cavity, directly behind the aortic arch. These results suggest a process of chondrosarcoma. The patient was diagnosed with myeloradiculopathy at the level of T5 due to primary spinal chondrosarcoma at the level of T4–5 with ASIA Score C. The surgery was conducted by the subdivision of spine and orthopaedics oncology, along with thoracic and cardiovascular surgery division. The patient underwent a complete wide resection of the tumor in one stage. Initially, patient positioned in left lateral position (Fig. 4), a thoracotomy incision on right hemithorax between 4th and 5th ribs performed, osteotomy performed on 5th–7th ribs to expose the right hemithorax (Fig. 5), one lung ventilation is performed by the anaesthesiologist, and tumor on the right hemithorax exposed, with the tumor behind the pleura parietal, hard consistency, immobile, well-defined margin (Fig. 3). A posterior midline approach connected to the thoracotomy incision conducted. Wide excision performed by exposing the tumor posterior to the spine. The tumor was cut in half to expose the lamina and procesus spinosus. Patient than repositioned into prone position. Laminectomy and exposure of the spinal cord is performed with ligation of the roots of the 3rd, 4th, 5th, 6th Vertebra thoracal (Fig. 6). The left 5th–7th ribs attached to the tumor vertebrae were cut and anterolateral soft tissue attachments (e.g. parietal pleura) at the non-affected side of the vertebrae were released (Fig. 7). The excision continues to remove the 4th, 5th, 6th vertebral body. Removal of the tumor continue to the anterior aspect of the spinal cord. After the removal of the tumor body, irrigation of the wound with Normal saline and aquabidest. Intervertebral titanium tubular ring cage was inserted to replace the vertebral body. After the stabilization of the vertebra, thorax and cardiovascular surgery perform thoracic cavity closure with mesh to close the pleura (Fig. 10). The spinal cord was covered by absorbable haemostatic gelatin sponge and the wound was closed after drain insertion (Fig. 11). We did biopsy on resected tumor (Fig. 12). Patient repositioned into supine position for insertion of bilateral chest tube for drains. After the procedure, the patient was performed Post surgery Xray examination (Fig. 13) and monitored in intensive care unit (ICU). On day 1 postoperative, the patient was still sedated with endotracheal tube (ETT) installed. Vital signs were normal. There was no leak founded from the wound. The patient was anemic with hemoglobin (Hb) level was 6.7 g/dL, thus packed red cell transfusion was planned. The drain was 20 mL. On day 2 postoperative, the patient was awake with absent motoric function. He was then extubated, but desaturation occurred. Thus, the patient was re-intubated. Vital signs were normal. There was no leak founded from the wound. The patient was still anemic with slight increase of Hb (7.6 g/dL). The drain was 25 mL/24 h. Over the following several days, the patient was infected with ventilator associated pneumonia (VAP) with leukocyte count of 25.360/μl. On day 9 postoperative, the patient was re-sedated due to supraventicular tachycardia (SVT) and ETT was still installed due to no improvement in ventilator weaning. Blood pressure was 195/111 mmHg, while other vital signs were normal. The patient's Hb level increased to 10.8 g/dL. The drain was 5 mL/24 h. On post operation day 12 the patient was installed tracheostomy tube to replace the ETT, the VAP condition is improving with leukocyte count of 18.820/μl.

The strength of this case report lies in presenting a rare and complex case of primary giant chondrosarcoma involving the T4-T6 vertebrae. The article provides a thorough description of the patient's presentation, diagnostic workup, surgical approach, and outcomes. We also discuss the rationale behind using a combined lateral and posterior approach for tumor resection, which ensures comprehensive removal by first addressing the anterior components before proceeding to the posterior elements.

Discussion

Spinal chondrosarcoma are commonly grow slowly with an insidious onset [[11], [12], [13]]. Most patients complained of localized back pain, swelling, and neurological deficits due to spinal cord compression [[13], [14], [15], [16]]. symptoms usually develop over a long period since most tumors are low-grade and slow-growing lesions [17]. The patient in this report was presented with a painful intra-spinal mass on the back (Fig. 1) radiating to the legs, along with signs of motoric deficit in both legs and sensoric deficits below the level of T4. The plain radiograph and CT Scan showed the characteristic features of primary chondrosarcoma (Fig. 2 and Fig. 3). These results were confirmed by the previous biopsy result, a grade I chondrosarcoma.

Chondrosarcoma is the second most common type of the primary bone cancer. It affects individual between the age of 40–60. Patient with history of enchondromas, multiple exostoses, ollier disease, or maffucci syndrome increase the risk to have the tumor [11]. In this patient, There was no history of lump before. Patient had no history of the same disease in his family.

Location and histologic grade determine the treatment approaches of chondrosarcoma [18]. The primary modality is surgical excision [19]. Surgery should aim at preserving or even improving functionality, relieving pain, and controlling local tumor recurrence, promising a prolonged survival [20]. The spectrum of oncologically established surgical procedures applicable to spinal column varies from the most complex en block resection to the simplest curettage [21]. En block resection for primary chondrosarcoma successfully performed, wide, with disease-free margins, provides the best results regarding local tumor control with reported rates of recurrence as low as 3–8 % [21,22]. Recurrence commonly appears within 3–5 years postoperatively [[22], [23], [24]]. Although en block resection with tumor-free margins is the optimum surgical treatment for spinal chondrosarcomas that guarantees a long recurrence-free interval and patient survival [21,24,25], at the same time, induces a significant surgical challenge [20,25], quite often requiring spondylectomy. The proximity of neurovascular and visceral structures combined with complex spinal anatomy makes the goal of wide margins difficult, which is not always feasible even if a meticulous preoperative plan has been considered [[25], [26], [27]]. Complications of such procedures comprising mainly of complicated wound, blood loss, and implant failure [25,27,28]. Radiation and chemotherapy have been used as adjuvant therapies postoperatively. However, their effect on patient survival and local tumor recurrence seems to be not significant due to multidrug resistance. Tumor with small cells and low percentage of cartilage matrix show more sensitivity to chemotherapy [19], while higher grade chondrosarcomas or patients who cannot tolerate a major surgery is indicated for radiotherapy [24]. To get the best results in removing spinal tumors, different surgical methods are used depending on how far the tumor has spread. Boriani et al. have suggested four types of approaches for removing thoracic spine tumors: from the front (anterior), from the back (posterior), starting at the front and then moving to the back, and starting at the back and then doing both front and back together. Type 4, which combines both front and back approaches, is ideal for larger tumors that are more complicated to remove. Because it's challenging to reach the upper thoracic spine from the front due to the nearby heart, major blood vessels, and other organs, surgeons often use different techniques to access this area. These techniques include a low cervical approach with sternotomy, unilateral or bilateral manubriotomy, or clavicular dissection. This case report describes a surgical technique that initially utilizes a lateral approach to expose the anterior aspect of the tumor, followed by a transition to a posterior approach for resection. This method ensures comprehensive removal of the tumor by first addressing the anterior components before proceeding to the posterior elements. We were able to perform a wide excision spondylectomy of T4-T6 vertebrae, along with posterior stabilization, intervertebral titanium tubular ring cage insertion, thoracotomy, and bilateral chest tube insertion. Thus, a long-term follow-up will be planned for this patient to assess any signs of recurrences.

Beside the histopathological grade of the tumor, the prognosis depends on the possibility of performing en bloc excision with appropriate oncologic margins [21,24,26]. A successful operation, in terms of complete tumor excision with disease-free margins is a major independent prognostic predictor of the disease, affecting critically both local tumor control and patient survival. York et al. estimated an overall 5- and 10-year survival rate at 64 % and 40 %, respectively [24]. Factors adversely affecting survival rates are considered an older age, higher tumor grade, inadequate surgical margins, and a local recurrence [29].

The postoperative radiography was done to evaluate the implants (Fig. 13). Other than the procedure-related findings, there was infiltrates and homogenous consolidation in both hemithorax, suggesting a process of bilateral pneumonia and pleural effusion.

Conclusion

As the primary modality for chondrosarcoma, surgery should aim at preserving or even improving functionality, relieving pain, and controlling local tumor recurrence, promising a prolonged survival. Besides the tumor grade, a successful operation, in terms of complete tumor excision with disease-free margins is a major independent prognostic predictor of the disease, affecting critically both local tumor control and patient survival. Spondylectomy enables wide or marginal resection of malignant lesions of the spine in most cases with acceptable morbidity. However, it induces a significant surgical challenge. The proximity of neurovascular and visceral structures combined with complex spinal anatomy makes the goal of wide margins difficult.
