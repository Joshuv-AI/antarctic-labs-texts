Patterns and determinants of short and long interpregnancy intervals among pregnant women in Ethiopia: a population-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Ritbano Ahmed Abdo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBoth short and long interpregnancy intervals have been associated with adverse health effects for mothers and infants. Short interpregnancy intervals (SIPI) are more common in low- and middle-income countries, including Ethiopia, while long interpregnancy intervals (LIPI) are less prevalent in these regions. Most research in Ethiopia has focused on short birth intervals. This study addresses both issues in its analysis.Objectivethis study aimed to assess the pattern of interpregnancy intervals and determinants of short ( <24 months) and long ( 60 months) interpregnancy intervals among pregnant women in Ethiopia.MethodsA population-based cross-sectional study was conducted using the 2019 Mini Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey (EMDHS). This analysis included all non-first-time pregnant women interviewed during the EMDHS 2019 who were pregnant at the time of the survey and whose immediately preceding pregnancy had ended in a live birth. Multinomial multivariable logistic regression, accounting for the complex survey design, was employed to estimate adjusted relative risk (aRRR) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to identify determinants of short and long interpregnancy intervals. Statistical significance was determined using 95% CIs. Data analysis was conducted using Stata version 17/MP.ResultsThe prevalence of SIPI and LIPI was 39.1% and 15.5%, respectively. Women from poor and middle-income backgrounds exhibited a significantly lower likelihood of experiencing short intervals compared to those from the poorest households. Survival of the index child was also associated with a decreased relative risk of SIPI. Conversely, older maternal age was associated with an increased likelihood of LIPI. Factors such as completing primary education and higher parity were significantly associated with a reduced relative risk of LIPI.ConclusionThe high prevalence of short interpregnancy intervals in Ethiopia underscores a critical public health concern and highlights persistent gaps in reproductive health optimization. While the cross-sectional nature of the data and wide confidence intervals in certain subgroups warrant cautious interpretation, these findings clearly demonstrate the need for targeted, context-specific interventions. Specifically, strategies must address socioeconomic barriers and expand maternal education. Furthermore, future longitudinal research is essential to more precisely track and understand birth spacing dynamics across diverse communities.Keywords: Interpregnancy interval, Birth spacing, Determinants, Multinomial analysis, EMDHS, Ethiopia

Background

Fertility is a critical component of population dynamics [1], significantly affecting the size and structure of populations in various regions. In Ethiopia, as in many sub-Saharan African countries, high fertility rates contribute to rapid population growth [2]. The country’s total fertility rate stands at 4.1 births per woman, with rural women averaging nearly three more children than their urban counterparts (4.6 vs. 2.3 births per woman) [3].

The interpregnancy interval (IPI)), also known as the birth-to-pregnancy interval (BPI, refers to the period of time that elapses between the birth of one child and the subsequent conception of another child [4–6]. This interval is significant in reproductive health, as it can influence maternal and child health outcomes, including risks of complications during pregnancy and delivery, and can affect the well-being of both the mother and the child [7–11].

A SIPI, officially defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) as an interval of less than 24 months between a live birth and the next conception, has been linked to a range of adverse consequences, including increased infant and child mortality, low birth weight, preterm deliveries, and stillbirths [7, 8, 12–21]. Additionally, women who experience SIPI face elevated risks of pregnancy-related complications such as preeclampsia, anemia, high blood pressure, and premature rupture of membranes [10, 13, 14, 20–24]. Conversely, long birth-to-pregnancy intervals, defined as intervals of 60 months or more [5], are associated with an increased risk of obstructed labor, fetal malpresentation and malposition, hypertensive disorders, and preterm birth [11, 14, 24]. Moreover, both short and long interpregnancy intervals are linked to elevated risks of maternal and fetal complications [2, 14]. This underscores the necessity for targeted interventions to effectively mitigate these risks among women of reproductive age.

The WHO recommends a minimum IPI of 24 months for optimal recovery and health of the mother and the newborn [5]. However, birth intervals are influenced by a complex interplay of factors, including social and cultural norms, reproductive behaviors, and the availability and utilization of reproductive health services [9, 25–28]. Empirical evidence from various cultural settings has identified factors such as breastfeeding practices, contraceptive use, maternal age, occupation, education, and the survival status of the index child as significant determinants of birth intervals [7, 9, 25, 29–40]. In Ethiopia, over half of women report SBPI, highlighting the need for targeted interventions [41]. In response to the high rates of maternal and newborn morbidity and mortality, the Ethiopian Federal Ministry of Health has implemented a multi-faceted strategy to enhance access to facility-based maternal and newborn services [42]. Throughout recent decades, Ethiopia has included child spacing programs as a part of its national health promotion strategy [43], however, short birth intervals remain prevalent in the country [41].

Despite the critical importance of analyzing the IPI to identify factors contributing to inadequate birth spacing, there is a significant deficit of research on this topic within the Ethiopian context. To date, no comprehensive studies have systematically assessed the patterns of birth intervals and their influencing factors among pregnant women in the country. Consequently, the aim of this study is to analyze the patterns of IPI and the determinants of both short and long interpregnancy intervals among pregnant women in Ethiopia, utilizing the EMDHS 2019 dataset.

Methods and materials

Study setting and period

This study utilized a population-based cross-sectional design, drawing on secondary data from the 2019 EMDHS. The data collection was carried out from March 21, 2019, to June 28, 2019. All the nine regional states of the country (Afar, Tigray, Amhara, Oromia, Somali, Southern Nations, Nationalities, and People’s Region (SNNPR), Benishangul Gumuz, Gambela, and Harari) and two city administrations (Addis Ababa and Dire Dawa) were included in the study [3].

Sampling procedure and sample size

The 2019 EMDHS utilized the census enumeration areas (EAs) established for the 2019 Population and Housing Census (PHC) as its sampling framework, encompassing a total of 149,093 EAs. Of these, 35,292 are located in urban settings while 113,801 are found in rural areas. On average, each EA consists of approximately 131 households, with rural EAs being smaller on average compared to their urban counterparts (119 households versus 170 households).

A two-stage stratified sampling method was implemented to select households across Ethiopia, ensuring a nationally representative sample. In the first stage, 305 EAs were chosen using probability proportional to size, with independent selections made within each sampling stratum (93 in urban areas and 212 in rural areas). In the second stage, a fixed number of 30 households from each cluster were selected using equal probability systematic sampling from an updated household listing. The EMDHS 2019 successfully interviewed a total of 8,885 women of reproductive age, including 784 pregnant women. Following the exclusion of 198 nulliparous women, 586 pregnant women (534 weighted sample) with at least one live birth prior to the pregnancy were identified for this study. The analysis utilized the individual record dataset, and further details on the sampling procedure can be found in the 2019 EMDHS report. Study variables and measurements

Outcome variable

The outcome variable of this study was the IPI, calculated by determining the duration between the date of the immediately preceding live birth and the conception date of the current pregnancy. To derive the IPI, the duration of the current pregnancy (gestational age at the time of the interview) was subtracted from the total elapsed time between the preceding live birth and the date of the interview. The resulting intervals were categorized based on WHO classifications: women with an IPI of less than 24 months were classified as having a SIPI and assigned a code of 1; those with intervals ranging from 24 to 59 months were considered to have an optimal interpregnancy interval, receiving a code of 0 (reference category); and women with an IPI of 60 months or more were classified as having a LIPI, assigned a code of 2.

Statistical analysis

Data cleaning, preparation, recoding, and analysis were performed using Stata version 17/MP. Descriptive statistics were generated to summarize both the independent and outcome variables. To produce nationally representative estimates and ensure the statistical validity of the findings, sample weighting was strictly applied to all analyses using the standard weight variable (v005) provided in the dataset.This weighting process was essential to adjust for the complex, two-stage stratified cluster sampling design, specifically compensating for the non-proportional allocation of samples across different geographic regions and urban-rural strata, as well as accounting for individual household non-response rates. The complex survey design effects were explicitly declared in Stata using the svyset command to ensure accurate calculation of standard errors and confidence intervals. To identify the independent predictors of SIPI and LIPI, a multinomial multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed using the optimal interpregnancy interval as the base (reference) outcome. This approach accounted for the complex survey design by explicitly incorporating stratification, clustering, and sample weighting. Initially, multinomial bivariate logistic regression was conducted to screen potential predictors; variables with a p-value < 0.25 were selected for inclusion in the final multivariable model. Statistical significance was established using a threshold of p-value < 0.05 and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The final measures of association were reported as Adjusted Relative Risk Ratios (aRRR) alongside their corresponding 95% CIs. Additionally, the mean Variance Inflation Factor (VIF = 1.09) was found to fall within an acceptable range.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics

The average age of pregnant women in the study was approximately 28.97 years. A significant proportion (51.9%) of women had no formal education, while those with primary education comprised 34.4% and only a small percentage had attained higher education (6.2%). The majority of respondents were from rural areas (74.8%). In terms of wealth, a notable percentage of women belonged to the poorest category (28.5%), while only a small proportion was classified as rich or richest. Most respondents were married (98.3%), and the predominant religion among the women was Muslim (43.7%). The data also revealed concerning literacy levels, with 64.5% of women unable to read at all (Table 2).

Most women had at least one living child, with 94.1% reporting their index child as alive. Awareness of modern contraceptives was high, with 94.8% indicating awareness. However, a substantial portion of women (76.6%) did not receive postnatal care (PNC). More women reported delivering at health facilities (53.8%) compared to home deliveries (46.2%). The data highlights the need for targeted interventions and support systems, especially concerning maternal health resources and education (Table 2).

Patterns of IPI among pregnant women in Ethiopia

Out of 534 respondents, 45.5% [95% CI:39.01–52.05] had an optimum birth to pregnancy interval, 39.1% [95% CI:32.82–45.70] reported shorter intervals, and 15.5% [95% CI:10.66–21.93] experienced long intervals.

Determinants of short and long interpregnancy intervals

Multinomial multivariable logistic regression modeling revealed that several maternal sociodemographic and reproductive characteristics serve as independent predictors of interpregnancy interval lengths. Regarding SIPI, literacy, household wealth, and the survival status of the index child were statistically significant. Interestingly, women who had no card available in the required language exhibited a substantially higher relative risk of experiencing a SIPI compared to those who cannot read at all (aRRR = 5.8; 95% CI: 1.26–27.83). Conversely, belonging to the middle and poor wealth index categories was associated with a significant reduction in the likelihood of a short interval (aRRR = 0.3; 95% CI: 0.12–0.74 and aRRR = 0.3; 95% CI: 0.14–0.87, respectively). Furthermore, the survival of the index child demonstrated an inverse relationship, with mothers of living index children showing a significantly lower relative risk of experiencing a SIPI (aRRR = 0.2; 95% CI: 0.05–0.87) (Table 3).

For LIPI, maternal age, literacy, education, and parity were significant predictors. Older participants exhibited an increased likelihood of experiencing a LIPI (aRRR = 1.2; 95% CI: 1.12–1.41). Mixed literacy levels were also strongly associated with longer intervals; the relative risk of a LIPI was higher for women who could only read parts of sentences (aRRR = 9.3; 95% CI: 2.16–40.13) as well as those capable of reading full sentences (aRRR = 7.3; 95% CI: 1.47–36.71) compared to their illiterate counterparts. Discussion

Prevalence of short and long IPI

This study aimed to assess the patterns of IPIs and determinants of short and long IPI among pregnant women utilizing data from the 2019 EMDHS. The results revealed that 39.1% (95% CI: 32.82–45.70) of the participants experienced a SIPI, while 15.5% (95% CI: 10.66–21.93) experienced a LIPI).

The observed prevalence of SIPI (39.1%) aligns closely with previous sub-national reports from Northwestern Ethiopia (43.4%) [30] and other developing regions of the country (46.0%) [33]. This consistency across regional datasets may be attributed to shared regional socioeconomic challenges, deeply rooted cultural norms around large family sizes, and persistent systemic barriers in accessing comprehensive reproductive healthcare and postpartum family planning resources.

When compared internationally, our observed SIPI rate was notably higher than findings reported in the Uganda (33.0%) ]38], the Democratic Republic of the Congo (30.2%) [39], Iran (28.5%) [44], the Asian Pacific region (33.8%) [45], Brazil (17.1%) [35], and Burkina Faso (23.1%) [46]. Conversely, our prevalence was lower than rates observed in studies from Uganda (52.5%) [25], Ghana (49.7%) [9] and Sudan (60.6%). These wide variations across different countries likely stem from structural differences in national health policies, varying levels of female empowerment and educational attainment, and differences in the strength of local implementation of postpartum contraceptive counseling.

On the other hand, this study found that 15.6% (95% CI: 10.6, 21.9) of pregnant women in Ethiopia experienced LIPIs. This prevalence is lower than the 34.1% reported in Brazil [35] but higher than the 7% observed in Southern Ethiopia [47]. Possible reasons for these differences include variations in healthcare access, cultural practices regarding family planning, and socioeconomic factors influencing women’s reproductive choices. Additionally, in regions with high LBPI rates, there may be a lack of awareness about the implications of extended intervals on maternal and child health. While LIPIs can increase the risk of complications such as obstructed labor, hypertensive disorders, malposition and fetal malpresentation, infections, maternal and fetal hospitalization, and preterm birth, there is a notable scarcity of studies assessing the prevalence of LIPIs in Ethiopia and other countries [14]. This highlights a critical gap in the literature that needs to be addressed to better understand the implications of LIPIs on maternal and child health outcomes.

Determinants short interpregnancy intervals

According to this study, women in the poor and middle wealth categories were associated with lower odds of SIPIs compared to women from the poorest wealth category. This finding is consistent with studies conducted in Ethiopia [27, 30], Ghana [9], other SSA countries [37] and India [40] countries, suggesting that economic factors play a significant role in reproductive health behaviors. Women with better economic resources may have improved access to healthcare, family planning services, and education, which can promote optimal birth spacing. The practical implication of this finding underscores the importance of addressing socioeconomic disparities in maternal health initiatives. Targeted interventions that focus on empowering women in poorer socioeconomic groups—such as enhancing access to family planning, education, and healthcare—could help reduce the prevalence of SIPIs. Additionally, integrating economic support programs, such as microfinance or educational scholarships, may further contribute to improved reproductive health outcomes in these vulnerable populations.

In addition, the survival of the index child was associated with a reduced risk of SIPIs, a finding supported by studies conducted in Ghana [9], Bangladesh [29, 48], Ethiopia [49], Uganda [38], India [40], and SSA [50]. This suggests that when mothers perceive their children as healthy and surviving, they may be more likely to take time before having subsequent pregnancies. The emotional and psychological factors related to child survival likely encourage a focus on the well-being of existing children, leading to more thoughtful family planning decisions. The practical implication of this finding emphasizes the need for comprehensive maternal and child health programs that not only focus on birth spacing but also enhance child survival rates. By investing in child health and ensuring that mothers have access to necessary resources and support, healthcare providers can potentially influence reproductive choices positively. Promoting awareness about the importance of both child survival and optimal birth spacing can lead to healthier family outcomes and better maternal practices across communities.

In contrast, this study found that women who did not possess a card with the required language during the interview were more likely to experience SIPIs compared to those who could not read at all. This suggests that the lack of effective communication tools, such as health cards available in an accessible language, hinders women’s ability to understand and utilize family planning resources. Efficient communication is crucial for empowering women with the knowledge needed to make informed reproductive choices. The practical implication of this finding highlights the necessity for health programs to focus on language accessibility and clear communication strategies. Providing educational materials and health resources in multiple languages and formats could enhance understanding among women, particularly those with limited literacy. By improving access to information, healthcare initiatives can better support women in making informed decisions about their reproductive health, ultimately contributing to the reduction of short birth intervals and promoting better maternal and child health outcomes.

Determinants of long interpregnancy intervals

In this study, it was observed that as maternal age increased, the prevalence of LIPIs also increased. This finding aligns with studies conducted in Bangladesh [29], SSA [50], Brazil [35], Ethiopia [31], and India [40]. While older women may intentionally delay childbearing due to health considerations, expanding household responsibilities, or a desire to prioritize the wellness of existing children, underlying biological mechanisms are likely major contributors to this trend. With advancing maternal age, there is a well-documented physiological decline in fertility, characterized by a reduction in both ovarian reserve and oocyte quality. Consequently, older women often experience a longer time-to-pregnancy (secondary subfertility) or a higher incidence of unrecognized early spontaneous miscarriages, both of which naturally and involuntarily prolong the interval between consecutive viable pregnancies. The practical implication of this trend underscores the need for tailored reproductive health interventions that combine age-specific contraceptive counseling with clinical awareness of the unique biological and obstetric risks faced by older multi-parous women.

Additionally, women who completed primary education were associated with lower odds of experiencing LIPIs, supported by research in Tanzania [51], SSA [37], and the DRC [39]. Improved education often correlates with greater awareness of reproductive health, increased access to family planning resources, and enhanced decision-making capabilities regarding childbirth. This indicates the necessity of investing in educational programs that empower young girls and women, as increased educational attainment can lead to healthier reproductive outcomes and reduced LIPIs.

Furthermore, this study found that multiparity was associated with lower odds of LIPIs, consistent with findings from Ghana [9] and Brazil [35]. Women who have experienced multiple births may be more familiar with the demands of pregnancy and childbirth, leading to different reproductive choices over time. The practical implication here is the importance of providing comprehensive support and healthcare services to multiparous women, ensuring they receive the necessary education and resources to make informed family planning decisions.

Lastly, the literacy level of women—categorized as able to read parts of a sentence, able to read a whole sentence, and not having a card with the required language—was associated with higher odds of LIPIs [9, 37, 50]. This finding is consistent with studies conducted in Ethiopia, SSA, and Ghana, indicating that effective communication and comprehension are critical for utilizing reproductive health services. The practical implication is that healthcare programs should focus on improving literacy and ensuring health resources are available in accessible languages to enhance women’s understanding of family planning options, ultimately reducing the prevalence of LIPIs and improving maternal health outcomes.

Strengths and limitations of the study

A significant strength of the analysis is the use of multinomial logistic regression, adjusted for complex survey design, which enhances the reliability and national representativeness of the findings. However, several limitations must be noted. First, the cross-sectional design prevents establishing causal relationships. Second, reliance on self-reported data introduces potential recall bias regarding exact pregnancy dates, and unmeasured confounders may still influence reproductive behaviors. Third, due to the data constraints of the 2019 mini-DHS dataset, key variables such as maternal medical conditions, husbands’ education, women’s employment, and fertility desire could not be evaluated, limiting our capacity for detailed subgroup analyses.

Additionally, secondary survey data carries inherent selection risks; potential volunteer and non-response biases may exist from individuals who declined participation or were unavailable. Although the strict application of sampling weights minimizes this bias, it remains a limitation. Furthermore, small sample sizes within specific sub-categories (e.g., high-parity or literacy) led to wide confidence intervals for certain variables. While this lower statistical precision requires caution when interpreting the exact magnitude of the relative risk ratios, the clinical direction of the associations remains valid and meaningful. Finally, because the data were collected in 2019, they may not capture recent post-survey shifts in healthcare policies, social norms, or economic conditions.

Conclusion

Short birth to pregnancy intervals were prevalent among a significant portion of pregnant women and were closely linked to factors such as literacy, socioeconomic status, and the survival of previous children. In contrast, long birth to pregnancy intervals were more common among older women and those with lower literacy levels, highlighting an increased risk associated with advancing age and educational attainment. These patterns indicate the need for targeted interventions to address the underlying factors that contribute to short and long birth intervals, ultimately promoting healthier reproductive outcomes.
