Atractylenolide III Alleviates the Lipid Metabolic Disorders in Ovariectomy‐Induced Estrogen‐Deficient Mice Through Repairing Intestinal Inflammation and Microenvironment
Authors: Xu Yanyan, Qu Yanyu, Geng Qiao, Gu Jiabo, Kong Desong, Jin Heiying
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo investigate the mechanism by which Atractylenolide III (ATIII) alleviates ovariectomy‐induced, estrogen‐deficient lipid metabolism disorders through the repair of intestinal inflammation and the barrier microenvironment.MethodsFemale C57BL/six mice (8 weeks old) were randomly assigned to three groups: a blank control group (Con, n = 10), a sham surgery group (Sham, n = 10), and an ovariectomized (OVX) group (n = 70). The OVX group was further subdivided into a model group (OVX + HFD), low‐ and high‐dose ATIII groups (ATIII‐L, ATIII‐H), an estradiol (E2) group, and groups receiving fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) from the blank control, model, or high‐dose ATIII donors. After 60 days on a high‐calorie diet, treatments were administered for 28 consecutive days. Serum, liver, and intestinal tissues, and cecal contents were collected from six randomly selected mice per group. Body weight was monitored; hepatic and colonic morphology was assessed by H&E staining; serum lipid profiles were determined using an automated biochemical analyzer; ELISA quantified estradiol and inflammatory cytokine levels; expression of colonic barrier‐related proteins was evaluated by Western blot; and gut microbiota composition was analyzed via 16S rRNA sequencing.ResultsUnder conditions of estrogen deficiency, a high‐calorie diet mimicking modern human intake predisposed mice to significant weight gain (p < 0.05) and dyslipidemia, accompanied by a spectrum of pathological alterations including intestinal barrier dysfunction (evidenced by downregulated tight junction proteins), systemic inflammation (reflected by elevated pro‐inflammatory cytokines), hepatic steatosis, colonic inflammatory damage, and gut microbiota dysbiosis. ATIII intervention effectively mitigated these abnormalities, as demonstrated by reduced body weight, improved lipid profiles, repaired hepatic and colonic injuries, upregulated intestinal barrier proteins, downregulated inflammatory cytokines, a tendency toward elevated estrogen levels, and enhanced gut microbial diversity.ConclusionsATIII ameliorates ovariectomy‐induced estrogen‐deficient dyslipidemia by repairing intestinal barrier function and modulating intestinal inflammation. Concurrently, it exerts beneficial effects on estrogen levels and gut microbiota composition, in which the gut microbiota plays a mediating role. The experiment demonstrated that the active ingredients of traditional Chinese medicine hold significant value in treating lipid metabolism disorders in perimenopausal women, and there is potential for further in‐depth research into the mechanism by which they enhance efficacy through modulating gut microbiota.Keywords: Atractylenolide III, gut microbiota, intestinal barrier, lipid metabolism, perimenopause

Introduction

Perimenopause represents a critical transitional phase in female reproductive aging, marked by declining ovarian function and fluctuating estrogen levels, often leading to autonomic and metabolic abnormalities. Symptoms such as menstrual disorders, low bone density, hot flashes, sweating, and anxiety are known as ovariectomy‐induced estrogen‐deficient syndrome [1, 2]. Epidemiological surveys reveal a dyslipidemia incidence as high as 69.9% in this population, underscoring the clinical significance of lipid metabolism monitoring and management in postmenopausal and hypoestrogenic women [3, 4]. Although hormone replacement therapy (HRT) can effectively mitigate clinical symptoms and modulate lipid profiles, its long‐term utility is often hampered by suboptimal patient adherence, driven by concerns regarding breast cancer risk and drug‐induced hepatotoxicity [5]. Moreover, the statins and fibrates commonly used in clinical practice also have certain limitations [6, 7]. Consequently, elucidating novel intervention strategies is essential to enhance therapeutic outcomes for estrogen‐deficiency‐associated dyslipidemia and ultimately augment patient quality of life.

Emerging evidence has underscored the pharmacological potential of Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) in modulating metabolic syndrome. Shenling Baizhu Powder (SLBZP), a classic spleen‐invigorating formula, has been clinically validated to significantly reduce serum levels of triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol (TC), and low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐c) in patients [8, 9, 10]. The herb Atractylodes macrocephala (known as Jun, or sovereign herb), which enters the Spleen and Stomach meridians, may help improve abnormal lipid metabolism by regulating the digestive function of the spleen and stomach [11]. Notably, Atractylenolide III (ATIII), as one of the main active ingredients of Atractylodes macrocephalus, has been confirmed to have pharmacological activities such as anti‐inflammatory, regulating intestinal homeostasis, and intervening in abnormal fat metabolism. However, most of the existing studies focus on non‐ovariectomy‐induced estrogen‐deficient models or single mechanisms [12, 13, 14]. Consequently, the potential interplay between estrogen deficiency and the intestinal microenvironment within the physiological context of perimenopause remains largely unelucidated.

This study aimed to investigate whether Atractylenolide III (ATIII) can ameliorate intestinal inflammation and barrier dysfunction, thereby alleviating ovariectomy‐induced estrogen‐deficient dyslipidemia. By employing a murine model of estrogen‐deficient dyslipidemia, we sought to elucidate the mechanisms underlying ATIII's regulatory effects on lipid metabolism, with a specific focus on the gut‐liver axis. Distinguishing it from previous investigations, this study offers a novel preliminary exploration into the mechanistic link between hypoestrogenism and intestinal microenvironmental disruption. Our findings not only expand the current understanding of perimenopausal metabolic disorders but also provide a theoretical foundation and potential targets for future therapeutic interventions.

Materials and Methods

Model Preparation and Grouping

Ninety 8‐week‐old female C57BL/six mice (body weight: 18–22 g) were obtained from the SPF Animal Center of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine (ethics number: ACU240502).

All mice were acclimated under controlled conditions (temperature: 22°C ± 2°C; 12 h light/dark cycle) and fed standard chow (10% fat, 70% carbohydrates, 20% protein by energy) with free access to drinking water for 7 days. Subsequently, the mice were randomly divided into three groups: Blank control group (Con, n = 10), without any surgery; the sham operation group (Sham, n = 10), the surgical procedure of mice was the same as oophorectomy, but no ovarian resection, only adipose tissue of the same size next to the ovaries. Mice in the oophorectomy group (OVX, n = 70) underwent bilateral oophorectomy. From postoperative Day 3, daily vaginal smears were performed for five consecutive days, and microscopic examination showed regular estrous cycle changes between the blank control group and the sham operation group. In addition, there was no obvious change in the cells of the ovariectomized mice. Simulated ovariectomy‐induced estrogen‐deficient modeling was successful [15, 16, 17]. After successful ovariectomy‐induced estrogen‐deficient modeling, the OVX and Sham groups were switched to the D12492 diet that simulated modern dietary structure (D12492 60% kcal from lard; Research Diets Inc., USA, fat for energy 60%, carbohydrates 20%, protein 20%) for 60 days. The blank control group was maintained with normal feed for 60 days. All mice drink freely during rearing. Compared with the Con group, the OVX + HFD group had increased serum total cholesterol (TC), triglyceride (TG) and low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C) (p < 0.01), and HE staining showed that the hepatocyte steatosis area in the OVX + HFD group was enlarged, and the body weight increase in the OVX + HFD group was > 25% higher than that in the Con group (p < 0.01), suggesting that the Dyslipidemia model in menopausal estrogen levels was successfully established.

Administration

The ovariectomized (OVX) mice (n = 70) were further randomly divided into seven groups (n = 10 per group): the model group (OVX + HFD), the low‐dose Atractylenolide III group (OVX + ATIII‐L), the high‐dose Atractylenolide III group (OVX + ATIII‐H), the estradiol group (OVX + E2), the group receiving fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) from the blank control group (OVX + Con‐FMT), the group receiving FMT from the model group, and the group receiving FMT from the high‐dose ATIII group (OVX + ATIII‐H‐FMT). The OVX + HFD group was administered 10 mL/kg of normal saline daily. The OVX + E2 group received 0.13 mg/kg of estradiol daily. The OVX + ATIII‐L and OVX + ATIII‐H groups received daily doses of 10 mg/kg and 20 mg/kg Atractylenolide III solution, respectively. The three FMT groups were administered 20 mg/kg of fecal microbiota suspension prepared from the Con, OVX + HFD, and OVX + ATIII‐H donor groups, respectively. All treatments were delivered via intragastric gavage once daily for 28 consecutive days [13, 14, 18]. The Con and Sham groups were given normal saline at a dose of 10 mL/kg via gavage once daily for 28 consecutive days. The feeding regimen was maintained throughout the administration period.

General Observation

Throughout the experimental duration, general parameters including mice mobility, mental status, food and water intake, physical appearance, body temperature, respiration, urine, and feces were monitored and documented daily. Body weight was recorded weekly for dose adjustment based on the most recent measurement.

Determination of Serum Biochemical and Inflammatory Indicators

Blood was collected from the orbital venous plexus of mice before and after administration in ordinary blood collection tubes, and the serum was separated by centrifugation (4°C, 3000 r/min, 10 min) after standing for 2 h and stored in a −80°C refrigerator. Prior to analysis, samples were thawed and assayed for TG, TC, LDL‐C (AU5800 series automatic biochemical analyzer, Beckman), estrogen (E2), endotoxin (LPS), TNF‐α, IL‐6, and IL‐1β (Wuhan Eliretech Biotechnology Co. Ltd., batch numbers are E‐EL‐0155, E‐EL‐L059M, E‐EL‐M0049, E‐EL‐M0044, E‐EL‐M0037, respectively).

Intestinal and Liver Pathological Examination

Before dosing, the liver and intestinal tissues of three mice were randomly selected from the Con group, Sham group, and OVX group, and their liver and intestinal tissues were rapidly removed after being sacrificed by cervical dislocation. After the end of the dosing cycle, the remaining mice are sacrificed in the same manner, and the liver and intestine are harvested. The collected liver and intestinal tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution, and the ethanol was dehydrated step by step after pruning, and the lumps were embedded in paraffin and sectioned. After hematoxylin–eosin (HE) staining, the morphological characteristics of liver tissue and colon mucosa, the infiltration density of inflammatory cells, the degree of pathological damage, and the morphological abnormality of glands were systematically evaluated by HE staining.

Detection of Intestinal Barrier‐Related Proteins

Total protein concentration in tissue supernatants was quantified using the BCA assay. Primary antibodies Claudin‐1 (Proteintech, USA), Occludin (Abcam, UK), and ZO‐1 (Proteintech, USA) were incubated overnight at 4°C; add goat anti‐rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:2500) and incubate at 37°C for 1 h. Developing, imaging under a gel imager, and semi‐quantitative analysis of protein bands were performed using Image J software, and the expression level of the target protein was expressed as the ratio of the target protein to Tubulin.

16S rRNA Sequencing of Gut Microbiota

Fecal samples were collected from all mice 24 h after the final gavage, rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C until processing. Total DNA was extracted from fecal samples using the GeneJET Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific; catalog number K0721). DNA concentration was quantified by the NanoDrop1000 spectrophotometer, and extraction quality was assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. Primers 338F and 806R were used to amplify the V3–V4 region of the sample by PCR, purify and quantify, and construct a sequencing library. The representative sequences of OTUs were compared with the template sequences in the Greengenes database (Release 13.8) to obtain the number of microflora of all OTUs at the taxonomic levels of phylum, class, order, family, genus, and species. The fecal intestinal microbiota was analyzed by α diversity analysis, β diversity analysis, and the Wilcoxon rank sum test. The Kruskal‐Wallis test was used to analyze the relative abundance of differential intestinal microbiota and the correlation of each index.

Statistical Methods

The sample size was calculated using the resource equation method combined with references to previous literature, taking into account the number of groups and the required error degrees of freedom. Initially, 10 mice were included per group to account for potential operational attrition and sample quality variation. From each group, six mice were randomly selected for final endpoint analysis, and all statistical outcomes are based on these experimental samples (n = 6). Continuous data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD). Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 24.0 software. Intergroup comparisons were performed with one‐way ANOVA, followed by t tests where applicable. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

To investigate estrogen's effect of estrogen level changes on various indexes in ovariectomy‐induced estrogen‐deficient mice, histopathological examination, serum index detection and protein index analysis were performed after 28 days of continuous administration of estradiol (Figure 1A). In terms of body weight, there was no significant difference in body weight between the Sham group and the Con group (p > 0.05), and the weight of the OVX + HFD group increased significantly (p < 0.05). Estradiol treatment significantly reduced the body weight of mice (p > 0.05) (Figure 1B). Meanwhile, the results showed that there was no significant difference in the Sham group (p > 0.05) compared with the Con group, while in the OVX group were significantly increased (p < 0.01). Compared with the OVX + HFD group, the estrogen level in the OVX + E2 group was significantly higher (p < 0.05) (Figure 1C). HE staining showed (Figure 1D) that the hepatocytes of mice in the Sham group were neatly arranged, while the hepatocytes in the OVX + HFD group showed significant swelling and granular degeneration. After estradiol treatment, hepatocytes are more orderly. In terms of colon tissue, the structure of colon tissue in the Sham group was basically normal, while the OVX + HFD group showed significant pathological changes, including villus fragmentation and inflammatory infiltration. After estradiol treatment, the colon structure was improved, and the inflammatory infiltration was reduced. In terms of lipid metabolism, compared with the Con group, the levels of LDL‐c, TG, and TC in the OVX + HFD group were significantly increased (p < 0.05), while the expression levels of LDL‐c, TG, and TC were significantly decreased by estradiol treatment (p < 0.05) (Figure 1E–G). In addition, compared with the Con group, the levels of IL‐6, IL‐1β, TNF‐α, and LPS in the OVX + HFD group were significantly increased (p < 0.05), while the levels of inflammation‐related indicators were reduced by estradiol treatment (p < 0.05) (Figure 1H–K). Compared with the Con group, the expression of Claudin‐1, Occludin, and ZO‐1 in the OVX + HFD group was significantly decreased (p < 0.05), while the expression of these colonic barrier‐related proteins was increased by estradiol treatment, and the difference was statistically significant (p < 0.05) (Figure 1L).

To evaluate Atractylenolide III's (ATIII) regulatory effects on ovariectomy‐induced estrogen‐deficient lipid metabolism in ovariectomy‐induced estrogen‐deficient mice, histopathological and serum markers were detected in mice after 28 days of continuous administration of Atractylenolide III (Figure 2A). The body weight data of mice showed that the weight loss of mice after Atractylenolide III treatment was statistically significant, especially in the high‐dose group (p < 0.05) (Figure 2B). The results showed that compared with the OVX + HFD group, the estrogen levels were significantly increased in both the OVX + ATIII‐L group and the high‐dose group (p < 0.05) (Figure 2C). HE staining analysis showed that (Figure 2D) showed that although fat vacuoles and inflammation were seen in the OVX + ATIII‐L group, the degree was reduced compared with that in the OVX + HFD group, while the number of lipotic hepatocytes and the number of fat vacuoles were significantly reduced in the OVX + ATIII‐H group, and the inflammation was also improved. Regarding colonic morphology, both the low‐dose and high‐dose groups demonstrated relatively intact villous architecture and orderly crypts, along with an absence of significant inflammatory infiltration. In addition, compared with the OVX + HFD group, the levels of LDL‐c, TG, and TC were significantly reduced in the low‐ and high‐dose Atractylenolide III groups (p < 0.05, p < 0.001) (Figure 2E–G).

Compared with the OVX + HFD group, the levels of IL‐6, IL‐1β, and TNF‐α in serum were significantly reduced by Atractylenolide III treatment, and the differences were statistically significant (p < 0.05, p < 0.001) (Figure 3A–C). In addition, the expression of LPS was statistically significant in both the low‐dose and high‐dose groups of Atractylenolide III (p < 0.05, p < 0.001) (Figure 3D). At the same time, Western blot analysis showed that the expressions of intestinal barrier stabilizers Claudin‐1, Occludin, and ZO‐1 were increased in both the low‐ and high‐dose groups of Atractylenolide III (p < 0.05, p < 0.001) (Figure 3E).

Based on the species abundance matrix, principal coordinate analysis (PCO) was performed to evaluate the differences in gut microbial communities at the OTU level among fecal samples from each group. The results showed distinct separation of colonic microbial communities before modeling, after modeling, and post‐treatment, with good intra‐group sample clustering, indicating high structural similarity within groups (Figure 4A). This demonstrates that ovariectomy combined with a high‐fat diet significantly altered the composition of the gut microbiota in mice. Compared with the positive drug group, both low‐ and high‐dose Atractylenolide III treatments maintained the homeostasis of the gut microbiota. Heatmap analysis at the genus level further revealed the distribution trends and relative differences in species abundance among groups. After administration, the relative abundance of Faecalibaculum decreased significantly, while that of taxa such as norank_f_Clostridia increased markedly (Figure 4B,C). Linear discriminant analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) was used to identify differentially abundant taxa between groups, revealing that the model group was significantly enriched in taxa such as Erysipelotrichales, Erysipelotrichaceae, and Dubosiella (Figure 4D). Erysipelotrichales is a Gram‐positive bacterial order involved in food digestion and nutrient absorption, particularly associated with obesity and metabolic diseases. Erysipelotrichaceae, a family within this order, plays an important role in the gut ecosystem by participating in carbohydrate metabolism and energy provision. Dubosiella, belonging to the Erysipelotrichaceae family, may be linked to host health status and immune regulation.

Based on experimental observations that interventions were associated with restored estrogen levels and beneficial shifts in gut microbiota composition, we employed fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) from control or ATIII‐treated donors to examine whether gut microbiota plays a mediating role in the lipid metabolic abnormalities of ovariectomized mice. Mice receiving FMT from blank control (OVX + Con‐FMT) or high‐dose ATIII‐treated donors (OVX + ATIII‐H‐FMT) exhibited significant suppression of body weight gain (Figure 5A) and elevated serum estradiol levels (Figure 5B). Histopathological assessment revealed markedly attenuated hepatic steatosis and inflammatory infiltration, along with improved colon mucosal integrity (Figure 5C). Consistently, serum levels of pro‐inflammatory cytokines (IL‐6, IL‐1β, TNF‐α) and endotoxin (LPS) were significantly reduced in these groups (Figure 5D). Gut microbiota analysis further indicated that the microbial composition of OVX + Con‐FMT and OVX + ATIII‐H‐FMT groups shifted away from the dysbiotic model profile and resembled that of healthy controls in PCoA (Figure 5E). Genus‐level heatmap (Figure 5F) and LEfSe analysis (Figure 5G) confirmed the reversal of OVX‐induced dysbiosis, notably through reduction in inflammation‐associated genera such as Dubosiella. Collectively, these findings underscore that the gut microbiota remodeled by ATIII is sufficient to mediate key therapeutic benefits, including weight control, inflammation resolution, tissue protection, and microbial re‐balance, thereby establishing the gut microbiota as a critical effector in ATIII's mechanism of action.

Discussion

Under physiological conditions, estrogen is involved in multiple aspects of lipid metabolism regulation. Normal estrogen levels exhibit anti‐inflammatory properties, which may help reduce the accumulation of “unhealthy” fat and confer protective effects on the cardiovascular system [19, 20]. This transition accelerates the development of dyslipidemia, elevates the risk of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases as well as other chronic conditions, and represents a significant factor influencing the health of middle‐aged and older women [21, 22].

Accordingly, focusing on the features of this population, the present study utilized an ovariectomy (OVX) combined with diet‐induced estrogen‐deficient dyslipidemia mouse model to simulate the physiological state of postmenopausal women under a modern dietary pattern. Atractylenolide III (ATIII)—an active constituent of Atractylodes macrocephala (Baizhu), the principal herb in the classical formula Shenling Baizhu Powder—was selected as the therapeutic agent for systematic observation and evaluation. The study demonstrated an association between rapid estrogen decline and the onset or progression of dyslipidemia and preliminarily explored its potential key mechanism, which may be related to intestinal inflammatory alterations and barrier impairment.

In the experiments, the OVX + HFD group showed a rapid decrease in serum E2 levels, accompanied by significant weight gain, abnormal lipid metabolism, hepatic steatosis, colonic inflammatory infiltration, and disruption of intestinal barrier structure. These alterations largely recapitulate common clinical features observed in estrogen‐deficient women, such as central obesity, hyperlipidemia, and a tendency toward non‐alcoholic fatty liver disease, indicating successful model establishment [23, 24, 25]. Interventions including estrogen supplementation, ATIII treatment, and fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) each exhibited ameliorative trends, providing preliminary support for the correlation between estrogen status and these changes [26, 27, 28, 29].

The finding that ATIII produced effects comparable to those of E2 raises the possibility that ATIII may act, at least in part, through pathways typically regulated by estrogen signaling [30, 31]. One plausible mechanism is that ATIII, potentially mediated via the gut microbiota, alleviates intestinal inflammation and repairs the intestinal barrier, thereby restoring a microenvironment favorable for the function of estrogen receptors and related pathways. In this study, colon tissues from the OVX + HFD group displayed pathological injury, with marked downregulation of the tight‐junction proteins Claudin‐1, Occludin, and ZO‐1. The compromised barrier integrity may facilitate the translocation of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and harmful gut bacteria into the systemic circulation, thereby triggering systemic inflammatory responses [32]. ATIII intervention significantly improved gut microbiota structure, attenuated inflammatory manifestations, and upregulated the expression of key intestinal tight‐junction proteins. Mice receiving FMT also exhibited improved metabolic parameters, reduced inflammation, and restored gut microbial balance, suggesting that the gut microbiota plays an active mediating role in the therapeutic effects of ATIII [33, 34]. Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms through which the gut microbial environment exerts this mediating effect require further in‐depth investigation.

This work illustrates the potential value of investigating traditional Chinese medicine for managing complex, multifactorial conditions such as perimenopausal metabolic syndrome, providing novel mechanistic insights that connect hormonal changes, gut ecology, and systemic metabolism. It highlights the therapeutic promise of targeting the intestinal microenvironment and paves the way for future microbiome‐based interventions in this population.
