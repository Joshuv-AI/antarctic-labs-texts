Vagus nerve stimulation combined with ketogenic diet versus ketogenic diet alone in the treatment of febrile infection-related epilepsy syndrome in children: efficacy and long-term prognosis study
Authors: Tianshuang Wang, Xiaotian Chen, Renqing Zhu, Yixue Wang, Xiaoyan Gong, Lifei Yu, Yi Wang, Yuanfeng Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the clinical efficacy and long-term functional prognosis of vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) combined with ketogenic diet (KD) in the treatment of children with febrile infection-related epilepsy syndrome (FIRES).MethodsA retrospective analysis was performed on inpatients with a diagnosis of FIRES. They were treated with either VNS + KD (with an interval < 5 days) or KD alone, in addition to standard therapy.ResultsEighteen children (nine females, nine males) with a median onset age of 8.1 years were studied. Before KD/VNS treatment, the average number of seizures (AS) per hour was 3.0 and the percentage of total duration of electrographic seizures (ESD) per hour was 65.5%. Median duration of anesthesia, duration of mechanical ventilation, and duration in ICU and hospital were 58.5, 39.5, 65.5, and 74.5 days, respectively. Four children received VNS + KD, and 14 received KD alone. The median time to intervention was 16.5 days after onset, and the median follow-up duration was 7.1 years. The VNS + KD group showed a significant reduction in average seizure frequency per hour (P = 0.002). However, no significant differences were observed in ESD or duration of status epilepticus (SE) (P > 0.05). And there were no significant differences between the groups in terms of duration of anesthesia, duration of mechanical ventilation, and duration in ICU and hospital (P > 0.05). At the last follow-up, 55.6% had moderate to severe disabilities, and 16.7% had died. The VNS + KD group exhibited significantly better functional outcomes as measured by the modified Rankin scale (P = 0.002). Younger age of onset was associated with increased odds of a poor outcome (P = 0.045).ConclusionFIRES patients experienced severe seizure burdens in the acute phase and had poor long-term prognoses. Combining VNS and KD treatments in the acute phase may represent a viable therapeutic strategy for reducing seizures and improving long-term function for pediatric FIRES.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00381-025-06957-4.Keywords: Vagus nerve stimulation, Ketogenic diet, Febrile infection-related epilepsy syndrome, Seizure burden, Functional prognosis

Introduction

Febrile infection-related epilepsy syndrome (FIRES) represents a severe epileptic condition classified as a subtype of new-onset refractory status epilepticus (NORSE). Previous studies have reported an incidence rate of approximately 1 in 1,000,000 [1, 2]. FIRES is characterized by super refractory status epilepticus (SRSE), which is generally resistant to multiple antiseizure medications (ASMs) and anesthetic agents. Status epilepticus (SE) is a condition that arises due to either a failure in the inherent mechanisms that terminate seizures or the activation of pathological processes that result in abnormally prolonged seizure activity [3]. SRSE is defined as refractory status epilepticus that persists or recurs at least 24 h after the initiation of anesthetic therapy or reappears during the reduction or withdrawal of anesthetics [4]. The recurrent or prolonged seizures lead to severe neurological impairments, significantly affecting cognitive and motor functions in pediatric patients. The duration of SE serves as a prognostic indicator, with extended durations correlating with a more severe prognosis [5]. Consequently, the early termination of SE is crucial for improving clinical outcomes in children.

The efficacy of the ketogenic diet (KD) in treating refractory epilepsy is well established, and our previous study has demonstrated that KD could effectively control SE in children with FIRES, without causing significant adverse reactions [6]. Furthermore, studies have indicated that early initiation of KD in refractory status epilepticus (RSE) and SRSE can reduce seizure frequency by 20–90% [7]. The FIRES guidelines recommend the early initiation of KD within the acute phase, preferably within the first week [8]. In addition to KD, vagus nerve stimulation (VNS), as a neuromodulation technique, has been employed for the treatment of refractory epilepsy. We previously reported a case of FIRES in which ASMs and KD were ineffective, but seizures were controlled after a few days following VNS implant [9]. Other studies also suggested that VNS could mitigate the severity of NORSE episodes. Consequently, it prompts the question of whether VNS combined with KD can offer superior seizure control and enhance long-term prognosis. This retrospective study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of VNS combined with KD in managing acute seizures and long-term prognosis among children diagnosed with FIRES at our hospital.

Methods

Inclusion criteria of patients

A retrospective analysis was conducted on inpatient cases at our hospital that met the following criteria: (1) onset age ≤ 16 years; (2) clinical diagnosis of FIRES [8]; (3) treatment with either VNS combined with KD (with an interval less than 5 days) or KD alone, in addition to standard therapy; (4) availability of comprehensive clinical data; (5) follow-up for at least 6 months after discharge. Exclusion criteria included (1) previous use of KD or VNS prior to treatment and (2) presence of contraindications for KD or VNS.

Pre-intervention information

The following clinical data was collected: demographic data (gender, age), clinical manifestations (including age of onset, symptoms at onset such as fever, seizures, headache, coma, cognitive and behavioral changes), seizure types, ASMs tried in hospital, past medical history, family history, and assessments such as EEG and MRI.

Continuous electroencephalogram (cEEG) recordings were obtained using the Nicolet Video-EEG monitor. Seizure burden (SB) [10] was quantified by calculating the average number of seizures (AS) and the percentage of total duration of electrographic seizures (ESD) per hour, as well as duration of SE. The seizure burdens were calculated prior to the intervention and at 1, 3, and 7 days post intervention. The assessment of SRSE encompassed the evaluation of SB, duration of mechanical ventilation, duration of anesthesia, duration in intensive care unit (ICU), and hospitalization.

Intervention

All patients underwent a pre-KD screening to evaluate their eligibility for KD therapy and subsequently underwent a 3-day fasting period to facilitate a rapid transition into ketosis. The KD was initiated with a fat-to-combined carbohydrate and protein ratio of 4:1. Administration of the KD was through a gastric tube, accompanied by the administration of glucose-free medications. Blood glucose levels and beta-hydroxybutyrate (BHB) levels were monitored every 12 h using finger-prick testing. Additionally, serum electrolytes, body weight, and intake-output details were recorded.

Children with FIRES typically exhibit SRSE resistant to multiple ASMs and anesthetic agents. To expedite the cessation of SE, and given the evidence supporting the KD and VNS as effective treatments for FIRES, comprehensive discussions with the patients’ guardians were conducted. With informed consent obtained from the guardians for the combined KD and VNS therapy, preparations for both interventions would be initiated. Following the exclusion of any relevant contraindications, KD and VNS treatment would be commenced. The patients underwent surgical implantation of the VNS device, which was activated 24 h post-implantation. A rapid ramp-up of stimulation parameters was implemented, with initial settings as follows [9, 11, 12]: output current of 0.2–0.5 mA, pulse width of 500 µs, frequency of 30 Hz, on-time of 30 s, and off-time of 5 min. In magnet mode, the current was set 0.1–0.2 mA higher than the output current. The device parameters were subsequently adjusted based on EEG recordings and clinical seizure activities. The current was increased by 0.2–0.3 mA every 2–5 days while keeping other parameters constant; alternatively, the duty ratio was increased if the current intensity remained unchanged.

Outcome data

This study included pediatric patients with FIRES who were hospitalized in our center between 2015 and 2023, with the last follow-up conducted in August 2025.

The functional outcome was evaluated using the modified Rankin scale (mRS), measuring the extent of disability of daily living. The mRS was determined by the neurologists at the last follow-up visit. The mRS scores range from 0 to 6 with each score representing a different level of disability:

Ethics and informed consent

The data collection and analysis of this study were approved by the institutional review board of the ethics committee of Children’s Hospital of Fudan University (clinical trial number: not applicable). The parents of all participants had been provided with written informed consent for the use of the children’s information for scientific research purposes.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were reported as the mean (standard deviation (SD)) when normally distributed and the median (interquartile ranges (IQR)) when not. Normality was visually assessed through frequency histograms. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. For comparisons between two groups, 2-tailed unpaired Student’s t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests were used for continuous variables, depending on normality, and a chi-square test was employed for categorical variables. Missing data for covariates was imputed by using either the simple mean or the most frequent proportion imputation.

To evaluate the efficacy of acute-phase VNS combined with KD in controlling seizures and improving long-term outcomes in children with FIRES, and considering that baseline characteristics may influence outcome assessment, the inverse probability weighting model (teffects ipw) was employed [13]. This approach mitigates bias in this non-randomized clinical study and balances the baseline covariates (including age at onset, gender, numbers of ASMs, timing of intervention, and seizure burden prior to intervention) thereby enhancing the reliability of the statistical results. We fitted a probit model incorporating duration of mechanical ventilation and anesthesia, duration in ICU and in hospital, SE duration, as well as mRS score at the last follow-up. The average treatment effect (ATE) of the treatment on outcomes was reported by estimating the weighted regression coefficients (β) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A linear mixed-model analysis was employed to assess the effects of various treatments on repeated measures of the seizure burdens (including AS and ESD). The interaction between day and treatment was also included in the linear mixed model [14]. In this model, the ID number of subjects was treated as a random effect, while the treatment was considered fixed effect. We reported the β coefficients along with their corresponding 95% CIs derived from the linear mixed model. Logistic regression analysis was performed to evaluate the factors associated with functional outcomes with results reported as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs. A P-value < 0.05, based on a two-tailed test, was deemed statistically significant. All analyses were performed using Stata version 16.0 (Stata Corp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Clinical characteristics of children

Detailed clinical information for all 18 FIRES patients is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

A total of 18 patients (9 females, 9 males) were enrolled. The median age of onset was 8.1 years (IQR: 6.7–9.2). All patients presented with focal seizures and generalized tonic-clonic seizures (GTCS); myoclonic seizures were monitored in one patient. Despite trying multiple ASMs (with a median of 5) and anesthetics (including midazolam and propofol), seizure control was not achieved.

According to cEEG recordings, patients with FIRES had a severe seizure burden. All of them experienced frequent seizures. The median AS was 3.0 (IQR: 2.0–8.5) and median ESD was 65.5% (IQR: 41.1–89.4%) prior to VNS/KD treatment (Table 1).

All patients were admitted to ICU for treatment and ventilator support. The median duration of anesthetic agent was 58.5 days (IQR: 41.0–83.6), and they required a long period of mechanical ventilation (median 39.5 days, IQR 23.0–59.0). The median duration in ICU was 65.5 days (IQR: 44.0–95.0). The median hospitalization duration was 74.5 days (IQR: 43.0–106.0). One patient self-discharged, and two cases underwent tracheostomy due to difficulty in extubating.

In addition to intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) and high-dose steroid pulse therapy, all children were treated with KD, with a median initiation time of 16.5 days (IQR: 13.0–20.0) after disease onset. As a national-level tertiary children’s hospital and pediatric medical center, our institution is often the recipient of referrals from regional hospitals for complex cases resistant to initial treatment. In this study, nine patients were transferred to our center after initial treatment attempts at their local hospitals were unsuccessful. Their prior length of stay at those hospitals ranged from 4 to 21 days. Notably, two patients had pre-existing liver dysfunction before the initiation of the KD. Furthermore, nine patients had severe infections, mainly pulmonary infections, prior to starting KD. One of these patients also had a fungal urinary tract infection caused by Candida albicans.

Four patients received combined VNS and KD treatment. All of them were transferred from regional hospitals and presented with a high seizure burden (Table 1). VNS implantation was performed at a median of 18.0 days (IQR: 13.8–23.8 days) after disease onset, with an interval of 1–5 days between the two interventions. The VNS stimulation parameters were rapidly titrated upward. The specific parameters at the time of SE resolution are detailed in Supplementary Table 1. No significant VNS-related adverse events were observed in any of the patients.

Seizure outcomes

In contrast, no statistically significant difference in ESD was observed between the two groups (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, the duration of status epilepticus (SE) did not differ significantly between the groups (Table 1).

Long-term functional outcomes

Patients were followed up from 2.0 to 10.3 years (median 7.1 years) post-discharge. At the final follow-up, the mRS was utilized to assess long-term functional prognosis of patients. The results showed no significant disability in 22.2% (4/18), slight disability in 22.2% (4/18), moderate disability in 22.2% (4/18), moderately severe disability in 5.6% (1/18), severe disability in 11.1% (2/18) and 3 (16.7%) patients were dead (Fig. 2B). The mRS scores of the VNS + KD group were significantly better than those of the KD group (β = −0.635, 95% CI: −1.045 to approximately −0.226, P = 0.002) (Fig. 2C, Table 1, and Supplementary Table 1).

To further investigate the factors related to outcomes, we divided the patients into two groups based on their mRS scores: 44.4% (8/18) children showed good outcomes and 55.6% (10/18) had poor outcomes. There was no significant difference in outcomes based on the sex, ASMs, intervention time, duration of anesthesia, mechanical ventilation, and duration in ICU and hospital. However, logistic regression analysis showed that the younger age of onset was significantly associated with higher odds of poor outcomes (OR = 2.668, 95% CI: 1.021~6.968, P = 0.045).

Discussion

FIRES is a catastrophic epileptic encephalopathy characterized by high seizure burdens and poor prognosis

FIRES often presents as SRSE, with a poor response to ASMs and anesthetics. The recurrent and prolonged seizures might result in significant functional impairments, emphasizing the importance of early identification and SE control during the acute phase to improve prognosis. Ten continuous minutes or a total duration of ≥ 20% electrographic seizures of any 60-min period of recording is defined as electrographic status epilepticus (ESE) [15]; thus, early detection of SE may facilitate prompt diagnosis and intervention [16]. In our study, all patients experienced frequent seizures and severe seizure burdens. Prolonged duration of mechanical ventilation and ICU stay can lead to significant family and social burdens.

FIRES also has a poor prognosis, particularly in terms of seizures and neurological impairments. In our study, after a median follow-up of 7 years, all patients exhibited varying degrees of functional deficits. The mortality rate was 16.7% (3/18) and 55.6% (10/18) showed moderate to severe disabilities. According to previous reports, a multicenter retrospective study has revealed that 93% of FIRES patients experienced refractory epilepsy, with only 18% exhibiting relatively normal cognitive function. The mortality rates in FIRES cases range from 10 to 30% [10, 17–19]. Additionally, 43.9% of patients experienced severe neurological impairments [20]. Long-term follow-up studies of patients with FIRES have shown that mRS scores generally stabilize after 1 year from disease onset [17]. In this study, all children were followed for at least 2 years, and the mRS scores at the last follow-up were thus considered representative of long-term functional outcomes. However, it should be noted that the mRS provides only a broad assessment of functional status. More detailed evaluations of long-term seizure and cognitive function in FIRES patients remain for further investigation.

Poor prognosis in FIRES may be attributed to various factors, including early onset, prolonged burst-suppression coma, SRSE, non-convulsive status epilepticus, and diffuse cortical edema, as noted in previous research [18]. Therefore, early recognition and intervention are crucial in improving the prognosis of FIRES. Given relatively severe conditions of the FIRES patients in our group, we found the poor outcome was associated with early disease onset, whereas the duration of SE did not significantly differ between good and poor outcome groups.

KD could be an effective method to reducing seizures in some patients

The pathogenic mechanisms underlying FIRES are not yet fully understood. Current theories suggest that FIRES arises from a complex interplay between genetic susceptibility and immune dysregulation, often triggered by infections. This leads to excessive activation of the central nervous system (CNS) inflammatory response. Brain autopsy in FIRES cases was characterized by infiltration of innate immune-related cells [21]. Furthermore, a significant elevation of cytokines/chemokines in cerebrospinal fluid has been observed in FIRES patients, indicating CNS-specific inflammation [22]. And previous studies have also indicated that both innate and adaptive immunity might play a role in the development of the disease.

Early treatment with anti-inflammatory agents, such as anakinra or tocilizumab, has shown promising results in controlling seizures in affected children [23, 24]. Anakinra, a recombinant human interleukin-1 receptor antagonist, functions by inhibiting IL-1β-mediated neuroinflammation. Numerous studies have indicated its potential efficacy in both the acute and chronic phases of FIRES, with a significant reduction in seizure frequency. Early administration may also decrease the duration of mechanical ventilation and ICU stay in pediatric patients [2, 25, 26]. Interleukin-6 (IL-6) is a notably elevated proinflammatory cytokine in the cerebrospinal fluid of FIRES patients. Tocilizumab, which targets the IL-6 receptor, thereby impeding the inflammatory cascade, has been shown in studies to assist in controlling refractory seizures when used in conjunction with conventional immunotherapy [26]. However, careful consideration of immunosuppression-related complications is necessary [27]. Other immunosuppressants, such as azathioprine and tacrolimus, have been reported, but the evidence remains limited [5, 28]. However, anakinra is not accessible in most places due to economic or geographical constraints. Additionally, the KD has been reported to exhibit anti-seizure effects through direct anti-inflammatory mechanisms. Specifically, the β-hydroxybutyrate produced by the KD can inhibit the action of the inflammatory cytokine IL-1β [29].

The guidelines recommend initiating the KD within 1 week in FIRES cases, which should be continued for at least 3 months in patients who respond well to the KD [8, 30]. Studies have shown that KD treatment during the acute phase could reduce seizure frequency by 50–70% [31, 32] and improve cognitive function in children with FIRES, although these findings were based on small case series [7, 32–34]. However, some evidence suggested that treatment of KD at the later stage of SE (after discharge) in FIRES children was associated with poorer outcomes, which could be attributed to the more severe clinical condition of these children [17]. In our study, the median KD initiation time was 16.5 days after disease onset, which might impact outcomes. Considering this factor, the present study employed inverse probability weighting and linear mixed models to compare outcomes between the two groups, with adjustment for baseline characteristics (including the timing of intervention initiation), thereby enhancing the reliability of the findings. Given that our center is a national-level tertiary hospital, a considerable proportion of patients (50%) were transferred to our facility without having received KD during the early acute stage, which undoubtedly contributed to the delayed initiation in some cases. Additionally, the presence of relatively severe infections in some children further constrained the early application of the KD. The duration of the acute phase in children with FIRES varies widely, ranging from several days to months [35]. In this study, although most patients did not initiate the KD within the first week of the disease course, KD was started during the acute phase in all cases. The results showed a reduction in seizure burden (including AS and ESD) following KD initiation (Fig. 1), suggesting that KD had a beneficial effect on seizure control during the acute stage. Furthermore, analysis of prognostic factors in this cohort did not reveal a significant correlation between the timing of KD initiation and clinical outcomes.

The combination of VNS and KD may alleviate seizure burden and improve long-term functional outcomes in FIRES patients

VNS, an FDA-approved therapy for refractory epilepsy, has been proven to reduce the frequency and severity of seizures. Furthermore, studies also suggested its potential role in controlling acute epileptic seizures. In conditions like RSE and SRSE, VNS has been found to be effective in reducing seizures [11, 36–39]. Research indicates that VNS in the acute phase of RSE/SRSE leads to a seizure reduction in 74% of cases, with a median SE control time of 8 days (range: 3–84 days) following VNS implantation [39]. It was reported that VNS may contribute to the management of status epilepticus by desynchronizing electrical rhythms and limiting seizure propagation. Furthermore, it has the potential to reduce the duration of anesthetic exposure and improve the consciousness level of patients [40]. However, there was relatively limited research on the effectiveness of VNS in the acute period of FIRES [9, 41, 42]. Compared to other types of NOSE, FIRES patients tended to require a longer time for SE control after VNS implantation (28 days) [11]. The mechanisms of action of VNS encompass modulation of neurotransmitters, regulation of neural networks, and attenuation of neuroinflammation [11].

In this study, we aimed to combine VNS with KD to achieve improved seizure control and favorable prognosis for children with FIRES. After thorough communications with the guardians, VNS + KD treatment was administered, taking into account the clinical conditions. The results indicated a significant reduction in seizure frequency in the VNS + KD group compared to the KD alone group. However, there was no significant difference in the ESD and SE duration between the two groups. The negative results may be due to the limited sample size; therefore, more cases or prospective controlled studies may be needed to further confirm the effectiveness of VNS + KD. There is currently no consensus regarding the optimal timing for VNS therapy, appropriate stimulation parameters, or the identification of ideal candidates for VNS, as interindividual differences may substantially influence treatment outcomes [43]. In terms of functional prognosis, patients in the VNS + KD group achieved better neurological outcomes at the final follow-up. On the one hand, VNS and KD exert anti-seizure effects through different mechanisms of action during the acute phase [11, 29, 44]. On the other hand, VNS could also modulate the seizure and cognitive abilities during the recovery phase, leading to better neurofunctional outcomes [40, 43, 45].

Our study suggested that the combination of VNS and KD during the acute phase of FIRES might represent a viable therapeutic strategy to improve seizure control and functional outcomes. Although current clinical guidelines do not recommend VNS as a first-line treatment, it could be considered earlier in the acute phase for FIRES patients with high seizure burden [43]. Despite the limited sample size of this study, it is important to note that prospective randomized controlled trials are particularly challenging in the pediatric population due to the rarity and severity of the syndrome. Therefore, further studies with larger cohorts and more robust clinical designs are warranted to better evaluate the efficacy of combined VNS and KD therapy.

Conclusions

FIRES patients often experience SRSE during the acute phase and have a poor long-term prognosis. For patients unable to access anakinra due to economic or geographical constraints, treatment with VNS combined with KD during the acute phase represents a potential therapeutic alternative. Preliminary evidence suggested that it might contribute to reduced seizure frequency in the acute stage and possibly improved long-term functional outcomes, offering a complementary strategy to enhance the overall management of FIRES.
