On placental and lactational transfer of IgG‐based therapeutic proteins – Current understanding and knowledge gaps from a clinical pharmacology perspective
Authors: Daphne Guinn, Katherine Kratz, Kristie Baisden, Sarah Ridge, Sonaly McClymont, Elimika Pfuma Fletcher, Tamara Johnson, Yow‐Ming Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMaternal medication use may expose the developing fetus through placental transfer or the infant through lactational transfer. Because pregnant and lactating individuals have been historically excluded from early drug development trials, there is often limited to no human data available to inform pharmacokinetics (PK) and safety in these populations at the time of drug approval. We describe the known mechanisms of placental or lactational transfer of IgG‐based therapeutic proteins and use clinical examples to highlight the potential for fetal or infant exposure during pregnancy and lactation. Placental transfer of IgG‐based therapeutic proteins may result in systemic exposure to the developing fetus. A lactational transfer may be associated with local gastrointestinal (GI) exposure in the infant and may also result in systemic exposure, although data are very limited as proteins have shown instability in the GI tract. Understanding of PK and pharmacodynamic (PD) effects of IgG‐based therapeutic proteins in infants exposed in utero as well as the potential exposure through human milk and its clinical implications is critical for developing treatment strategies for pregnant or lactating individuals. We share the current knowledge gaps and considerations for future evaluations to inform PK, PD, and the safety of IgG‐based therapeutic proteins for safe use during pregnancy and lactation. With the increasing use of IgG‐based therapeutic proteins in treating chronic diseases during pregnancy and lactation, there is a need to improve the quantity and quality of data to inform the safe use in pregnant and lactating individuals.

INTRODUCTION

The management of chronic disease during pregnancy and lactation has evolved over recent years to focus on disease control with continued medication therapy to maintain maternal health while seeking healthy infant outcomes. 1 , 2 The developing fetus or infant may be exposed when medications are taken by a pregnant or lactating individual through placental or lactational transfer, respectively. Pregnant and lactating individuals have been historically excluded from early drug development trials, leading to no or limited available human data at the time of drug approval to inform pharmacokinetics (PK) and safety in these populations. Post‐approval pregnancy studies, when performed, commonly focus on the safety of medications related to the risk of major congenital malformations or other adverse maternal or fetal outcomes. 3 , 4 However, there are considerable data gaps related to changes in PK during pregnancy and the potential implication of dosing in pregnant individuals. Historically, post‐approval clinical lactation studies have not been performed. When they are performed, they may only estimate infant exposure via human milk (i.e., milk‐only studies), leaving a knowledge gap regarding the effects of the medication on the breastfed infant and their systemic exposure to therapeutic proteins. The majority of available data are from post‐approval pregnancy and lactation studies for drugs used to treat conditions that are common in females of reproductive age and have high utilization rates. 3 Thus, many literature examples discussed in this manuscript are studies in conditions such as inflammatory bowel disease (IBD).

Overall, there is a paucity of PK data available related to drug use during pregnancy and lactation. For immunoglobulin G (IgG)‐based therapeutic proteins, the data are especially scant with published data generally limited to a small number of monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) that have been prescribed in the last two decades. Additionally, based on an FDA internal review of 157 therapeutic proteins approved between 1960 and 2020, only 15 of 105 mAbs had available clinical PK data reporting concentrations in human milk (unpublished data).

In this review, we aim to (1) describe what is known regarding the mechanisms of placental and lactational transfer of therapeutic proteins, and (2) highlight the potential for fetal or infant exposure and some of the observed effects on infants that are associated with maternal use of IgG‐based therapeutic proteins. We will also describe current knowledge gaps and emphasize the need for future evaluation to inform PK and the safety of IgG‐based therapeutic proteins in pregnant and lactating individuals and their infants. This is not a systematic review of published literature; instead, relevant published clinical examples are used to illustrate the data gaps and considerations for collecting additional data.

MECHANISMS OF PLACENTAL TRANSFER FROM THE PREGNANT INDIVIDUAL TO FETUS OF ANTIBODIES AND THERAPEUTIC PROTEINS

To assist in understanding the mechanisms of the placental transfer of IgG‐based therapeutic proteins, we reviewed the physiological process involved in the placental transfer of nutrients, solutes, and antibodies from the pregnant individual to the fetus that are necessary for fetal health and development. 5 Placental transfer involves passive and active transport mechanisms. Examples of passive transfer mechanisms include solvent drag, which allows for the movement of water‐containing solutes and nutrients, and simple diffusion, which involves concentration or electrical gradients and often allows for the movement of medications with a molecular weight of <600 Da. In terms of active transport mechanisms, transcellular transfer uses transport proteins in the microvilli or basal membrane of the placental syncytiotrophoblast, a multinucleate epithelial layer that is in direct contact with the maternal and fetal circulation. 6 Transcellular transfer includes a variety of mechanisms, such as channel transport, facilitated diffusion, carrier‐mediated active diffusion, endocytosis, and exocytosis. Endocytosis begins with fluid phase uptake and plasma membrane invagination to form a vesicle containing solutes and soluble macromolecules. Depending on the characteristics of the cargo in the vesicle, this transfer process may or may not be mediated by receptors. Upon transcellular transfer through the syncytiotrophoblast, the cargo must then traverse the fetal capillary endothelium to cross from the maternal to the fetal blood (Figure 1). 6 , 7 , 8

IgG is generally considered the only antibody class that is substantially transferred through the placenta. The placental transfer of endogenous IgG antibodies to the fetus through endocytosis mechanisms in the syncytiotrophoblast confers passive immunity to the fetus. The neonatal fragment crystallizable receptor (FcRn) expressed on the syncytiotrophoblast and fetal capillary endothelium plays a role in the placental transport of IgG. 9 In the ex vivo perfused placenta, IgG can bind with a high affinity to FcRn in the acidic pH of the endosome but is unable to bind at the cell surface at a physiological pH. Broadly, the transcytosis process of IgG can be described as involving three steps: (1) IgG in maternal circulation is captured into endosomes through the endocytosis process (i.e., fluid‐phase uptake) at the syncytiotrophoblast membrane in contact with maternal circulation at physiological pH, (2) IgG binds with FcRn preventing enzymatic degradation as endosomes become more acidic when they travel intracellularly from the maternal side to the fetal side of the syncytiotrophoblast, and (3) ultimately IgG is released into the fetal circulation upon fusion of the endosome with the syncytiotrophoblast membrane that is in contact with the fetal circulation at physiological pH. 6 , 10

Several factors are important to the placental transfer of endogenous IgG, including gestational age, maternal levels of total IgG, IgG subclass, the nature of antigen, and placental integrity. The transfer of maternal IgG to the fetus can begin as early as 13 weeks of gestation. 6 The fetal serum concentration of IgG increases throughout pregnancy with the fetal/maternal ratio of >1 at birth. 11 The greatest amount of IgG is transferred to the fetus in the last 4 weeks of pregnancy for full‐term infants; as such, infants born at <33 weeks have significantly lower IgG levels. 6 , 12 The IgG subclasses are differentially transferred to the fetus, with IgG1 typically found to be the most abundant later in pregnancy. 7 , 11 The difference in IgG subclass transfer may be due to the different affinities of the subclasses for the IgG‐transporting FcRn receptor. 6 , 13 In animal studies, intact IgG molecules or Fc fragments of IgG were more likely to pass into fetal circulation than antibody fragments that did not contain the Fc portion (i.e., F(ab')2 fragments). 6 , 14 The physiological understanding of the placental transfer of endogenous IgGs described above is relevant to the placental transfer of IgG‐based therapeutic proteins and the considerations of maternal use of these therapeutic proteins during pregnancy.

Placental transfer to the infant of IgG‐based therapeutic proteins with an Fc domain

As of December 2023, the FDA has approved 136 new IgG‐based therapeutic proteins that contain an Fc domain; these products include mAbs, Fc‐fusion proteins, antibody‐drug conjugates, and mAbs in combination with other molecules, accounting for ~ 50% of approved therapeutic proteins. The Fc domain in these approved Fc‐containing therapeutic proteins belongs to the subclass of IgG1 (93 products), IgG4 (26 products), IgG2 (14 products), and hybrid structures (three products). 15 The structure of the IgG‐based therapeutic protein and its affinity for the FcRn is a consideration when assessing the extent of potential placental transfer. By binding to FcRn, Fc‐containing therapeutic proteins have long elimination half‐lives compared with smaller protein drugs, 16 and they could persist after birth in the plasma of an infant exposed in utero. An understanding of the therapeutic protein elimination half‐life in the infant is important to inform how long the therapeutic protein may persist. Like endogenous IgGs, the placental transfer of Fc‐containing therapeutic proteins is expected to increase throughout pregnancy, 17 therefore, the timing of administration of these drugs during pregnancy is an influencing factor of the potential infant exposure when measured after birth.

CLINICAL EXAMPLES OF PLACENTAL TRANSFER OF IgG‐BASED THERAPEUTIC PROTEINS

There are limited available clinical data describing the placental transfer of IgG‐based therapeutic proteins. Many of the clinical studies are related to mAbs for the treatment of autoimmune conditions that are frequently diagnosed in females of reproductive potential, such as IBD, rheumatic diseases, and dermatologic diseases. 18 While this is not a systematic review of the published literature, it includes select clinical examples illustrating factors that have been shown to contribute to the knowledge of placental transfer of IgG‐based therapeutic proteins. The drug concentration data summarized in this review cannot be directly compared across studies because clinical assessments differed in terms of study design, primary objectives, sample collection, and use of potentially different bioanalytical methods. The heterogeneity in the data that is collected poses a challenge when assessing the use of therapeutic proteins during pregnancy. However, the ratio of infant‐to‐maternal drug concentrations at birth may provide insights into the extent of in utero placental transfer. Overall, the literature reviewed demonstrates that studies have small number of subjects with available data and that the type of IgG‐based therapeutic protein in combination with its target may influence the extent of placental transfer. Variable extent of placental transfer among monoclonal antibody, Fab’ fragment, and Fc‐Fusion protein products

Like the endogenous IgGs, mAbs can interact with FcRn, which facilitates their placental transfer. 17 However, the reported extent of placental transfer varies for mAbs, even when they are within the same IgG subclass. This difference in transfer among mAbs in the same class may be attributed to the target of the mAb. The target as a potential influencing factor to placental transfer was suggested by data from a sub‐study of the Pregnancy in Inflammatory Bowel Disease And Neonatal Outcomes (PIANO) study. 19 The study evaluated the amount of placental transfer observed between IgG1 mAb products to treat IBD, including infliximab, adalimumab, golimumab, ustekinumab, and vedolizumab. Placental transfer was expressed as the infant‐to‐maternal drug concentration ratio on the day of birth. For the IgG1 subclass mAbs that target tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFα), including infliximab, adalimumab, and golimumab, a placental transfer ratio of >1 was observed. For another IgG1 mAb called ustekinumab with a different target of IL‐12 and IL‐23, the placental transfer ratio was also >1. In contrast, a placental transfer ratio of <1 was observed for an IgG1 mAb, vedolizumab, that targets integrin α4β7. Thus, all IgG1 mAbs are not transferred equally across the placenta. A possible explanation for this is that the amount of the IgG1 mAb that is placentally transferred depends on the target of the IgG1 mAb.

In addition to the target of the IgG‐based therapeutic protein possibly impacting the degree to which it is transferred across the placenta, the structure of the IgG‐based therapeutic protein may also impact its placental transfer. For example, certolizumab pegol is formed with a Fab’ fragment of an IgG1 fused to a polyethylene glycol moiety that replaces the Fc portion of the antibody. 20 Without the Fc portion of the antibody, certolizumab pegol cannot bind to the FcRn. The findings of the CRIB study (NCT02019602), a dedicated PK study to evaluate placental transfer by measuring plasma drug concentrations in the pregnant individual, umbilical cords, and infants at delivery and at 4 and 8 weeks post‐delivery, suggest little‐to‐no placental transfer of certolizumab pegol from pregnant individuals to infants. Sixteen pregnant individuals at >30 weeks of pregnancy received certolizumab and 14 infants completed the study. Maternal certolizumab levels at delivery were within the expected therapeutic range. Among the 14 infants, 13 had no quantifiable certolizumab levels at birth, and 1 had a level of 0.042 μg/mL. No infants had quantifiable certolizumab levels at 4 and 8 weeks post‐delivery. The lack of an IgG Fc domain and a resulting inability to bind FcRn, which mediates the transfer of antibodies across the placenta, is hypothesized to contribute to the little‐to‐no placental transfer of certolizumab. 19 , 21 , 22 , 23 While Fc‐fusion proteins contain the Fc domain and have the potential to interact with the FcRn, reports on etanercept, an Fc‐fusion protein with Fc domain of IgG1, showed low placental transfer (0.04 μg/mL in infant serum, which was 3.6% of the maternal concentration 19 to 0.08 μg/mL, which was 7.4% of the maternal concentration 20 ) despite etanercept administration throughout pregnancy, including in the third trimester. 24 , 25 Although the Fc‐fusion proteins may be expected to have similar affinity for the FcRn as mAbs containing Fc domain in the same IgG subclass, placental transfer of etanercept was considerably lower than seen with other IgG1 mAbs. This may be due to etanercept's lower affinity for the FcRn. 26 Thus, Fab fragments and Fc‐fusion proteins demonstrate that the structure of the therapeutic protein impacts placental transfer.

Pharmacokinetics in both pregnant individuals and their infants exposed in utero and potential pharmacodynamic implications

The maternal IgG‐based therapeutic protein concentration is not always predictive of the concentration in the infant exposed in utero through placental transfer. This concept was demonstrated in a dedicated, prospective, placental transfer PK study conducted in Denmark, New Zealand, and Australia involving 80 patients with IBD exposed during pregnancy to infliximab or adalimumab. 27 Pooled analysis of the data from pregnant individuals and their infants in this study allowed an estimation of the half‐life of infliximab and adalimumab in pregnant individuals and in infants exposed in utero. As shown in Table 1, the half‐life of both infliximab and adalimumab was longer in infants exposed in utero (33 and 26 days, respectively) than in their respective pregnant individuals (8–10 and 14 days, respectively). These data suggest that the half‐life of mAbs in infants exposed in utero cannot simply be estimated based on the half‐life observed in their respective pregnant individuals.

Characterizing the PK, including the duration of persistence of mAbs in systemic circulation in infants exposed in utero, is important to inform whether the exposure in utero can be associated with potential PD effects in infants after delivery. Generally, mAb concentrations in infants exposed in utero decrease after delivery when compared with mAb concentrations seen at birth. 19 The PIANO study reported that the observed median serum concentration of infliximab in infants exposed in utero decreased after delivery. Although the serum concentration decreased over time in exposed infants, infliximab remained in the infant circulation and was measured at concentrations up to 7 μg/mL at a median of 129.5 days after delivery. For non‐pregnant adults treated with infliximab, a clinical response correlates with a serum trough concentration between 3 and 7 μg/mL. When infliximab is compared with another anti‐TNFα, adalimumab, observed serum concentrations in infants exposed in utero were up to 2.2 μg/mL at a median of 129.5 days after delivery. The observed adalimumab concentrations were below the serum trough concentration of 5 μg/mL considered to be necessary for a therapeutic response in adults. As neither infliximab nor adalimumab is approved for any indication in infants, the clinical significance for the infant of the observed serum concentrations of these drugs after birth is unknown. While the comparison to the therapeutic levels for the indicated population could provide some insight into the potential degree of target suppression, the pharmacodynamic profiles of these mAbs in infants may differ from those in the indicated adult population.

Gestational timing of exposure to mAbs and implication of the in utero exposure in infants

The timing of administration of IgG‐based therapeutic proteins during pregnancy may be an important consideration, especially for IgG mAbs. Because the transfer of maternal IgG to the fetus can begin as early as the beginning of the 2nd trimester and continue to increase through the 3rd trimester, 6 , 28 it is anticipated that the timing of the administration of the therapeutic IgG mAb during pregnancy may impact the extent of the in utero exposure and persistence of the mAb in fetal circulation. For rituximab, an anti‐CD20 antibody, literature reported variations in the amount of placental transfer. The ratio of the infant‐to‐maternal rituximab concentration was 0.28 in one case report with the shortest duration of treatment. 29 The serum concentration was lower in the neonate at birth than in the pregnant individual who was treated for 4 weeks in the third trimester only (i.e., 30–34 weeks of gestation). Whereas, two other case reports described the ratio of the infant‐to‐maternal rituximab concentration as 1.20 30 and 3.30, 31 indicating higher concentrations in the infant exposed in utero than in the pregnant individual who was treated in the second and third trimesters for 12 weeks (i.e., 16–28 weeks of gestation) and 14 weeks, (i.e., 16–30 weeks of gestation), respectively. 30 , 31 The authors attributed these differences to the timing and duration of administration to the pregnant individual (3rd trimester only vs. 2nd and 3rd trimester). In all reported cases, infant B‐cell depletion was observed at birth with recovery occurring over a few months without reported infections in those infants.

Altered immune state in infants associated with in utero exposure to certain mAbs

One of the potential safety concerns is an altered immune state in the infant when exposed to certain mAbs in utero (e.g., those with immunosuppressive properties). Such potential safety concerns have been illustrated by the IgG‐based therapeutic protein's mechanism of action and based on reports of abnormal immune function biomarkers found in the exposed infants. 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 For example, in a case series, eculizumab, a mAb that inhibits complement C5, was reported to have varying cord blood concentrations from undetectable up to 20% of the maternal levels. 33 In this case series, there was ~90% blockade of C5 in the newborns that were exposed in utero; the observed extent of C5 blockade has the potential to compromise newborn immunity. 34 Eculizumab cases showed that the PD effect lasts longer than mAb's concentration in the infant and that C5 blockade in the infant can be high even when the infant‐to‐maternal ratio is considered low. This indicates that infant‐to‐maternal ratio alone may not provide enough information to predict whether the placental transfer may be clinically relevant.

MECHANISMS OF TRANSFER OF ANTIBODIES AND THERAPEUTIC PROTEINS INTO HUMAN MILK AND FROM LACTATING INDIVIDUAL TO INFANT

Molecular weight is an important factor in determining the passage of drugs and therapeutic proteins into human milk, with those < 600 Da being reported to passively diffuse into human milk. 35 Additionally, drugs that have high lipid solubility, are weak bases, and/or have low protein binding are also associated with increased transfer into human milk. Some drugs have been reported in human milk at higher levels than would be expected from simple diffusion, and in some cases, exposures can be attributed to active transport via transporters. For example, the breast cancer resistance protein (BCRP) is a transporter that has been observed to be over‐expressed in lactating individuals as compared with non‐lactating individuals. 36 Diffusion and the active transport mechanisms generally occur for small molecules but not for large proteins. 37 , 38

During the initial post‐partum period, the spaces between the breast tissue epithelial cells are wider and can allow for larger molecules, such as endogenous antibodies and IgG‐based therapeutic proteins, to pass. However, those spaces close within 7 days, 39 and the mechanisms by which antibodies enter human milk change. It is known that IgA, the most common antibody class reported in human milk, is transferred into human milk. 40 Apart from IgA, IgG has been reported to make up <10% of detected immunoglobulins in human milk, with IgG1 and IgG2 accounting for more than 90% of the IgG subclasses in human milk. 41 For the IgG‐based therapeutic proteins, the transport mechanisms for IgG are the most relevant and are presented herein. It has been reported that FcRn is expressed in the epithelial cells of the mammary glands. 42 There is limited information available for its role in lactational transfer, but it is likely that IgG may cross into human milk through FcRn. This potential transfer mechanism and the role of IgG in human milk with a potential importance in gut homeostasis and immunity in infants is an area of active study. 41 It is not clear whether IgG‐based therapeutic proteins without the Fc domain of IgG have a mechanism for transfer into human milk.

Considerations of IgG‐based therapeutic protein transfer to breastfeeding infant

As concentrations of some IgG‐based therapeutic proteins have been found in human milk, the question of the impact, if any of these proteins on the human milk‐fed infant is important to address. IgG‐based therapeutic proteins ingested via human milk could have local adverse effects on the infant GI tract. Systemic exposure in an infant via human milk may lead to other adverse effects. 43 When an IgG‐based therapeutic protein is found in human milk, it is important to understand whether systemic absorption occurs in the infant ingesting the human milk.

During the neonatal period, absorption of endogenous antibodies can occur due to FcRn in the gut but is unlikely to occur past the first few days of life. 40 , 44 Furthermore, published studies have reported limited antibody survival beyond the stomach. For example, one study showed that 10%–15% of orally administered IgGs were recovered as intact IgGs in infants' stools as determined with an agglutination assay against Escherichia coli antigens. 45 Although IgG is found in human milk, the GI absorption in humans is reported to be limited, suggesting their role in mucosal immunity. 44 In addition to limited absorption, proteins are known to degrade in the GI tract, which is the reason that IgG‐based therapeutic proteins are generally given via non‐enteral routes of administration. However, clinical data confirming the extent of infant exposure from IgG‐based therapeutic proteins are limited and are usually confounded by in utero exposure if used during pregnancy.

CLINICAL EXAMPLES OF HUMAN LACTATIONAL TRANSFER OF IgG‐BASED THERAPEUTIC PROTEINS

There are few lactation studies that evaluate IgG‐based therapeutic protein concentrations in human milk and even fewer that evaluate exposure to the infant receiving human milk. The available studies generally have limitations, such as small sample sizes, short collection periods relative to the long half‐life of mAbs, and insufficient infant serum sampling to allow for analysis. 46

Presence of IgG‐based therapeutic proteins in human milk

The PIANO sub‐study provided a large, prospective evaluation of human milk transfer of several mAbs from lactating individuals with IBD to their infants. 47 For infliximab, 66% of the lactating individuals had at least one human milk sample with a detectable concentration (Table 2). The maximum human milk infliximab concentration was detected between 24 and 48 h after infusion, with detectable concentrations at 168 h. Adalimumab was detected in human milk in 9.5% of the lactating individuals who had samples collected, with maximum concentrations observed between 12 and 24 h post‐injection. At 7 days post‐injection, adalimumab was undetectable in the human milk samples. Ustekinumab was detected in human milk of 66.7% of the lactating individuals who provided samples, with peak concentrations observed between 12 and 72 h after injection.

When considering the transfer of non‐Fc‐containing Fab' fragment product, certolizumab, a milk‐only lactation study (NCT02154425) measured concentrations in human milk of 17 lactating individuals at least 6 weeks postpartum. 48 Among 13 individuals who had quantifiable levels in milk, certolizumab concentrations were <1% of the maternal plasma concentration observed with therapeutic dosing, indicating little to no certolizumab transfer from plasma to human milk. These data suggest that small amounts of mAbs are transferred to human milk but do not elucidate the clinical impact on infants receiving human milk; therefore, additional studies are needed.

Contribution of lactational transfer to infant exposure

Assessing the extent of infant exposure through lactational exposure is challenging. For infants who experienced in utero exposure through placental transfer and are receiving human milk, the contribution of lactational transfer to the infant's systemic exposure is difficult to evaluate. Instead, the observed drug concentrations in infant blood samples represent the combined exposure through placental and lactational transfer. In females of reproductive potential with chronic conditions, treatment that is given during pregnancy is often continued during breastfeeding. Therefore, separating the contribution in the infant through in utero exposure compared with human milk may not be possible, making it challenging to determine how the infant was exposed and whether the impact on the infant is related to exposure while in utero or from human milk. Thus, it can be challenging to provide therapeutic recommendations about breastfeeding to the lactating individual.

There is indirect evidence suggesting no or not detectable lactational transfer of IgG‐based therapeutic proteins. In a case report for Fc‐fusion protein, etanercept, the maternal treatment continued throughout pregnancy and postpartum. 25 At birth, umbilical cord concentrations were ~3% of the concentration in maternal blood. Serum concentrations in the infant decreased while receiving human milk. At 12 weeks after delivery, etanercept was no longer detected in the infant's serum despite its detection in human milk (3.5 ng/mL) and with infant ingestion of at least 800 mL/day of human milk. The authors concluded that the decrease in concentration over time in infant serum during the period of receiving human milk suggested no transfer via human milk. Similarly, in several case reports, infliximab was not detected in the human milk of infliximab‐treated lactating individuals, and infant infliximab serum level was considered to be the result of in utero exposure. 49 , 50 , 51 , 52

Concentrations in maternal serum not indicative of human milk concentration or infant exposure

Postpartum maternal serum concentrations of mAbs do not correspond to human milk concentrations. In a case report for adalimumab, an IgG1 subclass mAb targeting TNFα, adalimumab was given until 30 weeks and delivery occurred at 38 weeks of gestation. Administration of adalimumab then resumed at 4 weeks postpartum. 53 Maternal blood and human milk samples were obtained before and after resuming adalimumab treatment postpartum. The human milk concentration of adalimumab increased from undetectable prior to treatment to 31 ng/mL at 6 days post‐injection. 53 Despite the increase in the concentration of adalimumab in human milk over time, the concentration in human milk was <1% of the corresponding concentration in maternal serum.

In another case report, a pregnant individual with Crohn's disease received adalimumab until 16 weeks of gestation, delivered at 37 weeks of gestation, and restarted adalimumab at 24 days postpartum; therefore, there was an approximately 24‐week treatment hiatus. 54 The individual's infant was exclusively breastfed for 4 months. Maternal blood samples were collected prior to restarting adalimumab and at days 3 and 13 after restarting treatment. An infant blood sample was obtained at delivery and at 3 months of age. The infant adalimumab concentration was 150 ng/mL at delivery and was undetectable at 3 months, despite being breastfed.

For natalizumab, an IgG4 subclass mAb targeting alpha‐4 integrin, a German Multiple Sclerosis and Pregnancy Registry was used to identify 17 patients who received natalizumab during lactation. 55 All patients received natalizumab during the third trimester of pregnancy. The natalizumab concentration was measured in the human milk of three patients and in the serum of two of their breastfed infants. Natalizumab was detected in human milk with concentrations at <1% of maternal serum values and was not detected in the serum of both breastfed infants.

DISCUSSION

Due to patient needs for treatment with IgG‐based therapeutic proteins during pregnancy and lactation, infants have the potential to be impacted through in utero exposure via placental transfer or through lactational transfer. As such, there is a need to collect information to inform the safe use of therapeutic proteins for pregnant or lactating individuals and their infants. The physiologic transfer of nutrients and endogenous proteins (e.g., IgG) across the placenta to the developing fetus and through human milk to the breastfeeding infant can provide some information about the transfer of IgG‐based therapeutic proteins. The available published literature on placental and lactational transfer of IgG‐based therapeutic proteins is limited, but growing, and provides initial insights and identifies knowledge gaps for future PK, PD, and safety evaluations (Figure 2).

Summary of knowledge gaps from published literature.

Knowledge gaps to fill to inform the safe use of IgG‐based therapeutic proteins during pregnancy and lactation

Our limited literature review reveals existing data gaps regarding placental and lactational transfer of IgG‐based therapeutic proteins and the need to evaluate the potential adverse effects on the infant, including the impact on the infant immune system for products with immunosuppressive properties.

PK data in infants exposed to IgG‐based therapeutic proteins via placental or lactational transfer is limited in the literature, therefore, it is imperative to collect PK data to better understand how the infant exposure may impact infant health and safety. The objectives of filling these PK data gaps should be placed on an enhanced understanding of whether enough IgG‐based therapeutic protein enters the fetal or infant circulation to have clinically meaningful PD effects; and whether the therapeutic protein effect can last longer than the measurable drug concentration in infant serum; and whether the effects are temporary or if these therapeutic proteins can alter the normal development of the infant immune system. Literature has been emerging in recent years to try to characterize the observed PD effects of placental or lactational transfer, including for infection risk, vaccine response, and immune system function for immunosuppressants; 56 , 57 , 58 , 59 , 60 , 61 , 62 however, available PD data remains too limited to inform clinical recommendations.

The available PK data in infants are often limited to a single timepoint, for example, at birth, that informed the extent of placental transfer based on the infant‐to‐maternal concentration ratio. When comparing mAbs, the reported extent of placental transfer in the literature is highly variable. While the placental transfer of mAbs is anticipated to be influenced by various factors, for example, the immunoglobulin type, the presence of an Fc region, and the timing of last the maternal dose prior to birth, there may be other factors that influence placental transfer of mAbs that have not yet been characterized. Further, the published data are often missing information on the duration of mAb presence in infant blood, the presence and duration of PD effects on the infant, and adverse clinical reactions in the infant, including safety concerns following administration of live attenuated virus vaccines. 63 , 64

There are limited published clinical lactation study data on the concentrations of IgG‐based therapeutic proteins in human milk. For mAbs, the literature reports inconsistency in the amount of mAb present in human milk. 65 , 66 Although review articles, in vitro data, and knowledge on immunoglobulins suggest it is unlikely that an IgG‐based therapeutic protein present in human milk will be absorbed through the infant's GI tract beyond the first few days of life and reach the systemic circulation, there are insufficient or confounded clinical data.

Most clinical data related to lactational transfer are from milk‐only lactation studies describing concentrations in human milk, 67 but these studies do not collect information related to systemic absorption in infants. There are some published clinical lactation study data that show low amounts of IgG‐based therapeutic proteins in human milk relative to maternal serum. Knowing the serum concentration of IgG‐based therapeutic proteins in infants, within the context of the therapeutic protein concentration needed for therapeutic effect in lactating individuals, may be useful to assess the potential risk to the breastfeeding infant. Assessment of PD effects to understand the impact of the presence of IgG‐based therapeutic protein concentration could also provide additional context, when feasible.

Published clinical lactation studies frequently do not include a description of any local or systemic effects in infants exposed to IgG‐based therapeutic proteins via human milk. Some of the effects may include adverse effects (e.g., diarrhea, skin rash), local effects on infants' GI mucosa associated with infant immunity and safety concerns following administration of live attenuated virus vaccines. 68 , 69 Further clinical lactation studies are needed to better understand the concentrations of IgG‐based therapeutic proteins transferred into human milk, the amount of local and systemic absorption in the human milk‐fed infant and the clinical concerns from infant exposure to the therapeutic protein via human milk. 70

Proposed actions for addressing knowledge gaps through conducting clinical studies

Gathering data to investigate the potential safety concerns resulting from in utero and lactational exposure to IgG‐based therapeutic proteins in infants may be perceived as challenging. Indeed, practical challenges exist with respect to conducting clinical studies to measure the exposure in infants, to gain insight on the PD response and assess safety implications. For example, challenges in conducting PK, PD, and clinical assessments in infants include the limited blood sampling over the study duration, the small volume of each blood sample, and the overall limited study population. However, thoughtful planning and logistical coordination can overcome these challenges. In addition to the literature resources, there are several FDA guidance documents providing helpful insight on various approaches, study designs, and considerations for conducting these studies in pregnant, lactating and pediatric populations. 63 , 64 , 70 , 71 Integrating pregnant and lactating individuals earlier in drug development and limiting exclusion is necessary to fill the data gaps. Modeling and simulation approaches can inform study design by leveraging available data in non‐pregnant individuals, and the model can be refined when some clinical data are available to facilitate clinical decision‐making. To move forward, collaboration among multidisciplinary scientific experts from academia, clinical practice, regulators, and industry is needed to gain further understanding of PK, PD, effects on infant immunity (local and systemic), and adverse effects of IgG‐based therapeutic proteins in pregnant and lactating individuals and their fetus or infant, respectively.

CONCLUSIONS

Treatment of pregnant and lactating individuals with IgG‐based therapeutic proteins can lead to exposure in their infants through placental and/or lactational transfer. Understanding both the systemic PK and PD effect of therapeutic proteins in infants exposed in utero and via human milk is critical for developing treatment strategies; however, there are significant knowledge gaps because pregnant and lactating individuals have been excluded in the early phase clinical trials, historically. With the increasing use of IgG‐based therapeutic proteins in the treatment of chronic diseases requiring treatment during pregnancy and lactation, there is a need to ensure that clinical assessment is guided by the clinical objectives and the product characteristics.
