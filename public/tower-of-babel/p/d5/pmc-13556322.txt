Heat requirement, not warming, governs the urban-rural disparity in spring phenology advancement
Authors: Can He, Xiaoyue Wang, Josep Peñuelas, Xuezhen Zhang, Chaoyang Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Urbanization has profoundly altered local climates and vegetation structures, resulting in pronounced differences in spring phenology between urban and rural areas. However, the mechanistic drivers of this urban-rural divergence, specifically regarding the rates of phenological advancement, remain poorly understood. Using satellite-derived phenology observations across 354 cities in China from 2001 to 2020, we show that the advancement of the spring green-up date (GUD) was significantly more rapid in urban areas than in their rural surroundings under continuous warming. This urban-rural divergence was observed in 47.7% of the studied cities, where urban advancement trends outpaced rural ones by an average of 0.37 ± 0.24 days/year, particularly in temperate warm-summer regions. By integrating chilling accumulation and thermal requirement models with interpretable machine learning, we identify accumulated heat demand as the primary driver of this divergence. Partial correlation analysis reveals that thermal requirements dominate GUD shifts, explaining 74% of the variance in urban areas and surpassing the influence of chilling accumulation (51%). Moreover, we find that vegetation structure modulates these shifts, as increased species richness amplifies urban GUD trends while tree density buffers rural shifts. Notably, current empirical models largely failed to reproduce both the spatial patterns and the magnitude of the urban-rural divergence in GUD trends. Our results redefine the hierarchy of controls on spring phenology in human-dominated landscapes, with critical implications for predicting ecosystem responses under accelerating urbanization.

Introduction

Vegetation phenology—the timing of recurring biological events in plants—serves as a sensitive indicator of ecological responses to environmental change and a key regulator of ecosystem functioning.1,2 Shifts in plant phenology can alter trophic and mutualistic interactions, triggering cascading effects on biodiversity, carbon cycling, and ecosystem stability.3,4 Among anthropogenic drivers, urbanization is a primary force reshaping land surfaces and local climates, thereby accelerating phenological shifts, particularly the spring green-up date (GUD).5,6,7,8 However, while the overall direction of these changes is well documented, the magnitude of these temporal trends—specifically, the rate at which phenological timing is advancing—remains poorly constrained along urban-rural gradients.

Numerous studies report earlier spring phenology in urban areas, typically attributing this to enhanced temperature sensitivity5,9,10,11 and increased impervious surface area (ISA).8,12,13 However, the strength and consistency of these urban-rural differences in temperature sensitivity remain contentious, with regional, species-specific, and methodological variability complicating interpretation.14,15,16 Understanding the magnitude of phenological trends necessitates a mechanistic perspective rooted in plant dormancy processes. In temperate perennials, dormancy unfolds in two sequential phases: endodormancy, which is alleviated through sufficient chilling accumulation (CA), and ecodormancy, which is overcome by fulfilling a species-specific heat requirement (HR). Despite their important roles, the extent to which chilling exposure and heat accumulation contribute to urban-rural differences in GUD trends remains unclear. Although experimental studies have confirmed that chilling is essential for dormancy release under natural conditions,17,18,19,20,21 its interaction with heat accumulation along urbanization gradients poses a critical challenge. This issue is further complicated by statistical artifacts and limitations in phenology models, as a recent work demonstrates that conventional parameterizations may inadequately represent chilling effects under a warming climate.20 In addition, large-scale assessments often fail to disentangle the relative contributions of co-varying factors such as vegetation functional traits, soil moisture dynamics, and temperature variability, thereby limiting mechanistic predictions of phenological responses in urban and rural ecosystems.5,22

Here we investigated the underlying mechanisms driving urban-rural divergence in the magnitude of spring phenological trends across 354 Chinese cities over the period 2001–2020. By integrating satellite-derived GUD,23 spatiotemporal attribution modeling,24 process-based chilling and HR models, and SHAP-enabled XGBoost regression,25 we compare the temporal evolution of GUD trends between urban areas and their rural counterparts. Specifically, we also explored the underlying mechanisms driving these urban-rural divergences. Finally, we assessed whether current ecosystem models could reproduce such spatial and interannual patterns, providing critical insights into the effects of future urbanization on ecosystem functioning.

Materials and methods

Definition of urban and rural boundaries

We adopted the urban area definition proposed by a global dataset of annual urban extents (1992–2020) from harmonized nighttime lights (NTLs),26 which delineates urban extents based on harmonized nighttime light (NTL) observations. This dataset provides a consistent global record of urbanization by integrating multi-source NTL data through a stepwise-partitioning framework. Specifically, we utilized the urban extents from the year 2020 as the basis for our analysis. To define the rural boundary, we constructed buffers of equal area around urban regions while excluding cropland, water bodies, and impervious surfaces based on the MCD12Q1 land use dataset,27 ensuring minimal anthropogenic influence (Figure 1). Vegetation phenology data

We utilized the MCD12Q2 phenology product from 2001 to 2020 to obtain annual vegetation phenological information.23 The MCD12Q2 dataset is a global phenology product derived from the MODIS sensor, developed and maintained by NASA. It provides pixel-level estimations of key phenological metrics at a spatial resolution of 500 m and an annual temporal frequency. In this study, we specifically used the start of season variable from the MCD12Q2 dataset as a proxy for the GUD. This parameter reflects the onset of vegetative activity in spring and serves as a key indicator for analyzing spring phenological dynamics.

To evaluate the robustness of MODIS-derived observations and capture fine-scale urban heterogeneity, we incorporated high-resolution remote sensing data for validation. We acquired surface reflectance imagery from the Landsat series (TM,28 ETM+,29 and OLI30) spanning 2004–2020 at a 30-m spatial resolution. The analysis focused on three representative northern cities: Beijing, Shenyang, and Changchun. Given the high aerosol loads and complex surface backgrounds inherent in urban environments, we selected the enhanced vegetation index (EVI) as the primary metric for phenological extraction. EVI offers a distinct advantage over standard indices by incorporating the blue band to mitigate atmospheric influences while simultaneously minimizing soil background interference. This could ensure a more precise representation of vegetation dynamics within highly fragmented urban landscapes.

Climatic data and auxiliary data

For the climate data used in partial correlation and sensitivity analysis, we utilized the monthly mean temperature and precipitation data from 2001 to 2020.31 These data have a spatial resolution of 1 km. Additionally, we used the downward shortwave radiation data from MCD18A1.32 For the CA and HR data, we use a global 1-km resolution daily near-surface air temperature dataset from 2003 to 2020.33 Based on this daily dataset, we further applied four widely used process-based phenology models to simulate historical phenological dynamics, aiming to evaluate whether current models can accurately reproduce the observed phenological trends across China. All data were resampled to match the resolution of the phenology data using bilinear interpolation.

To systematically investigate the spatial heterogeneity of phenology, we synthesized a comprehensive dataset comprising 17 environmental determinants, placing particular emphasis on biotic interactions and vegetation structure. For biodiversity, we utilized the global woody angiosperm diversity dataset,34 specifically selecting the Hill number of order q = 2 (inverse Simpson index). This metric, derived from aggregated occurrence records, represents the effective number of dominant species; although provided at a coarse spatial resolution of 100 km, we employed it as a proxy for regional species pool constraints that shape local canopy phenology. For vegetation structure, we incorporated the global tree density data,35 which estimates tree counts at approximately 1-km resolution based on ground-sourced forest inventories, serving as a robust indicator of stand-level biomass and inter-individual competition. Complementing these biotic metrics, we integrated a suite of abiotic and anthropogenic variables, including DEM data,36 impervious surface data,37 carbon dioxide concentration data,38 soil moisture data,39 population density data,40 Köppen climate classification data,41 N uptake data,42 and soil carbon content data. The sources of temperature, precipitation, and radiation data are the same as those in the sensitivity analysis. Among these, the climate factors were preprocessed using the mean values from November to May. The historical data of the phenology model were also validated using CA and HR data.

Fine-scale data processing and vegetation index calculation

To verify the robustness of our findings at a finer scale, we utilized 30-m resolution Landsat imagery. To ensure temporal continuity across the multi-sensor dataset, we used Landsat 7 ETM+ as the reference standard. Landsat 5 TM and Landsat 7 ETM+ data were treated as consistent due to their similar spectral bandwidths and response functions.43 For Landsat 8 OLI, we applied the linear transformation coefficients to harmonize its surface reflectance with the ETM+ standard,44 thereby mitigating systematic biases arising from sensor differences. This step mitigates systematic biases arising from differences in sensor bandwidths and spectral response functions. Subsequently, we calculated the EVI from Landsat surface reflectance as follows:(Equation 1)EVI=2.5×ρNIR−ρREDρNIR+6×ρRED−7.5×ρBLUE+1where ρNIR, ρRED, and ρBLUE represent the surface reflectance in the near-infrared, red, and blue bands, respectively. The coefficients 6 and 7.5 are utilized to correct for atmospheric aerosol scattering, while the value 1 accounts for canopy background adjustment.45

Fine-scale phenology retrieval using 30-m Landsat data

To reconstruct continuous phenological trajectories, we applied the interquartile range (IQR) method to eliminate outliers, followed by a Savitzky-Golay (S-G) filter to smooth the time series.46 Specifically, given the temporal discontinuity of Landsat observations, we first reconstructed daily EVI time series via linear interpolation. Based on the S-G fitted growth curves, we employed the dynamic threshold method to determine phenological metrics. We calculated the seasonal EVI amplitude (the difference between the peak and baseline EVI) for each pixel, and the GUD was defined as the day of the year when the rising curve during spring first reached 50% of the seasonal amplitude.47

Trend analysis and statistical sensitivity

For both the 500- and 30-m resolutions datasets, we quantified temporal trends in the GUD and climatic variables using the Theil-Sen slope estimator. The statistical significance of these trends was assessed through the Mann-Kendall test at a significance level of p < 0.05.48 Utilizing the 500-m resolution data, we further investigated the climatic sensitivity and causal mechanisms underlying phenological shifts. Partial correlation analysis was employed to determine the optimal preseason lengths (1–6 months post-October) for GUD responses to temperature, precipitation, and downward shortwave radiation while controlling for confounding variables. Quantification of chilling and heat accumulation

To quantify CA and HR, we employed a multi-model ensemble consisting of 11 chilling and 5 heat requirement models,21 as detailed in Table S2. Model calculations were driven by daily temperature data spanning from November 1 of the preceding year to the date of the observed phenological onset. To ensure the spatial robustness of the observed urban-rural divergence in thermal requirements, we applied this modeling framework across both 500-m and 30-m resolutions. Temperature inputs were resampled using bilinear interpolation to align precisely with the spatial grids of the respective phenological datasets. Furthermore, we employed convergent cross mapping (CCM) to detect causal links between thermal forcing and GUD trends.50

Spatial driver attribution via machine learning

We employed an XGBoost model coupled with SHAP (Shapley additive explanations) analysis to quantify the spatial drivers of GUD trends across multiple dimensions, including climatic, soil, anthropogenic, and vegetation factors.51 The model integrated climatic factors (temperature, precipitation, radiation), soil properties, anthropogenic factors (impervious surface), and vegetation characteristics. To ensure spatial consistency, all explanatory variables were resampled to align with the respective phenological grids (500 m and 30 m). Specifically, the quantification of ISA was adapted to capture the relevant urban context at each scale. At the 500-m resolution, ISA was calculated as the fractional coverage within each pixel. Conversely, for the 30-m resolution analysis, ISA was derived as the percentage of impervious cover within a 500-m buffer surrounding each pixel.52

Results

Non-uniform temporal trends of GUD in urban and rural regions

We quantified urban-rural divergence in GUD trends across 354 cities in China, using pixel-level Mann-Kendall trend tests with Theil-Sen slope estimation. Urban and rural zones were delineated using predefined city boundaries and equal-area buffering to ensure spatial comparability (Figure 1). The urban-rural magnitude difference (Δmagnitude) was defined as the absolute GUD trend in urban areas minus that in corresponding rural areas over the period 2001–2020. We observed that most cities exhibited positive urban-rural differences, indicating stronger GUD trends in urban areas (Figure 2A). Specifically, 285 out of 354 cities (80.4%) showed Δmagnitude >0, with 57.2% being significant (p < 0.05), while only 4.0% exhibited significantly negative values (Figure 2B). Overall, the magnitude of GUD trends was significantly higher in urban areas than in rural counterparts. A consistent pattern of divergence was also observed across different climate zones, with all seven Köppen-Geiger climate types predominantly exhibiting positive Δmagnitude values (Figure 2C). The high-resolution analysis conducted across three representative cities supports our macro-scale findings, demonstrating that the magnitude of GUD advancement was significantly higher in urban areas than in their rural surroundings (p < 0.01). Specifically, the mean advancement rate in urban areas reached 3.00 ± 1.28 days/year, whereas the rate in rural areas was 2.64 ± 1.41 days/year (Figure S1). Spatial attribution of non-uniform urban-rural GUD trends

To investigate the spatial drivers underlying urban-rural differences in the magnitude of GUD trends, we first examined the distribution of GUD trends across all the cities. We observed a systematic amplification of spring phenological shifts in urban areas (Figure 3A), where GUD advanced one-fifth faster than in rural areas (−0.6 ± 1.0 vs. −0.5 ± 0.7 days/year). Based on the directionality of trends, we further classified cities into four types (Figure 3B): U−R− (convergent significant advancement), U+R+ (convergent significant delay), U+R−, and U−R+ (divergent shifts). Among them, U−R− cities were the most prevalent, accounting for 70.2% of cases, while divergent types were less frequent. Notably, in the U−R− type, 71.5% of cities exhibited faster GUD advancement in urban than in rural areas, demonstrating the role of urbanization as a phenological accelerator. Overall, for both U−R− and U+R+ types, the magnitude of GUD trends was consistently greater in urban areas than in their rural counterparts (Figure S2). To further uncover the drivers of trend magnitude, we applied an explainable machine learning framework using XGBoost coupled with SHAP analysis and revealed that vegetation structure was the most influential factor differentiating urban and rural GUD dynamics (Figures 4A and S3). Specifically, species richness was the dominant predictor in urban areas (mean |SHAP| = 0.11), while tree density was most important in rural regions (mean |SHAP| = 0.07). Stratified SHAP analysis further showed that increases in species richness led to nonlinear amplifications of GUD trend magnitude in urban zones. Partial correlation analyses, controlling for CA in HR models and vice versa, revealed stronger HR-GUD relationships in urban areas than in rural regions (Figures 6C, 6D, 6G, and 6H). In U−R− cities, urban HR-GUD correlation coefficients were much higher (0.86 ± 0.03 vs. 0.81 ± 0.04). Although the mean CA-GUD correlation values in urban areas were slightly higher than those in rural areas in both U+R+ (urban: 0.66 ± 0.06; rural: 0.64 ± 0.05) and U−R− zones (urban: 0.69 ± 0.06; rural: 0.69 ± 0.05), they remained consistently weaker than those of HR across all city types. Moreover, HR models had significantly higher pixel-wise significance frequencies than CA models. Spatially, HR models also showed stronger coherence with GUD patterns than CA models across diverse hydrothermal regimes (Figures S6 and S7). To assess causality beyond empirical correlation analyses, we employed CCM at the city level. For each HR/CA model, causal strength (ρ) was estimated across cities with significant trend changes, and then fit average ρ curves against library size. CCM results demonstrated that urban areas exhibited stronger causal influences than rural regions for both HR and CA models, while HR models consistently established more robust causal connections to phenological dynamics compared with CA models in both U+R+ and U−R− types (Figure 6I).

The dominance of thermal forcing mechanisms was further validated at a fine spatial scale. Based on the 30-m resolution GDD model, the long-term trends in HR within urban areas remained significantly distinct from their rural surroundings (p < 0.01). Partial correlation analysis revealed that a vast majority of urban pixels at the 30-m scale maintained a strong dependence on thermal forcing, with a mean correlation coefficient (r) of 0.91, comparable with that of rural areas (r = 0.90). In contrast, the explanatory power of chilling requirements remained limited at this fine scale, with a lower mean correlation (r = 0.70) and only 35.7% of pixels showing significant correlations (Figure S8). These multi-scale observations consistently indicate that urbanization amplifies spring phenological responses primarily by enhancing thermal forcing accumulation, a process that becomes more evident when resolving fine-scale impervious surface heterogeneity.

Representation of urban-rural GUD trends in empirical phenological models

To evaluate the ability of existing spring phenology models to reproduce long-term trends in GUD, we tested four widely used process-based models using 1 km daily temperature and aligned to our phenology data: GDD, SW, parallel (PM), and sequential (SM). CA was initiated on November 1 of the previous year, and HR accumulation began on January 1 of the current year. Model parameters were optimized with root mean-squared error as the objective function for calibration. Although all four models achieved reasonable agreement in GUD simulation, their ability to replicate the magnitude of urban-rural GUD trends over time were substantially limited (Figures S9 and S10).

At the city scale, observational data exhibited a statistically significant divergence in GUD trends between urban and rural areas (p < 0.01; Figure 7A). Specifically, urban areas showed a markedly stronger advancing trend of −0.94 ± 0.08 days/year compared with −0.83 ± 0.07 days/year in rural areas. In contrast, simulated trends from all four models were clustered near zero, ranging from −0.62 to −0.42 days/year, indicating a systematic underestimation of trend magnitude. At the pixel scale, the disparity between modeled and observed trends became even more pronounced (Figure 7B). Observations showed a high frequency of significant pixels (36.1% in urban and 31.7% in rural) and a substantial mean trend magnitude (1.93 ± 1.93 and 1.60 ± 1.49 days/year, respectively). However, for the four models, both the proportion of significant pixels (all below 7.0%) and the mean magnitude of these trends (all below 0.80 days/year) were far lower than the observed values, suggesting that current process-based models struggle to capture strong, local phenological shifts driven by urbanization.

Finally, regarding trend direction consistency, while the overall agreement between modeled and observed trends reached approximately 60.7%, a breakdown by significance type reveals critical limitations (Figure 7C). The proportion of pixels where both the model and observation showed significant trends was negligible, accounting for only 3.0% of the total. The majority of the directional agreement stemmed from cases where neither trend was significant (31.5%) or where only the observation was significant (24.6%). This indicates that, even when models correctly predict the direction of change, they often lack the sensitivity to detect the statistical significance of that change.

Discussion

Our results reveal a pronounced urban-rural divergence in the magnitude of spring phenological trends, with urban vegetation exhibiting a significantly greater magnitude of change than its rural counterparts (Figure 2). Unexpectedly, this divergence is not driven solely by regional climate warming, but is deeply rooted in structural and ecological discrepancies between urban and rural vegetation. In urban environments, elevated species richness is associated with amplified phenological trend magnitudes, consistent with emerging evidence that biodiversity enhances ecosystem sensitivity to climatic drivers.3,14,53,54 Conversely, rural areas are characterized by higher tree density and greater woody biomass, which stabilizes local microclimate through canopy-mediated biophysical feedbacks (e.g., evaporative cooling and radiative shading).55,56 This biomass-mediated thermal buffering decouples local microclimate from regional trends,57 explaining why rural phenology exhibits lower climate change velocity than urban counterparts despite similar macro-warming exposure. These findings underscore how anthropogenic modification of vegetation structure creates divergent biophysical feedbacks across urban-rural gradients.

Our pixel-level analyses, utilizing three independent methods, revealed no consistent urban-rural divergence in temperature sensitivity (Figure 5). While previous studies have often emphasized temperature sensitivity or warming trends as primary drivers of phenological shifts,58 our findings indicate that the divergence in GUD trend magnitude may not be solely governed by sensitivity shifts. Instead, our mechanistic analyses identify variations in HR as the dominant driver (Figures 6 and S7). HR trends exhibited strong spatiotemporal coherence with GUD trajectories in both advancing and delaying regions, whereas CA showed inconsistent explanatory power across models. This inconsistency likely reflects known limitations in current phenological model parametrizations, particularly in their representations of chilling dynamics and transitions between endodormancy and ecodormancy. Additionally, coarse spatial resolution of temperature inputs may obscure critical physiological processes that occur at finer scales. The most important contribution of our analyses is that we used the CCM to explore causality, and we consistently showed that urban HR exerted stronger causal influence on GUD dynamics than both rural HR and all CA models. This pattern reinforces the role of HR as a key determinant of phenological change in anthropogenically altered environments.

The cross-scale validation using 30-m Landsat imagery robustly confirms the observed urban-rural phenological divergence. By demonstrating that urban GUD advancement significantly outpaces rural shifts at this finer resolution, we establish that the accelerated spring onset in cities is a persistent ecological phenomenon rather than a spatial artifact of coarse sensor resolution. Notably, the amplified explanatory power of ISA at the 30-m scale suggests that coarse-resolution satellite products likely underestimate the localized impacts of urbanization due to mixed-pixel effects. Nevertheless, ISA consistently ranks secondary to biological factors across both spatial scales. This hierarchical pattern implies that, while urbanization intensity facilitates phenological shifts, it acts primarily as a modulator of underlying biological and climatic drivers, rather than as the fundamental determinant.

Our mechanistic analysis confirms that the intensification of HR trends is the fundamental driver of urban-rural phenological divergence at both scales. The greater magnitude of HR shifts detected at the 30-m resolution likely stems from the geographical sensitivity of our high-latitude validation sites, which are inherently predisposed to more rapid warming responses. More importantly, the intensified dependency on HR in fine-scale analysis suggests that, under the strong heat constraints of northern latitudes, the thermal environment becomes the nearly exclusive regulator of spring onset. This consistent dominance of heat requirements, in contrast to the weak influence of chilling, confirms thermal forcing as the fundamental driver of urban-rural phenological divergence.

We further evaluated the performance of four widely used spring phenology models (GDD, SW, SM, and PM) in reproducing historical GUD trends and found that their ability to capture the magnitude and spatial heterogeneity of these trends remained constrained. This limitation underscores the need to incorporate additional ecological and physiological processes—such as microscale biotic interactions,59 soil-vegetation feedbacks,24,60 and specific trait dynamics—into future modeling frameworks. Current models’ oversimplification of these processes limits their utility for projecting urban-rural phenological dynamics under climate change (Figure 7).

Overall, our findings have important implications for urban ecosystem management.61 In urban areas, enhancing biodiversity in greenspaces may boost ecological responsiveness to climate drivers but could also introduce greater phenological variability—creating trade-offs between resilience and predictability.48 Rural systems, while structurally more stable, show relatively sluggish responses to climate change, emphasizing the need for targeted interventions (e.g., silvicultural practices to modulate tree density) to enhance adaptive capacity. Collectively, our findings highlight the urgency of region-specific adaptation strategies to maintain phenological stability amid warming. The inability of current phenological models to fully capture spatial-temporal dynamics underscores the need to explicitly integrate vegetation structural traits and biodiversity into predictive frameworks. Such advancements will be essential for forecasting ecosystem responses and supporting nature-based policy intervention under accelerating global change.
