Cancer literacy and attitudes among school-going adolescents in a rural district of Tamil Nadu, India: A Cross-sectional study
Authors: Neethu George, Rock B. Dharmaraj, Neeraj Vinod Mohandas, R Bristly Christopher, Selvaraj Mylsamy, Shruthi Elancheliyan, Deepika Selvaraj, Kabilkumar Premakumar, Ashwinkumar Suresh, Tamilarasan Muniyapillai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Cancer remains a leading cause of global mortality, with a substantial disease burden in India across all age groups. While prevention strategies have proven to be effective, adolescence offers a crucial window for establishing lifelong health behaviors and cancer awareness. This developmental period is vital for building health literacy and understanding cancer prevention as habits formed during these years often persist in adulthood. This study was conducted to assess the cancer literacy and attitudes among school-going adolescents in Perambalur district, Tamil Nadu, South India, as well as identifying their independent predictors of inadequate literacy and attitudes.MATERIALS AND METHODS:A cross-sectional study was conducted among 764 school-going adolescents (grades 8–12) in four randomly selected schools in Perambalur district, Tamil Nadu. Data were collected using a pretested semistructured questionnaire administered through the interviewer method. The questionnaire assessed cancer-related knowledge (11 items, maximum score 33) and attitudes (9 items, maximum score 27) using a three-point Likert scale. Data analysis was performed using SPSS v26, [IBM Corp. Released 2019. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp] employing descriptive statistics, Chi-square test, independent t-test, and one-way ANOVA and multivariable linear regression with P < 0.05 considered significant.RESULTS:Among 764 participants (56.9% males), the mean age was 14.13 ± 1.20 years. The mean knowledge and attitude scores were 25.9 ± 2.76 and 21.04 ± 2.36, respectively. While 61.8% knew the correct definition of cancer and 67.7% believed early detectable cancer is curable, only 34.9% were aware of cancer-preventive vaccines. Female students showed higher knowledge scores (26.39 ± 2.50) compared to males (25.67 ± 2.90, P = 0.002). Parental education significantly influenced knowledge scores, with children of graduate/postgraduate parents showing higher scores (P < 0.001). Urban residents demonstrated higher knowledge scores (26.46 ± 2.94) than rural residents (25.67 ± 2.59, P = 0.04). Age positively correlated with attitude scores (r = 0.23, P < 0.001). Multivariable analysis showed male gender (B = 0.70, 95% CI: 0.32–1.09) and maternal graduate education (B = 1.12, 95% CI: 1.03–2.27) as statistically significant predictors of knowledge scores (Adjusted R² =62%), while age (B = 0.42, 95% CI: 0.28–0.56) predicted attitude scores (Adjusted R² =48%).CONCLUSION:While adolescents demonstrated moderate cancer literacy and positive attitudes, significant gaps exist in knowledge about preventive vaccines, risk factors, and common cancers. Educational interventions targeting these gaps, particularly focusing on sociodemographic disparities in cancer literacy, are essential for enhancing cancer prevention literacy among adolescents.

Introduction

The World Health Organization (WHO) has defined adolescence as a critical period spanning the second decade of life (10–19 years), characterized by significant physical, psychological, and social transformations.[1] During this formative phase, the trajectory of an individual’s health and disease risks is established, underscoring the importance of cultivating healthy behaviors.[2] This period is marked by substantial life changes that exert a profound and enduring influence on health-related behaviors.[3] India is home to the largest adolescent population in the world approximating to 253 million.[4]

Globally, cancer constitutes a major public health burden, posing a significant threat to morbidity and mortality.[5] The 2022 report from the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) revealed an alarming trend: An estimated 20 million new cancer cases and 9.7 million cancer-related deaths occurred worldwide.[6]

In India, the annual burden of new cancer cases is substantial, reaching approximately 1 million, and the significantly high mortality rates due to cancer are primarily attributed to late diagnosis.[7] As per the 2021 report of the Indian Council of Medical Research, in the state of Tamil Nadu, the age-adjusted incidence rates of cancers were 71 per 100,000 and 84.4 per 100,000 for males and females, respectively. A significant barrier to effective cancer control is the pervasive presence of myths and misconceptions fueled by a lack of literacy, which perpetuates the stigma associated with the disease.[8] This stigma significantly influences public perception of cancer, creating substantial challenges for cancer control efforts, encompassing prevention, treatment, and palliative care.[9]

Early identification of personal risk factors and the implementation of preventive measures are paramount in the fight against cancer. Literacy of modifiable risk factors serves as a crucial foundation for promoting positive health outcomes within the community.[10] Adolescence presents a unique window of opportunity for targeted cancer risk education and intervention. This age group is particularly receptive to learning and education while simultaneously grappling with the onset of risky behaviors. By increasing adolescents’ literacy of the impact of modifiable lifestyle factors on cancer risk, we can potentially influence their long-term health behaviors.[11] To ensure the continued progress of the nation in terms of health and economic development, it is imperative to equip adolescents with adequate cancer literacy.[3]

Multiple studies have consistently shown that adolescents possess limited knowledge regarding the link between health behaviors and cancer risk.[1213] While enhancing cancer literacy among adolescents is crucial for fostering lifelong healthy behaviors, significant knowledge gaps remain regarding their perceptions of cancer risk factors and their preferences for receiving cancer education.[10] Hence, this cross-sectional study was conducted among school-going adolescents in Perambalur district, Tamil Nadu, South India, with the objective of assessing their cancer literacy and attitudes, as well as identifying their independent predictors of inadequate literacy and attitudes.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

A cross-sectional study was carried out at high schools in Perambalur district of Tamil Nadu for a period of 1 month from August to September 2023.

Study participants and sampling

The study population comprised school-going adolescents from classes 8th to 12th. Using multistage sampling technique: Stage 1 (Geographical sampling): High schools in the catchment area of rural and urban health centers affiliated with the tertiary care medical college were listed as the sampling frame. Stage 2 (School selection): Six schools were selected using computer-generated random numbers. Out of these, four schools provided institutional consent (response rate: 66.7%). Stage 3 (Student selection): All eligible students from classes 8th to 12th in the consenting schools were included through universal sampling, excluding those absent during data collection or having examinations.

Sample size determination

Sample size was calculated using the formula n = Z²pq/d², where Z = 1.96, P = 50% (assumed prevalence of cancer literacy), q = 100-p, and d = absolute precision of 5%; the minimum sample size came up to 384 subjects. Since we had access to more eligible students from the four consenting schools and to increase the power of the study, we included 764 participants.

Data collection tool and technique

Data were collected using a semistructured questionnaire, pretested among 30 adolescents (excluded from final analysis). The questionnaire demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha: 0.73).

A questionnaire was used as a study tool, and data were collected by the interviewer method, which consisted of sociodemographic and basic characteristics such as age (years), sex, education, occupation, residence, parent education and occupation, and number of siblings. The questionnaire then contained domain-based questions.

Section on literacy about cancer: The domain included questions to which the respondents must clearly agree/disagree or stay neutral/don’t know. The questions were related to the knowledge about the causes and risk factors and protective factors of cancer. Eleven questions were under the literacy section. The final scores were taken by adding the responses assigning 3-Agree, 2-Neutral/Don’t know, and 1-Disagree. The maximum score of each question was “3”, and the minimum was “1”; hence, the total score for knowledge section ranged from 11 to 33.

Section on attitude about cancer: The domain included questions to which the respondents must clearly agree/disagree or stay neutral/don’t know. The questions were related to attitudes such as following cancer prevention methods like having healthy diet, regular physical activity, and their attitude on cancer patient whether they avoid them or not. Under attitude section, nine questions were present. The final scores were taken by adding the responses assigning 3-Agree, 2-Neutral/Don’t know, and 1-Disagree. The maximum score of each question was “3”, and the minimum was “1”; hence, the total score for attitude section ranged from 9 to 27.

Section on practice about cancer prevention: The participants were questioned about their practice to preventive measures, including vaccination status (particularly HPV), regular use of sun protection measures (sunscreen application, protective clothing, avoiding peak sun hours), and engagement in cancer screening programs. Health-promoting behaviors were assessed through questions about daily fruit and vegetable consumption, daily physical activity patterns, and maintenance of healthy body weight. The questionnaire also explored participants’ proactive health-seeking behaviors such as regular medical checkups and cancer screening attendance. Additionally, participants were asked about their information-seeking practices, including cancer-related internet searches, participation in cancer awareness programs, and sharing of cancer-related knowledge within their peers/social networks.

Ethical consideration

The study commenced after obtaining Institution Ethics Committee approval (IECHS/IRCHS No: 401- 18/07/2023), parental consent, and student assent.

Statistical analysis method

The collected data were entered into Microsoft Excel through Google Forms and analyzed using SPSS version 26 [IBM Corp. Released 2019. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp]. Descriptive statistics were outlined with frequencies, proportions, and percentages and were summarized using mean with standard deviation. The overall mean score, standard deviation (SD), and 95% confidence interval of mean were calculated for all participants. The association of dichotomous categorized dependent variables was analyzed using the Chi-square test. The continuous dependent variables were analyzed using independent t-test and one-way ANOVA test. Variables that showed statistical significance (P < 0.05) in the bivariate analysis were further included in multivariable linear regression models to identify independent predictors of cancer literacy and attitudes. Standardized β coefficients with 95% confidence intervals were reported. For all analyses, P value < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

Results

Most of the study participants [457 (59.8%)] were living in rural areas. Table 1 shows the distribution of parent’s education and occupation characteristics. In examining the parental characteristics of the study population, educational attainment showed relatively similar patterns between fathers and mothers, though fathers had a slightly higher proportion of graduates/postgraduates at 37.43% compared to mothers at 34.29%. Most of both parents had some form of school education, with 56.15% of fathers and 61.91% of mothers falling into this category. Among fathers, 34.16% held salaried positions or were pensioners, while the majority (65.05%) worked in agriculture or business or as daily wage workers, with only a minimal 0.79% being unemployed. In maternal employment, patterns showed that 21.20% held salaried positions; 43.97% were involved in agriculture, business, or daily wage work; and a substantial proportion (34.81%) were unemployed, likely serving as homemakers [Table 1].

Literacy and attitude score among participants

The cancer literacy scores among the adolescents showed a mean of 25.9 (±2.76 SD), with a median of 26 and a mode of 27. The literacy scores ranged from a minimum of 18 to a maximum of 33. Similarly, the cancer attitude scores demonstrated a mean of 21.04 (±2.36 SD), with both the median and mode at 21, indicating a symmetrical distribution. The attitude scores ranged from 13 to 27.

Literacy, attitudes, and practice related to cancer

Overall, 472 (61.8%) participants were aware of the correct definition of cancer and only 385 (50.4%) agree that cancer has a genetic predisposition. A total of 517 (67.7%) believe that early detectable cancer is curable. Only 267 (34.9%) had knowledge regarding the usage of vaccines that can prevent cancer. The details are given in Figure 1.

Responses to the questions on literacy related to cancer

690 (90.3%) participants believe that cancer prevention is important, but only 297 (38.9%) know how to lower their risk of getting cancer. 354 (46.3%) try to avoid a cancer patient, and 72 (9.4%) believe that they have control over the risk of developing cancer. 472 (61.8%) believe that cancer can be cured to a certain extent. The details are given in Figure 2.

Responses to the questions on attitude related to cancer

Only 37 (11.2%) of the females were vaccinated against Human Papilloma Virus (HPV) which causes cervical cancer. 114 (14.9%) use sunscreen to prevent the risk of developing skin cancer. 566 (74.1%) claimed that they try to advise others not to smoke tobacco and drink alcohol, and 632 (82.7%) actively listen and share the knowledge about cancer prevention. 637 (83.4%) participants claimed to be physically active for at least 30 min a day. Risk factors and lifestyle behaviours related to cancer

Overall, 738 (96.6%) adolescents agree that smoking is a risk factor for cancer; however, only 529 (69.2%) believe that excess alcohol usage leads to cancer. 455 (59.6%) participants agree unhealthy eating habits can lead to cancers in the future, but only 294 (38.5%) agree that eating a high-fat, low-fiber diet will cause cancer. Only 267 (34.9) believe that overweight and obesity is a predisposing factor to cancer. 487 (63.7%) prioritize eating at least a bowl of fruits/vegetables every day [Figures 1 and 3].

Sources of information regarding cancer

279 (36.5%) of the participants have searched and read about cancer on the Internet. The majority [648 (84.8%)] have heard of lung cancer, but only 370 (48.4%) have heard of breast cancer, which is the most common cancer among males and females in India. However, 565 (74%) have heard of leukemia, which is the most common childhood cancer in India. Only 42 (5.5%) have attended any educational programs related to cancer.

Literacy scores showed significant differences across several sociodemographic variables. Female students demonstrated higher literacy scores (26.39 ± 2.50) compared to males (25.67 ± 2.90, P = 0.002). Parental education significantly influenced literacy scores, with children of graduate/postgraduate parents showing higher scores compared to those with no formal education (father’s education: mean difference = 1.3, P < 0.001; mother’s education: mean difference = 1.57, P < 0.001). Regarding maternal occupation, children of salaried/pensioner mothers had significantly higher scores compared to those in agriculture/daily wage work (mean difference = 0.71, P = 0.004). Urban residents demonstrated higher literacy scores (26.46 ± 2.94) than rural residents (25.67 ± 2.59, P = 0.04). Age (r = 0.04, P = 0.80), father’s occupation (P = 0.56), and family history of cancer (P = 0.08) showed no significant association with literacy scores [Table 2].

Multivariable linear regression analysis revealed several significant factors associated with cancer literacy among adolescents. The literacy score model demonstrated strong explanatory power (Adjusted R² =62%, F = 6.26, P < 0.001), with male students showing significantly higher literacy scores compared to females (B = 0.70, 95% CI: 0.32, 1.09). Notably, maternal education emerged as a strong predictor, with adolescents of graduate/postgraduate-educated mothers demonstrating significantly higher literacy scores (B = 1.12, 95% CI: 1.03, 2.27) compared to those whose mothers had no formal education. For the attitude score model (Adjusted R² =48%, F = 12.68, P < 0.001), age emerged as a significant positive predictor (B = 0.42, 95% CI: 0.28, 0.56), indicating improved attitudes with increasing age. Interestingly, neither father’s education nor mother’s occupation showed significant associations with literacy scores, and place of residence (urban versus rural) did not significantly influence either literacy or attitude scores [Table 4].

Discussion

This study provides an overview of cancer literacy and attitudes among the school-going adolescents in Perambalur district, Tamil Nadu, South India. It identifies factors associated with literacy as well as attitude scores offering valuable insights regarding cancer literacy among the adolescent population. It is one of the few studies which have explored the awareness of factors related to cancer in a younger age group. Similar studies among adolescents have been conducted in the developed countries, but it is crucial to investigate the specific factors at play within India.[14]

In this study, 61.8% of the adolescents were aware of the definition of cancer which was comparable to the report by Al-Naggar et al.[15] from Malaysia. However, it is lower compared to 97% literacy reported by Giuseppe et al.[16] from adolescents in Italy. In this study, 84.8% have heard of lung cancer, but only 48.4% have heard of breast cancers, both of which are the most prevalence cancers in India among males and females, respectively. These results were not consistent with the report of Giuseppe et al.,[16] where only 26.3% have heard of prostate cancer and 58% were aware of breast cancer, which are the most prevalent cancers in Italy. This difference can be due to the difference in health educational systems between the nations. Cancer education among students in India is generally considered to be less comprehensive, with significant gaps in awareness about cancer risk factors, early detection methods, and preventative measures, while in the developed nations, cancer education is often more integrated into school curriculums, with a greater emphasis on preventative behaviors and accessible information for students across different age groups.

Participants in this study are familiar with various cancers, but most of them have heard mainly about lung cancer followed by leukemia. However, Abraham et al.[13] from Wisconsin in the United States reported that the adolescents had good knowledge regarding a wide variety of cancers, particularly skin, breast, and lung cancers. Moreover, a few participants in that study displayed knowledge regarding the diagnostic process, including the primary site of cancer, staging, and the biopsy process.[13] Several factors may contribute to this disparity. In the United States, high literacy rates, robust public health infrastructure, extensive cancer education campaigns targeting youth, readily available information, and an emphasis on preventative measures like healthy lifestyles likely play a significant role. These advantages are less common in India, where cancer awareness and access to care are more limited.

The study conducted by Rashid et al.[17] among adolescents in India concluded that the literacy and attitude toward cancer increase with age. This was consistent with the results of this study, which found that age is a statistical significant positive predictor of attitude toward cancer. Similar results were reported by Koirala et al.[18] from Nepal and by Makungu et al.[19] from Tanzania. Recent studies show that the cognitive and critical thinking among adolescents increases with age.[20] This can be largely due to the development of formal operational thinking which enables them to progress beyond concrete thinking and grapple with abstract concepts, hypothetical scenarios, and complex reasoning.[21]

Nutritional literacy is very crucial in the prevention and management of noncommunicable diseases.[22] It empowers individuals to make informed food choices, leading to a healthier diet that reduces the risk of developing chronic conditions. McDonald et al.[23] from Australia concluded that 26% of the adolescents recalled diet as a risk factor for cancer as opposed to 59.6% of the participants in this study who agreed that unhealthy eating habits can lead to cancers in the future. A similar study conducted in the United Kingdom reported only 6.5% literacy.[10] A recent study conducted by Bookari et al.[24] in Saudi Arabia revealed that almost half of the adolescents had poor nutritional literacy.

Good knowledge about cancer risk factors is crucial as it can significantly impact a person’s ability to take preventive measures. Individuals with greater awareness can actively modify lifestyle choices to reduce their risk, while those lacking knowledge may not take necessary precautions. 96.6% and 69.2% of the participants in this study were aware that smoking and alcohol, respectively, were risk factors for cancer, which was consistent with the results of Giuseppe et al.,[16] which reported 97.9% and 69.5%. 30% of the participants from Australia were specific about sun safety risks, while in this study, only 14.9% of the participants were worried about it. Similar studies done in western countries agree with sun safety risks.[25] This difference is probably due to the sunbathing culture of the western countries which is not very prevalent in India.

The mean (± SD) scores for literacy and attitude in this study were 25.9 (±2.76) and 21.04 (±2.36), respectively. The study conducted by Dolatkhah et al.[26] among adolescents in Iran reported the mean (± SD) scores for knowledge and attitude regarding cancer as 42.51 (±3.13) and 52.11 (±3.24), respectively. The difference in the scores is mainly due to the higher number of questions in the study tool used by Dolatkhah et al.[26] in both the domains. The study conducted in Nigeria by Kanmodi et al.[27] reported a low mean (± SD) score of 5.4 (±2.54) for knowledge domain. The disparities in cancer literacy scores across studies can be attributed to three primary factors. Methodological differences, particularly varying numbers of questions in assessment tools, directly impact the scoring ranges, as seen in an Iranian study which used more comprehensive questionnaires yielding higher scores. Sociodemographic variations among study populations, including differences in educational systems and healthcare infrastructure across countries, significantly influence knowledge levels. Additionally, varying exposure to health information and cancer awareness campaigns in different regions contributes to these disparities, as reflected in the contrasting scores between Iranian and Nigerian studies.

In this study, the male gender was found to be a statistically significant positive predictor of literacy regarding cancer. These results were contrary to the report by Makungu et al.[19] from Tanzania where only a minority of the males had a favorable knowledge regarding cancer screening. A recent study done among adolescents in India has shown that females exhibited 18% lower likelihood of health literacy compared to males.[28] The study conducted by Tsuda et al.[29] in Japan also reported that the knowledge scores regarding cancer were higher among women. Differences in cancer literacy between genders across countries can be attributed to a complex interplay of factors including societal gender norms, access to healthcare, education levels, cultural perceptions of health, socioeconomic disparities, and variations in cancer risk factors specific to each gender.

Studies have shown that maternal education has a large impact on the children’s knowledge regarding health.[30] Educated mothers are more likely to know about and practice good health habits, which can lead to better child health outcomes. In this study, mothers who were graduates or postgraduates was a statistically significant positive predictor of literacy regarding cancer. Tsuda et al.[29] also concluded that higher education among mothers was an influential factor for improved knowledge among young adults.

Limitations and recommendations

While these findings provide valuable insights to stimulate further research into cancer prevention literacy, attitudes, and lifestyle behaviors among young populations, it is crucial to interpret the results within the context of several inherent limitations. First, the cross-sectional design of the study precludes a prospective evaluation of how predictor variables influence literacy and health-related behaviors, thereby hindering the establishment of definitive cause-and-effect relationships. Second, the study’s geographical scope is limited to a single district within the state of Tamil Nadu, necessitating cautious generalization of the findings to other regions. Third, the questionnaire employed in this study has not been validated; however, the internal consistency of the domain-specific questions was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha.

Based on the study findings, implementation of targeted educational interventions focusing on cancer-preventive vaccines and risk factors is essential to address the identified knowledge gaps. Development of family-centered literacy programs is crucial, considering the significant influence of maternal education. Specific interventions for rural areas should be designed to bridge the urban–rural knowledge disparity. Integration of cancer literacy programs into school curricula, with emphasis on preventive aspects and common cancers, would be beneficial. Creating age-appropriate educational materials is important, considering the positive correlation between age and attitudes. Regular assessment of cancer literacy among adolescents would help monitor progress and adapt interventions effectively.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that school-going adolescents possess moderate levels of cancer literacy and generally positive attitudes. While India does not have an established cancer literacy health policy, the National Programme for Prevention and Control of Non-Communicable Diseases (NP-NCD) includes cancer prevention and control initiatives. While most adolescents understood the basic concept of cancer and recognized the curability of early-detected cancers, significant knowledge gaps existed regarding cancer-preventive vaccines and risk factors. Maternal education emerged as a strong predictor of cancer literacy, with children of graduate/postgraduate-educated mothers showing significantly higher literacy scores. Gender differences were evident, with males showing higher literacy scores in multivariate analysis. Age emerged as a significant predictor of attitude scores, indicating improved attitudes with increasing age.
