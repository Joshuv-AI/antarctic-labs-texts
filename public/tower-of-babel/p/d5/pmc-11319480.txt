Circadian rhythm sleep loss impairs motor inhibition more than motor execution in continuous action
Authors: Ziying Wang, Tingwei Jiang, Haodan Xu, Chuan Wang, Rixin Tang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Under total sleep deprivation, both inhibitory and motor control are impaired. However, how circadian rhythm sleep loss caused by irregular sleep pattern affects motor inhibition and execution in continuous actions remains unknown. This study utilized a pointing task to investigate the question over 30 days. During regular trials, participants were instructed to tap on a specified location, while in countermanding trials, they were required to countermand their current action. Additionally, there was a control group performed the same task following a normal 24-h rhythm. The results indicated that the decrease in accuracy and the increase in movement time in countermanding trials were more prominent in the shift work group. In contrast, there was no significant difference in reaction time between the two groups. Notably, the shift work group outperformed the control group in terms of movement time in regular trials and radial displacement in countermanding trials. Overall, these results show that circadian rhythm sleep loss predominantly affects inhibitory control, rather than motor control, underscoring the nuanced impacts of sleep disruption on differential aspects of cognitive and motor functions.

Introduction

The 24-h rotation of the Earth impacts not only day and night but also creates circadian rhythms for living organisms. Staying up late or rising early is generally unpleasant for most individuals. Nevertheless, in today’s society, numerous professions such as seafarers1, health care workers2, firefighters, police officers, and factory workers3, require working during nighttime hours4,5. Extended travel across multiple time zones can also lead to circadian rhythm misalignment6. Disruption of circadian rhythms can result in poor sleep quality, low mood, anxiety, and cognitive impairment7. In practical work scenarios, inhibitory control and motor control are essential cognitive abilities that can be significantly affected8. For example, when performing an action, it may be necessary to abruptly stop and then execute a new action. Sleep loss will impair the performance of this task. Thus, this study aimed to investigate how circadian rhythm sleep loss over a 30-day period influenced motor inhibition control and motor execution in a continuous pointing task.

Circadian rhythm sleep loss

The human circadian system regulates sleep, wakefulness, alertness, and cognitive performance. According to a widely accepted theory, the human circadian system comprises two processes: the C (circadian) process and the S (sleep homeostasis) process9,10. The S process represents sleep homeostasis, which accumulates with each waking episode. This process also leads to poorer cognitive performance with longer periods of continuous wakefulness11. In contrast, the C process reflects the endogenous circadian rhythm, with a certain phase difference from the S process. It typically peaks in the early evening to counteract the sleep pressure of the S process, promoting wakefulness before gradually decreasing. This process leads to cyclical changes in cognitive performance11. Under normal sleep conditions, these two processes overlap, allowing cognitive performance to remain relatively stable during awake periods of around ten hours. However, in reality, there are always certain circumstances that can disrupt the regularity of circadian rhythm sleep, thus disrupting the coordination of these two processes and impairing cognitive performance.

There are various patterns that can lead to circadian rhythm sleep loss. Per the International Classification of Sleep Disorders-Third Edition (ICSD-3) criteria, the circadian rhythm sleep loss under investigation in this study is led by irregular sleep–wake rhythm patterns12. This loss is characterized by the absence of a clearly defined sleep–wake pattern, often involving multiple short sleep episodes within a 24-h period. Individuals with this loss must sleep when their alertness levels are high, leading to poor sleep quality13. Conversely, they are required to be awake during periods of low alertness and high sleep pressure, resulting in compromised neurocognitive performance and increased drowsiness14. Therefore, irregular sleep–wake rhythm disorder encompasses both sleep deprivation and being awake during the circadian nadir for alertness15.

To streamline the study design and avoid unnecessary complexity, this study concentrated on investigating the overall effects of sleep loss caused by irregular sleep patterns rather than cyclical circadian rhythm. This is because the cyclicity of C process partially compensates for the effects of sleep loss. For example, in an experiment involving total sleep deprivation, it can be observed that subjective sleepiness, psychomotor vigilance, cognitive throughput, and recall memory all show a slight improvement after staying awake continuously for 32 h compared to the early morning hours after a night of sleep deprivation16. Can it be said that sleep deprivation actually enhances cognitive performance? Obviously not, because compared to the early morning of the previous day (after normal sleep), cognitive performance shows a significant decline. This is the result of the overlapping of the C process and S process. Since the C process improves early, cognitive performance in the morning will partially recover, but the decline in the S process caused by sleep deprivation is not reversed, so cognitive performance at the same time as the previous day is still weakened.

Therefore, different research objectives lead to different experimental designs. Studies that focus on the overall sleep loss effect need to minimize the influence of the circadian rhythm as much as possible. Typical within-subject designs involve alternating days of sleep deprivation with normal sleep days, with tasks performed at consistent times on both types of days to facilitate comparison of differences4,17. Between-subject designs entail separating participants into groups experiencing sleep deprivation and normal sleep, with tasks also carried out at consistent times18,19. These methodological approaches aid in minimizing the impact of circadian misalignment. Conversely, studies that focus on the circadian rhythm typically only use within-subject designs, where task performance is measured multiple times within a day and correlated with rhythm indicators like melatonin and body temperature16,20,21. By opting for a between-subject design and conducting tasks at fixed times, this study underscored its focus on investigating the effects of sleep loss rather than delving into circadian rhythm research.

One distinctive aspect of this study was the utilization of a relatively extended period lasting 30 days. In contrast, most laboratory studies have centered on short-term total sleep deprivation, typically lasting 24 h without sleep22–25. The rationale behind this choice was rooted in the understanding that the effects of long-term circadian rhythm sleep loss might not be as immediately observable as those resulting from total sleep deprivation10. Moreover, the human body possesses compensatory mechanisms to mitigate the impact of mild sleep loss21,26–28. Therefore, extending the study timeline was deemed essential to accentuate the consequences of sleep deprivation by accumulating sleep debt29–31. Furthermore, total sleep deprivation is less common in real-world settings compared to chronic partial sleep deprivation32. Professions such as seafaring33 and naval service34 often involve prolonged periods at sea characterized by disrupted sleep patterns. While total sleep deprivation may yield more pronounced effects, its ecological validity may not necessarily surpass that of the approach adopted in this study.

Effects of sleep loss on inhibitory control

Inhibitory control, a vital component of executive function, encompasses the capacity to surmount internal or external distractions, regulate attention, thoughts, behaviors, emotions, and make decisions that align with goals35. This cognitive function is notably sensitive to the effects of sleep deprivation. In the commonly employed Go-NoGo paradigm, participants are tasked with swiftly responding to a sequence of stimuli. While the majority of the stimuli prompt a response (Go stimuli), a small fraction requires participants to refrain from responding (NoGo stimuli), representing a form of response inhibition. In conditions of sleep loss, individuals are more prone to errors in such tasks, often mistakenly pressing a key in response to NoGo stimuli36–38. In addition to the Go-NoGo paradigm, the Stroop task is frequently utilized to assess inhibitory control, with many studies demonstrating that sleep loss adversely affects reaction time and accuracy in this task39–41. From a neurophysiological standpoint, inhibitory control is closely linked to the function of the prefrontal cortex42–44. Interestingly, sleep deprivation has been shown to significantly impact brain activity in this brain region45–47. Taken together, the body of evidence indicates that sleep deprivation impairs inhibitory control.

However, the conventional Go-NoGo paradigm may not fully capture the intricacies of inhibitory control. The Go-NoGo paradigm primarily assesses responses based on the motor action of pressing a key, raising the possibility that motor execution plays a role in these experiments. For instance, van Peer et al. implemented the Go-NoGo paradigm in a shooting scenario, where participants had to decide whether to shoot (by pressing a key) based on a dummy depicting either a gun or a mobile phone8. In this context, reaction time was considered indicative of action tendency. Intriguingly, their findings showed that partial sleep deprivation did not have a significant impact on reaction time but notably decreased task accuracy. This disparity implies the existence of separate mechanisms governing inhibitory control and motor control.

Furthermore, the typical paradigm places inhibitory control before the action rather than during the action process. In the case of key pressing, successful inhibition means the key pressing no longer occurs, effectively inhibiting the entire key press action. In reality, many inhibitions occur during the motor process, such as sudden braking while accelerating in driving. Key pressing tasks are not well-suited to investigate motor inhibition and execution in continuous actions. This concern was addressed by Kevin et al. through a modification of the Go-NoGo paradigm into a pointing task48. In this revised task, participants were presented with three dim red dots on the screen, with one dot transitioning to bright red, indicating the need for a quick tap (Go stimulus). Occasionally, the bright red dot would swiftly move after a slight delay, requiring participants to promptly countermand the tapping action (NoGo stimulus). By implementing this innovative approach, Kevin et al. heightened the motor action element of key pressing, enabling the assessment of additional parameters such as hand movement speed and timing. It is also possible to investigate motor inhibition and execution in a continuous action. Their comparison of alcohol's effects on this task revealed impairment in inhibition accuracy rather than in execution indicators. Given these advantages, this study also employed the similar pointing task.

Is motor execution impaired by sleep loss?

The impact of sleep deprivation on motor execution remains uncertain. The variability in findings across motor control concepts and paradigms contrasts with the more consistent results in studies on inhibitory control. Based on daily experience, our neuromuscular function appears to be relatively robust, and this is generally supported by evidence. For instance, grip strength, a key indicator of neuromuscular function, remains largely unaffected by sleep deprivation41,49,50. Similarly, standing balance50 and obstacle navigation abilities23 appear stable under conditions of sleep loss. Even seemingly intricate tasks like handwriting exhibit minimal kinematic changes in response to sleep deprivation49. However, neuromuscular function is just one influencing factor of motor execution, perception and movement planning also have an impact on motor execution51.

Experimental evidence suggests that motor control tasks with high perceptual information integration demands are susceptible to the effects of sleep loss. For instance, the basic maintenance of standing balance may not be notably impacted by sleep deprivation50, but the capacity to regulate posture in the presence of external disturbances is compromised52. In a study by Pedão et al., two distinct motor tasks were employed: one task involved static object holding, while the other required rhythmic shaking of the object at a specified frequency53. Findings from force measurements on the object indicated that sleep deprivation led to a decline in grip force coordination during the shaking task, while stability was maintained during the holding task. Nevertheless, it is essential to acknowledge that the pointing task utilized in this study did not necessitate continual adjustments. From a perceptual standpoint, the impact was unlikely to be substantial.

The scant discussion regarding the impact of sleep loss on motor plan is notable. A study by Fournier et al. addressed this gap by employing a multiple action plan task to explore this aspect18. In this study, participants were presented with two stimulus cues representing action 1 and action 2, but were required to carry out action 2 before action 1. Some trials introduced conflicting actions between the two, increasing the complexity of the action plan. Results revealed that conflicting trials led to slower reaction time and increased lapses, indicating partial repetition costs. The study indicated that participants in the sleep-deprived group exhibited more lapses overall, although there was no significant rise in partial repetition costs. This suggests that while sleep deprivation may impair action plan in a general sense, it does not significantly affect all components of motor plan. Consequently, formulating a determinate hypothesis regarding motor plan under conditions of sleep loss is challenging.

Finally, the 30-day protocol implemented in this study necessitates consideration of potential practice effects. While the impact of practice effects on inhibitory control may not be substantial, given the challenging nature of training inhibitory control skills54,55, the scenario may differ for motor control. Notably, Jasper et al. observed enhanced performance in a motor task following sleep deprivation21. In their study, participants were tasked with dynamically adjusting grip force according to on-screen instructions. Contrary to expectations, grip force tracking precision improved rather than deteriorated under conditions of sleep deprivation. One plausible explanation for the unexpected performance improvement may lie in Jasper et al.'s utilization of a circadian rhythm protocol, encompassing a total of 12 tasks across a two-day timeframe, thus underscoring the potential influence of practice effects. Consequently, the presence of practice effects could mitigate the deterioration of motor execution.

The present study

In this pilot study, the primary focus was on investigating the impact of circadian rhythm sleep loss on motor inhibition and motor execution within a continuous pointing task. Several unique aspects distinguish this research. Firstly, the study concentrated on the effects of sleep loss resulting from circadian rhythm disruption, rather than delving into the intricacies of circadian rhythm itself (both the study's paradigm and data do not support an in-depth examination of circadian rhythm). Secondly, to heighten ecological validity and account for the potentially subtler effects of circadian rhythm sleep disorder, an extended period of 30 days was implemented. Thirdly, adjustments were made to the inhibitory control paradigm to discern cognitive inhibitory control from motor control during task performance.

Specifically, this study utilized the pointing task introduced by Kevin et al.48 and incorporated a touchscreen to capture participants' reaction time, movement time, accuracy, and tap radial displacement (the distance between the tap location and the target location). The task comprised regular trials, where participants were instructed to promptly tap on the illuminated target dot, and countermanding trials, where a cue prompted participants to countermand their action and tap back on the original location. Two participant groups were randomly assigned: the shift work group, subjected to disrupted sleep patterns due to shift work in a controlled laboratory setting, and the control group maintaining regular sleep and daily routines. Both groups completed the pointing task eight times every four days over a 30-day period. Subjective sleep quality assessments were administered thrice during the study to evaluate sleep loss presence in both groups. Prior to the study, a pre-test was conducted within the shift work group to ensure comparable baseline conditions between the two groups.

Our hypothesis posited that sleep loss would yield distinct effects on both motor inhibition and motor execution within the same task. Drawing from consistent findings in prior sleep deprivation research regarding inhibitory control, we anticipated a compromised inhibitory control performance in this study, as evidenced by (1) a decline in countermand accuracy among participants in the shift work group. Furthermore, it was expected that (2) participants' movement time in countermanding trials in the shift work group would increase, given the necessity to countermand their actions. Conversely, considering the inconsistent findings alluded to earlier, it was hypothesized that motor execution would remain relatively intact. Nonetheless, given the multiple stages involved in action, varied outcomes across different metrics were anticipated. Specifically, we predicted (3) longer reaction times in the shift work group due to the involvement of stimulus perception and motor plan. Similarly, (4) the movement time in countermanding trials for the shift work group was expected to be prolonged as a result of the increased time required for action re-planning. Conversely, neuromuscular function was projected not to be impaired, leading to the expectation that (5) there would be no significant inter-group discrepancy in movement time during regular trials. Similarly, (6) radial displacement, reflecting tapping variance, was not expected to show substantial inter-group difference.

Materials and method

Ethic

The research protocol received approval from the Ethics Review Committee of the Naval Medical Center of the People's Liberation Army and the Ethics Review Committee of Nanjing University. Before the study, participants provided written informed consent to participate. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Participants

We first recruited 11 male volunteers (Mean age = 23.4, SD = 2.6) from the university recruitment platform to participate in the shift work group. They received a higher remuneration and underwent an additional medical examination free of charge before the start of the study. Based on the age distribution of the shift work group, we recruited 12 male participants (Mean age = 23.6, SD = 2.7) to serve as the control group. These participants received lower remuneration. There was no significant difference in age between the two groups of participants (t20.9 = − 0.20, p = 0.845). All participants were right-handed, in good overall health, and had no infectious, chronic, psychological, or visual disorders. The two groups of participants were not aware of each other.

Study design

The study was a 2 (group, between-subjects: shift work/control) × 3 (stage, within-subjects: early/middle/late) × 2 (trial type, within-subjects: regular trials/countermanding trials) mixed longitudinal design. During the 30-day study, a pointing task was conducted once every four days. The program recorded reaction time, movement time, accuracy and radial displacement. A total of 8 measurements were taken, with the average of the sessions 1, 2, and 3 combined into the early stage, the sessions 4, 5, and 6 combined into the middle stage, and the sessions 7 and 8 combined into the late stage. Each session consisted of 150 trials, including 120 regular trials and 30 countermanding trials, and required 15 min to complete. In addition, subjective sleep quality was measured three times during the early, middle, and late stages of the study.

Laboratory environment

The shift work group conducted the study in a special closed laboratory. This laboratory was unable to communicate with the outside world and lacked natural light exposure. Tablets, VR glasses, and paper books were allowed to be carried into the laboratory, but none of the electronic devices were connected to the Internet. The laboratory was divided into a sleep chamber and a work chamber. The sleep chamber provided beds for the participants to rest and sleep, and remained in a darkened state. Additionally, each bed was equipped with blackout curtains to further block out light. The tasks, on the other hand, were conducted exclusively in the work chamber, which maintains constant indoor lighting and a temperature of 26 ℃.

The control group undergone the study in a normal psychology laboratory in the university, where temperature was controlled under the same conditions as the shift work group. Participants were free to leave the laboratory and engaged in unrestricted activities after each task was completed.

Sleep quality scale

We used the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI)56 to measure the subjective sleep quality of the participants. This self-assessment scale includes evaluations of sleep duration, sleep disturbances, sleep quality, and subjective energy levels (for details see supplementary information). The higher the score, the more abnormal sleep events there are, indicating poor sleep quality. We assessed the sleep quality of the both groups three times during the early, middle, and late stages of the study. The participants should self-assess their sleep conditions during the previous study period. Measurements were taken on the days 2, 16, and 30 (session 1, between session 4–5, and session 8) in the shift work group, and days 1, 13, and 29 (session 1, session 4, and session 8) in the control group.

Pointing task

Equipment

The pointing task was conducted using a Windows laptop equipped with a Lenovo 23.6-inch touch screen, with a resolution of 1920 × 1080 pixels and a refresh rate of 60 Hz. The task was programmed using MATLAB 2021 along with Psychtoolbox-357. The touch screen was positioned on a table at a height of approximately 75 cm. The screen was tilted at a 15° angle, with the lower edge approximately 15 cm away from the body (Fig. 1). Task procedure

As shown in Fig. 2, the pointing task was adapted from the work of Kevin et al.48. At the beginning of each trial, four dots appeared on the screen, with the top three dots being dark red and the bottom dot being white. The diameter of all dots was 50 pixels. The white dot was horizontally positioned at the center of the screen, and vertically positioned 100 pixels above the bottom. The two red dots on the sides were positioned 320 pixels away from the edges horizontally (one-sixth of the screen width), and vertically positioned at the middle of the screen. The middle red dot was horizontally positioned at the center of the screen, and vertically positioned 876 pixels above the bottom. This arrangement ensured that the three red dots were equidistant from the white dot.Figure 2Procedure of the pointing task. Participants initially placed their fingers on the white dot and waited. Procedure of the pointing task. Participants initially placed their fingers on the white dot and waited. After a random delay of 1–2 s, a random red dot would illuminate. In regular trials, participants simply tapped on the illuminated red dot. However, in the countermanding trials, the dot would suddenly displace after a delay of 0.15 s. After the displace, the participants were required to immediately stop tapping the red dot and instead tapping on the white dot again.

Participants were instructed to continuously press and maintain contact with the white dot while focusing their attention on the three red dots positioned above it. After a random delay of 1–2 s (uniform distribution), one of the red dots changed from dark red to bright red. Participants were required to swiftly release the white dot and press the bright red dot as quickly as possible. These regular trials account for 80% (120 trials) in one session. To prevent spending too much time on motor plan, we required the participants to tap the screen within 1 s after releasing the white dot. If no tap was detected within the allotted time, the trial was considered invalid (not marked as failed) and was excluded from the analysis.

During 20% of the trials (30 trials), the movement of the participant's finger from the white dot triggered either a leftward or rightward displacement (fixed 100 px) of the red target dots. The displacement would occur fixedly 0.15 s after the participants released their fingers from the white dot. Once the participants detected the displacement, they were not allowed to tap on the bright red dot (inhibiting the current action), but required to tap the starting white dot (performing a new action). This movement also had a time limit, and within 1 s after the displacement of the red dot, participants had to tap the screen to prevent overthinking. If no tap was detected after the time limit, the trial was considered invalid (rather than failed) and would be excluded from the analysis. Once a tap was detected within the time limit, the trial would immediately end, regardless of where the participant tapped (even if they wrongly tapped on the bright red dot). Subsequent data analysis would be based on the coordinates of the tap to determine the correctness.

Altogether, each session consisted of a total of 150 trials, including 120 regular trials and 30 countermanded trials. The order of different types of trials was completely random. The three positions of the red target dot were also in a random order, but their frequencies were balanced. Specifically, in 120 regular trials, the left, middle, and right positions each appeared 40 times. In 30 countermanded trials, the left, middle, and right positions each appeared 10 times. The interval between trials was set to 2 s.

Pre-test

Because group was a between-subjects design, we conducted a pretest task before the shift schedule for the shift work group (which could be considered as session 0) to measure their performance in that task. The control group, on the other hand, did not undergo an additional pretest as there were no manipulations applied to this group. We would compare the task results from session 0 for the shift work group and session 1 for the control group to examine if the initial states of the two groups were similar.

General procedure

Shift work group

The study by Ma et al.58 provided us with a reference shift work schedule, which can be used to explore circadian rhythm sleep loss. Therefore, our research was based on their paradigm to conduct shift work (shift work pattern remained the same, with offset in timings). Participants resided in a controlled laboratory environment for a duration of 30 days. Before entering the laboratory, they performed a pre-test pointing task. After entering the laboratory, the participants were randomly divided into three groups, each consisting of 3–4 individuals. Each day was divided into 7 work shifts. These work shifts would slide down one level each day, as shown in the Fig. 3. The participants should work 2–3 times per day according to the assigned groups and schedule. While one group was working, the other two groups were allowed to rest, entertainment, and sleep inside the laboratory. The daytime timeframe was divided into three time periods, and the participants were scheduled to perform the pointing task every 4 days during their assigned work shift based on their groups. Specifically, the task was performed on days 2, 6, 10, 14, 18, 22, 26, and 30. On days 2, 16, and 30 (session 1, between session 4–5, and session 8), the participants were required to complete the PSQI sleep questionnaire in morning. During the other work shifts, participants were assigned other tasks that were not relevant to the current study.Figure 3Shift work schedule. Control group

Participants in the control group were instructed to visit the laboratory and complete the study in the daytime. They were also divided into three groups and performed the task at the same time in the shift work group. For example, shift work group A performed the task during the first daytime work shift in session 1, while control group A′ also completed the task at the time of first work shift. shift work group A in session 2 performed the task during the second work shift, control group A′ also performed the task at time of the second work shift, and so on. On days 1, 13, and 29 (session 1, session 4, and session 8), participants completed the PSQI questionnaire. Outside of the study sessions, participants were not constrained by the study and were free to engage in their daily activities. However, we asked them to maintain a regular sleep schedule, avoid strenuous exercising and consuming caffeine-containing beverages before the study.

Data analysis

We employed mixed linear regression in R to model various indicators, followed by conducting ANOVA tests on the independent variables of the regression model. Mixed linear regression was chosen instead of direct ANOVA due to the complexity of the design (including both within-subject and between-subject variables), the non-equivalent group design, and the variability in the number of trials across groups. Mixed linear regression models are better equipped to handle such complex data59–61. Specifically, in R, we utilized the “lmer” and “lme4” packages62,63 to fit the following regression models for the pointing task: Dependent Variables ~ Stage * Group * Trial Type + (1 | Subject). For the sleep questionnaire data, the model used was: PSQI ~ Stage * Group + (1 | Subject). Subsequently, “anova” function in R was applied to the regression models, i.e., anova(model), to calculate the effects of independent variables. For variables exhibiting interaction effects, further simple effects analyses were conducted using t-tests. We also conducted a power analysis using the “pwr” package in R.

Reaction time

Figures 7(1, 2) and 8(1) visually present the results of reaction time. The results of the reaction time indicated a marginally significant main effect of stage (F2, 105 = 2.70, p = 0.072, f2 = 0.05). When considering the descriptive statistics, there was a decreasing trend in reaction time, with 0.467 s in the early stage, 0.451 s in the middle stage, and 0.450 s in the late stage, which could indicate practice effect. None of the other main effect and interaction were significant. The non-significance of the trial type main effect was expected, as there was no available information to differentiate trial types at this pointing task movement. The non-significance of the main effect of group and interaction effects suggested that the shift work did not interfere with reaction time.

Movement time

Figures 7(3, 4) and 8(2) visually present the results of movement time. The results of the movement time showed a significant main effect of trial type (F1, 105 = 1358.61, p = < 0.001, f2 = 12.94) and an interaction effect between group and trial type (F1, 105 = 14.57, p = < 0.001, f2 = 0.14). The significant main effect of trial type was evident as countermanding trials involved an additional inhibited process. The interaction effect between group and trial type warranted further analysis. Simple effect analysis revealed that in regular trials, the movement time of the shift work group (0.51 s) was faster than that of the control group (0.55 s), with a significant difference (p = 0.011). However, in countermanding trials, there was no significant difference (p = 0.218) in movement time between the shift work group (0.98 s) and the control group (0.94 s). We also conducted a supplementary analysis to examine the trial type difference in movement time between the two groups. The results indicated that the difference in the shift work group was greater than the control group (0.48 vs. 0.39, p < 0.001). These results indicated that the shift work group exhibited faster movements in regular trials but similar movements in countermanding trials. Since countermanding trials involved inhibitory process and new motor plan, while regular trials did not, this significant interaction suggested that the inhibitory process or motor plan of the shift work group was affected. No significant effects were observed for other independent variables.

Accuracy

Figures 7(5, 6) and 8(3) visually present the results of accuracy. The accuracy results revealed a significant main effect of trial type (F1, 105 = 53.04, p = < 0.001, f2 = 0.51) and an interaction effect between group and trial type (F1, 105 = 4.25, p = 0.042, f2 = 0.04). Similar to the results of movement time, the main effect of trial type was expected, as countermanding trials were inherently more challenging. The significant interaction effect between group and trial type was particularly meaningful. In regular trials, the accuracy of the shift work group was 0.94 and the control group was 0.91. In countermanding trials, the accuracy of the shift work group was 0.76 and the control group was 0.81. However, simple effect analysis showed that both comparisons did not reach statistical significance (ps = 0.19 & 0.17). Similar to the approach taken for movement time, we conducted a supplementary analysis to examine the difference in trial type between the two groups. The results showed that the difference in the shift work group was greater than the control group (0.19 vs 0.10, p = 0.04).

Therefore, it indicated overall that countermanding trials had a stronger detrimental effect on accuracy for the shift work group. No significant effects were found for the remaining independent variables.

Radial displacement

Figures 7(7, 8) and 8(4) visually present the results of radial displacement. We also observed a significant main effect of trial type (F1, 105 = 7.66, p = 0.007, f2 = 0.07) and an interaction effect between trial type and group (F1, 105 = 18.65, p < 0.001, f2 = 0.18). The radial displacement in regular trials (12.00 px) was higher than that in countermanding trials (11.43 px). Considering that the analysis of radial displacement excluded failed trials, the fact that participants were able to maintain accurate tap even after experiencing countermanding may indicate a higher level of performance in these trials. Clearly, the significant interaction effect between group and trial type was worthy of further discussion. Simple effect analysis revealed that in regular trials, the radial displacement of the shift work group (12.0 px) was virtually equivalent to that of the control group (12.0 px), with no significant difference (p = 0.79). However, in countermanding trials, the radial displacement of the shift work group (10.5 px) was much lower than that of the control group (12.3 px), with a significant difference (p = 0.002). This effect was worth noting because it was in the opposite direction of movement time and accuracy. In countermanding trials, their precision in making (correct) tap was even better than that of the control group.

Discussion

The current study aimed to examine how circadian rhythm sleep loss affect motor inhibition and motor execution in a pointing task. Over the course of the 30-day study, shift work group followed a three-shift work schedule, while control group maintained a regular routine. This shift work schedule caused damage to the participants' sleep quality, as evidenced by significantly lower subjective sleep quality in the shift work group compared to the control group during all three stages of the study. In particular, during the late stage, the difference became greater, indicating the cumulative sleep debt. A pre-test was conducted on the shift work group since a between-subjects design was used. The results showed no significant initial differences between the two groups.

Building upon this framework, our results revealed that circadian rhythm sleep loss significantly affected accuracy, movement time, and radial displacement, primarily through the notable interaction of trial type and group. The statistical power of these interactions was 0.64, 0.19, and 0.54 respectively. However, there was no discernible influence on reaction time. Subsequent simple effects analysis indicated that the drop in accuracy and uptick in movement time during countermanding trials were more pronounced in the shift work group, implying a lesser adaptation to such tasks compared to the control group. Conversely, in terms of radial displacement, no significant disparity between the groups was observed in regular trials; however, in countermanding trials, the shift work group actually surpassed the control group. These findings collectively suggest that circadian rhythm sleep loss impaired motor inhibition, while its impact on motor execution was relatively modest.

Motor inhibition

In our study, we assessed motor inhibition mainly through the measurement of accuracy. The results indicated a significant interaction between trial type and group. Further analysis of simple effects revealed that in regular trials, the detrimental effect of countermanding trials on accuracy was stronger in the shift work group. This result clearly indicated that the motor inhibition of the shift work was impaired. Another indicator, movement time, which also involved the inhibitory process, showed similar results with a significant interaction between trial type and group. Similarly, in regular trials, the shift work group had similar movement time compared to the control group, while in countermanding trials, the shift work group had significantly slower movement time compared to the control group. This meant that the shift work group consumed more time to countermand tapping, indicating impairment in the motor inhibition. In summary, our first and second hypotheses were validated. The shift work group had lower accuracy and longer countermanding time (movement time in countermanding trials).

These results align with previous research on the effects of sleep loss. As early as 2000, Harrison and Horne65 noted that sleep loss can disrupt the ability to inhibit interference. They suggested that this impairment may be linked to the activity of the prefrontal cortex (PFC). Numerous studies have shown that sleep loss significantly disrupts PFC neural activity45,46,66. The PFC is closely associated with executive functions, including inhibitory control67. When individuals are sleep-deprived, they exhibit significantly increased reaction times and error rates when performing classic inhibitory control tasks like the Go/NoGo paradigm36,37,41,68.

Similar performance decrements associated with sleep loss have been observed in other inhibitory control tasks, such as the Stroop task39–41. In general, there is extensive evidence of the detrimental effects of sleep loss on inhibitory control. The feature of this study lied in further extrapolating this conclusion from total sleep deprivation to circadian rhythm sleep disorders. In addition, we placed the discussion of inhibitory control during the process of motor (motor inhibition). The outcome of impaired inhibitory control was consistent whether it was inhibiting the occurrence of an action or countermand an ongoing action.

Furthermore, we found that the impairment of motor inhibition did not improve with time. We did not find any significant main effects or interactions related to movement time and accuracy across stages. In other words, not only did the control group's performance show little change over time, but the same for the shift work group. The phenomenon of minimal practice effects in inhibitory control in the control group could be understood, as previous research has yielded similar results. For example, Invernizzi et al.54 used 12-week (consisting of six one-hour lessons) physical training to improve inhibitory control in adolescents. Only the varied practice group showed significant improvement in inhibitory control, while the constant practice group showed limited improvement. Similarly, Hartmann et al.55 employed a Go/NoGo task with 12 blocks of 80 trials to train participants' inhibitory control. However, they did not find any significant main effects or interactions in reaction time or accuracy across sessions. It is evident that improving inhibitory control is not an easy task, and in the absence of specific training in our study, it was foreseeable that the relevant measures did not improve significantly.

Since the practice effect of motor inhibition was small, it would be expected that the presence of cumulative sleep debt would lead to a decline in performance over time for the shift work group. However, this was not consistent with the actual data. We speculated that it might be due to the body's compensatory mechanisms at play. In fact, human body possesses some ability to compensate for sleep loss. For instance, during sleep deprivation, the lateral prefrontal cortex, bilateral inferior parietal lobes, and bilateral temporal cortex exhibit enhanced neural activity46,69,70. Hormonal regulation also serves as a compensatory mechanism71,72. Perhaps the presence of these mechanisms resulted in the stabilization of inhibitory control for the shift work group at a certain level, without further decline. In addition, cognitive strategies might also play a role. Although no overall speed-accuracy tradeoff phenomenon was found (in fact, increased movement time, but decreased accuracy), at the individual level, we found a positive correlation between PSQI scores and both movement time and accuracy in the countermanding trials. Also, PSQI scores were significantly negatively correlated with radial displacement. This meant that participants who perceived poor sleep quality had higher movement time, higher accuracy and lower radial displacement, indicating a speed-accuracy tradeoff. In other words, some participants employed cognitive strategies to stabilize their task performance. In summary, compensatory mechanisms in the human body and individual cognitive strategies might have counteracted the cumulative sleep debt, preventing further decline in task performance.

Motor execution

In contrast to motor inhibition, motor execution was not significantly disrupted by sleep loss. Firstly, hypothesis 3 was not supported as there was no notable difference in reaction time between the shift work group and the control group. Hypothesis 4 was confirmed as the shift work group did display higher movement time in countermanding trials, possibly due to a slower inhibitory process. Additionally, hypotheses 5 and 6 were exceeded in their expectations. In regular trials, the movement time of the shift work group was markedly lower than that of the control group. Similarly, in regular trials, the radial displacement between both groups was nearly identical; however, in countermanding trials, the shift work group showed even smaller radial displacement compared to the control group. In conclusion, motor execution remained resilient in the face of sleep loss. It appeared that perception, motor plan, and motor movement were not significantly impaired.

From a neuromuscular standpoint, the detrimental effects of sleep loss do not seem apparent. Simple tasks such as grip strength41,49,50, maintaining stand50, and overcome obstacles23 do not show significant changes due to sleep loss. In this research, we primarily employed movement time and radial displacement as metrics for evaluating neuromuscular function. The hypothesis underlying these measures posits that if neuromuscular function was compromised, movement speed may decline, and stability might be affected, leading to longer movement times and increased radial displacement. The outcomes of this study largely aligned with this premise, as no notable increase in radial displacement was observed in the shift work group in comparison to the control group, and there was no significant disparity in movement time during regular trials compared to the control group.

However, neuromuscular standpoint failed to elucidate the superior performance of the shift work group. In actuality, the shift work group demonstrated quicker movement times in regular trials compared to the control group and exhibited reduced radial displacement in countermanding trials. Given the opposing directions of the interactions between movement time and radial displacement, we proposed the existence of a phenomenon akin to a speed-accuracy tradeoff in motor control. This tradeoff suggested that individuals traded longer movement times for smaller radial displacement. The findings related to the PSQI supported this assertion. In countermanding trials, there was a positive correlation between PSQI scores and movement time, but a negative correlation with radial displacement. This indicates that individuals perceiving poorer sleep quality were inclined to adopt this tradeoff strategy.

When discussing the movement time in countermanding trials, it was crucial to consider the motor plan. Subsequent to inhibiting the previous action, a new motor plan was required to tap the white dot. It was challenging to discern whether the significant interaction in movement time stemmed from impaired inhibitory (countermanding) processes or compromised motor plan. Nevertheless, if a distinction had to be made, we posited that the impaired inhibitory process likely played a primary role. This assertion was corroborated by the significant accuracy results, which directly indicate motor inhibition impairment due to sleep loss. Conversely, no statistically significant outcomes were found for reaction time, which partly reflects the speed of the motor plan. These findings suggest that the motor plan may not have been compromised.

This result was in contrast to the findings of Fournier et al., where the reaction time in the action plan task was significantly higher in the sleep deprivation group18. However, their task was more complex as participants had to execute two actions based on symbolic cues, with the actions differing not only in direction but also in the left or right hand. In contrast, the task in our study was a simple pointing task before the target dot displacement, resembling a choice reaction task. In fact, Ghanbari et al. demonstrated that 24-h sleep deprivation did not significantly impair simple reaction time and choice reaction time73. Additionally, the task in our study was repeated multiple times, potentially resulting in a highly automated motor plan. This was evidenced by the marginally significant main effect of reaction time in stages. Highly automated processes are less prone to the deleterious effects of sleep deprivation74,75. Hence, the conflicting results could be attributed to the disparities in task paradigms. The task employed in this study may not have exerted sufficient pressure on the motor plan to detect the effects of sleep loss. Further research is warranted to explore whether circadian rhythm sleep loss impacts the motor plan.

Finally, we also analyzed the changes in motor execution over time. Overall, we only observed a marginally significant main effect of stage on reaction time and no significant interaction between stage and group. This partially eliminates the possibility that the differences in motor control between the two groups were solely attributed to varying practice effects. Nonetheless, as discussed in the section on motor inhibition, considering the accumulation of sleep debt, we would anticipate the shift work group to display a decline in performance as the stages progressed. Remarkably, the results indicate that both groups manifested similar performance changes across stages. This once again suggests the existence of a compensatory mechanism that mitigates the exacerbating effects of sleep loss.

Limitations

This study had several limitations due to objective constraints, which can be summarized in the following three aspects. First, this study conducted a 30-day closed experiment to investigate the effects of long-term circadian rhythm sleep loss, which was a major strength of this study. However, this led to difficulties in participant recruitment and experimental support, resulting in only 11 participants from the shift work group completing the experiment. This insufficient sample size affected the effect size of the data and made the study resemble a pilot study. Therefore, we suggest for future research to expand the sample size or include recovery period data if feasible. Second, the study design was subjected to objective constraints. For instance, the tasks were conducted every 4 days, resulting in unequal distributions of the rhythms. Third, despite controlling for task duration and measuring participants' sleep quality, there were still other irrelevant variables that were difficult to effectively control. These factors include social isolation, lack of natural light exposure, differences in diet, daily screen use, any stress factors and potential interference from other tasks due to the enclosed environment of the shift work group. Furthermore, we were unable to monitor the activities of the participants in the control group once they left the laboratory, despite providing verbal instructions.

Conclusion

In this 30-day shift work study utilizing pointing tasks, the findings revealed a substantial impairment in motor inhibition due to circadian rhythm sleep loss, while motor execution remained largely unaffected. Specifically, in countermanding trials, the decline in accuracy and the increase in movement time were more pronounced in the shift work group, indicating that it was more difficult for the shift work group to complete the inhibitory task. In contrast, there was no significant difference in reaction time between the two groups. In regular trials, the shift group had shorter movement time. Similarly, in countermanding trials, the shift work group also had smaller radial displacement. These results suggest that the shift work group did not experience significant impairment in motor plan and execution during the pointing task. In summary, under the influence of circadian rhythm sleep loss, impairments in motor inhibition were more pronounced, while motor execution remained a certain level of robustness.
