Differential introgression of mutations reveals selection in spruce
Authors: Dafu Ru, Hui Zhu, Zhiqiang Lu, Yongshuai Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNatural selection plays a pivotal role in maintaining genetic boundaries by counteracting the potentially deleterious effects of alleles introduced through hybridization. Here, we investigated this process through a comparative population genomic analysis of three endemic varieties of Picea likiangensis (vars. likiangensis, linzhiensis, and rubescens) across the Qinghai-Tibetan Plateau (QTP) and adjacent regions.ResultsLeveraging genome-wide single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) data from 193 individuals, we documented pervasive genetic admixture among these taxa, yet observed a pronounced asymmetric pattern of introgression: synonymous polymorphisms (Hs) exhibited significantly higher introgression levels than non-synonymous polymorphisms (Hn). This pattern indicates that purifying selection may actively eliminate maladaptive non-synonymous mutations, whereas neutrally evolving loci continue to undergo gene flow. Moreover, the relative hybrid index (ωH = Hn/Hs) was lower in populations adjacent to pure rubescens stands, suggesting stronger selection against introgressed alleles in the core range of the recipient taxon rather than a simple effect of geographic distance. In addition, recombination rate was significantly associated with introgression bias, implying that recombination facilitates the removal of maladaptive genomic segments following hybridization.ConclusionsOur findings underscore the pivotal role of comparative population genomics in elucidating how natural selection shapes introgression and contributes to the maintenance of genetic boundaries during speciation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12915-026-02684-7.

Background

Natural selection plays a significant role in the introgression process, which involves the transfer of genetic variation from a donor population to a recipient population following hybridization and subsequent crosses [1–3]. Genetic variation, particularly non-synonymous mutations, affects the fitness of hybrids by potentially introducing beneficial or harmful effects on traits [4, 5]. Consequently, these introduced alleles may undergo different selective processes. One such process is the purging of deleterious mutations from the donor taxa, which helps improve the fitness of the hybrids [4]. This purging process can significantly alter the relative proportions of synonymous to non-synonymous variations, leading to diverse rates of introgression [6, 7].

Characterizing the patterns of introgression can significantly enhance our understanding of speciation genetics. For instance, if natural selection impedes the immigration of an allele with non-synonymous variations, this allele might be linked with “speciation genes,” which could reduce gene flow between populations [8, 9]. Furthermore, selection can impair migration due to the negative effects of the novel genomic background or the genetic load of non-synonymous variations [10, 11]. Despite advances in next-generation genomic sequencing and the availability of comprehensive genetic methodologies and analytical tools for examining introgression on a genomic scale [2, 12, 13], disparities in introgression patterns between synonymous and non-synonymous variations remain largely unexplored.

To explore these patterns more comprehensively, we introduce the relative hybrid index ωH (= Hn/Hs), a novel metric that quantifies the relative proportions of introgressed components in the hybrid genome contributed by the donor parent lineage for non-synonymous (Hn) and synonymous (Hs) variations, respectively. The ωH index offers several advantages: it provides a direct measure of the relative proportion of non-synonymous mutations to synonymous ones, allowing us to infer the selective pressure and evolutionary force shaping introgression patterns [14, 15]. Specifically, a ωH value less than 1.0 suggests stronger purifying selection against non-synonymous mutations introduced via introgression. However, the ωH index also has limitations, primarily its reliance on broad genomic averages, which may obscure selection pressures acting on specific genes or genomic regions. Additionally, it does not account for potential confounding factors such as demographic history or linkage disequilibrium.

In our study, we employ the ωH index to investigate introgression dynamics among three morphologically distinct yet genetically interconnected varieties of Picea likiangensis (vars. rubescens (RUB), likiangensis (LIK), and linzhiensis (LIN); Fig. 1a and Additional file 1: S1) endemic to the Qinghai-Tibetan Plateau (QTP) and adjacent regions [16]. These taxa, characterized by large effective population sizes (> 104) and extensive historical gene flow, exhibit complex hybridization patterns [17–21]. Notably, RUB populations in the Kangding and Ranwu Lake regions (elevations > 3800 m) originated through hybridization during/after recent secondary contacts, following the initial inter-lineage divergence occurred around 1.48 to 5.2 million years ago (mya) [17, 19]. The secondary contacts were triggered by northward/upslope range expansions of LIK and LIN in response to past climatic fluctuations. While previous studies have documented widespread introgression among these taxa [19], the selective forces shaping these patterns remain poorly understood—a critical gap given the fundamental role of natural selection in population divergence and speciation. Our population genomic analyses reveal complex patterns of genetic exchange in the two transition zones, with marked differences in non-synonymous versus synonymous introgression rates. Specifically, we observe significantly higher proportions of introgressed synonymous variants (Hs) compared to non-synonymous variants (Hn) across all sampled populations, consistent with selective removal of deleterious non-synonymous mutations. Furthermore, ωH values are significantly lower in populations adjacent to pure RUB ranges, suggesting stronger purifying selection in core areas of this variety’s distribution. These findings, coupled with significant correlations between recombination rates and introgression patterns, suggest recombination may facilitate the elimination of maladaptive genomic segments during hybridization. This study demonstrates the utility of comparative population genomics for dissecting the complex interplay between selection and gene flow in shaping speciation processes, particularly in recently diverged taxa experiencing secondary contact.

Results

Full-length transcriptome analysis

The SMRT sequencing on the Sequel platform of Pacific Biosciences generated 0.46 million read-sequences from the P. likiangensis tree “C010-2,” totaling 28.32 G bases. The average read length was 61.28 kb, with an N50 value of 121.94 kb. The circular consensus sequences (CCSs) were classified into 368,013 full-length non-chimeric (FLNC) sequences and non-full-length sequences, which were used for isoform clustering. This clustering resulted in 39,882 isoform sequences with an N50 length of 2711 bases. Further refinement and error correction processes yielded a final set of 18,289 sequences, with an N50 length of 2721 bases (Additional file 1: Fig. S1). The longest unigene/isoform extended to a length of 8.5 kb. These sequences were designated as the reference sequence set for subsequent analyses. For functional annotation we used seven databases (Additional file 1: Fig. S2). A total of 18,582 coding regions were predicted using ANGEL v2.4 (www.github.com/PacificBiosciences/ANGEL; Additional file 1: Fig. S3).

Genetic variation and introgression among spruce populations

Our sequencing effort yielded 880 Gb of raw data for the 193 study samples (Additional file 1: Table S1). The average mapping ratio was 74.70% (s.d. = 2.42%; Additional file 1: Table S2), slightly higher than the ratio obtained when using the reference sequence of Norway spruce (71.50%; as described in [19]). Specifically, for the “C010-2” reference, only 76.03% of the short-reads were successfully mapped. In the case of P. breweriana, the mapping ratio was 72.74%. Using our newly generated reference, strict quality control measures resulted in 67,271 and 82,918 SNPs without missing bases in the KANGDING and RANWU sample-sets, respectively.

We confirmed the hybrid origin of the spruce populations within the Kangding region and the Ranwu Lake region through several different analyses. Analyses of MDS and PCA showed that (Fig. 1b and Additional file 1: Figs. S4a, b), spruce trees collected from the Kangding region occupied intermediate positions between LIK and RUB, while those from the Ranwu Lake region exhibited intermediate positions between LIN and RUB. The inclusion of P. purpurea samples didn’t altered the intermediate positions for the trees from the Kangding and Ranwu Lake regions, which fell outside the range delineated by P. purpurea and LIK (Additional file 1: Fig. S4c). This indicated that P. purpurea contributed little to the spruce trees collected from the two genetic transitional zones. Additionally, ADMIXTURE clustering showed that complanata contributed either little or nothing to spruce genomes within the Kangding zone (Additional file 1: Fig. S4d), despite its close phylogenetic relationship with LIK [19, 20, 24]. Thus, we excluded the P. purpurea samples and the complanata samples from subsequent analyses.

The clusters inferred from ADMIXTURE confirmed the mosaic composition of spruce trees in both the Kangding and Ranwu Lake regions (Additional file 1: Figs. S4d–f and S5a, b). The estimated hhs per gene for the populations within both regions were less than 1.0, and the D3 statistics yielded values below 0.0, collectively suggesting a hybrid origin for the spruce trees of the Kangding and Ranwu Lake regions. To quantify the extent of introgression, we used HyDe to estimate the introgression proportion (H; Fig. 1c, d). Within each zone, RUB contributed a larger proportion of genomic material to hybrid genomes, especially in the populations adjacent to pure RUB. In detail, LIK contributed approximately 39.06% of loci within the Kangding zone, while LIN contributed 49.69% within the Ranwu Lake zone (Fig. 1c, d). H values varied across populations, ranging from 0.07 to 0.59 in the Kangding zone, and from 0.42 to 0.68 in the Ranwu Lake zone (Additional file 1: Table S3). These variations indicate the diverse ancestry proportions present within each zone.

For the populations in either zone, we found higher γ (the proportion of components contributed by RUB) in the populations near the pure RUB. Further, in the case of the 11 populations in the Kangding region, a notable negative correlation was observed between γ and the geographical distance to the pure RUB (Pearson’s correlation coefficient r = 0.97, P < 0.001) (Fig. 1e). This correlation was echoed in the Ranwu Lake zone, which displayed a similar negative correlation between γ and the geographical distance to the pure RUB (r = 0.96, P < 0.001; Fig. 1f). Even after employing multi-matrix regression analysis to account for the influence of local climatic conditions, the effect of geographical distance on the ancestry proportions remained significant (P < 0.05). This observation confirmed the role of geographic distance in constraining the extent of introgression from either LIK or LIN to the RUB populations.

Patterns of differential introgression

To investigate genome-wide patterns of differential introgression, we applied the BGC framework. Parental populations were rigorously defined using genetic clustering (Additional file 1: Figs. S4 and S5) and geographical criteria (Additional file 1: Fig. S1). For the Kangding zone, we used 9 pure RUB and 18 pure LIK individuals; for the Ranwu Lake zone, we used 9 pure RUB and 18 pure LIN individuals. To minimize false positives caused by neutral allele frequency differences, we focused on ancestry-informative polymorphisms (AIPs), defined as bi-allelic SNPs with an allele frequency difference ≥ 0.3 between the two parental taxa. This filtering resulted in 8,469 AIPs tested for the Kangding zone and 10,948 AIPs for the Ranwu Lake zone being used as input for the BGC analysis. Our BGC analysis revealed widespread genomic evidence of differential introgression, identifying thousands of outlier SNPs across both study regions. Based on the recombination rates estimated by LDhelmet v1.9 [25], we found a negative correlation between recombination rates and the number of BGC outliers displaying either excess ancestry or restricted introgression in each zone (Fig. 2b, c and e, f). In addition, the observed number of loci with parental excess or restricted introgression in both zones significantly exceeded those anticipated in randomization scenarios (P < 0.05; Additional file 1: Fig. S6), indicating that the introgression from LIK and LIN to RUB might have been impeded by selection. Interestingly, we found that the genes with outliers in the Kangding zone were associated with “reproductive system development,” “developmental process involved in reproduction,” “regulation of seed development,” “germ cell development,” and “pollen germination.” Similarly, the outlier loci in the Ranwu Lake zone were associated with “negative regulation of flower development,” “flavonoid biosynthetic process,” “response to temperature stimulus,” “reproductive shoot system development,” and “pollen development” (Additional file 1: Fig. S7). Furthermore, the genomic distribution of these outlier loci showed that they were scattered across the genome (Additional file 1: Figs. S8 and S9). Despite the influence of random drift in generating introgression outliers [26], our results underscore the inadequacy of random drift alone in explaining the observed correlations and support the role of selection in shaping introgression patterns in two zones.

Spatial distribution estimated ωH

The estimated values of ωH were 0.63 and 0.83 for the populations within the Kangding and the Ranwu Lake zones, respectively (Additional file 1: Table S3). Through bootstrap tests with 100 pseudorandom datasets, it was evident that these ωH values all were significantly below 1.0 (P < 0.05). The range of ωH estimates across the 23 populations situated within the two zones varied from 0.26 to 0.91 (Fig. 3 and Additional file 1: Table S3). The congruent patterns of ωH < 1 across the Kangding and Ranwu Lake regions pointed to the accumulation of numerous non-synonymous mutations from LIN or LIK, which might have faced stronger selection pressure compared to synonymous mutations in both zones. This may rule out the possibility of these patterns occurring by chance. Particularly for the Kangding zone, the estimated Hn for each of the four populations proximal to pure RUB ranged from 0.017 to 0.05. If the selective evolutionary processes filtered introgressed alleles in an untargeted mode and didn’t discriminate the effects of non-synonymous and synonymous mutations in the introgressed fragments, the Hn and Hs would be approximately equal no matter in any population with diverse introgression proportions. However, the ωH were positively correlated with the geographic distance to pure RUB (Fig. 3b, c), indicating that many non-synonymous variations from hybridization were purged more severely in the populations near the pure RUB. In addition, the four populations located near pure RUB in the Kangding region showed notably low ωH, ranging from 0.26 to 0.45 (Additional file 1: Table S3). These observations might indicate that most alleles with non-synonymous mutations sourced from LIK/LIN potentially exerted a detrimental impact on hybrid individuals in two zones and hindered the movement of alleles from LIK/LIN to RUB.

Discussion

The balance between natural selection and gene flow is a critical determinant of the microevolutionary dynamics underlying the origin and maintenance of species [27, 28]. In this study, we leverage the ωH index—a metric quantifying the relative introgression of non-synonymous (Hn) to synonymous (Hs) polymorphisms—to dissect the evolutionary forces shaping hybrid genome composition in Picea likiangensis genetic transition zones. Our analyses reveal complex interplays between selection, recombination, and geography that collectively modulate introgression dynamics, with particular emphasis on the purging of non-synonymous variants in hybrid genomes.

Genomic variation in genetic transition zones

While genomic data confirm the hybrid origin of Kangding and Ranwu Lake populations (Fig. 1a–d and Additional file 1: Fig. S4, S5b), several lines of evidence reject models of single, recent hybridization events [29]. First, the heterogeneous distribution of hybrid indices (Fig. 1) across both genetic transition zones suggests prolonged periods of secondary contact rather than discrete admixture pulses. This interpretation aligns with paleoclimatic records indicating recurrent range expansions/contractions of spruce taxa across the QTP during the Pleistocene [17, 22]. Second, BGC analyses reveal α/β outliers that exceed expectations under neutral introgression, with negative α outliers showing site-specific excesses of LIK/LIN-derived alleles and β outliers indicating signals of restricted introgression (Fig. 3). These positive α outliers instead shows excess of RUB-derived alleles. These patterns indicate that selection may have actively shaped hybrid genome composition during multiple generations of backcrossing.

The functional enrichment of reproduction-related GO terms among BGC outliers (Additional file 1: Fig. S7) provides critical insights into the targets of selection. Genes involved in reproductive isolation—such as those regulating pollen tube growth, germ cell development, flower development and seed development—are expected to experience divergent selection pressures in hybrid zones [30]. Our findings suggest that such reproductive incompatibilities may contribute to the maintenance of genomic divergence despite ongoing gene flow, a phenomenon observed in other plant hybrid zones [31]. Moreover, the estimated RUB ancestry proportions for each population in either zone declines with geographic distance (Fig. 1e, f and Additional file 1: Table S3), additionally highlighting the role of geographic distance in shaping the spatial distribution of introgression from LIK and LIN to RUB.

The early divergence (~ 1.48–5.2 mya) among the three P. likiangensis varieties provides a critical historical context for the observed patterns of selection against introgressed alleles. Our finding that purifying selection is strongest in populations near the core range of the recipient (rubescens) taxon suggests that the genomic divergence accumulated over millions of years manifests as stronger selection against maladaptive alleles in the parental genomic background. This pattern aligns with observations in other long-lived tree genera with deep divergence histories. In European oaks (Quercus spp.), for instance, ancient divergence has not prevented pervasive introgression, yet strong selection maintains species integrity by acting against deleterious introgressed alleles, leading to asymmetric gene flow [32, 33]. Similarly, in poplars (Populus spp.), genomic analyses reveal that introgression is common but selectively filtered, with gene flow being reduced for genomic regions that may disrupt local adaptation [4, 34]. The concordance of our results with these studies underscores that in trees, deep divergence does not preclude hybridization but does establish a landscape where selection can effectively filter introgressed genomes based on their functional impact. This reinforces our conclusion that it is the strength of selection, particularly against non-synonymous mutations in the recipient’s core range, rather than geographic distance alone, that is a primary determinant of introgression patterns.

Natural selection shapes the pattern of introgression

The consistent observation of ωH < 1 across both genetic transition zones (Additional file 1: Table S3) provides robust evidence that natural selection actively purges non-synonymous mutations introduced through introgression. This selective filtering operates through spatially structured mechanisms: near the core of RUB’s range, non-synonymous introgression (Hn) decays more rapidly than synonymous introgression (Hs), yielding markedly lower ωH values (Fig. 3). This pattern aligns with theoretical predictions that hybrid fitness declines toward parental range centers due to the accumulation of Dobzhansky-Muller incompatibilities (DMIs), which are shielded from purging in pure populations but exposed in hybrids [35]. The Kangding zone—proximal to RUB’s range core—exemplifies this dynamic, with uniformly low ωH values (0.26–0.45) reflecting stringent selection against LIK-derived alleles.

Despite bidirectional gene flow between RUB and LIK/LIN [19], ancestry proportions skew toward RUB, suggesting selection disproportionately eliminates non-synonymous alleles from donor lineages (LIK/LIN) to RUB. This asymmetry implies that introgressed variants neutral in parental populations may become deleterious in hybrids—a phenomenon termed cryptic incompatibility [36, 37], where alleles escape purging in parental taxa due to compensatory mutations, only to disrupt hybrid fitness through transgressive epistasis. Notably, ωH < 1 persists even in populations with large effective sizes (Ne > 104), underscoring the strength of selection required to counteract hybrid load—the accumulation of weakly deleterious alleles in admixed genomes [5]. These results demonstrate that spatially varying selection, rather than drift, dominates this process, with selection intensity peaking near RUB’s range center where genomic divergence are maximized [26, 31].

Recombination and selection maintain divergence

The significant correlations between recombination rates and introgression outliers (Fig. 2) reveal how genomic architecture shapes the fate of introgressed alleles. Regions with suppressed recombination exhibit elevated proportions of BGC α outliers, particularly those carrying LIK/LIN-derived alleles. This pattern aligns with the “recombination barrier hypothesis,” where reduced recombination enhances selection against introgressed haplotypes by maintaining linkage between foreign alleles and deleterious mutations [37, 38]. Two complementary mechanisms likely underpin this dynamic. First, in low-recombination regions, loci involved in hybrid incompatibility may form a “selective sieve” that purges introgressed alleles. Second, the skewed ancestry composition of hybrid genomes (predominantly RUB) amplifies frequency-dependent selection against introgressed alleles. As RUB ancestry increases in genetic transition zones near its range core, the likelihood of negative epistatic interactions between introgressed alleles and native variants rises, particularly in low-recombination regions where physical linkage persists. Together, these mechanisms explain how recombination landscapes and ancestry-driven selection interact to filter introgressed haplotypes, reinforcing genomic boundaries despite pervasive gene flow.

This interplay between recombination and selection carries profound evolutionary implications. First, natural selection exhibits a dual role: it acts as a barrier to gene flow by purging maladaptive alleles while potentially facilitating adaptive introgression through the retention of beneficial variants. While our study documents strong purifying selection against non-synonymous mutations, future work should explore whether introgressed alleles confer adaptive advantages in the extreme environments occupied by RUB. Second, selection pressures vary spatially across the hybrid landscape, forming a geographic mosaic where populations near range centers experience stronger purifying selection than those at edges, reflected by the dynamic of ωH. This spatial heterogeneity underscores the need for range-wide genomic surveys to capture divergent selective regimes and their implications for reproductive isolation. Finally, genomic architecture critically shapes speciation dynamics: the concentration of BGC outliers in low-recombination regions suggests these areas act as “speciation hotspots” where hybrid incompatibilities accumulate, while high-recombination regions may serve as conduits for adaptive introgression. Together, these insights enhance our understanding of how selection, geography, and genomic context interact to govern the evolutionary trajectories of hybrid zones.

Limitations of the ωH index and future directions

While the ωH index offers critical insights into selection pressures on coding variants, several methodological limitations warrant consideration, as they highlight avenues for future research. First, its reliance on genomic averaging may obscure locus-specific selection, as ωH compares broad averages rather than individual genes or regulatory regions. Integrating gene expression or chromatin accessibility data could resolve this by pinpointing candidate barrier loci. Second, ωH does not account for detailed demographic history, which can skew introgression patterns independently of selection. Combining demographic modeling with selection scans—for instance, using diffusion approximations—could disentangle these confounding effects.

Third, our study utilized a transcriptome-derived SNP dataset. We acknowledge that this approach inherently biases the sampling toward expressed genes, potentially underrepresenting variation in non-coding regulatory regions or silent genomic areas. While this bias is present, our large sample size and the substantial number of expressed genes captured in this study (18,289 loci) provide a robust genome-wide estimate of selection on coding sequences. The consistent and strong patterns we observe (e.g., ωH < 1) are unlikely to be artifacts of this sampling strategy but rather reflect a biological signal of pervasive purifying selection. Furthermore, our sampling protocol was designed to ensure comparability across populations: young leaves were collected from mature trees across all populations to minimize tissue-specific expression bias. Nonetheless, future studies employing whole-genome sequencing will be essential to capture the full spectrum of genomic variation—including structural and regulatory variants—driving hybrid incompatibilities.

A fourth consideration is our use of a P. tabuliformis reference genome for visualizing genomic distributions. We selected this reference after finding that it provided superior mapping resolution and chromosomal anchoring compared to the available P. abies genome at the time of analysis. However, we recognize that mapping to a phylogenetically distant reference may preferentially retain more evolutionarily conserved genes and regions, potentially influencing the inferred genomic landscape of introgression. Despite this, our primary conclusions regarding differential introgression between synonymous and non-synonymous mutations are drawn from analyses based on our P. likiangensis-specific transcriptome reference, which mitigates this concern for the ωH calculations. The Pinus genome was used solely for visualizing the physical distribution of outliers post-identification. The ongoing development of high-quality Picea genomes will undoubtedly facilitate more precise genomic contextualization in the future.

Finally, ωH focuses solely on coding variation, overlooking the potential roles of regulatory or structural variants in the estabilishment of hybrid incompatibilities. Emerging approaches, like long-read sequencing and epigenomic profiling, have the potential to fill this gap and reveal how non-coding variants contribute to genomic incompatibilities, further refining our understanding of the genomic architecture of hybridization.

Future research should prioritize functional validation of BGC outliers using CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing or viral-induced gene silencing to confirm their roles in the evolution of spruce hybrids. Experimental common garden studies could quantify hybrid fitness across environmental gradients, testing whether selection pressures vary spatially. Comparative genomic analyses of genetic transition zones at different developmental stages (incipient vs. mature) would further clarify how genomic architecture evolves over time. Together, these approaches would provide a more comprehensive understanding of hybrid zone dynamics, bridging the gap between genomic patterns and the evolutionary forces shaping them.

Conclusions

This study reports an evolutionary pattern that genes/alleles with non-synonymous mutations appear lower levels of introgression than those with synonymous mutations in spruce genetic transition zones, facilitating the understanding that the interplay between natural selection, recombination, and geography shapes the spruce genomic architecture in genetic transition zones. By extending the utility of the ωH index and integrating multiple lines of genomic evidence, we provide a nuanced perspective on the evolutionary forces governing introgression in P. likiangensis. Our findings underscore the dynamic nature of hybrid genomes, where spatially varying selection and genomic architecture collectively determine the fate of introgressed alleles. As genomic resources for non-model organisms continue to expand, such integrated approaches will become increasingly critical for understanding the mechanisms underlying speciation and the maintenance of biodiversity.

Methods

Full-length isoform sequencing

We used the SMRT sequencing method based on the zero mode waveguide at the Sequel platform of Pacific Biosciences [39] to produce long reads from a P. likiangensis tree designated as “C010-2.” According to the Pacific Biosciences protocols, which included use of the Clontech SMARTer PCR cDNA Synthesis Kit and the BluePippin Size Selection System protocol, we prepared the Iso-seq library for the “C010-2” specimen. We assessed the quality of this library using multiple methods: the RIN value (7.7), the 25S/18S value (1.83), the A260/280 (2.07), and the A260/230 (2.37). Then we processed these long-read sequences using the SMRTlink v8.0 software from Pacific Biosciences (www.pacb.com/support-/software-downloads) to generate the circular consensus sequences (CCSs). The processing options employed were as follows: –minLength 50, –maxLength 15,000, –minPasses 1. The CCSs were classified into the full-length non-chimeric (FLNC) sequences and non-full-length sequences, achieved by identifying the adapters used in the library preparation as well as the polyA tails. Further sequences refinement was performed using the arrow tool (http://www.github.com/PacificBiosciences-/GenomicConsensus) for sequence polishing. To rectify any error present in these isoform sequences, we employed LoRDEC v0.3, which was based on Illumina RNA-seq data [40]. We used ‘cd-hit-est’ from CD-HIT v4.8.1 package [41] to do the redundancy removal with the following specified options: “-c 0.95 -T 6 -G 0 -aL 0.00 -aS 0.99 -AS 30”.

Functional annotation was performed using diamond v0.8.365 [42], hmmscan within the HMMER v3.1 package [43] (http://www.hmmer.org) and blast v2.7.1 [44] (http://www.blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov). Our annotation process encompassed seven distinct databases including NCBI nucleotide sequences (nt), non-redundant protein sequences (nr), Protein family (Pfam; http://pfam.sanger.ac.uk/), euKaryotic Ortholog Groups (KOG, http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), Swiss-Prot (http://www.ebi.ac.uk/uniprot/), KEGG (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/), and Gene Ontology (GO; http://www.geneontology.org/). For predicting coding regions, ANGEL v2.4 was used with a parameter setting of “min_angel_ aa_length 50” (http://www.github.com/PacificBiosciences/ANGEL). Subsequent to the coding region prediction, polymorphisms within these regions were divided into synonymous and nonsynonymous variants.

Sampling, sequencing, and single nucleotide polymorphism calling

Young leaves were sampled from mature, healthy trees during the early-growing season (May–June) across two genetic transition zones: 89 trees from 11 localities in the Kangding zone and 104 trees from 12 localities in the Ranwu Lake zone (Additional file 1: Table S1). To represent the genetic diversity across these zones comprehensively, we organized our samples into two sample-sets: the “KANGDING” sample-set, consisting of 89 trees exclusively from the Kangding zone, complemented by an additional 57 pure samples originating from regions outside of either zone. Similarly, the “RANWU” sample-set comprised 104 trees exclusively from the Ranwu Lake zone along with the same 57 pure samples from areas outside of the two zones (Additional file 1: Table S1). Among the 57 pure samples in each sample-set, 46 represented pure samples of LIK, RUB, or LIN, while an additional 11 trees belonged to P. brachytyla var. complanata (complanata). Moreover, for our phylogenetic analysis, we included a single tree of P. breweriana as an outgroup. Notably, in some subsequent analyses, we expanded our dataset by including 40 trees of P. purpurea [18] to examine its potential genetic contributions to the genomes of spruce trees originating from the Kangding zone.

Populations “A36,” “A37,” and “A38” within the Kangding zone are situated on the same mountain but at varying altitudes. Similarly, populations “C009” and “C010” within the Ranwu Lake zone share the same mountain but differ in altitudes. Additionally, populations “C013” and “C014” within the Ranwu Lake zone also belong to the same mountain but exhibit distinct altitudes. Transcriptomic sequencing of the 193 samples from two transition zones was performed as previously described [19, 24] using the Illumina Genome Analyzer (HiSeq 2500). To ensure data quality, the generated raw reads underwent quality control using the FASTX-toolkit (http://hannonlab.cshl.edu/fastx_toolkit/). The quality control procedure included the following steps: (1) bases with a low quality (Phred quality score ≤ 20) were masked and trimmed if located at the read’s end, and (2) reads with low-quality bases > 95% of read length or with a length < 30 bases were discarded. Additionally, these reads were trimmed to remove any adaptor and repetitive telomere sequences.

The filtered reads were mapped to the “C010-2” reference sequence using BWA-MEM v0.7.16a-r1181 with default settings [45]. Then the Picard-tools v1.92 (https://picard.sourceforge.net/) were used for multiple tasks: (1) assignment of read group information, including library, lane, and sample identity; (2) sorting the SAM-format files; (3) removal of marked duplicate reads; and (4) generation of BAM-format files. For SNP and genotype calling, two approaches were employed. Firstly, ANGSD v0.928 [46] was employed, which accounts for genotype uncertainty and directly estimates the site-frequency-spectrum (SFS) for all samples without the necessity to call genotypes; Secondly, genotypes and single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were performed using READS2SNPS v2.0 [47, 48], followed by a Bayesian test to consider potential false positive SNPs.

We used the ANGSD v0.928 software [46] to generate beagle-format files from the filtered reads of all spruce samples. The criteria for this procedure included a minimal mapping quality (MQ) score of 30 and filtered bases with a minimal quality score of 20 in BAM-format sequence data files. In this context, the data of P. breweriana served as an outgroup. We used the “-dosaf” option to calculate the site allele frequency (SAF) likelihood, based on the genotype likelihood model of SAMtools v0.1.19 [49] at all sites. We then implemented the realSFS method, employing the expectation maximization algorithm [50], to perform a maximum likelihood estimation of the unfolded SFS.

We used READS2SNPS v2.0 [47, 48] for genotype and variant calling, configuring a series of parameters, which included setting the minimum read depth at 10, the minimum mapping quality at 20, and the minimum base quality at 20. To expedite this analysis, we harnessed thirty parallel threads. If the posterior probability of a homozygote or heterozygote exceeded 0.95, the resulting genotype call was duly recorded in the variant calling file (VCF). Additionally, it’s worth noting that SNPs failing to pass the filter of false positive test were identified and marked. These marked false positive SNPs, characterized by their higher heterozygosity and rejection of the HWE test conducted using VCFtools v0.1.12b [51], were a consistent feature. Thus, we mostly used the SNPs identified by READS2SNPS v2.0 to perform the population genomic analyses.

The spruce tree labeled as “MSZ-42–01,” which was used in the previous study [19], has been subjected to two separated sampling instances. In this regard, we use the sequences obtained from these instances to assess the quality of the SNPs called by the READS2SNPS v2.0 method, as described earlier, leveraging the duplicated sequencing data. Both sets of Illumina reads have undergone the same data processing pipeline. After the exclusion of sites with missing bases, the two duplicated sequencing runs produce sequences that exhibit no nucleotide differences.

Genetic clustering

Based on the SNPs identified by READS2SNPS, PLINK v1.07 [52] was used to reduce linkage disequilibrium among SNPs using the parameters ‘–indep-pairwise 50 5 0.2’. Clustering analyses were conducted using ADMIXTURE v1.23 [53]. ADMIXTURE v1.23 was executed with varying numbers of genetic clusters (K) from 1 to 20. The optimum number of clusters for the KANGDING and RANWU sample-sets was determined through the cross-validation.

MDS and PCA utilized genotype likelihoods and matrices computed by ANGSD (-doCov 1 -minMapQ 30 -minQ 20 -GL 2 -doMajorMinor 1 -doMaf 1 -doIBS 1 -doCounts 1 -makeMatrix 1 -minMaf 0.05 -SNP_pval 2e-6). Results were processed and visualized using R v4.1.3. These analyses were performed for the trees collected from both zones and the pure samples. In addition, we conducted a PCA on an extended sample-set (including 57 pure samples of LIK, RUB, LIN, and complanata, 193 trees from the two zones, and 40 P. purpurea samples), to confirm that P. purpurea contributed few or no genetic components to the hybrid spruce tree genomes in the Kangding and Ranwu Lake zones.

Detecting signals of introgression

To effectively detect introgression signals while accounting for the potential impacts of incomplete lineage sorting (ILS) arising from ancestral polymorphisms, we used the HyDe method [54, 55] to estimate the introgression proportion denoted as H. We also manually calculated the proportion of genetic components from RUB (denoted as γ = 1.0-H). Particularly, we extracted the non-synonymous and synonymous polymorphisms based on the reference sequence and SNPs (identified by READS2SNPS), and then computed Hn and Hs respectively. This approach allowed us to compute the value of H (Hn and Hs) for each population within the Kangding and Ranwu Lake zones, as well as for each respective zone. We computed ωH for each population from either zone, determining significance levels through bootstrap sampling.

Unlike an ILS-only model, introgression can generate distinct patterns of pairwise coalescence times, which can be approximated using simple metrics of pairwise sequence divergence. Based on this rationale, we detected introgression signals using the hhs and the D3 statistic, both grounded in the concept of genetic distance (dxy) across three samples/populations [56, 57]. The D3 statistic was computed based on dxy values determined by VCFtools [51]. Utilizing Python3 scripts (https://github.com/yongshuai-sun/hhs-omei), we computed the hhs statistic, which relies on ancestry-informative polymorphisms (AIPs), defined as sites with an allele frequency difference ≥ 0.3 between parental taxa. Loci lacking positive FST values were excluded [29]. As a result, when hhs < 1.0 and D3 ≠ 0.0, there is a signature of introgression.

Computing recombination rates

We used LDhelmet v1.9 [25] to estimate population-scaled recombination rates, based on the sequences of 18 LIN trees. The population-scaled mutation rate (0.0024) was computed using observed heterozygosity data for LIN trees [38]. Phased haplotypes for variants at each locus were generated using beagle v5 [58, 59], stipulating that SNPs with no more than 20% missing bases were retained. In our LDhelmet analysis, we applied a block penalty of 5, following recommendations from recent studies [37, 60]. To enhance recombination estimation, we inferred the ancestral allele distribution at each site in LIN through a parsimony-based method, comparing it with the P. breweriana sequence [25]. When confronted with a dimorphic site in the sequence data matrix, if the allele of the P. breweriana sequence was non-missing and unequivocally represented one of the four possible nucleotides, aligning with one of the two alleles observed in LIN, then it was designated as the ancestral allelic state. Otherwise, it was treated as an unknown state, and a uniform distribution was set as a prior over the ancestral allele. To account for the inherent uncertainty in ancestral allele reconstruction, we set the prior for the putative ancestral allele at 0.91, while assigning 0.03 as priors for the other three bases. For the estimation of the mutation matrix in LIN, we exclusively considered biallelic SNPs, following the methodology of Chan et al. [25].

To reduce computational consumption, LDpop [61] was used to generate a lookup-table for the transcript sequences of LIN. We computed a likelihood lookup table for a grid of ρ values (-rh 0.0,0.01,1.0,1.0,100.0) for each set of haplotypes (-n 36). The “-th” parameters were set to 0.0024, and we opted for the “—approx” option. Then we converted the lookup-table format using the “convert_table” tool within the LDhelmet package. We used the “rjMCMC” procedure with a burn-in of 100,000 and ran the Markov chain for 1 million iterations. SNPs with estimated recombination rates higher than 20 × θ [37] were excluded. Because recombination rates between SNPs located in different exons would introduce bias, we removed SNP-pairs located in different exons and their associated rates of recombination, according to mapping between the transcriptome of “C010-2” and the reference genome of P. abies [62]. Finally, we summarized recombination rates per locus.

Analysis of Bayesian genomic clines

We used Bayesian genomic clines (BGC) implemented in bgc v1.03 to identify outliers along the genome with differential introgression patterns, based on deviations between genome-wide average introgression and locus-specific introgression [63, 64]. Two cline parameters, α and β, were used to indicate the excess of parental genetic composition and restricted introgression, respectively [63]. We fitted the bgc genomic cline model for each of the KANGDING and RANWU sample-sets, using the MCMC algorithm with two chains (each with 80,000 iterations, a 50,000-iteration burn-in and saving sample every 5 iterations), after a preliminary analysis with 50,000 iterations and a 25,000-iteration burn-in. Parental populations were defined based on the ADMIXTURE results and geographic isolation. Default settings were used for other options. A R function “get_bgc_outliers.R” was applied to detect the outliers of α and β if the 99.9999% confidence interval did not overlap zero for each SNP loci by setting the “qn” parameter [65]. We used the “estpost” function in bgc v1.03 to examine the mixing and convergence of the calculation. Outliers with α > 0 indicated an excess of parental contribution from RUB to the hybrid population, while outliers with α < 0 indicated an excess of parental contribution from LIK/LIN to the hybrid population in the Kangding/Ranwu Lake zones. And outliers with β > 0 indicated restricted introgression into the hybrid population. For the Kangding (RUB × LIK) and Ranwu Lake (RUB × LIN) zones, AIPs were used to run the bgc analysis.

To examine the relationship between differential introgression and recombination rates, we aggregated the number of α outliers (both positive and negative) and positive β outliers. Subsequently, we calculated the Pearson’s correlation (r) between the number of outliers and the estimated recombination rates (ρ) for each genetic transitional zone. All ρ values were classified into ten bins (587 loci per bin). The outlier counts were then determined for both α and β in each bin, and these counts were used to calculate correlations in R v4.1.3. In addition, we examined the presence of shared outliers with positive α (or positive β) in the two genetic transitional zones. We used randomization tests to examine whether the shared loci were over-represented in the two zones. Furthermore, we performed gene ontology (GO) analysis using TBtools-II v2.200 [66] on the outlier loci from the Kangding and Ranwu Lake zones, respectively. To learn about the genomic distribution of these outlier loci, we aligned these genes with outliers on the genome reference of Pinus tabuliformis [67], assuming synteny between the genomes of Picea and Pinus.

Relationships between geographic distances and either γ or ωH

To examine the hybrid index cline, we used a multiple-matrix regression approach to quantify how geographical distances influenced the extent of parental contribution from pure RUB to populations within genetic transitional zones. First, we calculated the proportion of ancestry represented by RUB (γ = 1.0-H) using HyDe v1.0.2 [54]. Second, we computed the geographic distance between each population within the two genetic transitional zones and the pure RUB populations, using the Python3 package “geopy.distance.” Finally, we computed the correlation between ωH and the geographical distance for each population within the two genetic transitional zones.
