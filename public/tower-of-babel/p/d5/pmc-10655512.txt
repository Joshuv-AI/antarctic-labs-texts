Prenatal whole‐exome sequencing in fetuses with increased nuchal translucency
Authors: Chunge Cao, Fang Liu, Yan Yang, Qing Zhang, Junfang Huang, Xinhong Liu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIncreased nuchal translucency (NT) is associated with an increased risk for genetic disorders. The aim of this study was to investigate the value of whole‐exome sequencing (WES) in detecting genetic abnormalities for fetuses with isolated first‐trimester increased NT.MethodsAfter the exclusion of aneuploidies and pathogenic copy number variants (CNVs) by quantitative fluorescent polymerase chain reaction (QF‐PCR) and chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA), WES was performed on 63 fetuses with isolated first‐trimester increased NT (≥3.5 mm).ResultsOverall, WES yielded a 4.8% (3/63) diagnostic rate for fetuses with isolated increased NT. Pathogenic variants were identified in 37.5% (3/8) fetuses that developed additional structural anomalies later in gestation, and no pathogenic variants were detected in increased NT that resolved or remained isolated throughout the pregnancy.ConclusionThis study provides powerful evidence to offer prenatal WES for increased NT only when additional abnormalities are present. Early detailed ultrasound to detect emerging anomalies can help physicians offer prenatal WES to fetuses with a greater likelihood of diagnosis.Keywords: chromosomal microarray analysis, genetic counseling, increased nuchal translucency, prenatal diagnosis, whole‐exome sequencing

INTRODUCTION

Nuchal translucency (NT) is an ultrasound measurement of the maximal thickness of the subcutaneous translucency between the skin and subcutaneous soft tissue overlying the cervical spine in a mid‐sagittal view of the face of a fetus at 11–14 weeks' gestation (Nicolaides et al., 1994). Increased NT is defined as a measurement ≥95th percentile by crown‐rump length‐adjusted percentiles (crown‐rump length range 45–84 mm) in accordance with the standards of the Fetal Medicine Foundation (Wright et al., 2008). Increased NT (≥3.5 mm) is associated with genetic disorders, which can result in adverse pregnancy outcomes, such as miscarriage, intrauterine fetal death, congenital heart disease, extensive skeletal dysplasia, and intellectual disability (Bilardo et al., 2007; Grande et al., 2015; Hellmuth et al., 2017; Nicolaides et al., 1994). Identification of the etiology of increased NT is essential to assist in genetic counseling, inform prognosis, establish recurrence risk, and direct clinical care. Chromosomal abnormalities can be identified in approximately 30% of increased NT by combining karyotype and CMA (Bardi et al., 2020; Bilardo et al., 2007; Stuurman et al., 2021). However, fetuses with negative results on karyotype analysis and CMA are still at high risk for adverse pregnancy outcomes, which indicates some as‐yet‐undiscovered underlying causes (Bilardo et al., 2007; Ghi et al., 2001).

In clinical practice, when karyotype and CMA are normal, couples are routinely recommended to await the fetal anatomy survey and echocardiogram to rule out structural abnormalities in the second trimester. In occasional cases with an increased NT and normal CMA, there is still a risk for genetic conditions even though no other abnormalities are detected in pregnancy (Yang et al., 2020). In consideration of the increased parental anxiety during the long wait before the second‐trimester scan, and the limitations of prenatal ultrasound for diagnosing childhood‐onset genetic diseases, a more efficient diagnostic assay is warranted to detect underlying disease‐causing variants in fetuses with isolated increased NT and negative results on karyotype analysis and CMA.

With decreases in costs and increases in the speed of bioinformatics analytical pipelines, exome sequencing has been increasingly used in the prenatal diagnosis of Mendelian diseases. Many studies on prenatal exome sequencing have successfully identified genetic diagnoses in fetuses with structural abnormalities, but diagnostic rates differed greatly in different phenotypic subgroups (Chen et al., 2020; Drury et al., 2015; Kucińska‐Chahwan et al., 2022; Lord et al., 2019; Petrovski et al., 2019; Sparks et al., 2020; Vora et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2021). Exome sequencing studies focused on isolated increased NT obtained diagnostic rates ranging from 0% to 21% (Chen et al., 2020; Daum et al., 2019; Drury et al., 2015; Lord et al., 2019; Mellis et al., 2022; Petrovski et al., 2019; Sparks et al., 2020; Xue et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2020). The substantially different diagnostic rates challenge the clinical application and cost‐effectiveness of WES. In this study, we determined the diagnostic yield of WES and explored factors that influence diagnostic rates in isolated first‐trimester increased NT.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethical compliance

This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region Maternal and Child Healthcare Hospital.

Study design and participants

Between June 2018 and December 2020, 169 singleton pregnancies with increased NT (≥3.5 mm) at 11–14 weeks' gestation were screened in Urumqi Maternal and Child Health Hospital. Among these fetuses, 23 (13.6%) presenting additional ultrasound abnormalities, including cystic hygroma, were excluded from this study. All couples who agreed to participate in the study provided written informed consent. At the time of enrollment, we carefully reviewed family and past obstetrics histories and conducted expert examinations of each couple. Fetal samples were collected by chorionic villus sampling (chorionic villi) or amniocentesis (amniotic fluid) according to the gestational age at the time of the invasive prenatal diagnosis. Parental blood samples were routinely collected at the time of the prenatal diagnostic procedure. For genetic testing, samples first underwent rapid aneuploidy testing using QF‐PCR. When QF‐PCR detected aneuploidy, no further testing was done. Samples with normal QF‐PCR results were arranged for CMA. DNA of cases with normal CMA was sent for trio WES with parental samples. The WES results and a formal report were provided directly to couples at the time of diagnosis with a turnaround time of 4–8 weeks. All pregnancy outcomes were followed up twice, at 6 and 18 months, respectively, after the expected date of birth.

Ultrasound examination

Ultrasound examination was performed by physicians qualified for prenatal ultrasound diagnosis. An E8 Voluson (GE) ultrasound system with a 3.5–5 MHz ultrasound probe was used. The procedure of prenatal ultrasound diagnosis was conducted according to the International Society of Ultrasound in Obstetrics and Gynecology practice guidelines (Salomon et al., 2011; Salomon et al., 2013).

Chromosomal microarray analysis

DNA was extracted from both fetal and parental samples with a DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Catalog No. 69504, Qiagen) following the manufacturer's protocol. DNA concentrations and quality were determined with a NanoDropOne spectrophotometer (Life Technologies, Thermo Fisher Scientific). DNA integrity was assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis.

CMA was performed on a CytoScan 750K platform (Applied Biosystems, Affymetrix). Briefly, 250 ng DNA was required, and the experiment was conducted following the manufacturer's protocol. Aberration analysis was performed with ChAS (Affymetrix). CNVs were categorized according to the joint consensus recommendation of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics and the Clinical Genome Resource (Riggs et al., 2021). Parents of fetuses with pathogenic or likely pathogenic copy number variants (CNVs) subsequently underwent CMA to determine whether the CNVs were de novo or inherited.

Whole‐exome sequencing

An Agilent SureSelect Human ALL Exon V6 Kit was used for whole‐exome DNA library construction. A HiSeq 2500 sequencer (Illumina) with 100 bp paired‐end sequencing mode was used to sequence the genomic DNA of the family. Raw image files were processed with CASAVA v1.82 for base calling and generation of raw data. Sequencing reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg19/GRCh37) with the Burrows–Wheeler Aligner tool, and PCR duplicates were removed with Picard v1.57 (http://picard.sourceforge.net/). The third‐party software GATK (https://software.broadinstitute.org/gatk/) was used for variant calling. Variant annotation and interpretation were conducted by ANNOVAR (Wang et al., 2010). Variants analysis targets coding exons and splicing regions for all putative disease‐causing genes curated from Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM) database, including candidate genes. Genes associated with RASopathies, inborn errors of metabolism, skeletal dysplasia, neurodevelopment disorders, generalized lymphatic dysplasia, cardiomyopathies, mitochondrial mutations, etc., were carefully analyzed. The clinical significance of variants was classified according to the guidelines of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics and the Association for Molecular Pathology (Richards et al., 2015). Pathogenic variants, likely pathogenic variants, and novel variants of uncertain clinical significance were shortlisted as candidate variants. Any variants considered disease‐causing were verified by Sanger sequencing. Family members, including proband, if available, were also examined by Sanger sequencing to test the origin of the variants. WES data were re‐analyzed based on prenatal and/or postnatal phenotypic changes, and linked to the latest ClinVar, ClinGen, OMIM, and Human Gene Mutation Database.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were done with SPSS (version 22.0). The two‐tailed Fisher's exact test was used to compare rates of diagnostic genetic variants between sub‐groups, and p < 0.05 was considered as significant difference.

Among the 23 cases with additional ultrasound abnormalities at the NT scan, 7 opted for termination of pregnancy (TOP) without further testing, 6 with aneuploidies opted for TOP, 1 developed intrauterine death, 9 born alive and follow‐up results were normal. Among the 44 fetuses that declined WES or with poor fetal DNA quality, 3 developed structural abnormalities later in gestation and opted for TOP, 1 with pulmonary sequestration continued pregnancy and underwent surgery after birth, 40 born alive and follow‐up results were normal. DISCUSSION

The aim of this study was to gain more insight into the diagnostic use of prenatal WES in fetuses with isolated increased NT and normal QF‐PCR and CMA and explore factors that influence diagnostic rates.

Studies using QF–PCR as a first‐line test showed that about 30.2%–51.3% of fetuses with NT >3.5 mm had common aneuploidies (Pan et al., 2016; Sinajon et al., 2020). This study yielded a diagnostic rate of 21.2% (31/146). The relatively low diagnostic yield in this study may partially be attributed to the exclusion of cases with additional ultrasound abnormalities in the first trimester. The low cost, rapid turnaround time, ability to detect significant abnormalities, and the high incidence of aneuploidies make QF‐PCR an ideal initial test prior to additional testing (Chitty et al., 2006; Langlois et al., 2011).

Practice guidelines have recommended CMA as first‐tier test when a fetal anomaly has been detected by ultrasound or other fetal imaging and is rapidly becoming the preferred diagnostic test for fetuses with structural malformations (American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, Society for Maternal–Fetal Medicine, 2016; Committee on Genetics, Society for Maternal–Fetal Medicine, 2016). Most recently, Mastromoro et al. (2023) performed a systematic review of the literature and meta‐analysis, to calculate diagnostic yields of genetic testing in fetuses with increased NT and cystic hygroma. They established that the incremental CMA detection rate was 4.1% for isolated increased NT (≥3.5 mm). The percentage of pathogenic findings in this study was 5.5%, which is, therefore, in line with most other studies (Mastromoro et al., 2023).

In this series of 63 pregnancies with isolated first‐trimester increased NT (≥3.5 mm), WES identified pathogenic variants in three cases, yielding an incremental diagnostic rate of 4.8%. This discrepancy in diagnostic rate between this and previous studies may be attributable to different cohort sizes, cohort ascertainment, cut‐off for increased NT, and criteria for variants interpretation (Mastromoro et al., 2023). In certain studies, such as one from Petrovski et al. (2019), did not distinguish increased NT with or without structural abnormalities, leading to a high diagnostic rate in those with structural abnormalities. Some studies, such as one from Daum et al. (2019), used 3.0 mm rather than 3.5 mm as the cut‐off for increased NT, leading to a relatively higher likelihood of diagnosis in the larger increased NT group. Among studies focused on isolated increased NT in the first trimester and used the 99th percentile (≥3.5 mm) as the cut‐off for increased NT, Yang et al. obtained a 5.5% (4/73) increase in diagnostic rate over CMA using medical exome sequencing (Yang et al., 2020); Mellis et al. obtained a diagnostic rate of 9.0% (14/155) using prenatal WES (Mellis et al., 2022). The recent systematic review and meta‐analysis by Mastromoro et al. established an overall 2.44% diagnostic yield for exome sequencing in fetuses with isolated increased NT (≥3.5 mm) (Mastromoro et al., 2023). The great discrepancy in diagnostic rate questions the application of WES in fetuses with isolated increased NT.

We observed a high diagnostic rate (37.5%) for increased NT with additional abnormalities later in gestation, which was consistent with Mellis et al.'s analysis (37.5% vs. 32.4%, Fisher's exact two‐tailed test; p = 1.00) (Mellis et al., 2022). We did not obtain any (likely) pathogenic variants for cases with isolated increased NT that resolved or remained isolated later in gestation, whereas Mellis et al. obtained a diagnostic rate of 1.8% (2/111). Note that one of the findings was chromosome 15 uniparental disomy, which was presumed to have been detected on prenatal SNP‐array analysis. Therefore, in Mellis et al.'s analysis, the diagnostic rate of WES may be as low as 0.9% (1/111). These low diagnostic rates for isolated increased NT without additional abnormalities are consistent with an existing body of evidence indicating that, after exclusion of chromosomal abnormalities, if a detailed ultrasound examination in the second trimester demonstrates resolution of the increased NT and absence of any major abnormalities, then the prognosis is likely to be good and the chances of delivering a baby with no major abnormalities are more than 95% (Bilardo et al., 2007; Bilardo et al., 2010; Souka et al., 2005).

In this study, among the eight fetuses with additional abnormalities later in gestation, only three were detected with diagnostic variants, which indicates that there are still underlying etiology undiscovered by prenatal WES. Sequential testing that CMA followed by WES is unable to detect certain types of variations, such as noncoding single nucleotide variants, small CNVs that affect single or few exons, and balanced structural rearrangements. Choy et al. evaluated the feasibility of applying genome sequencing (>30‐fold) in prenatal diagnosis for 50 fetuses with increased NT, and yielded a twofold increase in diagnostic yield (32.0%, 16/50); moreover, they identified two cryptic insertions and two inversions; follow‐up study further demonstrated the potential pathogenicity of an apparently balanced insertion that disrupted gene BMPR2 at the insertion site (Choy et al., 2019). Whole‐genome sequencing is a holistic approach for comprehensive detection of the disease‐causing genetic variants with a low input‐DNA requirement (≈100 ng) and a rapid turnaround time (18 ± 6 days) compared with that (31 ± 8 days) of the CMA plus WES sequential strategy, and it is proposed to be beneficial in prenatal diagnosis (Saunders et al., 2012; Talkowski et al., 2012; Willig et al., 2015; Zhou et al., 2021). However, it is still not revealing all the causative variants and relevant clinical studies in prenatal diagnosis are limited. Compared with WES, whole‐genome sequencing would yield a larger number of VUS, which prevents the value of the clinical application.

Increased NT was the most common phenotypic expression in fetuses with RASopathies (Stuurman et al., 2019), and was associated with a 10% risk of Noonan syndrome, a disorder belonging to the RASopathies or RAS/MAPK syndromes (Ali et al., 2017). Prenatal exome sequencing studies have implicated many genes of the Ras‐MAPK pathway in increased NT (Chen et al., 2020; Drury et al., 2015; Fu et al., 2018; Lord et al., 2019; Mellis et al., 2022; Petrovski et al., 2019; Sparks et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2020). In this study, all three genes (PTPN11, RIT1, and SHOC2) with diagnostic variants are those of the Ras‐MAPK pathway, which confirms that increased NT is associated with a high risk for RASopathies (Sinajon et al., 2020). Given that WES currently remains relatively costly and time‐consuming, and there is inevitably VUS and secondary findings which complicate data interpreting and results report, the RASopathy gene panel testing could be considered as an alternative detection method. Literature showed that, for cases with NT ≥3.5 mm and associated anomalies, the frequency of RASopathies, 7.69%, appears to be higher than in apparently isolated cases, being only 1.44% (Mastromoro et al., 2023). It was recommended that, after a normal CMA, RASopathy prenatal testing should be offered when any prenatal ultrasound finding suggestive of lymphatic dysplasia (cystic hygroma, increased NT or nuchal fold, hydrops, effusions, other lymphatic anomalies) or suggestive congenital heart disease (like valvular dysplasia and/or hypertrophic cardiomyopathy) is found alone or in association, except for isolated increased NT below 6 mm or isolated increased nuchal fold (Scott et al., 2021). However, since the RASopathy gene panel does not cover all genes associated with increased NT, there is a risk of missed diagnosis. Patients should be completely informed of advantages and limitations of each testing strategy before testing is ordered, and adequate genetic counseling should be provided in clinical practice.

WES can significantly improve the yields of diagnostic variants, it also increases the chance of detecting VUS, secondary findings, and incidental findings (Westerfield et al., 2014). The limitations of fetal phenotyping especially complicate the interpretation of VUS for their pathogenicity in the prenatal setting. It is of great significance to expand the knowledge base on fetal phenotyping–genotyping relationships and establish a public database to document this growing knowledge to assist in accurate data interpretation and genetic counseling in future clinical practice. Secondary findings (SFs) are genetic variants, unrelated to the primary presentation and actively sought in genes that are part of a defined list. The SFs list includes a total of 73 genes on the SF v3.0 version and is updated on an annual basis by the ACMG Secondary Findings Maintenance Working Group (Miller et al., 2021). Incidental findings are pathogenic and likely pathogenic variants identified in genes unrelated to the test indication that are not part of the ACMG SFs gene list. ACMG has suggested that, in the prenatal setting, laboratories should consider reporting VUS in genes that fit the fetal phenotype, especially in autosomal recessive conditions if the VUS is found in trans with a pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant; if the patient consents to SFs analysis, variants identified in the ACMG SFs genes should be reported; highly penetrant incidental findings, but known to cause moderate to severe childhood‐onset disorders, are recommended to be reported; SFs and incidental findings should be discussed with the patient as part of the pre‐test counseling and informed consent process with an opt‐out option of the reporting of these variants (Monaghan et al., 2020).

The development of next‐generation sequencing technology expands the content of sequencing tests, including multigene panels, exome sequencing, and genome sequencing, leading to ever larger amounts of variants to be identified on patient samples. As evidence on variants evolves, previous classifications may later require modification. For example, the discovery of new relationships between diseases and genetic variants may make the reinterpretation of variants to be necessary, the availability of variant frequency data among large populations has led many uncertain significance variants to be reclassified as benign, and testing additional family members may result in the reclassification of variants. Interpretation of prenatal WES results is especially complicated as the characteristic phenotypes of many disorders may not be present at this stage, which highlights the importance of periodical reanalysis of sequencing data based on phenotypic changes, as well as the changes of variant knowledge. It is recommended that laboratories suggest periodic inquiry by health care providers to determine whether knowledge of any variants of uncertain significance, including variants reported as likely pathogenic, has changed (Richards et al., 2015). The health care provider, clinical laboratory, patient, and family may share the responsibility of re‐contact. These expectations should be explicitly delineated as part of the informed consent process before the sample is obtained. Regarding the responsibility of re‐contact and the informed consent process, see the ACMG policy statement on patient re‐contact after revision of genomic test results (David et al., 2019).

WES can greatly increase the diagnostic yield in fetuses with structural abnormalities and those initially presenting with non‐isolated increased NT (Chen et al., 2020; Drury et al., 2015; Emms et al., 2022; Lord et al., 2019; Petrovski et al., 2019; Sparks et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2020). Thus, in this study, we excluded initially non‐isolated cases, and only focused on those initially isolated, which could greatly reduce the detection rate in this study. One limitation of this study is the limited sample size. Future prospective studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to draw useful conclusions regarding the value of WES in fetuses with isolated increased NT.

In conclusion, the present data support the application of WES for increased NT only when additional structural abnormalities are present. Early detailed ultrasound to detect emerging anomalies can help physicians offer prenatal WES to fetuses with a greater likelihood of diagnosis.
