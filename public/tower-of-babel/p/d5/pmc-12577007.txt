Clinical characteristics and risk factors for pathological fractures in children with Staphylococcus aureus osteoarticular infections: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Yingtie Cui, Yunzhen Zhang, Linfeng Wang, Feng Wang, Yong Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo identify the clinical characteristics of and risk factors for pathological fractures secondary to osteoarticular infections (OAI) in children.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of 159 children with acute OAI treated at our institution between July 2012 and June 2024. Patients were divided into a pathological fracture group (n = 24) and a no-fracture group (n = 135). Data analyzed included age, sex, time to admission, Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU) admission and length of stay, non-orthopedic admission, disseminated infection, delayed surgery, precipitating factors, initial symptoms, pre-hospital peak temperature, inflammatory markers, extent of infection, affected bone diameter, bacteremia, pathogen (MSSA or MRSA) and susceptibility, antibiotic use, number of infected sites, surgical method, number of pre-fracture and total surgeries, postoperative fever duration, recurrent fever, length of stay, and number of hospitalizations.ResultsThe median time to pathological fracture was 55 days, which was significantly positively correlated with age (r = 0.719, P < 0.001). Children aged ≤ 49.5 months constituted the largest proportion of the fracture group (70.8%), followed by children aged 7–15 years (29.2%). The femur was the most commonly affected bone (29.2%), followed by the fibula (25.0%) and tibia (20.8%). Univariate analysis revealed that the pathological fracture group had a higher proportion of children aged ≤ 49.5 months, higher rates of bacteremia, disseminated infection, and PICU admission, more frequent surgical delays, a greater number of pre-fracture and total surgical procedures, more hospitalizations, longer postoperative fever duration, a larger extent of infection, and a smaller affected bone diameter (all P < 0.05). C-reactive protein (CRP) and procalcitonin (PCT) levels were also significantly elevated in the fracture group (P < 0.05). Conversely, no significant differences were found in sex, age as a continuous variable, PICU stay duration, non-orthopedic admission, precipitating factors, pre-hospital symptom duration, initial symptoms, pre-hospital peak temperature, concurrent septic arthritis, number of osteomyelitis sites (≥ 2 or ≥ 3), number of total infected sites (≥ 3), surgical method, recurrent fever, white blood cell (WBC) count, neutrophil percentage (NE%), neutrophil count (NE), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), or the time for these markers to normalize. Antimicrobial susceptibility and usage patterns were also similar between groups (all P > 0.05). Binary logistic regression analysis identified disseminated infection (OR 22.6), age ≤ 49.5 months (OR 13.8), elevated PCT, a larger extent of infection, and a smaller affected bone diameter as independent risk factors for pathological fracture (all P < 0.05).ConclusionYounger age is a critical determinant for earlier and more rapid development of pathological fractures in pediatric OAI. Age ≤ 49.5 months, disseminated infection, elevated PCT, a larger extent of infection, and a smaller diameter of the affected bone are independent predictors of this severe complication. Prophylactic immobilization with a cast or brace should be strongly considered for patients with these risk factors to prevent fracture and subsequent displacement.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13052-025-02138-w.

Introduction

Acute osteoarticular infections (OAI), encompassing acute hematogenous osteomyelitis (AHO) and septic arthritis (SA), are among the most common severe bacterial infections in children. The incidence in developed countries is approximately 80 per 100,000 children annually [1], with a substantially higher rate of up to 200 per 100,000 in developing nations [2]. Staphylococcus aureus is the predominant pathogen, accounting for 70–90% of pediatric OAI cases [3, 4]. Within this species, methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) poses a significant global health challenge [5], and the formation of MRSA biofilms in osteomyelitis presents additional therapeutic hurdles [6].

While most children recover with appropriate treatment, a subset develops chronic complications, including pathological fractures, growth arrest, limb length discrepancy, avascular necrosis, joint stiffness, chronic dislocation, and chronic osteomyelitis, with reported rates ranging from 5.5% to 9% [7, 8]. Pathological fracture, a particularly debilitating sequela, occurs in approximately 5% of children with S. aureus OAI [9]. Previous research has identified prolonged fever and delayed source control as risk factors for long-term orthopedic complications [8], underscoring that timely diagnosis and adequate treatment are crucial for minimizing morbidity and improving outcomes [10].

Current literature on chronic complications of pediatric OAI often examines these sequelae collectively. Studies focusing specifically on pathological fractures are scarce, consisting mainly of case reports or single-factor analyses, particularly in the pediatric population. This lack of robust data hinders the development of evidence-based strategies for the prevention and management of pathological fractures. Therefore, this study aimed to comprehensively investigate the clinical characteristics and identify the risk factors associated with pathological fractures secondary to S. aureus OAI in children. We hypothesized that specific clinical, laboratory, and imaging parameters could predict the occurrence of pathological fractures, thereby providing valuable insights for early identification, prevention, and treatment.

Patients and methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective cohort study included data from 159 children diagnosed with acute OAI who underwent surgical treatment at the Department of Orthopedics, Hebei Children’s Hospital, between July 2012 and June 2024. Based on the radiographic evidence of a subsequent pathological fracture, patients were divided into a pathological fracture group (n = 24) and a no-fracture group (n = 135). The patient selection process is detailed in Fig. 1. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Hebei Children’s Hospital (No. 2025046) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participants.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were: (1) Diagnosis of acute OAI with symptom duration < 2 weeks [11]; (2) Abnormal findings on ultrasound (e.g., joint or subperiosteal fluid collection) and/or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI); (3) Confirmation of purulent fluid via aspiration; (4) All cases confirmed by intraoperative pathological examination; (5) Positive culture for S. aureus from pus or blood; (6) Inflammatory markers obtained within 72 h of admission; (7) Age between 0.43 months and 15 years; (8) Community-acquired infection involving only long bones of the limbs; (9) Guardian consent.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) Concurrent hematologic diseases, malignancies, or immunodeficiencies; (2) Subacute or chronic osteomyelitis; (3) Osteomyelitis without abscess formation not requiring surgery; (4) Hospital-acquired infections; (5) Isolated septic arthritis or osteomyelitis not involving long bones; (6) Osteomyelitis secondary to open fractures, internal implants, or contiguous spread.

Definitions and measurements

Pathological fracture

Defined as a fracture occurring at the site of osteomyelitis after the resolution of acute local symptoms, following minimal or no trauma (e.g., falling while walking, twisting while standing up), and confirmed by plain radiography.

Bacteremia

Defined as the identification of S. aureus in blood cultures in a patient with a consistent clinical course [12].

Disseminated infection

Defined as the presence of multifocal infections, deep vein thrombosis (DVT), pneumonia, empyema, encephalitis, septic emboli to the lungs or skin, and/or pericarditis [13–15]. Admission to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU) was primarily due to sepsis, septic shock, and/or respiratory failure secondary to the infection.

Delayed surgery

Defined as surgical intervention performed after the third day of hospitalization, based on the criteria by McNeil et al. [8].

Age Categorization

Age was dichotomized based on the optimal cut-off value of ≤ 49.5 months, determined by a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis for predicting pathological fracture (AUC = 0.608; sensitivity: 70.8%, specificity: 57.8%; see Supplementary Table 1 for details).

Extent of infection and bone diameter

Measurements were performed on radiographs taken 2–4 weeks after disease onset (Fig. 2). This timeframe was chosen to allow for the full development of radiographically visible osteolytic changes, which are often minimal in the first two weeks of infection.

Treatment protocol

Antibiotic therapy

Blood cultures and baseline inflammatory markers were drawn upon suspicion of OAI, prior to the administration of the first dose of empirical antibiotics, to ensure the accuracy of initial measurements. Upon admission, empirical broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy was initiated. Treatment was subsequently tailored based on antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) results. For MRSA infections, vancomycin, teicoplanin, or linezolid was prioritized. For disseminated MSSA infections, initial intravenous (IV) therapy with these agents was also used, followed by de-escalation to first-generation cephalosporins or penicillins (e.g., oxacillin). For non-disseminated MSSA, first-generation cephalosporins were the primary choice. IV antibiotics were continued until the patient was afebrile and C-reactive protein (CRP) levels normalized, followed by a 2-week course of oral antibiotics. This resulted in a total antibiotic treatment course of approximately 4 to 6 weeks, consistent with current pediatric guidelines for complicated osteomyelitis.

Surgical treatment

All patients with confirmed abscesses underwent surgical intervention, including incision and drainage, cortical drilling or fenestration, and placement of a continuous irrigation-drainage system. The affected limb was immobilized postoperatively in a functional position using a brace or cast, with the duration determined by the extent of bone destruction on radiographs.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY). Continuous data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range (IQR). Group comparisons were performed using the independent t-test or Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical data were presented as frequencies and percentages and compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. The correlation between age and time to fracture was assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation. For the ‘time to normalization’ analyses of inflammatory markers, patients whose baseline levels were within the normal range were excluded from the respective comparison. Variables with a P-value < 0.10 in the univariate analysis and those of high clinical importance were included in a binary logistic regression model to identify independent risk factors. Results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Incidence and clinical characteristics of pathological fractures

From an initial pool of 282 children treated for S. aureus OAI, 159 met the inclusion criteria. Of these, 24 (15.1%) developed a pathological fracture. The overall incidence among the initial 280 patients was 8.5%. The median age of the 24 children with pathological fractures was 34.0 months (IQR 8.75–93.00), with 15 (62.5%) being male. Children aged ≤ 49.5 months (approximately 4 years) accounted for 70.8% (17/24) of cases. The most frequently affected site was the femur (n = 7, 29.2%), followed by the fibula (n = 6, 25.0%) and tibia (n = 5, 20.8%) (Fig. 3).

The median time from symptom onset to fracture was 55 days (IQR 48.2–71.0). A significant, strong positive correlation was observed between age and time to fracture (Spearman’s r = 0.719, P < 0.001). Children in the younger age group (≤ 49.5 months) developed fractures significantly earlier (median 53 days) than older children (> 49.5 months; median 74 days) (P = 0.003). Following the fracture, 23 cases were managed conservatively (22 with a cast/brace, 1 with traction), with one case requiring an external fixator. At the final follow-up, 11 patients (45.8%) had required or were awaiting further surgery for malunion or nonunion. Univariate analysis of risk factors

The univariate analysis of demographic and clinical characteristics is detailed in Table 2. The fracture group demonstrated a significantly higher proportion of children aged ≤ 49.5 months (P = 0.010), along with higher rates of disseminated infection (P < 0.001), PICU admission (P = 0.001), delayed surgery (P = 0.008), and bacteremia (P = 0.016). Furthermore, patients in the fracture group underwent more pre-fracture surgeries (P = 0.020) and total surgeries (P < 0.001), required more hospitalizations (P < 0.001), and had a longer postoperative fever duration (P = 0.038). Radiographically, they presented with a significantly larger extent of infection (P < 0.001) and a smaller affected bone diameter (P < 0.001). In terms of initial symptoms, 51.6% (82/159) of patients presented with fever, either alone (23.9%) or with swelling/pain (27.7%), with no significant difference between groups (P = 0.172). In contrast, there were no significant differences between the groups regarding sex, age as a continuous variable, PICU stay duration, non-orthopedic admission, precipitating factors, time to admission, initial symptoms, pre-hospital peak temperature, concurrent septic arthritis, the number of infected sites, surgical method, or recurrent fever (all P > 0.05).

Variables showing significance or near significance (P < 0.10) in the univariate analysis were entered into a binary logistic regression model. Discussion

This retrospective study identified five independent risk factors for pathological fractures secondary to S. aureus OAI in children: age ≤ 49.5 months, disseminated infection, elevated PCT, a larger extent of infection, and a smaller affected bone diameter. To our knowledge, this is one of the most comprehensive studies to focus specifically on this severe sequela, providing novel insights into its predictive factors.

The incidence of pathological fracture in our surgically treated cohort was 15.1% (24/159), and 8.5% (24/280) in the broader assessed population. This is higher than the 5% rate reported by Belthur et al. [9]. This discrepancy may be due to our inclusion of a surgically managed cohort with confirmed abscesses, who likely represent a more severe spectrum of disease, as well as a broader age range that includes infants, who are known to have a higher risk. In a single center cohort study, severe outcomes were also linked to more aggressive disease presentations [16]. Studies have shown that fever is present in approximately 50–70% of children with AHO at diagnosis [17], which aligns with our cohort’s presentation when combining fever as a sole or concurrent symptom.

Our findings highlight that younger age is a critical risk factor. Children ≤ 49.5 months not only constituted the majority of the fracture group but were also identified as an independent predictor of fracture. Furthermore, we demonstrated a strong correlation where younger age was associated with a shorter time to fracture. This suggests that the immature immune system and more fragile, porous bone structure of very young children may render them more vulnerable to rapid and extensive bone destruction by S. aureus [18]. This underscores the need for heightened vigilance in this age group.

A novel finding of our study is the role of biomechanical and imaging parameters. Both a larger extent of infection and a smaller bone diameter were independent risk factors. This is the first study, to our knowledge, to quantify and confirm these relationships in a clinical pediatric cohort. A larger area of infection logically implies more widespread structural weakening. A smaller bone diameter means there is less cortical bone to withstand physiological loads once it is compromised by infection. This aligns with findings from a murine model of S. aureus osteomyelitis, where infection led to significant bone deformation and increased fracture risk related to the volume of bone involvement [19].

Disseminated infection and elevated PCT levels were also strong independent predictors, reflecting the impact of systemic disease severity on local outcomes. Disseminated infection is a hallmark of severe, invasive S. aureus disease [20] and was previously identified as a risk factor for overall long-term complications in OAI [21]. PCT is a well-established biomarker for the severity of bacterial infections, with high levels correlating with sepsis and organ damage [22]. Our results show that PCT levels in the fracture group were markedly elevated, suggesting that a profound systemic inflammatory response is linked to more destructive local bone disease. Similarly, the longer postoperative fever duration observed in the fracture group indicates a more profound and persistent systemic inflammatory response to the severe underlying infection, rather than being a simple consequence of the surgical procedure itself.

An important finding from our comprehensive multivariate model is that while factors like delayed surgery and PICU admission were risk factors in the univariate analysis, they did not remain significant independent predictors after adjusting for confounders. This contrasts with studies where delayed source control was an independent predictor of overall chronic complications [8]. Our finding suggests that the effects of these variables may be mediated by, or confounded by, more direct measures of disease severity and patient vulnerability. For instance, a patient with delayed surgery might have a higher risk primarily because their infection was inherently more severe to begin with (as reflected by high PCT and dissemination), rather than the delay itself being the core causal factor. This highlights the importance of using a multivariate approach to disentangle complex interrelationships between risk factors.

Our recommendation for prophylactic immobilization warrants discussion. The primary purpose is to provide external support to a bone structurally compromised by infection, thereby preventing fracture from physiological loading. While immobilization carries a theoretical risk of DVT, this complication is rare in children and was not observed in our cohort. In high-risk patients, particularly those with extensive lytic lesions, the benefit of fracture prevention outweighs this low risk. Even in compliant older children, the weakened bone may be susceptible to fracture during routine activities, making immobilization a prudent protective measure.

It is crucial to interpret the high fracture incidence in the context of immobilization practices. In our cohort, immobilization was typically applied postoperatively for pain control and support, rather than as a targeted prophylactic strategy based on initial risk factors. The fact that 15.1% of these surgically treated children still fractured suggests this standard approach is inadequate. Our findings, therefore, do not question the efficacy of immobilization itself but rather highlight the need for its earlier, proactive application in patients identified with the high-risk profile at admission.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective, single-center design and the absence of an a priori sample size calculation may limit the statistical power and generalizability of the findings. Second, the observational nature precludes a definitive assessment of treatment effects, such as antibiotic duration. An important third limitation is the inability to analyze the impact of surgical technique variability. Factors such as the extent of debridement, the size of cortical windows, and the specific duration of postoperative immobilization were subject to surgeon discretion and patient-specific factors. These unquantified variables could act as confounders, and future prospective studies are needed to standardize and evaluate the impact of different surgical strategies on fracture risk. Fourth, the follow-up duration varied, potentially leading to an underestimation of complications with delayed presentation, such as growth arrest. Fifth, the study was limited to surgically treated S. aureus OAI, excluding culture-negative or non-surgically managed cases, which often have better prognoses [23]. Finally, the ROC curve for determining the age cut-off had a modest AUC, and we have provided further diagnostic metrics (Supplementary Table 1) to contextualize its limited discriminatory power as a standalone predictor; the relationship between age and fracture risk may also be non-linear.

Conclusion

Younger children are more susceptible to the rapid development of pathological fractures following S. aureus osteoarticular infections. Age ≤ 49.5 months, the presence of disseminated infection, an elevated procalcitonin level, a larger radiographic extent of infection, and a smaller diameter of the affected bone are independent risk factors for this severe complication. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for impending fracture in children with this risk profile and should strongly consider early prophylactic immobilization with a cast or brace to prevent fracture and its associated morbidity.
