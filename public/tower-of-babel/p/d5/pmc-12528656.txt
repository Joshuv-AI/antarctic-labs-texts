An enhanced HMM map matching algorithm incorporating personal road selection preferences
Authors: Yingxue Zhang, Haowen Yan, Xiaomin Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Hidden Markov Model (HMM)-based map matching algorithm presents several significant limitations. First, the candidate road segment generation process heavily depends on geometric features while largely ignoring semantic attributes and spatiotemporal context of the road network. Second, the probability modeling phase often fails to account for drivers’ individualized road selection preferences. To address these issues, this study proposes an improved HMM-based map matching algorithm that incorporates drivers’ personalized road selection preferences (PP-HMM). In the candidate road segment generation stage, a multi-dimensional fused scoring function is constructed by integrating spatial distance, directional similarity, road segment semantic attributes, and temporal factors, enabling more accurate ranking and selection of candidate segments. Moreover, by extending the state transition and observation probabilities of the HMM framework, the proposed method integrates various drivers’ personalized road selection preferences observed during actual route selection, including basic route attribute preferences, road network structural characteristics, driving behavior traits, and temporal dynamics, thereby establishing a more comprehensive transition probability model. Experimental comparisons with traditional ST-HMM algorithms demonstrate the enhanced performance and robustness of the proposed approach across diverse road network environments.

Introduction

In recent years, the rapid development of location-based services and mobile social networks has led to the widespread application of Global Positioning System (GPS) technology in vehicles and intelligent terminals. These devices are typically equipped with GPS modules and are capable of collecting large volumes of trajectory data, including mobile location sequences and vehicle status information. GPS-based trajectory data analysis has become a core component in various domains such as traffic navigation, route planning, and driving behavior research. However, due to inherent inaccuracies in GPS devices, complex urban road structures, and signal occlusion, the positioning of GPS-equipped vehicles and mobile entities—represented by GPS sampling points—may deviate from their actual positions on the road network.

Map matching algorithms play a crucial role in intelligent transportation and navigation systems1,2 by accurately mapping discrete GPS trajectory points onto corresponding road segments (Fig. 1), thereby directly influencing the accuracy and reliability of downstream applications3,4. Nevertheless, challenges persist due to uncertainties in GPS signals and the intricate configurations of road networks.Fig. 1Schematic diagram of map matching.

Schematic diagram of map matching.

The fundamental objective of map matching is to infer the true position or reconstruct the actual trajectory of a moving object by integrating observed GPS coordinates with the topological or spatial structure of the road network5. By determining the precise location of the moving object within the road network based on GPS latitude and longitude data, accurate alignment between GPS sampling points and corresponding road segments can be achieved, enabling effective error correction. Over the past decade, researchers have proposed numerous map matching algorithms to address these challenges. In 2009, Newson and Krumm6 introduced a HMM-based map matching algorithm that effectively handles the uncertainty of trajectory data and the complexity of road networks, establishing itself as a mainstream approach in this field. This method considers both geometric and topological features, fully exploits trajectory information, and transforms the matching problem into a probabilistic state estimation task, demonstrating strong robustness and adaptability. However, this algorithm exhibits certain limitations: (1) During the candidate road segment generation phase, it primarily relies on geometric features—such as projection distance and direction deviation angle—while neglecting important semantic attributes of roads (e.g., road level and lane count) and temporal dynamics (e.g., time-dependent variations in road weights); (2) The probabilistic modeling process fails to systematically incorporate drivers’ personalized road selection preferences, such as preferences for shortest-time or shortest-distance routes, road grade preferences, and habitual driving behaviors.

To address these issues, this study proposes an enhanced HMM-based map matching algorithm that integrates drivers’ personalized road selection preferences. The proposed algorithm improves the accuracy and personalization capabilities of map matching by designing a multi-constraint candidate road segment generation mechanism and extending the HMM probability modeling framework to account for individualized road selection tendencies. Specifically, the main contributions of this work are threefold: (1) A multi-constraint candidate road segment generation mechanism: A composite scoring function incorporating geometric spatial features (spatial distance, directional deviation), road semantic attributes (road grade, lane number), and temporal factors is developed to enable refined scoring and selection of candidate road segments; (2) Comprehensive integration of drivers’ personalized road selection preferences: By modifying the state transition and observation probabilities of the HMM, the model incorporates drivers’ basic path attribute preferences (shortest time or distance), road network structural feature preferences (average road level, frequency of level changes), driving behavior characteristics (speed habits, frequently traveled paths), and time-varying multi-source preferences; (3) Experimental validation and application: Comparative experiments conducted against the ST-HMM algorithm demonstrate the effectiveness and superior performance of the proposed method across diverse road network environments.

Related work

Classification of map matching algorithms

Extensive research on map matching algorithms has produced substantial advancements, which can be broadly categorized into four primary types: Geometry-based map matching algorithms7–10, topology-based map matching algorithms11–14, probability-based map matching algorithms15–24, and machine learning-based approaches25–28.

Geometry-based map matching algorithms primarily rely on spatial features such as distance, angle, and shape to perform matching. However, these methods do not consider the connectivity between road segments, resulting in limited stability and accuracy, particularly in complex urban environments. Topology-based algorithms improve upon this by incorporating both proximity and network connectivity from a topological perspective. While they offer enhanced efficiency and accuracy, their performance remains sensitive to data noise and sparse trajectory sampling.

Probability-based map matching algorithms treat GPS coordinates as random variables and trajectories as stochastic processes. These methods define confidence regions—typically elliptical or rectangular—around each sampling point and compute likelihood scores based on spatial distances within those regions. The most probable path is then determined using probabilistic inference. Despite their theoretical advantages, such methods often suffer from high computational complexity and implementation difficulty.

Advanced algorithms, including Kalman filtering16, fuzzy logic models17, and HMM approaches6, integrate both geometric and topological information. Among them, the HMM algorithm proposed by Newson and Krumm is widely recognized as a classic method in the field, demonstrating strong robustness in handling GPS noise and complex road networks. Nevertheless, traditional HMM algorithms predominantly depend on geometric and topological features, neglecting drivers’ personalized road selection preferences—an important factor in real-world applications.

With the advancement of machine learning technologies, researchers have increasingly applied deep learning and reinforcement learning techniques to map matching tasks. For example, some studies leverage trajectory data to train neural network models for predicting the corresponding roads of trajectory points. However, these methods typically require large-scale labeled datasets and face challenges related to model interpretability, limiting their practical deployment.

Although the aforementioned methods have achieved promising results in various scenarios, they still exhibit limitations in addressing complex traffic environments and individual driving behaviors. Notably, few studies have explored how drivers’ personalized road selection preferences influence map matching outcomes, despite its significance in real-world navigation systems. Therefore, integrating personalized road selection preferences into map matching algorithms represents a critical direction for future research.

Optimization of HMM map matching algorithm accuracy

Improvements in the accuracy of classical HMM-based map matching algorithms can be grouped into three major categories: (1) Enhancing the probabilistic modeling framework to refine accuracy. For instance, Liu et al.29 introduced driving direction into transition probability calculations. The ST-Matching algorithm30 integrates spatiotemporal features, geometric topology, and speed constraints, employing Gaussian distributions to model observation probabilities and computing transition probabilities based on vehicle speed, GPS observations, and measured positions. (2) Pre- and post-processing of trajectory data to enhance matching precision. Zhou31 proposed manually labeling error-prone points before applying standard HMM matching to other points. Wu et al.32 used genetic algorithms to further optimize HMM-derived matching results, improving segment-level accuracy. Wöltche33 developed a novel approach combining Markov decision processes and reinforcement learning to dynamically handle outliers and noise clusters. (3) Incorporating additional data sources to improve accuracy. For example, the EHMM algorithm34 and STD-Matching algorithm35 utilize instantaneous directional information, the OHMM algorithm36 incorporates road width data, and the IVMM algorithm37 introduces speed-related parameters to enhance matching precision.

Optimization of map matching algorithm efficiency

Efficiency optimization is a crucial requirement in practical implementations of map matching algorithms: (1) Reducing computational overhead through controlled expansion of candidate road segments. Huang et al.38 proposed extracting common patterns from historical trajectory data to limit the search space of candidate segments, thereby improving efficiency. Raymond et al.39 replaced road segments with intersections, transforming line-point analysis into point-point analysis and significantly reducing geometric computation complexity. Shiteng40 and Xie41 improved efficiency by segmenting trajectory data and minimizing the number of state transition probability computations. (2) Utilizing spatial indexing structures to accelerate candidate road segment retrieval. Song et al.42 implemented R-tree indexing to build an efficient road network index, enabling rapid identification of sub-trajectory-associated road segments. This method further enhances efficiency by analyzing position change rates to segment trajectories and focusing on key points rather than entire paths. Xiaomeng et al.43 proposed a grid-based map matching model that uses hash indexing for fast candidate segment lookup, effectively lowering time complexity. Wei et al.44 and Yuguang45 constructed indexing structures based on grid partitioning and road network rasterization, enabling rapid localization of candidate segments via binary search algorithms.

Current limitations of map matching algorithms

Despite significant progress in map matching technology, existing algorithms still face challenges in handling complex traffic conditions and capturing individual driving behaviors. In particular, few studies have considered the impact of drivers’ personalized road selection preferences on matching accuracy, although this factor plays a vital role in real-world applications. For example, Ceikute and Jensen46 proposed an improved navigation service by comparing driver behavior with trajectory data in terms of route length, popularity, and travel time. Dai et al.47 leveraged large-scale trajectory data to provide personalized driving recommendations. Zheng et al.48 developed a system that infers users’ historical travel paths by analyzing behavioral patterns and integrating them into route reasoning. Huang et al.38 identified recurring patterns in historical trajectory data and applied them to map matching tasks. Gao et al.49 proposed a preference-driven matching algorithm that combines local driving habits with global dynamic programming for road network alignment. Moreover, current algorithms often struggle to balance computational efficiency and accuracy when dealing with high-density, high-dynamic traffic data. Real-time performance and scalability are especially critical in large-scale deployments, where algorithms must deliver accurate results rapidly. Hence, developing efficient and reliable map matching algorithms capable of adapting to complex environments and personalized needs remains a key research focus.

In conclusion, integrating drivers’ personalized road selection preferences into map matching algorithms while enhancing real-time performance and accuracy constitutes one of the most pressing challenges in future research.

HMM-based map matching

HMM is a probabilistic framework widely employed in time series analysis. It describes a process where a hidden Markov chain generates an unobservable state sequence, and each state stochastically produces an observable value sequence38. In the context of a road network, it is assumed that the movement of a moving object follows a Markov process, meaning the current state depends solely on its preceding state and is independent of earlier historical states. While the actual road trajectory traversed by the moving object cannot be directly observed, its corresponding outputs can be inferred from GPS sampling points. Leveraging this principle, the HMM model can estimate the most probable hidden state sequence that corresponds to the observed sequence, thereby reconstructing the actual motion trajectory based on a series of GPS sampling points.

In HMM-based map matching algorithms, the location information collected by GPS devices is treated as the set of observations, while the actual positions of these devices are modeled as the set of hidden states. This transformation allows the map matching problem to be reformulated as a state estimation problem within the framework of HMM. System framework

The proposed algorithmic framework, as illustrated in Fig. 2, consists of two primary components: Candidate Road Set Generation and Feature Analysis. During the data preprocessing phase, trajectory data and road network data are systematically processed to generate the candidate road set. In the Candidate Road Set Generation stage, a multi-dimensional fusion scoring approach is applied to accurately rank and filter the candidate segments, thereby identifying the most probable matching road segments. The Feature Analysis phase focuses on refining the observation and transition probabilities: The observation probability integrates both spatial distance and directional deviation, while the transition probability incorporates the influence of drivers’ personalized road selection preferences. Candidate road segment generation

In the HMM-based map matching process, the generation of candidate road segments represents a critical stage in model construction. The primary objective of this phase is to identify potential road segments from the entire road network that are associated with the current trajectory point, thereby forming the basis for subsequent calculations of state transition probabilities and observation probabilities. In this study, the candidate road segment generation process begins with the application of an adaptive R-tree-based spatial indexing technique, which efficiently narrows down the search space and reduces computational overhead across the entire road network. Subsequently, a multi-dimensional fusion scoring mechanism is applied to accurately rank and filter the candidate set, enabling the selection of the most probable matching road segments.

Drivers’ personalized road selection preferences

Drivers’ personalized road selection preferences refers to the differential choice behavior exhibited by individuals based on their utility evaluation of available routes within a multi-modal transportation environment50. Research suggests that experienced drivers engage in a complex risk–benefit trade-off during route decision-making, where the criteria extend beyond minimizing expected travel time to include assessments of delay risk probability and potential for travel time optimization51,52. Specifically, drivers with an optimistic risk attitude tend to choose the theoretically shortest-time path while underestimating the impact of traffic congestion53, whereas risk-averse drivers place significant emphasis on route familiarity, aligning with the principles of cognitive economy theory54. From a probabilistic standpoint, when a road segment exhibits low overall cost and high individual preference probability, its likelihood of being included in the actual trajectory increases significantly55. This study identifies seven core route selection factors grounded in transportation behavior theory and the discrete choice model framework, which are integrated into the HMM map matching algorithm.

These core route selection factors include:

Basic route attributes Minimum travel distance56 and minimum travel time57 serve as fundamental variables in conventional route selection decisions;

Road network structural characteristics Average road grade58 and frequency of road grade changes59, reflecting driving comfort and cognitive workload;

Driving behavior preferences Speed habits60 represent individual tendencies in speed selection, while habitual routes61 indicate path dependency behaviors;

Temporal dynamic factors Time-of-day patterns62 capture variations in commuter decision-making during peak and off-peak periods.

Enhancement of transition probabilities

In conventional HMM, the computation of transition probabilities primarily depends on geometric distances between road segments or trajectory points. However, this approach has inherent limitations, as it fails to fully capture the multi-dimensional decision-making preferences that drivers exhibit during real-world route selection. To improve the model’s ability to characterize the rationality of continuous trajectories, this study incorporates seven core route selection factors: Minimum travel distance, minimum travel time, average road grade, frequency of road grade changes, speed preferences, habitual route usage, and temporal patterns. These factors collectively form the basis for constructing a more comprehensive and behavior-aware transition probability function.

Traditional transition probability models rely exclusively on geometric distances, thereby overlooking the influence of drivers’ personalized road selection behaviors. To address this limitation, the present study extends the classical transition probability framework into a joint probabilistic model that integrates seven categories of drivers’ personalized road selection preferences. This enhancement enables a more accurate representation of dynamic transition tendencies and strengthens the modeling of trajectory continuity and rationality. Algorithmic solution

The optimal matching path problem within the HMM framework can be effectively solved using the Viterbi algorithm, a dynamic programming technique. The Viterbi algorithm identifies the most probable sequence of hidden states—corresponding to the optimal sequence of matched road segments—by iteratively computing joint probabilities based on observation and transition probabilities. At each step, it records the preceding state that leads to the current maximum likelihood, ultimately determining the endpoint of the optimal path.

For the enhanced HMM map matching algorithm that incorporates drivers’ personalized road selection preferences, these behavioral factors—including shortest travel distance, minimal travel time, average road grade, frequency of road grade changes, speed habits, habitual route usage, and temporal patterns—require holistic optimization across the entire trajectory rather than isolated point-level matching. The Viterbi algorithm ensures that the resulting path satisfies both geometric consistency (local spatial constraints) and behavioral continuity by recursively computing cumulative probabilities. The recursive formula for the maximum likelihood probability is defined as:18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta_{t} (e_{j} ) = \max \left[ {\delta_{t - 1} (e_{i} ) \cdot P(e_{j} \left| {e_{i} } \right.)} \right] \cdot P(o_{t} \left| {e_{j} } \right.)$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta_{t} (e_{j} )$$\end{document} denotes the maximum likelihood probability of road segment \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$e_{j}$$\end{document} at time \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P(e_{j} \left| {e_{i} } \right.)$$\end{document}* and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P(o_{t} \left| {e_{j} } \right.)$$\end{document} represent the enhanced transition probability and observation probability, respectively. Through this recursive computation, the final optimal matching path is determined, and the complete path is reconstructed by backtracking through the recorded predecessor states.

After completing road segment-level matching, point-level matching is further performed. The procedure is as follows: If the target trajectory point can be directly projected onto the optimal matching road segment, the projection point is selected as the matching location, if the projection falls outside the segment (e.g., on its extension), the distances between the trajectory point and both endpoints of the road segment are computed, and the closer endpoint is chosen as the matching point.

By integrating these enhancements, the proposed HMM-based map matching algorithm not only achieves accurate road segment-level matching but also enables precise localization of trajectory points on the road network, thereby significantly improving both the accuracy and robustness of the overall map matching process.

Conclusion

This study addresses the limitations of conventional HMM-based map-matching algorithms by proposing an enhanced HMM algorithm—PP-HMM, which incorporates drivers’ individualized road selection preferences. Through the construction of a multi-dimensional fusion scoring function, the algorithm enables more precise filtering of candidate road segments by integrating geometric spatial features, road semantic attributes, and spatiotemporal contextual information. Furthermore, during the probability modeling phase, the algorithm systematically incorporates multiple aspects of driver behavior, including drivers’ basic path attribute preferences (shortest time or distance), road network structural feature preferences (average road level, frequency of level changes), driving behavior characteristics (speed habits, frequently traveled paths), and time-varying multi-source preferences. This integration enhances the algorithm’s alignment with real-world driving patterns.

Experimental results demonstrate that the PP-HMM algorithm significantly outperforms the ST-HMM algorithm in terms of matching accuracy across diverse urban road networks. Specifically, the PP-HMM achieved average precision rates of 94.90%, 94.11%, and 94.41% in the road networks of Beijing, Hangzhou, and Wuxi, respectively, along with a notable reduction in single-point processing time. In-depth case studies further confirm the algorithm’s effectiveness in handling complex road environments and improving trajectory stability.

In summary, the PP-HMM algorithm offers a more efficient, accurate, and personalized approach to map matching, making a meaningful contribution to the development of intelligent transportation systems. Future research directions include incorporating additional real-time traffic data, such as traffic flow and congestion levels, which enhance the algorithm’s adaptability in dynamic environments. Moreover, optimizing computational complexity and exploring strategies for efficient deployment on resource-constrained platforms, such as in-vehicle navigation systems and mobile devices, that can broaden its practical applicability and enable more accurate location-based services for end users.
