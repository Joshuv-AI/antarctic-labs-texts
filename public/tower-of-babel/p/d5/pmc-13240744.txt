Developmental Timing and Network Directionality in the Triple-Network Model of Anorexia Nervosa
Authors: Kelsey E Hagan
Published: 2026

Anorexia nervosa (AN) is a severe disorder of self-starvation that results in objectively low body weight and has devastating medical and psychosocial consequences, including one of the highest mortality rates of all psychiatric disorders (1). Despite the substantial morbidity and mortality associated with AN, the outcomes of current treatments for this disorder are disappointing (2). First-line psychotherapies (namely, family-based treatment) result in full symptom remission for less than a half of adolescents with AN. Psychosocial treatments for adults with AN have limited effectiveness (2), as translating skills used to achieve weight restoration in a supportive setting (e.g., inpatient, residential) to the patient’s home can be challenging, and pathological food restriction can reemerge. There are no established brain-based treatments (including medication) for AN. There is an urgent need to improve treatment outcomes for this potentially lethal disorder, and a better understanding of the biobehavioral mechanisms of AN may provide insights into treatment targets.

One promising framework that may advance understanding of AN is the triple-network model of psychopathology (3). This model posits that alterations in functional connectivity among the brain’s salience network (SN), frontoparietal/central executive network (CEN), and default mode network (DMN) may underpin multiple forms of psychopathology. Broadly, the DMN has been implicated in self-referential thinking and rumination, and the CEN has been linked to cognitive control and task-relevant decision making. The SN has been associated with salience detection, interoception, and valuation and in facilitating switches from DMN to CEN engagement when a salient external stimulus is detected. Research has demonstrated altered functional connectivity within and between the SN, CEN, and DMN in AN, and brain-behavior analyses have suggested that these disturbances may be linked to AN psychopathology (4). However, its application to AN (and eating disorders in general) has been limited, particularly with respect to developmental timing and directionality of network interactions.

In a recent study published in Biological Psychiatry: Global Open Science, Taniguchi et al. (5) conducted one of the first tests of the triple-network model in a Japanese multisite study of female, treatment-seeking patients with AN (n = 111) and matched healthy volunteers (n = 131) who underwent resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI). Using independent component analysis (ICA), the authors compared within- and between-network intrinsic functional connectivity of the SN, CEN, and DMN in AN and healthy volunteer groups. Results indicated decreased intrinsic connectivity between the SN (insula) and the right CEN in AN compared with healthy volunteers. Additionally, lower connectivity within the SN (anterior cingulate cortex) and DMN were observed among participants with AN relative to healthy volunteers. Moreover, in the AN group, lower within-network intrinsic connectivity of the SN (particularly the anterior cingulate cortex) was associated with higher scores on the shape and weight subscales of the Eating Disorders Examination Questionnaire. Given that the SN is implicated in valuation, the authors posited that dysfunction in this network may lead to overvaluation of shape and weight, prompting behaviors aimed at exerting control over them (e.g., restrictive eating). Taniguchi et al.’s (5) approach is exciting, as it moves the field of eating disorders from studying regions of interest and brain networks in isolation toward an integrated understanding of brain network function in AN. These findings provide a meaningful starting point for further interrogation of open questions about the triple-network model in AN. For example, it remains unclear when triple-network alterations emerge in AN, whether they reflect developmental vulnerability to AN or illness-related effects (e.g., state, scar), and how they contribute to the maladaptive restrictive eating central to AN psychopathology. Together, these questions—when triple-network alterations emerge and how information flows between networks—are critical for identifying mechanistic targets for interventions in AN (Figure 1).

As Taniguchi et al.’s (5) sample primarily comprised adults with AN, testing the triple-network model in adolescents with AN using developmental approaches is an important next step. Brain networks undergo dynamic reorganization during adolescence, and alterations within the triple network may underpin the development of psychiatric disorders that commonly emerge during adolescence (6). For example, findings from a developmental sample (the Philadelphia Neurodevelopmental Cohort) suggested that lower intrinsic connectivity between the DMN and CEN is linked to several dimensions of psychopathology, including mood, psychosis, externalizing behavior, and fear (7); eating disorders were not included in this analysis. The typical age of onset of AN is 15.5 years, coinciding in time with brain network development. A developmental approach could reveal whether AN alters the development of the triple network or is a consequence of preexisting alterations within it. A prospective longitudinal design following young adolescents at risk for AN (by virtue of parental history of AN and/or elevated polygenic risk scores) over time as they pass through the peak age of onset could test this question and inform neurobiological targets for prevention or early intervention. As prospective longitudinal data collection is resource intensive, the richly phenotyped, multimodal Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study or other similar large-scale samples (e.g., the IMAGEN study) could be leveraged to answer this question.

A related unanswered question is whether functional connectivity alterations observed in acute AN [as in Taniguchi et al. (5)] are consistent across the early and chronic stages of AN and whether they persist after illness recovery. Currently, functional connectivity alterations in AN appear to be partially state dependent, with longitudinal studies demonstrating normalization of DMN and CEN connectivity following recovery, although some alterations persist beyond recovery (8). Key limitations of existing research in this area have been modest sample sizes, limited follow-up, and heterogeneous definitions of recovery. A cross-sectional developmental design that incorporates adolescents at risk for AN, with recent AN diagnoses (<6 months), with established AN (at least 12 months), and who have recovered from AN (at least 12 months) as well as matched healthy volunteers could provide insights into the timing of triple-network change (state or trait) and the reversibility of these changes (scar).

In addition to identifying the time course of triple-network alterations in relation to AN illness stage, another future direction is to clarify the directionality of connectivity alterations within the triple network among individuals with AN. Taniguchi et al. (5) used resting-state functional connectivity and ICA, methods that have provided foundational insights into potential biomarkers of psychopathology. However, a key limitation of resting-state functional connectivity is that it does not capture the directional influence of one brain region (or network) on another brain region (or network). Furthermore, rs-fMRI, by definition, occurs when participants are at rest (e.g., not performing a neurocognitive task). Thus, rs-fMRI cannot provide insight into cognitive processes salient in AN, such as the decision making underlying restrictive eating or valuation of body cues. Effective connectivity (often modeled with dynamic causal modeling [DCM]) is a method that can circumvent these limitations and enable explicit tests of how these networks coordinate with one another during disease-relevant processes.

Effective connectivity enables modeling of how neural activity in one region (or network) causally influences another, either at rest or in response to task demands. Applied to the triple-network model, effective connectivity can be used to test hypotheses about whether the SN appropriately engages the CEN and suppresses the DMN in response to relevant cues. For example, the food-choice task captures restrictive food choice, and task performance correlates with food consumption in the laboratory among individuals with AN (9). Thus, DCM applied to food-choice task data collected during fMRI scanning could elucidate the triple-network dynamics when restrictive eating processes are engaged and provide valuable insights into treatment targets.

In closing, Taniguchi et al. (5) pave the way for an integrated understanding of brain network coordination in eating disorders that can inform intervention development. Critical next steps are to determine whether triple-network alterations reflect developmental vulnerability, acute illness effects, or scars of eating disorders, as well as the directionality of alterations in triple-network communication. Answering these questions may identify treatment targets and ultimately improve treatment outcomes for a disorder in which current therapies fail many patients.
