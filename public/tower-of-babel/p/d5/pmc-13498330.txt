Sleep-Disordered Breathing and Decreased Bone Mineral Density: Masking Effects of Body Mass Index
Authors: Wiaam Y. Elkhatib, Virend K. Somers, Prachi Singh, Jan Bukartyk, Joshua M. Bock, Sreeja Sompalli, Soumya Vungarala, Alexandria B. Danyluk, Abel Romero-Corral, Naima Covassin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe relationship between sleep-disordered breathing (SDB), bone mineral density (BMD), and risk of osteoporosis is unclear. Obesity, a condition often comorbid with SDB, has been associated with high BMD, yet its impact on bone health remains controversial. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the implications of obesity in the relationship between SDB and BMD.MethodsA total of 225 adults aged 20–65 years were enrolled in this study. Participants were nonsmokers, free of major cardiovascular diseases, and not receiving treatment for SDB. Participants completed overnight polysomnography and whole-body dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) scans. SDB was defined as apnea-hypopnea index of ≥5 events/hour. Total body BMD (g/cm2), T scores, and Z scores were obtained from DEXA scans. Height and weight measures were collected for computation of body mass index (BMI, kg/m2). Multivariable linear regression analysis was conducted to test the association between SDB and BMD metrics and the contribution of relevant covariates.ResultsParticipants with SDB (n = 74, 32.9% of the sample) had higher total-body BMD (p = 0.003), T score (p = 0.017), and Z score (p = 0.011) compared to non-SDB controls. In multivariable models adjusted for demographic characteristics, SDB was not significantly associated with total-body bone measures. However, when including BMI, significant inverse relationships between SDB and total body BMD (p = 0.046), T score (p = 0.049), and Z score (p = 0.049) became apparent. Such associations persisted after controlling for lifestyle, clinical and sleep variables (fully adjusted models, total BMD, p = 0.036; T score, p = 0.037; Z score, p = 0.034). In sensitivity analysis, there were no associations between SDB and bone measures in models adjusted for body fat in lieu of BMI.ConclusionAn inverse, albeit modest, association between SDB and BMD exists, and this relationship emerges when considering BMI but not body fat. Hence, our data suggest that elevated body mass rather than adiposity may mask the negative association between SDB and bone health, thus possibly explaining prior conflicting evidence. Longitudinal investigations corroborating these findings and evaluating their implications for osteoporosis risk in patients with SDB are needed.

Introduction

Sleep-disordered breathing (SDB) is characterized by aberrant respiration during sleep and presents most commonly as repetitive collapse of the upper airways (obstructive sleep apnea, OSA). Disordered breathing events cause intermittent hypoxemia and arousals from sleep, activating multiple disease mechanisms including sympathetic activation, oxidative stress, and inflammation, resulting in disrupted sleep and daytime symptoms. It is estimated that >50 million Americans and 1 billion individuals worldwide suffer from SDB, with a substantial proportion of them being undiagnosed and thus untreated [1]. Notably, the prevalence of SDB continues to trend upwards, partly as a function of the aging population and the unabated obesity epidemic [2, 3], as both advanced age and excess body weight are major risk factors for SDB [4]. SDB is an established determinant of adverse outcomes, with compelling evidence linking it to increased risk of multiple diseases including cardiovascular disease and cancer [5, 6]. Furthermore, SDB increases odds of disability and premature mortality [7, 8], thus placing a significant burden on public health.

A growing body of evidence suggests SDB may also have implications for bone health and osteoporosis risk. Osteoporosis ensues from microarchitectural degradation of bone tissue and low bone mineral density (BMD), leading to increased bone fragility, bone pain, and vulnerability to fractures. Osteoporosis presents in roughly 20% of the population, disproportionately affecting women and older adults, and increases functional impairment and worsens quality of life [9, 10].

Several investigations have identified SDB as an independent predictor of increased osteoporosis risk [11–13], but there are reports suggesting it may instead be protective [14, 15]. Data on the association between SDB and BMD are especially inconsistent. Studies have found that patients with SDB had higher [15–17], lower [18–20], or similar [21, 22] BMD compared to nonapneic controls. While heterogeneity of study samples and outcome measures may be implicated in such disparate findings, the potentially confounding role of obesity has received little attention. Obesity is a well-known determinant of SDB risk and contributes to the adverse consequences of poor sleep [23–25]. However, its implications for bone health remain a matter of debate, with evidence pointing towards both detrimental and protective effects [26, 27]. A favorable role of obesity on bone metabolism is supported by pooled data indicating overall lower risk of osteoporosis in obese individuals, and is corroborated by the positive correlation between body mass index (BMI) and BMD [28]. Nevertheless, other substantial bodies of work indicate a deleterious impact of obesity on bone metabolism [29–31]. The relationship between obesity and bone health may also be a function of obesity assessment, namely whether BMI or body fat measures are used to define obesity. As such, obesity may play a role in the complex interplay between SDB and bone metabolism. Building on this evidence, we sought to investigate the association between SDB, total body and regional BMD, and the potential implications of obesity in the relationship.

Methods

Participants

The study population was drawn from a cohort of consecutive subjects aged 20–65 years who were recruited for research studies conducted at the Cardiovascular/Sleep Laboratory at Mayo Clinic from 2005 to 2023 and underwent in-laboratory polysomnography (PSG) and total body dual energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) for body composition assessment. Approval of the Institutional Review Board was obtained, and participants signed a written informed consent prior to participation. Participants were nonsmokers, nondiabetic, free of major health disorders and not receiving treatment for SDB. From an initial sample of 235 individuals, 10 were excluded due to lack of DEXA data (n = 1), no PSG (n = 1), age <20 years (n = 5), or use of SDB treatment (n = 3), yielding an analytic sample of 225 participants.

Each participant underwent a study visit at the Mayo Clinic Clinical Research and Trials Unit (CRTU). Height and weight were measured to the nearest 1 cm and 0.1 kg, respectively, and used for computation of BMI (kg/m2). Waist and hip measurements were used to calculate waist-to-hip ratio (WHR). Intake surveys were administered to collect demographics, medical history, and medication use. Menopausal status was self-reported. Smoking history was defined as past use of cigarettes or tobacco products. Alcohol use was quantified as the reported number of alcohol drinks consumed per week. Physical activity was determined from the question “How many times per week do you exercise?” Habitual sleep duration was assessed as the self-reported number of hours of sleep during a 24-h period. The Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS) [32] was used to assess daytime sleepiness, with scores ranging from 0 to 24 and a score greater than 10 suggesting excessive daytime sleepiness.

Polysomnography

Each participant underwent an attended overnight PSG in the CRTU (E-Series [n = 26], Grael [n = 199]; Compumedics, Melbourne, Australia). Montage included electroencephalography, chin and leg electromyography, electrocardiography, electrooculography, pulse oximetry, snore sensor, body position sensor, thoracic and abdominal respiratory effort belts, oronasal airflow by thermal sensor, and nasal pressure transducer. PSG recordings were scored according to standard criteria [33] by a registered PSG technologist. Apnea was defined as a 90% or greater drop in airflow lasting 10 s or longer. Hypopnea was defined as a 30% or greater decrease in airflow lasting 10 s or longer and accompanied by a 4% or greater drop in oxygen saturation. The apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) was calculated as the total number of apnea and hypopnea episodes normalized by hours of sleep. SDB was defined as an AHI of ≥5 events/hour. SDB severity was further classified as mild (AHI 5-14.9) or moderate-to-severe (AHI ≥15). Additional PSG variables included sleep efficiency, arousal index, and nadir oxygen saturation (SpO2).

Dual-Energy X-Ray Absorptiometry

Whole-body DEXA scans (Lunar Prodigy [n = 23], Lunar iDXA [n = 202]; GE Healthcare, Madison, WI) were performed by certified operators. Quality control body mass phantoms were used to ensure scanner calibration. Data were analyzed using auto-analysis features of the densitometer software (GE Encore version 17 SP5). Total body and regional (legs, pelvis, spine, trunk, ribs, arms, and head) BMD (g/cm2) measures were calculated. Total body T scores were computed as SD from a young healthy reference population, and Z scores were calculated as SD from an age-matched reference population. Total body fat percentage, fat mass, and lean mass were also quantified.

Statistical Analysis

Normally distributed data were presented as mean and SD, while skewed continuous variables were presented as median and interquartile range (25th and 75th percentiles). Nominal data were expressed as count and percentage. Results were stratified into SDB and non-SDB groups. Independent t tests and Mann-Whitney U tests were used for between-group comparisons, as appropriate. Pearson’s Chi-squared tests were applied to compare frequency distributions between groups.

Multivariable linear regression analysis was run to assess the association between SDB and BMD metrics and the contribution of relevant covariates. Model 1 included demographics (age, sex, and race/ethnicity). BMI was added to model 2, and model 3 was further adjusted for lifestyle variables (smoking history, alcohol use, and exercise). Habitual sleep duration, ESS score, and sleep medication use were included in model 4. Lastly, history of hypertension and vitamin D/calcium supplementation were added to model 5. Effect modifications of sex, BMI, sleep duration, and daytime sleepiness were assessed. Menopausal status and use of contraceptives were corrected for in models including females only. In a sensitivity analysis, DEXA-derived body fat percentage and fat mass measures were included in lieu of BMI in the regression models. Scanner models and anti-hypertensive medication use were also assessed as covariates in sensitivity analysis. Absence of collinearity was verified by a variance inflation factor of <5. Regression coefficients and SE were reported. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 28 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY), and p values of <0.05 were considered significant.

Results

SDB was found in 74 (32.9%) patients, of which 42 had mild SDB and 32 had moderate-to-severe disorder. Those with SDB were older, had a higher BMI, WHR, body fat percentage, fat mass, and lean mass relative to those without SDB. Cases were also more likely than controls to be male and to use sleep medications. Sleep variables showed that nocturnal SpO2 nadir was lower while the arousal index was higher in individuals with SDB compared to those without. Discussion

Our study shows that, in univariate analysis, individuals with SDB have higher total BMD, T score, and Z score than controls. Nevertheless, negative albeit modest associations between SDB and DEXA-derived measures of bone health emerged after taking into account BMI in multivariable-adjusted models. Thus, elevated body mass may mask the potential detrimental impact of SDB on skeletal health.

Prior evidence on the implications of SDB for BMD is conflicting. Some studies found, in line with our crude results, higher BMD in individuals with sleep apnea [15–17]. In a large (n = 832), population-based sample of the elderly, Sforza et al. [15] found that those with an AHI of ≥15 had higher BMD and T scores, as measured at the lumbar spine and at the femur, than those with an AHI of <15. By contrast, other reports indicate lower BMD values in individuals with sleep apnea compared to controls [18–20]. Of note, these studies included only males. Additionally, others failed to find any evidence of a relationship between SDB and DEXA-derived bone health metrics [21, 22]. Pazarli et al. [22] did not observe significant differences in BMD, T scores, and Z scores at the lumbar spine, femoral neck and total femur when comparing controls (AHI <5) to SDB groups.

With respect to osteoporosis risk, SDB has been found to be detrimental in most cross-sectional and longitudinal studies [11–13, 18, 19]. Patients with moderate and severe sleep apnea were more likely to have osteopenia or osteoporosis than individuals without sleep apnea [13]. Using a large national medical health insurance claims database, Chen et al. [11] matched 1,377 patients with a diagnosis of sleep apnea to a cohort of 20,655 patients without sleep apnea. In multivariable adjusted analysis, patients with sleep apnea were 2.7 times more likely to develop osteoporosis than non-apneic patients over a 6-year follow-up period. Nevertheless, contrasting findings have also been reported. Two cross-sectional analyses of a population-based cohort of adults aged ≥65 years showed that individuals with osteopenia or osteoporosis had lower AHI than those with normal BMD [14, 15], suggesting that sleep apnea may confer protection against osteoporosis.

Discrepancies across the aforementioned studies can be plausibly attributed to methodological differences, including differences in study population, sample size, and SDB diagnostic criteria. Additionally, a critical yet elusive modulator of the interplay between SDB and bone metabolism may be obesity. The relationship between sleep apnea and obesity is complex and likely bidirectional [23–25]; while obesity predisposes to SDB and contributes to unfavorable outcomes, sleep apnea also causes metabolic derangements that promote fat gain. As it pertains to bone health, Liguori et al. [18] reported lower BMD and T scores in 92 male patients with OSA compared to 50 age- and BMI-matched non-OSA patients. Conversely, Chen et al. 2017 [16] found higher hip BMD and T score in males with sleep apnea than in controls despite similar BMI, and this positive association withstood adjustment for multiple confounders, including BMI. Another study reported higher lumbar and femoral T scores in older male and female patients with OSA, but this relationship remained independent of other covariates only among patients with low BMI (<24 kg/m2) [17]. Elucidating the role of obesity in the link between sleep apnea and skeletal health is complicated by the unclear impact of excess body weight per se on bone integrity.

The classical concept that obesity protects bone health is grounded on the positive association between BMI and BMD coupled with overall lower risk of osteoporosis exhibited by obese individuals, as summarized in a meta-analytic work [28]. The favorable impact of increased body mass on osteogenesis is thought to be mediated by high physical loading and strain [26]. Additionally, the adipose tissues are a major source of estrogens in both males and females, which facilitate bone formation [35]. On the other hand, these beneficial effects have been called into question as excess adiposity, particularly visceral fat accumulation, is associated with low-grade chronic inflammation [36] which impairs bone metabolism [37]. Insulin resistance, aberrant adipokine production, vitamin D deficiency, and sedentary behaviors are also thought to be implicated in accelerating bone loss in obese individuals [26]. In support of a detrimental effect of obesity on bone integrity are data from studies reporting inverse associations between BMD and both total fat mass and visceral fat [30, 31] with heightened vulnerability to osteoporosis in individuals with high body fat percentage [29]. Relatedly, the definition of obesity deserves consideration. Although obesity is canonically determined by BMI, this crude anthropometric index does not differentiate between fat and fat-free mass. As such, BMI-based categorization may lead to misclassification, as a wide range of body fat percentages exist at any given BMI [38, 39]. To evaluate the relevance of the obesity definition for our observations, in a sensitivity analysis we tested whether adjusting for DEXA-derived adiposity measures would alter our findings and found that no significant association between SDB and bone loss emerged when controlling for body fat percentage or fat mass instead of BMI. This suggests that greater overall body mass rather than excess adipose tissue masks this relationship.

Of note, regional DEXA data indicated that SDB was an independent, negative predictor of spine BMD. This finding is consistent with evidence by Yuceege et al. [13] and Wang et al. [40] who showed that sleep apnea was negatively associated with lumbar spine BMD. Nevertheless, null [21] or positive associations [14, 15] with this skeletal site have also been reported.

Evidence implicates intermittent hypoxia, the hallmark of SDB, as a pathophysiological mechanism linking this sleep disorder to abnormal bone metabolism. Hypoxia inhibits osteoblast differentiation, decreasing bone matrix mineralization and bone formation, while stimulating osteoclastogenesis and osteoclast-mediated bone resorption [41, 42]. The molecular effects of hypoxia are largely orchestrated through hypoxia-inducible factor signaling, which may impair bone remodeling and reduce bone perfusion by modulating osteogenesis and angiogenesis [41]. Exposure to hypoxia promotes acidosis, which upregulates osteoclast formation and activation and suppresses osteoblast differentiation, contributing to bone resorption and bone matrix deterioration [43, 44]. Generation of reactive oxygen species during cyclic hypoxemia-reoxygenation episodes, along with increased inflammation, sympathetic overdrive, and endocrine disruption, may also mediate aberrant bone function in relation to SDB [37, 45–47]. These mechanisms may also underlie the potential adverse effects of sleep fragmentation and sleep deprivation, as typically experienced by patients with SDB. Short sleep duration has been identified as an independent predictor of low BMD and increased risk of osteoporosis in some studies [48], albeit others found a link between long sleep and poor skeletal health [49], or an increased risk associated with both ends of the sleep duration continuum [34]. Additionally, vitamin D deficiency is more common in those with SDB, and it may contribute to bone loss in these patients [50].

The first-line intervention for sleep apnea is continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) [51], which prevents apneas and hypopneas by preserving upper airway patency. In spite of its proven efficacy against disordered breathing events, evidence of broader health advantages conferred by CPAP is limited, partly due to suboptimal adherence and heterogeneity in the patient population [52, 53]. Few studies have investigated the effects of CPAP on bone function and skeletal health. Tomiyama et al. [54] showed that levels of plasma cytokines and urinary C-terminal cross-linking telopeptide of type I collagen, a bone resorption marker, decreased after 3 months of CPAP therapy in patients with severe OSA, but there were no appreciable changes in osteocalcin or bone-specific alkaline phosphatase (bone formation markers). Theorell-Haglöw et al. [55] found increased osteocalcin after 24 weeks in patients using CPAP at least 4 hrs/night and increased vitamin D in those with severe OSA and excessive daytime sleepiness. While evidence is sparse, these findings highlight the potential influences of treatment adherence with CPAP and SDB phenotype on bone health, in line with other health-related outcomes. It is worth noting that, although CPAP remains the cornerstone of SDB management, other pharmacological, surgical, and behavioral interventions are available and may be recommended depending on patient characteristics. To this end, novel incretin medications have been gaining enormous popularity and tirzepatide, a combined glucagon-like peptide-1 and glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide receptor agonist, has been recently approved for obesity-related sleep apnea. While tirzepatide may convey superior health benefits relative to CPAP and the presence of relative receptors in bone, its impact on bone metabolism in patients with SDB, or other populations, is largely unknown [56].

Strengths of the current study include the use of gold-standard, in-laboratory PSG to assess sleep and the inclusion of only untreated SDB patients. Aside from SDB, our population was relatively healthy, without major comorbidities confounding the results. In our analysis, we controlled for several covariates that may affect the association between SDB and BMD measures, including physical activity, smoking history, and use of sleep medications. Sensitivity analyses also ruled out influences from antihypertensive therapy and use of calcium/vitamin D supplements.

A few limitations should be acknowledged. We did not measure circulating bone turnover markers or vitamin D levels, and we were unable to further characterize the relationship between SDB severity and bone health due to the relatively small number of patients in each SDB severity category. Insufficient statistical power may have precluded identification of significant effect modifiers of the observed associations. Because we did not scan isolated skeletal subregions typically imaged to determine osteoporosis risk (e.g., femoral neck, lumbar spine), our results may not be directly comparable to prior data. However, whole-body scans provide information on total and regional body composition and are often used as general health screening tools. In addition, BMD measures from total body DEXA have been shown to be moderately to strongly correlated with hip and spine BMD from dedicated scans [57]. Our observations should also be interpreted in light of limitations inherent to the DXA technique, including influences of bone size and soft tissue on BMD estimates [58, 59]. Although these dependencies may affect BMD assessment in obese patients and partially explain the positive correlation between BMI and BMD, total body BMD measurements appear to be minimally affected, with lower precision errors than regional sites [58, 59]. As our findings have been obtained from a cross-sectional study, further longitudinal examinations corroborating these findings and elucidating underlying mechanisms are warranted. Lastly, as our assessment of physical activity was based on a single self-reported question, our approach may not have comprehensively captured the influence of exercise on bone metabolism. Validated instruments such as the International Physical Activity Questionnaire should be used in future studies [60]. In conclusion, this study shows that elevated BMI masks the negative association between SDB and BMD, suggesting that SDB may be an independent though modest risk factor for poor skeletal health and that early interventions to preserve bone health in SDB patients should be encouraged.
