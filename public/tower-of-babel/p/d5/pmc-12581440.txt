Beyond the bedside: unravelling the impact of non-nursing tasks on clinical nurses and healthcare delivery
Authors: Ahmad Rajeh Saifan, Ayman Abed Aldarawsheh, Nabeel Al-Yateem, Hanan F Alharbi, Fatma Refaat Ahmed, Zyad T Saleh, Safa’a Al-Ryahna, Khulud Ahmad Rezq, Dana Anwer Abujaber
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNurses are essential to high-quality healthcare delivery, yet their ability to provide patient-centered care is increasingly compromised by the burden of non-nursing tasks (NNTs)—duties that fall outside the professional scope of nursing, such as administrative work, patient transport, and housekeeping. This study explores how NNTs affect the roles and experiences of Jordanian nurses.AimTo investigate Jordanian nurses’ perceptions and interpretations of NNTs, and how these tasks influence their professional practice and the broader healthcare delivery system.MethodsA qualitative exploratory design was employed, using focus group interviews with 38 experienced nurses from three major hospitals in Jordan. Thematic analysis was conducted to identify patterns in how nurses perceive and experience NNTs in their clinical roles.ResultsTwo main themes emerged: (1) the paradoxical nature of NNTs, which simultaneously challenge core nursing identity while fostering adaptability, and (2) the broad spectrum of non-nursing tasks performed by nurses, including administrative duties, support services, and tasks typically assigned to other healthcare professionals. These roles significantly influenced job satisfaction and perceived care quality.ConclusionsNNTs have a complex and often detrimental impact on nursing practice in Jordan. Addressing them through clear job role definitions, adequate staffing, and support role reinforcement is essential. These findings underscore the need for policy reforms that realign nursing responsibilities with professional standards to enhance both nurse well-being and patient care quality.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Non-nursing tasks, Clinical nurses, Healthcare delivery, Jordan, Professional practice, Nursing workload

Introduction

Nursing in the 21st century plays a critical role in ensuring coherent and continuous healthcare for patients [1]. Nurses are frontline professionals, dedicated to addressing individuals’ needs and maintaining public health through accurate diagnoses and health education [2, 3]. However, the nursing profession increasingly faces challenges that detract from these responsibilities—chief among them is the burden of non-nursing tasks (NNTs) [1, 3, 4].

Non-Nursing Tasks (NNTs) are defined as duties carried out by nurses that fall outside their professional scope and do not require clinical nursing knowledge or judgment. These include administrative work, housekeeping, patient transport, and clerical roles typically assigned to ancillary staff [5, 6]. The significance of NNTs lies in their misalignment with nurses’ core responsibilities of direct patient care and clinical decision-making. While job descriptions and institutional policies vary, NNTs are distinguished by their peripheral nature to professional nursing competencies. In this study, we adopt an operational definition reflecting both literature and context: NNTs are tasks that do not require nursing-specific judgment and are not essential to the nursing process, as understood by practicing nurses in Jordan. Task ambiguity in Jordan’s healthcare system—exacerbated by staff shortages, accreditation pressures, and informal delegation—necessitates conceptual clarity to guide policy and workflow reforms.

While the term “Non-Nursing Tasks” is widely used, it is not without criticism. Recent literature, including Grosso et al. (2019), has debated its conceptual adequacy and proposed alternatives such as “out-of-role tasks” or “ancillary duties” to better reflect the blurred boundaries and evolving roles in healthcare settings. In this study, we use “NNTs” as a functional term that aligns with both participant language and regional literature, while acknowledging this ongoing terminological evolution in the field.

International studies estimate that 35% to 62% of nurses’ work shifts are consumed by NNTs [7, 8]. These tasks have proliferated due to cost-cutting, staffing reductions, and diminished support roles [9, 10]. This trend increases workload and detracts from the quality of nursing care. Alarmingly, NNTs have become normalized in some training environments, influencing student nurses’ perceptions of the profession [11].

Ambiguity in defining what constitutes an NNT versus a legitimate nursing task is a key issue globally. Scholars emphasize that NNTs are often ancillary to patient care or fall below a nurse’s professional practice scope, thereby creating role confusion and reducing job satisfaction [5, 6, 12, 13]. Studies from Italy, South Africa, and elsewhere highlight the widespread presence of these tasks and their impact on care quality and professional identity [14].

Context matters. In Jordan, for instance, tasks such as IV cannulation are considered part of nursing practice, whereas they may not be in countries like the USA or UK [15, 16]. A cross-sectional study by Ayasreh et al. (2022) found that many nursing students in Jordan observed practicing nurses routinely performing NNTs, which shaped their views on the profession [17].

Despite the growing global literature, there remains a dearth of context-specific, qualitative research in developing countries. Most existing studies rely on Western-developed tools and quantitative methods that may not capture the cultural and structural nuances of local healthcare systems [14, 18]. Understanding NNTs in Jordan therefore requires qualitative exploration of how nurses interpret and experience these tasks within their professional roles.

This study addresses that gap by exploring the perceptions and interpretations of NNTs among Jordanian nurses. Specifically, it investigates how nurses describe the NNTs they encounter, how they interpret these tasks in relation to their core responsibilities, and what implications this has for patient care and healthcare delivery. The aim is to generate insight that informs both local practice and broader policy discussions on role clarity and care quality.

Methodology

Design and method

This study employed a qualitative descriptive approach grounded in phenomenology to explore nurses’ lived experiences with non-nursing tasks (NNTs) in Jordanian hospitals. Phenomenology was selected as the guiding methodology because the aim was to uncover and understand the subjective, first-person meanings nurses attach to performing tasks outside their professional scope. This approach is particularly suited for exploring complex, context-specific phenomena rooted in individual perception and experience. The focus on nurses’ personal interpretations aligns with phenomenological emphasis on meaning-making, rather than theory generation or cultural immersion, which would be more consistent with grounded theory or ethnography respectively (Creswell & Poth, 2018; Polit & Beck, 2021).

Although phenomenological research commonly employs individual interviews, this study utilized focus group interviews as a valid adaptation within the phenomenological framework. Focus groups were chosen to stimulate shared reflection and elicit rich, comparative insights into nurses’ lived experiences with non-nursing tasks. The interactive setting allowed participants to build upon one another’s perspectives, uncovering layered meanings and common experiences that may not emerge in one-on-one interviews. This approach aligns with phenomenological goals when managed carefully and analyzed with attention to individual meaning within the group dynamic (Bradbury-Jones et al., 2009; Gill, 2020).

Settings

The healthcare system in Jordan consists of four main sectors: governmental, private, university, and military [19]. The current study was conducted in three large hospitals representing three different sectors: governmental, university, and private. These hospitals were selected for several reasons. They are located in Amman, the capital of Jordan, where approximately 40% of the Jordanian population resides, and where the majority of healthcare services are provided. Nurses working in these hospitals come from diverse regions of Jordan, which aligns with the study’s objectives of gathering representative views on the subject of investigation. Furthermore, these hospitals feature multidisciplinary departments and a substantial number of nursing staff, facilitating the data collection process.

Study participants

In qualitative research, a fundamental principle involves selecting participants with good knowledge and experience related to the research topic [20]. To ensure this, purposive sampling was employed. Participants needed to have the capacity to offer rich and in-depth insights into the phenomenon being investigated. Eligibility criteria required that participants (a) had worked as registered nurses for at least two years, including a minimum of three months of recent practice preceding the study; this ensured that their insights and experiences were both adequate and current, (b) worked in clinical departments such as medical-surgical and intensive care units, and (c) were directly involved in providing patient care. Nurses who did not provide direct patient care and those in managerial positions were excluded. Managerial or administrative nurses were not included because the study’s aim was to capture the lived experiences of frontline nurses who directly balance patient care with non-nursing tasks. Managers’ perspectives, while valuable, may be influenced by organizational pressures or administrative compromises related to staffing and task allocation, and therefore would not fully reflect the experiential dimension that this study sought to explore.

To confirm eligibility, unit managers assisted in identifying nurses who met the inclusion criteria, and during recruitment, participants were asked to report their years of experience, current role, and unit assignment. This process provided assurance that the sample consisted of knowledgeable and experienced nurses who had direct exposure to non-nursing tasks and could provide rich, experience-based accounts.

Guest et al. (2017) state that the ideal focus group size ranges from 4 to 10 participants [21]. This range ensures that each participant has sufficient opportunity to contribute to the discussion and provide detailed responses while also being manageable for the moderator [22]. A larger group might limit the depth of responses, as participants may feel pressured to share speaking time with others [21]. In the current study, a total of six focus group interviews were conducted, two of which contained seven participants and the remaining six for each. The research sample included 38 experienced and dedicated nurses from three major Jordanian hospitals. The selection of participants was conducted meticulously to ensure a diverse and comprehensive representation of the nursing workforce in Jordan, thus encompassing various perspectives and experiences related to NNTs.

Data collection method

Each focus group session was carefully planned to create a comfortable and respectful environment. They were held in quiet, private spaces conducive to open discussion. The sessions were audio-recorded to capture the entirety of the conversations accurately. In addition to recording, the researcher took comprehensive notes to capture non-verbal cues and group dynamics, which are integral to understanding the context and nuances of the discussions.

Taking notes alongside audio recording served multiple purposes: (1) to capture non-verbal cues, group dynamics, and interactions that are central to focus group methodology but cannot be fully conveyed through audio recordings alone, and (2) to provide backup observations in case of technical recording issues. Because one of the main strengths of group interviews lies in the dynamics of communication and interaction among participants, note-taking was important to ensure these features were adequately documented. In line with recommended practices for focus groups, note-taking and moderation were handled separately: one researcher moderated the session and paid full attention to guiding the discussion, while the other discreetly took notes on group dynamics and non-verbal behaviors. To minimize distraction, the note-taking was brief, unobtrusive, and explained to participants at the start of each session so they understood its purpose. Participants were reassured that the audio recordings—not the notes—would serve as the primary data source. This transparency and emphasis on confidentiality helped ensure that note-taking did not interfere with the discussion or participants’ comfort.

To reduce common limitations of focus groups, the moderator actively encouraged participation from all members by inviting quieter participants to share their views and by gently limiting the dominance of stronger voices. At the beginning of each session, participants were reminded that there were no right or wrong answers and that all viewpoints were equally valuable. To minimize social desirability bias, the moderator emphasized confidentiality and stressed the importance of sharing personal experiences rather than expected responses. Non-verbal cues were observed closely, and when hesitancy was noted, participants were prompted to elaborate in a supportive way. These strategies helped create a balanced discussion environment where multiple perspectives could be voiced honestly and respectfully.

The focus groups typically lasted between one to two hours, depending on the depth of the discussion. Participants were encouraged to openly share their experiences and views on non-nursing tasks in nursing practice. The interactive nature of focus groups not only provided in-depth qualitative data but also allowed for the exploration of consensus and divergence among participants’ views and experiences.

The study employed an interview guide, outlined in Table 1, to help explore the experiences and perspectives of nurses regarding NNTs. It initiated with participants introducing themselves, focusing on their professional background, including certifications, qualifications, and experiences. The guide then progressed through a series of questions that were designed to uncover the participants’ definitions and personal experiences with NNTs and identify common NNTs within their work environments. Although an operational definition of NNTs was introduced at the start of each session—based on the literature—to provide participants with a shared baseline understanding, the first interview question deliberately asked them to describe their own understanding. This was done to explore how participants personally interpreted the concept, whether they accepted, rejected, or modified the provided definition, and to capture any additional meanings arising from practice. Participants were encouraged to reflect on and validate this definition based on their professional experience, while also expressing their own interpretations and perspectives. This openness allowed for a range of views to emerge, and this divergence was not suppressed but treated as an important aspect of the phenomenon under investigation. Data analysis

All audio recordings were transcribed verbatim in the original Arabic language by the primary author, rather than by a research assistant, to preserve fidelity and authenticity of participants’ expressions and to deepen the researcher’s immersion in the data. Self-transcription was intentionally employed to foster deep engagement with the data and to support the interpretive nature of phenomenological inquiry. Each interview was transcribed immediately after completion and systematically stored for analysis. Listening to the audio recordings and repeatedly reviewing the transcripts enhanced the researcher’s immersion in the lived experiences shared by the participants.

Data collection and analysis were conducted concurrently, in accordance with the guidance of Moser and Korstjens (2018), allowing early insights to inform subsequent data collection. The study followed the six-phase framework for thematic analysis proposed by Braun and Clarke (2019), which is compatible with descriptive phenomenological research when used to capture the essence of participants’ experiences. The first phase involved thorough familiarization with the data through active listening and multiple readings of the transcripts. In the second phase, initial codes were generated line-by-line, capturing meaning units reflective of how participants interpreted non-nursing tasks in their practice. These codes were then grouped and refined into broader categories during the third and fourth phases, where emerging patterns were organized into potential themes and reviewed for coherence. The fifth phase involved defining and naming themes in alignment with participants’ voices and the study’s phenomenological focus. The final phase synthesized these findings into a coherent narrative that illustrates the shared meanings and diverse perceptions surrounding NNTs in the Jordanian nursing context.

Throughout the analysis, attention was given to preserving the richness and depth of the participants’ accounts, with themes grounded in their own words. Although group interactions were part of the focus group format, the analysis emphasized individual experiential meaning, consistent with phenomenological intent.

To enhance the credibility of the analysis, initial coding was conducted by the primary author, followed by regular discussions with co-authors to review and refine the emerging themes. These collaborative discussions served as a consensus-building process, ensuring that the thematic structure reflected shared interpretations and minimized individual researcher bias. Although formal member checking was not carried out after data collection, the focus group format allowed participants to affirm, clarify, or challenge shared ideas during discussions, offering a form of internal validation. Also, The research team acknowledged the potential influence of their professional backgrounds and prior assumptions about NNTs. To enhance reflexivity, the lead researcher maintained a reflective journal during the transcription and coding process, noting personal reactions and emerging interpretations. Regular team discussions also served as a platform to critically examine interpretations and challenge potential bias, helping to ensure that the themes remained grounded in participants’ voices rather than the researchers’ expectations.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Applied Science Private University (Ref# 2022-2023-3-4). Participation in this study was voluntary and carried no risk participants. Each potential participant received an invitation letter from the primary author. A detailed explanation of the study objectives and their nature was provided to each nurse in the unit to the greatest extent possible. Participants were then given a consent form to sign at their convenience. The nurses retained the right to participate or withdraw from the study at any stage of the research process. The interviews were conducted in a location deemed safe and public, chosen for its adherence to general safety principles, and privacy. All interviewees were assured that their accounts would be kept confidential. Their discussions were not to be shared with anyone except the authors of the research, and their real names were substituted with pseudonyms for privacy protection.

Findings

Participants

Thirty-eight experienced nurses from three prominent Jordanian hospitals took part in this study. Participants included an equal number of male and female nurses, with 19 participants of each gender. The participants ranged from 24 to 48 years old, such variation in age allowed for insights into how different age groups perceive and cope with NNTs within their nursing practice. The study included nurses with varying years of experience, from 1 to 24 years. The departments represented in the study included the Intensive Care Units (ICU), the General Medical and Surgical department, the Oncology Department, and the Pediatric Department.

Emerging themes

The findings from this qualitative exploration into the lived experiences of nurses in Jordanian hospitals reveal a complex mosaic of challenges and adaptations within the nursing profession. At the heart of our investigation are two pivotal themes that collectively reveal the evolving landscape of nursing work and its implications for patient care, professional identity, and healthcare delivery systems. The first theme, “Navigating the Dual Realms of Care: The Paradox of NNTs in Jordanian Nursing Practice,” probes into the multifaceted nature of NNTs and their pervasive presence in the daily routines of nurses. Following this, the second theme, “Beyond the Bedside: The Multifaceted Realm of Nursing Practice,” further unpacks the specifics of NNTs, categorizing them into distinct subthemes such as clerk and management tasks, transporting tasks, physician tasks, other health professionals’ tasks, and communication and support roles.

Theme 1: Navigating the dual realms of care: the paradox of NNTs in Jordanian nursing practice

This theme explores the complex landscape of NNTs and their profound implications on nursing identity, patient care quality, and healthcare delivery in Jordan. It probes into the dualities that nurses navigate between their prescribed roles and the exigencies of the healthcare environment that demand versatility beyond traditional nursing boundaries.

The testimony of nurses reveals a pervasive engagement with NNTs, sometimes eclipsing their core duties. For example, one participant’s expression of spending “most of my time doing jobs outside of my job description” (Nurse 38, female, 12 years’ experience, medical-surgical unit, public hospital) not only highlights the prevalence of NNTs but also suggests an erosion of the core nursing identity, prompting a reevaluation of what constitutes nursing work within these settings.

Another nurse remarks “We spend around 50% of our time doing other tasks such as clerk or porter tasks” (Nurse 17, male, 6 years’ experience, ICU, university hospital) points to a systemic issue within the healthcare infrastructure—a blurring of roles that may lead to inefficiencies and dilute the specialized care nurses are trained to provide. This phenomenon underscores a critical tension between the necessity of multifaceted roles in response to resource constraints and the imperative to maintain the integrity of nursing as a profession centered on patient care.

The divergent perceptions of NNTs among nurses underscore the complexity of integrating such tasks into nursing practice. Some participants’ acceptance of NNTs as a “traditional aspect of our job in Jordan” (Nurse 17, male, 6 years’ experience, ICU, university hospital) contrasts sharply with other nurses overwhelmed response, indicating a spectrum of adaptability and resilience among nursing professionals. One participant reported:

A bunch of us are honestly feeling the pinch because of these extra tasks that aren’t nursing work. It’s pretty tough when you’ve got your usual nursing stuff to do, and then there’s this heap of other duties dumped on you. Really makes you wonder how we’re supposed to manage all that without burning out. (Nurse 4, female, 8 years’ experience, pediatric unit, private hospital)

These contrasting perspectives highlight the subjective nature of NNTs’ impact, influenced by individual experiences, the hospital environment, and the evolving context of healthcare demands.

Some participants have embraced a pragmatic approach to NNTs out of necessity, different from others’ feeling of being overburdened, which captures the nuanced realities of nursing work. This dichotomy reflects broader themes of professional identity, job satisfaction, and the sustainability of nursing practice in environments that demand constant adaptability.

The discussion around the definition and scope of NNTs, as expressed by some participants, reveals a profound ambiguity and discontent surrounding the expectations placed on nurses. This ambiguity challenges the profession to delineate more clearly the boundaries of nursing work, ensuring that nurses can fulfill their primary commitment to patient care without being overextended by peripheral tasks. One participant reported:

Some of us are scratching our heads over what counts as our job and what doesn’t. There’s a lot of back and forth about these extra tasks we’re getting, and honestly, it’s kind of a mess. It feels like we’re being pulled in a million directions, trying to take care of our patients but also stuck doing all these other things that don’t seem like they should be our problem. We need to get a clear line drawn so we can focus on what we’re here for – taking care of people, without being stretched too thin over stuff that’s not really nursing work. (Nurse 5, male, 15 years’ experience, medical-surgical unit, public hospital)

Participants’ comments about performing tasks typically assigned to unlicensed staff highlight a critical issue of workforce planning and resource allocation in healthcare. This situation not only exacerbates the workload on nurses but also raises concerns about the quality of care and the efficient use of nursing skills.

Furthermore, the reflections of some nurses on the potential benefits of NNTs introduce an important counterpoint to the discussion. These insights suggest that, under certain conditions, NNTs can offer opportunities for professional growth, cross-disciplinary collaboration, and enhanced patient care. This perspective invites a nuanced consideration of how NNTs might be integrated into nursing practice in a way that respects nurses’ expertise while fostering a holistic approach to healthcare. Some participants reported:

You know, despite all the grumbling about these extra tasks we’re doing that aren’t strictly nursing, some of us are seeing a good thing here. It’s like, these tasks could actually be a good thing. They’re giving us chances to grow, work closer with colleagues from other departments, and even help our patients in ways we hadn’t thought about before. Makes you think, maybe there’s a way to just do these tasks into our day-to-day without being so nervous about it, kind of mixing our know-how with a broader view of caring for everyone. (Nurse 8, female, 10 years’ experience, oncology unit, university hospital)

The theme culminates in the collective concern for balancing NNTs with essential patient care, as voiced by some participants. This concern encapsulates the core challenge facing the nursing profession in Jordan and, by extension, globally: how to reconcile the demands of a complex healthcare system with the fundamental mission of nursing to provide compassionate, patient-centered care. One participant said:

A lot of us are talking about this issue of doing these extra bits, the NNTs and making sure our patients get the care they need. It’s pretty much the big puzzle we’re all trying to solve, not just here in Jordan but everywhere. How do we keep up with all the stuff the healthcare system throws at us while staying true to what nursing’s really about? (Nurse 12, male, 7 years’ experience, ICU, public hospital)

In conclusion, the detailed exploration of NNTs through the voices of Jordanian nurses reveals a multifaceted issue at the heart of nursing practice. It calls for strategic interventions to clarify the role of nurses, optimize the healthcare workforce, and ensure that the primary focus on patient care remains uncompromised. This theme, therefore, not only sheds light on the current state of nursing in Jordan but also contributes to the broader discourse on the future of nursing practice in an ever-evolving healthcare landscape.

Theme 2: Beyond the bedside: the multifaceted realm of nursing practice

This thematic exploration probes into the broad spectrum of NNTs that nurses in various healthcare settings find themselves undertaking. It reveals the adaptability of nurses as they navigate roles that extend beyond traditional patient care, emphasizing the critical yet often overlooked contributions they make to healthcare delivery.

Clerk and management tasks

Participants explained that “clerk’s duties” referred to a variety of administrative responsibilities that should ordinarily be handled by ward clerks or administrative staff. These included preparing and updating patient files, ensuring admission and discharge paperwork was completed, arranging consents for procedures, entering data into hospital information systems, answering phone calls, and directing patients or families to relevant services. Several nurses emphasized that these tasks, while important for hospital operations, diverted significant time away from direct patient care.

We are responsible for managing everything for patients. This includes managing their admission and discharge. We do the clerk’s duties. (Nurse 29, female, 14 years’ experience, medical-surgical unit, university hospital).

Some of the interviewees described them as references to patients and relatives regarding several administrative, managerial, and clerk concerns. All these NNTs were viewed by several nurses as distractors from doing their job perfectly.

I know the names and services of many insurance companies. Patients and relatives keep asking us about some procedures and investigations, and whether they are covered by insurance companies. (Nurse 33, male, 9 years’ experience, pediatric unit, private hospital).

Six nurses raised concerns regarding accreditation. Although these nurses respected the importance of accreditation and quality improvement, they talked about the extra burden added to their tasks because of these accreditations.

The hospitals should assign specific staff to help with accreditation. I work 48 h every week. Because of quality training and accreditation, I sometimes work more than 60 h a week. (Nurse 34, female, 18 years’ experience, medical-surgical unit, public hospital)

Transportation tasks

Participants also described transporting tasks, which refers to the physical logistics of patient care, including the transportation of patients and samples, as a significant component of the nurses’ workload. The lack of support from transporters or other staff was mentioned by many participants: “I do all this alone and on my own time,” (Nurse 33, male, 9 years’ experience, pediatric unit, private hospital), reflecting the inefficiencies and gaps within the healthcare system that nurses bridge through their efforts. Although in many healthcare systems nurses may be expected to accompany patients during certain transfers (e.g., to procedures), participants emphasized that they considered routine transport duties—such as moving patients between wards or carrying samples to laboratories—as non-nursing work. In their hospitals, these responsibilities were typically assigned to porters or support staff. When such staff were unavailable, nurses absorbed these tasks in addition to their core responsibilities, which led them to perceive them as outside their professional scope. This necessity for nurses to step into roles that extend beyond their traditional scope highlights a critical area for operational improvement within healthcare institutions.

Carrying out many of the physicians’ tasks

Furthermore, participants reported carrying out many of the physician’s tasks, with several indicating that the blurring of boundaries between nursing and physician roles is particularly pronounced in government hospitals. One participant reported:

A bunch of us have noticed that we’re ending up doing a lot of the doctors’ work, especially in the government hospitals. It’s like the lines between what nurses and doctors are supposed to do are getting fuzzy. Feels kind of crazy sometimes, trying to juggle our stuff plus some of theirs. (Nurse 19, female, 11 years’ experience, ICU, public hospital).

Participants’ narratives regarding job descriptions point to a systemic issue of role overlap that undermines the clarity of professional responsibilities. The practicality of nurses taking on tasks traditionally reserved for physicians underscores a pragmatic approach to patient care but also signals a need for clearer delineation of duties and better inter-professional collaboration. One participant reported:

From what everyone’s saying, it’s pretty clear there’s a mix-up with who’s supposed to do what. Nurses are stepping into doctors’ shoes more than we probably should, and it’s all because we’re trying to keep things moving for our patients. It’s practical, sure, but it’s also a bit of a mess. We definitely need to sort out these roles better and get everyone working together smoother. (Nurse 34, female, 18 years’ experience, medical-surgical unit, public hospital).

Some participants also expressed frustration with inter-professional communication, particularly difficulties in contacting physicians or delays in their responsiveness. While these concerns reflect important teamwork and workflow challenges, they were not classified in our analysis as non-nursing tasks. Instead, they were treated as contextual factors that exacerbate nurses’ workload and contribute to perceptions of role strain.

Tasks of other health professionals such as pharmacists, physiotherapists, and dietitians

Participants also reported carrying out other health professionals’ tasks, with the expansion of nursing roles to include activities typically performed by pharmacists, physiotherapists, and dietitians. This reveals a versatile yet often overextended nursing workforce.

One nurse expressed frustration, saying:

I have to do some of the pharmacist’s tasks. Some medications should be prepared by the pharmacist such as chemotherapy. Medication should be collected by the porters. The pharmacists should be available to answer some questions about some medications by patients. Imagine that nurses should do all these tasks. (Nurse 10, male, 7 years’ experience, oncology unit, university hospital).

Nurse 10’s comment reflected not only the burden of medication preparation but also the perception that pharmacists should be more available for patient counseling. While pharmacology and patient education about medications are part of nursing curricula, participants explained that the complexity of certain treatments (e.g., chemotherapy) and the absence of pharmacists at the bedside shaped their perception that in-depth counseling was being shifted inappropriately to nurses.

A similar perception was seen with nutritional care. Although nutrition assessment and dietary advice are fundamental components of nursing care, some participants described them as non-nursing tasks, noting that dietitians were primarily responsible for counseling patients. For example:

Nurses follow everything related to the patient’s diet. I mean everything from checking the files, asking patients about what they want to eat, and screening the food trays before giving them to patients. (Nurse 32, female, 16 years’ experience, pediatric unit, university hospital).

These comments do not suggest a lack of knowledge, but rather illustrate how organizational norms, workload pressures, and the presence of other professionals influence nurses’ perceptions of their role boundaries. In practice, this sometimes led them to categorize tasks that are part of holistic nursing care—such as medication education and nutrition—as ancillary or non-nursing duties when these were seen as belonging to another profession.

Moreover, nurses cited additional examples, such as controlling visitors, repairing devices, managing medical equipment, sanitizing and disinfecting devices, and organizing the emergency cases cart.

Communication and supporting patients and family members

Communication and Supporting Patients and Family Members were also other major tasks that nurses do alone frequently. The relational aspect of nursing, characterized by significant interaction with patients and their families, places nurses in a unique position to offer emotional and informational support. However, as one nurse articulated, “The problem is we are not allowed to answer questions about the patient’s condition or treatment plans,” (Nurse 21, male, 5 years’ experience, medical-surgical unit, private hospital) there exists a tension between the nurses’ accessible position and the limitations of their role. This account contrasted with other participants who described providing information about diet and medications, suggesting that nurses’ communication roles may be shaped by unit culture, institutional policy, or personal interpretation of professional boundaries. This delicate balance underscores the emotional labor inherent in nursing and the need for supportive structures to enable nurses to navigate these complexities effectively.

In integrating these quotations and observations, “Beyond the Bedside: The Multifaceted Realm of Nursing Practice” seeks to illuminate the diverse and complex roles that nurses fulfill within the healthcare ecosystem. It underscores the imperative for a reevaluation of healthcare practices and policies to ensure that nurses can focus on their primary role in patient care, supported by an infrastructure that recognizes and values their multifaceted contributions to health and healing.

Discussion

Our qualitative investigation, marking a pioneering endeavor in Jordan to explore nurses’ perceptions of NNTs, underscores a significant, yet complex, challenge faced by the nursing profession. Aligning with the observations in the previous study [6] and supported by literature from various global contexts [23], our findings reveal a striking commonality in the nature and prevalence of NNTs across different healthcare settings, despite geographical and systemic variations [24, 25].

The engagement of Jordanian nurses in a wide array of NNTs, from clerical duties to patient transport, mirrors the global landscape where such tasks have become increasingly integrated into the nursing role, contributing to an erosion of core nursing responsibilities [25, 26]. This encroachment not only diverts nurses from patient-centered care but also instigates a sense of role ambiguity, challenging the professional identity and satisfaction of nurses, akin to findings from studies in Italy [6], South Africa [18], and South Korea [27].

Role ambiguity emerged as one of the most salient themes in our study. Nurses reported uncertainty over whether tasks such as patient transport, medication counseling, and nutritional care should be considered part of their role. For instance, Nurse 33 described routine patient transport as an NNT because in their hospital these duties were usually assigned to porters, whereas in other contexts transfers are considered standard nursing responsibilities. Similarly, Nurse 10 perceived in-depth medication counseling as a pharmacist’s duty and nutritional guidance as the dietitian’s domain, despite both pharmacology and nutrition being part of nursing curricula and care plans. These views reflected not a lack of knowledge, but organizational realities: the absence of pharmacists or dietitians in daily practice, workload pressures that deprioritized holistic care, and inter-professional norms that shaped perceptions of role boundaries. Nurse 34’s frustration about difficulties in contacting physicians also highlights this complexity. While such concerns relate more to inter-professional collaboration than to NNTs per se, they illustrate how systemic factors influence how nurses experience and interpret their responsibilities. A further example of this ambiguity was the contradiction between Nurse 21, who reported being restricted from discussing treatment plans, and other participants who described providing dietary and medication information. This divergence likely reflects unit-level culture or institutional policy, underscoring how organizational context shapes nurses’ communication roles. Clarifying these expectations through administrative perspectives would be an important direction for future research. Finally, when participants such as Nurse 38 spoke of tasks outside their “job description,” they referred to perceived rather than formal HR-defined roles. Within a phenomenological framework, these perceptions are valid and meaningful, since they capture the lived experience of role erosion. Future research could build on this by triangulating such perceptions with formal job descriptions to further validate and contextualize role boundaries.

The study also revealed variability in how participants defined and interpreted NNTs. Some nurses viewed these tasks as an accepted part of their job, while others saw them as burdensome and misaligned with their professional role. Rather than undermining the study’s coherence, this diversity was treated as a meaningful finding. It reflects the real-world ambiguity surrounding task boundaries in clinical environments, shaped by institutional culture, staffing norms, and individual adaptation. At the same time, it is important to clarify that NNTs should be defined as tasks outside the nursing scope, whereas systemic issues such as staff shortages, burnout, and inter-professional tensions are contextual drivers or consequences rather than NNTs themselves. This distinction strengthens conceptual clarity and underscores the need for clearer role delineation in practice.

While many participants described NNTs as a source of professional frustration, a few viewed them more positively. These nurses saw certain tasks as opportunities for skill development, interprofessional collaboration, and a broader understanding of healthcare processes. For instance, Nurse 8 described these tasks as “giving us chances to grow,” emphasizing the potential for professional development when NNTs are approached with institutional support and recognition. This duality in perception suggests that not all NNTs are inherently detrimental; under the right conditions, they may offer learning experiences that enhance nurses’ adaptability and teamwork.

Echoing Yen et al. (2018), our study further elucidates that many NNTs, such as cleaning and administrative tasks, do not necessitate nursing judgment or knowledge, indicating a potential for delegation or streamlining [13]. This observation suggests a universal potential to reassess the allocation of tasks within healthcare settings to enhance nursing efficiency and focus on patient care.

The normalization of NNTs, especially among nursing students, as reported by Ayasreh et al. (2022), signals a pressing need for curriculum reforms and educational interventions that delineate the scope of nursing practice [17]. Educating future nurses on the distinction between nursing and non-nursing activities, alongside training in advocacy and negotiation skills, could empower them to navigate and influence their professional roles more effectively.

Our findings, alongside corroborative international evidence, highlight the tangible impact of NNTs on both patient care and nurse well-being. The association of NNTs with missed nursing care [24], burnout [5], and job dissatisfaction [18] underscores the urgency of addressing this pervasive issue. Strategies to mitigate the impact of NNTs, including the redesign of healthcare workflows and the reinforcement of support roles, are essential for safeguarding the quality of patient care and enhancing nurses’ professional fulfillment. These recommendations directly reflect participant insights—for example, the repeated calls for task delegation align with themes expressed by Nurse 12, who highlighted the strain of balancing essential care with non-core responsibilities.

In light of the consistent global prevalence of NNTs, our study advocates for a re-evaluation of healthcare practices and policies that recognize and respect the unique contributions of nurses. By fostering a more supportive and efficient healthcare environment, we can ensure that nurses in Jordan, and globally, are empowered to dedicate their expertise to what they do best: providing high-quality, patient-centered care.

In conclusion, this study contributes to the growing body of literature on NNTs by providing a detailed and in-depth understanding of how these tasks are perceived and managed by nurses in a specific developing country context. Our findings not only reinforce the global nature of the challenge posed by NNTs but also highlight the need for targeted solutions that address the unique cultural and systemic complexities of each healthcare setting. By doing so, we can better support nurses in fulfilling their essential role in healthcare delivery, ultimately enhancing patient outcomes and nursing professional satisfaction. While the findings are specific to Jordan’s healthcare system, the detailed description of participants’ roles and institutional settings offers contextual insight that may be applicable to similar middle-income healthcare systems facing staff shortages and role ambiguity. Readers should assess relevance based on institutional, cultural, and policy parallels.

Conclusions and implications for practice

This study highlights the significant impact of NNTs on Jordanian nurses, emphasizing the need for strategies in practice, management, and education to mitigate their burden. Streamlining nursing roles by delegating NNTs to support staff and leveraging technology can allow nurses to focus more on patient care. Clear job descriptions and adequate staffing are crucial, alongside fostering a culture of collaboration to improve task allocation, job satisfaction, and retention. Educationally, revising curricula to include critical thinking and advocacy prepares students to navigate NNTs effectively. Addressing these challenges is essential for enhancing patient care and nursing satisfaction, calling for unified efforts from all healthcare stakeholders to optimize nursing practice and outcomes. These recommendations are grounded in the lived experiences of participants, as illustrated in themes that emphasized both the burden and, for some, the developmental value of NNTs.

By adopting a refined and contextually grounded definition of NNTs, this study contributes to clarifying a concept often conflated with general task delegation or informal role extension. Establishing this conceptual boundary enhances the interpretability of our findings and supports the development of targeted interventions aimed at realigning nursing roles with their professional scope. In particular, role ambiguity—especially regarding boundaries between nurses, physicians, pharmacists, and dietitians—needs to be addressed through clearer institutional policies, workforce planning, and professional education. Doing so would ensure that nurses’ expertise is directed toward core responsibilities and reduce the perception of essential but overlapping care activities as non-nursing tasks.

While this study offers important insights into how nurses perceive the impact of NNTs on healthcare delivery, it does not directly measure system-level outcomes such as care efficiency or patient satisfaction. As such, the findings should be interpreted as reflective of perceived implications rather than empirically established effects. Future studies may also integrate managerial perspectives to complement staff nurses’ experiences, thereby offering a more comprehensive understanding of how NNTs are defined, delegated, and managed within healthcare organizations.

Study limitations

One limitation of the study is that it relies on nurses’ subjective experiences and perceptions. Although these accounts provide valuable frontline insights, the study does not include direct measures of patient care outcomes or system performance indicators. Additionally, the study was conducted in three major hospitals in Amman, which may not fully represent nursing experiences in other regions or types of healthcare facilities within Jordan.

While focus groups enabled rich, interactive discussion, they may have also encouraged participants to conform to group norms, potentially limiting the expression of dissenting views. To mitigate this, the moderator actively encouraged quieter participants, gently managed dominant voices, emphasized confidentiality, and reminded participants that there were no right or wrong answers. These measures helped reduce the risk of conformity and encouraged balanced participation, although some group influence cannot be entirely ruled out.

Furthermore, although data coding and analysis were discussed among the research team, initial coding was conducted by a single researcher, which may have introduced interpretive bias. Some participant perceptions of NNTs may also have reflected broader issues of role ambiguity—such as boundaries with physicians, pharmacists, and dietitians—rather than strictly non-nursing tasks. Contradictions in accounts, such as one nurse reporting restrictions on discussing treatment plans while others described providing dietary or medication information, further highlight how institutional policies and unit cultures may shape these perceptions. As administrative perspectives were not included, such differences could not be fully explored or verified. While this reflects an important contextual reality, it underscores the need for conceptual clarity when interpreting our findings.

Finally, managerial nurses were excluded from participation to focus on the lived experiences of frontline staff. While this decision strengthened the methodological coherence of the study, it also meant that perspectives influenced by administrative responsibilities, organizational compromises, or institutional pressures were not captured. Future research may therefore integrate both frontline and managerial viewpoints to provide a more holistic understanding of how non-nursing tasks are perceived, allocated, and managed within healthcare organizations.
