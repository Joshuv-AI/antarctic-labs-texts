In vitro evaluation the spectrum of antibacterial activity of botanical extracts and activity enhancement with outer membrane permeabilizers
Authors: Cassandre Gregory, Jeffrey Langland
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnique from Gram-positive bacteria, Gram-negative bacteria have an outer membrane containing lipopolysaccharide. This distinction has important implications for antibiotic therapies where this complex outer cell membrane presents a significant barrier to the entry of drugs, such as antibiotics, into the cell. Many botanical extracts are purported for their antibacterial properties. In this study, several botanical extracts were compared for their antibacterial activity against various Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. Gram-positive bacteria were found to be more sensitive to the antibacterial effects of these botanical extracts, including Larrea tridentata, Eucalyptus glabra, Hypericum perforatum, Coptis chinensis and Hydrastis canadensis. For Gram-negative bacteria, various permeabilizers including EDTA, spermidine and polyethyleneimine, have been identified which facilitate the passage of the antibiotics through the outer membrane. These permeabilizers were tested to improve the efficacy of botanical extracts against Gram-negative bacteria where an increase in antibacterial activity was observed but was dependent on the type of permeabilizer used (EDTA being most effective) and the botanical extract (primarily observed with Larrea tridentata, Eucalyptus glabra, Coptis chinensis, and Salvia officinalis). These results characterize the spectrum of antibacterial activity of several commonly used botanical extracts in the presence and absence of membrane permeabilizers. The research performed supports the use of permeabilizers to potentially improve the antibacterial efficacy of botanical extracts and therapeutics.Keywords: Permeabilizer, Antibiotic, Resistance, Gram-negative, Gram-positive, Botanical

Introduction

Gram-positive bacteria have been shown in many studies to be more sensitive to antibacterial treatments than Gram-negative [1, 2]. This is due to the presence of a thick lipopolysaccharide (LPS) layer that surrounds Gram-negative bacteria that renders them less permeable and more resistant to drugs that are targeting them. These LPS molecules consist of three primary structures: an outer membrane lipid A, an oligosaccharide at the core, and an O antigen. The outer membrane is linked electrostatically with intercalated divalent cations (Ca2+ and Mg2+) that enhance LPS structural integrity, creating a harder to penetrate barrier [1]. This fortified barrier can resist invasive material and other hydrophilic compounds which lessens the potential for bacterial infection unlike their more susceptible Gram-positive counterparts [2].

Conventional antibiotics are the traditional course of treatment for bacterial infections. Resistance of bacteria to antibiotics is built up and evolved over time through different mechanisms such as efflux pumps, neutralization of the antibiotic, and enzymatic modification or genetic mutation of the target site. In addition, for Gram-negative bacteria, the selective permeability of the outer membrane is mediated by porin proteins, which function as narrow channels allowing essential nutrients and smaller hydrophilic molecules to pass while excluding many antibiotics. As an example, in Gram positive organisms, β-lactam antibiotics typically pass through porins, and hydrophobic drugs can diffuse through membranes [3]. However, Gram-negative bacteria, due to their less permeable outer membrane (OM), exhibit high antibiotic resistance to β-lactams and quinolones. This intrinsic resistance to antibiotics by Gram-negative bacteria make them a significant healthcare concern.

Botanical application in medicine has been a tradition in cultures worldwide for thousands of years. Historically, empirical evidence was the primary approachused for botanical medicine both in the treatment and prevention of ailments, such as skin and respiratory infections [4]. Plant medicine is not the typical course in Western practice but there is efficacy and benefit in naturally sourced intervention. Over 8000 phenolic compounds have been found in various plant species including flavonoids, polyphenols, benzophenones, benzoquinones, each of which have been found to demonstrate antimicrobial properties in vitro [4]. Botanicals have be administered in a variety of forms including tinctures, teas, topicals, capsules, tablets, and oils, all with differing potencies and dosages.

To remedy the heightened resistance in Gram-negative bacteria, OM permeabilizers have been a necessary addition to formulations that target these specific pathogens. Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) is a common permeabilizer that acts as an ion chelator in conjunction with antibiotic therapeutics to effectively decrease microbial activity. Its’ mechanism is to separate the divalent cations that contribute to polyanionic interactions that strengthen the thick LPS of Gram-negative bacteria [2]. EDTA can weaken these interactions and allow drugs to infiltrate and induce their bactericidal effects. It is also often used as a treatment for metal poisoning from ions like lead and mercury due to its high metal affinity [5]. Spermidine is a polyamine that aids with increasing the susceptibility of bacteria to antibiotics such as chloramphenicol, trimethoprim, and β-lactams due to the electrostatic interference of cell wall cations [6]. Polyethyleneimine (PEI) is another known chemical permeabilizer that has no direct bactericidal effect but can aid in augmenting antimicrobial activity. PEI is a weak base and a polycationic agent which contains derivatives like poly-L-lysines and protamine that contribute to increasing permeability of the OM [7].

In this study we established the minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) of thirteen purported antimicrobial botanicals against various Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. The MBC of the more botanically-resistant Gram-negative bacteria were then re-evaluated using the botanical extract paired with OM permeabilizers including EDTA, spermidine and polyethyleneimine.

Materials and methods

Chemicals andreagents

Mueller Hinton broth (MHB) and Mueller Hinton agar (MHA) were obtained from Hardy Diagnostics (Santa Monica, CA). Ethanol, spermidine, polyethyleneimine, and EDTA were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich Chemicals.

Bacterial strains: Bacterial cultures of Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC 14775), Streptococcus pyogenes (ATCC 12344), Bacillus cereus (ATCC 10876), Escherichia coli (ATCC 11229), Salmonella enterica (ATCC 49223), Shigella sonnei (ATCC 29930), Klebsiella pneumoniae (ATCC 13883), Proteus vulgaris (ATCC 33420), Enterococcus faecalis (ATCC 19433), Mycobacterium smegmatis (ATCC 14468), Micrococcus luteus (ATCC 10240), and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (ATCC 35554) were obtained from Hardy Diagnostics (Santa Monica, CA).

Botanical extract preparation

Plant material was obtained from the following sources: Eucalyptus globulus leaf (201,455–31), Larrea tridentata leaf (201,255–31), Hypericum perforatum aerial (202,160–31), Hydrastis canadensis root (201,545–31), Arctostaphylos uva ursi leaf (209,605–31), Turnera diffusa leaf (201,380–51), Rosmarinus officinalis leaf (209,935–51) and Salvia officinalis leaf (209,950–31) from Starwest Botanicals (Sacramento, CA); Anemopsis californica leaf (FRX1495) from NutriCargo (Clifton, NJ); Coptis chinensis root (57915FPP) from Mayway Corp (Oakland, CA); and Juniperus communis berry (33,766) from AmeriHerb (Ames, IA); Baptisia tinctoria root (449P1) from Penn Herb (Philadelphia, PA); and Coleus forskohlii root from KAN Phytochemicals (Haryana, India). All plant material was verified by qualified botanical specialists using herbal pharmacopoeia monographs and reference keys and/or with high-performance thin- layer chromatography (HPTLC). A voucher specimen of all plant material was deposited in a repository at the Sonoran University of Health Sciences, Ric Scalzo Institute for Botanical Research. For each plant, 50 g of dried plant material was ground to a fine powder using a high-speed blender followed by resuspension in 50% ethanol at a ratio of 1:10 (dried plant material: extraction solution) and mixed by rotation at 60 rpm at room temperature for 24 h. The botanical debris was removed by centrifugation (3,000xg for 10 min) and the supernatant sterilized by filtration through a 0.2 µm filter. One ml of the final extracts was dried to completion and the concentration of non-volatile constituents determined to range from 62–102 mg/mL.

Bacterial growth studies

For bacterial inhibition studies, minimum bactericidal concentrations (MBC) were determined. Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MIC) were not measured due to turbidity of the broth cultures which occurred following the addition of several of the botanical extracts making reliable determination of MIC values difficult. For MBC determination and growth studies, 18-h cultures (5 × 10⁸ colony-forming units/ml (CFU/mL)) were diluted into media (1:1000 dilution; MHB) followed by the addition of indicated concentrations of each botanical extract or null control. The cultures were incubated at 37 °C with aeration (by continuous rotation) for 24 h. The MBCs were determined by serial dilution onto MHA plates and incubation for 24 h at 37⁰C. The MBC was identified by determining the lowest concentration of the treatment that reduced the viability of the bacteria by ≥ 99.9%. For the botanical extracts, the MBC was listed as the total concentration of non- volatile constituents present in the extract since the active constituent(s) has not been isolated or identified.

Permeabilizer preparation

EDTA, spermidine, polyethyleneimine 100X stocks were prepared in water. Final concentrations of each permeabilizer for treatment were: 0.5 mM EDTA, 10 mM spermidine, and 200 µg/mL polyethyleneimine. Permeabilizer stock solutions were added to the MHB solutions at the same time as the botanical extracts and MBC values determined as described.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using a paired t-test. Samples with statistically significant differences compared to vehicle treated samples are indicated with asterisks (*p = 0.01–0.05; **p = 0.001–0.01; ***p < 0.001).

Results

Comparing the effects of botanical extracts on Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria

To compare the antibacterial efficacy of various botanical extracts, five Gram-negative, six Gram-positive bacteria and Mycobacterium smegmatis were evaluated using thirteen different extracts: Eucalyptus glabra, Larrea tridentata, Hypericum perforatum, Anemopsis californica, Coptis chinensis, Hydrastis canadensis, Juniperus communis, Coleus forskohlii, Rosmarinus officinalis, Baptisia tinctoria, Salvia officinalis, Turnera diffusa, and Arctostaphylos uva ursi. These botanicals were selected based on their historical and anecdotal use for bacterial infections. As shown in Fig. 1, each bacterium was treated with each of the plant extracts and their MBC (µg/mL) was determined. The general trend affirmed that Gram-negative bacteria were more resistant to botanical extracts. For example, in Fig. 1A, when the Eucalyptus glabra extract was cultured with the various bacteria, all six Gram-positive bacteria, including Streptococcus, Staphylococcus, Enterococcus, Bacillus, Mycobacterium, and Micrococcus, were effectively inhibited. The highest MBC was approximately 40 µg/mL with Streptococcus while the lowest MBC was approximately 9 µg/mL with Staphylococcus, Bacillus and Micrococcus. Alternatively, the Gram-negative bacteria demonstrated the opposite effect by being highly resistant to the botanical application. Salmonella, Escherichia, Shigella and Pseudomonas were completely resistant up to the higher dose of Eucalyptus (150 µg/mL) (Fig. 1A). Klebsiella and Proteus were inhibited by the Eucalyptus extract but required a much higher dose compared to the Gram-positive bacteria (100 and 110 µg/mL, respectively). A similar trend of preferential antibacterial effects against Gram-positive bacteria was seen with Larrea tridentata, Anemopsis californica, Coptis chinensis, Salvia officinalis, and Turnera diffusa (Fig. 1B, D, E, K and L). Several of the botanical extracts, including Hypericum perforatum, Hydrastis canadensis, Juniperus communis, Coleus forskohlii, and Rosmarinus officinalis were unable to inhibit growth of any of the Gram-negative bacteria, including Klebsiella and Proteus, over the range of doses tested (Fig. 1C, F, G, and H). The Arctostaphylos uva-ursi extract demonstrated the most broad-spectrum activity where the extract was able to inhibit the replication of all the bacteria, Gram-negative and Gram-positive, except for Pseudomonas (Fig. 1M). The activity of the various botanical extracts against Gram-positive bacteria did vary with the most potent extracts from Eucalyptus glabra and Larrea tridentata (Fig. 1A and B). When bacterial cultures treated with an equivalent amount of vehicle (ethanol), no inhibition in replication was observed (Fig. 1N). Figure 1 established that botanical extracts may be effective options as antibacterials for Gram-positive bacteria.

Evaluation of botanical extracts in the presence of permeabilizers against Gram-negative bacteria

It has been previously reported that OM permeabilizers can be paired with antibacterial treatments to facilitate with the degradation of the LPS layer that surrounds and fortifies Gram-negative bacteria [2, 5–7]. As evidenced in Fig. 1, most Gram-negative bacteria were highly resistant to antibacterial botanicals that were otherwise very effective at inhibiting Gram-positive bacteria. Eight of the botanical extracts, Eucalyptus glabra, Larrea tridentata, Hypericum perforatum, Anemopsis californica, Coptis chinensis, Hydrastis canadensis, Juniperus communis, and Salvia officinalis demonstrated none to little activity against Gram-negative bacteria. Therefore, these eight extracts were further evaluated for potential antibacterial activity against Gram-negative bacteria in the presence of known OM permeabilizers. The four most resistant bacteria, Escherichia, Shigella, Pseudomonas, and Salmonella, were selected for these assays. Figure 2 shows the effects of the three OM permeabilizers that were combined with the botanical extracts and their collective effect on bacterial growth. All three permeabilizers, EDTA (0.5 mM), spermidine (10 mM), and polyethyleneimine (200 ug/ml), when tested alone without botanical extract addition were unable to inhibit the replication of any of the bacteria (Fig. 2, labelled as ‘None’). EDTA combined with Eucalyptus, Larrea, Coptis, Hydrastis, or Salvia extracts decreased the MBC dose of these extracts to 35–100 µg/ml (Fig. 2). Previously these botanical extracts had no effect on the growth of these bacteria (Fig. 1), but when used conjointly with EDTA they were successful in inhibiting the growth of the Gram-negative bacteria. For the Anemopsis and Juniperis extracts, addition of EDTA was only able to increase the activity of the botanical against Shigella and Salmonella, respectively (Fig. 2). EDTA had no effect on cotreatment with the Hypericum extract. Somewhat similar results were observed with the OM permeabilizer, spermidine. Spermidine combined with Eucalyptus, Larrea, Coptis, or Salvia extracts decreased the MBC dose of these extracts to 10–100 µg/ml (Fig. 2). Spermidine also increased the activity of the Hypericum extract against Shigella, Pseudomonas, and Salmonella and the activity of Juniperis extract against Shigella (Fig. 2).

Polyethyleneimine was the least effective OM permeabilizer regarding the improvement of the activity of the botanical extracts. Polyethyleneimine only increased the anti-bacterial activity of the Larrea extract but reduced the MBC to approximately 50 µg/ml for all four Gram-negative bacteria tested (Fig. 2). This data suggests that various OM permeabilizers can increase the antibacterial activity of botanical extracts against Gram-negative bacteria but that correct pairings are necessary for optimal activity.

Discussion

Bacterial antibiotic resistance is a major concern and threat to public health. In recent years, a 15% rise in antibiotic resistance has been observed in infections caused by Escherichia coli, Salmonella spp., and Neisseria gonorrhoeae [8]. Whereas some antibiotics have broad-spectrum activity against numerous bacteria, other antibiotics are only effective against specific groups of bacteria. This research sought to evaluate the spectrum of antibacterial activity of various botanical extracts. As shown, most Gram-negative bacteria were ineffectively inhibited by nearly all of the botanical extracts tested, compared to various Gram-positive bacteria which were much more sensitive. Only one botanical extract, Arctostaphylos uva ursi, was shown to have a more broad-spectrum of activity against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. These results potentially support the use of these botanical extracts for Gram-positive associated bacterial infections and a potential source of novel Gram-positive bacteria targeting antibiotics. For Gram-negative bacteria, it has been shown that these bacteria are intrinsically more resistant to antibiotics due to the low permeability of the outer LPS layer that surrounds the bacteria [9]. This LPS layer is likely responsible for the lack of sensitivity of Gram-negative bacteria to the antibacterial botanical extracts used in this study. Since the OM of Gram-negative bacteria make them resistant to hydrophobic antibiotics, these results may suggest that the antibacterial constituents present in the botanical extracts are likely more hydrophilic in structure [10].

In 2019, 1.27 million deaths were primarily attributed to six bacterial pathogens, four of which included the Gram-negative bacteria Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Acinetobacter baumannii [11, 12]. Linked to this, the World Health Organization has listed Gram-negative bacterial species as the highest priority for new antibiotic development [13]. Various OM permeabilizers are known to increase the sensitivity of Gram-negative bacteria to antibiotics. These OM permeabilizers increase the degradation of the LPS layer that surrounds and fortifies Gram-negative bacteria [2, 5–7, 14, 15]. Results demonstrated that treatment with EDTA and spermidine OM permeabilizers had an enhancing effect on antimicrobial activity of several botanical extracts against Gram-negative bacteria. However, there were variations and specificity related to which botanical extracts were enhanced and towards which bacteria. For example, EDTA increased the antibacterial activity of five of the eight botanical extracts tested, including Hydrastis. In comparison, spermidine also increased the activity of five of the eight extracts tested, but did not improve Hydrastis, but did improve Hypericum which was not seen with EDTA. The permeabilizer, polyethyleneimine, was only effective at enhancing the activity of the Larrea tridentata extract, which was also improved by EDTA and spermidine. These results likely suggest that the active antibacterial constituents present in the extracts may vary considerably structurally where specific OM permeabilizers are more effective than others depending on the extract used.

In addition, some OM permeabilizers only improved the antibacterial activity of the botanicals extracts towards specific bacteria. For example, EDTA increased the activity of Anemopsis against Shigella (and partially Salmonella), while having no improved effect on Escherichia or Pseudomonas. Spermidine greatly improved the activity of Juniperis against Shigella, while having no effect on the other bacteria tested. Structurally the OM of bacteria varies considerably between bacterial species. Since different types of LPS are present in different genera of Gram-negative bacteria, the effectiveness of OM permeabilizers likely varies according to the OM structure [16, 17].

Conclusion

As botanical extracts may be considered as therapeutics for treating bacterial infections or the isolation of new antibiotics, the addition of OM permeabilizers in the treatment protocol may be considered depending on the identity and structure of the bacteria. The results demonstrated that several botanical extracts had potentially efficacious activity against several Gram-positive bacteria. With the addition of OM permeabilizers, the activity of these botanical extracts became more broad-spectrum against Gram-negative bacteria as well. These results support the need to examine the potential source of botanicals for novel and effective antibiotics to help combat the increasing threat of antibiotic resistance [18–20].
