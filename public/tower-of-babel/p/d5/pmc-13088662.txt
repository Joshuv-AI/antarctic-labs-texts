Impact of neoadjuvant imatinib duration and tumor response on recurrence-free survival in locally advanced gastrointestinal stromal tumors: a retrospective multicenter cohort study
Authors: Haoyu Zong, Youwei Kou, He Song, Zhongmin Lan, Yongrun Mu, Qifeng Xiao, Xin Jin, Yunlong Li, Shuai Xiang, Yiyao Huo, Mengjie Zhou, Xiaoyan Zhang, Xin Wu, Jianwei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeoadjuvant imatinib (IM) is widely used in locally advanced gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs) to improve resectability and reduce recurrence, but optimal duration and the prognostic role of tumor response remain unclear.MethodsWe performed a multicenter retrospective cohort study of patients with locally advanced GISTs who received preoperative imatinib and surgery between 2014 and 2024 at four tertiary centers in China. Tumor response was assessed by Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) 1.1, and a novel parameter, the Percent Tumor Response Index (PTRI), was proposed to integrate baseline size with shrinkage rate. Recurrence-free survival (RFS) and overall survival (OS) were analyzed by Kaplan–Meier and Cox regression.ResultsAmong 168 patients (median treatment 7.5 months), all achieved disease control: partial response (PR) in 52.4% and stable disease (SD) in 47.6%. Median tumor size decreased from 10.0 to 7.0 cm (p < 0.001). After a median follow-up of 53 months, 3- and 5-year RFS rates were 88.6% and 83.2%, and OS rates were 97.6% and 92.0%. Using maximally selected rank statistics (MSRS), 8 months was identified as a potentially meaningful threshold. Patients treated ≤ 8 months had superior RFS compared with > 8 months. In multivariable Cox regression analysis, a preoperative imatinib duration exceeding 8 months (HR 2.40, p = 0.034) and a postoperative mitotic count > 5/5 HPF (HR 2.31, p = 0.042) were independently associated with worse RFS, whereas a higher Percent Tumor Response Index (PTRI), a newly proposed exploratory metric, was associated with a reduced risk of recurrence, corresponding to a 32% risk reduction per unit increase (p = 0.002).ConclusionsIn our cohort, a preoperative imatinib duration exceeding 8 months for locally advanced GISTs was associated with inferior RFS. Elevated PTRI predicted favorable recurrence outcomes, whereas higher postoperative mitotic count indicated poorer prognosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-026-04291-w.

Introduction

GISTs are the most common type of mesenchymal tumor found within the gastrointestinal tract [1, 2]. In the management of primary GISTs, surgery continues to be the predominant therapeutic approach of definitive treatment; however, for large tumors or those located at anatomically challenging sites such as the gastroesophageal junction, duodenum, or distal rectum—where upfront surgery is unlikely to achieve R0 resection or would require multivisceral resection—patients remain at high risk of postoperative recurrence or metastasis therefore require neoadjuvant therapy [3–6]. Some prospective and retrospective studies have shown that neoadjuvant imatinib therapy effectively reduces tumor size, thereby decreasing surgical complexity, increasing the probability of achieving R0 resection, facilitating organ-preserving procedures and reducing local recurrence, all of which are expected to improve long-term oncological outcomes [7–9]. The following clinical questions remain unresolved. On the one hand, the optimal duration of imatinib treatment also remains uncertain. Although standardized criteria for defining the maximal response to neoadjuvant therapy—namely, the absence of significant tumor changes on two consecutive imaging assessments—have been established, overtreatment remains common in clinical practice, potentially increasing the risk of tumor progression due to drug resistance [10–12]. Current clinical evidence from several studies suggests that the median treatment duration typically ranges from 4 to 18 months [13–16]. And most of these studies were single-center clinical trials or included a small series of patients. On the other hand, current research on tumor response has primarily focused on reporting the median tumor shrinkage rate following neoadjuvant therapy, whereas the precise relationship between treatment response and long-term postoperative outcomes remains insufficiently investigated.

Currently, robust tools are lacking to determine the optimal duration of neoadjuvant therapy and to clarify the clinical significance of treatment response. In this study, we aim to explore the impact of neoadjuvant imatinib duration and tumor response in our patient cohort. Also, we aim to identify factors influencing postoperative outcomes in patients receiving neoadjuvant therapy.

Materials and methods

Patients and study design

This multicenter retrospective study analyzed a consecutive cohort of patients with locally advanced GISTs who received neoadjuvant imatinib therapy; owing to the retrospective observational design, no a priori sample size calculation was performed. Surgical resection was subsequently performed at the National Cancer Center, the Chinese PLA General Hospital, Shengjing Hospital, and the First Hospital of China Medical University between January 2014 and May 2024. To ensure comparability across institutions, a uniform set of predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria was prospectively agreed upon and consistently applied at all participating centers. Patient identification and case selection were based on institutional surgical and oncology databases, and all eligible patients meeting the criteria during the study period were consecutively enrolled. To minimize inter-center variability, data collection was standardized using a unified case report form, which predefined variable definitions, measurement units, and outcome assessments. The use of preoperative imatinib and the optimal timing of surgery was determined through multidisciplinary discussion. The exclusion criteria were: [1] age < 18 years; [2] patients with a prior or current diagnosis of other malignancy; [3] patients with recurrent and metastatic diseases; [4] patients with tumors located in the distal rectum; and [5] patients with missing information. The flow chart is presented in Fig. 1. Missing data were minimal (representing < 5% of the cohort) and exhibited no systematic pattern across centers, supporting the assumption of missing completely at random.

Study flow

STROBE checklist was used as the reporting guide for our study. This study adhered to the Helsinki Declaration and received approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Cancer Center (S2024-332-02). All data were collected and analyzed in compliance with local data protection regulations, and patient information was fully anonymized. Written informed consent was waived by the IRB because the study was observational and noninvasive, but verbal consent was obtained from each patient.

Definition of locally advanced GISTs

This study defined “locally advanced GISTs” as primary, non-metastatic, and non-recurrent GISTs that met at least one of the following criteria: [1] large tumor burden (maximum diameter > 10 cm) or anticipated need for multivisceral and/or extended resection, with a high intraoperative risk of bleeding, tumor rupture, or iatrogenic dissemination; [2] tumor location in anatomically critical sites, such as the gastroesophageal junction, where surgical resection carries a substantial risk of functional impairment; [3] a high likelihood of inability to achieve an R0 resection with upfront surgery; or [4] an increased risk of postoperative recurrence, complications, or mortality as determined by multidisciplinary team assessment. Patients meeting these criteria were considered appropriate candidates for neoadjuvant imatinib, with the aim of tumor downsizing, reducing surgical complexity, and increasing the likelihood of achieving an R0 resection, as determined through multidisciplinary team discussion.

Data collection and treatment administration

The electronic medical record system was used to extract various sociodemographic and clinical variables, including gender, age, body mass index (BMI), smoking history, drinking history, family history of cancer, duration of preoperative imatinib, tumor location, postoperative mitotic count, mutational status, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification, type of surgery, multivisceral resection, surgical technique, margin status, duration of surgery and blood loss in surgery. Family history of cancer was defined as the occurrence of cancer in any first- or second-degree relatives of the individual. Postoperative mitotic count was reported from the surgical pathology specimen in mitoses per 5 mm2 [which is equivalent to 50 high-power fields (HPF) for traditional-field microscopes]. Surgical margin status was classified as: R0 (microscopically negative), R1 (microscopically positive but grossly negative), or R2 (macroscopically positive).

Patients who met the criteria for locally advanced GISTs were evaluated by a multidisciplinary team (MDT) and considered candidates for neoadjuvant therapy. Following MDT consensus, neoadjuvant imatinib was initiated at a standard dose of 400 mg/day in all patients and was maintained at this dose throughout the treatment period. During neoadjuvant imatinib therapy, interim imaging assessments were routinely performed at 2.3 months (IQR, 1.9–2.6) intervals using contrast-enhanced computed tomography. Radiologic evaluations were primarily used to monitor treatment response dynamics and to confirm disease stability. Maximal radiologic response was defined as the absence of significant tumor changes on two consecutive imaging assessments. After surgery, adjuvant imatinib treatment was also started at 400 mg/day and continued for the recommended 3 years.

Imaging assessment and tumor response evaluation

Baseline tumor size was determined by measurements obtained from abdominal and pelvic CT imaging at initial diagnosis. Post-treatment tumor size was determined based on the most recent preoperative CT scan. All contrast-enhanced CT scans were independently reviewed by two board-certified radiologists, each with more than 10 years of experience in abdominal imaging, who were involved throughout the entire study period. Any discrepancies were resolved by consensus. All tumor size were defined as the largest dimension measured on the axial contrast-enhanced CT slice where the tumor margins were most clearly delineated and the tumor appeared largest. Tumor responses to IM were evaluated according to the RECIST 1.1 criteria [17].

Tumor shrinkage rate (%) was calculated as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text{Tumor shrinkage rate}=\left(1-\frac{\text{Post-treatment tumor size}}{\text{Baseline tumor size}}\right)\times100\%$$\end{document}

To account for the influence of baseline tumor burden, we further defined a derived parameter, PTRI, calculated as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{PTRI}=\frac{\text{Tumor shrinkage rate}}{\text{Baseline tumor size}}\times100\%$$\end{document}

PTRI was conceptualized as a composite response metric integrating baseline tumor burden and radiologic tumor shrinkage into a single continuous variable. By normalizing the tumor shrinkage rate to baseline tumor size, PTRI was intended to capture the joint effect of tumor burden and treatment responsiveness. This metric was used to evaluate whether, for the same degree of tumor shrinkage, patients with larger baseline tumors had different outcomes compared to those with smaller tumors. Conversely, we also examined whether, among patients with similar baseline tumor size, the degree of tumor shrinkage impacted prognosis.

Follow-up

The follow-up was conducted via outpatient visits or telephone interviews. Routine postoperative surveillance included contrast-enhanced computed tomography of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis every 6 months during the first 3 years after surgery and annually thereafter. Additional imaging was performed at the discretion of the treating physicians when recurrence was clinically suspected. RFS was defined as the interval from primary tumor resection to recurrence (encompassing local recurrence and distant metastasis) or the last follow-up date. OS was calculated from the date of primary tumor resection to death or the last follow-up date. All survival and recurrence data were updated as of October 2024.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as means with standard deviations for normally distributed data, and medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) for non-normally distributed data, while categorical variables were reported as counts and proportions. Continuous variables were compared between groups using the Student t-test or Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher exact test. Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared by log-rank analysis between groups. Univariable and multivariable analyses were conducted by the COX regression model. Candidate variables for inclusion in the multivariable model were selected based on univariable analysis (P < 0.20) and established clinical relevance, including covariates with prior evidence of prognostic significance in GISTs management. Variables were entered into the multivariable models using a forced-entry approach. Multicollinearity among covariates was assessed using variance inflation factors. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. Subgroup analyses were conducted to assess whether potential covariates (gender, age, tumor location, neoadjuvant treatment duration > 8 months, postoperative mitotic count > 5/50HPF, Ki67 > 10%) modified the relationship between PTRI and RFS. The significance level was set at P < 0.05 for all tests. Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 24 and R 4.1.3 software.

Results

Patient characteristics

Table 1 summarizes the baseline characteristics of the study cohort. A total of 168 patients with locally advanced GISTs who received IM therapy were included. The majority of patients were male (63.1%), with a mean age of 58.1 ± 10.5 years. The median tumor size at diagnosis was 10.0 cm (IQR, 7.3–14.8). The stomach was the most common primary site, identified in 106 patients (63.1%), followed by the small bowel in 36 patients (21.4%). The remaining cases comprised tumors originating from the duodenum, rectum, or esophagus. The median duration of neoadjuvant imatinib therapy was 7.5 months (IQR, 6.0–12.0). A total of 104 patients (61.9%) received preoperative treatment for less than 8 months, whereas 64 patients (38.1%) underwent treatment for more than 8 months. Pathological analysis revealed that KIT exon 11 mutations were the predominant genetic alteration, detected in 153 patients (91.1%), whereas KIT exon 9 mutations and other mutations were identified in only 12 patients (7.1%) and 3 patients (1.8%), respectively. Following surgery, the majority of patients (n = 118, 70.2%) exhibited a mitotic count of ≤ 5 per 5 HPF, whereas a minority (n = 50, 29.8%) demonstrated a mitotic count of > 5 per 5 HPF.

Tumor size reduction

Preoperative imatinib (IM) was temporarily discontinued for a median of 13 days (range, 7–16 days) prior to surgery. After a median of 7.5 (IQR: 6.0–12.0) months of preoperative IM treatment, the median tumor size prior to initiation of neoadjuvant IM was 10.0 cm (IQR, 7.3–14.7), which significantly decreased to 7.0 cm (IQR, 5.0–9.0) following treatment (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2A). Tumor size reduction was observed in 165 patients (98.2%), while 3 patients (1.8%) exhibited tumor progression (Fig. 2B). Among these progressive cases, one harbored a KIT exon 15 mutation, another carried a compound exon 11 and 17 mutation, and the third had a Kit exon 11 mutation. According to RECIST 1.1 criteria, disease control was achieved in all cases, with PR observed slightly more frequently than stable disease (n = 88, 52.4% vs. n = 80, 47.6%). Among the 168 patients, a total of 30 experienced disease recurrence. The recurrence rate was significantly higher in patients with stable disease (SD), with 17 patients (26%) affected, compared to 13 patients (17%) in the PR group (p < 0.05).

Resection of locally advanced GISTs after IM treatment

Surgical profiles were summarized in Table 2. Among the 168 patients with locally advanced GISTs, partial gastrectomy was the most frequently performed surgical procedure, accounting for 106 cases (63.1%). Duodenal segment resection and abdominoperineal resection were each performed in 20 patients (11.9%), which was slightly more frequent than small bowel segment resection, undertaken in 16 patients (9.5%). The remaining 6 patients (3.6%) underwent other less common types of surgery, including pancreaticoduodenectomy and gastroepiploicectomy. Failure of neighboring organ preservation necessitated additional procedures in 29 patients, with the most common being combined distal pancreatectomy with splenectomy (n = 9), splenectomy alone (n = 7), and less frequent interventions including partial diaphragmectomy (n = 5), distal esophagectomy (n = 4), partial colectomy (n = 3), left hepatectomy (n = 3), and peritonectomy (n = 2). Successful R0 resections were achieved in 163 patients (97.0%).

Outcome

The median follow-up duration was 53.0 months (IQR, 30.8–89.0), with 30 patients (17.9%) experiencing recurrence and 9 patients (5.4%) dying from tumor-related causes. The most common site of metastasis was the retroperitoneum (n = 5), followed by the liver (n = 3) and esophagus (n = 1). The 3-year RFS and OS rates were 88.6% and 97.6%, respectively, with projected 5-year rates of 83.2% and 92.0%. (Figure 3). The time to 25% event occurrence for RFS was 7 months, whereas the median RFS had not been reached. Similarly, the 75th percentile OS was 103 months, and the median OS was not reached at the time of analysis.

Using the MSRS, 8 months was identified as a potentially meaningful threshold for preoperative imatinib duration (Figure 4). Bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations) confirmed robust internal stability, with the cut-off value centrally positioned within the distribution (Supplementary Figure S1). Baseline clinicopathologic characteristics were compared between patients receiving ≤ 8 months and > 8 months of preoperative IM. As shown in Table 3, key baseline variables, including tumor size, location, mutation status, mitotic count, and Ki-67 index, were generally comparable between the two groups. Survival analysis demonstrated that treatment duration had a significant influence on RFS, with patients in the long-term preoperative treatment group exhibiting inferior outcomes compared with those receiving short-term therapy (Log-rank, p = 0.040; Figure 5). Univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were applied to identify factors associated with RFS (Table 4). Univariable analysis revealed that RFS was significantly associated with the duration of preoperative imatinib therapy (HR: 2.09, 95% CI: 1.02–4.29, p = 0.044), the PTRI (HR: 0.70, 95% CI: 0.56–0.88, p = 0.002), and the postoperative mitotic count (HR: 2.31, 95% CI: 1.12–4.77, p = 0.024). To minimize potential selection bias, a multivariable Cox regression analysis was conducted. Prior to model construction, we confirmed the absence of significant multicollinearity (all adjusted GVIF < 1.2; Supplementary Table S1) and validated the proportional hazards assumption (Schoenfeld residuals, P = 0.217; Supplementary Table S2). The multivariable Cox regression analysis results demonstrated that mitotic count, PTRI, and duration of preoperative IM therapy were independent prognostic factors for RFS in patients with locally advanced GISTs. Specifically, a higher mitotic count (HR: 2.31, 95% CI: 1.08–4.65, p = 0.042) and longer preoperative IM treatment duration (HR: 2.40, 95% CI: 1.10–5.38, p = 0.034) were significantly associated with poorer RFS, whereas a lower PTRI (HR: 0.68, 95% CI: 0.54–0.87, p = 0.002) was associated with improved RFS (Table 3). In contrast, univariable Cox regression analysis showed no significant associations between OS and any of the evaluated factors. We evaluated the calibration of the model using 1000 bootstrap resamples. The bootstrap-corrected calibration curve demonstrated reasonable agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes (Supplementary Figure S1).

Discussion

The optimal duration of neoadjuvant therapy remains a subject of debate, and the prognostic significance of tumor response following surgery has yet to be clearly defined. To the best of our knowledge, this is the largest multi-center study to date evaluating neoadjuvant imatinib duration and tumor response in patients with locally advanced primary GISTs. Our results suggest that, in this retrospective cohort, a preoperative imatinib duration exceeding 8 months for locally advanced GISTs was associated with inferior RFS. Elevated PTRI significantly reduced the risk of recurrence. At the same initial tumor size, a greater tumor shrinkage rate was associated with longer RFS time. When the tumor shrinkage rate was comparable, smaller initial tumor size correlated with prolonged RFS. Apart from neoadjuvant imatinib duration and PTRI, elevated mitotic count (postoperative) was significantly associated with worse RFS.

Based on our findings, MSRS identified an 8-month threshold beyond which prolonged neoadjuvant imatinib treatment was associated with a higher risk of recurrence. Other studies indicate that the median duration of neoadjuvant imatinib therapy ranges from 4 to 18 months [18–21]. Tielen et al. [22] observed that the maximum benefit time of preoperative IM treatment was 32 weeks in patients with locally advanced GISTs. Wang et al. [23] found that the median time required for achieving the earliest PR was 3.7 months and the median time to reach a plateau in tumor response was 6.1 months. Using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, Sun et al. [24] determined that the optimal duration of preoperative IM treatment for locally advanced GISTs was 8 months. GISTs exhibit temporal heterogeneity in their therapeutic response to imatinib due to dynamic modulation of downstream signaling pathways. Specifically, initial treatment leads to inhibition of KIT phosphorylation and suppression of critical cascades such as MAPK and PI3K/AKT, promoting apoptosis and growth arrest. Over time, compensatory mechanisms—including upregulation of survival pathways and adaptation via gene expression changes—emerge, creating a biologically defined window wherein maximal tumor response occurs [25, 26]. Prolonged neoadjuvant imatinib therapy may increase the risk of secondary resistance, as supported by molecular evidence of secondary KIT mutations (notably in exons 13 and 17) and activation of downstream signaling pathways such as PI3K/AKT [27, 28]. However, it is crucial to interpret the association between treatment duration exceeding 8 months and inferior RFS with caution. Given the retrospective nature of this study, causality cannot be established. The decision to prolong neoadjuvant therapy beyond 8 months was likely influenced by clinical factors such as slow tumor shrinkage, persistent large tumor burden, or high surgical complexity requiring extended downsizing.

Assessing tumor response to therapy is critical for evaluating treatment efficacy and guiding clinical decision-making in oncology [29, 30]. Based on the RECIST criteria, reported response rates are largely consistent across published studies. The tumor response outcomes observed in our study consist with previous reports [15, 26, 31]: the median tumor size reduction was 30%; the PR rate was 52.4%, while the stable disease rate reached 47.6%. Notably, in this study, none of the patients who received neoadjuvant therapy experienced disease progression during treatment, and all achieved disease control at the end of therapy, highlighting the substantial efficacy of the treatment regimen. Furthermore, the median tumor reduction of 30% in our cohort, remains clinically relevant and aligns with the established threshold for treatment efficacy in imatinib-treated GISTs patients. These similarities reinforce the reliability of RECIST-based evaluations in this tumor type, while also highlighting the potential influences of cohort-specific characteristics on radiographic outcomes.

In this study, we proposed the PTRI as a composite response metric integrating baseline tumor burden and radiologic tumor shrinkage into a single continuous variable. PTRI was designed to account for inter-patient heterogeneity in baseline tumor size, thereby contextualizing radiologic response within the initial disease burden. It was found to be significantly associated with RFS according to multivariable Cox regression analysis. A higher PTRI indicated that, for a given baseline tumor size, a greater tumor shrinkage rate was associated with a reduced risk of recurrence. This observation may be explained by tumor biology. Under a constant baseline volume, a larger shrinkage rate implies robust sensitivity to imatinib, often driven by dominant mutations such as KIT exon 11. This sensitivity is typically accompanied by lower intratumoral heterogeneity, reducing the likelihood of resistant subclones [32, 33]. Similarly, for an equivalent tumor shrinkage rate, a smaller baseline tumor size corresponded to a lower recurrence risk. One possible underlying mechanism is that genomic complexity (including somatic mutation counts and copy-number alteration burden) increases with tumor size and mitotic activity; therefore, smaller tumors are less likely to harbor extensive subclonal architecture that can drive post-therapy regrowth [34–36]. Furthermore, it is important to note that, as a ratio-based metric, the absolute clinical interpretation of a one-unit increase in PTRI is inherently abstract in its clinical interpretation. A one-unit increase in PTRI represents an incremental change in the ratio of tumor shrinkage to baseline size. This complexity underscores the need for careful consideration when translating PTRI into clinical practice. While PTRI provides valuable prognostic insight, its utility as a standalone marker is still exploratory and requires further validation in larger, independent cohorts.

This study carries significant clinical implications. On the one hand, insufficient treatment duration may lead to suboptimal tumor control and an increased risk of postoperative recurrence, whereas prolonged therapy may expose patients to unnecessary toxicities, increased costs, and compromised compliance. Identifying a potentially meaningful therapeutic duration window is therefore essential for optimizing patient outcomes. More importantly, the eight-month treatment duration may serve as a potentially clinically meaningful prognostic stratification signal, which may assist in risk assessment and the tailoring of postoperative surveillance strategies. On the other hand, our study introduces the PTRI as a novel response parameter that integrates baseline tumor volume with tumor shrinkage rate. PTRI demonstrated a significant association with recurrence risk, suggesting that it may provide a complementary perspective on therapeutic efficacy beyond conventional RECIST-based morphologic measurements. RECIST remains the most widely adopted response criterion. However, its one-dimensional framework implicitly assumes that identical percentage reductions reflect equivalent biological effects, regardless of baseline tumor size. This assumption may not consistently hold in GISTs. Prior studies have shown that baseline tumor burden correlates with recurrence risk, genomic heterogeneity, and the potential presence of resistant subclones, suggesting that the same degree of shrinkage may not convey uniform biological significance or prognostic implication across varying initial tumor volumes [37–39]. Against this backdrop, PTRI was designed to incorporate both baseline size and treatment-related reduction, thereby offering an interpretation of radiologic response that may align with the biological differences associated with tumor burden. Clinically, PTRI may contribute to improved risk stratification, guide postoperative management, and facilitate individualized treatment strategies. However, despite the demonstration of an independent association with clinical outcomes in our analyses, PTRI should be regarded as an exploratory measure. Validation in independent cohorts, preferably within prospective study settings, is required before PTRI can be considered a predictive biomarker suitable for routine clinical decision-making.

It should be emphasized that the 8-month threshold identified in this study was derived using MSRS. Although MSRS is a well-established exploratory approach for detecting potential inflection points in continuous variables, it is inherently data-driven and therefore susceptible to overfitting, particularly in analyses based on a single cohort of modest size. To mitigate this limitation, internal validation was performed using bootstrap resampling, which demonstrated relative stability of the MSRS-derived threshold. Nonetheless, this cut-off should be construed as a hypothesis-generating result rather than a definitive clinical recommendation. Further validation in independently assembled cohorts, preferably within a prospective study design, is warranted before consideration for routine clinical implementation.

Several limitations of this study should be noted. Firstly, given its retrospective design, the study is inevitably influenced by selection bias and the results need to be further verified by prospective multicenter randomized controlled trials. Secondly, owing to the lack of tumor density measurements (Hounsfield unit values) in CT imaging, tumor response in this study was assessed exclusively according to RECIST criteria, without consideration of Choi parameters. This methodological limitation restricted our PTRI analysis to unidimensional volumetric changes, thereby potentially overlooking important functional response information. Thirdly, the multivariable analysis included a limited number of recurrence events relative to covariates. This resulted in a low events-per-variable ratio, which increases the risk of overfitting. While bootstrap validation demonstrated acceptable calibration, residual overfitting cannot be entirely ruled out. Thus, findings should be cautiously interpreted as preliminary, with external validation in independent cohorts required. Fourthly, as patients with KIT exon 9 mutations or wild-type GISTs constituted only a small proportion of the study population, subgroup-specific conclusions for these genotypes could not be robustly assessed. Future studies with larger sample sizes will be required to allow more comprehensive analyses of less common mutational subtypes.

Conclusion

In summary, our data suggest that, in this retrospective cohort of locally advanced GISTs, a preoperative imatinib duration exceeding 8 months was associated with inferior RFS. Elevated PTRI was associated with favorable recurrence outcomes, whereas higher postoperative mitotic count predicted poorer prognosis.
