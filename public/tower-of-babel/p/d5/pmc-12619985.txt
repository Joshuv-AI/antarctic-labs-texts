Marine Heatwaves Transform Coral Symbioses With Enduring Effects
Authors: Alexander Van Nynatten, Ross Cunning, Kristina L. Tietjen, Julia K. Baum
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTClimate change‐amplified extreme weather events are reshaping ecological communities globally. On coral reefs, heatwaves typically disrupt the obligate coral–algal symbiosis, with symbiont identity a prime determinant of coral resilience to these thermal extremes. Yet, whether heatwaves have long‐term effects on coral symbioses remains unclear due to a lack of longitudinal symbiont data. Here, we report on a decadal coral symbiont survey (2013–2023), spanning the most prolonged tropical marine heatwave on record (2015–2016) and its aftermath. Concomitant with mass coral mortality, we document wholesale transformation of the symbiont assemblages in two coral species, the legacy of which was persistent for 7 years post heatwave. We also found evidence suggestive of a symbiont's local extinction, of local human disturbance impeding symbiont recovery, and of new coral recruits hosting symbiont assemblages distinct from survivors. Our study demonstrates heatwaves can have long‐lasting impacts on symbioses raising concern for coral resilience to future heatwaves.

Introduction

Extreme weather events, from heatwaves and wildfires to droughts and floods, are now significant agents of change in ecological communities globally (Gu et al. 2023; Harris et al. 2018; IPCC 2023). In the ocean, marine heatwaves (MHW) are of greatest concern, causing mass mortality of marine foundation species and restructuring entire ecosystems (Smith et al. 2021; Wernberg et al. 2024). However, the legacies of these episodic events on marine biodiversity remain poorly understood compared to their direct, acute biotic and socioeconomic impacts, such as harmful algal blooms, seabird die‐offs, and fisheries closures (Smith et al. 2021; Starko et al. 2025). Heatwave legacies may include shifts in the incidence, abundance, and dominance of species, with concomitant impacts on species interactions, community composition and diversity, and overall ecosystem function (Bastos et al. 2020; Smale et al. 2019). Amongst the potential important long‐term consequences of heatwaves, disrupted ecological symbioses are likely given interspecific variability in sensitivity and response capacity to thermal stress (Bérard et al. 2015; Hector et al. 2022; Wall et al. 2021), but remain little studied. With heatwaves increasing in frequency, intensity, and duration under climate change, advancing understanding of how they are altering these fundamental ecological relationships and the persistence of these changes, is now of critical importance for predicting and managing their future impacts.

Coral reefs are the most vulnerable of all marine ecosystems to climate change, in large part because of their sensitivity to MHWs (IPCC 2023). Temperature anomalies of as little as 1°C can result in damaging coral bleaching caused by corals expelling their obligate symbionts, photosynthetic algae in the family Symbiodiniaceae (LaJeunesse et al. 2018). Some corals that recover from bleaching may swap Symbiodiniaceae taxa, trading host‐specialist symbiont species (e.g., Cladocopium latusorum) with more heat‐tolerant generalists (e.g., Cladocopium C15 and Durusdinium trenchii) (Berkelmans and Van Oppen 2006; Fisher et al. 2012). However, many corals have developed intricate co‐evolutionary relationships with specific Symbiodiniaceae taxa (Armstrong et al. 2023; Johnston et al. 2022; Starko et al. 2023), that, under stable conditions, improve fitness through more efficient allocation of resources when compared to corals hosting generalist symbionts (Van Oppen and Medina 2020). Failure to recover these co‐evolved symbiont species could impair coral resilience under ambient conditions, but unlike their coral hosts, the recovery trajectories of symbiont species are not well studied (Elder et al. 2023; Stat, Loh, et al. 2009), especially over long time periods.

Many coral reefs have already been significantly impacted by MHWs revealing a wide range of recovery trajectories (Graham et al. 2011). The full recovery of coral assemblages is expected to take more than a decade (Hughes et al. 2018) but has been recorded on reefs in Panama (Edmunds et al. 2019) and Western Australia (Gilmour et al. 2013). In many cases, coral assemblages that recover differ from those observed before the heatwave, arising from interspecific differences in coral resilience to MHWs due to contrasting host life history traits (Graham et al. 2024; Loya et al. 2001; Van Woesik et al. 2011). Corals classified as having competitive life‐history characteristics, favouring resource acquisition under ideal environmental conditions, typically experience higher mortality during MHWs than “stress‐tolerant” corals (Baum et al. 2023; Darling et al. 2012) but often come to rapidly dominate reefs following major disturbance events, in some cases surpassing pre‐heatwave abundance (Edmunds et al. 2019; Lachs et al. 2024; Morais et al. 2024). Coral survival also varies due to differing capacities to switch or shuffle symbionts (Baker 2001; Berkelmans and Van Oppen 2006; Cunning et al. 2018), but much less is known about how the recovery of interspecific variation in symbiont assemblages hosted by corals contributes to the long‐term resilience of coral reefs to MHWs.

Chronic human disturbance can also transform coral and Symbiodiniaceae species assemblages. This often results in the exchange of specialist species for more generalist stress‐tolerant taxa (Claar, McDevitt‐Irwin, et al. 2020; Côté and Darling 2010; LaJeunesse et al. 2010), similar to transformations observed post‐heatwave. Chronic human disturbance may prolong the time generalist symbiont communities endure post‐heatwave, and could permanently transform symbiont community composition through the cumulative impacts of acute and chronic stressors, leading to local symbiont extinctions. Simulations suggest extinction risks are associated with variation in symbiont/host fidelity and life‐history traits (Doña and Johnson 2020; Fabina et al. 2013), but have not been investigated in natural systems. Improved understanding of the long‐term impacts of MHWs on Symbiodiniaceae community stability, and reef resilience more broadly, is urgently needed as these stressors increase in frequency and intensity, with the potential to deplete pools of symbiont species (Cunning et al. 2024; Fabina et al. 2013) and reshape symbiont communities (Palacio‐Castro et al. 2023; Quigley et al. 2022).

Here, we investigate recovery trajectories of corals and their symbiont assemblages following marine heatwave‐induced mass coral mortality. Focusing on the recovery of two competitive coral species, Montipora aequituberculata and Pocillopora grandis, we conducted reef surveys over a decade, from 3 years before to 7 years after the 2015–2016 El Niño at its epicenter, Kiritimati atoll (Figure 1). Using image analyses of the reef benthic community and internal transcribed spacer 2 (ITS2) DNA metabarcoding of coral symbionts, we tested the hypotheses that extreme MHWs will cause enduring transformations of corals and their symbionts, and that chronic local human disturbance will further entrench heatwave legacies. Specifically, we expected that associations with host‐adapted symbiont species would be transformed, potentially including local symbiont extinctions, due to the contraction of niche space resulting from coral host mortality and the extended duration of environmental conditions favoring stress‐tolerant symbiont species. We further expected that chronic local human disturbance from persistent nearshore pollution and fishing pressures (Baum et al. 2023) would inhibit pre‐heatwave symbiont recovery, by facilitating survival of corals hosting stress‐tolerant symbionts and preventing the re‐establishment of host‐adapted symbiont lineages due to a lack of suitable habitat for recruitment. These analyses, utilizing some of the longest‐running near‐continuous surveys of corals and symbionts to date, provide key insight into the recovery and resilience of corals and their symbionts to MHWs that are becoming increasingly common and damaging.

Materials and Methods

Study Site and Design

Located in the central equatorial Pacific Ocean, Kiritimati is the world's largest atoll by landmass (388 km2; 150 km in perimeter). The atoll's coral reefs are exposed to a spatial gradient of chronic local human disturbance (Figure 1a), with reefs near the main villages and infrastructure on the northwest coast damaged by dredging, pollution, sewage runoff, and subsistence fishing pressure, while those along the north, east, and south coasts have been minimally affected (Baum et al. 2023; Magel et al. 2020; Watson et al. 2016). The cumulative effect of these impacts on recovery was evaluated using an established metric developed for Kiritimati (Baum et al. 2023; Claar, McDevitt‐Irwin, et al. 2020; Starko et al. 2023) that treats chronic human disturbance as a square‐root‐transformed continuous scale or five relative categories based on natural breakpoints in this scale (see Text S1 and Table S1). Kiritimati also experienced one of the most extreme MHWs ever recorded during the 2015–2016 El Niño, leading to prolonged ocean warming and severe coral bleaching and mortality (Figure 1b,c) (Baum et al. 2023).

Coral symbionts and benthic cover composition were surveyed over a decade, during 11 expeditions to Kiritimati between August 2013 and October 2023 (Table S2). Collection of coral tissue samples and visual surveys of coral cover were conducted at 17 and 37 long‐term monitoring forereef sites, respectively, at depths from 10 to 12 m spanning the human disturbance gradient (Figure S1; Tables S3 and S4). Visual surveys and tissue sampling focused on two coral species, Montipora aequituberculata (Bernard, 1897) and Pocillopora grandis Dana, 1846 (Hoeksema and Cairns 2025), identified based on gross morphological characteristics. These two species were selected because they both have competitive life history characteristics and experienced substantial mortality (> 98%) during the 2015–2016 MHW (Baum et al. 2023).

Coral Surveys and Sample Collection

Coral cover was calculated using 4865 photographs of the benthos beneath a 1 m2 gridded quadrat placed haphazardly adjacent to a 60 m transect along the 10–12 m isobath. During each expedition, 26–32 benthic photographs were taken at each site (Table S3) using a Canon digital camera that was white‐balanced at depth on each dive. CoralNet (Beijbom et al. 2015) was used to project and classify the substrate at 100 random points on each photoquadrat image based on a custom label set (Baum et al. 2023). After manually confirming the accuracy of points assigned to M. aequituberculata and P. grandis by CoralNet, the percentage of coral cover for each species, relative to the total substrate, was calculated for each site separately by first summing all points assigned to each species from all the quadrats conducted at each site and dividing these values by the total number of identifiable points for each site. See Text S2 for more details.

Individual M. aequituberculata (8–102 cm) and P. grandis coral colonies (8–70 cm) were tissue‐sampled and photographed, and for expeditions conducted from 2014 and onward, tagged and resampled during subsequent expeditions whenever possible. Coral biopsies (< 1 cm3) were collected from these corals using a small steel chisel and kept on ice in individual sterile sample bags during dive days and processed each evening. Coral subsamples were processed by separating as much coral tissue as possible from the aragonite skeleton using physical scraping with a sterile razor blade or brushing using compressed air and preserved in 400 μL of guanidinium buffer. The near complete mortality of these species during the MHW prevented the resampling of most colonies tagged before the heatwave during post‐heatwave expeditions, necessitating new colony sampling (Figures S2 and S3). Colonies sampled for the first time post‐heatwave were considered survivors if their maximum diameter, measured in ImageJ (Abràmoff et al. 2004) from birds‐eye photographs, exceeded the size expected for a coral that recruited in April 2016 or later, based on species‐specific annual extension rates (Pratchett et al. 2015).

DNA Extraction and Metabarcoding

DNA was extracted for ITS2 DNA metabarcoding from 856 samples representing 551 coral colonies (Table S4) using a guanidinium‐based protocol designed for Symbiodiniaceae (Stat, Pochon, et al. 2009). The ITS2 region of the Symbiodiniaceae nuclear genome was amplified by PCR using ITS‐DINO and ITS2Rev2 primers (Pochon et al. 2014; Stat, Pochon, et al. 2009). ITS2 amplicon sequence libraries were prepared for multiplex‐sequencing using a modified version of the Illumina 16S Metagenomic Sequencing Library Preparation kit and were sequenced on the MiSeq platform (Illumina) using 2 × 300 paired‐end read chemistry. See Text S3 for more details.

SymPortal (v 0.3.24) (Hume et al. 2019) was used to cluster and combine ITS2 amplicon sequence variants (ASV) into ITS2 type profiles based on distinct and repeated patterns of sample‐to‐sample variation in ASV incidence and relative read abundance (RRA). ITS2 type profiles are more representative of Symbiodiniaceae taxonomic diversity than the multicopy ITS2 ASVs generated using ITS2 DNA metabarcoding (Hume et al. 2019). Samples with fewer than 1000 total post‐processing sequencing reads were removed from the analysis. To account for sequencing run variation in the assignment of ITS2 type profiles, occasionally observed from the drop out of minor ITS2 sequences (Howells et al. 2020; Hume et al. 2020), Cladocopium ITS2 type profiles were clustered into representative groups, hereafter referred to as lineages, if they had between‐profile square‐root transformed weighted UniFrac distances < 0.0035 and visualised using dendrograms generated with the hclust function in the R package ape (Paradis and Schliep 2019). See Text S4 for more details. To exclude sequencing errors, cross‐sample contamination, rare, and physiologically irrelevant taxa, fragmented ITS2 type profiles in the genus Cladocopium and ITS2 profiles with less than 10% of the RRA in each sample were excluded from analyses of pre‐heatwave symbiont recovery.

Statistical Analyses

Significant differences in coral cover across expeditions were evaluated using a Kruskal‐Wallis rank sum test with expedition set as the independent variable and the coral cover attributed to each species as a percent of total substrate as the dependent variable with the kruskal. test function in the stats package (R Core Team 2023). To identify which expeditions were statistically similar, a Dunn's multiple comparison test with Benjamini‐Hochberg multiple test correction was conducted using the dunn.test function in the dunn.test package (Dinno 2014). Coral recovery in 2023 was measured for each site separately by taking the total substrate cover attributed to each coral species in 2023 and subtracting this value from the minimum value for substrate cover observed at the same site between 2016 and 2019.

A binomial logistic regression with date post‐heatwave treated as a continuous explanatory variable was used to evaluate the rate of recovery of the pre‐heatwave Cladocopium latusorum lineage, and Cladocopium generally, after the heatwave using the glm function in the stats package (R Core Team 2023). As pre‐heatwave symbiont lineages were not detected post‐heatwave in M. aequituberculata, we tested whether this could have been due to the small post‐heatwave sample size of this species (n = 8). To do this, we compared it to P. grandis, which had much higher post‐heatwave sampling (n = 166), and tested whether pre‐heatwave lineages were detectable in P. grandis after rarefying to the same small sample size as M. aequituberculata (n = 8; 1000 random subsamples). The cumulative detection probability P c = 1−(1−p̄) n (Schmidt and Pellet 2009) was used to determine the likelihood of not detecting the post‐heatwave C15 lineage in M. aequituberculata if it had been present prior to the heatwave. The per sample detection probability (p̄) was set to 0.1 matching cutoffs used for the detection of symbiont species in each sample and n was set equal to the total number of M. aequituberculata samples collected pre‐heatwave (n = 235).

To contrast the symbioses of colonies classified as survivors with all other samples collected in 2023, a PERMANOVA based on Bray‐Curtis dissimilarities was calculated treating the classification of colonies as survivors as the explanatory variable and the RRA of Symbiodiniaceae lineages hosted by each P. grandis colony as the response variable using the veg.dist and adonis2 functions in vegan (Oksanen et al. 2017).

Binomial regressions were used to test for relationships between the incidence of pre‐heatwave Symbiodiniaceae lineages hosted by P. grandis colonies across sites exposed to different levels of human disturbance treated as a continuous explanatory variable. Mann–Whitney U tests were used to compare Durusdinium RRA before and after the heatwave using the wilcox. test function in the stats package (R Core Team 2023). Only samples collected from sites categorised as very low, medium, and very high disturbance sampled at least once before the heatwave and again in 2023 were tested. p values were corrected for multiple testing using the Bonferroni method with the p.adjust function in the stats package (R Core Team 2023).

Results

Marine Heatwaves Reorganise Symbioses

Prolonged thermal stress from the 2015–2016 MHW transformed symbiont assemblages, concomitant with major host mortality. In Pocillopora grandis, the heatwave significantly reduced coral cover, and only one of the 176 colonies sampled in 2014–2015 survived the event (Figure 2a; Table S5; Figure S2). This sole survivor, and all other surviving P. grandis colonies first sampled in 2016 during or after the heatwave, exclusively hosted Durusdinium. This post‐heatwave symbiont assemblage stands in stark contrast to pre‐heatwave conditions for P. grandis , where one Cladocopium lineage, characterized by C42 and C1 ITS2 sequences diagnostic of Cladocopium latusorum (Burgess et al. 2024; Turnham et al. 2021), was detected in all samples collected (n = 239) with only a small proportion of these colonies (i.e., 2.0%–14.5% by expedition) hosting primarily background levels (< 50% RRA) of Durusdinium (Figure 2a; Figure S2). By 2017, C. latusorum was again detected in 4 of 9 colonies sampled, but this was a distinct lineage (Figure 2b) that was only first detected in P. grandis in 1 of 101 colonies sampled early in the heatwave (2015c) (Figure S2). Two and three years following the heatwave, the incidence of Durusdinium in P. grandis samples remained higher than Cladocopium, and the two distinct lineages were roughly equal in abundance as the pre‐heatwave lineage began to return (Figure 2a). Seven years after the heatwave (2023), by which time P. grandis coral cover had returned to pre‐heatwave levels, the pre‐heatwave lineage was once again most prevalent, appearing in 84.8% of colonies (n = 101/119 colonies) (Figure 2a). The lineage that had first emerged during the heatwave persisted in 7.6% of colonies (n = 9), while the incidence of Durusdinium (18.6%) had returned to pre‐heatwave values (Wilcoxon rank‐sum test, W = 200, p = 0.976; Figure 2a; Figure S4).

The MHW had an even more significant and enduring impact on M. aequituberculata . All tracked coral colonies sampled prior to the heatwave perished (n = 166). One surviving M. aequituberculata colony, that was first sampled 11 months into the heatwave, hosted a Cladocopium lineage characterised by C15 ITS2 sequence variants not observed in any of the 235 M. aequituberculata samples collected before the heatwave, which were dominated by C31 ITS2 sequences profiles (Figure 2; Figure S3). Minimal recovery of M. aequituberculata coral cover as a percent of the total substrate was observed throughout the study (median = +0.07%; Figure 2a; Figure S5) and as such, few colonies could be sampled post‐heatwave across the atoll. The C15 lineage remained the only symbiont lineage observed in the 4 M. aequituberculata colonies sampled post‐heatwave, apart from one colony first sampled in 2023 that hosted Durusdinium exclusively (Figure 2a; Figure S3). Two lines of evidence suggest that the transformation of symbioses observed after the heatwave in M. aequituberculata holds despite the limited post‐heatwave sample size for this species, due to its near extinction on the atoll. First, the detection of pre‐heatwave symbiont lineages in P. grandis remained significantly greater than 0 even when colonies were rarified to match the number of post‐heatwave M. aequituberculata samples (one sample t‐test, t = 173.68, p < 0.001). Second, the cumulative detection probability of the C15 symbiont lineage was > 0.99 based on the widespread sampling of M. aequituberculata prior to the heatwave, suggesting that this symbiont lineage would have had to have been very rare to have gone undetected had it been present in this species before the heatwave.

Post‐Heatwave Symbiont Recovery Can Be Protracted

Recovery of the pre‐heatwave C. latusorum lineage in P. grandis was positively related to the number of years post heatwave (binomial regression, z = 6.692, p < 0.001; Figure 3a). This model predicts that 50% of P. grandis colonies recover the pre‐heatwave lineage 4.44 years after the heatwave and that 95% recovery of the pre‐heatwave lineage across P. grandis colonies is predicted to take almost a decade (9.38 years) (Figure 3a). Similar, but shorter, timelines are observed for the recovery of both lineages in the genus Cladocopium (50% of colonies = 2.06 years; 95% of colonies = 8.11 years; Figure 3b). The recovery of the pre‐heatwave symbiont lineage in P. grandis was largely driven by colonies that were first observed and sampled, and likely recruited, after the heatwave, rather than switching or shuffling of symbionts in surviving tagged corals (Figure 3c; Figure S2). In total, 98 of the 113 colonies (86.7%) first sampled in 2023 hosted the pre‐heatwave symbiont lineage. However, this proportion was smaller for corals classified as survivors (63.6%), and a significant difference in symbiont assemblages was observed when surviving colonies were compared to the rest of the 2023 P. grandis population (PERMANOVA: R 2 = 0.07, p = 0.002; Figure S6).

Human Disturbance Impedes Recovery of Pre‐Heatwave Symbionts

Finally, the recovery of the pre‐heatwave symbiont lineage was significantly negatively related to the exposure level to chronic local human disturbance at a given site (binomial regression, z = −3.642, p < 0.001; Figure 4a). These differences mirror enduring elevated Durusdinium RRA at very high disturbance sites observed when samples collected in 2023 were compared to samples collected pre‐heatwave (Wilcoxon rank‐sum test, w = 685.5, p = 0.015; Figure 4b), in part because of higher survival of colonies exclusively hosting Durusdinium at these sites. Durusdinium was not hosted exclusively in any colony of either species before the heatwave and was observed in only 2% of samples (n = 4) with RRA above 50% (Figure 4b). Yet, in 2023, 4 P. grandis colonies (3.4%) classified as survivors, 5 P. grandis colonies (4.2%) first sampled in 2023, and 1 M. aequituberculata colony (33%) exclusively hosted Durusdinium (Figure 4b; Figures S2 and S3).

Discussion

Our decade‐long survey of 856 coral samples revealed that coral symbioses in both species were entirely disrupted, concomitant with enormous losses (> 98%) in coral cover associated with a prolonged MHW. The recovery of coral cover and pre‐heatwave symbioses was negligible in both species for at least 3 years after the heatwave (2017–2019) and by 7.5 years after the heatwave (2023) was only observed in P. grandis and not M. aequituberculata , a species driven close to local extinction by the MHW. Significant changes to the incidence and abundance of the heat‐tolerant symbiont genus Durusdinium remained at a subset of reef sites in both species after the MHW. In P. grandis , higher Durusdinium abundance was associated with colonies that survived the heatwave and colonies at sites exposed to high human disturbance. Here we discuss factors underpinning the long‐term impacts of the MHW on these two coral species and the implications of these heterogeneous recovery trajectories for the conservation and restoration of coral reefs in an age of intensifying environmental transformation.

Our study indicates that 7 years of ambient temperatures is not a sufficient time for the recovery of some populations of coral species and their symbionts after a major heatwave. By 2023, M. aequituberculata coral cover remained negligible or non‐existent across sites, starkly contrasting pre‐heatwave conditions when this competitive coral species dominated many of the atoll's fringing reefs (Baum et al. 2023), as well as significantly contrasting the recovery observed in P. grandis at the same sites and timeframe. The recovery of coral cover after acute disturbance events can take years to decades, a time frame that may now exceed the frequency interval of major disturbance events like MHWs (Hughes et al. 2018), and even if disturbance intervals are long enough for recovery, coral communities can still differ substantially from pre‐heatwave states (Loya et al. 2001; Van Woesik et al. 2011). The same is likely true for symbiont assemblages, but decade‐long investigations at this level are rarely undertaken, and our study is one of the first to evaluate long‐term recovery in the context of symbionts as well as the coral host.

A holobiont‐scale interpretation of recovery further accentuates the differing trajectories of the two species and provides an even more negative outlook for M. aequituberculata . For host‐flexible symbionts, the loss of one host species may pose little risk; however, most symbiont species have co‐evolved with specific hosts, establishing optimal symbiotic exchange of resources in corals (Fabina et al. 2012; Thornhill et al. 2014) and other species relying on heritable symbioses (Bennett and Moran 2015). Both coral species in this study transmit symbionts vertically and host highly co‐evolved symbionts that, apart from a few rare observations in other scleractinian and antipatharian corals (Claar, McDevitt‐Irwin, et al. 2020; Wagner et al. 2011), are reciprocally host‐specific at Kiritimati (Claar, McDevitt‐Irwin, et al. 2020) and more generally across the Pacific (Thornhill et al. 2014). The recovery of P. grandis coral cover was accompanied by widespread re‐establishment of pre‐heatwave symbionts (101 of 119 colonies in 2023), whereas the heatwave‐driven collapse and limited recovery of M. aequituberculata contributed to the loss of Montipora‐C31 symbioses. Although some coral endosymbionts may persist transiently outside hosts, symbionts with high levels of host integration, such as C31, are thought to lack this capacity (Pochon et al. 2010). The scarcity of alternative ecological niches may have led to a reef‐wide loss of C31, and the few M. aequituberculata colonies sampled after the heatwave hosted Durusdinium and C15. C15 symbionts, occasionally referred to as a thermally tolerant generalist lineage (Thornhill et al. 2014), have been observed in Montipora corals elsewhere and have distributions associated with warmer waters (LaJeunesse et al. 2010). The cumulative effect of mass mortality events and transformations of symbiont assemblages in coral communities recovering from MHW with more thermally tolerant lineages poses a significant risk to Symbiodiniaceae‐coral relationships. Evolutionarily distinct symbiont lineages that specifically rely on threatened host species for survival may be co‐endangered or at risk of replacement with more fit generalist symbiont species (Bennett and Moran 2015; Kiers et al. 2010). Apart from simulations (Doña and Johnson 2020; Fabina et al. 2013), the risk of symbiont extinction in corals is not well studied, in part due to the lack of long‐term studies at this scale of biodiversity.

Longitudinally tracking corals for 7 years post‐heatwave also provides a novel view of the rapid, dynamic, and persistent ecological processes associated with reef recovery. The widespread recovery of the P. grandis pre‐heatwave symbiont lineage appears to have arisen from recruits seeded by corals that survived in deeper waters or unsurveyed cooler refugia. The reproductive strategies and larval dynamics of many Pocillopora species facilitate rapid recovery following significant disturbance events (Edmunds et al. 2019; Lachs et al. 2024; Tsounis and Edmunds 2016). This species' ability to vertically transmit symbionts via eggs or larvae that then disperse great distances (Thornhill et al. 2017), should similarly facilitate the rapid recolonization of symbiont communities from refugia, especially in habitat generalist species like P. grandis that exist across a wide range of reef types and depths (Burgess et al. 2024). Contrasting this recovery of pre‐heatwave symbiont lineages, we observed the persistence of Durusdinium in surviving colonies re‐sampled at later stages of the survey and other Cladocopium lineages, C15 in M. aequituberculata and a second distinct C. latusorum lineage in P. grandis , that first appeared in our survey early in the heatwave. Previous studies have shown that adult and juvenile corals can host different symbiont assemblages on the same reef, but this has primarily been attributed to differential uptake of symbionts from the environment (Ali et al. 2019; Quigley et al. 2017), rather than differences in the timing of a colony's establishment. The post‐heatwave recovery of seaweeds (Gurgel et al. 2020) and soil microbiomes in forests recovering after wildfires and in reforested ecosystems (Dove et al. 2022; Wall, Egan, et al. 2020) reveals differences in the genetic diversity of populations adapted for surviving disturbance events and those that thrive and re‐establish once ambient conditions are restored. However, contrasting trends have also been observed, such as during a severe outbreak of sea star wasting disease in which genomic variation was consistent in surviving adults and recruits (Schiebelhut et al. 2018). This variation in recovery trajectories likely stems from differences in the magnitude of the disturbance. High mortality can create space for the recruitment of species with genotypes distinct from the pre‐disturbance population (Gurgel et al. 2020). However, we show that this is not always the case, and for the population of P. grandis surveyed in this study, it is the surviving colonies that show post‐heatwave differences in symbiont composition, particularly on highly disturbed reefs where recruitment is low, preventing the recovery of the pre‐heatwave lineage from thermal refugia. Long‐term monitoring and thermal refugia assessment strategies developed for host corals (Beyer et al. 2018; McClanahan et al. 2024) could be adapted for coral symbioses to better understand dissimilarities in coral reef resilience and recovery across heatwave intensities and guide management.

Our results also indicate that the recovery of pre‐heatwave coral symbioses is delayed at sites exposed to high human disturbance. Durusdinium incidence and RRA remained significantly higher than observed pre‐heatwave at sites exposed to high human disturbance. The heatwave‐induced transformation of symbiont communities to heat‐tolerant Durusdinium lineages in our study is consistent with most other studies of symbiont communities exposed to significant heat stress (Baker 2001; Cunning, Silverstein, and Baker 2015; Cunning, Yost, et al. 2015), as well as the response observed across reefs recovering from recurrent heatwaves (Berkelmans and Van Oppen 2006; LaJeunesse et al. 2009; Palacio‐Castro et al. 2023). However, the duration of time that Durusdinium persists in colonies after MHWs end is not well understood and has been hypothesized to range from 2 years to decades (LaJeunesse et al. 2009; Palacio‐Castro et al. 2023). The persistence of heat‐tolerant generalists may have long‐lasting fitness consequences as ecosystem models (Ortiz et al. 2013), controlled experiments (Cunning, Silverstein, and Baker 2015; Matsuda et al. 2023), and in situ surveys (Wall, Kaluhiokalani, et al. 2020) suggest colonies hosting Durusdinium can experience reduced metabolic performance relative to colonies hosting Cladocopium under ambient water temperatures. Reducing local human disturbance is known to improve the recovery of reefs (Carilli et al. 2009; Côté and Darling 2010), and large‐scale transitions to Durusdinium dominance may represent a less resilient reef state (Scott et al. 2024), and can lead to higher coral mortality (Claar, Starko, et al. 2020). Notably, all but one colony that hosted this genus prior to the heatwave perished in our survey. The potential impacts of Durusdinium are nuanced and persistent natural variation in the proportion of corals hosting Durusdinium across depth and light gradients exists across many reefs (Burgess et al. 2024; Cunning, Yost, et al. 2015; Innis et al. 2018), suggesting these symbiotic relationships can be beneficial long‐term under certain environmental conditions (Kemp et al. 2023; Turnham et al. 2023). While addressing climate change requires a globally coordinated effort beyond the scope of reef managers and conservation authorities, our results suggest that mitigating other local stressors may help preserve symbiont biodiversity and provide corals with an array of symbiotic lineages optimal for the resilience of these species to future stressors.

Conclusions

The increasing frequency and intensity of marine heatwaves has the potential to prevent typical recovery trajectories needed to restore pre‐heatwave states in ecosystems such as coral reefs (Connell 1978; Hughes et al. 2018). We found that these trends make the recovery of symbiont communities in corals increasingly unlikely due to the protracted duration of the recovery of pre‐heatwave symbionts, the potential for local extinctions of host‐adapted symbiont species, and the additional delays in recovery associated with chronic stressors. Long‐term or permanent shifts in symbiotic relationships are predicted to have negative impacts on corals (Scott et al. 2024), potentially amplifying the effects of these microscopic changes in symbiont assemblages to broader‐scale transformations of coral reefs and marine biodiversity as a whole.
