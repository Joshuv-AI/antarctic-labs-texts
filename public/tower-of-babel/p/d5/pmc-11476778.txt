Biopharmaceutical pipeline funded by venture capital firms, 2014 to 2024
Authors: So-Yeon Kang, Mingqian Liu, Jeromie Ballreich, Ravi Gupta, Gerard Anderson
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractVenture capital (VC) firms fund biopharmaceutical research and development (R&D) while incurring substantial financial risk. VC firms seek to invest in clinical areas with the greatest potential for financial return. Using a combination of data for clinical trials and VC investment deals between January 2014 and March 2024, we found that approximately 75% of VC investments were allocated to clinical trials studying small-molecule drugs compared to biologics or gene therapies, without substantial changes over the study period. Most of VC firms’ investment in biopharmaceutical R&D was concentrated in phase 1 and phase 2 clinical trials. This trend has increased in recent years, with phase 1 trials accounting for nearly half of total deals and capital investments in 2023. VC investments were concentrated in several therapeutic areas, including cancer.Keywords: biopharmaceutical industry, prescription drug policy, investment in research and development, drug development

Introduction

Recent public policies have raised concerns about their potential adverse impact on the level and characteristics of biopharmaceutical research and development (R&D). The Trump Administration's International Pricing Index proposal, the Inflation Reduction Act, and several states’ Prescription Drug Affordability Boards have all focused on different categories of drugs manufactured by the biopharmaceutical industry. Investors in pharmaceutical drug development must consider these policies and their potential impact when making investment decisions.

Venture capital (VC) firms invest in a wide range of industries beyond biopharmaceuticals. These firms invest in industries and innovative products that they believe will accrue the greatest financial return, such as self-driving cars, artificial intelligence, and financial services. Venture capital firms invest funds for a set period of time into small, privately held companies that have limited tangible assets, typically when the companies do not generate revenue, such as during their early R&D stages.1 Venture capital firms seek to achieve the highest possible returns, although most ventures ultimately fail—only 1 in 5 American startup companies survive beyond 5 years.1 Venture capital firms will invest in biopharmaceuticals vs other industries based on relative potential returns. Their role is critical, as a substantial proportion of new drug development, particularly preclinical and early-phase clinical trials, was funded by VC firms and a change in their investment strategies could influence new drug development.2

Biopharmaceutical companies without established revenue streams, such as startup companies, in the preclinical and clinical research stage rely heavily on VC funding to sustain their R&D efforts, including initiation of clinical trials.3 Venture capital firms’ funding has an average duration of 18 to 24 months, and their decision to invest or divest is based on the projected profitability, clinical promise, and the valuation of the investee companies during each funding round.1 Venture capital firms must decide whether to invest in specific clinical areas such as cancer and whether to invest in small molecules, biologics, or gene therapies. However, it remains unknown whether policies influencing the profitability of the pharmaceutical industry and of specific product pipelines affect the investment portfolios of venture capitalists. While a recent analysis examined trends in “early-stage” and “late-stage” (ie, funding rounds for startup companies such as Series A and B) VC investments in biopharmaceutical products,4 little evidence exists regarding changes over time in clinical trial–stage drug development and the characteristics of these drugs funded by VC firms.

In this study, we examined the changes in characteristics of the biopharmaceutical R&D pipeline funded by VC firms between January 2014 and March 2024 using clinical trials data and proprietary VC investment deals data.

Data and methods

Data sources

We used proprietary VC deal history data sourced from PitchBook, a leading third-party investment data provider, which tracks global VC investment deals across all industries and has been used in prior peer-reviewed research on VC activities and the National Venture Capital Association's quarterly report.5-9 Pitchbook data also provide clinical trials records sourced from ClinicalTrials.gov for the firms that received VC investment and are included in the parent deal data. Using VC deal and clinical trials data, we identified each investee company's primary industry, business areas of focus, size of each deal measured in US dollars, and date of the investment deal. The data exclude companies without a record of VC investment. Research in financial markets uses a similar approach and acknowledges the limitation of such data.10

We then used the Clarivate Cortellis Drug Database to classify the conditions/diseases studied in the clinical trials into therapeutic categories.10,11 The combination of these 3 data sources allowed us to analyze differences by drug categories (small molecules, biological drugs, cell and gene therapy), start and completion dates of clinical trials, clinical trial phases, and disease conditions tested in the clinical trial and related primary therapeutic area. We classified combined phase 1/2 trials as phase 2 and combined phase 2/3 trials as phase 3, in line with the approach used in other studies.12

Study sample

We first identified all VC investments in companies with clinical research–stage biopharmaceutical products from January 2014 to March 2024 and focused on investment deals of 1 million US dollars or more, adjusted for inflation to 2024.13 The sample selection flow is described in Appendix Figure S1.

To create the study cohort, we identified biotechnology and pharmaceutical companies based on their primary industry information. Among these, we then identified companies that received investment from VC firms at any time from January 2014 to March 2024. If a company did not have any clinical trials recorded during the period, the company was excluded from the sample.

Identifying the R&D pipeline associated with the VC investment

A biopharmaceutical company can have multiple, simultaneous deals with ongoing clinical trials that fund 1 or more different drugs in development. However, information on whether an investment is tied to a specific drug, and if so, which drug, is not available. In order to capture information about the drug pipeline of the company at the time of the investment, we constructed investment company–deal–clinical trial combinations. Startup companies must raise sufficient capital to sustain their operations until the next round of funding (eg, Series B and C) while maintaining ownership of the product. The average wait time between funding rounds in 2023 was 24 to 30 months.14 When a VC identifies a promising investment, a new fund-raising round generally occurs with specific near-term R&D milestones, such as the initiation of a phase 1, 2, or 3 clinical trial, to reach the next phase of funding. Given the intervals between investment deals and between those deals and clinical trials, we implemented the following identification strategy.

There are 2 types of deal cohorts based on the status of associated clinical trials: pre-initiation clinical trials and ongoing clinical trials. We assumed that a company's product pipeline is represented by both pre-initiation and ongoing clinical trials at the time of the VC deal. The pre-initiation cohort was defined as new clinical trials for small molecules, biologicals, or gene therapies that were initiated sometime during the subsequent 2 years (24 calendar months) after receiving VC funding (cohort 1). We chose this time period based on the average duration of 2 years between funding rounds.14 For example, if a deal date is August 2019, we assigned the deal to the company's new clinical trials started between August 2019 and July 2021.

For the ongoing clinical trials cohort, we identified trials that had already begun but were not yet completed when the second round of funding started. We identified any additional fund-raising deals before the primary completion date of the trial (cohort 2).

We combined these 2 cohorts of company–deal–clinical trial combinations that together represent the clinical-stage R&D efforts of the company at the time of the funding. The data structure is described in the Appendix Methods.

Analysis

We described the R&D characteristics associated with new investment deals initiated by VC firms for each year from January 2014 to March 2024. We examined trends in R&D characteristics, as follows: (1) clinical trial intervention type (small molecules, biologics, and gene therapies), (2) clinical trial phase, and (3) therapeutic areas of focus (eg, cancer, cardiovascular diseases).

To understand the size of investment committed based on different R&D characteristics, we computed the mean and median capital invested per deal. All investment estimates were inflation adjusted to 2024 dollars using the US Consumer Price Index. We examined changes in total capital invested and median size of capital invested per deal by clinical trial intervention type, clinical trial phase, and therapeutic area. Since the total level of VC investment is subject to many factors, most notably interest rates, we also examined relative investment changes.15 We computed the percentage of (1) number of deals for each clinical trial phase, intervention type, and therapeutic areas and (2) total capital investment using the total number of deals and the total capital invested that occurred in a given year as the denominator by the same set of R&D characteristics.

To address investment deals that were linked to multiple clinical trials involving different types of drugs and phases, we classified deals into those involving phase 1, 2, or 3 trials exclusively and those spanning 2 or more phases. Likewise, we categorized deals into those involving small molecules, biological agents, or gene therapies exclusively, or those involving 2 or more product categories.

Results

We identified 3749 VC deals associated with 6501 clinical trials involving 1936 clinical-stage biopharmaceutical companies receiving VC investment from January 1, 2014, to April 3, 2024 (Appendix Figure S1). In our study sample, the VC deals were concentrated in biopharmaceutical companies located in the United States (57.4%), followed by those in Europe and Canada (20.7%), Asia (19.1%), and other countries (2.8%). The mean investment per deal was $40 million and the median was $20 million. Cancer-focused deals accounted for 27% of all deals, while 45% of deals involved phase 1 clinical trials and 74% involved small molecules (Table 1).

Characteristics of biopharmaceutical research and development pipeline funded by VC firms over time

The total capital invested for small-molecule drug–related clinical trials was $5.8 billion in 2014, which peaked in 2021 ($25.6 billion), and then decreased to $10.0 billion in 2023. The total capital invested for biologic product–related clinical trials showed a similar trend ($1.4 billion in 2014, $7.7 billion in 2021, and $791.05 million in 2023) (Figure 1). Venture capital firms’ investments in the clinical-stage biopharmaceutical pipeline remained concentrated in clinical trials studying small-molecule drugs from 73.8% in 2014 to 76.1% in 2023 and increasing slightly (78.2%) in the first quarter of 2024 (Appendix Figure S2). The median capital invested per deal involving small-molecule drug–related clinical trials increased from $17.9 million (interquartile range [IQR], $7.92–$39.60 million) in 2014 to $20.60 million in 2023 ($9.45–$60.62 million) after adjusting for inflation (Appendix Figure S3).

Venture capital firms’ investments in the clinical-stage biopharmaceutical pipeline were concentrated in phase 1 and 2 clinical trials, with greater concentration in recent years. The total capital invested in phase 1 clinical trials increased from $2.5 billion in 2014 to $5.7 billion in 2023, while the amount invested in phase 3 decreased from $486.85 million in 2014 and $431.63 million in 2023 (Appendix Figure S4). The percentage of VC deals investing in phase 1 clinical trials increased from 35.3% in 2014 to 49.8% in 2023, with a higher percentage (62.8%) in the first quarter of 2024 (Figure 2). Similarly, the share of total capital invested in phase 1 clinical trials increased from 30.6% in 2014 to 44.7% in 2023, and 61.2% in the first quarter of 2024. The median capital invested per deal involving phase 1 clinical trials increased from $16 million (IQR, $7.92–$33.58 million) in 2014 to $21.0 million (IQR, $8.59–$52.24 million) in 2023, and increased to $57.0 million (IQR, $16.23–$120.0 million) in the first quarter of 2024 (Appendix Figure S5).

Discussion

The growth of funding in companies conducting phase 1 clinical trials suggests that VCs are increasingly willing to invest in startup companies when the risk of failure is greatest. The recent increase in large pharmaceutical firms’ acquisition of startup companies may have influenced interest in VC firms engaged in early-clinical-stage endeavors,16 as the pharmaceutical firms aimed to secure their share in the VC firm early enough to receive a financial return on their investment.

Despite an increase in the funding for gene therapy–related clinical trials, clinical trials involving small-molecule drugs continued to account for the greatest proportion of the biopharmaceutical pipeline funded by VC firms. Venture capital funding was highly concentrated in specific therapeutic areas, including cancer and neurology/psychiatry drugs, which is substantially different from the allocation of National Institutes of Health (NIH) funding or disease burdens reported in prior research.17 This suggests that Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and payment incentives may influence VC investments as well as the science that NIH funds. Policy strategies to increase the relevance of VC investment in biopharmaceuticals that hold a greater potential to improve public health will be an important area for further research.

As seen in our study, clinical trials funded by VC firms take place in companies across various countries. Given the global nature of pharmaceutical drug R&D, the impact of US policy changes is not confined to the US market. However, US policies will have a large impact on investment decisions since a large share of profits from the biopharmaceutical companies are earned in the United States. Future research should explore potential differences in market and R&D responses to these policy changes across the global market.

This study has several limitations. First, we used proprietary VC investment data and validating the coverage and accuracy of the data may be challenging given the private nature of investment activities. However, in contrast to other data sources, such as Crunchbase, we found that Pitchbook data provide the most comprehensive coverage and reliability as evidenced by its wide adoption in various peer-reviewed health care–related publications and reputable business and research organizations’ reports.5-9 Second, we conducted a descriptive analysis and did not examine causal relationships between VC investment and R&D characteristics. Third, our analysis focuses on the characteristics of the biopharmaceutical pipeline for companies receiving VC investment, using individual deals as the unit of analysis. Future research is warranted to compare the initiation and characteristics of clinical trials sponsored by various funding sources. Fourth, we focused on examining characteristics of the biopharmaceutical R&D pipeline funded by VC firms and did not estimate the investment made before clinical trials. Since our study includes investment deals that fund phase 2 or phase 3 clinical trials, the life stages of the companies included in the study varied. Future research is warranted to examine how capital investment in preclinical research fosters successful transition to the clinical stage. Fifth, this study did not examine the relationship between specific pricing policies and regulatory policies, which can influence the biopharmaceutical R&D pipeline.
