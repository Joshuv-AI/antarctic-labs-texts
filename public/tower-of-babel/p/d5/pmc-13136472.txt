Development and prognostic evaluation of an integrative programmed cell death index in pancreatic cancer based on multiple cell death modalities
Authors: Junqian Zhang, Lei Ni, Shuoguo Li, Ruinuo Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pancreatic cancer has exceptionally high mortality and is often clinically silent early on, with outcomes remaining poor; however, reliable tools that substantially refine prognostic stratification are still scarce. Programmed cell death (PCD) is intimately involved in tumor progression and treatment response and may provide clinically informative biomarkers. In this study, we systematically integrated genes related to 14 PCD modalities to develop a cell death index (CDI) in the TCGA cohort, followed by external validation in three independent GEO cohorts (GSE62452, GSE28735, and GSE57495). We further characterized associations between CDI-defined risk groups and the tumor microenvironment as well as predicted drug sensitivity. An eight-gene CDI signature was established and enabled effective risk stratification: patients in the high-CDI group exhibited significantly worse overall survival, and principal component analysis demonstrated clear separation between high- and low-CDI groups. Immune profiling indicated that the high-CDI group displayed an immune-cold phenotype with globally reduced immune infiltration, and drug sensitivity prediction suggested lower responsiveness to commonly used chemotherapeutic agents, including oxaliplatin and gemcitabine. Collectively, the CDI provides a practical framework for prognostic assessment and potential therapeutic stratification in pancreatic cancer, and may facilitate subsequent mechanistic studies and the development of individualized treatment strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-04846-z.

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer remains one of the most lethal malignancies, characterized by aggressive tumor biology and an insidious clinical presentation with largely non-specific symptoms. The median survival is approximately 4 months, and the 5-year survival rate is about 13% [1]. In 2022, pancreatic cancer accounted for an estimated 510,566 new cases worldwide, representing 2.6% of all incident cancers and ranking 12th by incidence, and for 467,005 deaths, representing 4.8% of all cancer deaths and ranking 6th by mortality—underscoring its disproportionate fatality burden [2]. In this context, prior work has emphasized that improving outcomes will require more effective experimental and computational models—together with machine-learning–enabled interrogation of the tumor microenvironment, alongside emerging biomarkers such as piRNAs—to better inform therapeutic development and optimization [3, 4].

Cell death is generally divided into accidental cell death (ACD) and programmed cell death (PCD). Notably, PCD encompasses a spectrum of mechanistically distinct modalities, including apoptosis, pyroptosis, necroptosis, ferroptosis, and other forms of regulated cell death [5]. NETosis is a neutrophil-driven death program defined by chromatin/DNA release and the formation of extracellular web-like structures [6], whereas alkaliptosis has been proposed more recently and is associated with metabolic dysfunction under alkaline conditions [7]. Collectively, PCD constitutes a fundamental host defense that restricts malignant cell survival and dissemination; however, tumor cells frequently circumvent these constraints through diverse strategies, including genetic alterations or epigenetic modifications affecting key regulators of PCD pathways [8]. Despite this conceptual framework, the relationship between PCD and pancreatic cancer remains incompletely delineated, with limited evidence defining disease-specific functional roles and relatively few systematic efforts to integrate multiple death programs in this context. Therefore, we propose a new metric, the cell death index (CDI), to enhance prognostic stratification in pancreatic cancer. This work characterizes inter-patient heterogeneity and links it to clinical outcomes to support more informed selection of targeted immunotherapeutic strategies.

Materials and methods

Data collection

PCD-related genes cover diverse programmed cell death modalities, including apoptosis, necroptosis, ferroptosis, pyroptosis, NETosis (netotic cell death), entotic cell death, lysosome-dependent cell death, parthanatos, autophagy-dependent cell death, oxeiptosis, disulfidptosis, cuproptosis, anoikis, and alkaliptosis. These genes were curated using GSEA gene sets and KEGG resources, supplemented by relevant review articles and additional manual curation, and after integrating genes from the 14 PCD-related programs, duplicates appearing across multiple modalities were removed for downstream analyses [5, 9]. Importantly, the final gene catalog systematically integrates regulatory genes spanning the 14 PCD programs. Specifically, we included 580 apoptosis-related genes, 52 pyroptosis-related genes, 88 ferroptosis-related genes, 367 autophagy-related genes, 101 necroptosis-related genes, 14 cuproptosis-related genes, 9 parthanatos-related genes, 15 entotic cell death–related genes, 8 netotic cell death–related genes, 220 lysosome-dependent cell death–related genes, 7 alkaliptosis-related genes, 35 anoikis-related genes, 5 oxeiptosis-related genes, and 16 disulfidptosis-related genes. In total, 1,517 genes (Supplementary Table 1) linked to programmed cell death (PCD) pathways were included for downstream analyses.

For subsequent analyses, TCGA RNA-seq expression profiles quantified as TPM (transcripts per million) were obtained and consolidated through the University of California, Santa Cruz (UCSC) database, including 176 pancreatic cancer samples and 4 normal samples. All expression values were log-transformed. To ensure accurate gene annotation, Ensembl IDs were converted to official gene symbols using the AnnoProbe R package, and corresponding clinical characteristics were obtained from the TCGA official portal. In addition, for external validation, we assembled an independent GEO cohort including microarray expression data (log-transformed) and matched clinical information from 170 pancreatic cancer patients (GSE62452, GSE28735, and GSE57495), as well as a single-cell RNA-seq dataset (GSE278688) obtained from the GEO database, and probe annotation and mapping were likewise completed using the AnnoProbe R package.

Expression profiling and annotation of PCD-related genes

In the TCGA-PAAD cohort, we compiled raw transcriptome data from 176 patients with pancreatic cancer and 4 normal tissue samples. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the “limma” R package [10], with thresholds of adjusted p < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 1. DEGs were visualized using the “pheatmap” and “ggplot” packages in R.

Construction of the cell death index

We intersected 1517 PCD-related genes with the DEGs derived from the dataset. The candidate gene set was further reduced and an optimal gene signature was established using LASSO Cox regression; the parameter corresponding to “lambda.min” was selected with the “glmnet” R package [11]. The CDI for each patient was calculated as: CDI = Σ(βi × Ei), where βi denotes the risk regression coefficient for each gene and Ei represents the corresponding gene expression level. For intuitive presentation, patients with pancreatic cancer were stratified into low- and high-CDI groups according to the median CDI value. In this context, a clustered heatmap integrating clinical features and CDI values was generated using the “pheatmap” package. Associations between CDI groups and clinical characteristics were evaluated using the Wilcoxon test, t test, and analysis of variance (ANOVA), as appropriate.

Functional enrichment analysis

Biological pathways potentially implicated by the DEGs and the genes selected by LASSO regression were identified using the “clusterProfiler” R package [12]. Additionally, GSVA and GSEA Base packages were applied to assess biological functional differences between the high and low CDI groups [12].

Tumor microenvironment analysis and drug sensitivity prediction

We assessed associations of CDI with immunomodulators and immune cell populations. Notably, single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) and the CIBERSORT deconvolution algorithm were used to infer tissue cellular composition from gene expression profiles [13]. We further performed drug sensitivity prediction using the R package “oncoPredict” [14], which leverages genomic profiles of cancer cell lines and their drug response data to estimate the sensitivity of patient samples.

Model training and external validation of the gene signature

External validation was performed using GEO datasets. Scatter plots illustrating the association between CDI stratification and survival outcomes were generated with the “ggplot” R package. PCA was carried out with the “stats” package. Kaplan–Meier survival analyses were conducted with the “survival” and “survminer” R packages to evaluate the relationship between overall survival (OS) and CDI. Accordingly, time-dependent ROC analyses for CDI were performed using the “timeROC” R package [15].

Development and evaluation of the nomogram survival model

Clinical features and CDI values for TCGA pancreatic cancer patients were collected, and univariable and multivariable Cox regression analyses were conducted. In this context, prognostic nomograms and calibration curves were developed using the “rms” and “survival” R packages [16]. Time-dependent ROC analyses for the nomogram were conducted with the “timeROC” R package [15]. Notably, the calibration curves were further used to evaluate the predictive validity of the ROC-based discrimination performance.

Based on the nomogram-derived risk estimates, participants in each dataset were stratified into high and low risk groups using the mean risk score calculated within the respective dataset as the cutoff, and Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated for these risk strata.

Single cell analysis

Single-cell RNA-seq data from GSE278688 were obtained from the GEO database and processed using the Seurat package [17]. Quality control filtering was performed to remove cells with fewer than 200 or more than 6,000 detected genes, UMI counts > 25,000, or mitochondrial UMI percentages > 25%. Tumor samples were selected for subsequent analyses. Data normalization and variance stabilization were performed using SCTransform, followed by dimensionality reduction with PCA and UMAP. Cell clustering was conducted using the FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions.

Statistical analysis

R version 4.5.0 was used for all statistical procedures. Two-group differences were evaluated with either the t test or the Wilcoxon test, whereas multi-group comparisons were examined by ANOVA. Kaplan–Meier plots were used to present survival, and the log-rank test was used to test for between-group differences. A two-sided p value below 0.05 was considered significant.

Construction of a prognostic gene signature for pancreatic cancer

We obtained transcriptomic profiles and matched survival information for the TCGA pancreatic cancer cohort. After intersecting TCGA-derived DEGs with the PCD-related gene set, we developed a prognostic model in the TCGA cohort using LASSO Cox regression and established an 8-gene signature (CEACAM6, MUC1, ERBB3, PERP, ARRB2, ADGRE2, TRAF1, and CD300A). In the final LASSO Cox model, genes with positive regression coefficients (β > 0) were defined as risk-associated genes, whereas those with negative coefficients (β < 0) were considered protective genes. Among these genes, five were derived from apoptosis, two from lysosome-dependent cell death, and one from anoikis (Fig. 3A, B). These genes are implicated in pancreatic cancer development and progression and may also be involved in immune microenvironment remodeling, metastasis, and drug tolerance. We then constructed a scoring formula based on the expression of these genes to calculate the CDI for each patient, and Fig. 3C illustrates the relationship between gene expression patterns and CDI. Our results show that CDI was significantly associated with overall stage and prognosis but showed no significant associations with sex, age, or individual T/N/M stages, suggesting that CDI primarily captures molecular features related to tumor cell death and may represent a potential independent prognostic factor (Fig. 3D–K).

Internal training and external validation of the predictive signature

We compared overall survival (OS) between pancreatic cancer patients with different CDI levels. The high CDI group exhibited a lower OS rate than the low CDI group (Fig. 4A). Principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrated clear separation between the two groups, and samples in the high CDI group formed a more compact cluster, supporting greater within-group consistency (Fig. 4B). Kaplan–Meier analysis further indicated a significant OS difference, with improved survival in the low CDI group (p < 0.001; Fig. 4C). For external validation, we used the merged GSE28735 and GSE57495 cohort and GSE62452 as two independent cohorts, and patients were dichotomized into high and low CDI groups using the median CDI within each cohort. Higher CDI was consistently associated with shorter survival time (Fig. 4A). Similarly, PCA showed distinct separation (Fig. 4B), and Kaplan–Meier analyses indicated better OS and lower mortality in the low CDI group (Fig. 4C). In addition, ROC analyses across the three cohorts supported the accuracy of CDI in predicting 2-, 3-, and 4-year OS in pancreatic cancer patients (Fig. 4D). In the training set, the AUC values at 2, 3, and 4 year OS were 0.741, 0.784, and 0.838, respectively, and the highest AUC in the validation sets reached 0.74.

Functional enrichment and immune analyses associated with CDI groups

We performed functional enrichment analyses for different CDI groups in the TCGA cohort. GO analysis indicated that DEGs were mainly enriched in biological processes related to cell adhesion, extracellular matrix organization/remodeling, and wound healing, and were significantly enriched in cellular components such as extracellular matrix and focal adhesions/adherens junctions, as well as molecular functions including cadherin binding and integrin/actin binding (p < 0.001; Fig. 5A). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment of DEGs between CDI strata in the PI3K–Akt and MAPK signaling pathways (p < 0.001; Fig. 5B), suggesting that intergroup differences may be linked to canonical survival/proliferation and stress-response signaling axes. Moreover, the high CDI group showed a tendency toward stronger tumor proliferative activity and aberrant activation of epithelial differentiation/keratinization-related programs (Fig. 5C).

Based on infiltration profiles derived from CIBERSORT and MCP-counter, differences in immune and stromal composition were observed between CDI groups (Fig. 6A, B). CIBERSORT analysis showed an increased proportion of M0 macrophages and a decreased proportion of naive B cells in the high CDI group (Fig. 6C). Notably, the expansion of M0 macrophages, which are considered precursors of tumor-associated macrophages, together with the reduction of naïve B cells, suggests a shift toward a less effective anti-tumor immune response in the high CDI group. Consistently, MCP-counter analysis indicated significantly lower infiltration scores for B lineage, cytotoxic lymphocytes, and total T cells in the high CDI group (Fig. 6D). The marked reduction of these key tumor-killing immune cell populations in the high CDI group suggests a more suppressive tumor microenvironment that may contribute to inferior survival outcomes, in agreement with previous reports [18].

Association between CDI and predicted drug sensitivity

We explored the relationship between CDI and drug sensitivity by estimating half-maximal inhibitory concentrations (IC50) for commonly used agents. Scatter plots and violin plots illustrating the correlations and between group differences for multiple pancreatic cancer drugs (Oxaliplatin, Gemcitabine, Olaparib, Trametinib, Erlotinib, Selumetinib, Afatinib, and MK-2206) are shown in Fig. 7. This analysis revealed that the high CDI group exhibited lower predicted sensitivity to standard pancreatic cancer agents such as Oxaliplatin and Gemcitabine, suggesting that patients with high CDI may derive less benefit from standard therapies and consequently experience poorer overall outcomes. Conversely, the high CDI group showed greater predicted sensitivity to inhibitors targeting the MAPK/EGFR axis, which is consistent with the enrichment of MAPK and adhesion–ECM-related pathways, implying that this subgroup may be more dependent on these signaling routes and may offer potential therapeutic entry points.

Development and evaluation of the nomogram survival model

To assess the independent prognostic value of CDI, we examined its association with outcomes using univariable and multivariable Cox regression models in pancreatic cancer. Univariable Cox regression indicated that CDI (HR = 4.26, 95% CI 2.63–6.92, p < 0.001)was a risk factor, and CDI remained an independent prognostic factor after multivariable adjustment (Fig. 8A, B). In the TCGA cohort, we constructed a nomogram incorporating CDI and N stage using multivariable Cox regression and stepwise regression to predict 2-, 3-, and 4-year OS (Fig. 8C). Kaplan–Meier curves showed significantly worse outcomes in the high score group compared with the low-score group (P < 0.0001; Fig. 8D). ROC analyses yielded AUC values of 0.732, 0.763, and 0.77 for predicting 2-, 3-, and 4-year survival, respectively (Fig. 8E). In addition, calibration curves further supported high accuracy of the nomogram in predicting 2-, 3-, and 4-year survival (Fig. 8F).

CDI-associated heterogeneity at the single-cell level

To further investigate CDI-related heterogeneity in the tumor microenvironment, we performed single-cell analysis using the GSE 278,688 dataset. After quality control and selection of tumor-derived samples, 71,154 cells were retained for downstream analysis. Major cell types were annotated based on canonical marker genes, including NKT cells, B cells, myeloid cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, ductal cells, mast cells, and acinar cells (Fig. 9A). Bubble plots of canonical marker genes supported the reliability of cell-type annotation (Fig. 9B). UMAP visualization showed that CDI scores were unevenly distributed across cell populations (Fig. 9C), and violin plots further showed relatively higher CDI scores in endothelial cells and fibroblast subsets (Fig. 9D). The eight CDI signature genes displayed distinct cell type–specific expression patterns across major cell populations (Fig. 9E). Taken together, these results indicate that CDI-related transcriptional features are heterogeneously distributed in the tumor microenvironment and are relatively enriched in stromal cell populations.

Discussion

Our study systematically interrogated 14 PCD gene sets in pancreatic cancer through a comprehensive integrative analysis. CDI was developed in the TCGA cohort and subsequently validated in external cohorts. Based on the CDI and clinical variables, the nomogram we further constructed demonstrated robust prognostic performance. We also assessed the associations of the CDI with immune regulation–related metrics and drug sensitivity. Collectively, this model may facilitate risk stratification and prognostic evaluation for patients with pancreatic cancer. It may also provide a foundation for subsequent mechanistic investigations and the identification of potential therapeutic targets.

PCD encompasses diverse mechanisms, and accumulating evidence indicates that PCD is closely linked to the initiation and metastasis of malignant tumors [8]. We established a signature comprising eight PCD-related genes (CEACAM6, MUC1, ERBB3, PERP, ARRB2, ADGRE2, TRAF1, CD300A) and observed that it was predictive of overall survival in pancreatic cancer patients.

The tumor microenvironment is closely associated with tumor initiation and progression and may facilitate evasion of immune surveillance and pharmacologic intervention [19]. Several immune cell activities were significantly elevated in the low-CDI group compared with the high-CDI group, including a greater proportion of activated B cells. Activated B cells exhibit high glycolytic and oxidative phosphorylation activity and may be associated with unfavorable outcomes under immune checkpoint blockade [20]. In addition, the high CDI group showed an increased proportion of macrophages predominantly of the M0 phenotype, and poorer prognosis was associated with higher M0 macrophage abundance, consistent with prior reports [21]. This may relate to the long-standing view that tumor-associated macrophages contribute to pancreatic cancer progression and metastasis [22]. Meanwhile, our analysis indicated that the high CDI group had lower predicted sensitivity to commonly used agents for pancreatic cancer, including oxaliplatin and gemcitabine, suggesting that patients with high CDI may be less likely to benefit from standard therapies and consequently have worse overall outcomes. This phenomenon may be related to enrichment of cancer stem cells and their associated mechanisms of drug resistance; previous studies have demonstrated that ARPC1B-positive cancer stem cells are closely associated with gemcitabine resistance [23].

In addition, analyses based on the CDI indicated that PCD-related genes provided strong and stable survival prediction in patients with pancreatic cancer; overall survival was relatively better in the low CDI group. The 3-year AUC was 0.784 in the TCGA cohort and reached up to 0.74 in the validation cohort. Moreover, after univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses incorporating key clinical characteristics, the CDI was confirmed as an independent prognostic indicator. We constructed a nomogram integrating the CDI with relevant clinical indicators, achieving a 4-year AUC of 0.77 in the TCGA cohort. Taken together, this study delineates the association between PCD and clinical outcomes in pancreatic cancer and offers a reference for mining potential therapeutic targets. The development of the CDI and its implications for predicting immune regulation and drug response may help enable more precise individualized treatment strategies.

Pancreatic cancer is a highly lethal malignancy of the digestive system. Owing to absent or non-specific early symptoms, patients are often diagnosed at an advanced stage, with limited therapeutic benefit; ongoing efforts are exploring modulation of the TME to improve the overall efficacy of multimodal treatment, including immunotherapy [24]. Previous studies of pancreatic cancer and PCD have largely focused on a single cell death modality, for example, elucidating roles in pancreatic cancer development and treatment response from the perspective of ferroptosis-, necroptosis-, or pyroptosis-related molecules [25–27]; however, systematic integration and comprehensive stratification across multiple PCD modalities within a unified framework remain limited. Therefore, in this study, we integrated 14 cell death modalities and constructed a comprehensive CDI scoring system. Based on CDI stratification, we performed enrichment analyses, immune analyses, and drug sensitivity analyses, and developed a clinical prediction model with strong discriminative capacity. Further work is warranted to elucidate the regulatory circuitry connecting genomic alterations with immune infiltration and to identify more effective therapeutic targets that may improve survival and prognosis in patients with pancreatic cancer. In addition, future studies may incorporate genetic causal inference approaches, such as Mendelian randomization, to further explore the potential roles of PCD-related genetic variants in pancreatic cancer development and treatment response, thereby strengthening the biological foundation and translational relevance of the CDI [28].

Conclusions

In summary, we established a pancreatic cancer–specific cell death index, revealed the association of PCD with prognosis in pancreatic cancer, and provided new evidence to support the identification of potential therapeutic targets. The CDI scoring model showed good prognostic discrimination in the pancreatic cancer cohort.
