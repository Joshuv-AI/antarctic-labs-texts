Application of multipoint flexible optical probe for intra-abdominal visceral perfusion monitoring
Authors: Toshio Horiuchi, Atsushi Miki, Yoko Tomioka, Yasunaru Sakuma, Takafumi Ohmiya, Takumi Teratani, Kazuhiro Endo, Itsuro Saito, Wataru Nishimura, Mutsumi Okazaki, Hironori Yamaguchi, Masaki Sekino
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe maintenance of postoperative blood flow is essential to ensure the optimal condition of transplanted organs. Further, early detection of circulatory disturbances is necessary to prevent graft failure, but is complicated by the closed space after surgery. Continuous and prompt inspection of organ blood flow can help to ensure post-operative blood flow monitoring. A recent study described the development of a wearable flexible sensor probe able to measure short-term changes in pulse waves and optical reflectance. Herein, we used this device to assess porcine kidney transplant models, including models of ischemia and congestion. Blood flow and organ surface color were measured using a sensor probe in the ischemia and congestion transplanted kidney models. Changes in the pulse wave and kidney color in the transplanted model were measured using multipoint mapping with a wearable device. The pulse wave signal decreased after arterial and venous clamping, and improved after clamp release. In the color sensor, the red and blue light reflectance signals decreased and increased after arterial clamping, respectively. Overall, these results show that this method effectively provides information to support the verification of ischemia and congestion of intra-abdominal transplanted organs, and can rapidly detect vascular circulatory disorders after surgery.Keywords: Transplantation, Graft failure, Blood flow, Wearable probes, Organ color, Pulse waveSubject terms: Biomedical engineering, Gastrointestinal models, Assay systems

Introduction

Organ transplantation has achieved a high success rate owing to improvements in perioperative and surgical management; however, vascular complications of organ transplantation still threaten patient and graft outcomes1. The direct inspection of transplanted organs cannot be performed postoperatively because of the closed space. Conventional diagnostic methods include patient examinations, blood tests, and ultrasonography. Patient examination involves auscultation and palpation of the abdomen, which is clinically important, but has the disadvantage of not being directly diagnostic.

In patients who have undergone transplantation, the simple detection of circulatory disturbances is insufficient; detecting such disturbances clearly and promptly is necessary, as immediate intervention is required to prevent graft failure2. In terms of diagnostic tests, blood sample tests are reliable, but cannot be performed multiple times a day. Furthermore, the exact time lag between the onset of blood flow disturbances in the transplanted tissue and detectable changes in blood test results currently remains unclear. Ultrasonography is also a reliable and minimally invasive method that can detect circulatory disturbances promptly; however, it requires skilled operators, demands effort for each examination, and necessitates that patients undress to undergo examination, which limits its practicality3. Therefore, novel methods for continuous monitoring of blood flow in intra-abdominal organs are required.

‘Wearables’ are small electronic devices that can be worn on the body to measure the body temperature, blood pressure, blood oxygen, respiratory rate, sound, and electrical activity of the heart, muscles, brain, and skin. Devices designed to solve these problems should have several features, including blood flow monitoring, area detection, portability, and continuous monitoring4. With recent developments in flexible electronic devices, it has become possible to implement multiple small sensors on film substrates5. Film substrates are sufficiently thin, soft, and flexible to conform to tissue surfaces. By combining multiple sensing elements, the use of these devices can enable multipoint and multiparametric blood flow monitoring6. Clinical trials have shown their utility and potential for continuous and multipoint measurement of blood flow in the human body7.

In gastroenterological surgery, direct continuous monitoring of the organ condition is difficult because of the closed space of the organ region. Guo et al. previously demonstrated the feasibility of continuous monitoring using a wearable kidney transplant model8, demonstrating successful direct and continuous monitoring of local tissue oxygenation, pulse oxygenation, and heart rate using a wearable device in a kidney transplant model.

We recently developed a new wearable device for plastic surgery4. which allowed the continuous monitoring of blood flow, temperature, and brightness of skin flaps in a clinical setting. Following this success, we wondered whether the new wearable device could also be applied for the measurement of blood flow in intra-abdominal organs. In this study, we evaluated whether a wearable implantable measurement device with a flexible multipoint sensor probe could distinguish between impaired circulation and normal blood flow in the intra-abdominal organs of large animals.

Discussion

Vascular complications are a major risk factor for renal transplantation, occurring in 3%–15% of cases9–13. Among these, vascular thrombosis is a major cause of early graft loss, accounting for approximately 5% of cases, and can lead to sensitization to human leukocyte antigens, thereby reducing the likelihood of successful re-transplantation14. As such, the early detection and treatment of vascular thrombosis are crucial for preventing graft dysfunction. In particular, transplant renal artery thrombosis and renal vein thrombosis, which occur in 0.2–3.5% and 0.1–4.2% of cases, respectively, require prompt and accurate diagnosis9,15,16.

Currently available techniques, such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), computed tomography (CT), and laser speckle contrast imaging (LSCI) have significant limitations for intra-abdominal monitoring because of the need for large equipment, high costs, and difficulty in accessing internal organs17. As such, there is a strong unmet need for portable devices capable of continuous and real-time monitoring of vascular conditions.

Although conventional postoperative ultrasound examinations can detect differences in blood flow to transplanted organs by examining blood flow itself and vascular resistance, however, they cannot detect differences in pathophysiology, such as congestion or ischemia. Congestion is caused by problems at the renal vein anastomosis itself, thrombus, or microthrombus in the kidney. Microthrombi, in particular, can be caused not only by rejection but also by serious conditions such as thrombotic microangiopathy, making it important to detect them at an early stage. Ischemia is not only a problem at the anastomosis of the renal artery, but is also important in determining the degree of ischemia-reperfusion injury. The device we have developed in this study has the potential to detect subtle differences in the color tone of the organs, which may allow us to further investigate the differences in pathological conditions in the future.

Wearable electronics offer a promising solution for addressing these challenges. Recent advances in nanotechnology and flexible electronics have enabled the development of lightweight, flexible devices capable of non-invasive monitoring6–8. In the present study, we successfully measured the blood flow pulse in the kidney parenchyma from the surface using an electronic device, and detected ischemic and congestive changes through colorimetric observation in a porcine model of vascular complications. This approach allows non-invasive, real-time, and continuous monitoring, which cannot be achieved using conventional imaging modalities.

Two types of hypoxic conditions exist in transplanted organs: ischemia and congestion. Guo et al. previously reported that the difference in the pattern of change in oxygenation was useful for distinguishing between these two conditions8. The experimental protocol induces complete occlusion of the renal artery or vein, creating a binary flow condition. Ischemia in clinical settings exists along a continuous spectrum and that the laboratory conditions do not fully replicate the subtler changes in perfusion seen in human renal transplants. Our primary aim in this study was to establish proof-of-concept for the device’s ability to detect gross perfusion changes. We successfully differentiated between ischemia and congestion by optically observing color changes, and validated this approach in a porcine model of renal ischemia and congestion. Monitoring with our wearable device enables the simultaneous assessment of both colorimetric and hemodynamic changes, thereby offering a new avenue for the early detection of vascular occlusion.

Furthermore, to accurately map blood flow over curved surfaces such as implanted tissues, it is essential to distribute an array of sensors across the target area. Recent developments in the design of flexible electronics have enabled the integration of multiple small sensors on film substrates, thus offering a promising approach for real-time blood flow monitoring in clinical settings7. This technology holds great promise for improving post-transplantation monitoring, ultimately contributing to improved graft survival and patient outcomes. We propose that this device has the potential to benefit all transplant recipients, as transplantation involves the invaluable resource of donor organs and it is essential to maximize the success of the procedure. Its application is particularly critical in patients presenting with risk factors for renal graft thrombosis. According to the literature, such risk factors include donor age (< 6 or > 60 years), intra- or postoperative hemodynamic instability, a history of thrombosis, and cold ischemia time exceeding 24 h18–21.

In conclusion, following transplantation, the abdominal cavity becomes a closed space, making the direct optical assessment of organ viability difficult. Currently, no implantable portable device exists to continuously monitor the blood flow and color changes in transplanted organs. Our flexible, wearable device enables noninvasive, real-time observation of blood flow dynamics and tissue color without the need for reoperation. Continuous and precise monitoring of pulse and color fluctuations may allow the early detection of vascular complications, leading to timely intervention and potentially improved graft survival. Overall, this approach represents a promising advancement toward safer and more effective postoperative management in transplantation medicine.

Methods

Ethics approval

All animal experiments were carried out in accordance with the institutional regulations for animal experiments, based on the governmental Guidelines for the Proper Conduct of Animal Experiments and Related Activities, and were approved by the Institutional Committee of Jichi Medical University (21015-01). The experimental procedures and reporting of this study adhere to the ARRIVE guidelines for animal research.

Structure of the wearable sensor

The wearable device comprising a sensor, cable, data transmitter, and tablet was constructed as previously described7. To obtain pulse waveform data, a reflective optical sensor (NJL5303R, Shinnihonmusen Corp, Japan) consisting of a green light-emitting diode (LED; 570 nm) and a phototransistor was used for each channel. The intensity of the backscattered light varied with changes in blood volume, which is synchronized with the heartbeat. This phenomenon occurs because the incident light emitted from the green LED is partially absorbed as it passes through blood and tissue. The wearable device also included color sensor (P12347, Hamamatsu Photonics, Japan) used to detect the tissue color with a photodiode with three colors of LED light (red, blue, and green) irradiated, reflected, and scattered in the tissue. Compromised circulation was detected when fluctuations were significantly reduced.

Porcine model of compromised circulation

To measure blood flow with this system in an impaired circulation, we used two porcine experimental models of ischemia and congestion. Two females and three male sangenton pigs were used for the ischemia and congestion experimental models (mean weight ± standard deviation: 29.6 ± 3.1 kg, #1–5). Under general anesthesia, the abdomen was opened through a midline incision. The left kidney was exposed and mobilized, and the renal arteries and veins were identified. An ischemic model induced by arterial blood flow obstruction was established by clamping the renal artery using a hemostat on the kidney. The congestion model provoked by venous blood flow obstruction was created by clamping the connecting vein on the kidney using a hemostat. Changes in parameters during the reperfusion period were measured in both models by releasing the clamp. The sensor probe was attached perpendicularly to the kidney surface using a transparent medical film (Tegaderm 3 M, USA). The pulse-wave signal was obtained for 1 min by ligating the artery and vein. Optical reflection measurements were performed during each period. ECG readings were recorded throughout the experiment. Tissue signals were measured in 4 channels of the sensor.

Analysis of measurements

The amplitude of the pulse wave was calculated using FFT analysis. Some parameters were adjusted: the spectrum of 1–2.5 Hz was used because the heart rate of the pig under general anesthesia was distributed over 60–150 beats per minute, while a pulse wave signal of 60 points was used to calculate the pulse power, corresponding to a pulse wave signal of 10 s.

To ensure objective and accurate evaluation of visible light, the color of the object was obtained by measuring the light reflection within the visible light spectrum, and was evaluated using either the color space or color spectrum recommended by the International Commission on Illumination.

The optical reflection components of the sensor probe, which measured the reflected light volume and light-volume outputs, were converted to XYZ values using the equation proposed by Hamamatsu Photonics5. The infrared signal from the color sensor was used to estimate the distance between the sensor and the tissue.

Statistical analysis

Blood flow and color brightness were measured in five pigs for each model. Continuous values were expressed as mean ± standard error. The collected datasets were categorized into pre-ligation (control), ligation (ischemia and congestion), and reperfusion groups. Values were analyzed for statistical significance using Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) and Tukey’s honest significant difference test. JMP version 17.2.0 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) was used for the statistical analyses. The significance level was set at p < 0.05.
