Prognostic impact of cardiac dysfunctions in patients with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction
Authors: Yan Zhou, Jasmine Melissa Marquard, Lars Nepper Christensen, Kiril Aleksov Ahtarovski, Kasper Kyhl, Christoffer Göransson, Lars Køber, Lene Holmvang, Frants Pedersen, Henning Kelbæk, Niels Vejlstrup, Jacob Thomsen Lønborg, Thomas Engstrøm
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe incidence and impact of cardiac dysfunctions after ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) are not understood. We aimed to characterize the prevalence of follow-up systolic and diastolic dysfunction and their associations with long-term outcomes after STEMI.Methods and resultsThis sub-study of the DANAMI-3 trial included 542 patients with STEMI. Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR)-defined systolic/diastolic impairment was applied to define systolic/diastolic dysfunction. Systolic dysfunction was defined as left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) < 50 %, and diastolic dysfunction as peak filling rate/end-diastolic volume (PFR/EDV) ≤ 2.55 by CMR three months after STEMI. The primary outcome was a composite of all-cause mortality and hospitalization for heart failure. Secondary outcomes were individual components of the primary outcome. During a median follow-up of 10.5 years, 103 patients died or were hospitalized for heart failure. A total of 101 and 232 patients had systolic and diastolic dysfunction at three months. Cox regression showed that LVEF was significantly associated with the outcomes, while PFR/EDV was not associated with any outcome. Systolic dysfunction (LVEF<50 %) was associated with the primary endpoint (adjusted hazards ratio [HR] 2.73), all-cause mortality (aHR 2.26), and hospitalization for heart failure (aHR 7.87). The incidences of isolated diastolic dysfunction, isolated systolic dysfunction and both cardiac dysfunctions were 150, 19 and 82. Patients with isolated systolic dysfunction and both dysfunctions were associated with the outcomes.ConclusionsCardiac dysfunctions derived from CMR-defined systolic/diastolic impairment showed in about half of the three-month STEMI-survivors. Systolic dysfunction regardless of diastolic dysfunction is a significant predictor of long-term outcomes after STEMI.Keywords: Systolic dysfunction, Diastolic dysfunction, ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction, All-cause mortality, Hospitalization for heart failure

Introduction

Before the era of mechanical reperfusion, up to 33 % of patients with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) developed subsequent heart failure [1,2]. With the advent and widespread use of timely percutaneous coronary interventions (PCI), the incidence of heart failure after STEMI has decreased significantly [1]. A contemporary cohort has shown that heart failure occurs in 16 % of patients with STEMI after PCI, of whom 11 % require hospitalization [3]. Notably, Heart failure hospitalizations peak at 4 % in the year following STEMI, declining to 1 % annually thereafter [4]. Despite advancements in revascularization, the risk of long-term mortality remains two to three times higher for patients with subsequent heart failure [3,5]. Therefore, therapeutic managements and targets besides timely revascularization are pivotal to improve survival outcome in these patients.

The characterization of different types of heart failure is important to improve the risk stratification and personalize management of heart failure after STEMI. Around 62–76 % of patients with heart failure after myocardial infarction have heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) (left ventricular ejection fraction [LVEF] < 50 %), however low LVEF was shown to be associated with either excess mortality or no significant influence in mortality in different studies [3,6]. The uneven outcomes may result from varying diastolic function which is not accounted for in these studies. The focus on the systolic function is obvious but recent knowledge indicates that diastolic function may also interfere with the clinical course after myocardial infarction. Therefore, studies involving the incidence and impact of overall cardiac dysfunctions including both diastolic and systolic abnormalities are warranted.

Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) allows for the precise measurement of left ventricular volume and peak filling rate (PFR) without geometric assumptions [7]. Peak filling rate/end-diastolic volume (PFR/EDV) has been proven to be a reliable parameter in determining diastolic dysfunction with high sensitivity and specificity [8]. Hence, we aimed to apply CMR-defined systolic/diastolic impairment in a STEMI population to determine (1) the incidence of diastolic and systolic dysfunction in the three-month STEMI-survivors; (2) the impact of follow-up systolic and diastolic dysfunction on long term outcomes.

Methods

Study design and population

This was a sub-study of the DANAMI-3 (Danish Study of Optimal Acute Treatment of Patients with ST-segment Elevation Myocardial Infarction) trial. All patients were admitted for primary PCI between March 2011 to February 2014. Patients with STEMI defined as acute chest pain with<12 h of duration and ST-segment elevation ≥ 0.1 mV in ≥two or more contiguous leads or documented newly developed left bundle branch block on electrocardiogram were included. The DANAMI-3 database comprises detailed clinical data, CMR data after PCI, procedural data, blood samples at baseline, medical prescription at discharge, and clinical outcomes. The rationale and main findings of the DANAMI-3 trial have previously been published [[9], [10], [11], [12]]. The patients with CMR analysis at three months post-STEMI were included in this study, so this sub-study represents relatively healthier post-STEMI survivors.

Cardiac dysfunction definition

CMR-defined systolic/diastolic impairment was applied to define systolic/diastolic dysfunction in this study. The time point of three-month was chosen because most patients exhibit the recovery of left ventricular function three months after acute myocardial infarction under the contemporary treatment [13]. Although we lacked later data on left ventricular function, a previous study found that there was no difference in final left ventricular function observed between patients with early (three-month) improvement and those with late (six-month and twelve-month) improvement [14]. Some patients did not undergo the follow-up CMR scan due to contraindications including claustrophobia, eGFR < 60, ferromagnetic materials, atrial fibrillation or other arrhythmia, clinical status, refusal and other reasons [15]. CMR was performed in a 1.5 Tesla scanner (Avanto [baseline] and Espree [follow-up] scanner; Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) using a 6-channel body array coil. Image analyses were done in CVI42 (Circle Cardiovascular Imaging Inc., Calgary, Alberta, Canada). Axial cine images were used to determine LVEF, PFR, and EDV.

PFR/EDV > 2.55 was used to determine normal diastolic function based on a previous study with a high specificity and sensitivity [8]. The cut-off was validated in the patients undergoing invasive or computed coronary angiography [8]. LVEF≥50 % was used to define normal systolic function based on European Society of Cardiology guideline [16]. When divided into two groups by diastolic function, patients with PFR/EDV ≤ 2.55 were in diastolic dysfunction group and patients with PFR/EDV > 2.55 were in no diastolic dysfunction group. When assigned into two groups by systolic function, patients with LVEF<50 % were in systolic dysfunction group and those with LVEF≥50 % were in no systolic dysfunction group. Further, patients were stratified into four groups. Patients with PFR/EDV > 2.55 and LVEF≥50 % were assigned into no dysfunction group. Patients with PFR/EDV ≤ 2.55 and LVEF≥50 % were considered with isolated diastolic dysfunction. Patients with PFR/EDV > 2.55 and LVEF<50 % were assigned into isolated systolic dysfunction group. Patients showing PFR/EDV ≤ 2.55 and LVEF<50 % were with both diastolic and systolic dysfunctions.

Clinical outcomes

The primary outcome was composite endpoint of all-cause mortality and hospitalization for heart failure. The secondary outcomes were all-cause mortality and hospitalization for heart failure. Follow-up started from the date of STEMI and lasted until the occurrence of the outcome, or end of the study (January 27th 2024). Events occurring from inclusion until 2016 were validated by an independent event committee and events following 2016 were obtained using hospital records.

Statistical analyses

Baseline characteristics were presented as medians with interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables and frequencies with percentage for categorical variables. Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to evaluate differences between groups for continuous variables and Chi Square test for categorical values. A two-sided P-value < 0.050 was considered statistically significant. The incidence of events was calculated for the outcome. Unadjusted and adjusted HR with 95 % confidence intervals (CI) were calculated by Cox proportional hazard analysis to show the association of cardiac dysfunctions with the outcomes. Further, PFR/EDV and LVEF as independent continuous variables were assessed to investigate associations with the outcomes.

For the adjusted analysis, we applied a tiered adjustment strategy to balance confounding control with events-per-variable (EPV) considerations [17]. From baseline variables that differed between groups and had clinical prognostic relevance, we defined nine candidate covariates: age, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, multivessel disease, low density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), post-PCI thrombolysis in myocardial infarction (TIMI) flow, Killip class at admission ≥II, calcium channel blockers at discharge and oral anticoagulants at discharge. We grouped these variables into three sets: Model 1 (core clinical): age, diabetes mellitus; Model 2 (extended clinical/angiographic): hypertension, multivessel disease, LDL-C, post-PCI TIMI flow, Killip class at admission ≥ II; Model 3 (discharge medication): calcium channel blockers at discharge, oral anticoagulants at discharge. For each adjusted Cox model, we targeted EPV ≥10 based on the total number of events in that analysis [17]. The exposure and each covariate were counted as one parameter. When the number of events permitted, all nine covariates were included. If the EPV threshold restricted the number of variables, model 1 covariates were given priority, followed by model 2 and model 3. Prognostic factors such as peak cardiac troponin-T and infarct size were not included because both are negatively correlated with LVEF [18,19] and represent intermediate variables on the causal pathway between STEMI and cardiac dysfunctions. Excluding such mediators avoids over-adjustment bias and ensures that the hazard ratios reflect the prognostic association of cardiac dysfunctions themselves. In addition, residual confounding from other clinical factors including more detailed measures of renal function and differences in discharge medication beyond the universal therapies may remain. The model was tested for linearity and proportional hazard and found valid. The interactions of age and sex with left ventricular dysfunction were tested.

No interaction between age and left ventricular dysfunction was found. Due to an interaction between sex and left ventricular dysfunction, sensitivity analysis on male and female patients was performed. Cox proportional hazard analysis was conducted in each group. Because of the low incidence of outcome in female patients, unadjusted HR was determined.

All statistical analyses were performed using SASGrid Studio (Release: 3.8, SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) and R Core Team (2019).

Ethical approval

All participants in the DANAMI-3 trial provided written informed consent. The trial was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by a local ethics committee. The trial was registered at ClinicalTrial.gov (NCT number: NCT01435408, NCT01960933).

Results

This sub-study of the DANAMI-3 trial included 542 patients with STEMI treated with primary PCI who survived the first three months after STEMI and got follow-up CMR scan. During a median follow-up of 10.5 years, 103 (19.0 %) patients died or were admitted for heart failure. The patient flowchart is presented in Fig. 1. The incidences of systolic dysfunction and diastolic dysfunction were 101 (18.6 %) and 232 (42.8 %), respectively. The incidences of isolated diastolic dysfunction, isolated systolic dysfunction, and both dysfunctions were 150 (27.7 %), 19 (3.6 %) and 82 (15.1 %), respectively (Graphical abstract).

Long-term follow-up

Patients with diastolic dysfunction were not associated with the composite outcome (Table 2). Notably, they were associated with a higher risk of hospitalization for heart failure (unadjusted hazards ratio [HR] 2.66, confidence interval [CI] 1.28–5.52, P = 0.009). But the association was attenuated after adjustment (adjusted HR 2.04, 95 % CI 0.98–4.27, P = 0.06). In the continuous analysis (Table 3), PFR/EDV was not associated with any outcome after adjustment. In contrast, systolic dysfunction was associated with a higher risk of the composite outcome (adjusted HR 2.73, 95 % CI 1.75–4.27, P < 0.0001), all-cause mortality (adjusted HR 2.26, 95 % CI 1.39–3.67, P = 0.001), and hospitalization for heart failure (adjusted HR 7.87, 95 % CI 3.83–16.16, P < 0.0001) (Table 2). LVEF as a continuous variable was associated with a lower risk of the composite outcome (adjusted HR 0.96, 95 % CI 0.94–0.98, P < 0.0001), all-cause mortality (adjusted HR 0.96, 95 % CI 0.94–0.98, P = 0.0003), and hospitalization for heart failure (adjusted HR 0.92, 95 % CI 0.90–0.95, P < 0.0001) (Table 3).

Under the four cardiac dysfunction categories, patients with isolated systolic dysfunction and both dysfunctions were associated with a higher risk of the outcomes (Supplementary material online, Appendix S1). Isolated diastolic dysfunction was not associated with the primary endpoint. Notably, the results for the isolated systolic dysfunction group should be interpreted with caution, as this subgroup was small (n = 19) and the corresponding hazard estimates may be imprecise.

Sensitivity analysis

In our study, 429 (79.2 %) patients were male. Among them, 197 (45.9 %) had diastolic dysfunction and 90 (21.0 %) had systolic dysfunction (Supplementary material online, Appendix S2). Male patients with LVEF<50 % were associated with an increased risk of the composite outcome (adjusted HR 3.02, 95 % CI 1.84–4.94, P < 0.0001), death (adjusted HR 2.71, 95 % CI 1.67–4.42, P < 0.0001) and hospitalization for heart failure (unadjusted HR 10.70, 95 % CI 4.43–25.81, P < 0.0001). The number of female patients in our study was 113 (20.8 %). Among them, 35 (31.0 %) had diastolic dysfunction and 11 (9.7 %) had systolic dysfunction (Supplementary material online, Appendix S3). Female patients with LVEF<50 % were associated with an enhanced risk of hospitalization for heart failure (unadjusted HR 6.04, 95 % CI 1.44–25.38, P = 0.014).

Discussion

In this sub-study of the DANAMI-3 trial, we evaluated the incidence of follow-up cardiac dysfunctions in the three-month STEMI-survivors treated with primary PCI and its impact on long-term outcomes. CMR-defined systolic/diastolic impairment was applied to identify cardiac dysfunctions. Cardiac dysfunctions were common at three months. LVEF, but not PFR/EDV, showed an independent association with long-term outcomes in continuous analyses. Systolic dysfunction (LVEF<50 %), with or without diastolic dysfunction (PFR/EDV ≤ 2.55), was associated with poorer long-term clinical outcomes. Notably, these findings apply specifically to STEMI survivors who were able to undergo CMR at three months. They may not reflect the risk profile or clinical course of higher-risk patients excluded due to early mortality or contraindications to CMR. This selection bias should be considered when interpreting the generalizability of our results. In addition, the association observed for isolated systolic dysfunction should be viewed cautiously because of the very limited sample size, which may restrict the precision of the hazard estimates.

In the era of early revascularization, heart failure following STEMI especially HFrEF has become less frequent [20,21]. A study on patients with myocardial infarction from 1990 to 2010 showed 16 % of patients developed heart failure within three days and 68 % of them were HFrEF (LVEF<50 %). During follow-up, 18 % developed heart failure and 58 % of them were HFrEF [6]. In contrast, a recent study found that only about 10 % of patients after STEMI developed heart failure with about half of them being HFrEF (LVEF<40 %) [20]. These studies defined heart failure by symptoms/signs of heart failure and LVEF based on Framingham criteria or contemporary guidelines [16,22]. In our study, systolic dysfunction accounted for about 39 % among all cardiac dysfunctions following STEMI which was less than previous studies. It may be a good sign of less frequent systolic dysfunction under current timely revascularization. However, the incidence rate of cardiac dysfunctions in our study was much higher than that of heart failure in previous studies [6,20]. Considering the difference in their definition of heart failure and our definition of cardiac dysfunctions, there may exist a large proportion of patients with cardiac dysfunctions without being clinically diagnosed with heart failure due to lack of obvious symptoms/signs. The different timing of the diagnosis may also make a difference. Furthermore, our definition of cardiac dysfunctions was based on objective imaging parameters may be more precise and allow for classification independent of symptoms.

Diastolic dysfunction is characterized by delayed relaxation, impaired left-ventricular filling and/or increased stiffness [23]. These parameters can be measured by left ventricular volume-time curve, which evaluate continuous volume changes in left ventricle [24]. CMR is a robust tool to depict the volume-time curve. PFR/EDV has long been used to quantify diastolic dysfunction since decades ago [25]. An early study applied PFR/EDV <2.5 to define abnormal left-ventricular filling at rest for patients with coronary artery disease [25]. A more recent study validated that PFR/EDV > 2.55 had a high sensitivity and specificity for normal diastolic function in a general population undergoing myocardial perfusion imaging [8]. On this basis, we applied ≤2.55 as cutoff in the present study. However, this threshold has not been specifically validated in patients with STEMI, which may raise the possibility of misclassification.

Diastolic dysfunction is gaining more attention for its predictive value in STEMI. It has been found that heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) after STEMI was associated with an over twofold risk of all-cause mortality. The incidence rate was 13 % during a mean follow-up of 7.6 years [6]. In our study, by contrast, isolated diastolic dysfunction was identified in 42.8 % of patients at three months after STEMI. But it showed no independent association with mortality. The relatively high prevalence of diastolic dysfunction in our cohort may reflect the high prevalence of subclinical abnormalities rather than clinical HFpEF. In contrast, HFpEF is generally diagnosed by a combination of symptoms and/or signs of heart failure, evidence of diastolic dysfunction, and preserved systolic function [26,27]. Symptoms and signs of HFpEF can be non-specific, usually dyspnea or impaired exercise tolerance [28]. Our definition, based solely on the imaging cutoff of PFR/EDV ≤2.55, may therefore identify a broader group of patients with impaired filling who do not meet clinical HFpEF criteria.

In addition, recovery of diastolic dysfunction after STEMI may also account for the inconsistent observation. Diastolic dysfunction was found present in over 80 % of the STEMI patients with preserved systolic function within the first week of PCI [29]. In our study, the proportion declined to 34 % three months after STEMI. A compensation of diastolic function may continue through potential mechanisms including increased heart rate and arterial resistance [30]. Yet, the timing of assessment may also be influential. Delayed-onset HFpEF was diagnosed at a mean of 4.6 years in a previous study, in contrast to the present study which evaluates the cardiac function following three months [6]. Further, diastolic dysfunction is commonly regarded as the cornerstone of HFpEF which is contributed together by other co-morbidities [31]. Patients with HFpEF are often very co-morbid and quantifying their diastolic dysfunction is significant to initiate treatment early even before the diagnosis of HFpEF. Importantly though, co-morbidities are often related to the same symptoms as in heart failure.

Importantly, when PFR/EDV was analyzed as a continuous variable, no independent association with adverse outcomes was observed after adjustment. This indicates that the absence of prognostic value for isolated diastolic dysfunction is unlikely to be only an artifact of the cutoff, but may also reflect the nature of the imaging-based marker and the selected STEMI population.

In all, our study found a strong predictive value of systolic function on clinical outcomes regardless of diastolic function. Patients with isolated systolic dysfunction and both cardiac dysfunctions three months after STEMI were associated with higher risk in clinical outcomes. The reasons for the divergence in outcome after systolic and diastolic dysfunction are yet to be determined. Conceptually, systolic dysfunction reflects a state of low cardiac output caused by the loss of viable myocardium and diastolic dysfunction reflects a rise in end-diastolic pressure caused by a complicated interplay of endothelium dysfunctions and inflammatory burden [32,33]. The higher risk in clinical outcomes brought by systolic dysfunction may be related to low output which has been proven before [34]. Diastolic dysfunction at three months may be adjusted to normal after the recovery of endothelium and clear of inflammation. It did not contribute to even worse prognosis than isolated systolic dysfunction in our study. In addition, diastolic dysfunction was associated with a higher risk of hospitalization for heart failure before adjustment. This predictive value may be partly attributable to its frequent co-occurrence with systolic dysfunction. The association disappeared when diastolic dysfunction was isolated. While its independent prognostic role appears limited in this well-treated STEMI population, it cannot be considered absent.

Limitations

There are also some limitations in this post hoc analysis. First, we observed an association between systolic dysfunction and worse outcomes. However, due to the hypothesis generating nature of this study, causality cannot be proven. Second, we only had objective signs of cardiac dysfunctions from CMR in these patients. The lack of subjective signs of heart failure may lead to missing diagnosis of specific types of heart failure. We did not know the frequencies of clinical heart failure. Therefore, we could not compare the effect of different types of heart failure and that of cardiac dysfunctions to the outcomes. Third, we applied hospitalization for heart failure as an outcome. This could have neglected patients developing heart failure without being re-admitted to hospital. Fourth, this study only included patients who survived to three months post-STEMI and were able to undergo CMR. Therefore, early post-STEMI deaths and high-risk patients with contraindications to CMR including low eGFR, atrial fibrillation or other arrhythmias were excluded. This introduces a survivorship bias and limits the generalizability of our findings to a relatively healthier post-STEMI population. Additionally, the cohort was predominantly male (79.2 %), which further limits applicability of the findings to female patients. Importantly, our design also omits acute-phase dysfunction and early post-infarction events, as imaging and outcome data begin at three months post-STEMI. Fifth, the cutoff of PFR/EDV was validated in a general population undergoing myocardial perfusion. But it was not validated in patients with STEMI specifically. This may lead to misclassification of diastolic dysfunction in the STEMI population. Sixth, this was a single-country study conducted in Denmark during the modern PCI era. The study population received very high rates of evidence-based medical therapy. Therefore, the results may not be directly generalizable to more diverse or less optimally treated populations. The relatively small number of patients in some subgroups, particularly isolated systolic dysfunction (n = 19), limits statistical power for those comparisons. Finally, although the long follow-up period is a strength, changes in practice pattern and medical treatment over that time could have influenced patient outcomes. In addition, cardiac dysfunctions may have fluctuated during follow up, which could have affected prognosis.

Conclusions

In patients with STEMI treated with primary PCI, there was a high frequency of CMR-defined impairment in left ventricular functions at three months following STEMI. LVEF, but not PFR/EDV, was independently associated with long-term all-cause mortality and hospitalization for heart failure. Systolic dysfunction (LVEF<50 %) regardless of diastolic dysfunction (PFR/EDV ≤ 2.55) was independently associated with an increased risk of the outcomes. Importantly, the observed cardiac dysfunctions were defined using CMR-derived imaging markers rather than clinical criteria and may not reflect symptomatic heart failure. Diastolic dysfunction showed limited independent prognostic value in isolation, although it was associated with heart failure hospitalization before adjustment and may contribute to risk when co-occurring with systolic impairment.
