The impact of standardized meal plans for patients hospitalized for anorexia nervosa
Authors: Joshua Borus, Rachel Lockwood, Gabriela Vargas, Lisa Mancini, Ramy Yim, Sara Forman, Tracy Richmond
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPatients hospitalized with Restrictive Eating Disorders (REDs) often face heightened anxiety and challenges around meals. Inconsistent meal delivery and food choice complexity further complicate care. The study evaluated the tolerability of a standardized, no-choice meal plan by assessing nutritional supplement use via oral and nasogastric (NG) tube as proxies for meal completion.MethodsA quality improvement initiative at a pediatric hospital replaced patient-selected meals with a 7-day rotating standardized meal plan. Adolescent patients could exclude up to five “dislike” foods. Electronic medical record (EMR) data from 10 months pre- and 6 months post-implementation (n = 302) were analyzed for rates of supplement via oral and NG tube in the first week of hospitalization.ResultsNG tube use decreased from 22% to 17% of patients and 11% to 6% of patient-days. Oral supplementation increased from 33% to 36% of patients and 18% to 19% patient-days. Changes were not statistically significant.ConclusionThe standardized meal plan was well tolerated. Future studies should assess psychological impacts and long-term outcomes of removing choice in meals.Implications and contributionThis brief provides initial evidence that a standardized meal plan in a hospital setting is well-tolerated and may provide benefits of reducing the psychological burden of choices for adolescents with REDs.

Introduction

Providing daily meals to patients with Restrictive Eating Disorders (REDs) hospitalized for malnutrition raises many opportunities for error and patient anxiety [1]. Consistently serving meals of varied content in a timely manner is challenging in the hospital setting. Errors in meals for patients with REDs heighten anxiety among patients, families, and staff. Additionally, patients with REDs often find making food choices challenging and require time-intensive support from dietitians and nursing staff [1, 2]. Meal-related challenges worsened with the COVID-19 pandemic when both the number and severity in patients hospitalized with REDs increased [4, 5].

Research shows that meal plans focused on standardized caloric prescription are well-tolerated by patients with REDs [6, 7], but few studies have looked at meal plans that minimize food choice. To minimize hospital kitchen errors, patient distress at mealtimes, and staff time, a new protocol was implemented in which adolescent patients were given a 7-day rotation of standard meals in place of daily food choice. To understand tolerability, we monitored the use of (1) nutritional supplement orally, and (2) via nasogastric (NG) tube as indicators of patients’ inability to complete meals.

Methods

In 2021, as part of a quality improvement effort, the inpatient RED protocol was changed from meals with prescribed caloric requirements but with dietitian-assisted self-selection to a standardized meal plan without choice. Meal plans of different calorie amounts were devised by the eating disorder specialized dietitian and rotated on a seven-day schedule. Patients were allowed to identify five “dislike” foods at the initial meeting with the dietitian that would not be included in their prescribed meals, though alternatives were added to make sure meals were calorically the same when disliked foods were avoided. Allowing five ‘dislike’ foods was felt to strike the balance between allowing for minor personal variation in preference with greatly reduced cognitive and emotional load negotiating meals. The protocol was instituted at pediatric hospital serving adolescent patients with a focus on nutritional rehabilitation and physical restoration as prelude to further care. Patients met at least one criterion for inpatient admission: weight less than 75% of target body weight, severe bradycardia, severe hypotension, lab abnormalities or refusal of oral intake for greater than 24 h.

Psychological support varied but would typically consist of five sessions a week with a mental health provider. Meal support included a staff member and in some instances a family member. Data were extracted from the patient Electronic Medical Record (EMR) to determine the rate of nutritional supplement use by mouth or nasogastric tube (NG) in the first seven days of hospitalization (percent of patients and percent of patient days). We then compared these utilization rates for the ten-month period before standardized meal plan implementation (3/1/2020–12/31/20) with the six months (1/1/2021–6/30/2021) post. This project was reviewed and deemed exempt by the Boston Children’s Hospital Institutional Review Board.

Results

EMRs of patients with REDs (n = 105) (Table 1) were reviewed over 10 months pre- and six months post-intervention. The percentage of patients requiring supplementation via NG tube in their first week of hospitalization dropped from 22% of patients before to 17% after the standardized meal plan was implemented. The percentage of patient-days supplementation via NG tube was required also dropped from 11% to 6%. The percentage of patients requiring oral supplementation increased from 33% to 36%, and the percentage of patient-days in the first week where oral supplementation was required increased from 18% to 19%. None of the changes in oral or NG supplementation were statistically significant.

Discussion

Providing meals is a key component of treatment for adolescent patients admitted to medical hospital with REDs [7]. Numerous studies have been conducted regarding acceptability of meals of different caloric content, but few have examined how meal choice factors into acceptability [6, 7]. After we implemented a standardized meal plan to simplify the logistical challenge of providing consistent, appropriate meals in a timely fashion for adolescent patients hospitalized with REDs, the rate of use of supplementation either orally or via NGT did not increase, suggesting this change was tolerated by patients. This was particularly reassuring in the setting of increased frequency and intensity of hospitalizations for REDs during the COVID-19 pandemic [3, 4].

There are some limitations to this study that should be acknowledged. Changes we ascribe to the standardized meal plan could be attributable to other factors, e.g. changes in staffing who were less versed in eating disorder care or broader effects of the COVID-19 pandemic, but the temporal association with our data limits concern for this. Although the standardized meal plan did not change meal completion rates and was therefore well-tolerated by adolescent patients, we do not know the psychological impact of limited meal-time choice on patients. On the other hand, there could be additional benefits of greater variety in food items in the non-choice meals compared to those that might have been chosen by patients [8, 9].

Our study demonstrates no decrease in meal completion when patients with restrictive EDs are given no choice compared to when they self-select their meals. Future research should address longer term outcomes of standardized meal plans, such as impact on patient length of hospitalization, readmission rates, patients’ feelings of food anxiety around mealtimes, and long-term markers of recovery.
