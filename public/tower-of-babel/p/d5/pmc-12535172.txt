Transcriptomic analysis reveals the role of the molybdate transporter2 in mediating nitrate stress tolerance in tomato seedlings
Authors: Xiaoting Zhou, Shujuan Yu, Li Tang, Xiaoru Liu, Qiyuan Lei, Li Yu, Yiran Xiong, Deyang Ye, Wenjie Wang, Kai Cao, Zhongqun He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSecondary soil salinization adversely affects plant growth in greenhouse in China. Previous studies have suggested that the putative molybdate transporter SlMOT2 may be involved in nitrate stress tolerance in tomatoes. However, the precise regulatory mechanisms remain unclear.ResultsCalcium nitrate stress significantly induced the expression of the SlMOT2 gene. In comparison with wild-type (WT) plants, SlMOT2-overexpressing (OE) plants exhibited enhanced tolerance to nitrate stress, as evidenced by lower malondialdehyde (MDA) content, reduced hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) accumulation, decline in nitrate accumulation, significantly increased antioxidant enzyme activity, and a higher net photosynthetic rate under nitrate stress conditions, whereas slmot2 knockout mutant plants showed nitrate stress sensitivity. An increasing sulfur level and less nitrate content were also found in overexpression lines of SlMOT2 under calcium nitrate stress. Transcriptomic analysis revealed that nitrate stress induced the upregulation of numerous genes, with the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in OE plants being significantly enriched in phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, abscisic acid (ABA) synthesis, amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism, and amino acid metabolism in comparison with WT plants.ConclusionsThe SlMOT2 gene confers nitrate stress tolerance in tomato plants, likely through the following molecular mechanisms: (1) enhancing the biosynthesis of antioxidant compounds to improve reactive oxygen species (ROS)-scavenging capacity and maintain photosynthetic efficiency;(2) activating plant hormone signaling transduction pathways to potentiate stress responses; (3) promoting sulfate uptake to rebalance excess nitrogen in planta, thereby establishing a new nitrogen-sulfur homeostasis. These findings establish a theoretical framework for improving nitrate stress resistance during tomato cultivation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07383-z.

Background

Tomato (Solanum lycopersicum L.), a solanaceous crop, is rich in beneficial compounds. Mature tomato fruits contain multiple beneficial compounds, including ascorbic acid, vitamin A, thiamine, riboflavin, and niacin in its mature fruits [1], making them nutritionally valuable and widely popular. As one of the most extensively cultivated greenhouse vegetables, the tomato plant has shown a growing economic importance in protected agriculture systems [2]. However, secondary soil salinization has emerged as a major constraint for sustainable, protected cultivation. Intensive monoculture practices and excessive fertilizer application in greenhouse systems have exacerbated soil salinity issues [3–5], leading to progressive declines in both the yield and quality of vegetable crops. Notably, NO₃⁻ and Ca²⁺ constitute the predominant anion and cation, respectively, in salinized greenhouse soils [6]. This underscores the urgent need to enhance nitrate stress tolerance in tomatoes by identifying key resistance genes.

Nitrate stress is one of the most common environmental stressors that severely affects plant growth and development. Studies have shown that salt stress significantly inhibits seed germination, as evidenced by the overall decline in germination rate, germination potential, relative germination rate, and relative germination potential observed in seven safflower cultivars under saline conditions [7]. Similar inhibitory effects have been reported in tomato seeds, with increasing NaCl concentrations causing significant reductions in the germination index, germination potential, and vigor index [8]. At the physiological level, salt stress has been shown to cause decreased stomatal conductance and photosynthetic enzyme activity while increasing antioxidant enzyme levels in peach rootstocks [9], as well as progressive reductions in chlorophyll content and stomatal conductance in citrus rootstocks [10]. Nitrate stress exhibits similar mechanisms to salt stress, and the oxidative damage induced by various stresses adversely affects the growth of maize seedlings [11], with excessive calcium nitrate (Ca(NO₃)₂) inducing osmotic stress, triggering reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, and causing membrane lipid peroxidation [12]. The accumulation of Ca(NO₃)₂ in soil impairs stomatal regulation, reduces leaf water potential, decreases transpiration efficiency and photosynthetic rates, and disrupts specific enzymatic activities and metabolic processes, ultimately resulting in oxidative stress and metabolic dysfunction [13]. Although previous studies have described nitrogen stress [14], the majority focused on low-nitrogen stress. However, the mechanisms underlying high-nitrogen stress induced by excessive nitrate and its key regulatory factors remain poorly understood [15].

The sulfate transporter superfamily is a crucial protein family that maintains normal physiological functions in organisms. By mediating the transmembrane transport of sulfate and related anions, it ensures the homeostasis of essential element translocation and metabolism [16–18]. Currently reported members mainly include sulfate transporters and molybdate transporters [19–25]. Molybdate transporters share high homology with sulfate transporters. In Arabidopsis thaliana, MOT1 and MOT2 were previously classified as members of the sulfate transporter (SULTR) family [26, 27].

Molybdate transporters primarily facilitate molybdate transport and participate in molybdenum cofactor biosynthesis and abiotic stress resistance [28–30]. The high affinity of MOT1 enables plants to acquire scarce molybdenum from soil at concentrations of approximately 10 nM, thereby sustaining normal growth [31]. Loss of function of OsMOT1;1 results in decreased molybdenum translocation from roots to shoots, lower molybdenum concentration in grains, and higher sensitivity to molybdenum deficiency [32].

Furthermore, molybdate is utilized for the biosynthesis of pterin-based molybdenum cofactor, which serves as an essential cofactor for redox enzymes such as nitrate reductase, sulfite oxidase, xanthine dehydrogenase, and aldehyde oxidase, thereby influencing nitrogen and sulfur metabolism [33, 34]. Molybdate transporters can indirectly affect plant tolerance to heavy metal cadmium by regulating molybdenum homeostasis [35]. They may also enhance salt tolerance by promoting copper uptake and abscisic acid biosynthesis [36]. Under freezing and osmotic stress, they maintain aldehyde oxidase activity to promote abscisic acid synthesis, thereby improving stress tolerance [37]. Additionally, by ensuring molybdenum supply, they indirectly bolster cellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging capacity, mitigating oxidative damage [38].

In our preliminary study, SlMOT2 was identified by transcriptome data screening [39], indicating its potential involvement in nitrate stress tolerance in tomato. We hypothesize that molybdate transporters play a significant role in tomato’s calcium nitrate stress tolerance. Furthermore, a comparative analysis of nitrate stress tolerance between WT and SlMOT2 transgenic tomato plants was conducted to perform preliminary functional characterization of this nitrate stress-responsive candidate gene. Using transcriptomic approaches, we investigated the metabolic pathways and regulatory networks associated with SlMOT2 to elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which this gene mediates the response of tomatoes to nitrate stress.

Results

Effects of nitrate stress on SlMOT2 gene expression in tomato seedlings

The experimental results (Fig. 1) showed that the relative expression levels of SlMOT2 in tomato seedlings exhibited an initial increase followed by a decrease over 0–24 h under calcium nitrate treatment (S) in comparison with the findings after the control treatment (CK). SlMOT2 expression under both CK and S treatments reached peak expression levels on day 5, with the peak expression in the S treatment being 2.56-fold higher than that in CK.

Effects of nitrate stress on growth parameters of transgenic tomato seedlings

We conducted calcium nitrate stress treatments on WT tomato plants, SlMOT2-overexpressing lines (OE2 and OEA), and mutant plants (T4 and TA). The expression levels of transgenic lines were shown in Fig. S1. The relative expression of SlMOT2 in OE2 and OEA were 26.06–62.87 times higher than WT, while decreased by 47.79%−77.20% percent in T4 and TA when compared with WT. As shown in Fig. 2, calcium nitrate stress significantly inhibited tomato seedling's growth. Under nitrate stress conditions, SlMOT2-overexpressing plants (OE2 and OEA) exhibited significantly greater plant height, fresh weight, and root length than WT plants. In contrast, SlMOT2 knockout mutants (T4 and TA) exhibited reduced growth parameters, including plant height, stem diameter, fresh weight and root length.

As shown in Fig. 3, calcium nitrate stress significantly inhibited the net photosynthetic rate (Pn), stomatal conductance (Gs), intercellular CO₂ concentration (Ci), and transpiration rate (Tr) in tomato seedlings to varying degrees. Under stress treatment (S), in comparison with WT plants, OE2 and OEA-overexpressing lines showed significantly greater Pn, Gs, Ci, and Tr, while these values were markedly lower in T4 and TA mutants.

Under calcium nitrate treatment, the maximum photochemical efficiency (Fv’/Fm’), maximal photochemical efficiency of PSII (Fv/Fm), actual photochemical quantum yield of PSII (Y(II)), and photochemical quenching coefficient (qP) in OE2 and OEA were significantly greater than those in WT plants, while non-photochemical quenching (NPQ) was significantly lower. In contrast, T4 and TA showed significant reductions in Fv’/Fm’, Fv/Fm, and Y(II), and a significant increase in NPQ.

Effects of calcium nitrate stress on SlMOT2 in regulating oxidative stress-related indicators in tomato seedlings

As shown in Fig. 4, under non-stress conditions, superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), and catalase (CAT) activities showed no significant differences between wild-type (WT) andSlMOT2 transgenic tomatoes and mutants. However, under calcium nitrate stress, in comparison with the values in WT plants, the antioxidant enzyme activities (SOD, POD, and CAT) in OE2 and OEA increased significantly by 24.12% and 23.66%, 15.99% and 17.29%, and 26.10% and 35.29%, respectively. In contrast, SOD and POD activities in the T4 and TA treatments decreased significantly by 12.58%, 29.89%, 22.12%, and 20.75%, respectively. Under both calcium nitrate stress and non-stress conditions, the malondialdehyde (MDA) content in OE2 and OEA decreased significantly by 10.50% and 12.65%, respectively, in comparison with the WT, whereas that in T4 and TA increased significantly by 6.72% and 7.23%, respectively.

Effects of SlMOT2 on proline content in tomato seedlings under calcium nitrate stress

As shown in Fig. 6, under normal treatment conditions, proline content showed no significant differences among the groups. However, under calcium nitrate (Ca(NO₃)₂) stress, the proline content increased significantly. In comparison with T4 and TA, the proline levels in OE2 and OEA were markedly elevated, increasing by 42.41% and 24.56% (OE2 vs. T4 and TA, respectively) and 36.77% and 29.33% (OEA vs. T4 and TA, respectively). These results suggest that SlMOT2 may enhance the adaptability of tomatoes to calcium nitrate stress by modulating proline accumulation, thereby maintaining cellular osmotic homeostasis and reducing water loss.

The response of SlMOT2 to nitrate uptake and sulfur level under calcium nitrate stress

As illustrated in the Fig. 7, under non-stress conditions, the total sulfur and hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) contents showed no significant differences between the WT and mutant lines (T4, TA), but both were lower than those in SlMOT2-overexpressing plants. Under calcium nitrate (Ca(NO₃)₂) stress, in comparison with T4 and TA, the OE2 and OEA lines exhibited significantly increased total sulfur and H₂S levels. Under calcium nitrate stress, the SlMOT2-overexpressing resulted in a 32.92% decrease compared to the slmot2 knockout mutants. In a sulfur-containing nutrient solution, SlMOT2- overexpression led to a relative increase of 12.77% in sulfate content compared to the mutant (Fig.S2). These results suggest that the SlMOT2 gene may enhance sulfate uptake under Ca(NO₃)₂ stress, promote sulfur assimilation and utilization, and thereby improve tomato nitrate stress tolerance.

Transcriptomic analysis

Quality control of transcriptome sequencing

Transcriptome sequencing was performed on WT and SlMOT2-overexpressing (OE) tomato plants treated with calcium nitrate. The raw reads obtained from each treatment ranged from 40,001,350 to 40,037,836 read pairs. After removing adapter sequences, reads containing > 10% unknown bases (N), and low-quality reads, the clean reads ranged from 39,412,124 to 39,497,198 read pairs, with Q20 ratios > 97.33%, Q30 ratios > 92.53%, and GC content between 41.80% and 42.76%. Principal component analysis (PCA) of transcriptome data from tomato overexpression and WT samples showed a clear separation betweenSlMOT2-overexpressing and WT plants along principal component 1 (PC1; 57.729% variance) and principal component 2 (PC2; 26.454% variance) (Fig. 8A), indicating that elevated SlMOT2 expression caused significant changes in tomato gene expression profiles.

Differential expression gene analysis

Analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) identified five comparison groups (Fig. 8C): OE(C) vs. WT(C) showed 1,052 upregulated and 604 downregulated DEGs; OE(S) vs. OE(C) showed 2,127 upregulated and 3,175 downregulated DEGs; OE(S) vs. WT(C) showed 2,597 upregulated and 3,758 downregulated DEGs; OE(S) vs. WT(S) showed 2,425 upregulated and 2,761 downregulated DEGs; and WT(S) vs. WT(C) showed 2,362 upregulated and 2,958 downregulated DEGs, demonstrating significant transcriptional changes in both transgenic and wild-type plants under nitrate stress.

Gene ontology analysis of DEGs

Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of the upregulated DEGs is shown in Fig. 9. Under non-stress conditions, DEGs between the overexpression and WT lines (OE(C) vs. WT(C)) were significantly enriched in biological processes, including response to oxygen-containing compound, response to stress and defense, and response to ABA. In the comparison between wild-type plants under nitrate stress versus non-stress conditions (WT(S) vs. WT(C)), the enriched biological processes included RNA modification, binding and metabolism, and cellular nitrogen compound metabolic processes. Molecular functions showed significant enrichment in nuclease activity, and cellular components were enriched in chloroplasts and plastids. Additionally, in the comparison between overexpression lines under nitrate stress and the WT under non-stress conditions (OE(S) vs. WT(C)), molecular functions were significantly enriched for catalytic activity. In the comparison between overexpression lines under nitrate stress and non-stress conditions (OE(S) vs. OE(C)), cellular nitrogen compound metabolic processes were significantly enriched. In the comparison between overexpression lines and WT plants under nitrate stress (OE(S) vs. WT(S)), calcium nitrate stress induced enrichment of responses to oxygen-containing compounds, stimuli and defense, chitin, organic substances, and organonitrogen compounds. In summary, SlMOT2 -overexpression showed specific enrichment for responses to oxygen-containing compounds, organic substances, and organonitrogen compounds, while the wild type showed specific enrichment for chloroplasts and plastids compared to the overexpression lines.

As evidenced by supplementaryFig.S3, GO enrichment analysis of downregulated DEGs revealed that the comparison between OE(C) and WT(C) showed significant enrichment in UDP-glucosyltransferase activity, glucosyltransferase activity, UDP-glycosyltransferase activity, and hexosyltransferase activity; the comparison between WT(S) and WT(C) exhibited significant enrichment in response to oxygen-containing compounds, response to organic substances, response to organic nitrogen compounds, and response to hormones; the comparison between OE(S) and WT(C) demonstrated significant enrichment in thylakoid, plastid thylakoid, photosynthesis, and photosynthetic membrane-related terms; the enrichment profile of the comparison between OE(S) and OE(C) was nearly identical to that of the comparison between OE(S) and WT(C); the comparison between OE(S) and WT(S) showed significant enrichment in obsolete plastid part, obsolete chloroplast part, chloroplast, thylakoid, and plastid, all of which are associated with photosynthetic membranes. Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes enrichment analysis of DEGs

As shown in Fig. 10, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis of upregulated DEGs revealed that under non-stress conditions, the comparison of SlMOT2-overexpressing lines and the WT (OE(C) vs. WT(C)) showed significant enrichment in the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway (plant, amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism, flavonoid biosynthesis, and biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids). A comparison of wild-type plants under nitrate stress versus non-stress conditions (WT(S) vs. WT(C)) showed significant enrichment of ribosome biogenesis in eukaryotes, porphyrin and chlorophyll metabolism, purine metabolism, and arginine biosynthesis. A comparison of SlMOT2-overexpressing lines under nitrate stress versus WT plants under non-stress conditions (OE(S) vs. WT(C)) showed significant enrichment in ribosome biogenesis in eukaryotes, amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism, and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis. A comparison of SlMOT2-overexpressing lines under stress versus non-stress conditions (OE(S) vs. OE(C)) showed significant enrichment in ribosome biogenesis in eukaryotes; degradation of valine, leucine, and isoleucine; metabolism of alanine, aspartate, and glutamate; and sulfur metabolism. A comparison of SlMOT2-overexpressing lines versus wild-type under nitrate stress (OE(S) vs. WT(S)) showed significant enrichment in phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, brassinosteroid biosynthesis, isoflavonoid biosynthesis, amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism, plant hormone signal transduction, and MAPK signaling pathway-plant. In summary, in comparison with the wild type under nitrate stress, SlMOT2- overexpressing lines showed significant upregulation of stress-responsive pathways, including amino acid synthesis and metabolism, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, and plant hormone signal transduction, demonstrating that the SlMOT2 gene is involved in regulating tomato stress (Fig. 11). These results provide systematic evidence at the metabolic pathway level that SlMOT2 participates in tomato’s adaptation to abiotic stress through the coordinated regulation of primary metabolic reprogramming, defense-related specialized metabolism, and phytohormone-mediated stress signaling.

Discussion

Spatiotemporal expression patterns of the SlMOT2 gene in tomato seedlings

The molybdate transporter protein is specifically responsible for the uptake and translocation of molybdate in plants, playing a critical role in the synthesis of molybdenum-dependent enzymes [27].These enzymes are involved in key processes, including nitrate assimilation, hormone biosynthesis, purine metabolism, and sulfite detoxification, which are essential for maintaining plant growth and stress resistance [40]. Analytical studies have revealed that, compared to wild-type control plants, the mot1.3-1 knockout mutant line exhibits reduced nitrogenase activity in both whole plants and root nodules. This reduction leads to a deficiency in fixed nitrogen and, consequently, impaired plant growth [41]. In this study, the expression of SlMOT2 in tomatoes rapidly increased under calcium nitrate stress, peaking at 5 d post-treatment, indicating a strong correlation between SlMOT2 and the nitrate stress response of tomatoes, which is consistent with previous findings (Fig. 1). Transcriptome analysis revealed altered gene expression in both SlMOT2-overexpressing and WT lines, with the overexpression line exhibiting a notably higher number of upregulated genes. This highlights the complexity of SlMOT2’s regulatory role in nitrate stress adaptation.

Effects of SlMOT2 on gas-exchange and chlorophyll fluorescence parameters in tomato under calcium nitrate stress

Photosynthesis provides essential energy and biomaterials for normal plant growth, and its efficiency serves as an indirect indicator of plant physiological status [42]. Under calcium nitrate stress, plants reduce transpiration to prevent water loss, which consequently impairs photosynthetic performance [43]. Previous studies have demonstrated that nitrate stress significantly reduces the net Pn, Tr, and Gs in tomato seedlings [44]. Consistent with previous findings, we observed reductions in all four measured gas-exchange parameters (including Gs) under calcium nitrate stress. However, SlMOT2-overexpressing lines maintained higher values than WT and mutant plants, suggesting that elevated SlMOT2 expression mitigates the inhibitory effects of calcium nitrate stress on photosynthesis. Chlorophyll fluorescence parameters precisely reflect the light energy absorption/conversion processes and directly influence biological pathways such as adenosine triphosphate (ATP) synthesis and electron transport [45]. Our data revealed a significant decline in Fv/Fm under calcium nitrate stress, which is consistent with a previous report describing Fv/Fm reduction in nitrate-stressed stress tomato [46]. Notably, SlMOT2-overexpressing lines exhibited a smaller decrease in Fv/Fm than WT and mutant plants, implying that SlMOT2 may stabilize PSII by facilitating electron transport. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying these effects require further investigation.

The SlMOT2 gene may potentially induce pathways associated with antioxidant defense and osmoregulation capacity

Calcium nitrate stress leads to excessive accumulation of ROS, causing cellular damage and inhibiting plant growth. Antioxidant enzymes maintain redox homeostasis by scavenging free radicals and decomposing H₂O₂ [47]. In this study, calcium nitrate stress triggered a significant increase in antioxidant enzyme activity in SlMOT2-overexpressing lines in comparison with WT plants (Fig. 4). GO analysis revealed marked upregulation of genes associated with catalytic activity, suggesting that SlMOT2 may enhance antioxidant capacity. KEGG pathway analysis showed significant enrichment in flavonoid biosynthesis, which is consistent with previous reports showing that calcium nitrate stress preferentially activates flavonoid synthesis, metabolism, and oxidoreductase regulation pathways [48]. Notably, the flavonoids in Atriplex canescens have been shown to mitigate salt stress by scavenging ROS [49]. SlMOT2 overexpression also significantly upregulated the metabolism of alanine, aspartate, and glutamate in comparison with the wild type. These amino acids function as osmolytes, reducing the osmotic potential to improve water uptake and minimize cellular damage [50]. In addition, proline content significantly increased (Fig. 6). Proline, a potent non-enzymatic antioxidant, effectively neutralizes hydroxyl radicals [51], alleviating salt-induced phytotoxicity [54]. Collectively, SlMOT2 may enhance calcium nitrate stress tolerance by modulating antioxidant enzymes and amino acid metabolism, warranting further mechanistic investigations.

SlMOT2 may be involved in plant hormone biosynthesis

Abscisic-acid biosynthesis rely on Moco-dependent enzymes. Further analysis revealed their association with phytohormone signal transduction pathways [53]. ABA is a ubiquitous endogenous phytohormone that serves as a crucial stress hormone in plants [54]. ABA improves nitrate stress tolerance in maize through regulation of osmotic homeostasis, ROS balance, and antioxidant defense mechanisms [55, 56]. The Atsultr3 mutant showed disrupted ABA biosynthesis, which affects ABA sensitivity during germination and root growth [57]. The quintuple mutant exhibited increased ABA and salt stress sensitivity, which was rescued by sulfide supplementation, demonstrating the role of SULTR3 in stress responses [58]. GO analysis revealed that elevated expression of SlMOT2 induces an ABA response. KEGG pathway analysis indicated that the sulfur metabolism pathway was relatively enriched in SlMOT2-overexpressing plants under calcium nitrate stress in comparison with WT plants (Fig. 12). Additionally, SlMOT2-overexpression led to significantly higher total sulfur and H₂S content than in WT (Fig. 7). Simultaneously, SlMOT2-overexpression under calcium nitrate stress led to the downregulation of glycine, serine, and threonine metabolism (Fig. 12). Previous studies indicated that glycine, serine, and threonine can enter the sulfur metabolic pathway to generate cysteine. The synthesized cysteine can be further utilized for glutathione biosynthesis to enhance cellular antioxidant capacity [59]. Moreover, it promotes ABA biosynthesis, thereby participating in plant stress response signaling [60]. Studies have also reported that sulfate plays a critical role in ABA biosynthesis and triggers ABA signaling, a process dependent on the accumulation of sulfate metabolite [61]. Therefore, we hypothesized that SlMOT2 enhanced calcium nitrate stress tolerance by increasing total sulfur content, which further promotes ABA accumulation under stress conditions.

Brassinosteroids are multifunctional steroid phytohormones that modulate plant growth and stress responses [62]. Studies have shown that exogenous brassinosteroids can enhance photosynthetic performance, ion homeostasis, and antioxidant systems under calcium nitrate stress, thereby improving plant growth [63]. In this study, KEGG pathway analysis revealed that under calcium nitrate stress, overexpression of the SlMOT2 gene led to significant enrichment in brassinosteroid biosynthesis in comparison with the wild type (Fig. 10). We hypothesized that the SlMOT2 gene promotes brassinosteroid biosynthesis, thereby enhancing tomato resistance under calcium nitrate stress. However, existing research also indicates crosstalk between the brassinosteroid and ABA signaling pathways [64]. Whether the upregulation of brassinosteroids observed in this study was associated with ABA remains unclear and warrants further investigation.

Influence of SlMOT2-induced metabolic pathways on tomato nitrate stress tolerance

Numerous studies have demonstrated that various metabolic processes in plants are involved in calcium nitrate stress responses, including phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, amino and nucleotide sugar metabolism, and the MAPK signaling pathway-plant. KEGG analysis and RT-qPCR data revealed that SlMOT2-overexpression under calcium nitrate stress potentially regulates nitrate stress tolerance-related pathways and the associated gene upregulation in tomatoes (Fig. 10). Notably, 4CL, CHIB, and MPK6 were significantly upregulated (Fig. 13), suggesting that SlMOT2-overexpression may enhance phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, amino sugar metabolism, and plant hormone signal transduction, thereby mediating calcium nitrate resistance. These findings align with the result of previous reports [65, 66]. Amino and nucleotide sugars are essential components of plant cell walls and glycoproteins, and play critical roles in environmental stress responses. Research indicates that metabolites from these pathways participate in cell-wall biosynthesis [67] and act as signaling molecules in plant hormone regulation and pathogen defense, thereby modulating stress resistance [68].

Furthermore, phenylpropanoids serve as precursors for most plant secondary metabolites, and are closely associated with plant growth, development, and stress responses [69, 70]. Previous proteomic analyses of salt-tolerant and salt-sensitive mulberry varieties have highlighted the critical role played by phenylpropanoid compounds in salt stress resistance [71, 72]. Additionally, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis and accumulation can suppress ROS generation, protecting photosynthetic systems and maintaining cell membrane integrity [73]. Similarly, ROS initiation during salt stress triggers mitogen-activated protein (MAP) cascades as well as hormonal and other metabolic pathways, facilitating ionic balance and osmotic equilibrium [74]. MAPK responds to osmotic stress by increasing osmolyte synthesis [75]. MAPKs are believed to maintain the ROS balance [76]. These findings suggested that SlMOT2 plays a pivotal role in the adaptation of tomato plants to calcium nitrate stress. However, the underlying mechanism remains unclear and require further investigation.

Methods

Plant materials

The tomato cultivar ‘Jinpeng No. 1’, which was obtained from Xi’an Jinpeng Seedling Co, Ltd. (China), was used in this study.

The study employed WT and transgenic ‘Micro-Tom’ tomato plants. Fifteen independent SlMOT2-overexpressing lines and mutants were previously generated. On the basis of the relative expression levels of SlMOT2 and the growth performance of the first generation, four representative lines were selected: two overexpression lines (OE2 and OEA) and two mutants (T4 and TA). Plump seeds from these lines were surface-sterilized and cultivated on a substrate. Transgenic second-generation plants were obtained by molecular screening.

Growth conditions and treatment

The experiment was conducted at the greenhouse of Sichuan Agricultural University’s Chengdu campus using uniformly plump seeds of the tomato cultivar ‘Jinpeng No. 1’. After germination, seedlings were cultivated in 50-cell trays with growth substrate under controlled environmental conditions: day/night temperatures of 26 °C ± 1/18°C ± 1 °C, 12 h/12 h light/dark cycle, Light intensity of 200± 25 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹, and 70% relative Humidity, with irrigation using 1/2 Hoagland’s nutrient solution every 3 days. When the seedlings reached the four true-leaf plus one apical bud stage, uniform plants were transplanted into pots (15 cm × 15 cm) containing a 3:1:1 (v/v/v) peat: vermiculite: perlite substrate mixture. After 5 days of recovery growth, experimental treatments commenced using 160 mmol·L⁻¹ calcium nitrate solution as the stress concentration determined from preliminary tests, with 400 mL of nutrient solution applied to each tray every 3 days. The experimental treatments included: (1) control group (CK) maintained on 1/2 strength Hoagland’s nutrient solution, and (2) nitrate stress treatment group (S) receiving 160 mmol·L⁻¹ calcium nitrate (Ca(NO₃)₂) solution and 1/2 strength Hoagland’s nutrient solution as the stress concentration.

Each treatment was conducted in triplicate, with 18 plants per replicate. Samples were collected at 0 h, 3 h, 6 h, 9 h, 24 h, and 5 days post-treatment, immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C for subsequent gene expression analysis.

The ‘Micro-Tom’ tomato plants were cultivated directly in pots (10 cm diameter × 10 cm height) using the same cultivation protocol as for ‘Jinpeng No. 1’. After 25 days of seedling growth, plants were subjected to two treatments: (1) control group (CK) maintained on 1/2 strength Hoagland’s nutrient solution, and (2) nitrate stress treatment group (S) receiving 240 160mmol·L⁻¹ calcium nitrate (Ca(NO₃)₂) solution as the stress concentration, which was determined through preliminary screening. Plants were sampled after 5 d of treatment for subsequent analysis.

Determination of seedling growth indicators

Plant height and stem diameter: Three tomato seedlings were randomly selected from each treatment group, and their height and root length were measured using a ruler with an accuracy of 0.01 cm.

Root length: Roots were scanned using a root scanner (MICROTEK Scan Maker i850) and analyzed with Microtek Scan Wizard Pro software to determine root length.

Biomass measurement: Three seedlings from each treatment group were washed clean with water, dried with absorbent paper, and their fresh weight was measured separately using an electronic balance with 0.001 g precision.

Measurement of photosynthetic gas exchange parameters and chlorophyll fluorescence parameters

We used an Li-6400XT (Li-6400, Lincoln, NE, USA) photosynthesizer to measure the light and gas parameters (photosynthetically active radiation: 800µmol·m−2·s−1; carbon dioxide (CO2): 400µmol·mol−1; ambient relative Humidity: 50–60%). PAM-2500 chlorophyll fluorescence analyzer (Walz, Germany) was used to measure chlorophyll fluorescence parameters.

Photosynthetic gas exchange parameters: Measured parameters included transpiration rate (Tr), intercellular CO₂ concentration (Ci), stomatal conductance (Gs), and net photosynthetic rate (Pn). Chlorophyll fluorescence parameters: Measured indicators included maximum photochemical efficiency (Fv/Fm), actual photochemical efficiency (Y(Ⅱ)), photochemical quenching coefficient (qP), and non-photochemical quenching coefficient (NPQ), following the method of Qaiser, Javed [77].

Measurement of oxidative stress biomarkers

Malondialdehyde (MDA) content was measured using the thiobarbituric acid (TBA) method [78]. Activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), and catalase (CAT) were determined according to the method of Cheng Yan et al. [79].

Hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) was detected by histochemical staining following the method of Daudi and O’Brien (2012) [80]. The proline content was determined using the acid ninhydrin colorimetric assay [81].

Determination of total sulfur and hydrogen sulfide content

Total sulfur content was quantified using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) [82], while hydrogen sulfide (H2S) levels were measured via the methylene blue colorimetric method [83].

Hormone analysis

An enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (China Agricultural University, Beijing, China) was used to determine the content of endogenous hormones with fresh weight: ABA [84]. Each sample was measured three times, repeatedly.

Sequence filtering, assembly, unigene expression analysis, and differential gene identification

Raw image data files obtained from high-throughput sequencing platforms (e.g., Illumina) were converted into raw sequenced reads (Raw Reads) through base calling. The data were then filtered using Trimmomatic software to remove adapter sequences, retaining a minimum read length of 20 bp. Quality control was performed on the filtered data using FastQC (version 0.11.5) to generate Clean Data (Clean Reads). The filtered data were further assembled to obtain Unigenes (Bio Project ID: PRJNA1256797).

Identification and analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

DEGs were identified based on the threshold criteria of |FC| >2 and p-value < 0.05. The identified DEGs were subsequently subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (RT‒qPCR) expression of related genes

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Tomato leaves from wild-type (WT) and SlMOT2-overexpressing lines were collected before and after calcium nitrate (Ca(NO₃)₂) stress treatment, immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent analysis. Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed using SYBR Green qPCR Mix (SL2690, Coolaber, Beijing, China) on an ArchimedS4 real-time PCR system. The tomato SlActin gene was used as an internal reference for normalization. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2(−ΔΔCT) method. The primer sequences used for RT-qPCR are listed in Table S1.

Data analysis and processing

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 22.0 (IBM, USA). Data processing and visualization were conducted with Excel 2019 (Microsoft, USA), Origin 2022 (OriginLab, USA), and Cytoscape 3.7.2 (for network analysis).

Conclusions

Under calcium nitrate stress conditions in this study, tomato plants exhibited reduced stomatal conductance, net photosynthetic rate, and Fv/Fm ratio. However, compared with wild-type (WT) controls, SlMOT2-overexpressing lines showed less severe impairment in these physiological parameters, accompanied by significantly enhanced activities of antioxidant enzymes. The slmot2 knockout mutants displayed increased sensitivity to calcium nitrate stress, indicating that SlMOT2 positively regulates calcium nitrate stress resistance in tomato plants. Based on the integrated analysis of physiological, biochemical, gene expression, and transcriptome data, under calcium nitrate stress, SlMOT2 induces multiple metabolic pathways, including the antioxidant system, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, abscisic acid (ABA) synthesis, nitrogen metabolism, sulfur metabolism, as well as amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism. These regulatory effects collectively contribute to the enhancement of calcium nitrate stress resistance in tomato plants.
