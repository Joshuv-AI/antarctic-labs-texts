Factors Associated with Migraine Attack Duration in Adults: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Tuğba Okluoğlu, Bengül Fatma Gölge, Mehmet Ali Sungur, Osman Özgür Yalın
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMigraine is a common neurological disorder with attacks lasting 4–72 h. Although duration is a key diagnostic criterion, the clinical and demographic factors associated with its variability are not well defined. Identifying temporal phenotypes may improve diagnostic accuracy and inform individualized management.MethodsIn this cross-sectional observational study, we evaluated 694 patients fulfilling ICHD-3 migraine criteria. Patients were categorized by attack duration into short (4–12 h), medium (13–24 h), and long (>24 h) groups. Sociodemographic features, migraine characteristics, associated symptoms, and disability measures were compared across groups. Multinomial logistic regression was performed using the short-duration group as the reference category to identify variables independently associated with medium- and long-duration attacks.ResultsOf 694 participants (84% female; mean age 36.66 ± 12.20 years), 33.3% (n = 231) experienced short, 28.2% (n = 196) medium, and 38.5% (n = 267) long attacks. Long-duration attacks were associated with older age (39.98 ± 11.36 vs. 34.09 ± 12.62 years, p < 0.001), higher female ratio (89.1% vs. 78.8%, p = 0.007), and lower educational level (p = 0.008). Patients with prolonged attacks had higher allodynia scores (median ASC 6 vs. 4, p < 0.001), greater pain intensity (VAS 9 vs. 8, p < 0.001), and more frequent nausea and vomiting (p = 0.002). Illness duration was the longest in the long-attack group (median 120 vs. 60 months, p < 0.001). Other clinical characteristics, including aura, localization, and chronic migraine rates, showed no significant differences. In multivariable analysis, older age and higher pain intensity remained independently associated with long-duration attacks, while lower educational level and higher pain intensity were independently associated with medium-duration attacks.ConclusionMigraine attack duration is associated with distinct demographic and clinical characteristics independent of attack frequency. In multivariable analysis, older age and higher pain intensity remained independently associated with long-duration attacks, while lower educational level and higher pain intensity were independently associated with medium-duration attacks. These findings suggest that attack duration could represent a meaningful clinical phenotype; therefore, frequency-based classification approaches might overlook patients with infrequent but prolonged and disabling attacks.

Introduction

Migraine is a common and disabling neurological disorder characterized by recurrent headache attacks lasting 4–72 h [1]. It affects approximately 15% of the global population and is among the leading causes of disability worldwide [2, 3]. Chronic migraine contributes disproportionately to this burden, with up to 45% of patients in specialty clinics reporting near-daily headaches [4, 5]. Nearly half of individuals with migraine meet criteria for preventive therapy [6]. Despite this substantial clinical impact, diagnostic classification still relies heavily on attack frequency, distinguishing episodic migraine (<15 headache days/month) from chronic migraine (≥15 days/month) [7, 8].

Attack duration, although a core diagnostic criterion, has received far less attention. While the International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition (ICHD-3) defines duration broadly as 4–72 h [9], clinical variability suggests distinct temporal phenotypes. Longer attacks have been associated with greater pain intensity, female sex, allodynia, and increased disability [4, 5]. The PAMINA study demonstrated that early attack characteristics may predict subsequent duration [10]. However, the determinants of prolonged attacks remain insufficiently understood, and duration is not incorporated into current phenotyping approaches. This study therefore aimed to investigate the demographic and clinical factors associated with migraine attack duration in a large outpatient cohort, with the goal of refining clinical characterization and informing potential classification adjustments.

Methods

This single-center, cross-sectional observational study was conducted in the neurology outpatient clinic of a tertiary care center between 2019 and 2020. The Institutional Ethics Committee of Istanbul Education Training Hospital approved the study (Decision No. 612/27.02.2015), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Adults aged ≥18 years who fulfilled the ICHD-3 diagnostic criteria for migraine were included. Patients with incomplete data regarding attack duration (n = 16) or attacks shorter than 4 h (n = 16) were excluded.

Sociodemographic and clinical variables were obtained through structured interviews and clinical assessment at the time of outpatient evaluation. Patient-perceived typical attack duration, assessed at the time of outpatient evaluation based on patient recall of their most recent typical migraine attacks, was categorized as short (4–12 h), medium (13–24 h), or long (>24 h). Since ICHD-3 defines migraine duration broadly as 4–72 h without providing subcategories for stratifying attack duration [9], a nested partitioning approach was employed to define these intervals. The total sample was first bifurcated by the overall median duration (24 h), and subsequently, the group below the median (4–24 h) was further subdivided based on its own internal median (12 h) to ensure distributional homogeneity. This categorization allows for a more granular analysis of duration-related clinical characteristics while remaining robust against the broad range of the standard diagnostic criteria. The collected variables included age, sex, education, smoking status, smoking pack-years, duration of migraine history (months), presence of aura, attack frequency, pain intensity measured using the visual analog scale (VAS), disability assessed by the Migraine Disability Assessment Scale (MIDAS), and allodynia evaluated using the Allodynia Symptom Checklist (ASC). An ASC score ≥2 (0–12 scale) was considered positive.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range). Group comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis tests for continuous variables and Pearson’s chi-square test for categorical variables. To identify variables independently associated with migraine attack duration, ordinal logistic regression was initially considered. However, because the proportional odds assumption was violated (test of parallel lines p = 0.005), multinomial logistic regression analysis was subsequently performed using the short-duration group as the reference category. Variables demonstrating clinical relevance or statistical significance in univariate analyses were entered into the multinomial logistic regression model. To evaluate the potential impact of confounders and ensure model stability, a sensitivity analysis was performed using a bootstrapping resampling method with 2,000 replications to compute bias-corrected accelerated (BCa) 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs were reported. A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 22.

Results

Among 694 migraine patients (84% female; mean age 36.66 ± 12.20 years), attack duration was short in 33.3% (n = 231), medium in 28.2% (n = 196), and long in 38.5% (n = 267), as shown in online supplementary Figure 1 (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000553571). Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics across the three duration groups are summarized in Table 1. Patients with longer attacks were significantly older than those with shorter attacks (p < 0.001), and the proportion of females increased progressively across duration groups (p = 0.007).

Regarding education levels, a statistically significant difference was observed across the study groups (p = 0.008). The median duration of education was found to be significantly lower in the long-duration group compared to the short-duration and medium-duration groups.

Smoking status (p = 0.842) and smoking pack-years (p = 0.434) did not differ significantly between duration groups. Systolic and diastolic blood pressure levels were also comparable across groups (p = 0.717 and p = 0.543). Family history of migraine did not differ significantly (p = 0.148).

Clinically, patients in the long-duration group (>24 h) were characterized by significantly longer disease durations (median 120 vs. 60 months; p < 0.001). Significant differences were also observed in terms of clinical severity; patients with prolonged attacks exhibited higher pain intensity scores on the VAS and more severe allodynia on the ASC (both p < 0.001; online suppl. Fig. 2, 3). Conversely, attack frequency and MIDAS scores did not differ significantly between the groups (p = 0.330 and p = 0.191, respectively).

Regarding associated symptoms, nausea and vomiting were significantly more frequent in the long-duration group (p = 0.002 for both). Photophobia, phonophobia, emotional stress, fasting, menstrual triggers, and other common triggers were similar across groups. Osmophobia showed a borderline trend toward higher frequency in medium-duration attacks (p = 0.059). The proportion of chronic migraine and presence of aura also did not differ significantly between groups.

In multinomial logistic regression analysis using the short-duration group as the reference category, lower educational level (OR: 0.925, 95% CI: 0.876–0.976, p = 0.005) and higher VAS scores (OR: 1.310, 95% CI: 1.123–1.528, p = 0.001) remained independently associated with attack duration. For long duration attacks, older age (OR: 1.026, 95% CI: 1.004–1.048, p = 0.021) and higher pain intensity measured by VAS (OR: 1.359, 95% CI: 1.174–1.574, p < 0.001) remained independently associated with long-duration attacks. Female sex showed a borderline association with prolonged attacks (OR: 1.702, 95% CI: 0.959–3.020, p = 0.069). Educational level, migraine disease duration, nausea, vomiting, and allodynia scores did not remain independently associated after multivariable adjustment. Furthermore, the bootstrap-based sensitivity analysis demonstrated that the independent associations for older age, lower educational level, and pain intensity remained robust, with stable significance levels across the 2,000 resamples (Table 2).

Discussion

Our findings indicate that migraine attack duration represents a clinically meaningful dimension of the migraine phenotype – one that substantially contributes to patient burden but is not incorporated into current diagnostic classifications. Although headache frequency remains central to the distinction between episodic and chronic migraine [9], our results suggest that attack duration captures a distinct clinical dimension independent of attack frequency. This clinical relevance complements recent evidence [11, 12] identifying frequency as the dominating predictor of productivity loss. While frequency may be the primary driver of aggregate health loss in large-scale models, our data highlight duration as a separate phenotypic marker that reflects an important aspect of the individual migraine experience.

One of the key findings of this study is the independent association between prolonged attack duration and advancing age. Patients in the long-duration group were, on average, nearly 6 years older than those with short attacks, and age remained independently associated with long-duration attacks in multivariable analysis. This finding aligns with earlier work by Wöber-Bingöl et al. [13] demonstrating migraine duration tends to increase as patients age. Female sex was more frequent among patients with prolonged attacks in univariate comparisons and showed a borderline association after adjustment. This pattern is consistent with hormonal influences described in clinical analyses [5, 14]. Perimenstrual migraine, for instance, exhibits distinctive temporal profiles, further supporting the role of sex hormones in modulating attack duration [14].

Educational attainment showed an inverse relationship with attack duration. In multivariable analysis, lower educational level remained independently associated with medium-duration attacks, although it did not remain independently associated with long-duration attacks. Although direct evidence linking education level to temporal migraine features is limited, lower education has been associated with greater migraine burden and increased risk of chronicity [15]. Moreover, randomized trials suggest that education-based interventions can reduce both attack frequency and attack duration [16], supporting a potential role of health literacy in modifying attack persistence. These findings may also reflect broader socioeconomic disparities in healthcare utilization; patients with lower educational attainment might face barriers to timely specialist care, potentially presenting to a tertiary center only after their attacks have become more persistent and challenging to manage.

Pain intensity emerged as the most consistent clinical correlate of attack duration. Higher VAS scores were independently associated with both medium- and long-duration attacks, suggesting that pain intensity is closely linked to the temporal persistence of migraine attacks. This association may reflect shared mechanisms between pain amplification and attack prolongation, including sustained trigeminovascular activation and central sensitization. The relationship between pain intensity and attack duration also has practical clinical implications, as patients with more intense attacks may require earlier and more aggressive acute treatment strategies to prevent progression into longer and more disabling episodes.

Longer migraine disease duration, higher allodynia scores, and more frequent nausea and vomiting were observed among patients with prolonged attacks in univariate analyses; however, these variables did not remain independently associated with attack duration after multivariable adjustment. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as markers of greater clinical burden rather than independent determinants of prolonged attacks. Taken together, the broader clinical clustering observed in univariate analyses appears to be largely attenuated after adjustment, with age and pain intensity emerging as the most consistent independent correlates of prolonged attacks.

From a mechanistic perspective, one possible explanation for prolonged attack duration may involve central sensitization processes. Neuroimaging studies have demonstrated progressive structural and functional changes in central pain-processing networks, including the brainstem, thalamus, insula, and cingulate cortex, among patients with chronic migraine [17, 18]. These findings support the hypothesis that repeated nociceptive activation reinforces central sensitization mechanisms that lengthen attacks over time [18]. However, since most neuroimaging studies were conducted in chronic migraine cohorts, the generalizability of these mechanisms to episodic migraine with prolonged attacks is uncertain.

Nevertheless, our clinical data suggest that sensitization-related processes may already be evolving in patients experiencing prolonged attacks. Although this interpretation remains speculative and was not independently confirmed after multivariable adjustment, increased attack persistence may still serve as a clinical signal of underlying neurobiological shifts, potentially emerging before the formal frequency criteria for chronic migraine are met.

Gastrointestinal symptoms, especially nausea and vomiting, were significantly more common among individuals with long attacks in univariate analyses. This observation is consistent with Kelman [4], who reported that these symptoms are markers of attack persistence and heightened brainstem involvement. However, nausea and vomiting did not remain independently associated with attack duration after adjustment, suggesting that their relationship with prolonged attacks may be partly mediated by other clinical variables, particularly pain intensity. By contrast, photophobia and phonophobia were uniformly prevalent across all groups, reflecting their roles as core diagnostic features independent of duration [1].

In our study, although the total sample size was 694, osmophobia data were available for only a subset of patients (n = 145 in total). This highlights a critical underreporting phenomenon in clinical practice. As noted by Briggs et al. [19], osmophobia is often an underrecognized and under-documented component of migraine unless specifically queried using standardized tools. Our borderline significance (p = 0.059) for medium-duration attacks likely reflects this missing data bias rather than a lack of clinical relevance. The observed directional trend supports the need for further investigation into the temporal relationship between migraine attack duration and olfactory hypersensitivity.

Importantly, neither attack frequency nor MIDAS scores differed among duration groups. This finding reinforces the conclusion that attack duration and attack frequency represent distinct axes of migraine burden. The lack of MIDAS difference may also reflect the frequency-weighted structure of this scale, which may be less sensitive to the burden imposed by infrequent but prolonged attacks. Therefore, patients with long-lasting attacks may experience substantial individual burden even if they do not meet conventional frequency-based thresholds for chronic migraine.

Although Thomas et al. [11] underscore the importance of considering duration as a meaningful clinical variable in productivity loss assessments, our findings highlight a significant methodological challenge: the subjectivity inherent in retrospective duration estimates can introduce substantial noise into the analysis. This points to a clear need for prospective studies using electronic diaries to capture precise, real-time data on attack onset, duration, intensity, associated symptoms, and treatment timing. Such approaches would help mitigate recall bias and clarify whether attack duration independently predicts long-term disability, treatment response, or progression toward chronic migraine.

Implications for Migraine Classification

The current ICHD-3 definition of chronic migraine relies exclusively on the threshold of ≥15 headache days per month [9], without incorporating attack duration. However, our findings suggest that this approach may overlook patients with relatively infrequent but extremely long and disabling attacks. Similar concerns have been raised in recent literature advocating for multidimensional phenotyping frameworks [11]. Our results support the view that attack duration may serve as an additional temporal marker of migraine burden, alongside frequency and intensity. These findings raise the question of whether attack duration should play a more prominent role in future revisions of migraine classification, either as a modifying specifier or as an additional severity marker.

Strengths and Limitations

Strengths of this study include its large sample size, systematic ICHD-3-based data collection, detailed clinical phenotyping, and the addition of multivariable regression analysis to identify factors independently associated with migraine attack duration. These features provide a multidimensional view of migraine burden beyond simple frequency counts. However, limitations should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and the reliance on patient self-reported attack duration, recorded at the time of evaluation, may introduce recall variability, particularly for longer or more severe attacks. In addition, although multivariable analysis was performed, the model could not account for several clinically relevant confounders, such as the use of triptans, preventive treatments, and medication timing. Finally, while ORs with 95% CIs were reported for the regression analyses, formal effect size measures were not calculated for all univariate group comparisons, which may limit the interpretation of the magnitude of differences between groups. Future prospective studies using electronic diaries, standardized treatment data, and comprehensive analytical models are needed to confirm the robustness and independence of these findings.

Conclusion

Migraine attack duration is a clinically significant yet underrecognized dimension of disease burden. In multivariable analysis, higher pain intensity was independently associated with both medium- and long-duration attacks, while older age remained independently associated with long-duration attacks and lower educational level with medium-duration attacks. These findings support attack duration as a meaningful temporal phenotype that is not fully captured by attack frequency alone. Therefore, classification systems based solely on frequency may overlook patients with infrequent but very long and debilitating attacks.

This frequency-centered approach could potentially lead to phenotypic misclassification in both clinical practice and epidemiological research. These results underscore the need to reconsider the role of attack duration in migraine phenotyping and highlight the potential value of integrating temporal markers into future revisions of migraine criteria.
