Patients with progressive supranuclear palsy need to be seen sooner and more frequently
Authors: Marian L. Dale, Farwa Ali, Shannon Anderson, Michiko Bruno, Martine Comeau, Kristophe Diaz, Lawrence I. Golbe, Lawrence S. Honig, Maria Schmidt, Chauncey Spears, Jessica Shurer
Published: 2023

The problem

Our current care practices are insufficient to meet the needs of patients with progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP). Survival in PSP relates to falls and dysphagia, symptoms which lead to injury, immobility, infection, and death. In a clinicopathological study of classic PSP-Richardson syndrome, the median survival was 5.6 years [1]; estimates of survival in other subtypes of PSP are several years longer. Yet, across movement disorder centers and general neurology clinics, patients with PSP are routinely scheduled in with diagnoses with relatively slower progression, primarily competing with patients with Parkinson’s disease for coveted movement disorder clinic slots. A long wait time for an initial or return neurology appointment is certainly problematic for a patient with Parkinson’s disease, but can be devastating in atypical parkinsonian syndromes that progress more rapidly.

PSP is the focus of this publication, although we acknowledge that the same challenges and principles discussed apply for other atypical parkinsonian syndromes, including corticobasal syndrome (CBS) and multiple system atrophy (MSA), which also entail high symptom burden and shortened life expectancy.

Consider the hypothetical, but not uncommon, case of patient X. This patient has known for months that something was wrong with their reading ability but was told that they have normal eyesight on an optometry exam. Their family has been noticing a change in their walking, balance, and aspects of their cognition. A primary care physician refers them to a general neurologist after their mobility and cognition have worsened to the point that the changes can no longer be ascribed to normal aging. Likely, they are diagnosed with Parkinson’s disease and a neurologist only begins to suspect atypical parkinsonism when patient X lacks a robust response to dopaminergic treatment and atypical features become more apparent on exam. Recognition of the diagnosis is even harder for subtypes of PSP beyond classic Richardson syndrome [2]. They may have waited several months to see the general neurologist, and, if they are relatively fortunate, are referred to a movement disorder specialty center soon thereafter. Either way, they must wait for receipt of their referral, for medical triage of the referral, and for insurance approval, all of which can take weeks to months. The movement disorders clinic calls them to schedule the initial appointment, which may be booked several months out.

By the time patients with PSP are seen at a movement disorder center, they may be two to three years into their disease trajectory, perhaps longer. They may be even approaching the midpoint of their average eight-year disease duration (the particular estimate depends on their initial symptoms and PSP subtype). Unfortunately, as we lack early biomarkers, these delays in clinical diagnosis and specialty care are the norm. Once they reach specialty care, a new patient visit may be 60 minutes. A return visit may be 30 minutes. During the specialist visit, a now lengthy accumulated history is slowly extracted (further hindered by aspects of the diseases themselves such as bradyphrenia and executive dysfunction), an exam with sufficient subtleties is accomplished, and then there may be only a few minutes left in the visit for conversation and education around medications, care partner needs, and the patient’s priority concerns. When the patient returns months later, they may already have had a few emergency department visits for falls, and the story repeats with more swallow difficulty, recurring urinary tract infections, and the new or worsening development of other subsequent symptoms.

Clinical trials in PSP have taught us that clinical rating scales and imaging findings detectably worsen over 6 months [3], and certain objective gait and balance measures worsen over 3 months [4]. Thus, if we do not see a patient with PSP every 3–4 months, we may be more reactive to symptoms than proactive. We may not always have time to build a strong rapport, and we find ourselves wishing for a stronger foundation when it comes time to talk about more emotionally charged conversations, such as around safety in the home and end of life planning. Patients and families deserve access to the right care at the right time, and ongoing time for these important discussions.

While we lack disease modifying treatments for PSP at present, patients and families still want and benefit from diagnostic clarity as well as from frank discussions around progression and clinical milestones [5, 6]. Neurology teams provide valuable information and insights specific to the diagnosis that inform important life choices. The sooner patients with PSP are referred to the correct specialists, the sooner they can build a comprehensive care team, individual plan of care, and be referred to diagnosis-specific resources. Equally important is that with a definitive diagnosis, an endless stream of new diagnostic tests is no longer a feature of their medical care. Access to specialists makes a difference for quality of life for a rare, quickly progressive neurodegenerative disease like PSP. Additionally, to efficiently test the mechanisms of action of many therapeutic clinical trials, trialists need access to patients at the start of their disease journey. Moreover, the longer it takes to receive a possible or probable clinical diagnosis of PSP, the higher the risk of the patient’s inability to travel for clinical trials due to symptom progression. Therefore, a faster pathway to diagnosis and the right support can both shape the experience of living with PSP and increase the number of positive trials in the future.

Current state of affairs

Healthcare providers managing patients with PSP are aware that long wait times are largely caused by lack of resources and complexities of the healthcare system. The world population is aging and there is a general shortage of neurologists to meet this demand [7]. We are aware of the benefits of team-based care, but also know that more movement disorders and cognitive specialists and affiliated physician assistants, nurses, rehabilitation therapists, and social workers are required [6,8,9,10,11]. The scarcity of neurology providers and difficulty accessing specialty care is even more pronounced in rural and socioeconomically disadvantaged populations [12,13]. Despite different healthcare systems, similar institutional challenges exist in countries outside of the United States. For example, our Canadian colleagues report that it is common for more than one neurologist to see the same patient with PSP before they are transferred to the correct specialty clinic, which creates burden on the medical system as well as confusion around the diagnosis and treatment plans for patients and families. While larger systemic issues are often beyond our control, with proper support at administrative, institutional, and national levels, neurology professionals are best equipped to recognize the unique needs of patients with PSP and suggest actionable solutions to improve access to care for this population.

CurePSP, a United States-based non-profit organization, maintains a network of medical centers across the United States and Canada designated as “CurePSP Centers of Care” (CoCs) for offering specialized care, support services, outreach programming, and research for PSP, CBS and MSA. A foundational goal of the CoC network is to foster collaborations across the centers and in surrounding communities that will lead to the improvement of care for the atypical Parkinsonian syndromes. A 2023 survey of the CoCs found that greater than one-third of center directors reported average clinic waiting times for new appointments of diagnosed or suspected atypical parkinsonism of four months or more (with 14% having waiting times of greater than 6 months). Almost all CoCs noted that they would ideally like to schedule appointments for patients with PSP within one month of referral, but that this would require more faculty, the addition of advanced practice providers (APPs; nurse practitioners or physician assistants), additional space in clinic, an ability to block specific slots or add on more clinic time, and more administrative support. The majority of CoCs reported scheduling 30-min appointments for return patients with PSP. Yet, they also noted how this frequently poses a challenge given the sheer complexity of concerns and treatment plans for atypical parkinsonism, and how time spent with a patient with PSP often goes significantly over allotted time, even more so than in Parkinson’s disease or other movement disorders.

Data from an anonymous online community survey created and disseminated by CurePSP was presented during the 2022 biannual meetings of the CoC network. Of the total of 234 patients and care partners who completed the survey (84.6% PSP, 8.6% CBS, 6.8% MSA), only 20% reported being diagnosed with atypical parkinsonism at their initial appointment with a neurologist and 46% reported an initial diagnosis of Parkinson’s disease. The leading perceived barrier to receiving comprehensive care among the participants was a lack of familiarity with atypical parkinsonism within the medical community. Second was the lengthy process to diagnosis (Fig. 1). In an open-ended, free-text survey question, one participant summarized their access to care challenges: “It was very hard to get my mother diagnosed, and there were essentially no treatments once she was. It became our job to explain PSP to most medical providers she encountered.” These clinical journeys and delays to diagnosis echo previous literature [4,5,13,14].

At the December 2022 CoC network-wide meeting, a PSP care partner and CurePSP volunteer shared: “We saw more than 10 doctors to figure out why my wife started to have difficulties. After a movement specialist diagnosed her with PSP, it still took 6 months to get an appointment at CurePSP center and to assemble her team. As much as the diagnosis is devastating, the uncertainty of diagnosis and lack of information causes additional anxiety. It makes you feel isolated and responsible. You just hope you are doing the right thing for your loved one. My request is for centers to figure out how to see first time patients within a month. Once a patient knows there is a Center of Care (Hope) for a diagnosis they do not understand, it is very frustrating and stressful to wait months to be seen by a knowledgeable doctor who specialized in their disease.”

Our proposal

We propose that medical institutions acknowledge the need for less wait time, and more frequent or longer visits for atypical parkinsonism

New patients with PSP would benefit from being seen within 1–2 months of referral and from longer and less rushed visits. Because every medical institution presents different challenges and strengths, we do not propose a one size fits all model, but rather discuss several scheduling strategies below, acknowledging patient-specific factors as well. Some return patients may benefit from longer visits, while others do better with shorter but more frequent visits. Patient and care partner preference, geographical distance from the clinic, and ability to travel all play a role.

Scheduling Strategy 1:

Author MLD holds 60-min “per provider” slots (provider discretion reserved slots) to accommodate new referrals for her PSP and CBS clinic. Separating out the scheduling matrix such that dedicated per provider slots exist for these patients (and their scheduling is not simply admixed with the scheduling of Parkinson’s visits) helps to reduce wait times. As a CurePSP Center of Care institution and in line with MLD’s clinical and research mission, these slots are pre-negotiated preserved slots filled only by patients with suspected PSP or CBS, but other institutions implementing this model may require a plan to allow schedulers to open any unused slots for patients with other movement disorders several working days prior to the scheduled clinic. Return PSP and CBS patients are seen approximately every 3 months in 30-min slots. Shorter and more frequent visits are helpful for building rapport and minimizing patient fatigue during visits.

Scheduling Strategy 2:

In author MS and MB’s clinics, visits with the neurologist occur less frequently, but systematically alternate with APP visits. This strategy generates a balanced combination of neurological expertise with APP expertise in topics such as symptom management and advanced care planning, and importantly allows the neurologist to accommodate more referrals for PSP.

Scheduling Strategy 3:

Our colleagues in cognitive neurology shared that they utilize a specific, extended follow-up appointment type to deliver a PSP diagnosis and to provide related education for the patient and care partner. After an initial 60-min consultation visit, they schedule a 45-min “diagnosis” discussion visit that is distinct from routine 30-min follow-up visits. In addition to ensuring dedicated time for discussion of the diagnosis, this structure allows for important interval diagnostic procedures such as DAT scans, neuropsych testing, and FDG-PET prior to formal diagnosis delivery. If such a structure is not logistically possible, the initial consult visit could be extended from 60 minutes to 90 minutes to allow more time for discussion.

Scheduling Strategy 4:

At other institutions in the CoC network, less frequent but longer interdisciplinary clinic visits decrease travel burden for patients and families and provide the convenience of seeing rehabilitation therapists, a social worker, a nurse, and a neurologist in the same day. Importantly, providers meet to align an individualized plan of care and prioritize patient goals. Interdisciplinary clinics increase team efficiency and communication, but the duration of the visits (often up to a half day) may be too fatiguing for some patients and can create an administrative burden with scheduling.

Per the 2023 survey to CurePSP CoCs, other attempts to mitigate wait times and expand upon limited clinic visit times include triaging to separate PSP from Parkinson’s disease referrals and limiting physical exam at follow-up visits to allow time for unharried discussion. Dedicated processes for accommodating appointments of diagnosed or suspected atypical parkinsonism were reported by 61% of CurePSP CoCs. Televisits can be especially helpful to conduct follow-up visits for patients who live at a distance from the clinic or for whom travel has become impossible due to symptom progression. We agree with ongoing advocacy efforts to preserve televisits beyond state barriers, and suggest in the interim that neurologists treating atypical parkinsonism consider obtaining additional licenses from bordering states if those states generate significant referrals.

It is important to balance access to care for newly diagnosed patients with PSP with ongoing quality of care for returning patients. Identifying needs and increasing access to neurology for historically marginalized groups, for whom barriers to care are more pronounced, is paramount [13]. For these reasons we emphasize that a degree of flexibility in providers’ schedules is critical. Relying solely on standardized templates generated by clinic administrators cannot accommodate the range of medically appropriate triage in atypical parkinsonism.

We propose a paradigm shift: we need to consider and treat PSP and other atypical parkinsonian syndromes more like we treat amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), rather than like Parkinson’s disease

Our recommendation is to create a care model whereby we treat atypical parkinsonism, not as a distant cousin of Parkinson’s disease, but proactively and with urgency, mirroring how our neuromuscular colleagues treat ALS. Indeed, the bulbar symptoms in atypical parkinsonism render their disease progression closer to ALS than to most cases of Parkinson’s disease after levodopa treatment [15–20].

While here we argue that PSP is not the same as Parkinson’s disease, we acknowledge the intricacy of proper care in Parkinson’s disease [21], and fact that it is more common and thus financially burdensome for the healthcare system. When doing a review of systems in idiopathic Parkinson’s disease, the neurologist largely functions as a geriatrician and medication adjustments necessarily result in chart messages further competing for clinicians’ time. Advanced procedures such as deep brain stimulation are offered and then require management. Interdisciplinary care delivery has been shown to improve quality of care and health-related quality of life in Parkinson’s disease [9,22]. We know that the knowledge and skills required to treat Parkinson’s are invaluable in diagnosing and caring for atypical parkinsonism. However, simply extending Parkinson’s disease care practices to the less common but devastating atypical parkinsonism diagnoses does not satisfy the unmet needs of patients with diseases that progress much faster to disability and death.

Bolstered by the generous support of the ALS Association, interdisciplinary clinics, with colleagues from neurology, social work, rehabilitation, and respiratory therapy, are now the standard in ALS care. Though the specific combination of specialists may be different for PSP and across centers, a solid and integrated team-based approach can enhance the experience with care for patients and boost the morale of clinicians. Interdisciplinary clinics for PSP are being established throughout the country, and the number of CurePSP CoCs has grown from 8 in 2017 to 33 in 2023, although CoCs vary in terms of team makeup, resources, and offerings. CurePSP is presently drafting guidelines for interdisciplinary care for PSP, CBS, and MSA through a dedicated working group, which will be disseminated in a separate publication. In addition, as suggested by a PSP care partner surveyed by CurePSP, we could learn from our oncology colleagues and incorporate a nurse navigator system with more accessible support lines for PSP.

Conclusion

We know that access to care issues in PSP will only increase as patients and their care partners seek emerging diagnostic biomarkers and neuroprotective treatments via clinical trials at academic centers. Due to both increasing demand and geographical and socioeconomic limitations, a proportion of patients with PSP will never be able to receive care at a specialized movement disorder or cognitive center. For this reason, it remains essential to augment efforts to educate general neurologists, APPs, and allied health clinicians in both the recognition and symptomatic treatment of PSP. In this vein, a consensus paper from CurePSP CoCs on best practices for management of PSP and CBS published in 2021 still serves as an important resource for all providers who care for this patient population [8].

On a larger scale, some health systems have successfully taken steps towards elevating their level of support and care for PSP. In the United States, PSP is included as a Compassionate Allowances Condition for expedited consideration for Social Security Disability [23]. In Japan, PSP has been listed on the Specified Disease Treatment Research Program of the Japanese government since 2003 [24].

We welcome further discussion, and we look forward to moving together toward treating PSP as dictated by the tempo of the disease and needs of the affected community, rather than by the conventions of our healthcare systems.
