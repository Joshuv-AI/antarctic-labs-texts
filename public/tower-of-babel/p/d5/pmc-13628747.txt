Life’s essential 8 factors and their impact on the risk of stroke and dementia: a Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Qijia Liu, Yusong Bian, Yidan Hu, Yan Li, Quan Li, Hai Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite advancements in the treatment of stroke and dementia, these conditions still contribute significantly to global health and economic burdens, highlighting the need for prevention. Although these diseases are linked to modifiable lifestyle factors such as diet, physical activity, and smoking, previous studies have lacked a systematic and comprehensive framework to fully elucidate these associations. To bridge this gap, this study comprehensively evaluates the causal links between lifestyle factors in “Life’s Essential 8” (LE8) and the risks of stroke and dementia, providing evidence for prevention strategies.MethodsThis study used a two-sample Mendelian randomization approach with genetic variants as instrumental variables to assess the causal effects of lifestyle factors on stroke and dementia risks. Data were sourced from large-scale GWAS and the UK Biobank, including clinical endpoints for stroke and dementia subtypes. The leading analysis utilized the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method, supplemented by weighted mode, MR-Egger, weighted median and simple mode approaches. Sensitivity analyses included MR-Egger intercept, Cochran’s Q test, leave-one-out analysis and MR-PRESSO.ResultsIncreased grain intake (OR = 0.469; 95% CI: 0.264–0.835; P = 0.010), higher HDL-C levels, and physical activity (OR = 0.927; 95% CI: 0.860–0.999; P = 0.048) reduced stroke risk. Conversely, smoking showed the strongest association with increased stroke risk (OR = 17.375; 95% CI: 1.041–289.999; P = 0.047), followed by genetically predicted high blood pressure for small-vessel stroke (OR = 7.520), prolonged sedentary behavior or TV watching (OR = 1.576), and higher salt intake (OR = 1.229; 95% CI: 1.037–2.291; P = 0.033). Genetically normal blood pressure status was associated with a lower risk of small-vessel stroke (OR = 0.132). For dementia, high salt intake and total cholesterol were associated with increased risk, whereas diastolic blood pressure showed a statistically significant inverse association with dementia risk (OR = 0.765; 95% CI: 0.617–0.949; P = 0.015).ConclusionsThis study emphasizes the role of lifestyle interventions, particularly dietary sodium reduction, smoking cessation, blood pressure management, and appropriate physical activity, in mitigating the risks of stroke and dementia. The findings provide strong evidence for prioritizing modifiable behaviors in public health strategies to alleviate the increasing global burden of these conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12883-026-05104-z.

Introduction

Stroke is the second leading cause of death and the third leading cause of death and disability combined (measured by disability-adjusted life-years lost - DALYs) among non-communicable diseases (NCDs) globally [1]. According to the study by Natalia S. et al., cognitive impairment and dementia remain highly prevalent following stroke, with more than 70% of stroke survivors exhibiting cognitive deficits [2]. The brain damage resulting from a stroke can accelerate the deterioration of cognitive functions and may also expedite the progression of Alzheimer’s disease. Globally, around 50 million people are currently living with dementia, a number that is expected to triple by 2050 [3]. Dementia is a neurodegenerative disorder characterized by a progressive decline in cognitive functions, including memory, executive function, and judgment, with Alzheimer’s disease being the most common form. Other types, such as vascular dementia, also exist. The increasing number of individuals affected by stroke and dementia represents a growing global health and economic burden.Therefore, the impact of preventing the occurrence of stroke and dementia is profound, playing a crucial role in alleviating the global health and economic burden. Encouragingly, research by Gill Livingston [4], Rutten-Jacobs [5], and colleagues indicates that stroke and dementia are closely associated with lifestyle factors, such as diet, physical activity, and the management of comorbidities like hypertension. Therefore, lifestyle modifications offer a promising strategy for reducing the risk of both diseases, and present a crucial avenue for mitigating the associated global health burden.

Modifiable lifestyle factors, such as diet, physical activity, smoking, and alcohol consumption, are increasingly recognized as critical determinants of health outcomes, including the prevention of stroke and dementia [6–8]. These factors present a unique intervention opportunity, as adjustments in these areas can significantly reduce disease risk. In response to the growing emphasis on lifestyle changes, the American Heart Association introduced Life’s Essential 8 (LE8) [9], the components of Life’s Essential 8 include diet, physical activity, nicotine exposure, sleep health, body mass index, blood lipids, blood glucose, and blood pressure. As a novel and comprehensive framework for evaluating cardiovascular health through a set of key modifiable behaviors (Fig. 1). Life’s Essential 8 (LE8) highlights the important role of lifestyle and physiological factors in preventing cardiovascular diseases, including heart disease, stroke, and dementia. While individual factors have been studied extensively, LE8 provides a holistic framework that integrates them into a cohesive metric. Understanding each component’s impact is critical for developing effective prevention strategies. Although causal pathways remain under investigation, targeting modifiable lifestyle behaviors offers a practical approach to reducing the global burden of stroke and dementia.

Mendelian randomization (MR) offers a key advantage in studying the relationship between LE8 and stroke or dementia by utilizing genetic variants as instrumental variables to infer causality [10]. MR helps minimize confounding and reverse causation, common limitations in observational studies [11]. By leveraging the random allocation of alleles, Mendelian Randomization (MR) provides a robust method to assess whether modifying LE8 lifestyle factors can reduce the risk of stroke and dementia. While associations between diet, physical activity, smoking, and alcohol with these conditions have been reported, causality remains unclear. This study uses MR to clarify these relationships, offering stronger evidence on the preventive potential of lifestyle interventions.

Methods

Study population

The UK Biobank is a longitudinal cohort study involving 502,383 participants, aged 40–69 years, who were recruited from 22 centers throughout the United Kingdom between March 2006 and October 2010 [12]. Participants underwent a variety of assessments, including self-reported questionnaires, verbal interviews, physical measurements, and blood sample collection. The study’s protocols received approval from external ethics committees (reference 11/NW/0382), and all participants provided informed consent [13].

Lifestyle factors Measures

The UK Biobank collected data on eight lifestyle factors via touchscreen questionnaires. Sleep indicators included sleep patterns, napping, insomnia, snoring, and duration. Dietary indicators covered intake of meats and beverages such as tea, alcohol, and coffee. Physical activity indicators included walking, vigorous exercise, TV watching, and computer use. Smoking indicators captured current status and age of initiation. Weight indicators comprised BMI, waist-to-hip ratio, body fat percentage, and waist circumference. Blood glucose indicators included fasting glucose, insulin, HbA1c, and anti-diabetic medication use. Blood pressure indicators included systolic, diastolic, pulse pressure, and antihypertensive medication use. Lipid indicators included total cholesterol, LDL, HDL, and lipid-lowering medication use. Detailed data are provided in the supplementary tables.”

Statistical analyses

We conducted a two-sample Mendelian Randomization (TSMR) analysis to assess causal relationships between LE8 lifestyle factors and stroke or dementia. Independent genetic variants associated with LE8 factors (p < 5 × 10⁻⁸) in European ancestry individuals were selected and cross-referenced with the PhenoScanner database to exclude potential confounders (r² < 0.001). MR analysis relies on three core assumptions: relevance (IVs strongly associated with exposure), independence (IVs not associated with confounders), and exclusion restriction (IVs affect outcomes only via exposure). Primary analyses used inverse variance-weighted (IVW) MR, complemented by MR-Egger regression and weighted median (WM) methods to account for potential pleiotropy. Heterogeneity among SNPs was assessed with Cochran’s Q test, and pleiotropy was evaluated using the MR-Egger intercept. Leave-one-out analysis examined the influence of individual SNPs. Analyses were performed using the TwoSampleMR package in R (v4.3.0), and the research workflow is shown in Fig. 2.

Ethics approval and data availability

All participants in the UK Biobank provided informed consent for the use of their anonymized data and biological samples in health-related research, potential contact for substudies, and access to their electronic health records. The UK Biobank is approved by the North West Multi-centre Research Ethics Committee as a Research Tissue Bank, and this study was conducted under application number 36,509 [14]. Data supporting the results are provided within the article and supplementary materials. Original UK Biobank data are available to authorized researchers via formal application (ukbiobank.ac.uk/enable-your-research), summary statistics from the genome-wide association study are freely available to the public [14].

Protective factors within lifestyle influencing stroke-related outcomes

Higher grain intake (OR = 0.47) and consumption of hot drinks at optimal temperatures (OR = 0.42) were associated with lower risk of large artery stroke. Adequate accelerometer-measured physical activity (OR = 0.93) and higher HDL cholesterol reduced risk across stroke subtypes, while dried fruit intake protected against small vessel stroke (OR = 0.51). Genetically normal blood pressure (no medication) was linked to lower small-vessel stroke risk (OR = 0.13), whereas genetically higher blood pressure (requiring medication) increased risk (OR = 7.520), highlighting blood pressure as a clinically actionable factor.

Risk factors within lifestyle contributing to stroke-related outcomes

Among the identified stroke-related risk factors, current smoking showed the largest effect estimate for large artery atherosclerotic stroke (OR = 17.375), followed by genetically predicted high blood pressure requiring medication for small-vessel stroke (OR = 7.520), increased daytime napping (OR = 4.417), prolonged TV watching or sedentary behavior (OR = 1.576), age at smoking initiation (OR = 1.353), and higher salt intake (OR = 1.229). Biomarkers related to blood glucose, lipids, blood pressure, and body weight were also associated with increased risk across stroke subtypes. Detailed statistics are provided in the supplementary.

Lifestyle factors impact on Dementia-related outcomes

Associations between modifiable lifestyle factors and dementia were generally weaker than for stroke. Higher salt intake increased risk of all-cause dementia (OR = 1.633), while vigorous physical activity showed an exceptionally high OR for vascular dementia (OR = 362.814). The extremely large OR and wide confidence interval for vigorous physical activity in relation to vascular dementia indicate substantial statistical imprecision. This unstable estimate may be attributable to weak instrumental variables, limited SNP availability, sparse outcome information, or potential outlier effects. Therefore, this association should be regarded as exploratory, and no specific causal inference was drawn from this result.Other factors, including moderate activity and daytime sleepiness, showed less pronounced associations, possibly due to limited SNP instruments, sample size, or variability in measurement. Discussion

This study identified several key lifestyle factors that are causally associated with the risk of stroke and dementia. Increased grain intake, higher levels of HDL-C, and regular physical activity were found to causally reduce the risk of stroke. Conversely, smoking and genetically predicted high blood pressure showed the largest risk associations with stroke, followed by prolonged sedentary behavior and higher salt intake. For dementia, high salt intake showed a relatively consistent adverse association, whereas the estimate for vigorous physical activity and vascular dementia was highly imprecise and was not interpreted as robust causal evidence.

Visualization of the data (Figs. 3,4,5,6) indicates that blood lipid levels, blood pressure, and body weight have a stronger impact on stroke risk than on dementia, likely due to differences in pathophysiology. Stroke, particularly ischemic stroke, is closely linked to vascular function, and abnormalities in LDL cholesterol, hypertension, and excess body weight directly impair vascular health, promoting atherosclerosis, reduced cerebral blood flow, and thrombus formation [13, 15]. Our study extends these findings by showing that modifiable factors such as diet and physical activity can mitigate the effects of high LDL-C on cerebrovascular health. In contrast, the impact of lipid abnormalities on dementia is more indirect and gradual, occurring via chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and dysregulated lipid metabolism. Despite these differences, stroke and dementia share key risk factors, including hypertension, smoking, and poor diet. We propose a continuum of neurovascular risk, where acute cerebrovascular events like stroke may precede and contribute to later neurodegenerative changes underlying dementia, with timing and magnitude of effects varying across conditions.

Hypertension is a major driver of stroke, particularly hemorrhagic stroke, as sustained high blood pressure damages vascular walls, reduces elasticity, and increases the risk of vessel rupture. It is also strongly linked to cerebral small vessel disease, which impairs white matter and small vessels, further elevating stroke risk. While hypertension is a recognized risk factor for dementia, its effect is more gradual, occurring through cumulative microvascular damage, white matter lesions, and chronic neurovascular dysfunction [16]. Our MR analysis confirms blood pressure as a clinically actionable factor: genetically higher blood pressure markedly increases the risk of small-vessel stroke (OR = 7.520), whereas genetically normal blood pressure is protective (OR = 0.13). Although holistic lifestyle interventions are recommended, our study only supports the individual causal effects of each LE8 factor, without directly comparing multifactorial versus single-factor interventions.While salt intake, alcohol, smoking, and sedentary behavior are not direct measures of blood pressure, they contribute to elevated or uncontrolled BP, which in turn increases stroke risk.

Regarding weight management, genetically lower BMI and waist-to-hip ratio were associated with reduced risk of stroke (OR = 0.85, 95% CI: 0.78–0.93) and dementia (OR = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.85–0.99), indicating that weight reduction is a modifiable factor contributing to lower neurovascular disease risk.

In summary, blood lipid levels, blood pressure, and body weight exert a more immediate and pronounced effect on stroke risk due to their direct impact on vascular health and atherosclerosis progression. In contrast, their influence on dementia is more indirect, acting through long-term neurodegenerative pathways. Importantly, weight reduction through lifestyle interventions appears to be a modifiable strategy that can lower the risk of both stroke and dementia, emphasizing the role of weight management in maintaining vascular and cognitive health.

Lifestyle habits that are easily modifiable, such as diet, were significantly associated with stroke and dementia. Specifically, increased salt intake was causally linked to higher risk of large artery atherosclerotic stroke (OR = 1.541) and all-cause dementia (OR = 1.632), consistent with previous studies [17]. Our Mendelian randomization analysis extends these findings by demonstrating a causal relationship between salt intake and these outcomes. This effect is likely mediated through elevated blood pressure, which promotes vascular damage, atherosclerosis, and reduced cerebral blood flow, thereby increasing the risk of ischemic and hemorrhagic strokes [18]. Sustained hypertension also accelerates small vessel disease, contributing to vascular dementia and other neurodegenerative conditions [19]. These results highlight that reducing salt intake is an actionable strategy to lower blood pressure and prevent both stroke and dementia, with important public health implications.

In our MR analysis, sleep-related findings were mainly driven by daytime napping, which showed an association with increased risk of large artery atherosclerotic stroke. Rather than implying that napping itself directly causes stroke, this result may indicate that excessive daytime sleep reflects an adverse cardiometabolic or sleep-health profile that is genetically linked to cerebrovascular vulnerability. Importantly, this association was more evident for stroke than for dementia-related outcomes, suggesting that sleep-related traits in our dataset may be more closely connected to vascular injury than to neurodegenerative endpoints. Therefore, our findings support the inclusion of sleep health within the LE8 framework, but they should be interpreted as evidence for stroke-related vascular risk rather than as a broad causal conclusion for dementia prevention.

Among all modifiable lifestyle traits identified in our analysis, smoking showed one of the largest effect estimates for stroke. Current smoking was strongly associated with large artery atherosclerotic stroke, and age at smoking initiation was also associated with stroke-related outcomes, indicating that both smoking exposure and smoking timing may be relevant to cerebrovascular risk. These MR findings extend previous observational evidence by supporting a potential causal contribution of smoking-related traits to stroke, particularly vascular stroke subtypes. Compared with the weaker and less consistent findings for dementia, the smoking-related associations were more clearly concentrated in stroke outcomes, reinforcing smoking cessation as a particularly actionable target for stroke prevention within the LE8 framework.Policy Implications.

The findings of this study highlight the critical importance of addressing modifiable lifestyle factors in reducing the risk of stroke and dementia. These findings have significant implications for public health policy, particularly in addressing health disparities across different socioeconomic groups. Lifestyle interventions—such as improving diet, increasing physical activity, and managing sleep health—are effective strategies to prevent both conditions. However, the implementation of such interventions must account for varying levels of access to healthcare and education, especially in low-income communities where these factors often contribute to poorer health outcomes. To translate these findings into actionable policies, targeted strategies should focus on increasing access to affordable healthcare, promoting health education, and encouraging behavioral changes at the community level. Public health campaigns can emphasize the benefits of a healthy lifestyle, but they must be tailored to address the specific needs of different populations, particularly those facing social and economic barriers. For instance, subsidized programs or community-based initiatives could be introduced to ensure that low-income groups have access to nutrition education, physical activity programs, and resources for managing chronic conditions like hypertension and diabetes.

Given resource constraints, it is crucial that interventions be both cost-effective and sustainable. Policymakers should prioritize strategies that leverage existing community resources, such as schools, community centers, and local healthcare providers, to deliver preventive care. Furthermore, integrating lifestyle education into routine healthcare visits and using digital health platforms can help bridge the gap in education and access, particularly for underserved populations. Ultimately, the goal should be to create a supportive environment where all individuals, regardless of their socioeconomic status, have the opportunity to make healthy lifestyle choices that can reduce the risk of stroke and dementia, and improve overall public health.

Limitation

Acknowledging the limitations of our study is important for understanding our findings. While Mendelian Randomization (MR) is a useful method for causal inference, it does have some limitations and potential biases. One issue is weak instrument bias, which happens when the genetic variants used as instruments do not strongly relate to the exposure, potentially leading to inaccurate results. Pleiotropy, where a genetic variant affects more than one trait, can also cause bias if the instrument influences the outcome through pathways other than the exposure. Additionally, population stratification, where genetic differences exist between subgroups in a population, may also lead to confounding.

Although MR is less affected by confounding compared to traditional observational studies, there could still be residual confounding from factors we didn’t measure. In our study, the genetic instruments used may have weak instrument bias. Since this is an exploratory study, the main goal was to examine the potential links between Life’s Essential 8 factors and stroke and dementia. Our sample mainly includes individuals from European populations, so we recognize that the findings may not apply to other populations with different genetic backgrounds or lifestyles. Future research should aim to use stronger instruments, expand the sample size, and include people from different ethnic groups and regions to confirm and broaden our results, while minimizing the effects of weak instruments and population differences.

Conclusion

Our study identifies several modifiable lifestyle and cardiometabolic factors associated with the risk of stroke and dementia. For stroke-related outcomes, increased grain intake was associated with approximately 53% lower odds of stroke, and accelerometer-measured physical activity was associated with approximately 7% lower odds. In contrast, higher salt intake was associated with approximately 23% higher odds of stroke, while smoking was associated with substantially increased odds of stroke. Blood pressure-related traits were also clinically important, with genetically predicted high blood pressure being associated with increased odds of small-vessel stroke, whereas genetically normal blood pressure status showed a protective association. For dementia-related outcomes, higher salt intake was associated with approximately 63% higher odds of dementia, and total cholesterol was associated with approximately 24% higher odds. In contrast, diastolic blood pressure showed an inverse association, corresponding to approximately 24% lower odds of dementia. The extremely large and imprecise estimate for vigorous physical activity and vascular dementia was considered statistically unstable and was not used to support any specific causal inference.

These findings emphasize the importance of modifiable lifestyle factors in neurovascular disease prevention, particularly sodium reduction, smoking cessation, blood pressure management, and appropriate physical activity for stroke prevention. For dementia, the evidence was less consistent, and further studies using stronger genetic instruments and larger dementia subtype datasets are needed to clarify the causal roles of physical activity, blood pressure, and lipid-related traits. Ultimately, comprehensive public health strategies targeting multiple modifiable risk factors may help reduce the global burden of stroke and dementia.
