Cost‐Effectiveness of Prehospital Ambulance Helicopter Transportation of Patients With Presumed Stroke in the Era of Mechanical Thrombectomy
Authors: Nicklas Ennab Vogel, Per Wester, Tobias Andersson Granberg, Lars‐Åke Levin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMechanical thrombectomy has emerged as standard of care in treatment of patients with acute ischemic stroke attributable to large‐vessel occlusion. Efforts to reduce the time from symptom onset to treatment initiation remain critically important to improve functional outcomes in patients treated with either intravenous thrombolysis, mechanical thrombectomy, or both. The use of ambulance helicopters in prehospital acute stroke care could potentially reduce symptom onset to treatment time to mechanical thrombectomy. This study aims to optimize the use of ambulance helicopters in prehospital transportation of patients with presumed acute stroke attributable to large‐vessel occlusion given economic constraints in health care budget.MethodsBy means of an economic model within a decision‐analytical framework, this study evaluates the cost‐effectiveness of complementary ambulance helicopters compared with road ambulances only for prehospital acute transportation of patients with presumed stroke and potential eligibility for mechanical thrombectomy, in regard to the number and locations of ambulance helicopters in Sweden.ResultsA single, optimally located complementary ambulance helicopter was cost‐effective compared with road ambulances only. The most cost‐effective solution in the base‐case scenario with the current 8 thrombectomy centers comprised 13 optimally located helicopters. It reduced mean onset to treatment time with intravenous thrombolysis and onset to treatment time with mechanical thrombectomy by 7.9 and 32.1 minutes, respectively, and produced health gains equal to 91 quality‐adjusted life‐years per year. When the willingness to pay per quality‐adjusted life‐years gained was set at Euro (EUR) 80 000, the incremental net monetary benefit per patient reached EUR 2240. The solution with 13 optimally located helicopters prevailed as the most cost‐effective in the extended scenario analysis with 11 thrombectomy centers, reaching an estimated incremental net monetary benefit per patient at EUR 1754. Furthermore, mean incremental net monetary benefit per patient reached global maximum (EUR 6046) at the geodesic distance of 165.2 km between patient location and the nearest thrombectomy center in the base‐case scenario, and at 238.5 km in the extended scenario with an estimated incremental net monetary benefit per patient at EUR 7994.ConclusionThis study demonstrates the cost‐effectiveness of ambulance helicopters as a complementary mode of transportation to road ambulances. The most cost‐effective solution of complementary ambulance helicopters comprises 13 optimally located heliports across Sweden.Keywords: air ambulances, ambulances, cost‐effectiveness analysis, decision making, ischemic stroke, organizational, thrombectomy

Clinical Perspective

This study succeeds in determining the cost‐effectiveness of complementary ambulance helicopters for prehospital transportation of patients with presumed acute stroke in Sweden, with respect to the number and locations of ambulance helicopters, and the geodesic distance between patient location and nearest thrombectomy center.

The implementation of an appropriate number of optimally located ambulance helicopters produces substantial health gains in patients with acute ischemic stroke by reducing the time from symptom onset to treatment start with intravenous thrombolysis and/or mechanical thrombectomy and comprises a cost‐effective complement to road ambulances in prehospital transportation of patients with presumed acute stroke.

The implementation of an appropriate number of optimally located ambulance helicopters produces substantial health gains in patients with acute ischemic stroke by reducing the time from symptom onset to treatment start with intravenous thrombolysis and/or mechanical thrombectomy and comprises a cost‐effective complement to road ambulances in prehospital transportation of patients with presumed acute stroke.

Because of its crucial role in curbing mortality and functional disability, mechanical thrombectomy (MT) has emerged as standard of care in the acute treatment of patients with acute ischemic stroke (AIS) attributable to anterior circulation large‐vessel occlusion with the rollout of second‐generation stent retriever devices. 1 , 2 The recent advent of next‐generation stent retrievers and large‐bore aspiration catheters may increase reperfusion rates to 90% in patients with large‐vessel occlusion. 3 Furthermore, an estimated 5% to 11% of patients presenting with AIS in the late time window from 6 and up to 24 hours from symptom onset are eligible for treatment with MT. 4 Nevertheless, efforts to reduce the time from symptom onset to treatment initiation remain critically important to improve functional outcomes in patients treated with intravenous thrombolysis (IVT), MT, or both. 5 One way of addressing prevailing issues of inaccessibility to and underuse of MT on the level of national or regional stroke systems of care is to increase the number of comprehensive stroke centers (CSCs) with 24/7 neurosurgical services and/or thrombectomy‐capable stroke centers (TSCs) with neurologic services available within at least 2 hours. 6 , 7 Lately, the use of mobile stroke units has gained attention in prehospital acute stroke management as a strategy associated with reduced onset to treatment time (OTT) for IVT, a higher IVT rate in patients with large‐vessel occlusion, and additional patients receiving IVT within the initial golden hour. However, the mobile stroke unit seems to have no effect on OTT to MT or the MT rate. 8 The use of helicopter emergency medical services (HEMS) in prehospital acute stroke care could potentially reduce OTT to MT and increase the MT rate. A patient‐level pooled analysis of 5 studies showed shorter OTT to MT in patients when HEMS transferred neurointerventionalists to perform thrombectomy at the first admitting hospital compared with the secondary transferred patients from the closest IVT‐ready hospital to a CSC under the so‐called drip‐and‐ship (DS) paradigm. However, the mothership paradigm, where patients are transferred directly to a CSC by HEMS, gave the shortest OTT to MT. 9 There are 10 heliports designated for HEMS operability in Sweden, and patients with presumed acute stroke comprise an estimated 6% of primary missions. 10 Yet, the clinical outcomes and costs associated with HEMS transportation of patients with AIS remain unknown. Evidence of the cost‐effectiveness of patient transportation with HEMS in comparison with road‐based emergency medical services (EMS) in patients with AIS is needed to inform decision‐making. Therefore, this study aims to optimize the use of ambulance helicopters in prehospital transportation of patients with presumed acute stroke attributable to large‐vessel occlusion, given economic constraints in health care budget.

Methods

Data supporting the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

By means of an economic model within a decision‐analytical framework, this study evaluates the cost‐effectiveness of prehospital ambulance helicopters compared with EMS road ambulances for transportation of patients with presumed acute stroke and potential eligibility for treatment with MT, in regard to the number and locations of ambulance helicopters in Sweden.

This study adheres to the reporting guidelines of Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards 2022.

Data

The study material consists of a consolidated data set comprising anonymized individual patient‐level registry data on patients with acute stroke between 2012 and 2017. It integrates EMS callout data from emergency call operator companies with stroke care data from the Swedish stroke registry (RIKSSTROKE), and data on inpatient health care episodes and eventual cause of death from the Swedish National Board of Health and Welfare. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 The data set matches 220 267 EMS records on callouts to patients with presumed stroke, with 124 484 case records of patients discharged from hospital with confirmed stroke diagnosis (encoded with International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision [ICD‐10]). A description of patient characteristics and selection criteria has previously been presented in detail. 5

Derived from the data set, the study population for analysis consists of 18 793 cases of patients with presumed AIS and potential eligibility for treatment with MT. It includes all patients with (ICD‐10–encoded) hospital discharge diagnosis codes for stroke attributable to cerebral infarction (I63) presenting with a National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score of ≥6 at hospital admission (n = 13 355), and all patients treated with MT despite presenting with a National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score of <6 at hospital admission (n = 164) during the study period (Figure S1 and Table S1). Patient transportation by HEMS accounts for 0.5% of all cases.

The study population for analysis also encompasses 5247 patient cases of false positives constituted by patients with intracerebral hemmorhage (I61) (n = 2945) and stroke mimics (n = 2302), to reflect the 28% of false positives reported among patients assessed with the prehospital stroke triage system termed the A2L2 test in Stockholm, Sweden. 15

Geographic Network Analysis

Modeling is performed in a 3‐step process (Figure 1). The first step consists of network analyses conducted within the software environment of ESRI ArcGIS Desktop 10.6.1. The analyses use the national road network from the Swedish national road database to calculate travel times for EMS, and a constructed network of geodesic distances for HEMS travel times. The built‐in Location‐Allocation tool in ArcGIS is used to solve the location problem. The range of heuristics used by the built‐in solvers of ArcGIS to find good solutions includes semirandomized initial solutions, a vertex substitution heuristic, and a metaheuristic.

The number of certified heliports in Sweden is 22. To create a demographically representative sample of potential locations for HEMS operability, an additional 13 uncertified heliports qualify as candidates for locating HEMS heliports in the model. Each patient case is represented by the pickup point of location in a catchment area covering ≈530 000 km2 over land and water, and with an estimated population of ≈10.4 million inhabitants. 16 The model assumes that every callout to a patient with presumed acute stroke receives the highest priority label. On the basis of reported numbers in the literature, the yearly capacity of an ambulance helicopter in terms of number of patient transportations converts into performing a maximum of 598 complete missions per year (Table 1). 17

The model limits the symptom OTT window for MT to 360 minutes. The median time from symptom onset to dispatch of EMS and/or HEMS on incoming emergency call is 51 minutes and derives from the data set of patient cases containing the study population for analysis. The reported median response time for EMS ambulances is 11 minutes (median time elapsed from callout to arrival at patient location), whereas the median time elapsed from hospital arrival to treatment start is 31 and 58 minutes for IVT and MT, respectively. 18 , 19 This leaves 177 minutes of travel time for EMS patient transportation from the pickup point of location to the nearest IVT‐ready hospital and 240 minutes of travel time to the nearest thrombectomy center. Travel times for EMS are calculated using the quickest path in the road network, calculated with road length and maximum allowed speed for each road link.

The fixed time intervals of the OTT window in the case for HEMS patient transportations differ from the EMS ditto with respect to time from callout to arrival at patient location. The median time from callout to takeoff is 6 minutes for HEMS. The travel time from helicopter base to pickup point of location, and then onwards to the nearest thrombectomy center, is calculated using geodesic distances and an average cruising speed of 270 km/h. The imposed onsite time of 20 minutes accounts for the time spent on patient conveyance at the pickup point of location and includes landing, patient onloading, and takeoff. The additional culvert time of 9 minutes accounts for landing, off‐loading, and relocation of patient on arrival at the admitting hospital. 17 Consequently, HEMS has 143 and 216 minutes at its disposal for traveling from helicopter base to patient location, and from patient location to the nearest thrombectomy center for preserving patients’ potential eligibility for treatment with IVT and MT, respectively.

The strategic decision problem is a p‐median facility location‐allocation problem with imposed maximum travel time constraints for patient transportations, and a minimum allocation of 50 MT interventions per year and thrombectomy center. 20 In the case for HEMS, let C be the set of thrombectomy centers, H be the set of helicopter bases where HEMS can be located at, P be the set of patient cases, and T be a 3‐dimensional matrix with travel times from each helicopter base to every patient case, and from every patient case to every thrombectomy center, where thpc = the time from base h via patient case p to center c. The network analysis solves the p‐median facility location‐allocation problem for n = 1, …., 29 helicopter bases. Additionally, it provides a solution comprising the current locations of the 10 helicopter bases in Sweden designated for HEMS operability, and solutions to EMS patient transportations under the DS and mothership paradigms, respectively.

In each solution, the model selects, among the available options, the combination of organizational paradigm (DS or mothership) and mode of transportation (EMS or HEMS) that generates the best clinical outcome in each individual patient case.

Costs and Health Effects

The patient‐level cost items consist of the individually estimated distance‐based cost for patient transportation with EMS or HEMS. They also include staffing and equipment costs associated with different treatment modalities (CVT, IVT, and/or MT); breakdown of these costs and the cost of EMS patient transportation has been described previously. 21 The cost of patient transportation by helicopter derives from statistics on HEMS operability and estimates of the annual cost associated with an ambulance helicopter obtained from literature, and as detailed in Table 1. 17 The modified Rankin Scale (mRS)–categorized first‐ and second‐year costs after stroke are obtained from the literature, and take on a societal perspective (Table 2). 22 Costs are calculated in Swedish krona and converted to year 2021 Euro (EUR) using the mean exchange rate for the year 2021 of EUR 1 = Swedish krona 10.515.

The individual, case‐specific patient age and admission National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score remain unperturbed, whereas calculated OTTs for IVT and MT vary with respect to the number and locations of helicopter bases, mode of transportation, organizational paradigm, and the number of thrombectomy centers in the model. Ergo the availability and clinical effectiveness of treatment modalities in individual patient cases shift across solutions.

Inserting patient age, admission National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score, and the case‐specific OTTs of individual patients into predictive generalized linear models for conservative therapy, IVT only, MT only, and IVT+MT returns the case‐specific, expected mRS 90‐day score in individual patients. Thus, for every solution output from the network analysis, the model estimates the mRS 90‐day score in every patient case, and for all possible combinations of mode of transportation, organizational paradigm, and treatment modality. Among the 16 available combinations, the model selects the mode of transportation, organizational paradigm, and treatment modality that generates the lowest mRS 90‐day score in each patient case, and for every solution. Thereon, utility weights from the literature apply to the selected mRS 90‐day scores. 23

Three‐year survival rates according to mRS categories 0 to 2, 3, 4, and 5 in patients with ischemic stroke are derived from the data set. At 3 years after stroke incidence, the age‐adjusted, annual survival rate trend among patients with ischemic stroke aligns with that of the Swedish reference population. 29 Thus, the age‐specific mortality rates for the Swedish reference population underlie survival rate calculations for the study population from year 4 and onwards. 30

The employed time‐inhomogeneous, discrete time Markov chain attains complete migration to absorption state after constructing 68 transition matrices of half‐year cycle length with patient state transition probabilities from all possible initial states (mRS score, 0–2, 3, 4, 5, and 6) to the absorbing state of mRS score 6 (dead). The discrete time Markov chain patient transition probability distribution and the age and mRS category distributions of the study population underpin the simulation of an adequate number of sequences (n = 100 000) to obtain outcome distributions of the remaining quality‐adjusted life years (QALYs) for patients of each mRS category. 31

Measures of Cost‐Effectiveness Within the Decision‐Analytical Framework

The selected cost‐effectiveness measures derive from patient‐level costs and QALYs and consist of the net health benefit (NHB), the net monetary benefit (NMB), and the incremental NMB (INMB). These metrics are ideal for cost‐effectiveness analysis with >2 comparators because the innate property as fixed quantity measures of cost‐effectiveness makes cross comparisons and ranking of comparisons possible. Nonetheless, although information on the relative cost‐effectiveness of comparators holds value, the center of attention for the cost‐effectiveness analysis comprises comparing solutions of varying number of optimally located HEMS with the solution relying on EMS only for patient transportations by road. The number and optimal locations of HEMS that attain the highest expected INMB, compared with the EMS‐only solution, is chosen as the most cost‐effective solution to HEMS transportation of patients to the nearest thrombectomy center because of presumed AIS and potential eligibility for treatment with MT.

The analysis covers the range between EUR 0 and EUR 200 000 for the maximum willingness to pay (WTP) per QALY gained range.

Modeling Assumptions and Scenarios

By applying the reported national MT rate at 7% of all confirmed cases of patients with AIS in Sweden during the year 2021, the modeling scenarios assume 1229 eligible candidates for treatment with MT per year among an estimated 1708 triage‐positive patient cases. 32 The maximum WTP per QALY gained is set at EUR 80 000 and represents the lowest cost per QALY gained among declined reimbursements of treatments of severe health conditions by the Swedish Dental and Pharmaceutical Benefits Agency. 33

The base‐case scenario assumes the current number and locations of thrombectomy centers in Sweden as per year 2023, comprising 7 CSCs and 1 TSC. It has recently been suggested that the most cost‐effective number of optimally located TSCs to complement the CSCs in Sweden is 4; of which the operational TSC in the base‐case scenario forms part of. 21 Hence, another 3 optimally located TSCs enter the model to complete the setup of thrombectomy centers for the extended scenario analysis.

Furthermore, the analysis sets out to clarify the association between mean INMB per patient and the distance between patient pickup point of location and the nearest available thrombectomy center. It encompasses determining at what distance the mean INMB per patient reaches global maximum in the full range of distances at varying thresholds for maximum WTP per QALY gained, in both modeling scenarios.

Deterministic Sensitivity Analysis

The deterministic sensitivity analysis examines the sensitivity in results by varying the maximum WTP per QALY gained in the range between EUR 0 and EUR 200 000.

Results

Implications of HEMS on Accessibility, OTT, and Organizational Paradigms

The introduction of HEMS in the prehospital stroke care system increased patient population access to MT treatment within 360 minutes from symptom onset. Going from 0 to 1 optimally located helicopter induced an upward leap by 1.8% points (from 95.0% to 96.8%) in the proportion of patients with access to MT, reaching the maximum attainable level (97.9%) with the introduction of 20 optimally located helicopters.

The solution with 20 helicopters also displayed the lowest mean OTT to MT (176.7 minutes), whereas the solution with 12 helicopters produced the lowest mean OTT to IVT (133.2 minutes), resulting in mean time savings equal to 34.0 and 8.1 minutes, respectively, in comparison with patient transportations by EMS only.

With the current 10 heliport locations for HEMS operability in Sweden, an ambulance helicopter is the preferred mode of transportation in 55.1 % of all patient cases. In comparison, with 10 optimally located dittos, it was 71.9%, entailing a reduction in mean OTT to MT by 8.6 minutes (189.3 versus 180.7 minutes).

The introduction of HEMS had implications for the ratio of DS/mothership too. With EMS only, the DS paradigm was favored (from a cost‐effectiveness perspective) in 3 of 10 patient cases. It was halved to a ratio of 3/20 with 7 helicopters and reached minimum of 1/10 with 20 helicopters (Table 3).

Treatment Modalities, Costs, and Health Outcomes

Although each solution with HEMS (n = 1, …, 29 helicopters) yielded a higher proportion of eligible patients with AIS receiving treatment with MT (MT only or IVT+MT) compared with EMS only, the highest attainable proportion was reached with and plateaued at 14 optimally located helicopters. Conversely, this solution marked the lowest proportion of patients receiving treatment with IVT only, too.

Costs kept soaring with every additional helicopter. Thus, the solution with 29 helicopters inflicted the highest incremental cost compared with EMS only, corresponding to a total of EUR 12.3 million in additional costs per year. The largest QALY gain per year, equal to 94 QALYs, was generated by the solution with 16 helicopters; it was repeated in solutions with 20, 25, and 29 helicopters.

The solution with 13 optimally located helicopters was the most cost‐effective in terms of estimated NHB and NMB (Figure 2). With the MT rate set at 7% and the maximum WTP per QALY gained set at EUR 80 000, the annually aggregated NHB and NMB summed up to equivalently 1657 QALYs and EUR 132.6 million, respectively, with the INMB per patient estimated at EUR 2240. Complementary HEMS becomes cost‐effective already with the implementation of a single, optimally located helicopter in comparison with EMS only. The NHB and NMB increased steadily with every additional helicopter and peaked at 13 helicopters. Thenceforth, the cost‐effectiveness of complementary HEMS plunged before turning into cost inefficiency with the implementation of ≥19 helicopters (Table 3).

The extended scenario analysis (with 7 CSCs and 4 TSCs) exhibited a similar pattern across competing solutions; the solution with 13 optimally located helicopters prevailed as the most cost‐effective complementary HEMS strategy, and the solution with 19 helicopters still marked the turning point for the cost‐effectiveness of complementary HEMS. The addition of 3 (optimally located) TSCs encompassed an overall improvement in MT accessibility across solutions. The solution with 21 optimally located helicopters provided access to MT treatment for 99.1% of eligible candidates. It contributed to higher estimates of NHB and NMB in the baseline comparator (the solution with EMS only). Indeed, the NHB and NMB increased in all solutions with helicopters too. Yet, the addition of 3 TSCs induced decrements in the relative NMB, making solutions with helicopters generally less cost‐effective in comparison with EMS only. Thus, the most cost‐effective solution (with 13 helicopters) had an estimated INMB per patient at EUR 1754 in the extended scenario analysis (Table ).

The analysis established at what distance between patient location and thrombectomy center that complementary HEMS becomes most cost‐effective compared with EMS only. The mean INMB per patient reached global maximum (EUR 6046) at the geodesic distance of 165.2 km in the base‐case scenario. In the extended scenario, global maximum was attained at the geodesic distance of 238.5 km, when the mean INMB per patient reached EUR 7994. The cost‐effectiveness of complementary HEMS perished at geodesic distances beyond 330.5 and 399.5 km, in the base‐case and extended scenario, respectively (Figure 3A). The geodesic distance required for attaining global maximum mean INMB per patient as well as the breakpoint distance for ceased cost‐effectiveness of complementary HEMS remained largely unwavered by varying thresholds for the maximum WTP per QALY gained in the base‐case scenario (Figure 3B).

The deterministic sensitivity analysis affirmed the robustness in results from varying the maximum WTP per QALY gained. The strategy with 13 optimally located helicopters prevailed as the most cost‐effective of all competing solutions in the maximum WTP per QALY gained interval between EUR 49 782 and EUR 200 000 in the base‐case scenario, and between EUR 42 351 and EUR 200 000 in the extended scenario. The baseline comparator of EMS only was the most preferrable solution in terms of cost‐effectiveness for the lower range of maximum WTP per QALY gained.

In health care systems characterized by a maximum WTP per QALY gained that surpasses the threshold value, HEMS becomes a cost‐effective complement to EMS only. When the thrombectomy center density is somewhere in between 0.77 and 1.05 per 1 million inhabitants in a catchment area of ≈500 000 km2, the most cost‐effective density of optimally located helicopters for patient transportations of MT‐treatment eligible candidates is ≈1.24 per 1 million inhabitants, equivalent to 13 optimally located helicopters in Sweden. Results indicate that HEMS is a cost‐effective complement to EMS only, up to geodesic distances of 400 km, and up to least 330 km between patient location and thrombectomy center.

The most cost‐effective solution of optimally located helicopters was insensitive to variations in the studied range of values for the maximum WTP per QALY gained and thrombectomy center densities.

Discussion

This interdisciplinary study combines individual, patient‐level registry data with current evidence from the literature to model and optimize prehospital transportation of patients with presumed acute stroke in Sweden, within the decision‐analytical framework of cost‐effectiveness modeling. Results show that ambulance helicopters comprise a cost‐effective complementary mode of transportation to EMS road ambulances in transporting patients with presumed AIS to thrombectomy centers, when the maximum WTP per QALY gained is set at EUR 80 000. The most cost‐effective number of optimally located ambulance helicopters in this study corresponds to a density of ≈1.24 ambulance helicopters per 1 million inhabitants, when the aggregated density of CSCs and TSCs ranges between 0.77 and 1.05 per 1 million inhabitants in a catchment area of 500 000 km2.

The study succeeded in determining at what geodesic distance between patient pickup point of location and the nearest thrombectomy center that the cost‐effectiveness of patient transportations by ambulance helicopters reaches global maximum. Furthermore, analysis managed to unveil the cost‐effective range of distances for HEMS patient transportations.

The sensitivity analysis demonstrated that the cost‐effectiveness of complementary ambulance helicopters arises at maximum WTP per QALY gained values above EUR 40 000 or EUR 50 000, depending on the density of thrombectomy centers in regard to population size and catchment area. The selected number of optimally located ambulance helicopters at the breakpoint value for cost‐effectiveness prevailed as the most cost‐effective solution for the remaining WTP per QALY gained range up to EUR 200 000. Conversely, results suggest that EMS only constitutes the most cost‐effective mode of transportation in patients triaged as eligible candidates for treatment with MT, when the maximum WTP per QALY gained falls short of EUR 40 000, or even EUR 50 000.

Contrary to a systematic literature review and meta‐analysis on HEMS transportation of patients with AIS, this study estimated substantial improvement in patient outcomes, attributable to increased accessibility and shortened OTT to MT. The divergency in results may in part be attributable to disparities in data and methods underlying analysis of HEMS in comparison with EMS. This study estimated the shortest travel times of each individual patient case for patient transportations by HEMS and EMS, under the mothership paradigm, and under the DS paradigm for the latter, for each and every scenario covered in the analysis. Thus, the same study population underpins all comparators in this study, whereas the meta‐analysis pooled data into 2 heterogeneous patient groups to compare HEMS with EMS on the basis of between‐group analysis. 34

The mothership paradigm with patient transportation by ambulance helicopter was preferrable in the large majority of patient cases in the most cost‐effective solution, displaying a mean OTT to MT similar to previous findings. 9

In line with previous findings on the cost‐effectiveness of optimally locating CSCs and TSCs, this study implies opportunities guided by the cost‐effectiveness framework of modeling for furthered development of acute stroke care services in the era of MT. 21

The comprehensive, consolidated data set that connects data from emergency call operator services with data from national health care and quality registries to create individual, patient‐level registry data provides detailed information on every patient‐case over a 6‐year study period, and a solid foundation for conducting cost‐effectiveness analyses, that brings strength to this study. Moreover, the cross‐sectional approach of applied health economics and operational research facilitates a comprehensive take on the economic evaluation of prehospital modes of transportation for patients with presumed acute stroke, enabled by predictive models that estimate the mRS 90‐day score for each mode of transportation, treatment modality, and organizational paradigm, in each and every patient‐case, and for all solutions. 5

Although the solvers provided by ArcGIS do not guarantee mathematically optimal solutions, it is unlikely that it has had any major impact on study results; all the numerical analyses are valid for the presented solutions. The consolidated patient‐level registry data for analysis stem from a 6‐year study period between 2012 and 2017. Therefore, results may not reflect current levels of reperfusion rates, and patient outcomes from IVT, MT, and IVT+MT, which poses another limitation to this study. Moreover, the reliability of results is limited to the Swedish health care setting.

Juxtaposing results from the base‐case and extended scenario analyses offers an indicative glimpse into the possible feasibility of developing a more comprehensive modeling framework in an attempt to provide even more cost‐effective solutions by combined optimization of thrombectomy centers and ambulance helicopters. Nevertheless, more studies on the cost‐effectiveness of ambulance helicopters in transportation of patients with presumed AIS and potential eligibility for treatment with MT are warranted to confirm results of this study.

Conclusions

Combining medicine with health economics and operational research may provide unique opportunities for modeling and optimizing prehospital modes of transportation in acute stroke care management of patients with presumed stroke and potential eligibility for treatment with MT. This study demonstrates that ambulance helicopters are a cost‐effective complementary mode of transportation to EMS road ambulances, and that the most cost‐effective solution of complementary ambulance helicopters comprises 13 optimally located heliports across Sweden.
