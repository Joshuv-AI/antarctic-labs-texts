Bidirectional Control of Emotional Behaviors by Excitatory and Inhibitory Neurons in the Orbitofrontal Cortex
Authors: Jihoon Kim, Mijung Choi, Jimin Lee, Inah Park, Kyungjin Kim, Han Kyoung Choe
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) plays a crucial role in mood disorders; however, its specific role in the emotional behaviors of mice remains unclear. This study investigates the bidirectional control of emotional behaviors using population calcium dynamics and optogenetic manipulation of OFC neurons. Fiber photometry of OFC neurons revealed that OFC excitatory neurons consistently responded to the onset and offset of aversive conditions, showing decreased activation in response to anxiogenic and stressful stimuli, including tail suspension, restraint stress, and exposure to the center of the open field. The selective activation of excitatory neurons in the OFC reduced the time spent in the center of the open field, whereas optogenetic activation of inhibitory neurons in the OFC induced the opposite behavioral changes. We also provided a brain-wide activation map for OFC excitatory and inhibitory neuron activation. Our findings demonstrate that excitatory and inhibitory neurons in the OFC play opposing roles in the regulation of emotional behaviors. These results provide new insights into the neural mechanisms underlying emotional control and suggest that targeting these specific neuronal populations may offer novel therapeutic strategies for emotional disorders.Keywords: Orbitofrontal cortex, Emotion, Optogenetics, c-Fos mapping

INTRODUCTION

Mood disorders, such as major depression and anxiety disorders, are debilitating psychiatric disorders that affect a significant portion of the world’s population [1, 2]. Structural and functional abnormalities of brain regions, such as the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), gyrus rectus, amygdala, and hippocampus, are associated with mood disorders [2-4]. Among these regions, the OFC is an important region linked to depression. A clinical study has revealed that the size of the OFC is reduced in patients with depression [5]. The activity of the OFC is differentially associated with depressive symptoms depending on the subregion. Specifically, posterolateral and medial OFC (mOFC) activity negatively correlates with depressive symptoms, whereas anteromedial OFC activity positively correlates with depressive symptoms [6]. Recent studies have also reported abnormalities in the functional connectivity of major OFC subregions in patients with depression [7, 8]. Clinical evidence also points to anatomical and functional changes in the OFC in patients with anxiety disorders. In post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), the size of the mOFC is decreased, and activation is attenuated in this area [2, 9]. Alterations in the OFC have also been reported in patients with panic disorders. The blood flow of the mOFC decreases during spontaneous panic attacks [10], while the resting-state activity of the mOFC is elevated in patients with panic disorders [11].

In both humans and mice, the OFC is located on the ventral surface of the frontal cortex. However, at the cellular level, there are structural differences in the OFC of humans and mice; therefore, the homology of the OFC between species should be studied at the functional level rather than at the structural level [12, 13]. Studies on rodents have revealed that their OFCs exhibit functional similarities to that of humans in decision-making, such as devaluation and reversal tasks [14, 15]. In humans, the OFC plays a significant role in emotional control, as evidenced by its involvement in emotional inhibition and the somatic marker hypothesis. Early studies suggested that the rodent OFC also contributes to emotional regulation, with lidocaine injections into the mouse OFC enhancing anxiety responses [16]. Pharmacological inhibition of the OFC using muscimol decreased anxiety behavior in an open-field test in rats [17]. In contrast, another study reported no effect of muscimol inhibition in the elevated plus maze [18]. These discrepancies warrant cell type-specific manipulation and monitoring of the OFC neurons during emotional processing.

Behavioral techniques for evaluating emotional behaviors related to depression and anxiety in rodents are relatively standardized, providing a platform for consensus among researchers. The development of optogenetics and calcium activity measurement techniques, combined with behavioral studies, has provided insights into the neuron-specific functions of brain regions associated with emotional behavior [19, 20]. In this context, we studied the role and responses of the OFC by applying cell-specific optogenetics and fiber photometry to behavioral paradigms, including the tail suspension test (TS), restraint stress test (RST), and open field test (OFT). Additionally, we aimed to lay the groundwork for cell type-specific circuit studies by creating brain-wide activity maps based on c-Fos expression following optogenetic stimulation of either excitatory or inhibitory neurons in the OFC.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Mice

All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Daegu Gyeongbuk Institute of Science and Technology (DGIST). Adult male C57BL6/J mice were reared in standard cages (16×36×12.5 cm) and housed under 12-h light/dark cycle (00:00 to 12:00 dark) with water and food available ad libitum. Mice were weaned from same-sex littermates at approximately 3~4 weeks of age. Mice of age 8~10 weeks were used in the experiments. After viral injection and optical fiber implantation, the mice were housed individually. All behavioral experiments were performed under dimly lit red light conditions.

Production of adeno-associated virus

Adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors targeting OFC interneurons were packaged as previously described, with modifications [21]. After the production of AAVs, their genomic copies were quantified using real-time qPCR. The AAVs were diluted in PBS to a final concentration of 1.07×1013 genomic copies per milliliter (GC/ml) for AAV.PHP.eB-CAG-EGFP and 1.05×1013 GC/ml for AAV.PHP.eB-mDlx-ChR2-mCherry before the experiments.

Stereotaxic surgery

The mice (8~10 weeks old) selected for the experiments were anesthetized with a mixture of ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) in 0.9% saline and placed in a stereotaxic frame (RWD Life Science) for viral injection. After disinfection and craniotomy above the target region, the virus was injected using a nanoliter injector system (World Precision Instruments) at an injection rate of <100 nL/min. The coordinates for the OFC virus injection were 2.65 mm AP, -1.25 mm ML, and -1.30 mm DV. Additionally, a 400 µm optical fiber with a numeric aperture of 0.39 (Neurophotometrics) was used for all experiments. For fiber photometry experiments, we injected 200 nL of a 1:1 mixture of AAV8-CaMKII-Cre-mCherry (1.0×1012 GC/ml; Addgene) and AAV1-syn-FLEX-jGCaMP7s-WPRE (2.0×1013 GC/ml; Addgene) into the OFC region. The optical fiber was placed unilaterally, 100 µm above the injection site. For optogenetic experiments, we injected 200 nL of AAV.PHP.eB-CAG-EGFP, AAV5-CaMKII-ChR2-EGFP (1.07×1013 GC/ml; Addgene), or AAV.PHP.eB-mDlx-ChR2-mCherry into the OFC region, and the optical fiber was placed unilaterally, 100 µm above the injection site.

Fiber photometry recording and analysis

Fiber photometry was performed as previously described [22]. Briefly, 465 nm (F465) and 405 nm (F405) LEDs were used for calcium-dependent and -independent excitation of GCaMP7s fluorescence, and the emitted signal was passed through a low-pass filter and demodulated using Synapse software (Tucker-Davis Technologies (TDT)). The F405 signal was used to correct motion artifacts and was normalized using the formula dF/F=(F465 – fitted F405)/fitted F405.

For peri-event histogram (PETH) analysis of tail suspension and restraint, the dF/F signal was sliced and aligned to the onset of events. The signal before the event onset was used as the baseline (FB) for z-normalization. Fz was calculated as (dF/F – mean(FB))/std(FB). To quantify the global calcium level, the mean and standard deviation of the entire session were used for the calculation: Fz=(dF/F – mean(dF/F))/std(dF/F).

Behavioral recording and analysis

During the tail suspension and restraint experiments, the behavior of the mice was recorded using a USB camera (10 frames per second), and the recorded video and fiber photometry signals were aligned using Synapse software. For open-field recording, EthoVision (Noldus, EthoVision XT 13) was used to capture and track animal locations. External cues were used to align the EthoVision animal tracking and fiber photometry signals.

In vivo optogenetic stimulation

Optogenetic stimulation was applied using a laser (MBL-FN-473-200mW; CNI laser), and Synapse software was used to control laser delivery. A 473 nm light was irradiated onto the animal through a 400 µm patch cord (doric) at a frequency, pulse width, and light intensity of 20 Hz, 10 ms, and 10 mW, respectively.

Stress exposure

For the tail suspension test, the mice were first attached to the patch cord and left in their home cage for 2 min. The tails of the mice were then grabbed by the experimenter’s hand for 15 s and freed for 1 min. This process was repeated three times to average the animals’ responses to tail suspension. To calculate the PETH of the tail suspension, a fiber photometry signal from 5 s before to 15 s after the onset of the tail suspension was used. For restraint, the mice were first attached to the patch cord and left in their home cages for 2 min. Subsequently, the mice were put in a 50 ml tube for 2 min and released into their home cage for another 2 min. For the PETH analysis, a fiber photometry signal from 5 s before to 5 s after the onset and offset was used. For the Fz response analysis, the minimum and maximum Fz values during 0~5 s from the onset or offset were used.

Struggling and freezing analysis

For quantifying struggling behavior, the end of the optic cannula was manually tracked at 1 s intervals using a custom-written MATLAB script. Head movement exceeding 5-pixel distance was classified as ‘Struggle’ and the rest was classified as ‘Freeze’. The fiber photometry signal was down-sampled to 1 Hz to align the head movement and calcium activity.

Open field test

For the open field test, the mice were first attached to the patch cord and placed in the center of an open field arena (29×29 cm). The center was defined as a square located in the middle of the open-field region with side lengths equal to half the length of the region. The area outside the center was defined as the border area. For the fiber photometry experiments, the position and calcium activity of the animal were recorded for 10 min. For the Fz comparison between the open and border arenas, Fz normalized to the global calcium level was used. For calcium peak analysis, the ‘islocalmax’ function was used to identify calcium transient. For optogenetic stimulation, the mice were first recorded for 5 min as a pre-stimulation baseline, and then optogenetic stimulation was applied for 5 min. The recorded videos were analyzed for quantification including center/border duration, locomotion, and velocity using EthoVision. Rearing behavior was manually labeled either ‘rearing’ or ‘not-rearing’, with rearing defined as instances where both front paws were detached from the floor.

Immunohistochemistry and confocal microscopy

To measure neural activity in response to the activation of OFC subpopulations, the mice were perfused and fixed in 1X PBS and 4- PFA after blood collection. Brains were isolated and post-fixed in 4- PFA at 4oC overnight. Following post-fixation for 24 hours, brains were incubated in 30% sucrose-containing PBS and then frozen in an OCT compound. Brains were sectioned at a thickness of 50 µm, and every sixth section was used for subsequent analysis. Sections were blocked in 0.3- triton X-100 and 5- normal donkey serum for 1 h at room temperature. They were incubated overnight at 4oC with a primary antibody (anti-c-Fos; #226004; SySy) diluted 1:1,000 in the blocking solution. Sections were washed in PBS-T for 5 min, three times, and then incubated overnight at 4oC in a secondary antibody diluted 1:500. The sections were washed again and stained with DAPI (1:1,000) before mounting on a glass cover. Images were obtained using a Nikon C2+ confocal microscope unless otherwise mentioned.

Automated c-Fos quantification

Brain-wide c-Fos quantification was performed as previously described [23]. Briefly, images were captured using a slide scanner (KF-FL-005; KFBIO) at 20X magnification. DAPI images were aligned with the Allen Brain Atlas (available from https://atlas.brain-map.org) using DeepSlice [24], followed by manual adjustment with QuickNII [25] and VisuAlign (RRID: SCR_017978). c-Fos images were used to train ilastik [26]. Both the atlas and c-Fos detection data were combined and quantified for each brain region using Nutils [27]. Brain-wide c-Fos expression was visualized in 3D using BrainRender [28].

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed using Prism (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA), MATLAB (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA), and R software (version 4.2.3). Paired t-tests were used for calcium signal comparison and optogenetic experiments in each group. Pearson’s correlation assessed the relationship between behavioral measurement and calcium signal. One-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey HSD post hoc analysis, was used to compare optogenetic experiments among the three groups. Repeated measure two-way ANOVA, followed by Bonferroni post hoc analysis, was used to compare locomotion, velocity, and rearing behavior in optogenetic experiments. Principal component analysis (PCA) was used for brain-wide c-Fos comparison across animals. Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by Dunn’s test, was used for ChR2/c-Fos colocalization comparisons. Additionally, a pairwise Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for c-Fos level comparison among the three groups.

RESULTS

OFCCaMKII neurons are inhibited during tail suspension

First, we determined the activity profiles of excitatory neurons in the OFC during adverse conditions. We injected a mixture of AAV vectors containing Cre DNA recombinase driven by the calcium–calmodulin (CaM)-dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII) promoter and AAV expressing the Cre-dependent calcium indicator GCaMP7s into the OFC of wild-type mice (Fig. 1A, B). A fiber optic cannula was placed above the AAV injection area to record the bulk calcium activity in freely behaving mice using a fiber photometry system (Fig. 1C). Sinusoidally modulated 405 nm (calcium-independent signal) and 465 nm (calcium-dependent signal) LED lights were used to excite GCaMP7s, and the emitted signal was demodulated and corrected for motion artifacts and fluorescence decay to accurately represent neural activity.

We first analyzed TS—a mild acute physical stress—and monitored the responses of OFC excitatory neurons (Fig. 1D). Upon TS, we observed a gradual decrease in the calcium levels of OFCCaMKII neurons, which was sustained throughout TS (Fig. 1E). When TS was terminated, a strong increase in the calcium levels of OFCCaMKII neurons was observed, with a transient mild dip (Fig. 1F, G). Although not all animals respond to the tail suspension in the same extent, average response was above the statistical threshold. These results indicates that OFCCaMKII neurons responded bidirectionally to both the onset and offset of mild physical stress.

OFCCaMKII neurons are inhibited during RST with transient recovery during active coping behavior

Next, we investigated OFC activity during RST, which has been reported to be involved in various stress- and anxiety-related responses (Fig. 2A) [29]. The results of RST revealed a gradual and strong decrease in the calcium levels in OFCCaMKII neurons, which persisted throughout the administration of RST (Fig. 2B). This outcome is similar to that of the TS. This reduced calcium level was restored when the RST was terminated, suggesting that OFCCaMKII activity is not specific to certain stressors but rather respond to various types of stress exposure (Fig. 2 C, D). Additionally, upon comparing calcium reduction in TS and RST, we observed a greater decrease in calcium levels during RST than during TS. This suggests that OFCCaMKII activity encodes the magnitude of the stress exposure (TS: -2.329±1.411; RST: -6.510±2.472; n=7, p=0.0193 by paired t-test).

We then investigated whether OFCCaMKII activity responds directly to stress exposure or is dynamically modulated by other internal factors such as active stress coping [30]. Struggling during the RST is an active stress-coping behavior, and by measuring head movement, we quantified struggling bouts during the RST (Fig. 2E). When aligned with the OFCCaMKII activity, we observed that the head movements and calcium activity shared similar temporal dynamics (Fig. 2F). To observe how these calcium fluctuations correspond to animals’ behavioral states, we categorized animals’ behavior into either ‘struggling’ and ‘freezing’ from animals’ head movement and found that OFCCaMKII calcium activity was significantly higher during active struggling than freezing behavior (Fig. 2G). Furthermore, we found a that the rise of OFCCaMKII calcium activity precedes than the onset of head movement (Fig. 2H; Fz, F (2.399, 14.39)=17.48, p<0.0001 by RM one-way ANOVA; movement, F (1.777, 10.66)=24.55, p=0.0001 by RM one-way ANOVA), suggesting that OFCCaMKII activity encodes not only the state of stress exposure but also active stress-coping activity during the stress exposure.

OFCCaMKII neurons dynamically respond to an anxiogenic environmental condition

Since OFCCaMKII activity is dynamically modulated by stress and active stress-coping behaviors, we further investigated the response of OFCCaMKII activity to environmental conditions with different levels of stress-inducing properties during free exploration. The OFT is a well-established behavioral paradigm that allows for voluntary spatial navigation between anxiogenic and anxiolytic area. We measured OFCCaMKII activity during the OFT, and calcium signals were aligned to the animal’s location to compare OFCCaMKII activity in anxiety-inducing or anxiety-relieving sections (Fig. 3A, B).

The mice spent significantly less time in the center of the arena compared to the border, and calcium signals were also reduced in the center relative to the border (Fig. 3C). The average calcium level, calcium transient frequency, and average amplitude of calcium transients were significantly lower at the center than at the border (Fig. 3D~F). Furthermore, we found a significant negative correlation between OFCCaMKII calcium activity and animal occupancy in the center (Fig. 3G). These suggest that beyond the anxiety-induced reduction in calcium activity, OFCCaMKII calcium activity was also reduced in anxiety-inducing areas during voluntary movement. To be noted, no correlation between OFCCaMKII calcium activity and animal movement (Fig. 3H) was observed, indicating that the observed differences in OFCCaMKII calcium activity between the center and border were not caused by movement. Interestingly, we also observed that the extent of center exploration and the average calcium levels during this exploration exhibited a negative but non-significant correlation (Fig. 3I). These findings suggest a potential non-linear relationship between the way OFCCaMKII neurons respond to anxiogenic condition and how it might modulate the exploration of anxiety-inducing environments.

OFCCaMKII and mDlx neurons bidirectionally control anxiety-related behavior

Considering the complex neural activity of OFCCaMKII neurons during both involuntary and voluntary adverse conditions, we examined the relationship between OFC activity and adverse-related behaviors. We used an optogenetic method to manipulate the neural activity of each excitatory and inhibitory neural subpopulation in the OFC during the OFT (Fig. 4A). We injected AAV vectors expressing channelrhodopsin-2 (ChR2) under either the CaMKII promoter or the inhibitory neuronal marker murine distal-less homeobox (mDlx) promoter [31, 32]. We injected an EGFP-expressing vector under the CAG promoter as a control. A fiber-optic cannula was placed over the OFC region to deliver the laser during the behavioral assay (Fig. 4B). Post-mortem analysis of the OFC region revealed a significantly higher percentage of ChR2+ neurons co-labeled with c-Fos, a neural activity marker, in the OFCCaMKII-ChR2 group (40.05%±28.11%, n=7; control vs. OFCCaMKII-ChR2, p=0.0158 by Dunn’s test) and the OFCmDlx-ChR2 group (36.65%±20.94%, n=6; control vs. OFCmDlx-ChR2, p=0.0134 by Dunn’s test) compared to the control group (6.87%±6.81%, n=6; H(2)=10.87, p=0.0012, Kruskal–Wallis test).

We conducted the OFT and measured the center duration with or without optogenetic stimulation in each experimental group. Compared to the baseline, we found no changes in the center duration in the control group during optical stimulation (Fig. 4D). However, we observed a significant reduction in the center duration in the OFCCaMKII-ChR2 group (Fig. 4E) and a significant increase in the center duration in the OFCmDlx-ChR2 group during optogenetic stimulation (Fig. 4F). Comparing these groups revealed a significant fold change: a decrease in the OFCCaMKII-ChR2 group and an increase in the OFCmDlx-ChR2 group compared to the control group (F (2, 17)=18.14, p<0.0001 by one-way ANOVA). In moving distance, we observed no significant differences with or without optogenetic stimulation (Fig. 4H~J), nor were there any differences between the groups (Fig. 4K; F (2, 17)=0.8562, p=0.4423 by one-way ANOVA). We further examined other behavioral features during open field test, including locomotion, maximum velocity, and rearing behavior in an one-minute resolution (Fig 4L, M, N). We found significant decrease in locomotion (F (18, 153)=1.641, p=0.0564 by RM two-way ANOVA) and rearing behavior (F (18, 153)=2.072, p=0.0093 by RM two-way ANOVA) in OFCCaMKII-ChR2 group shortly after optogenetic stimulation, which has been associated with high anxiety [33]. Maximum velocity was rarely affected by optogenetic stimulation of either neural population. Overall, these results suggest that OFC activity can bidirectionally modulate anxiety-related behaviors via the activation of OFCCaMKII or OFCmDlx neurons, suppressing or promoting exploration of anxiety-inducing environments, respectively.

Brain-wide functional mapping of OFCCaMKII and OFCmDlx neural network

OFC neurons project to various cortical and subcortical areas, including the amygdala, basal ganglia, and mediodorsal thalamic areas [34]. To link the previously built anatomical map of the OFC to its functions, we constructed a brain-wide functional map following population-specific optogenetic manipulation of the OFC. To investigate the effects of the activation of each OFC subpopulation on OFC downstream circuits, we optogenetically stimulated either excitatory or inhibitory OFC neuronal subpopulations and stained brain sections covering the entire forebrain with an anti-c-Fos antibody to visualize neural activation (Fig. 5A). The images of these brain sections were analyzed through a pipeline composed of machine-learning-based atlas registration, c-Fos detection, and automatic quantification of c-Fos-positive nuclei in each brain region (Fig. 5B).

We first compared the brain-wide activation patterns using PCA (Fig. 5C), which revealed separate brain-wide activation patterns among the control, OFCCaMKII, and OFCmDlx activation groups. This suggests that each subpopulation has a distinct impact on different OFC downstream regions. Notably, the clusters of OFCCaMKII and OFCmDlx did not align linearly opposite to the cluster of controls. Rather, both optogenetically stimulated clusters were located in the positive direction of the control cluster in the PC1 axis, albeit with a difference in the PC2 axis. This prompted a detailed examination of both OFCCaMKII-activated and OFCmDlx-activated brain regions. We compared c-Fos induction across brain regions in the OFCCaMKII and OFCmDlx activation groups with that in the non-stimulated control group. Both OFCCaMKII and OFCmDlx induced c-Fos expression in most brain regions (Fig. 5D), whereas either OFCCaMKII or OFCmDlx activation suppressed c-Fos expression in other brain regions. We mapped key brain regions that were highly responsive to OFCCaMKII activation (11 regions) and other regions that were more responsive to OFCmDlx activation (four regions) in comparison with previously reported OFC projection [35] (Fig. 5E~G). We also examined these brain areas for the expression of fluorescent protein conjugated to ChR2. We detected OFCCaMKII neural projection in the majority of cortical areas, and most of the subcortical areas except lateral septum (LS) ventromedial hypothalamus (VMH), and arcuate nucleus of the hypothalamus (ARH). We could not detect fluorescent signals from OFCmDlx neuron, suggesting inhibitory neuron are more likely to function within the OFC than long-range projection. When we compared OFCCaMKII neural projection pattern with c-Fos activity, we found most regions were highly activated after optogenetic stimulation. Dorsal and lateral cortical structures exhibited high activation following OFCCaMKII optogenetic stimulation. In particular, the motor cortex (MO), gustatory cortex (GU), somatosensory cortex (SS), AI, and visceral cortex (VISC) were highly activated in the OFCCaMKII-activated group and to a lesser degree in the OFCmDlx activation group. Several amygdala nuclei, including the lateral amygdala (LA), central amygdala (CEA), and basolateral amygdala (BLA), were preferentially activated in the OFCCaMKII-activated brain. LS, ARH, periventricular thalamus (PT), and VMH were more activated during OFCmDlx stimulation than during OFCCaMKII activation. Most of these regions are linked to emotional control and motivated behavior. Although optogenetically constructed brain-wide activation maps may not fully recapitulate the endogenous brain-wide activation pattern triggered by the natural dynamics of OFC neurons, these activation maps suggest a functional link between OFC neurons and the bidirectional control of emotional behavior.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we investigated the role of the OFC in emotional behavior. The activity of the OFC excitatory neurons decreased in adverse situations, such as TS or RST, and in anxious states, such as at the center of the OF (Fig. 1, 2). Given these changes in the activity of OFC excitatory neurons are sensitive to the timing and magnitude of the stressors and environmental context, OFC might play a crucial function in encoding emotional state in the brain. Optogenetic activation of these neurons reduced center resident time, inducing anxiety behavior (Fig. 3), which is consistent with a previously observed reduction in anxious behavior following pharmacological inhibition of the OFC [17]. Furthermore, the optogenetic activation of OFC inhibitory neurons induced anxiety. Collectively, these findings indicate that the OFC bidirectionally regulates anxiety behavior mediated by an excitation-to-inhibition balance. In general, anxiety-mediating neural circuits exhibit increased activity in anxious situations, and artificial activation of these neurons increases anxious behavior [36, 37]. Interestingly, we found a behavioral pattern that differs from that of traditional anxiety-mediating neurons. Recent study by Gehrlach and colleagues [38] reported similar patterns in the posterior insular cortex (pIC), which warrants a new theoretical framework to understand emotional control by neural circuits. Our findings reinforce the importance of the OFC in emotion processing but also call for the proposal of a new emotional model that includes neural circuits playing non-canonical roles, such as the OFC and the pIC.

Open field test is mostly utilized to measure anxiety-related behavior. Especially, the time spent in the center zone serves as a measure of anxiety as in our manuscript. To less extent, open field test is also used to measure other behavioral traits, such as novelty seeking. Locomotive activity during open field test has been utilized to measure the habituation to environmental novelty [39, 40]. We analyzed locomotor activity during open field test with 1-minute time binning and found significant decrease in locomotion in OFCCaMKII-ChR2 group in the first minute of optogenetic stimulation. Impulsivity is usually measured via attentional task [41]. However, previous study on behavioral characteristics of impulsive animals has reported that rearing behavior is more frequently observed in impulsive animals than self-controlled animals [42]. To address this, we have measured rearing frequency during open field test and found that OFCCaMKII-ChR2 group showed significant less rearing during optogenetic stimulation. However, as rearing behavior is also affected by anxiety level [33], it is unclear whether impulsivity or anxiety were affected. In addition, we analyzed locomotive velocity during open field test, another parameter for impulsivity [43], and found no difference. Other study has reported a positive correlation between impulsive trait and the center duration in open field test [44], yet as it is also a key parameter for anxiety-related behavior, it is unclear how anxiety and impulsivity are affected respectively.

The connectivity of the OFC strongly indicates that this region plays a significant role in emotional control. Human and rodent studies have consistently shown that the OFC receives input from sensory processing regions and projects it to the amygdala, basal ganglia, and thalamus [34, 45, 46]. Recent study has reported that OFC to BLA excitatory circuit bidirectionally control depression-like behaviors after exposure to stress, suggesting OFC can control emotional behavior via downstream circuit [47]. In humans and non-human primates, the OFC connects with the ACC and vmPFC [46]. Our findings add functional connection information to the foundation of existing anatomical connections. Because excitatory and inhibitory neurons in the OFC alter emotional behavior in opposite directions, we performed brain-wide c-Fos screening while optogenetically stimulating these neurons. These revealed significant activation of areas near the amygdala and insular cortex by OFCCaMKII stimulation, which exhibited anxiogenic effects. When compared with OFCmDlx stimulation, more regions are strongly responsive to OFCCaMKII stimulation, suggesting possible importance of OFC excitatory neuron in shaping brain-wide activity for emotional behavior. Most of these regions are reported to be connected to OFC, though the neural mechanism of how these circuits function to regulate emotional process needs to be examined in a circuit-by-circuit manner in further studies. Of particular interest is determining which neural population, among those exhibiting distinct activation patterns mediated by CaMKII- and mDlx-positive neurons, displays a more pronounced impact in emotional control. Overall, these anatomical and functional findings underscore the OFC's critical role in emotional regulation and highlight the need for future studies directly investigating the circuitry between the OFC and its major target areas.

The OFC exhibits a high degree of cellular heterogeneity in mediating various behaviors. Imaging and recording studies at single-cell resolution during reversal learning—a well-established OFC-mediated behavior—have demonstrated this cellular-level heterogeneity [14, 15]. In this study, we focused on two neuronal subpopulations of the OFC, defined by differential promoter activation, using either the CaMKII or mDlx promoters. This approach showed a significant difference in the activity of cellular groups in the bidirectional control of emotional behavior through optogenetic stimulation, as well as in bulk calcium dynamics measured by fiber photometry. Nevertheless, considering the functional heterogeneity of the OFC reported in previous studies, higher-resolution research may be necessary. Specifically, in-vivo imaging techniques with single-cell resolution that target genetically and anatomically defined OFC subpopulations could provide deeper insights. Through these advanced methods, we would enhance our understanding of how diverse cellular components of the OFC mediate emotional control.
