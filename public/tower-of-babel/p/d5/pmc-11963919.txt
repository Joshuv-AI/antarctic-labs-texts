Hypertension Prevalence, Treatment, and Control 90 Days After Acute Stroke Among Mexican American and Non‐Hispanic White Adults
Authors: Mellanie V. Springer, Deborah A. Levine, Dehua Han, Lynda D. Lisabeth, Lewis B. Morgenstern, Robert D. Brook, Devin L. Brown, Darin B. Zahuranec, William J. Meurer, Erin Case, Rachael Whitney
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHigh blood pressure (BP) increases recurrent stroke risk.Methods and ResultsWe assessed hypertension prevalence, treatment, control, medication adherence, and predictors of uncontrolled BP 90 days after ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke among 561 Mexican American and non‐Hispanic White (NHW) survivors of stroke from the BASIC (Brain Attack Surveillance in Corpus Christi) cohort from 2011 to 2014. Uncontrolled BP was defined as average BP ≥140/90 mm Hg at 90 days poststroke. Hypertension was uncontrolled BP or antihypertensive medication prescribed or hypertension history. Treatment was antihypertensive use. Adherence was missing zero antihypertensive doses per week. We investigated predictors of uncontrolled BP using logistic regression adjusting for patient factors. Median (interquartile range) age was 68 (59–78) years, 64% were Mexican American, and 90% of strokes were ischemic. Overall, 94.3% of survivors of stroke had hypertension (95.6% Mexican American versus 92.0% non‐Hispanic White; P=0.09). Of these, 87.9% were treated (87.3% Mexican American versus 89.1% non‐Hispanic White; P=0.54). Among the total population, 38.3% (95% CI, 34.4%–42.4%) had uncontrolled BP. Among those with uncontrolled BP prescribed an antihypertensive, 84.5% reported treatment adherence (95% CI, 78.8%–89.3%). Uncontrolled BP 90 days poststroke was less likely in patients with stroke who had a primary care physician (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 0.45 [95% CI, 0.24–0.83]; P=0.01), greater stroke severity (aOR per‐1‐point‐higher National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score, 0.96 [95% CI, 0.93–0.99]; P=0.02), or more depressive symptoms (aOR per‐1‐point‐higher Personal Health Questionnaire Depression Scale‐8 score, 0.95 [95% CI, 0.92–0.99] among those with a history of hypertension at baseline; P=0.009).ConclusionsGreater than one third of survivors of stroke have uncontrolled BP at 90 days poststroke in this population‐based study. Interventions are needed to improve BP control after stroke.

Hypertension (defined as either ≥140/90 or ≥130/80 mm Hg depending on the guideline) increases recurrent stroke risk. 1 , 2 Recent guidelines recommend a goal blood pressure (BP) of <130/80 mm Hg in survivors of stroke to reduce recurrent stroke risk. 1 Identifying undertreatment and suboptimal control can inform clinical practice, policies and interventions to ensure survivors of stroke with hypertension receive high‐quality, guideline‐concordant care.

Prior studies have reported the prevalence and predictors of BP control among stroke survivors in the United States using cross‐sectional national data. 3 , 4 , 5 One limitation of this approach is that time since stroke ranged from months (subacute) to years (chronic). In the general population regardless of history of stroke, Mexican American (MA) adults are less likely than non‐Hispanic White (NHW) adults to be aware that they have hypertension, less likely to be on antihypertensive treatment, and less likely to have controlled BP. 6 , 7 Whether MA and NHW adults differ in hypertension prevalence and control after stroke is unclear. The prevalence of hypertension, treatment, control, medication adherence, and predictors of uncontrolled BP 90 days after acute stroke, when recurrent stroke risk is the highest, particularly in MA adults, remain unclear. We therefore sought to identify the prevalence of hypertension, treatment, control, medication adherence, and predictors of uncontrolled BP 90 days after acute stroke in a population‐based cohort of MA and NHW survivors of stroke in Corpus Christi, Texas.

Methods

Reasonable deidentified data requests for the data that support the findings of this study will be considered by the corresponding author based on existing data use agreements and consent documents.

Study Population

The BASIC (Brain Attack Surveillance in Corpus Christi) project is a population‐based stroke surveillance study conducted in a nonimmigrant community of primarily MA and NHW adults in Nueces County, Texas. 8 Details are described elsewhere. 8 Briefly, Nueces County is a predominantly urban location, where 95% of the population resides in the city of Corpus Christi on the Texas gulf coast. 8 Corpus Christi is situated more than 100 miles from potential referral centers in San Antonio and Houston. 8 The geographic location provides the opportunity for complete case capture of stroke in the county. 8 BASIC ascertains all cases of acute cerebrovascular disease presenting to the emergency department or directly admitted to any of the 7 hospitals in Nueces County through active and passive surveillance.

Trained study staff identify stroke cases based on rigorous criteria. Stroke physicians validate stroke cases using source documentation following international clinical criteria. 9 For patients with multiple strokes during the time period, only the first acute stroke captured in BASIC for each patient was included. At the time of their stroke hospitalization or soon after, patients (or proxies for patients unable to participate) complete an in‐person, structured interview. Bilingual abstractors conduct the interview in English or Spanish per patient preference. This project was approved by both the University of Michigan and the Corpus Christi Health Systems' Institutional Review Boards. All subjects or their proxies provided written informed consent. This article follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology reporting guidelines. 10

To be eligible for the BASIC cohort, patients had to have a diagnosis of ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke, be 45 years or older, and reside in Nueces County for 6+ months per year. We excluded cohort participants with an incomplete baseline interview and missing all BP readings. Only NHW and MA individuals were included given the small number of other racial and ethnic groups in the cohort. Eligible participants were diagnosed with stroke between 2011 and 2014. Included participants had a visit at baseline which was before hospital discharge or shortly thereafter, and at 90 days after the baseline visit.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was uncontrolled BP, defined as BP ≥140/90 mm Hg at 90 days after stroke. 11 The secondary outcome was BP≥130/80 mm Hg at 90 days after stroke. 2 Trained BASIC study staff measured BP 90 days after stroke using a standard automated BP measurement arm monitor (Omron model 700 series; Omron, Mannheim, Germany), validated to be accurate by the Association for the Advancement of Medical Instrumentation. After 5 minutes of rest, BP was measured 3 times in the right arm of participants at 15‐second intervals. The average of the 3 readings represented the participant's BP measurement at 90 days. 2

Additional secondary outcomes measured 90 days poststroke included hypertension prevalence, treatment for hypertension, and adherence to antihypertensive medications. Hypertension was defined as uncontrolled BP (≥140/90 mm Hg for primary analysis and ≥130/80 for secondary analysis); or self‐report of prescribed antihypertensive confirmed by BASIC study staff's review of participants' medication bottles at the 90‐day outcome visit; or history of hypertension before the index stroke abstracted from the medical record. Treatment for hypertension was defined as self‐report of current antihypertensive use, confirmed by BASIC study staff's review of participants' medication bottles for those who reported being prescribed antihypertensive treatment. Adherence to antihypertensive medications was defined as self‐report of never missing a dose, in a typical week, of any BP medications that they were currently taking. 12 , 13

Covariates

We selected covariates using the Andersen Behavioral Model of Health Service Use for Vulnerable populations, which describes factors associated with health care use, and based on availability in the BASIC cohort. 14 Covariates were measured at the time of index stroke (baseline) except where noted and included participants' age at time of stroke, sex, race or ethnicity (MA or NHW), marital status (married or living with someone versus single), education (<high school, high school, or >high school), insurance coverage (no insurance, government, or private insurance), and having a primary care physician. Acculturation was determined by whether the baseline interview was conducted in English or Spanish.

Clinical covariates were either abstracted from the medical record or assessed using validated scales. Medical comorbidities were abstracted from the medical record and included diabetes, history of end‐stage renal disease, history of coronary artery disease or myocardial infarction, history of atrial fibrillation, history of heart failure, body mass index calculated from weight and height obtained from the medical record, elevated cholesterol, history of stroke, current cigarette smoking, and excessive alcohol use. We used the short form of the Informant Questionnaire on Cognitive Decline in the Elderly (IQCODE), a validated instrument to assess prestroke cognitive status, and medical record history of dementia/Alzheimer's disease to categorize patients as normal cognition (IQCODE score ≤3), mild cognitive impairment (IQCODE score 3.1–3.43), and dementia (IQCODE score ≥3.44 or dementia/ Alzheimer's disease history). 15 , 16 Cut‐points for the IQCODE were selected based on prior research. 17 Depressive symptoms were measured using the Personal Health Questionnaire Depression Scale‐8 90 days after stroke. The Personal Health Questionnaire Depression Scale‐8 asks how often the individual experienced depressive symptoms over the previous 2 weeks. Higher scores indicate greater depressive symptoms. Degree of disability at the time of stroke was measured using the modified Rankin Scale and classified as ≤ slight (score of 0–2) or ≥ moderate (score of 3–5). Stroke type (ischemic versus hemorrhagic) was classified by study neurologists. Initial stroke severity was measured by the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score, which ranges from 0 to 42, which was directly abstracted from the medical record or determined by BASIC study staff based on review of the documented initial neurological exam.

Statistical Analysis

We performed descriptive statistics comparing the groups with controlled and uncontrolled BP at 90 days poststroke. We used chi‐square tests to determine the unadjusted association between categorical variables and uncontrolled BP; 2 sample t tests on normally distributed continuous variables; Wilcoxon rank sum tests on continuous variables with nonnormal distribution. We calculated the prevalence of uncontrolled BP in the total population; the prevalence of hypertension; the proportion of participants with hypertension who were prescribed an antihypertensive; and the proportion of participants with hypertension on antihypertensives who have uncontrolled BP. Among those with uncontrolled BP, we calculated the proportion treated for hypertension and the percentage of adherence to antihypertensives. These prevalences and proportions were calculated for the total cohort and by race, ethnicity, sex, and age. Differences in prevalences between racial and ethnic groups, sex categories, and age categories were calculated with mid‐P exact test. We used logistic regression to assess the predictors of uncontrolled BP and included all covariates. We performed the full model in the total population, and then repeated the model in the subpopulation with a history of hypertension at baseline, that is, before the index stroke. We explored the incremental effect of antihypertensive medication adherence on the odds of uncontrolled BP by adding it to the final models. We used multiple imputation to impute missing values of covariates using the imputation and variance estimation software IVEware. We generated a total of 10 imputed data sets and performed the full model on each imputed data set (Figure S1). Regression results were pooled across the 10 imputed data sets using Rubin's rules. We did not impute missing values of medication adherence for the regression model in which medication adherence was added. OpenEpi and SAS were used to perform the analyses.

Results

Population Description

We identified 1614 BASIC participants with stroke between March 2011 to April 2014. Of the eligible patients, 1053 were excluded because they had incomplete baseline interview data, had missing BP readings, or self‐reported as a race or ethnicity other than MA or NHW. There were 561 participants in the final cohort (Figure S2). Median (interquartile range) age was 68 (59–78) years, 64% were MA, and 90% of strokes were ischemic. Among all MA and NHW patients with stroke, included participants were younger, had lower stroke severity, and were more likely to have ischemic than hemorrhagic stroke than excluded participants (median (interquartile) age of 71 [60–81]) years, National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score 5 [2–14], and 82% ischemic strokes for excluded patients with stroke; Table S1. Hypertension Prevalence, Treatment, Control, and Adherence (BP ≥140/90 mmHg)

Overall, 94.3% of survivors of stroke had hypertension (95.6% MA versus 92.0% NHW; P=0.09) at 90 days poststroke (Figure [A]). Of these, 87.9% were prescribed an antihypertensive (87.3% MA versus 89.1% NHW; P=0.54). Among those prescribed an antihypertensive medication, 39.4% were uncontrolled with no difference by ethnicity (39.5% MA [95% CI, 34.2%–45.2%] versus 39.0% NHW [95% CI, 31.9%–46.7%]; P=0.92; Table 2). In the total population regardless of whether prescribed an antihypertensive, uncontrolled BP was present in 38.3% of survivors of stroke. Of these survivors of stroke, 84.2% reported having been prescribed an antihypertensive and 84.5% of those treated reported treatment adherence (82.9% MA [95% CI, 75%–88.7%] versus 87.5% NHW [95% CI, 77%–93.8%]; P=0.43; Table 3).

In the subpopulation with a history of hypertension at baseline, the odds of uncontrolled BP were lower in those with more depressive symptoms (OR, 0.95 [95% CI, 0.92–0.99]) or greater stroke severity (OR, 0.96 [95% CI, 0.93–1.00]; Table 4).

In the subpopulation with data on antihypertensive medication adherence, none of the variables was associated with uncontrolled BP in the full cohort. Among those with a history of hypertension at baseline, the odds of uncontrolled BP were lower in those with more depressive symptoms (OR, 0.96 [95% CI, 0.93–1.00]; Table S2).

Hypertension Prevalence, Treatment, Control, and Medication Adherence (BP ≥130/80 mmHg)

Using the BP criterion of 130/80 mm Hg, 95.7% of survivors of stroke had hypertension. Among those, the majority (87.9%) were prescribed an antihypertensive. Among those prescribed an antihypertensive, the majority (61.7%) had uncontrolled BP. Nearly all (99.0%) of those with uncontrolled BP reported having been prescribed an antihypertensive, with 82.4% of them reporting adherence (Figure [B]).

The prevalence of hypertension was higher among MA than NHW patients with stroke (97.5% [95% CI, 95.3%–98.8%] MA versus 92.5% [95% CI, 87.9%–95.5%] NHW, P=0.007) and among men than women (97.6% [95% CI, 94.9%–98.9%] versus 93.8% [95% CI, 90.3%–96.2%]) respectively, P=0.03. The prevalence of uncontrolled BP was also higher among MA than NHW patients with stroke (63.4%[95% CI, 58.4%–68.2%] MA versus 54.0% [95% CI, 47.1%–60.8%] NHW, P=0.03) and among men than women (65.7% [95% CI, 60.1%–71%] versus 54.2% [95% CI, 48.3%–60%] respectively, P=0.005). Older adults ≥65 years old with hypertension were more likely than adults 45 to 64 years old to be prescribed an antihypertensive medication (89.5% [95% CI, 85.6%–92.5%] versus 82.5% [95% CI, 77.0%–87.0%] respectively, P=0.02), had higher medication adherence (87.3% [95% CI, 81.3%–91.6%] versus 75.6% [95% CI, 67.2%–82.5%], P=0.01), and had lower prevalence of uncontrolled BP (55.8% [95% CI, 50.4%–61.1%] versus 66.0% [95% CI, 59.7%–71.7%], P=0.02). Discussion

In this population‐based sample of MA and NHW patients with stroke 45 years or older, we found that more than one third of survivors of stroke had uncontrolled BP 90 days after stroke. Using a stricter definition of uncontrolled BP of ≥130/80, almost two thirds of survivors of stroke had a BP ≥130/80 at 90 days after stroke. The prevalence of BP ≥130/80 was higher in survivors of stroke who were MA, younger, and male. More than 90% of survivors of stroke had a diagnosis of hypertension. The majority of patients with hypertension had been prescribed an antihypertensive medication. The prevalence of uncontrolled BP at 90 days after stroke was similar in patients with hypertension prescribed an antihypertensive medication as in the total cohort. More than 80% of patients with uncontrolled BP reported adherence to their medications. Having a primary care physician, more depressive symptoms, and higher stroke severity were associated with lower odds of uncontrolled BP at 90 days after stroke. More depressive symptoms were associated with lower odds of uncontrolled BP in the subsample of the cohort with data on medication adherence who had a history of hypertension at baseline. Higher stroke severity was associated with lower odds of BP ≥130/80 at 90 days after stroke, which was also found in the subsample of the cohort with a history of hypertension at baseline.

Other studies have reported similar prevalence of uncontrolled BP after stroke in the United States, but lower prevalence of hypertension. Among participants of the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, uncontrolled BP was present in 35.5% to 37% of survivors of stroke. 4 , 5 Our similar prevalence of uncontrolled BP 90 days after stroke extends previous findings by showing that BP control is similarly low during the time that recurrent stroke risk is highest. The prevalence of hypertension in our study (94% for hypertension defined as ≥140/90 or 96% for hypertension defined as ≥130/80) was higher than what has been reported in previous studies (72%—78%). 3 , 4 , 5 The lower prevalence of hypertension in other studies might be because previous studies used self‐report to diagnose hypertension 3 , 4 . Some Hispanic adults may face barriers that affect hypertension control such as dietary choices that include elevated sodium intake, limited options for engaging in exercise, financial barriers, and limited engagement in self‐measured BP monitoring. 18 , 19 , 20 Interventions that include counseling patients with stroke on modification of lifestyle behaviors and supervised physical activity have been effective in lowering systolic BP after stroke. 21 Interventions specifically tailored for Hispanic survivors of stroke have had mixed results. Some lifestyle counseling interventions for Hispanic survivors of stroke have lowered systolic BP but others have had no effect on systolic BP compared with usual care. 22 Interventions are needed to improve BP control after stroke given the reduction in future risk of cardiovascular events and mortality associated with systolic BP reduction. 23 , 24 , 25

Similar to other studies, we found high levels of antihypertensive medication treatment and adherence in survivors of stroke. High levels of adherence to antihypertensive medications after stroke has been reported in a national sample of survivors of stroke. 26 We have previously reported a similar level of antihypertensive medication adherence in a partially overlapping BASIC cohort sample. 12 The majority of patients with stroke with hypertension are prescribed an antihypertensive. 4 , 5 Therefore, antihypertensive medication adherence does not appear to contribute to the observed prevalence of uncontrolled BP. Health systems and payers should implement effective strategies to improve BP control, such as self‐measured BP monitoring and standardized protocols for clinicians to manage BP. 27 , 28

We identified factors associated with BP control after stroke. Having a primary care physician, more depressive symptoms, and higher stroke severity were associated with lower odds of uncontrolled BP. Although our study was not designed to identify what mediates the relationship between those factors and BP control, one possible mediating factor is greater engagement with the health care system. Survivors of stroke with depressive symptoms 29 and more severe stroke may interact more with health care providers leading to greater surveillance and management of BP. These findings underscore the importance of poststroke medical follow‐up for BP management.

Demographic predictors of uncontrolled BP differed in our study compared with previous reports. Others have found that survivors of stroke of older age 3 , 4 , 30 and female sex 3 , 4 have increased odds of uncontrolled BP after stroke. In our predominantly ethnic and racial cohort of MA and NHW individuals, survivors of stroke who were younger, male, and MA had higher prevalence of BP ≥130/80. However, age, sex, and ethnicity were not associated with BP ≥140/90 in our cohort or in the fully adjusted models. Predictors of BP control after stroke may differ by race or ethnicity, which may in part be due to individual lifestyle choices influenced by social determinants of health such as region of residence and that contribute to abdominal obesity, increasing risk for uncontrolled BP. 31

Strengths of our study include the prospective evaluation of BP control in a large ethnic and racial cohort with clinician‐adjudicated stroke. Among limitations, we were unable to assess BP control later than 90 days poststroke because BP was measured at a single time point. Residual confounding by unmeasured demographic and clinical predictors of BP control is possible. Given that patients in our cohort, on average, had mild stroke severity, the prevalence of uncontrolled BP that we report may not be generalizable to patients with severe stroke. Medication adherence was self‐reported, but recall bias is suspected to be low given that the participant was asked to report adherence over the short time frame of 1 week. Furthermore, we did not have data on medication adherence for the entire cohort. However, the missing data on medication adherence is unlikely to have influenced our results as only 2 participants in the full sample and 5 participants in the subgroup of those with a history of hypertension at baseline were missing data on adherence.

Conclusions

In this population‐based study, greater than one third of survivors of stroke have uncontrolled BP 90 days after stroke. Having a primary care physician after stroke is associated with a lower odds of having uncontrolled BP 90‐days poststroke. Interventions are needed to improve poststroke BP control among all survivors of stroke, particularly MA individuals, men, younger survivors of stroke, survivors with fewer depressive symptoms, and those with less severe stroke.
