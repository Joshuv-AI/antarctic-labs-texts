Integrated physiological, element content, transcriptomic and weighted gene co-expression network analysis provide insights into the response mechanisms of Areca catechu to Zn/Fe deficiency stress
Authors: Jing Yang, Weiying Li, Shangjia Du, Qiyuan An, Chuang Cui, Mengying Ding, Yicheng Wang, Guangzhen Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Zinc (Zn) and iron (Fe) deficiencies are pervasive abiotic stresses that severely limit the productivity and nutritional quality of tropical crops, including the economically important Areca catechu. To elucidate the comprehensive response mechanisms of areca to Zn/Fe deficiencies, we conducted an integrated multi-omics analysis. This combined physiological assessment, elemental profiling, transcriptomic sequencing, and weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) on seedlings subjected to prolonged (45 day) Zn/Fe deficiency. Our results showed that this prolonged stress significantly altered leaf microstructure, reduced photosynthetic carbon (C) assimilation, and triggered tissue-specific compensatory accumulation of Fe (in L3 leaves under Zn deficiency) or Zn (in L3 leaves under Fe deficiency). Transcriptome sequencing revealed that phenylpropanoid/flavonoid biosynthesis and xyloglucan metabolism pathways were consistently enriched among differentially expressed genes (DEGs) across tissues. Seven stress-specific modules and hub genes related to Zn/Fe deficiency in areca were identified by WGCNA. These results demonstrated that phenylpropanoid/flavonoids metabolism and xyloglucan-mediated cell wall remodeling are central to Zn/Fe deficiency adaptation, while identified transporters and transcription factors (TFs) form critical regulatory networks in Zn/Fe homeostasis in A. catechu. Our study demonstrated the core adaptive pathways (phenylpropanoid/flavonoid and xyloglucan metabolism) and regulatory networks for areca’s adaptation to Zn/Fe deficiency, providing genetic resources for breeding nutrient-efficient areca varieties.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09096-3.

Background

Zinc (Zn) and iron (Fe) are essential micronutrients for plant growth and development [1]. Notably, Zn deficiency affects approximately 50% of the world’s agricultural soils, making it the most widespread micronutrient limitation in crops [2]. As an essential trace element, Zn serves as a catalytic or structural cofactor for numerous enzymes, transcription factors (TFs), and proteins critical for plant growth [3–4]. Notably, Zn enhances photosynthetic efficiency in plants by functioning as a cofactor for key chloroplast enzymes, including carbonic anhydrase and RuBisCO [5–6]. Conversely, Zn deficiency impairs key metabolic processes such as photosynthesis and transpiration [7–8]. Furthermore, intracellular Zn homeostasis is tightly regulated by Zn transporters. The zinc/iron-regulated transporter-like proteins (ZIPs) family was first identified in plants and is involved in Zn uptake and homeostasis [9]. Yeast functional characterization further established ZIP proteins as broad-spectrum divalent cation transporters, mediating the cellular uptake of Fe2+, Zn2+, Co2+, Mn2+, Cu2+, Ni2+, and Cd2+ [10]. Another important group of membrane transporter proteins, heavy metal ATPase (HMA), has also been confirmed to facilitate the transmembrane absorption and transport of Zn2+ through ATP hydrolysis [11].

Fe is equally vital for plant development, participating in DNA synthesis, energy production, and chlorophyll biosynthesis [12]. As a core component of photosystem proteins (Psb, Psa), Fe-S clusters, and ferredoxin, Fe deficiency disrupts chlorophyll production and induces interveinal chlorosis [13]. Therefore, Fe deficiency can interfere with the production of chlorophyll and affect the entire photosynthetic machinery, resulting in interveinal chlorosis in plants [14]. Fe is an essential component of the respiratory chain, which facilitates redox reactions. Fe deficiency also compromises respiratory chain function and alters nitrogen metabolism. For example, in Cucumis sativus, Fe deficiency differentially affected N metabolism in roots and leaves. The concentration of amino acids in Fe-deficient roots significantly decreased, while it increased in the corresponding leaves [15]. Fe status also impacts plant immune responses, playing a significant role in defending against pathogen invasion [16]. Both Fe deficiency and excess can have harmful effects in plants.

The Fe homeostasis is strictly regulated in plants by several key regulation factors, such as iron regulatory factor (IRF), and basic-helix-loop-helix (bHLH) TFs [17–18]. Recent studies have revealed that multiple bHLH TFs regulate Fe homeostasis by modulating Fe uptake and metabolism [19]. Arabidopsis bHLH104 interacts with ILR3 (IAA-LEUCINE RESISTANT3) to regulate the Fe deficiency responses and maintain Fe homeostasis, while bHLH156 facilitates Fe homeostasis by interacting with the regulatory factor IRO2 (the Fe-related bHLH transcription factor 2) [20–21]. Furthermore, crosstalk between Zn and Fe homeostasis is well documented [22]. Fe deficiency in plants can lead to excessive uptake of Zn. Alternatively, when Zn is in excess, the Fe utilization of the plant cells is reduced, and plants exhibit symptoms induced by Fe starvation [23]. In A. thaliana, IRT1 has been identified as an Fe transporter under Fe deficiency, and its expression is enhanced under such conditions. Meanwhile, under Fe deficiency, IRT1 exhibits broad substrate specificity, mediating the uptake of manganese (Mn), Zn, and cobalt (Co) [24]. The precise regulation of Zn-Fe dynamic balance is critical for plant growth and development.

Unlike model annual plants such as A. thaliana and O. sativa, which complete their life cycle within several months and exhibit rapid systemic responses to nutrient fluctuations. As an important perennial tropical economic crop, A. catechu exhibits unique physiological traits that sharply differ from annual model plants. The areca palm possesses a long lifespan, slow growth rate, and persistent long-term nutrient demands, meaning it sustains and adapts to micronutrient deficiency for far longer periods than short-lived annual plants. Consequently, the mechanisms of ion homeostasis in areca palm, tissue-specific metal redistribution, and long-term stress acclimation are fundamentally distinct from those well characterized in model species. The Zn/Fe nutritional status directly determines the yield and economic sustainability of areca, yet its adaptive strategies to Zn/Fe deficiency remain largely unclear. Current research on Zn/Fe metabolism in A. catechu mainly focuses on the identification of transporter gene families. As we previously reported [25], members of the ZIP family in A. catechu, such as AcZIP3 and AcZIP4, were significantly upregulated in roots under Zn/Fe deficiency conditions, suggesting their involvement in Zn/Fe uptake from the rhizosphere. Specifically, Zn and Fe deficiencies have been documented to disrupt chlorophyll biosynthesis, impair photosynthetic efficiency, and ultimately lead to reduced biomass accumulation and nut yield [25]. However, a comprehensive understanding of the transcriptional and metabolic networks underlying Zn/Fe deficiency responses in A. catechu is still lacking. To address this gap, we integrated transcriptomics with weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) to identify key genes, regulatory networks, and metabolic pathways underlying Zn/Fe deficiency adaptation in A. catechu seedlings. This approach novel insights into the molecular mechanisms of micronutrient homeostasis in perennial tropical crops and offers potential targets for improving nutrient use efficiency in areca cultivation.

Materials and methods

Plant material

This study used A. catechu plant material collected by the Chinese Academy of Tropical Agricultural Sciences (Wenchang City, China). The permission to collect plant samples was obtained from the Agricultural Research, Education, and Extension Organization of China. The specimens were stored at the Hainan Normal University (deposition number: hnu-zgz0087). Areca seedlings (six-month-old) with consistent growth conditions were selected for nutrient deficiency treatment. The formula for the nutrient solution was shown in Table S1. Areca seedlings were respectively cultured in whole nutrient solution (CK), Zn-deficient nutrient solution (-Zn), and Fe-deficient nutrient solution (-Fe). Keep the ventilation pump inflated twice a day (30 min each time). Each sample was mixed from three biological replicates.

Observation of leaf microstructure

Fixation: Cut fresh leaves of the sample to approximately 3 mm × 5 mm and place them in FAA fixative solution for fixation at room temperature for 12 h. Dehydration: Remove the sample from the fixative and sequentially place it in ethanol solutions of 10%, 20%, 30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 95%, and 100% concentrations. Each gradient should be used for 1.5 h. To ensure complete dehydration, the sample must be dehydrated in 100% ethanol 2–3 times. Infiltration: After dehydration, sequentially place the sample in mixed solutions of resin and ethanol in the ratios of 1:3, 1:1, and 3:1 at room temperature for 2–3 h. Then, transfer the sample into pure resin for 12 h. Embedding: Add 500 µL of embedding agent to the embedding mold, place the sample inside, and, once the sample is stable in the embedding agent, seal the mold and leave it at room temperature for at least three days. Sectioning, Spreading, and Mounting: Use a Leica 2235 microtome (Germany) to cut sections with a thickness of 5 μm.

Detection of element content and xyloglucan content

Remove all above-ground parts from the samples. Then, dry the samples in an oven at 80 ℃ for 2–3 days until thoroughly dried. To remove apoplastically adsorbed metal ions, the roots were desorbed. Fresh roots were washed on ice with a 5 mM Pb(NO₃)₂ solution for 30 min. Subsequently, the roots were rinsed three times with distilled water and once with ultra-pure water to remove the Pb(NO₃)₂ solution and any displaced ions. Finally, place the roots in an oven at 80 ℃ for 2–3 days until thoroughly dried. Sample pretreatment: Weigh 0.3 g of the sample into the digestion tank, add 6 mL of nitric acid, and digest until complete. Add 0.5 mL of perchloric acid for fuming. After cooling, quantitatively transfer the contents of the digestion tank to a 50 mL centrifuge tube with high-purity water, make up to 50 mL, and mix thoroughly. The contents of K (potassium), Ca, Mg, and Fe were determined by the ZEEnit700P atomic absorption spectrometer (Analytik Jena AG company, Germany). The quantitative analysis of xyloglucan was performed using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), and the specific procedures were carried out in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions (Beijing Huabodeyi biology science and Technology Co., ltd, China).

RNA extraction, cDNA library preparation, and sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from the roots (R) and leaves (L1 and L3) of areca seedlings using the Quick RNA isolation kit, according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Subsequently, individual RNAs from L1, L3, and R tissues from three biological replicate plants were used for library construction. cDNA libraries were constructed using a NEBNext®Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (NEB, Ipswich, MA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The resulting cDNA libraries were sequenced at Biomarker Technologies Corporation (Beijing, China) using an Illumina HiSeq™ 4000 platform. Stain with toluidine-blue staining for 60–90 s, then rinse under slow running water for 3–5 min. After removing excess color, dry the slide and mount it with neutral balsam. The areca genome used as the reference for transcriptome mapping in this study was one that we previously generated and assembled [26]. The datasets generated and analyzed during the current study are available in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) repository(Accession number PRJNA1303856).

DEGs enrichment analyses

To ensure the accuracy of subsequent analyses, it is necessary to ensure that the Reads are of high enough quality. Reads were filtered to remove low-quality reads and adapters. Adapter-trimmed reads (Trimmomatic v0.39) were aligned to the areca reference genome using HISAT2 (v2.2.1). The gene expression profiles were analyzed as fragments per kilobase of gene model per million mapped reads (FPKM) based on the RNA-seq data. Differential expression genes (DEGs) were determined via DESeq2 (v1.34.0), with significance thresholds set at |log₂FC| > 1 and FDR-adjusted *p* < 0.05. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis was performed using BMKCloud (www.biocloud.net).

Gene co-expression network analyses

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA v3.4.1) was employed to construct transcriptomic networks. Module Eigengenes (MEs) and connectivity metrics were derived for all detected modules. Hierarchical clustering of MEs facilitated module grouping, with trait correlations visualized in clustered heatmaps (pheatmap R package). Hub-gene networks from high-significance modules were exported to Cytoscape (v3.7.1) for interaction visualization using the Cytoscape software (v3.7.1).

Results

Effects of Zn/Fe deficiency stress on morphological and physiological characteristics of A. catechu

To determine the effects of Zn/Fe deficiency treatment on the morphological and physiological characteristics of areca seedlings, areca seedlings in -Fe or -Zn nutrient solutions were cultured for 45 days. Then, the microstructure of the first and third leaves of areca seedlings was observed, and compared with the CK1, the vane thickness of L1 and L3 of areca seedlings under Fe deficiency treatment decreased significantly (Fig. 1A, B). Furthermore, under the -Fe treatment, a marked decrease in abaxial epidermis thickness was detected in L1 leaves, contrasting with unaffected L3 leaf phenotypes. Compared with CK1L1 and CK1L3, the mesophyll cell density was increased significantly in -FeL1 and -FeL3 leaves (Fig. 1A, B). In addition, the effect of Zn deficiency on the leaf microstructure of areca seedlings was also analyzed. Under the -Zn treatment, compared with CK2L1 and CK2L3, the vane thickness of -ZnL1 and -ZnL3 of areca seedlings was decreased significantly, while the mesophyll cell density was increased significantly. These results indicated that Fe deficiency or Zn deficiency stress for a certain period of time could lead to significant changes in leaf microstructure of areca seedlings (Fig. 1C, D). Comparison between Zn deficiency and Fe deficiency showed that both stresses caused similar reductions in leaf blade thickness and increases in mesophyll cell density, indicating conserved anatomical adaptation to micronutrient deficiency.

Elemental remodeling in areca seedlings under Zn/Fe deficiency

The contents of various metal elements (including Mg (magnesium), Ca (calcium), Fe, Mn, Cu (copper), and Zn) in areca seedlings under Zn/Fe deficiency stress were detected. In our previous study, we only focused on the changes in Zn and Fe content in areca [26]. In this study, we further analyzed the changes in the contents of other elements. In the treatment of Fe deficiency stress, the Fe content in L1 leaves and roots was lower than that of control in areca seedlings, while the Zn content increased significantly in L3 leaves. In addition, in the roots of Fe-deficient seedlings, the contents of Mg were significantly increased, while the content of other elements were not significantly changed (Table S2). Under the Zn deficiency stress, the contents of Zn and Mn in the -Zn-L1 leaves were lower than those of the CK-L1 leaves, while the contents of Fe and Ca in the -Zn-L3 leaves were significantly increased compared to those of the CK-L3 leaves. Compared with CK-R, Ca content in -Zn-R increased significantly, while the opposite is true for Zn (Table S2). In addition, correlation analysis showed that there was a significant positive correlation between Mn and Ca content under Fe treatment. Under Fe deficiency stress, the Mg content in L3 was significantly negatively correlated with the Fe content, and the Mg content increased by 16.4% compared to the control. Under Zn deficiency stress, there was a significant negative correlation between Fe content and Mg content (Fig. 2A, B). In addition, we also tested the relative contents of non-metallic elements (N and carbon - C) in areca seedlings under Fe or Zn deficiency stress (Fig. 2C, D). Under Fe deficiency stress, the N content in areca seedlings did not change significantly, but the C content in -Fe-L1 and -Fe-L3 was significantly reduced compared to CK-L1 and CK-L3. Under Zn deficiency, the first leaf (L1) exhibited a significantly lower C content than its control, while its N content remained unchanged. In contrast, the third leaf (L3) and roots showed a significant decrease in N content, with no significant change in C content compared to their respective controls (Fig. 2C, D).

Transcriptome changes in response to Zn/Fe deficiency stress

To reveal the molecular mechanism of tolerance to Zn/Fe deficiency stress, RNA-seq analysis was performed (Fig. 3A). A total of 27 libraries were constructed for RNA-seq, and 185.61 Gb of clean data were obtained. In all the 27 libraries, the Q30 value ranged from 92.38% to 93.42%, averaging 93.04%. The CG content ranged from 47.04% to 49.24%, averaging 47.69%. Approximately 85.36%-94.28% of the clean reads were successfully mapped to the A. catechu reference genome (Table S3). Pearson’s correlation coefficient analyses and principal component analysis (PCA) suggested that the sequencing data were suitable for subsequent biological analysis (Fig. 3B, Figure S1). The distribution of gene expression showed that the largest number of genes was detected in the 1 ≤ FPKM < 100 group (Table S4). Furthermore, all annotated unigenes were functionally classified based on the annotated Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG terms. In terms of biological processes, the GO terms with the highest number of genes were metabolic process (Fig. 3C). The enrichment analysis of DEGs

In order to reveal the molecular mechanism of A. catechu in response to Zn/Fe deficiency stress, we compared gene expression levels in the samples of areca exposed to Zn/Fe deficiency stress. In this study, DEGs were identified and divided into six groups for comparison. A total of 1355, 1780, 1123, 1340, 2377, and 4537 significant DEGs for groups CK-L1 vs. -Fe-L1, CK-L1 vs. -Zn-L1, CK-L3 vs. -Fe-L3, CK-L3 vs. -Zn-L3, CK-R vs. -Fe-R, and CK-R vs. -Zn-R were identified, respectively (Figure S2A, F). Notably, compared with other groups, Zn/Fe deficiency stress resulted in the production of more DEGs in roots. In addition, we also identified 205 common DEGs in the three Zn deficiency stress comparison groups (Figure S2G). Meanwhile, we found that 198 common DEGs overlapped at all the three Fe deficiency stress comparison groups (Figure S2H). Moreover, among these common DEGs, we identified 44 non-redundant TF genes in three Zn deficiency stress comparison groups, including five WRKY, five AP2/ERF-ERF, and five NAC TFs (Figure S3A, Table S6). Eight WRKY, four AP2/ERF-ERF, and other common TFs were found in the three Fe deficiency stress comparison groups (Figure S3B, Table S7). This result suggested that the response to Zn/Fe deficiency in areca may be regulated by these TFs.

To further reveal the response mechanisms of areca seedlings under Zn/Fe deficiency, KEGG and GO enrichment analyses were performed (Fig. S4). In the CK-L1 vs. -Fe-L1 comparison, DEGs were most significantly enriched in photosynthesis - antenna proteins (ko00196), and the largest number of DEGs was assigned to phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (ko00940) (Fig. 4, Table S8). In CK-L1 vs. -Zn-L1, the top three enriched pathways were phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, photosynthesis - antenna proteins, and flavonoid biosynthesis (ko00941) (Fig. 4, Table S8). For CK-L3 vs. -Fe-L3, DEGs were markedly enriched in flavonoid biosynthesis, glutathione metabolism (ko00480), and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (Fig. 4, Table S8). In CK-L3 vs. -Zn-L3, significant pathways included glutathione metabolism, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, plant-pathogen interaction (ko04626), flavonoid biosynthesis, and phenylalanine metabolism (ko00360) (Fig. 4, Table S8). In roots, the top three enriched pathways in CK-R vs. -Fe-R were phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, starch and sucrose metabolism (ko00500), and amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism (ko00520), whereas in CK-R vs. -Zn-R, they were flavonoid biosynthesis, linoleic acid metabolism (ko00591), and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (Fig. 4, Table S8). Notably, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis and flavonoid biosynthesis were significantly enriched across all comparisons, indicating their consistent involvement in Zn/Fe deficiency responses (Fig. 4). The numbers of DEGs involved in phenylpropanoid metabolism were 5, 9, 18, 18, 54, and 25 in CK-L1 vs. -Fe-L1, CK-L1 vs. -Zn-L1, CK-L3 vs. -Fe-L3, CK-L3 vs. -Zn-L3, CK-R vs. -Fe-R, and CK-R vs. -Zn-R, respectively (Table S9). These results suggested that DEGs related to phenylpropanoid metabolism were consistently associated with Zn/Fe deficiency stress responses in areca, implying a potential role for this pathway in adaptation to micronutrient deficiency.

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of DEGs among different groups

GO enrichment analysis was also performed and the enriched GO terms were ranked by p-value, and then the top 10 terms were shown in Fig. S4. Among the BP GO terms, most enriched terms were involved in secondary metabolites, biosynthetic processes, and transport, such as “secondary metabolite biosynthetic process” (GO:0044550), “glycerol transport” (GO:0015793), “flavonoid biosynthetic process” (GO:0009813), and etc. (Fig. S4, Table S10). Notably, CC GO terms “photosystem I” (GO:0009522), “photosynthetic membrane” (GO:0034357) and “photosystem II” (GO:0009523) were significantly enriched in the “CK-L1 vs. -Fe-L1” group, indicating Fe deficiency stress affected photosynthesis of areca leaves (Fig. S4). Furthermore, MF GO terms “transporter activity” (GO:0005215) and “transition metal ion binding” (GO:0046914) were significantly enriched in the “CK-R vs. -Fe-R” and “CK-R vs. -Zn-R” groups, which suggests metal Fe transporters play a significant role during Fe/Zn deficiency stress in areca (Fig. S4). Xyloglucan is a polysaccharide that mainly exists in plant cell walls and plays an important role in maintaining the structure and function of the cell walls. Here, we found that the DEGs in all comparison groups were significantly enriched in the xyloglucan metabolic process pathway and cell wall biogenesis pathway, suggesting that Zn/Fe deficiency stress affected the stability of the cell walls in the leaves and roots of areca (Fig. S4). In the xyloglucan metabolic process pathway, nine, 10, six, nine, 17, and 13 DEGs were identified in the CKL1 vs. -FeL1, CKL1 vs. -ZnL1, CKL3 vs. -FeL3, CKL3 vs. -ZnL3, CKR vs. -FeR, and CKR vs. -ZnR comparison groups, respectively (Table S9). Notably, among the six comparison groups, we identified three commonly DEGs in the xyloglucan metabolic process pathway, including three members of the xyloglucan endotransglucosylase/hydrolase protein family (AC06G002440, AC06G002400, and AC10G002240). We further identified the expression pattern of xyloglucosyl transferase (AcXTH), a key enzyme in the xyloglucan metabolic pathway that maintains the “dynamic remodeling” of the cell wall. The results showed that the expression levels of AcXTH in the treatment group was decreased compared with the CK group, which was consistent with the results of the transcriptome (Figure S5). Furthermore, under the stress of Zn and Fe deficiency, the content of xyloglucan in the leaves and roots of areca seedlings decreased significantly (Figure S6). It is suggested that these genes may respond to the Zn/Fe deficiency stress of areca leaves and roots by regulating the metabolism of the xyloglucan ( Table S9).

In conclusion, KEGG and GO enrichment profiles demonstrated that both Zn deficiency and Fe deficiency consistently enriched DEGs involved in phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, flavonoid biosynthesis, and xyloglucan metabolic processes, representing core conserved responses. Meanwhile, some pathways showed treatment-specificity: photosynthesis-related pathways were more strongly affected by Fe deficiency, whereas linoleic acid metabolism was specifically enriched under Zn deficiency.

Expression patterns of key structural genes in the phenylpropanoid metabolism pathway under Fe/Zn deficiency stress

A total of 158 genes encoding 20 enzymes in the phenylpropanoid metabolism pathway were identified (Fig. 5; Table S11). We also found that most key genes of the phenylpropanoid metabolism pathway were highly expressed in the roots, including phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), cinnamate-4-hydroxylase (C4H), and 4-coumarate-CoA ligase (C4L) involved in the general phenylpropanoid pathway and hydroxycinnamoyl-CoA shikimate/quinate hydroxycinnamoyl transferase (HCT), cytochrome P450 (C3H), caffeic acid 3-O-methyltransferase (COMT), caffeoyl-CoA 3-O-methyltransferase (CCoAMT), cinnamoyl-CoA reductase (CCR), cinnamyl-alcohol dehydrogenase (CAD), and peroxidase (PER), which are involved in lignin biosynthesis. For the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway, we identified a series of key genes that were significantly induced by Fe/Zn deficiency stress (Fig. 5). Candidate genes involved in Zn/Fe deficiency stress

A system for maintaining Zn and Fe homeostasis is prevalent in plant cells. In order to explore the mechanism of maintaining the homeostasis of Zn/Fe under the stress of Zn or Fe deficiency in areca, the common DEGs were identified in different comparison groups. Between the CK-Leaf1 vs. -Fe-Leaf1 group and the CK-Leaf1 vs. -Zn-Leaf1 (group1), we identified 488 common DEGs (Figure S7A). KEGG enrichment analysis showed most common DEGs were significantly enriched in flavone and flavonol biosynthesis (ko00944) and alpha-linolenic acid metabolism (ko00592) pathways (Figure S7A, Table S12). Furthermore, 605 common DEGs were identified in group two ((CK-Leaf1 vs. -Fe-Leaf1) vs. (CK-Leaf1 vs. -Zn-Leaf1)). Among these common DEGs in group two, four metabolic pathways, including glutathione metabolism (ko00480), phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (ko00940), flavonoid biosynthesis (ko00941) and phenylalanine metabolism (ko00360), were significantly enriched (p-value < 0.05) (Figure S7B, Table S13). For group three ((CK-Root vs. -Fe-Root) vs. (CK-Root vs. -Zn-Root)), 1623 common DEGs were identified. Enrichment analysis also showed these common DEGs in group three were significantly enriched in flavonoid biosynthesis (ko00941) and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (ko00940) pathways (Figure S7C, Table S14). Notably, the common DEGs of the three groups were significantly enriched in the flavonoid biosynthesis and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathways, suggesting that genes related to flavonoid and phenylpropanoid metabolism may play an important role in response to Zn/Fe deficiency stress and maintaining of Zn/Fe homeostasis in areca.

Furthermore, among these common differential genes, we identified a series of TFs in group one, group two, and group three, respectively (Figure S7D). Among them, a total of 10 common TFs were identified in these three groups, including MYB105, WRKY3, ERF109, etc., which may play an important role in the homeostasis of Zn and Fe in areca (Figure S7D). Additionally, we also obtained the gene expression profile of 10 common TFs (Table S15). In areca leaf1 and leaf3 samples, MYB105, WRKY19a, and CIR1 were found to be highly expressed under Zn deficiency stress (Figure S7E-F). Compared to areca leaf, WRKY19a was biasedly expressed in areca root under Fe deficiency stress (Figure S7G).

Construction of co-expression networks and identification of metal transporter protein associated with Zn/Fe deficiency stress

To further understand the gene expression patterns of areca seedlings responding to Zn/Fe deficiency stress, WGCNA was performed with 27 samples of transcriptomic data (Fig. 6A). A heatmap plot of topological overlap in the gene network also was drawn (Figure S8). Then, a correlation analysis between the distinct modules and the tissue samples was performed (Fig. 6B). Notably, seven of the 15 modules had a significant positive correlation (r > 0.8, p < 10− 3) with a single tissue (Fig. 6B). The MEpurple (r = 0.84), MElightcyan (r = 0.84), MEpink (r = 0.89), MEgrey60 (r = 0.88), MEbrown (r = 0.84), MEblue (r = 0.83), and MEyellow (r = 0.8) were specifically correlated with CK-Leaf3, CK-Leaf1, -Fe-Leaf1, -Zn-Leaf1, -Fe-Root, CK-Root, and -Zn-Root, respectively (Fig. 6B). The remaining eight modules showed low or non-significant correlations (|r| < 0.6, p > 0.05) and were not further analyzed. Moreover, the unigenes in these modules were most highly expressed at the “-Fe-Leaf 1,” “-Fe-Root,” “-Zn-Leaf 1,” and “-Zn-Root” samples, as shown by the heatmap diagrams (Fig. 7A). Among the 15 identified modules, seven exhibited a significant positive correlation (r > 0.8, p < 10⁻³) with specific tissue/treatment groups. To further understand the mechanism of the response to Zn/Fe deficiency stress in areca, the GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of the unigenes in the four modules (MEpink, MEbrown, MEgrey60 and MEyellow) were performed. The enriched GO terms among the “MEpink (-Fe-Leaf 1)” module genes included metabolic process and catalytic activity (Table S16). In the “MEbrown (-Fe-Root)” module, fatty acid degradation and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathways were significantly enriched (Table S17). Similarly, “MEyellow (-Zn-Root)” module genes were also significantly enriched in the phenylpropanoid and flavonoid biosynthesis pathways (Table S18).

Moreover, four co-expression networks were constructed based on the weight values of genes in these modules, and hub genes were identified based on high intramodular connectivity (top 100 weighted connectivity values) within each significant module (Fig. 7B-E, Table S19). For example, in the “MEpink (-Fe-Leaf 1)” module, we obtained five TFs with highly connectivity, including DOF1 (AC10G27950), ARF11 (AC05G007930), bHLH78 (AC10G093610), GRF2 (AC12G094240), and MYB96 (AC15G006800). Most TF genes belong to the bHLH, MYB, and WRKY families. Notably, seven metal transporter protein coding genes were identified in “MEbrown (-Fe-Root)” and “MEyellow (-Zn-Root)” networks, including MFS (AC02G060810), CCX1 (AC05G002700), ABC (AC12G014900), HMA37 (AC02G0412620), HAK6 (AC16G042060), ABCG14 (AC01G049410), and HMA4 (AC08G025290) (Fig. 7B, E). Discussion

Zn and Fe serve as essential co-factors for numerous enzymes, playing critical roles in both human and plant metabolism and health [27]. While extensively studied in model plants like Arabidopsis, rice, and wheat [28–30]. Research on areca palm remains limited. Our previous studies demonstrated that Zn/Fe deficiency significantly reduces chlorophyll biosynthesis and photosynthetic capacity in areca seedlings [25]. In this study, the determination of Zn/Fe concentration in the leaves and roots of areca seedlings indicated that the uptake and distribution of these two ions share a tissue-specific compensatory pathway. When Zn is deficient, the intracellular Fe concentration increases in leaves, and vice versa, which was consistent with findings in other plant species. Further, the deficiency of Zn/Fe can also cause disturbances in the content of other metal ions, which may further affect the physiological state of the plants. In addition to Zn and Fe, we observed alterations in the content of other metal ions such as Ca, Mn, Cu, and Mg under deficiency conditions. The positive correlation between Mn and Ca under both Zn/Fe deficiency stress suggests that the homeostasis of divalent cations may be regulated through the coordinated use of shared transporters or signaling pathways. Under Zn deficiency stress, the increase in Ca and Fe contents may activate Fe-uptake related genes through the Ca2+ signaling pathway, promote Fe2+ transport, and form a Ca-Fe synergistic compensation mechanism. This remodeling of the metal ion may represent a compensatory strategy to maintain cellular functions under Zn/Fe deficiency stress in areca. For non-metallic elements, the significant reduction of C content in areca leaves under Fe/Zn deficiency stress further indicated impaired photosynthetic efficiency, consistent with zinc’s role in RuBisCO activation and iron’s function in chlorophyll biosynthesis [13, 31–32].

Flavonoids are plant secondary metabolites with multiple biological functions, including antioxidant activity and metal chelation [33–34]. In addition, flavonoids were effective antioxidants and free radical scavengers and involved in the regulation of Fe homeostasis [35]. Notably, our results showed that the most common DEGs under Zn/Fe deficiency were enriched in flavonoid biosynthesis pathways, suggesting flavonoids may be functionally associated with the maintenance of Zn/Fe homeostasis, consistent with their known roles as metal-chelating antioxidants in other plant species. Meanwhile, enrichment analysis also revealed that the xyloglucan metabolic process pathway and cell wall biogenesis pathway were central to A. catechu’s deficiency response. The consistent enrichment of these pathways implied areca has made similar adaptive adjustments under Zn/Fe deficiency conditions. Notably, this study used a 45-day Zn/Fe deficiency treatment (single time point), which reflects long-term adaptive responses but cannot capture dynamic early-stage transcriptional and physiological changes. Future studies with multiple time points will help elucidate the sequential regulatory mechanisms of A. catechu in response to Zn/Fe deficiency.

Comparison of Zn/Fe deficiency responses revealed both conserved and divergent adaptive strategies in areca. Conserved responses included leaf anatomical remodeling, reduced carbon assimilation, activated phenylpropanoid/flavonoid metabolism, and xyloglucan-mediated cell wall remodeling. Divergent responses were reflected in tissue-specific metal ion redistribution: Fe deficiency mainly induced compensatory Zn accumulation in L3 leaves, while Zn deficiency enhanced Fe and Ca accumulation in L3 leaves. These shared and distinct responses reflect a flexible regulatory network that balances Zn/Fe crosstalk and maintains cellular homeostasis under different micronutrient limitations.

Furthermore, plant Zn/Fe homeostasis is tightly regulated by TFs, such as bHLH, WRKY, Iron Deficiency-responsive Element-binding Factor1/2 (IDEF1/IDEF2), and NAC family [36–38]. For instance, four bHLH TF genes bHLH38, bHLH39, bHLH100, and bHLH101 were induced to up-regulate expression in Arabidopsis under Fe-deficient stress. Furthermore, these genes also interact with FER-like Fe deficiency-induced transcription factor (FIT) to regulate the expression of FRO2 and IRT1 [39–40]. Similarly, OsFIT (OsbHLH156), OsbHLH56, OsbHLH60, OsbHLH58, OsbHLH59, and OsbHLH63 were also involved in Fe homeostasis in Oryza sativa [41–42]. In the early stage of Fe deficiency treatment, the TF IDEF1 can regulate the expression of Fe transport-related genes by binding CATGC elements in the promoter region, thereby mediating the early response of plants to Fe deficiency [36]. A NAC TF family member, NAM-B1, was identified as an important regulator of Fe and Zn homeostasis in Triticum aestivum‌ [43–44]. Consistent with these findings, our transcriptome and co-expression analyses identified key candidate TFs (e.g., WRKY3, MYB105, bHLH78) potentially involved in Zn/Fe crosstalk and deficiency responses in areca.

Beyond TFs, transporters are crucial for Zn/Fe uptake and translocation, including major facilitator superfamily (MFS), ATP-Binding cassette (ABC), zinc/Iron permease (ZIP), natural resistance-associated macrophage protein (NRAMP) and heavy metal-associated (HMA) [12, 27]. In our previous study, we identified and analyzed the AcZIP [25]. According to the Zn/Fe ion content and AcZIPs genes’ expression pattern, several AcZIP genes, especially AcZIP3 and AcZIP4, were identified to be involved in the transport and absorption of Zn and Fe in areca seedlings under Zn/Fe-deficient conditions. In our study, WGCNA revealed that additional transporters (AcMFS, AcCCX1, AcABC, AcHMA4, AcHMA37, AcHAK6) showed significant differential expression Fe/Zn-deficient seedlings, highlighting their potential roles in areca Zn/Fe homeostasis. We also performed a systematic comparison with their well-characterized homologs in model plants. These homologous genes exhibit different physiological functions in different species. In O. sativa, OsHAK1, OsHAK7, and OsHAK10 were reported to be involved in K+ uptake in root [45]. OsHMA9 is induced by high concentrations of Cu/Zn/Cd ions and is mainly expressed in vascular bundles and anthers. Furthermore, compared to the wild type (WT), the hma9 knockout mutant showed significantly increased the absorption of Cu2+ in roots [46].

In conclusion, by integrating ionomic, transcriptomic, and WGCNA data, we established a regulatory model for A. catechu in response to Zn/Fe deficiency. First, Zn/Fe deficiency triggers tissue-specific ion redistribution, including compensatory accumulation of Fe or Zn and altered Ca/Mg homeostasis. Subsequently, these changes are accompanied by transcriptional activation of phenylpropanoid/flavonoid biosynthesis and xyloglucan metabolism, which may be related to cell wall remodeling and stress adaptation. Then, WGCNA identified four key stress-associated modules that precisely correspond to Zn/Fe-deficient leaf and root tissues. Within these modules, hub TFs (bHLH, MYB, WRKY) and metal transporters (AcHMA37, AcHAK6, AcABC) form a core regulatory network that modulates metal uptake and translocation. The alteration of the expression pattern of transporters also responds to the remodeling of metal element contents in areca leaves and roots under Fe/Zn deficiency. However, to further solidify these conclusions and precisely delineate their substrate specificity, future validation utilizing specialized metal-sensitive yeast mutants will be crucial to unequivocally confirm their transport capabilities and tolerance mechanisms under more physiologically relevant conditions in areca.

Conclusion

This integrated study deciphered the molecular mechanisms underlying areca’s response to Zn/Fe deficiency stress. Our findings demonstrated that prolonged micronutrient stress induces significant physiological alterations, including leaf structural modifications and C accumulation decreases, accompanied by compensatory accumulation of Fe under Zn deficiency and vice versa. Transcriptomic and WGCNA analyses systematically revealed the central involvement of phenylpropanoid/flavonoid biosynthesis and xyloglucan metabolism pathways in Fe/Zn deficiency stress response. Through co-expression network analysis, we identified stress-specific modules and seven metal transporters, which may play significant roles in Zn/Fe deficiency stress. These results provide novel insights into the regulatory networks governing micronutrient homeostasis in areca palm and establish a valuable genetic foundation for developing improved cultivars with enhanced nutrient efficiency.
