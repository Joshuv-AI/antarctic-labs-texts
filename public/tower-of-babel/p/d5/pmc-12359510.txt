Kun–Qi birth cohort (KQBC) study for the incidence, aetiology and risk factors of chronic kidney disease in Chinese children: a cohort profile
Authors: Yiwen Shen, Tianyi Liu, Jiaojiao Liu, Xia Han, Juanjuan Wu, Li Xie, Xingdong Chen, Biao Xu, Yinghua Sun, Suxiang Xu, Qinyi Zhang, Na Xie, Linlin Zhang, Jianfeng Ji, Weizhi Mo, Yuanxin Liu, Xinghua Shi, Jun Shen, Wanshan Qiu, Jian Huang, Qian Shen, Yihui Zhai, Hong Xu, On behalf of the KQBC group

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe Kun–Qi birth cohort (KQBC) was established to investigate the incidence, aetiology and risk factors of chronic kidney disease (CKD) in early childhood (0–6 years). Additionally, the study analysed developmental phenotypic trajectories and their associated paediatric diseases.ParticipantsInfants registered or residing in Kunshan and Qidong, Jiangsu Province, who participated in the Chinese Child Healthcare Programme (0–6 years) were enrolled. The baseline population was stratified into enhanced and basic cohorts. General characteristics, periconceptional and perinatal information and health check-up data were collected from each participant. Dual screening (urinary ultrasound and urine dipstick tests) was conducted during scheduled check-ups. Structured CKD questionnaires were administered, and biosamples (urine and dried blood spots) were obtained for the long-term storage and analysis using standard procedures. Based on the proposed referral procedure, participants with abnormal results were referred for further diagnosis and evaluation.Findings to dateThe KQBC enrolled 10 127 infants (5392 boys and 4735 girls) with a mean age of 2.11±1.68 months. Urinary system ultrasound and urine dipstick screening showed positivity rates of 8.9% (900/10 083) and 6.5% (532/8233), respectively. Baseline ultrasound screening facilitated timely surgical intervention in six children. Among the 7965 respondents, only 13% demonstrated awareness of paediatric CKD. Additionally, 7273 dried blood spots and 7990 urine specimens were collected and stored. A paediatric CKD screening platform was developed.Future plansAccording to our plan, the KQBC will be followed up for 3–6 years to investigate the incidence, aetiology and risk factors of early onset CKD. In-depth developmental phenotypes, such as urinary multiomics and ultrasound imaging, should be studied for longitudinal paediatric kidney health. We aim to develop multimodal risk prediction models by integrating genomics and artificial intelligence algorithms. The KQBC also enables prospective cohort studies addressing extensive research questions.Trial registration numberNCT06018831.Keywords: Child, Community child health, Paediatric nephrology, PUBLIC HEALTH

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a common, chronic and non-communicable disease characterised by progressive kidney damage. CKD poses a significant threat to public health,1 affecting nearly 10% of adults and over 850 million people worldwide.2 Paediatric CKD is severe and has a high incidence rate, affecting 15–74.7 children per million3 and leading to significant physical and mental health problems. Once CKD progresses to end-stage renal disease (ESRD), mortality increases 30–150 times compared with that of healthy peers,4 resulting in substantial healthcare expenditures.5 6 Due to its insidious onset, awareness of CKD remains low, and diagnosis is often delayed. More than 70% of children are not diagnosed until stages 3–57. Consequently, early detection, identification of high-risk individuals and prompt intervention are crucial to reducing disease incidence, slowing disease progression and improving outcomes.8

Unlike in adults, CKD in children is most often congenital or genetic and represents a leading cause of ESRD.9 Congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract (CAKUT) are common and significant birth defects, accounting for 30%–40% of congenital malformations in China.10 Urinary system ultrasound is a primary strategy for early screening.11 Previous international studies have reported CAKUT-positive rates of approximately 0.96%–5.5% in ultrasound screenings of healthy infants,12,14 with 75% detected postnatally.13 Developed countries, such as Germany, Japan and Italy, initiated neonatal urinary system ultrasound screening programme in the 1990s. In Japan, over 30 years of screening has shown that delaying dialysis by 2.5 years in screened and diagnosed CKD cases would result in substantial cost-effectiveness and reduced annual dialysis expenditure by approximately 40 million yen.15 Our previous screening of 8827 high-risk infants in Shanghai revealed a CAKUT-positive rate as high as 13.5%,16 and children with obstructive kidney disease underwent timely surgical intervention to preserve renal function. Furthermore, urine dipstick screening facilitates the early detection of glomerular diseases. Since 1973, Japan has conducted urine screening in school children, and this long-term strategy has delayed the peak age of CKD progression to uraemia by 11 years.17 In Taiwan, China, since 1990, urine dipstick screening of primary and junior school students has reduced the incidence of severe proteinuria from 10.5% to 7.1%.18 Certain hereditary kidney diseases can be detected early through dual screening and genetic testing.

13 countries and regions have implemented urinary system ultrasonography and urine screening programmes for children as of today.19 However, awareness of paediatric CKD remains low in China, and regional development is highly imbalanced. Thus, early CKD screening that is suitable for China’s national conditions, easy to promote and aligned with advanced international standards is an urgent problem. Although we previously explored CKD screening in specific high-risk paediatric populations, comprehensive epidemiological data are lacking, particularly for paediatric general populations. Additionally, most earlier studies were cross sectional, leaving a gap in longitudinal follow-up results.

Accordingly, we established a prospective and general population-based Kun–Qi birth cohort (KQBC), using paediatric CKD as the primary outcome. Urinary system ultrasound and urine dipstick screening (dual screening) combined with a maternal and child health information system, a 0–6 child healthcare programme and a follow-up for 3–6 years are ongoing. The objective of the KQBC is to reveal the epidemiological characteristics of CKD in 0–6 years and develop multimodal risk prediction systems. We hope that the KQBC will contribute more Chinese data on CKD.

Cohort description

Who is in the cohort?

Infants aged 0–12 months were recruited from the general population (regardless of health status) as registered or permanent residents and voluntarily participated in national child healthcare check-ups for children aged 0–6 years in Kunshan and Qidong in Jiangsu Province. In 2019, Jiangsu Province completed its maternal and child health information system, enabling the integration of hospitals’ electronic medical records. Based on our previous research, which reported a CKD screening abnormality rate of 13.5% in high-risk infants16 and considering a 10% loss to follow-up rate, statistical calculations indicated that the study required a minimum sample size of 9900 cases. In recent years, the number of infants in Kunshan and Qidong has been approximately 10 000 and 3000, respectively. As branch hospitals of the Children’s Hospital of Fudan University (CHFU), Kunshan and Qidong Maternal and Child Health Hospitals have nearly 8100 and 2500 infants, accounting for over 80% of the infants in the two cities. Therefore, we planned to recruit approximately 8000 infants from Kunshan Maternal and Child Health Hospital and nearly 2000 from Qidong to form the baseline study population. Moreover, we selected 10% of all participants from communities with higher administrative cooperation and adherence as the enhanced cohort, while 90% were designated as the basic cohort. Informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection.

The primary outcome of the KQBC was paediatric CKD. We hypothesised that genetic factors, periconceptional and perinatal environments and maternal diseases were associated with the incidence and progression of paediatric CKD. The study design and protocol were registered in a clinical trial registry. Recruitment procedures

The recruitment sites were in Kunshan and Qidong, including the Maternal and Child Health Hospitals and their community health service centres. The recruitment was conducted by paediatric healthcare physicians.

Before recruitment began, the research team from the CHFU organised training for paediatric healthcare physicians at the Maternal and Child Health Hospitals in both cities, covering the screening project, procedures and inclusion/exclusion criteria. Each hospital then provided further training on screening and referral procedures for physicians at affiliated community health service centres.

During recruitment, physicians identified eligible infants while establishing health examination records. They introduced the screening project to parents in outpatient clinics, obtained written informed consent, distributed the ‘Kidney Diary’ handbook (with an information letter) and scheduled screening.

The Maternal and Child Health Hospitals were responsible for child health record establishment within their jurisdictions. For infants from other areas, community paediatricians established records at local centres and referred eligible cases to the Kunshan and Qidong Maternal and Child Health Hospitals for kidney disease screening, ensuring the timely completion of referral records.

Follow-up procedure

On enrolment, participants underwent a urinary system ultrasound and urine dipstick screening, completed a baseline CKD questionnaire and provided biosamples (urine and dried blood spots). During the follow-up, dual screening was conducted simultaneously with routine childcare. For the basic cohort, the second urine dipstick screening, dried blood spot and follow-up questionnaire were conducted at 1–2 years, while the third assessment, conducted at 2–3 years, will mostly mirror the baseline, except that the urine samples will not be collected. For the enhanced cohort, participants will undergo the same procedures as in the first year (urine dipstick screening, urinary system ultrasound screening, urine sample collection, dried blood spot collection and follow-up questionnaire) annually until the age of 6 years (figure 1).

As shown in figure 1, clinical data from the periconceptional period, infancy (0–1 year), early childhood (1–3 years) and preschool period (3–6 years) will be collected or extracted from multiple public platforms and data sources. First, from the Jiangsu Maternal and Child Health Information System, the following will be obtained: demographic information, including name, healthcare number, date of birth and sex; birth history, including birth weight, gestational age, body length, head circumference, mode of delivery and Apgar score; birth defects; pregnancy history and childbirth history, including maternal age at delivery, body mass index (BMI), mode of conception, pregnancy complications, medication history, prenatal ultrasound screening, non-invasive DNA testing and amniotic fluid conditions. Second, routine child healthcare examination data, including height, weight, head circumference, BMI, blood pressure and the appearance of the eyes and ears, will be collected from the Maternal and Child Specialty Electronic Medical Record System. Ultrasound reports and original image data were extracted using a PACS/RIS workstation. Finally, we used the hospital’s electronic medical record system to obtain the outcome data. Participants’ healthcare numbers were used as index variables across multiple data sources.

During follow-up, comprehensive strategies were implemented to keep participants engaged in the study. Parents received a ‘Kidney Diary’ handbook at enrolment for recording screening results and physical examination data. Contact information, including the referral assistant’s phone number, email address and WeChat QR code, was provided in both the handbook and the collaborative hospitals for consultation. To maximise participant retention, we established a WeChat referral group to facilitate timely communication among parents, site paediatricians and paediatric kidney experts. In addition, we provided a green channel (fast track) for abnormal participants, referred to as the CHFU for convenient follow-up visits. Specialists were assigned to offer consultations and treatment, provide convenience and enhance compliance. Moreover, an automated text message platform was used to remind the participants of their schedules and follow-up appointments. In fact, based on our 20 years of screening experience in Shanghai, China, we have established WeChat follow-up groups and a streamlined referral pathway. Drawing on this experience, we expected the 3-year follow-up rate for the KQBC study to reach approximately 80%. This prediction was supported by previous screening data20 from Shanghai as well as the high level of participant engagement observed during the initial phase of this study.

Study measures

Urinary system ultrasound and urine dipstick screening

Ultrasound screening was performed by professionally trained sonographers using the following standardised ultrasound devices and probes: GE Voluson E8 with C1-5-D and 11 L-D probes, GE Voluson E10 with C1-5-D and 9 L-D probes, Philips Affiniti 70 with C5-1 and L12-5 probes and Philips Epiq 5C with C5-1 and L12-5 probes. The participants were examined in the supine or prone position. The examination procedure included the assessment of kidney location, number, morphology, size and symmetry. The anteroposterior renal pelvic diameter (APRPD) was measured in the transverse view, with ≥5 mm defined as pelvic dilation (mild: 5–9.9 mm; moderate: 10–14.9 mm and severe: ≥15 mm). For bilateral dilation, the more severe side was recorded. Cases of renal calyx dilation and renal parenchymal thinning were upgraded to one severity level. The renal parenchymal thickness and calyx diameter were measured, and cortical blood flow was evaluated to assess renal perfusion. Additional renal abnormalities were documented, such as cysts, masses, calcifications and crystals. The examination also included an evaluation of the bilateral ureters and measuring ureteral diameter, wall thickness, morphology and orifices. The bladder morphology, wall integrity, thickness and surface characteristics were also recorded. At least eight images were stored for each participant (bilateral kidney transverse and longitudinal views, bilateral renal blood flow and bladder transverse and longitudinal views), with additional images captured for positive findings. The ultrasound department of the CHFU provided standardised reporting templates (online supplemental appendix 1). Quality control, including image review and report standardisation assessment, was conducted quarterly by the collaborative hospitals’ ultrasound departments and the Jiangsu Province Ultrasound Quality Control Platform. Specifically, a subset of ultrasound images was independently reviewed by two experienced sonographers every quarter. The level of agreement between observers was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa coefficient to quantify interobserver reliability. Discrepancies were discussed and resolved through consensus meetings, and feedback was provided to all participating sonographers to enhance consistency and standardisation further.

The urine samples were analysed using a Mission U120 Ultra Urine Analyser with 14-parameter urinalysis test strips (ACON Laboratories Inc., China). The following parameters were analysed: leucocytes, urobilinogen, microalbumin, protein, bilirubin, glucose, ascorbic acid, specific gravity, ketones, nitrite, creatinine, pH, occult blood and urinary calcium levels. Urinary calcium levels were interpreted after dividing them by urinary creatinine levels (in the same units). Urinary dipstick testing was performed in the site laboratories of the collaborative hospitals, which held certifications from the National Clinical Laboratory Centre for quality assessment. Daily internal quality control of urinary chemical analysis was conducted using standard solutions (Yihua Medical Technology Co., Ltd., China).

According to the proposed referral protocol, participants with abnormal screening results were referred to CHFU (figure 2). Ultrasonographic screening was performed using a stratified referral strategy. For patients with suspected severe urinary system abnormalities, including APRPD≥15 mm, ureteropelvic tract dilatation (UTD) with febrile urinary tract infection (UTI), kidney crystals or space-occupying lesions, referral was recommended within 2 weeks. Patients with small kidneys, renal agenesis, duplex kidneys, renal cysts, ureteral dilatation or distortion, ectopic openings, distal ureteral cysts or bladder cysts were recommended for scheduled referrals. Additionally, referral was recommended if the UTD worsened or if mild UTD persisted beyond 12 months. For APRPD between 5 and 14.9 mm, regular follow-up every 1–3 months at the collaborative hospitals was advised. Furthermore, persistent urinary dipstick screening abnormalities warranted referral, such as ≥2 positive results for urinary leucocytes, occult blood, protein, microalbumin, calcium and glucose, which might indicate UTI, glomerular disease or metabolic disorders. Notably, due to potential false-positive or false-negative results in urinary calcium screening using a urine dipstick, cases with abnormal urinary calcium/creatinine ratios required comprehensive evaluation incorporating ultrasound findings, family history and biochemical re-examination to verify diagnostic accuracy.

The flowchart of the referral procedure. APRPD, anteroposterior renal pelvic diameter; CAKUT, congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract; MCH, maternal and children health hospital; UTD, ureteropelvic tract dilatation; UTI, urinary tract infection; Yr, year.

For participants meeting the referral criteria, a paediatric nephrology screening clinic was established at the CHFU, where a team of advanced specialists provided services. Interventions were implemented based on CKD type and treatment principles. For instance, for participants with renal dysplasia, comprehensive evaluations included urinary sediment, urinary microprotein series and urinary system ultrasound verification, followed by further assessment of renal function and renal dynamic scintigraphy when necessary. Diagnostic procedures for patients with persistent glycosuria included urinary sedimentation, blood glucose testing, biochemical analysis and endocrine function examinations. Trio whole-exome sequencing was performed to screen for genetic factors. Finally, the expert team arranged follow-up plans, and clinical professionals maintained follow-up records.

CKD questionnaires

Questionnaire 1 was completed at the recruitment sites to collect potential risk factors, and Questionnaire 2 was completed at the follow-up sites. Questionnaire 1 collected information on family history of kidney disease, awareness of paediatric CKD, awareness of dual screening and lifestyle habits, including smoking during pregnancy, alcohol consumption, drinking water, takeout frequency, medications, occupational exposure and education level. Questionnaire 2 collected information on new cases of family history of kidney disease, recent kidney disease diagnosis and hospitalisation (table 1). Biosample collection

In this study, we collected small blood samples from the participants to obtain dried blood spots for long-term storage. At room temperature (20–25 ℃), capillary tubes (approximately, 40 µL per tube) were used for blood spot sampling, with each tube immersing one blood spot, collecting three blood spots in total, ensuring that each blood spot diameter was >8 mm, with blood penetrating to the reverse side and blood spots being consistent in size on both sides. After air drying the dried blood spots at room temperature for 3 hour, every 100 filter papers were bundled with rubber bands and temporarily stored in a 4 °C refrigerator (not exceeding 48 hours). Two trained investigators then transported the samples to the site laboratory for long-term storage in a –80 °C freezer.

Urine samples were collected during urine screening. Laboratory technicians distributed the urine collection bags, cups (Longjian Medical Technology Co., China) and labelled urine tubes (Corning Inc., USA). They guided parents to attach the urine collection bags and instructed them to deliver the samples to the laboratory within 2 hour. For children aged over 3 years, first-morning urine (the first urination after waking up before any physical activity) was required. After urine collection, laboratory technicians checked whether the collection was qualified, and if the urine was contaminated with faeces, recollection was required. The collected urine samples were transferred into two tubes. One tube was used for testing and recording results; the other tube was registered and temporarily stored in a 4 °C refrigerator (not exceeding 24 hours) and then transferred by investigators to the site laboratory’s –80 °C freezer.

For those who screened positive and were suspected of having hereditary kidney disease, ethylenediamine tetraacetic acid (EDTA)-anticoagulated whole blood samples were collected from each child and their parents. Samples were temporarily stored at 4 °C (not exceeding 1 week) after collection at room temperature (20–25 ℃). Designated clinical professionals transferred the samples to Chigene Translational Medicine Research Centre Co. (Beijing). Trio whole-exome sequencing was completed within 1–3 months. Gene interpretation meetings were conducted with paediatric kidney and clinical genetics experts from the CHFU to analyse genetic reports.

Paediatric CKD screening platform

We developed a paediatric CKD screening platform comprising seven core modules: cohort baseline information, status updates, ultrasound and urine screening results, growth and development parameters, systemic phenotypes, biosample information and CKD questionnaires, with a total of 204 standardised data fields—to integrate the multidimensional clinical data collected by the KQBC and support clinical research. The platform development included the following key steps: (1) designing a homogeneous data collection protocol; (2) establishing a multicentre data entry and quality control system; (3) implementing a hierarchical access control mechanism; (4) developing data-sharing agreements and (5) assembling a professional data management team.

Outcomes: abnormal findings in screening

The screening results were obtained from reports provided by the site ultrasound and laboratory departments. Paediatric outpatient physicians completed the registration and recorded the screening results, with designated personnel responsible for data entry, management and statistical analysis. Participants with positive screening results underwent further local follow-up or were referred to the CHFU for a definitive diagnosis with close monitoring of disease outcomes. Subsequently, the professional clinical team extracted detailed information on paediatric CKD using the Jiangsu Province Maternal and Child Health Information System and the Electronic Medical Record System of the CHFU.

Statistical methods

Quantitative data were presented as mean±SD, whereas categorical data were expressed as numbers (percentages). Sample size calculations were performed using power analysis and sample size (PASS) 2021 software. Based on a CKD screening positivity rate of 13.5%16 and an anticipated dropout rate of 10%, with a 95% confidence level, a minimum sample size of 9900 infants was determined to be necessary. Multivariate logistic regression was used to investigate the association between periconceptional and perinatal factors and early postnatal urinary system ultrasound screening outcomes in the offspring. Ultrasound screening results were the dependent variable, and periconceptional and perinatal clinical factors were treated as independent variables. ORs and 95% CIs were calculated after adjusting for all potential covariates with a significance level of 0.05.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in any way in the study design and conduction of this research.

Urinary system ultrasound screening had been completed in 10 083 baseline participants by the end of February 2024, identifying 900 cases with abnormal findings (896 urinary system abnormalities and 4 non-urinary system abnormalities), with an abnormality rate of 8.9%. Among the 900 cases, the majority were urinary tract dilatation (UTD, 82%), followed by renal crystals, duplex kidney/duplicated renal pelvis, bilateral renal hyperechogenicity and renal cysts. Additionally, one case of Wilms’ tumour was identified. Baseline ultrasound screening facilitated early surgical intervention in six children, of which one child was diagnosed with Wilms’ tumour at 3 months of age, and five children were diagnosed with severe hydronephrosis due to ureteropelvic junction obstruction (UPJO) at 1.5 months of age. All five cases underwent surgery, while renal function was still normal. In the remaining case of UPJO with acute renal insufficiency, renal function returned to normal after surgery. Urine dipstick screening was completed in 8233 participants, with 532 cases showing initial abnormalities, representing an abnormality rate of 6.5%. The most common finding was leukocyturia (88.7%), followed by haematuria. Notably, one case of renal glycosuria was detected and, subsequently, confirmed as resulting from an SLC5A2 gene mutation through genetic testing. A total of 7965 baseline survey questionnaires were collected using an online questionnaire platform, achieving a response rate of 78.7%. The survey results revealed that only 13% (1039 cases) of the respondents were aware of paediatric CKD, similar to a recent CKD awareness survey among Chinese adults.21

We performed a preliminary association analysis using baseline questionnaire data to examine several environmental factors, including parental drinking water type (purified water, mineral water, boiled water, filtered water and others), weekly frequency of takeout food consumption, smoking, alcohol consumption and occupational exposure. However, none of these environmental factors were found to be significantly associated with abnormal findings on ultrasound or urine screening in the offspring. Several factors may account for these results, including (1) limited exposure characterisation due to the classification of drinking water only by type without chemical analysis, (2) generally healthy dietary habits indicated by low takeout food consumption, (3) low prevalence of maternal smoking, alcohol use and occupational exposures leading to insufficient statistical power and (4) potential recall bias inherent in retrospective questionnaire data affecting accuracy.

This platform integrates clinical information from various sources, including Maternal and Child Health Information System, Maternal and Child Medical Record System, Picture Archiving and Communication System/Radiology Information System (PACS/RIS) Workstations, Hospital Electronic Medical Record Systems, KQBC screening results, biosamples and developmental phenotype data (table 4). Currently, the platform supports data entry and queries, with plans to implement additional data analysis and report generation functions. We successfully integrated the baseline screening data from 10 022 participants. The screening platform will be continuously updated to include dynamic parameters, such as child healthcare growth and development data, ultrasound and urine screening follow-up results, genomic data, multiomics analysis results from biosamples and renal ultrasound imaging.

Future plans

We have a comprehensive follow-up plan for 3 or 6 years. This study was supported financially by various grants. This study focused on the first phase of establishing a baseline population. Subsequent research will include data collection plans for ages 1–6 years (preschool period). Between the ages of 1 and 6 years, dual screening data and biosamples will be collected by collaborative medical institutions during routine child healthcare visits at 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, 4, 5 and 6 years. Additionally, we will use the Jiangsu Maternal and Child Health Information System to collect physical measurement data, including height, weight, blood pressure and child health examination information. We plan to conduct multiomics research on genomics, metabolomics, proteomics and ultrasound imaging using biosamples collected at various stages to identify novel biomarkers associated with kidney growth and paediatric CKD. The multimodal risk prediction models for paediatric CKD will be developed and validated by integrating artificial intelligence algorithms, clinical information, multiomics data, ultrasound imaging data and biomarkers.

Strengths and limitations

Our cohort demonstrated three significant strengths compared with the existing birth cohorts. The KQBC represents the first prospective birth cohort focusing on paediatric CKD as the primary outcome, implementing a pioneering dual screening strategy combining urinary system ultrasonography and urine dipstick testing during early infancy in a general population. This non-invasive and effective screening strategy enables the early detection of early onset CKD in children, supports the development of a screening system suitable for China’s specific conditions and reveals the incidence and disease spectrum of early onset CKD. Second, collecting and storing genetic factors, environmental information, urine samples, dried blood spots and ultrasound imaging data facilitate multiomics research to identify risk factors and novel biomarkers for early onset CKD, enabling the establishment of multimodal prediction models. Finally, establishing a CKD screening platform with longitudinal follow-up data supports subsequent in-depth developmental phenomics and related diseases.

This cohort study has some limitations. First, participants were recruited from a population that voluntarily presented at maternal and child health examination sites, which may have induced a selection bias due to their stronger awareness of kidney health. The study collected urine samples and dried blood spots containing small blood volumes from participants but did not include whole blood samples (except in cases of suspected hereditary kidney disease). Moreover, no parental- or pregnancy-related biosamples were routinely preserved. We plan to obtain informed consent from families willing to participate in future studies to collect the previously unmentioned biosamples.
