How well do we write for patients? Longitudinal analysis of the readability and complexity of 4.6 million ophthalmic clinical letters
Authors: Ariel Yuhan Ong, Christine A Kiire, Siegfried K Wagner, Quang Nguyen, Peter B M Thomas, Pearse A Keane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesEffective clinician–patient communication is a fundamental pillar of high-quality clinical care and can influence patient engagement, treatment adherence and clinical outcomes. However, the extent to which clinical letters meet recommended readability standards remains largely unquantified. We aimed to benchmark this at scale in an ophthalmology setting—the highest-volume outpatient specialty in the United Kingdom (UK) healthcare service.MethodsOver 4.6 million outpatient clinic letters written for 804 986 patients across 17 subspecialty services were included. Standard readability scores (including Flesch Reading Ease (FRE), SMOG Index, Automated Readability Index) were calculated for letters written between 2013 and 2025 at Moorfields Eye Hospital Foundation Trust, UK. Complex linguistic features such as lexical density and type-token ratio were also evaluated, and rule-based measures were applied to identify letters addressed directly to patients. Using the FRE as the primary outcome, readability was compared across subspecialties, and temporal trends were evaluated.ResultsThe median FRE was 51.1 (IQR 41.9–58.5), indicating college-level readability which was substantially poorer than recommended for patient-facing materials. Overall, 95.3% of letters were less readable than recommended, and these patterns extended across all readability metrics evaluated. Readability varied across subspecialty services (p<0.001), with limited association with sociodemographic covariates such as age and ethnicity. There was a significant decline in FRE over the study period (−0.64 points/annum, p<0.001). Rate of change varied across subspecialty services and was more pronounced in some such as neuro-ophthalmology (−1.24 points/annum, p<0.001), with few being stable (optometry, p>0.05). The use of second person terms was very low (median 0 per 100 words, IQR 0.0–1.1). There was a small annual increase in the likelihood of letters being addressed directly to patients (OR 1.123, 95% CI 1.121 to 1.25), although the proportion remained low overall (2.25%).DiscussionClinical correspondence across ophthalmology services remained well above recommended readability thresholds and has become more complex over time, with substantial variability across subspecialty services. These findings will provide an empirical basis for local quality improvement initiatives. We hope that the methodological framework will also serve as a valuable resource for other specialties.ConclusionOur findings highlight a persistent gap between policy recommendations and real-world practice, underscoring the need for targeted interventions and innovative approaches to support clearer patient-centred communication to optimise patient outcomes.

Introduction

Effective clinician–patient communication can influence patient engagement, treatment adherence and clinical outcomes, and is considered to be a fundamental pillar of high-quality clinical care.1–3 Clinical correspondence (particularly letters written by clinicians) is a key contributor to this. The practice of sending copies of clinical letters directly to patients is well established in the National Health Service (NHS) in the United Kingdom (UK), where these letters serve several key purposes simultaneously: representing the primary formal record of a clinical consultation to promote continuity of care within secondary or tertiary care services, communication with primary care services, and importantly, serving as an aide memoire for patients and their carers.4 National policy has increasingly emphasised the importance of making these documents accessible and patient centric. Key drivers for this shift include the 2018 Academy of Medical Royal Colleges (AoMRC) guidance, ‘Please, write to me’, which established the best practice of writing letters directly to patients rather than about them.5 More recently, the Getting It Right First Time (GIRFT) programme’s ‘Clinically-led Specialty Outpatient Guidance’ has reinforced the need for clear and timely communication as an integral component of efficient and effective outpatient services.4

Despite these national directives, the extent to which clinical correspondence meets recommended readability standards remains largely unquantified. Although many validated readability assessment tools have been developed, existing studies are fragmented, with inconsistent choice of instruments and methodologies. In addition, studies on this topic are limited to small-scale audits in specialties such as plastic surgery and paediatrics.6–10 This has neither been studied at scale nor in an ophthalmology setting, which is the highest volume clinical specialty within the NHS today,11 meaning that a large-scale, quantitative investigation can help establish a robust benchmark, identify specific linguistic challenges, and inform targeted quality improvement initiatives. This has particularly important implications because 16% of adults in countries such as the UK are functionally illiterate,12 even as low literacy has been associated with poor health outcomes.13 More readable patient education materials have been shown to improve patient understanding,14 suggesting that readability is a critical interface between patient–clinician communication and health literacy. Poorly readable clinical correspondence has direct patient-centred consequences—when letters are not written for the reader, they may undermine informed consent, shared decision-making, engagement and equitable access to care, thereby widening existing health disparities.

Although the practice of writing directly to patients is not routine in many other health systems, with some generating clinical records but not producing clinical letters at all,15 there has been a gradual movement to allow patients to access clinician-generated records through patient portals and mechanisms such as OpenNotes.16 As the move towards transparency increases internationally, understanding the linguistic demands of clinical correspondence becomes relevant beyond the UK, and may offer unique insights into how clinical documentation is interpreted by patients in different models of communication.

This study therefore aims to perform a comprehensive quantitative evaluation of the readability and linguistic complexity of patient-facing clinical letters across all ophthalmic subspecialties at one of the largest specialist eye hospitals worldwide.

Methods

This was a retrospective cohort study at the Moorfields Eye Hospital NHS Foundation Trust, London, UK. Moorfields encompasses 27 networked sites that together serve a socioeconomically and ethnically diverse catchment population. At present, this includes 6 million people across London, representing up to 9% of the UK population.

Data source

The study utilised outpatient clinic letters derived from the local electronic health record (EHR) system (OpenEyes, Apperta Foundation). Example letters are presented in online supplemental figure 1. Letters could be addressed to—and therefore written for—healthcare professionals or patients and copied to the other party. Only one copy of each letter written to the primary recipient (the ‘intended audience’) was included in this study. The letters were authored between 1 January 2013 and 1 November 2025, and cover the full spectrum of ophthalmic subspecialties and ophthalmology-adjacent services including orthoptics and optometry.

Tabular data including patient demographics (age at visit, sex, self-reported ethnicity with codes mapped to the NHS Data Dictionary,17 Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD) as the official measure of socioeconomic deprivation in England18) and clinical visit metadata (subspecialty service, date) were also extracted from the Moorfields Data Warehouse.

Feature extraction and definitions

To quantify readability, each letter was processed using the textstat library in Python to calculate standard readability scores from a suite of validated readability formulae. We selected the Flesch Reading Ease (FRE) and Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level (which translates the FRE to a United States school grade level) to provide general estimates of textual difficulty based on sentence length and word complexity; the Simple Measure of Gobbledygook (SMOG) Index to provide stricter grade-level estimates driven by polysyllabic words; the Automated Readability Index, also a grade-level estimate but which is based on mean characters per word and mean words per sentence, and which mitigates syllable-based distortions introduced by abbreviations common in ophthalmology; and the type-token ratio, which compares the number of distinct words to the total number of words to capture lexical diversity.

To quantify specific linguistic and grammar/structural features, the spaCy natural language processing library was employed to assess the mean sentence length for sentences containing more than three words (to exclude domain-specific clinical shorthand or abbreviated clinical expressions); lexical density (the ratio of content-bearing words such as nouns, verbs, adjectives and adverbs to the total word count); and the frequency of nominalisations (abstract nouns derived from verbs or adjectives, which are markers of impersonal prose).

A simple rule-based measure was applied to assess whether letters were being written to patients, by calculating second-person language (‘you’, ‘your’ and ‘yours’, all case insensitive) as a ratio of occurrences per 100 words.

The use of multiple common and complementary metrics allows a broader assessment of linguistic complexity over any single one alone. These measures are summarised in online supplemental table 1.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Python version 3.12 with the pandas, numpy, statsmodels and scipy libraries. Normality was assessed by visual inspection of histograms and Q-Q plots given the large sample size.

For the descriptive analyses, continuous data were reported with the median and IQR, and categorical data with number and percentage. The χ2 test was employed to conduct univariate analyses for categorical data, and the Mann-Whitney U test and Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous data. Outcomes were considered statistically significant at a level of p<0.05.

Socio-demographic characteristics were analysed using the patient as the unit of analysis. Letter count per patient was modelled using a negative binomial generalised linear model with a log link to account for over-dispersion, and coefficients were exponentiated to obtain incidence rate ratios (IRRs) with 95% CIs.

For all analyses relating to readability and linguistic complexity, the unit of analysis was the letter. Readability metrics were compared across subspecialties using non-parametric tests. Overall differences were assessed using the Kruskal-Wallis test, followed by post hoc pairwise Dunn’s tests with Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate correction to examine the differences between subspecialty services. To quantify the magnitude and direction of these pairwise differences, Cliff’s delta was calculated for each comparison to provide an effect size estimate independent of sample size.

A multivariable linear regression model was employed to estimate the association between patient sociodemographic characteristics and subspecialty service with readability. Age, sex, ethnicity, deprivation (IMD), subspecialty and year were included as covariates. As some patients contributed multiple letters, standard errors were clustered at the patient level to account for within-patient correlation.

Temporal trends were examined using univariate linear regressions within each subspecialty, including year as a covariate. FRE was selected as the primary readability metric for these analyses because it is widely used in the health literacy literature, provides a continuous scale that allows for a more nuanced interpretation of differences rather than the other grade-level metrics, and is less prone to extreme values. These analyses were primarily descriptive and focused on effect sizes and trends rather than formal hypothesis testing across a defined family of comparisons. As such, multiple-comparison correction was not applied.

Binary logistic regression was used to model temporal trends in letters classified as patient-addressed, with time (years) as the predictor. Second-person pronoun density was treated as a continuous variable and binarised to distinguish letters addressed directly to patients versus isolated and/or incidental second-person language (eg, templated phrasing for safety netting advice). A random sample of 200 letters was drawn from the full dataset, with stratification across density values (1–10 per 100 words). Letters were manually reviewed by one ophthalmologist with 8 years’ experience (AYO). Based on this exploratory review, a pragmatic threshold of at least six second-person pronouns per 100 words was selected to classify letters as patient addressed for subsequent analyses.

Results

Characteristics of the patient cohort

The dataset comprised 4 648 289 letters from 804 986 patients (median 3, IQR 1–6 letters per patient) over the 13 year study period. There were slightly more female patients in the cohort (52.5%), with a median IMD decile of 5 (IQR 3–7) and age of 53 (IQR 31–69) years at the baseline visit. Of the patients with available ethnicity data, White was the the most common ethnicity, followed by other, Asian/Asian British, Black/Black British and Mixed. Patient characteristics are summarised in online supplemental table 2.

Letter count per patient differed across demographic and clinical subgroups. Male patients had 1.9% more letters than females (IRR 1.019, 95% CI 1.013 to 1.026). Patients self-identifying as Black/Black British had 13.1% more letters (IRR 1.132, 95% CI 1.119 to 1.145), whereas individuals from other ethnic groups had 11.2% fewer letters (IRR 0.888, 95% CI 0.881 to 0.896) compared with White patients. Older age at first presentation (IRR 1.005 per year, 95% CI 1.006 to 1.006) and less socioeconomic deprivation (IRR 1.003 per IMD decile, 95% CI 1.002 to 1.005) were associated with very marginal increases in letter count. Subspecialty service was the strongest predictor, with certain services such as medical retina (IRR 6.421, 95% CI 6.333 to 6.510) and uveitis (IRR 7.774, 95% CI 7.610 to 7.943) producing six to eight times the number of letters per patient relative to the reference ophthalmic accident and emergency service (online supplemental table 3). The subspecialty services with the highest letter count were medical retina (25.4%), glaucoma (20.0%) and cataract (10.9%); distribution across all 17 subspecialty services is detailed in online supplemental table 4.

Characteristics of the clinical letter corpus

The corpus of letters had a median count of 98 words (IQR 72–141), with 15.4 words (IQR 12.8–19.0) per sentence and six sentences (IQR 5–9) per letter. The lexical density ratio was high (0.53, IQR 0.49–0.58), with a nominalisation count of 4 (IQR 2–6). The type-token ratio, which evaluates the diversity of words used, had a median ratio of 0.74 (out of 1.0; IQR 0.68–0.81).

Overall, readability was poorer than the recommended target range for healthcare communication, which takes literacy rates in the general population into account. FRE had a median value of 51.1 (IQR 41.9–58.5), indicating substantially lower readability than the suggested reference range of 70–90 for patient-facing materials. This corresponded with a Flesch-Kincaid Grade of 10.5 (IQR 9.0–12.5), which is equivalent to a 10th grade reading level in the USA. Findings were consistent across the SMOG Index (median 13.0, IQR 11.7–14.6) and the Automated Readability Index (median 10.9, IQR 9.0–13.3) (figure 1). Overall, 95.3% of letters fell below the recommended FRE range, and 87.1%, 99.4% and 85.9% scored above the grade-level recommendations for the Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level, SMOG Index, and Automated Readability Index respectively. Example letters illustrating the extremes of readability are presented in online supplemental figure 1.

Variation in readability across subspecialties

Using FRE as the primary metric, there were substantial differences in readability across subspecialty services, with lower scores indicating poorer readability (figure 2). Pairwise effect size analysis showed that medical retina consistently produced the least readable letters, with medium-to-large negative effect sizes (δ −0.23 to −0.51) relative to all other services. Letters from the ophthalmic accident and emergency (A&E), vitreoretinal, uveitis, ocular oncology, and neuro-ophthalmology services were more difficult to read on average (δ −0.03 to −0.38), but remained more readable than medical retina (δ +0.23 to +0.33). In contrast, strabismus, paediatric ophthalmology, optometry, and orthoptics produced the most readable letters (figure 2, online supplemental figures 2 and 3).

Flesch Reading Ease score (median with IQR) across all ophthalmic subspecialty services. Boxplots demonstrate variation in readability between subspecialty services, with distinctly lower readability scores in the medical retina service and others. These patterns are supported by pairwise Dunn’s tests and by Cliff’s δ effect sizes.

A multivariable model adjusting for sociodemographic covariates (age, sex, ethnicity, deprivation) in addition to subspecialty was employed at the letter level. Standard errors were clustered at the patient level to account for the possibility of multiple letters per individual. Negative coefficients indicate lower readability (ie. more complex text), while positive coefficients indicate higher readability. Subspecialty service accounted for the largest proportion of variation, with marked differences seen across subspecialties, and with medical retina letters being substantially more complex (β −6.394, 95% CI −6.499 to −6.290, p<0.001). Age, sex, IMD and ethnicity demonstrated a minimal association too small to be of practical significance despite the statistical significance, likely owing to the large sample size. Sensitivity analyses involved the addition of stylistic clusters (data-driven groupings of letters based on structural and lexical features) as covariates to the model to mitigate unmeasured author-level variation (online supplemental figure 4).

Trends in readability over time

Overall, median FRE declined over the study period (slope −0.64 points/annum, p<0.001), which indicates that ophthalmic clinic letters have gradually become less readable over time (figure 3). Subgroup analyses by subspecialty showed some variability in both baseline readability and rate of change, with declines in readability being more pronounced in some services than others. For example, neuro-ophthalmology demonstrated one of the steepest reductions (slope −1.24 points/annum, p<0.001), while others declined by −0.31/annum only (paediatric ophthalmology, p<0.001). In contrast, the optometry service did not demonstrate any significant change (p>0.05), while the letters in the orthoptics service became slightly more readable over time (slope +0.27, p=0.004) (figure 4).

Trends in letters addressed to patients

The use of second person terms was very low (median 0 per 100 words, IQR 0.0–1.1). Only a minority of letters (2.25%) were classified as patient-addressed. There was a small annual increase in the likelihood of letters being addressed directly to patients over time, with the odds increasing by 12.3% per year (OR 1.123, 95% CI 1.121 to 1.25, p<0.001) from a low baseline rate.

Discussion

In this large longitudinal study spanning a 13-year period, we have mapped readability and linguistic complexity across more than 4.6 million ophthalmic clinical letters, providing the first such benchmark of its kind at scale.

We found that the majority of ophthalmology letters were consistently written at higher than recommended reading levels. This adds empirical weight to longstanding concerns that clinical language may impose a significant cognitive burden, especially for those with limited health literacy, which is known to be associated with ophthalmic outcomes and is considered to be one of the best predictors of health status.19 Clear clinical communication plays a key role in this regard, especially as patients have expressed a strong preference to receive letters about their clinical care20 and have reported that it increases comprehension of their diagnosis and management while reducing confusion and anxiety.5 21 This will become increasingly important with the rise in telemedicine and virtual clinics,22 where clinical correspondence may become the only communication a patient will receive. Poor readability in clinical communication may also have implications for patient safety, for example due to misunderstandings about follow-up arrangements, monitoring requirements or treatment instructions.

The results of this study align with and extend the literature, where prior reviews of patient education materials in healthcare report typical reading levels far above the recommended sixth to eighth grade range (US education system) for accessible communication.23–26 Despite these concerns, little work has examined routine clinical correspondence, bar a handful of small-scale audits in specialties such as plastic surgery and paediatrics.6–10 None has focused on ophthalmology—a specialty with particularly high communication demands due to the technical language which is often used to describe imaging findings, disease processes, and interventions. This study provides the first specialty-wide evidence base for understanding communication complexity in ophthalmology by examining routine correspondence at unprecedented scale and granularity, offering a model that can potentially be applied to other specialties in medicine where technical language, workflow pressures and documentation norms may also shape the accessibility of patient communication.

In this study, subspecialty service and cluster-derived stylistic writing patterns (as a proxy for author-level identification) accounted for a large proportion of variation, while patient demographics showed statistically significant but practically negligible effects on readability. This suggests that variation in communication quality may predominantly reflect service-level culture and documentation requirements for each service. The higher readability observed in paediatrics, optometry, and orthoptics letters may reflect differences in communication context and documentation structure. In paediatric ophthalmology, correspondence is often implicitly or explicitly directed toward parents or caregivers, which may encourage simpler language use, whereas optometry and orthoptics documentation tends to be more templated and structured, which facilitates more consistent readability.

Many communication-heavy subspecialties (eg, medical retina, uveitis, ocular oncology, neuro-ophthalmology) may rely on more technical terms and detailed imaging descriptions that introduce further layers of complexity. Improving clarity may therefore be best approached through targeted quality improvement initiatives, consideration of standardisation of patient-facing documentation, and/or integration of readability checks into EHR template design. Letter templates represent a system-level intervention that can drive readability improvements at scale and underpin initiatives such as the recent ‘Write to Me’ project which has been introduced at Moorfields to improve readability.

Despite the introduction of national guidance encouraging clinicians to write directly to patients from the AoMRC in 2018,5 we have found a trend towards worsening readability scores over time. Even with the small annual increase in the likelihood of letters being addressed directly to patients, the total proportion remains vanishingly small. This highlights the limited influence of policy alone on real-world clinical documentation practices. An important contextual factor (underexplored in the literature but central to interpreting these findings) is the inherent difficulty clinicians face in writing letters that must function simultaneously as medical record, interprofessional communication, and patient-facing communication. The dual audience complicates efforts to simplify language, and means that even well intentioned efforts to write directly to patients can be constrained by the need to record precise clinical information and findings or justification for management decisions. Time pressures compound these challenges and further reduce opportunities for detailed explanations or deliberate simplification of technical terminology.

As such, there may be a need for approaches that separate the medical record from the patient-facing explanation while keeping both aligned. One practical avenue is the co-design of a separate patient-facing digital platform that can generate accessible interpretations of clinic letters using large language models.27 A growing number of publications have employed large language models to translate clinical text into patient-friendly language,28 which could potentially improve understanding and engagement without adding to clinician workload or requiring clinicians to potentially compromise the clinical or legal completeness of their documentation. However, deploying such tools at scale, while preserving patient safety and the accuracy of the clinical record from the risk of confabulations, will be challenging. Robust validation and human-in-the-loop review is therefore likely to remain necessary following deployment. Determining the appropriate mode of integration (patient-facing vs EHR integration) also presents a complex sociotechnical challenge that requires careful thought, requiring explicit consideration of implementation timelines, sustainability and resource constraints, particularly as EHR integration may require longer-term strategy investment. At the same time, the increasing trend of using ambient voice technologies to scribe clinical consultations also has the potential to reshape clinical documentation workflows and influence the readability and structure of clinical letters. While recent pilot tests have shown unfavourable results for readability in terms of verbosity and complexity,29 these findings may reflect early stage deployments which could improve with further optimisation as adoption grows.

Strengths and limitations

The key strength of this study lies in the extensive analysis of the largest corpus of clinical letters assembled to date, enabling more stable, generalisable, and representative estimates of readability than previous studies based on small samples in the tens and low hundreds. For the first time, this scale has also enabled detailed exploration of variation across subspecialties and over time, offering more nuanced insights into how communication practices evolve within a high-volume specialty. In addition, we have moved beyond single-metric audits to examine multiple readability indices and measures of jargon use and semantic complexity, which together provide a broader and more comprehensive assessment of textual complexity and to mitigate biases inherent to any single approach, especially as readability formulae are limited in their ability to account for specialised terminology and abbreviations, and semantic tools may not fully capture the complexity of ophthalmic terms.

A key limitation is the absence of author-level identifiers, which precludes assessment of variation between individual clinicians. To address this, we clustered letters using structural and lexical features as a data-driven proxy for writing style, so as to enable partial adjustment for unmeasured author-level heterogeneity. In addition, methods used to classify the intended audience of letters are inherently imperfect and may not fully reflect how correspondence is ultimately used or interpreted in practice, particularly where letters have to serve dual clinician–patient communication purposes. As such, readability metrics should be interpreted as proxies for textual complexity rather than definitive measures of accessibility. While the analysis quantifies readability and linguistic complexity, it does not directly measure patient comprehension or examine the link between these factors and behavioural outcomes (eg, attendance at follow-up appointments, treatment adherence). Both of these would help in determining which aspects of communication matter most to patients or have the highest impact on healthcare outcomes. In addition, previous research on the readability of patient-facing educational materials suggests that higher readability improves patient understanding, although with mixed evidence on direct health outcomes and patient adherence, which would be interesting to explore further in the context of direct patient correspondence.14 Nevertheless, the findings establish a robust baseline from which outcome-focused analyses can be developed in future work. This may also include exploring effects of accessible clinical correspondence on cross-specialty communication, medical errors and patient safety.

Finally, temporal trends should be interpreted with caution. Although a single EHR system was used throughout the study period, and data-driven writing style clusters were included as a proxy for documentation practices, modelling year as a linear trend may not fully capture non-linear changes over time. In particular, shifts in clinical practice, documentation habits or institutional policies may introduce period or cohort effects that are not accounted for in this analysis.

Conclusion

In summary, clinical correspondence across ophthalmology services remains less readable than recommended and has become more complex over time. These findings highlight a persistent gap between national recommendations and real-world practice, which underscores the need for targeted interventions and innovative approaches to support clear and patient-centred communication. These findings will provide an empirical basis for local quality improvement initiatives. We hope that the methodological framework will also serve as a valuable resource for other specialties, particularly those involving cross-specialty care for vulnerable patient populations with complex comorbidities, including paediatric and geriatric populations. More broadly, we intend for these findings to contribute to the ongoing national conversation around patient-facing communication and the implementation of GIRFT and AoMRC principles, with the potential to directly enhance patient understanding and engagement—potentially internationally as well.
