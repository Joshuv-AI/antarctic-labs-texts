Beyond antibiotics: engineered metal nanozymes for resistance-evading antibacterial therapy
Authors: Yongfang Wang, Can Wu, Xiaotong Sun, Tong Ye, Chao Liu, Anqi Liu, Xuan Wu, Xin Pang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBacterial infections rank as the second leading cause of death globally, driven primarily by the alarming rise of multidrug-resistant (MDR) pathogens. These pathogens severely undermine the efficacy of conventional antibiotics and pose a grave threat to modern medical practice. To counter this urgent crisis, engineered metal nanozymes have emerged as a promising alternative therapeutic strategy. Leveraging their intrinsic enzyme-mimetic catalytic activity to generate bactericidal reactive oxygen species (ROS), metal nanozymes achieve potent broad-spectrum antibacterial action while minimizing the risk of resistance development. Beyond core ROS generation, these nanozymes employ sophisticated complementary mechanisms, including bioactive ion release and stimuli-responsive dual functionality. This multifaceted approach enables simultaneous pathogen eradication and protection of host tissue. Such unique therapeutic attributes, combined with high stability, persistent catalytic activity, favorable biocompatibility, precise tunability, and the potential for synergistic catalytic cascades, establish metal nanozymes as compelling candidates for next-generation antibacterial therapies. This review comprehensively summarizes the fundamental catalytic mechanisms underlying their antibacterial action and details advanced rational design strategies to optimize nanozyme performance. Furthermore, diverse metal-based nanozyme platforms for combating bacterial infections are classified and analyzed, highlighting recent advances in synergistic combination therapies that amplify therapeutic outcomes. Finally, we critically address persistent translational challenges and propose feasible strategies to advance these innovative platforms toward safe and effective clinical deployment against resistant infections.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Metal nanozymes, Reactive oxygen species, Combination therapy, Bacterial infection, Catalytic activity

Introduction

Bacterial infections impose a catastrophic global health burden, now recognized as the second leading cause of mortality worldwide [1]. Antibiotics, once foundational to infectious disease management, face unprecedented challenges due to the relentless proliferation of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) [2, 3]. This crisis originates from the core process of resistance to antibacterial therapy, a phenomenon where bacteria evolve biochemical mechanisms such as enzymatic drug inactivation, target modification, or efflux pump overexpression to survive previously effective treatments. This crisis threatens to regress modern medicine to a pre-antibiotic era, with the World Health Organization attributing 700,000 annual deaths directly to AMR. Projections suggest this toll could surpass 10 million by 2050 without decisive intervention [4]. Critically, this crisis goes far beyond infectious diseases, fundamentally compromising modern medical interventions such as surgery, chemotherapy, and transplantation. To appreciate the gravity of this challenge, it is essential to understand how bacteria resist traditional antibacterial therapies. Conventional antibiotics typically target specific cellular processes, such as cell wall synthesis, protein translation, or DNA replication. Bacteria develop resistance through mechanisms including the expression of enzymes that degrade the antibiotic (e.g., beta-lactamases), modification of the antibiotic’s target site, or overexpression of efflux pumps that expel the drug before it can act. Furthermore, recent studies highlight that resistance is influenced by community interactions within microbial ecosystems, where collective behaviors can slow resistance evolution or allow less resistant strains to survive. These complex, evolved biological defense mechanisms necessitate novel therapeutic strategies capable of circumventing traditional drug-target interactions.

Artificial nanozymes, which are engineered nanoparticles that mimic the catalytic activity of natural enzymes, have emerged as a potential solution to the challenges posed by antibiotic resistance. Unlike traditional antibiotics that often rely on a single molecular target, many antibacterial nanozymes employ multi-pronged mechanisms of action, primarily through the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) that damage bacterial lipids, proteins, and DNA simultaneously. This non-specific, multi-target attack significantly increases the genetic barrier for bacteria to develop resistance compared to conventional drugs [5]. Among them, metal-based nanozymes exhibit exceptional promise due to their intrinsic catalytic versatility, which arises from two key biological parallels: metals serve as essential cofactors in natural enzymes, and their rich redox chemistry supports diverse catalytic reactions [6]. The field witnessed transformative advancement following the discovery of Peroxidase (POD)-like activity produced by Fe3O4 nanoparticles (IONPs) in 2007, which stimulated intensive research into their potential as therapeutic candidates [7].

Metal nanozymes primarily eradicate bacteria through enzyme-mimetic ROS generation [8, 9]. Specifically, POD-like nanozymes convert endogenous hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) into hydroxyl radicals (•OH), while oxidase (OXD)-like variants reduce oxygen (O2) to superoxide anions (•O2−) [10, 11]. The resulting ROS inflict broad-spectrum damage through bacterial membrane disruption, degradation of essential biomolecules (DNA, proteins, lipids), and induction of metabolic collapse. Critically, this multi-target oxidative assault minimizes resistance evolution due to the intrinsically low mutagenicity of ROS-mediated killing, positioning metal nanozymes as potent solutions against multidrug-resistant (MDR) infections.

Beyond ROS generation, many metal nanozymes deploy sophisticated complementary bactericidal strategies. Bioactive metal ions (e.g., Fe2+, Mo4+, Ag+) released from nanozymes selectively disrupt bacterial metabolism and membrane integrity [12–14]. Remarkably, certain nanozymes exhibit microenvironment-dependent duality. While generating bactericidal radicals against pathogens, they simultaneously mimic antioxidant enzymes in host microenvironments [15–17]. This intelligent switching behavior is exemplified by MOF-encapsulated copper-doped carbon dots (ZIF-Cu/C-dots), which scavenge excess ROS to reduce TNF-α/IL-1β expression while releasing Zn2+ ions achieving > 90% antibacterial efficiency [18]. Such dual functionality mitigates host inflammation while maintaining antibacterial potency. The therapeutic sophistication of metal nanozymes stems from their precise engineerability [19, 20]. Their catalytic activity and selectivity can be precisely optimized through strategic control of size, morphology, composition, and surface chemistry, enabling tailored design for specific therapeutic applications [21, 22].

This review comprehensively discusses the burgeoning role of engineered metal nanozymes in resistance-evading antibacterial therapy. The appeal of metal nanozymes lies not only in their ability to circumvent antibiotic resistance pathways but also in their unique position as stable (particularly regarding storage and resistance to environmental degradation compared to biological enzymes), tunable, and multifunctional agents. We first elucidate the fundamental catalytic mechanisms and activity regulation strategies that underpin their functionality. While their catalytic proficiency and selectivity in complex physiological environments continue to be optimized and often still fall short of the precision exhibited by natural enzymes, their advantages, such as enhanced stability, cost-effective production, and the ability to integrate multiple catalytic functions within a single particle, are actively advancing their therapeutic development. We then systematically analyze major classes of antibacterial metal nanozymes and their operational modalities. Building on this foundation, we highlight innovative synergistic strategies, particularly the integration of metal nanozymes with external energy sources (e.g., light, ultrasound) to amplify bactericidal efficacy (Fig. 1). Finally, the translational barriers and future directions are discussed, underscoring the potential of metal nanozymes as next-generation therapeutics against MDR infections.

Catalytic mechanism of metal nanozymes

Metal nanozymes exhibit a unique catalytic duality, generating ROS to eradicate pathogens while scavenging ROS to protect host cells. This functional switch is intelligently governed by the distinct physicochemical gradients between infection sites and healthy tissues, with key micro-environmental cues such as pH and H2O2 concentration dictating their catalytic behavior. The potent bactericidal effect is primarily driven by POD-like and OXD-like activities. In the acidic, H2O2-rich milieu of bacterial infections, POD-mimetic nanozymes efficiently catalyze the conversion of H2O2 into highly aggressive •OH, while OXD-like nanozymes facilitate the reduction of ambient O2 to •O2−. These radical species launch a multi-pronged oxidative assault on bacterial cells, causing pervasive damage to lipid membranes, structural proteins, and genetic material, ultimately leading to rapid bacterial lysis (Fig. 2A). This broad-spectrum, non-specific damage mechanism presents a formidable barrier to the evolution of bacterial resistance. Conversely, within the neutral-pH environment of host tissues, the nanozymes intelligently shift their catalytic profile to emulate protective antioxidant enzymes. They exhibit catalase (CAT)-like activity, decomposing H2O2 into harmless H2O and O2, and superoxide dismutase (SOD)-like activity, dismutating •O2− into O2 and H2O2, thereby quenching oxidative cascades at their inception (Fig. 2B). In summary, the therapeutic precision of metal nanozymes is rooted in this intrinsic, microenvironment-driven catalytic switching. This autonomous regulatory mechanism ensures the generation of a lethal ROS flux precisely where needed for effective antibacterial action, while simultaneously mitigating collateral oxidative damage to surrounding healthy tissues, thereby achieving a self-regulated and highly selective therapeutic outcome.

Peroxidase-like activities

Research on POD-mimicking metal nanozymes has revealed increasingly complex catalytic paradigms beyond classical Fenton chemistry. Systems featuring Fe, Cu, Co, and Pt active centers demonstrate that nanozyme-mediated oxidation often involves multi-step cascade reactions rather than singular electron transfers [23–26]. The landmark identification of intrinsic horseradish peroxidase (HRP) activity by Gao team in IONPs ignited the field, yet early mechanistic interpretations remained constrained to surface-level Fenton reactions [27, 28]. Transcending this limited perspective, Dong et al. uncovered a dynamic bulk-to-surface electron transfer network governing IONP catalysis. Their model reveals four synergistic processes: (1) surface Fe2+ initiates radical generation while undergoing oxidation. (2) lattice Fe2+ continuously regenerates surface sites via Fe3+-O-Fe2+ electron highways. (3) outward migration of oxidized Fe3+ creates vacancy defects. (4) progressive bulk oxidation ultimately transforms magnetite to maghemite. This paradigm shift underscores how nanoscale catalysts leverage collective electronic behaviors rather than isolated surface chemistry (Fig. 3A) [29].

Computational approaches now illuminate previously inaccessible aspects of nanozyme catalysis. For iron sulfide (FeSx) systems, density functional theory simulations coupled with experimental validation demonstrate that POD activity hinges on orbital interactions between metal 3d electrons and H2O2 frontier orbitals. FeS2 nanozymes outperform Fe3S4 due to formation of inner-sphere complexes with H2O2, enabling stronger adsorption (− 10 to 5 eV interaction range), greater charge transfer (0.12e vs. 0.05e), and lower energy barriers for radical generation (Fig. 3B) [30]. Such atomic-level understanding enables rational design of electron transfer pathways. Comprehensive nanozyme analysis requires correlating structural features with enzymatic function. Integrated characterization of Fe3O4/AgNP composites illustrates this approach: X-ray diffraction confirms crystalline phase purity while electron microscopy visualizes nanoscale integration on cellulose scaffolds. Functionally, chromogenic assays with TMB/H2O2 reveal characteristic POD kinetics, and Michaelis–Menten analysis quantifies substrate affinity (Km = 0.387 mM for TMB), demonstrating biomimetic efficiency rivaling natural horseradish peroxidase (Fig. 3C-E) [31].

Current mechanistic research increasingly recognizes the co-regulation by surface, subsurface, and bulk atom contributions. Advanced optimization strategies now exploit interfacial electron migration, oxygen vacancy engineering, and d-orbital coupling to enhance activity. However, critical limitations persist, including catalytic efficiencies still trailing natural enzymes, incomplete understanding of dynamic pathway regulation under physiological conditions, and inherent trade-offs between oxidative aggression and structural stability. Addressing these requires innovations in multi-enzyme cascades, stimuli-responsive nanozymes activated by endogenous or exogenous methods, and standardized activity benchmarking protocols.

Oxidase-like activities

Oxidases represent a functionally diverse enzyme superfamily characterized by their substrate specificity, including amino acid oxidases targeting amine groups, glucose oxidases acting on CH-OH moieties, polyphenol oxidases modifying phenolic compounds, sulfite oxidases processing sulfur groups, and cytochrome c oxidase handling ferrous ions [32, 33]. These biocatalysts universally facilitate oxidation–reduction reactions where molecular O2 serves as the terminal electron acceptor, undergoing reduction to ROS through fundamental pathways such as univalent reduction to •O2− or divalent reduction to H2O2 [34]. This oxygen-activating capability holds profound therapeutic significance, driving substantial research into metal-based nanozymes that replicate oxidase functionality.

Novel thorn-like silver vanadate Ag4V2O7 nanorods (NRs) exemplify advanced OXD mimics, directly catalyzing chromogenic substrate oxidation without requiring exogenous H2O2. Comprehensive characterization reveals their catalytic prowess. The time-dependent spectral analysis demonstrates continuous TMB oxidation, gas chromatography–mass spectrometry identifies characteristic reaction products, while electron paramagnetic resonance spectroscopy coupled with radical trapping confirms •O2− generation through enhanced O2 dissolution and activation. This bactericidal ROS production mediates membrane disruption and cellular disintegration (Fig. 4A-C) [35]. Similarly, Au@PdNi nanozymes exhibit exceptional intrinsic OXD activity validated through targeted mechanistic studies. Radical scavenging experiments using p-benzoquinone (a specific •O2− scavenger) substantially suppress oxidation kinetics, implicating •O2− as primary catalytic mediators. Direct detection of these transient radicals was achieved via spin trapping electron spin resonance (ESR) spectroscopy, where characteristic adduct signals emerged exclusively in nanozyme-containing systems. This multi-modal validation establishes metal alloy nanozymes as potent oxygen-activating catalysts [36].

Within the oxidase family, glucose oxidase (GOX) holds particular biomedical relevance due to its ability to generate endogenous H2O2. Natural enzyme catalysis involves a histidine-mediated Brønsted base mechanism for glucose activation, followed by hydride transfer to flavin cofactors and subsequent oxygen reduction. Pioneering work revealed that uncapped gold nanoparticles functionally emulate this process under physiological conditions, catalyzing glucose oxidation to gluconic acid with concomitant H2O2 production [37]. Contemporary research suggests these nanozymes achieve catalytic mimicry through hydroxide-mediated activation pathways. Theoretical calculations support this mechanism, demonstrating the exceptional affinity of gold for alcohol C-H bond cleavage and preferential 2-electron oxygen reduction pathway to H2O2 (Fig. 4D,E) [38, 39]. This biomimetic H2O2 generation establishes self-sufficient antibacterial systems that bypass exogenous reactant requirements.

Collectively, cumulative evidence indicates OXD-like nanozyme activity stems from synergistic metal redox cycling, electron rearrangement dynamics, and engineered lattice defects. Despite significant advances, fundamental challenges remain unresolved. The intricate regulatory mechanisms governing catalytic kinetics under physiological conditions require deeper elucidation, while long-term activity preservation demands innovative stabilization strategies. Future development should prioritize heterostructured active site engineering guided by advanced computational models, targeted defect modulation, and integration with external energy fields to enhance both catalytic efficiency and operational stability.

Catalase-like activities

CAT enzymes, featuring iron-porphyrin prosthetic groups, serve as essential guardians of cellular redox homeostasis by decomposing H2O2 into H2O and O2 [40, 41]. This activity protects tissues from oxidative damage while ameliorating hypoxic stress. Inspired by this natural defense system, CAT-mimicking nanozymes have emerged as sophisticated therapeutic agents capable of regulating ROS in biological environments [42]. Understanding their atomic-scale catalytic mechanisms represents a critical foundation for rational nanozyme engineering.

Computational studies reveal distinct mechanistic routes for H2O2 decomposition, each governed by surface chemistry and environmental conditions. On Au (111) surfaces under neutral pH, homolytic base-like dissociation dominates, initiating through energetically favorable O–O bond cleavage to generate •OH. These radicals readily recombine into H2O and O2 with minimal energy barriers. Conversely, heterolytic acid-like pathways become thermodynamically preferred under alkaline conditions where pre-adsorbed hydroxide species facilitate proton transfer. This pH-dependent mechanistic switch highlights how nanozyme surfaces dynamically adapt to their microenvironment, a crucial feature for physiological functionality [43].

Beyond conventional dissociation pathways, (Co3O4)n nanozymes exhibit an energetically efficient bi-H2O2 associative mechanism. In this cooperative process, two co-adsorbed H2O2 molecules undergo concerted transformations. One molecule dissociates into hydroxyl and hydroperoxyl intermediates while the other provides protons for H2O formation. The remaining species then spontaneously recombine into O2, completing a highly exothermic catalytic cycle with exceptionally low activation barriers. Experimental validation confirms this pathway outperforms both acid-like and base-like dissociative routes in energy efficiency, suggesting new design principles for next-generation nanozymes (Fig. 5A,B) [44].

These studies establish that CAT-like activity arises from multiple interdependent pathways. The dominant mechanism depends critically on surface composition, adsorbed species, and local pH. While multi-metal synergy and optimized electron transfer have advanced catalytic performance, fundamental challenges persist. Inefficient active site accessibility and competitive pathway selection continue to limit decomposition efficiency and specificity. Overcoming these limitations requires atomic-level engineering of adsorption energetics and development of microenvironment-responsive catalytic centers.

Superoxide dismutase-like activities

SOD enzymes serve as essential cellular sentinels, catalytically dismutating •O2− into H2O2 and molecular O2 to preserve redox homeostasis [45]. Despite their biological importance, natural SOD faces translational limitations including immunogenicity risks, structural fragility, narrow operational windows, and prohibitive manufacturing costs. These constraints have accelerated the development of nanozymes as robust artificial SOD mimics capable of functioning in complex biological milieus [46, 47].

Early mechanistic models for cerium oxide nanozymes emphasized simplistic Ce3+/ Ce4+ redox cycling through oxygen vacancy mediation. This paradigm failed to capture the intricate surface coordination chemistry governing radical scavenging (Fig. 5C) [48]. Contemporary research reveals a more sophisticated catalytic cycle where proton-coupled electron transfer dominates. Superoxide radicals first undergo reversible protonation to form hydroperoxyl intermediates that adsorb onto cerium active sites. Subsequent recombination reactions yield H2O2 and O2 as terminal products. Crucially, density functional theory calculations demonstrate that catalytic efficiency hinges on surface electronic structure, with hydrogen-treated CeO2(111) surfaces exhibiting dramatically reduced energy barriers compared to pristine interfaces (0.78 eV versus 1.46 eV). This atomic-level understanding establishes surface engineering as a critical design principle for enhancing SOD-like performance (Fig. 5D-G) [49].

SOD-mimetic nanozymes confer potent cytoprotection by intercepting superoxide radical chains at their inception, converting these destructive species into less reactive H2O2 for downstream detoxification. This capability holds particular promise for mitigating infection-associated inflammation and tissue damage. Current antibacterial strategies increasingly leverage these nanozymes within cascade systems, where SOD-like activity synergizes with POD or CAT functions to amplify oxidative stress against pathogens while protecting host cells (Fig. 6). Despite significant advances, critical limitations hinder clinical translation. Limited selectivity for superoxide over other reactive species compromises therapeutic precision, while inadequate biofilm penetration restricts efficacy against persistent infections. Concerns regarding long-term biodistribution and metabolic fate further necessitate rigorous safety evaluation. Future breakthroughs will require multi-faceted approaches, including immune-responsive multi-enzyme assemblies, microenvironment-activatable nanozymes triggered by infection-specific pH or redox gradients, and targeted delivery systems employing biodegradable carriers functionalized with pathogen-specific ligands. Overcoming these barriers remains essential for realizing the full potential of SOD-mimetic nanozymes in resistance-evading antibacterial therapy.

Precision control of catalytic activity

Precise control over catalytic activity is fundamental to advancing metal nanozymes from promising biomimetics into reliable antibacterial agents. This activity, defined by their ability to accelerate redox reactions central to bactericidal ROS generation, is quantifiable via classical enzyme kinetics parameters like the Michaelis constant (Km), drawing a direct analogy to natural enzymes. However, a critical distinction lies in the engineerable origin of this function. Unlike the fixed active sites of biological enzymes, nanozyme catalysis emerges from tunable physicochemical attributes. Therefore, achieving precision control requires the orthogonal modulation of key material properties including size, morphology, surface chemistry, composition, ions or molecules and environmental responsiveness. The following sections analyze how each parameter uniquely directs not only catalytic kinetics but also specificity and therapeutic intelligence, thereby bridging their biomimetic promise to effective antibacterial performance.

Size

Particle size stands as a cornerstone parameter governing nanozyme catalytic efficiency, primarily by dictating the specific surface area and the accessibility of catalytically active sites. A robust inverse correlation between particle size and catalytic enhancement is consistently observed across diverse nanozyme systems [50]. This size-dependent activity paradigm is vividly exemplified by IONPs. Empirical studies reveal a distinct hierarchy in their POD-like activity for oxidizing 3,3’,5,5’-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB), with catalytic efficiency descending as follows: 30 nm > 150 nm > 300 nm. This trend starkly underscores the dominance of nanoscale surface effects where smaller particles expose a greater proportion of atoms capable of participating in catalysis [28].

The principle extends powerfully beyond simple oxides. For instance, OXD-mimicking single-atom Fe–N/C catalysts, synthesized from carbonized Fe-Zn zeolitic imidazolate framework (ZIF) precursors, exhibit a dramatic surge in catalytic turnover when particle size is reduced from the microscale (2.2 μm) down to the nanoscale regime (120 nm). This significant boost is directly attributed to the maximized exposure and accessibility of the atomically dispersed Fe-N4 active sites, which are the primary catalytic centers [51]. Crucially, the profound impact of size transcends catalytic generation of ROS to encompass their scavenging, a vital function for modulating inflammation and promoting tissue repair. Ruthenium nanoparticles (RuNPs) serve as a compelling case study for antioxidative nanozymes. Ultra-small RuNPs (sRuNPs, ≈2.0 nm), predominantly featuring surface-oxidized Ru atoms, demonstrate a remarkably superior capacity for scavenging diverse ROS compared to their medium-sized (≈3.9 nm) or larger-sized (≈5.9 nm) counterparts, where metallic surface atoms prevail (Fig. 7A) [52]. The enhanced mitigation of oxidative stress observed in the smallest particles highlights the pivotal interplay between size reduction, resultant surface chemistry alterations, and catalytic function at the nanoscale.

Fundamentally, the enhanced catalytic efficacy arising from reduced nanozyme dimensions operates through two synergistic mechanisms: (1) a geometric effect, where the increased surface-to-volume ratio exponentially amplifies the density of accessible active sites available for substrate interaction, and (2) an electronic effect, involving accelerated electron transfer kinetics due to shorter charge carrier diffusion pathways. Consequently, achieving precise size control emerges not merely as an option but as a paramount strategy for nanozyme performance optimization. Future research must prioritize the development and refinement of sophisticated bottom-up synthesis techniques—such as atomic layer deposition or microemulsion templating—capable of engineering monodisperse sub-10 nm catalysts with near-atomic-level precision. Success in this endeavor is essential for systematically narrowing the persistent activity gap between natural enzymes and their artificial nanozyme counterparts, a critical milestone for realizing their full therapeutic potential.

Morphology

The morphology of metal nanozymes, including shapes such as spherical, rod-like, or sheet-like structures, exerts a profound and often decisive influence on their biocatalytic activity, offering an additional, critical dimension for performance fine-tuning. This intricate structure–activity relationship is evident across diverse material systems and length scales, demonstrating how shape and exposed crystal facets dictate reaction pathways and efficiencies.

At the level of macro/microstructure, VO2 nanozymes illustrate morphology-dependent POD-like activity. Specifically, one-dimensional VO2 nanofibers outperform nanorods and nanosheets (NSs) in terms of sensitivity for H2O2 detection, suggesting advantages related to aspect ratio, surface defect density, or charge transport along the elongated axis [53]. Delving deeper into crystalline phase and atomic arrangement, the catalytic behavior of MnO2 polymorphs provides a striking example. β-MnO2 possesses intrinsically higher lattice oxygen mobility and adsorbed oxygen content compared to the α- and δ-phases. This unique characteristic directly translates to a significantly enhanced capacity for generating critical antibacterial radicals, namely •OH and 1O2, thereby underpinning its markedly superior antibacterial efficacy [54].

The most precise level of morphological control involves facet engineering at the atomic scale. Facet-controlled Pd nanocrystals vividly underscore the critical importance of tailoring surface atomic structure. Pd nanocubes predominantly exposing {100} facets exhibit substantially stronger OXD-like and POD-like activities than octahedra dominated by {111} facets. Computational insights reveal the origin of this disparity; calculations show a more favorable O2 adsorption energy on the {100} facets (-1.40 eV) compared to {111} facets (-0.85 eV), coupled with a lower energy barrier for O2 dissociation (0.31 eV vs. 0.67 eV). Furthermore, the homolytic cleavage of H2O2, a key step in POD-like activity, is calculated to be energetically more favorable on the Pd {100} surface than on the {111} surface (Fig. 7B) [55]. This demonstrates how atomic-level surface topography directly governs the energetics of catalytic steps.

Collectively, these diverse examples illuminate that multiscale morphological engineering, spanning from macroscopic architectures down to the precise arrangement of atoms on specific crystal facets, exerts profound control over reaction kinetics, selectivity, and ultimately, biocatalytic efficiency in metal nanozymes. Future advancements in this arena necessitate achieving precise hierarchical control across these interconnected length scales to unlock novel and tailored functionalities. This ambitious goal demands a synergistic integration of predictive computational modeling (e.g., density functional theory for facet-specific energetics and reaction pathways), advanced nanofabrication techniques capable of controlled anisotropic growth and facet-selective synthesis, and operando characterization methods to probe dynamic structural changes under working conditions. Ultimately, such integrated, multidisciplinary efforts will accelerate the rational design and realization of truly programmable nanozymes, effectively bridging fundamental laboratory discoveries in nanoscale catalysis to transformative practical applications in diagnostics, targeted therapeutics (including resistance-evading antibacterial therapy), and environmental remediation.

Surface modification

Surface modification represents a sophisticated paradigm for precisely tuning nanozyme catalysis through electronic structure, steric accessibility, and interfacial synergy. This multifaceted approach directly governs substrate binding affinity, electron transfer kinetics, and catalytic turnover. Key evidence demonstrates that surface ligands induce profound electronic perturbations in active sites. Unmodified AuNPs exhibit superior POD-like activity compared to amino- or citrate-coated counterparts. This attenuation stems from the insulating effect of surface coatings, which impede charge transfer between substrates and catalytic centers [56]. The catalytic efficacy of functional groups grafted onto Co3O4 follows a distinct hierarchy NH2− > SH− > COOH− > OH−, directly correlating with ligand electronegativity and their capacity to facilitate interfacial electron transfer [57].

Ligand size is also crucial to regulate substrate accessibility to active sites. Cysteine (Cys) modification of AuNRs enhances POD-like activity toward TMB by providing favorable local environments. Conversely, bulky glutathione (GSH) ligands impose significant steric hindrance, increasing the substrate-active site distance and markedly diminishing catalytic efficiency (Fig. 7C,D) [58]. This underscores the delicate balance between surface functionalization for biocompatibility and preserving catalytic accessibility. Breakthroughs emerge from rational hybrid design. Pd NPs anchored on ultrathin two-dimensional Cu(II)-porphyrin-derived MOF exemplify interfacial engineering. This configuration achieves an exceptionally low Michaelis–Menten constant (Km = 2.33 mM) for H2O2 decomposition, far surpassing individual components. The synergy arises from enhanced electron transfer at the Pd/MOF interface and optimized substrate adsorption/activation [59].

Beyond catalytic activity, surface modification also plays a pivotal role as a strategy for enhancing the biocompatibility and targeting capabilities of metal nanozymes. Techniques such as polymer coating and the introduction of functional groups can significantly improve the interaction of nanozymes with biological environments. For instance, the addition of hydrophilic polymers can enhance the stability and solubility of nanozymes in biological fluids, while specific functional groups can facilitate targeted interactions with cellular receptors. The path forward for effective surface engineering demands the concerted optimization of three critical dimensions: electronic coupling at the active interface, nanoscale spatial confinement governing substrate accessibility, and biocompatibility within complex biological environments. To achieve this, pioneering research must prioritize several key avenues. Firstly, the development of intelligent dynamic surface systems is essential; these systems would incorporate stimuli-responsive, enzyme-mimicking ligands capable of enabling spatiotemporal control over catalytic activity, adapting to specific microenvironments or therapeutic windows. Furthermore, leveraging the predictive power of machine learning offers immense potential to rapidly screen vast ligand libraries and establish robust quantitative surface-activity relationships, significantly accelerating the design cycle. Concurrently, employing advanced in situ spectroscopic techniques under physiologically relevant operando conditions is crucial to elucidate the dynamic interfacial processes and reaction mechanisms in real time during catalysis. Ultimately, bridging the persistent gap between synthetic precision achieved in the laboratory and demonstrable in vivo biological efficacy remains the paramount challenge. Successfully overcoming this hurdle is fundamental to translating the promise of surface-engineered nanozymes into clinically viable, robust antimicrobial therapeutics capable of evading resistance mechanisms.

Composition

Strategic manipulation of metal nanozyme composition represents a cornerstone in tailoring their catalytic functionality. By incorporating heteroatom dopants or precisely adjusting elemental stoichiometries, researchers can fundamentally reconfigure electronic structures and optimize active site exposure. This section examines how such compositional engineering overcomes intrinsic limitations of monometallic systems.

The introduction of dopant atoms induces lattice distortions that create catalytically favorable environments. Such synergistic effects of different metal elements can lead to improved catalytic performance, as seen in bimetallic nanozymes such as Fe-Cu combinations, which exhibit superior catalytic properties compared to their monometallic counterparts. When ruthenium atoms are integrated into CeO2 nanozymes, the resulting Ru/CeO2 system exhibits a 3.7-fold enhancement in POD-like activity over pristine CeO2. Advanced spectroscopic analyses confirm this improvement originates from Ru-induced oxygen vacancies that increase the density of accessible Ce3+ active sites [60]. Parallel phenomena are observed in Fe-doped MoS2 NSs, where Fe incorporation reduces charge transfer resistance and facilitates the generation of •OH during POD-mimetic reactions [61].

Beyond single-element doping, hybrid architectures leverage interfacial synergism for performance amplification. A compelling example is Pt@carbon dot (CD) nanozymes, where Pt+ species anchored to oxygen-functionalized CD surfaces through hydroxyl/carbonyl coordination exhibit modulated charge distribution. This configuration achieves a Km of 61.1 μM for TMB oxidation, merely one-seventh of natural HRP, while simultaneously elevating the maximum reaction rate (Vmax) sevenfold [62]. This dramatic improvement underscores the profound impact of dopant selection on the electronic properties of active sites and substrate affinity. Such dramatic parameter inversion underscores how dopant selection governs both electronic band structures and substrate binding thermodynamics.

Crucially, catalytic outcomes depend not only on dopant identity but also on precise elemental proportions. In AuPt alloy nanozymes, GOX-like activity peaks at an optimal Au:Pt molar ratio (0.75:0.25), yielding 1.4-fold higher activity than pure AuNPs. Density functional theory calculations reveal that the work function differential between Au (5.54 eV) and Pt (6.13 eV) drives spontaneous electron transfer from Au to Pt domains. This electron redistribution synergistically enhances glucose oxidation at Au sites while accelerating H2O2 reduction at Pt centers [63]. The universality of stoichiometric optimization extends to metal–organic frameworks. Bimetallic Fe3Ni-MOFs demonstrate fivefold greater POD-like activity than monometallic Fe-MOFs, attributable to Ni-mediated acceleration of the Fe3+/Fe2+ redox cycle. Transient absorption spectroscopy confirms this process increases •OH generation kinetics by facilitating interfacial electron shuttling [64].

Compositional engineering serves as a cornerstone for tailoring the electronic structure and catalytic functionality of metal nanozymes. By adjusting the ratios and types of metals used in the synthesis of nanozymes, researchers can fine-tune their electronic structures and optimize their catalytic efficiency. This compositional optimization not only enhances the catalytic activity but also improves the stability of nanozymes under various conditions, making them more suitable for practical applications in biomedical fields. Future advancements demand a deeper mechanistic understanding of quantitative composition-activity relationships through integrated computational modeling and experimental validation. Critical priorities encompass achieving atomically precise control over dopant spatial distribution and alloy homogeneity via advanced synthetic methodologies, while simultaneously elucidating the complex interplay between composition-induced lattice strain, defect density dynamics, and interfacial charge redistribution using in situ and operando characterization techniques. Ultimately, these concerted efforts must converge toward the rational design of multi-component nanozyme systems exhibiting programmable catalytic properties specifically engineered for demanding resistance-evading antibacterial therapeutics.

Ions or molecules

The catalytic proficiency of metal nanozymes is dynamically sculpted by their chemical milieu, where ions and molecules serve as allosteric modulators. Understanding these extrinsic regulatory mechanisms is paramount for deploying nanozymes in complex biological environments, particularly for antibacterial applications where localized ion fluxes and metabolite concentrations vary dramatically.

Cations can reconfigure nanozyme surfaces through amalgamation or coordination chemistry. AuNPs, for instance, display negligible CAT-like activity in isolation but undergo dramatic functional transformation upon Hg2+ exposure. Counterion-specific effects emerge strikingly: Hg(NO3)2 treatment forms Au@Hg amalgams that enhance both POD and CAT activities, while HgCl2 primarily boosts CAT functionality through surface-adsorbed Hg+ species [65]. Similarly, iron phosphate (FeP)-coated AuNPs exhibit up to 20-fold activity amplification via interfacial electron relay at Au/FeP junctions. Critically, activity diminishes when FeP shell thickness exceeds 2.3 nm or when thiol ligands passivate Au surfaces, confirming the hybrid interface as the catalytic epicenter (Fig. 8A) [66].

Beyond metallic ions, endogenous metabolites exhibit sophisticated regulatory roles. Adenosine triphosphate (ATP) enhances the POD-like activity of polyethylene glycol (PEG) and citrate-capped AuNPs through an unconventional mechanism. Unlike classic radical-mediated pathways, isothermal titration calorimetry reveals ATP stabilizes the diimine charge-transfer complex of oxidized TMB. This product-stabilization effect, which is observed identically in natural HRP systems, lowers the reaction activation barrier without altering primary catalysis kinetics [67]. This finding not only clarifies the unique role of ATP in nanozyme-catalyzed POD-like reactions but also provides novel molecular-level insights into how biological small molecules can fine-tune nanozyme activity.

In summary, microenvironmental ions and biomolecules function as precision regulators that dynamically modulate metal nanozyme catalysis through three cardinal mechanisms: interfacial electron transfer, geometric reconstruction of active sites, and stabilization of reactive intermediates. Comprehensive understanding of these context-dependent regulatory principles is fundamental for predicting nanozyme behavior in pathophysiological microenvironments, particularly within infected tissues exhibiting dynamic ion gradients and metabolite fluctuations, and for engineering adaptive nanozyme systems capable of real-time antibacterial responses. Future research must prioritize elucidating synergistic effects among microenvironmental factors, deciphering spatiotemporal dynamics of modulator-nanozyme interactions using in situ characterization techniques, and developing stimuli-responsive nanozymes with topological activity control. Collectively, these advances will establish next-generation resistance-evading antimicrobial agents that dynamically counteract bacterial defense mechanisms, ultimately conquering AMR.

Endogenous stimulation

The catalytic activity of metal nanozymes demonstrates significant potential for specific modulation by endogenous biological microenvironmental cues [68, 69]. Leveraging endogenous stimuli such as pH, temperature, and H2O2 concentration at infection sites can activate or enhance the activity of metal nanozymes. Among these intrinsic stimuli, pH-dependent responses are particularly noteworthy due to their high adaptability to the dynamic conditions characteristic of pathogen infection sites. The naturally acidic microenvironment (pH 4.0–6.5) prevalent at these sites serves as a potent endogenous activation signal for select metal nanozymes. For example, the Zn-CuO nanozyme exemplifies this responsiveness, exhibiting peak POD-like activity at pH 5.0 due to optimized H2O2 dissociation kinetics under acidic conditions, while alkaline environments suppress its catalytic proficiency [70]. More sophisticated behavior emerges in Co-Ru bimetallic nanozymes (APCR), which employ a biomimetic peroxisome mechanism for microenvironment-adaptive catalysis. Within acidic infection niches, APCR orchestrates specific H2O2 adsorption at Co sites and ROS desorption at Ru sites, generating bactericidal •OH radicals that disrupt phospholipid bilayers and inhibit ATP synthase. Crucially, as tissue repair progresses toward neutral pH, APCR transitions to scavenge excess ROS and suppress pro-inflammatory cytokines, actively promoting vascular endothelial regeneration. This pH-gated dual-functionality enables a sequential therapeutic strategy: initial pathogen eradication followed by regenerative support (Fig. 8B,C) [71]. Complementing this approach, ZnO-CuS heterostructures achieve intelligent activity switching between POD-like and CAT-like functions. Acidic microenvironments trigger •OH-mediated bacterial membrane destruction, while neutral conditions activate ROS scavenging to mitigate oxidative damage during healing phases. Such self-regulating systems intrinsically balance antibacterial potency with host tissue protection (Fig. 8D-F) [72].

The ability to utilize endogenous stimuli for the activation of nanozymes allows for a more targeted and effective therapeutic response, as the nanozymes can remain inactive in healthy tissues but become activated in pathological conditions, thereby minimizing potential side effects. Recent studies demonstrate the unparalleled capacity of metal nanozymes to intelligently toggle between bactericidal and regenerative modes in direct response to pathological pH gradients. However, current research remains disproportionately focused on pH as a singular regulatory input, overlooking other disease-relevant endogenous stimuli. These include glutathione overexpression in resistant biofilms, lactate accumulation in hypoxic infections, and enzyme–substrate interactions within microbial communities. Future innovation must transcend this pH-centric paradigm through developing multimodal nanozymes capable of orthogonally responding to combinatorial signals such as redox gradients, metabolite concentrations, and resistance biomarkers. Such advanced systems will be indispensable for addressing pathogen heterogeneity and dynamic host–pathogen interactions within complex infectious microenvironments.

Exogenous stimulation

While endogenous stimuli (e.g., pH, GSH) exploit microenvironmental cues for nanozyme activation, their efficacy is often constrained by intrinsic physiological heterogeneity and dynamic signal fluctuations at lesion sites, limiting spatiotemporal precision. In contrast, exogenous stimuli, including light, ultrasound, magnetic fields, and thermal energy, offer a promising alternative for precise spatial and temporal manipulation of nanozyme catalysis. These modalities provide tunable therapeutic intensity and minimized off-target effects for targeted antibacterial applications [73].

Optical stimulation, in particular, represents a particularly versatile platform. By flexibly tuning parameters such as wavelength, intensity, and irradiation duration, it enables spatiotemporal control over catalytic reactions and facilitates synergistic integration of photothermal and photodynamic effects to amplify performance. For instance, Cu2MoS4 nanosheets harness NIR-II irradiation to generate localized hyperthermia, synergistically amplifying their OXD- and POD-mimetic activities for enhanced ROS production [74]. Beyond thermal effects, photoresponsive molecular reconfiguration enables dynamic control. Azobenzene-conjugated AuNP nanozymes undergo reversible cis–trans isomerization under alternating UV–vis light, precisely modulating catalytic site geometry to overcome traditional irreversibility constraints [75]. This photo-switchable mechanism overcomes the irreversibility limitations of conventional nanozymes and provides a blueprint for intelligently responsive catalytic systems.

Complementary physical fields provide additional dimensions for regulation. US-responsive BiPt nanozymes coated with platelet-bacterial hybrid membranes exhibit 300-fold catalytic enhancement under acoustic cavitation, achieving broad-spectrum efficacy against methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE) pathogens in diverse infection models, including osteomyelitis, myositis, and pneumonia (Fig. 9A-H) [76]. For magnetic regulation, alternating magnetic fields (AMF) enable real-time activity modulation through magnetothermal or magnetomechanical effects. Specifically, field intensity variations directionally alter nanomaterial surface electron distribution, thereby tuning substrate binding affinity and catalytic kinetics. Thermal stimulation, either independently or coupled with photonic/magnetic inputs, further enables dynamic control via temperature-responsive materials or energy conversion mechanisms [77].

Despite the compelling spatiotemporal precision and synergistic potency of exogenously activated nanozymes, their clinical translation faces persistent challenges. These encompass comprehensive biosafety evaluation of long-term energy exposure, mechanistic understanding of multi-physical field synergies, and crucially, developing intelligent systems integrating three key capabilities: pathogen-targeted recognition, multi-field coordination for deep-tissue activation, and bionic delivery strategies for precise infection localization. Success in these areas will establish a new generation of resistance-evading antimicrobials capable of dynamically countering evolving bacterial defenses.

Antibacterial therapy of metal-based nanozymes

Monometal nanozymes

Single-metal nanozymes have emerged as a highly promising class of antibacterial agents, particularly for the complex challenge of chronic wound healing. Their appeal lies in capitalizing on robust enzyme-mimetic activities to exert potent antibacterial effects, offering a compelling alternative to conventional antibiotics [78, 79]. Critically, the most advanced designs transcend simple catalysis by incorporating sophisticated responsiveness to the dynamic wound microenvironment, enabling intelligent, context-dependent therapeutic actions. A prime example of this intelligence is found in Cu nanodots (NDs). These systems operate through a pH-gated dual-mode switching mechanism. Under the neutral pH conditions characteristic of healthy tissue, they primarily function as reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS) scavengers, protecting host cells from collateral damage. However, upon encountering the slightly acidic milieu typical of bacterial infection sites, they undergo a functional shift, catalyzing the conversion of endogenous H2O2 into highly cytotoxic ROS for targeted bacterial eradication [80].

Beyond pH sensitivity, the integration of multi-enzyme cascade reactions within a single nanostructure represents a powerful strategy to amplify therapeutic efficacy and functionality. Ruthenium-proanthocyanidin (Ru-PC) NPs exemplify this principle through their quadruple enzyme-mimetic activities. While ruthenium metalloenzymes have previously demonstrated POD activity and glutathionase properties, their standalone efficacy remains limited. The innovative coordination with natural polyphenol procyanidins (PCs) enables unprecedented therapeutic versatility. In infected wound microenvironments characterized by mild acidity, Ru-PC NPs exhibit enhanced POD-like activity while simultaneously depleting bacterial GSH, thereby disrupting redox homeostasis and amplifying oxidative damage. RNA sequencing analysis provides molecular-level insights into this antibacterial mechanism, revealing that Ru-PC NPs effectively interfere with multiple essential metabolic pathways in MRSA, including glycolysis, tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA), pyruvate metabolism, and cysteine synthesis. As the wound environment transitions toward neutral pH during healing, the nanoparticles dynamically switch to CAT-dominated activity, generating oxygen to alleviate hypoxia while leveraging the synergistic antioxidant effects of SOD, GSH-Px, and procyanidins to clear residual ROS and RNS. This sophisticated functional integration enables Ru-PC NPs to concurrently address bacterial elimination through metabolic disruption, oxygen supply, free radical scavenging, and macrophage polarization from M1 to M2 phenotype. The resulting microenvironment optimally supports cell proliferation, angiogenesis, and tissue regeneration, demonstrating the remarkable potential of monometal nanozymes for achieving complete wound management through microenvironment-responsive catalytic switching (Fig. 10A-F) [81].

Optimizing the catalytic performance of nanozymes necessitates overcoming microenvironmental barriers inherent to bacterial infections. Hypoxia, induced by high bacterial metabolic oxygen consumption and exacerbated by biofilm formation, severely limits the activity of oxygen-dependent nanozymes, such as those mimicking GOX. Innovative solutions involve combining nanozymes with oxygen-carrying moieties. For instance, integrating perfluorooctane bromide (PFOB), a potent O2 reservoir, with Pt NPs effectively restores local oxygen tension. This oxygenation boosts the GOX-like activity of Pt, accelerating glucose conversion to gluconic acid and consequently acidifying the local environment. The resulting acidic pH then acts as a trigger, sequentially activating the NADH oxidase-like, POD-like, and OXD-like activities of the Pt NPs. This cascade generates a potent synergistic burst of ROS for enhanced antibacterial action. Crucially, following pathogen clearance, the inherent CAT-like and SOD-like activities of the Pt NPs come into play, scavenging residual ROS. This restoration of redox homeostasis facilitates the critical transition of the wound from the inflammatory phase to the proliferative healing phase. This sophisticated approach embodies a multi-level regulatory strategy combining oxygenation to break the hypoxia barrier, pH-initiated cascade catalysis for potent antibacterial action, and redox-balanced activity switching to support tissue repair. It achieves spatiotemporal synergy, effectively coordinating pathogen elimination with the orchestration of a regenerative microenvironment (Fig. 10G) [82].

In summary, monometal nanozymes represent a significant advancement, demonstrating the capacity for intelligent, environment-responsive switching between potent antibacterial modes and pro-repair functions. This dynamic behavior is masterfully mediated through mechanisms such as pH gating, integrated enzyme cascades, and strategic microenvironmental modulation. However, the intrinsic catalytic repertoire of systems based on a single metal center remains fundamentally constrained by the chemistry of that single active site. This limitation becomes particularly apparent when confronting the multifaceted clinical realities of complex infection microenvironments, which present simultaneous challenges including severe hypoxia, resilient biofilm barriers, dysregulated persistent inflammation, and the ever-present threat of bacterial resistance evolution. Successfully translating these promising agents into clinical practice will therefore demand significant advancements in achieving greater catalytic selectivity for bacterial targets over host cells and ensuring enhanced long-term biological stability under complex physiological conditions. Addressing these challenges paves the way for exploring more complex architectures, such as multi-metallic or composite nanozymes, potentially offering broader catalytic capabilities and enhanced functionality to meet the intricate demands of advanced antibacterial therapy.

Metal alloy nanozymes

Building upon the foundation of monometal systems, metal alloy nanozymes represent a sophisticated evolutionary leap. They harness the unique synergy arising from the electronic interactions and structural refinements inherent in combining multiple metal components. This deliberate multi-metal engineering effectively addresses key limitations often encountered in single-metal nanozymes, particularly concerning catalytic breadth, activity intensity, and adaptability to dynamic infection microenvironments. The resultant materials offer not only superior biocatalytic performance but also enhanced cost efficiency, presenting a compelling platform for advanced antibacterial strategies [83]. The power of this synergistic approach is vividly illustrated by CoSe2. Here, the intrinsic electronic interplay between Co and Se atoms activates a remarkable triple enzyme-mimetic profile, encompassing POD, CAT, and OXD activities. This integrated catalytic system operates through a self-sustaining cascade cycle. OXD-like activity initiates the process by converting O2 into •O2−. POD-like activity then harnesses H2O2 to generate highly cytotoxic •OH. Crucially, CAT-like activity concurrently decomposes residual H2O2 to regenerate O2, effectively closing the loop and establishing a continuous “ROS burst and O2 regeneration” cycle. This elegant self-amplifying mechanism enables CoSe2 nanozymes to achieve exceptional efficacy, eradicating 99.9% of drug-resistant Escherichia coli (E. coli) at remarkably low concentrations, demonstrating the potency unlocked by bimetallic synergy [84].

Structural design further augments the catalytic prowess of alloy nanozymes. Two-dimensional metal nanostructures, characterized by their exceptionally high specific surface areas and maximally exposed active sites, provide a significant advantage. Pd@Ir bimetallic nanosheets exemplify this structural benefit. Synthesized via precise seed-mediated growth, these nanosheets allow fine-tuning of the Ir/Pd atomic ratio and exhibit excellent biocompatibility. They manifest intrinsic multienzyme activities, including potent OXD-, POD-, and CAT-like functions, significantly outperforming their monometallic Pd NSs and Ir NPs counterparts in key oxidase and peroxidase metrics. DFT calculations provide mechanistic insight, revealing a lower O2 adsorption energy on the Pd@Ir surface compared to pure Pd. This favorable energetics facilitates the crucial conversion of ground-state triplet oxygen to highly reactive singlet oxygen. In models of infected wounds, Pd@Ir NSs exert potent antibacterial effects by inducing overwhelming bacterial oxidative stress through the synergistic generation of 1O2 and •OH, effectively disrupting cellular integrity (Fig. 11A,B) [85].

Pushing the boundaries of nanozyme design beyond conventional alloying, cutting-edge strategies now achieve atomic-level precision in active site engineering. One pioneering approach leverages the programmable self-assembly driven by nucleic acid-metal ion coordination. Utilizing single-stranded nucleic acids as versatile templates, researchers have successfully fabricated tunable nucleic acid (NA)-Ag@Pt nanozymes. Further functionalization with specialized functional nucleic acids (FNAs) yields advanced FNA-Ag@Pt constructs exhibiting significantly enhanced POD-like activity. Critically, both novel nanozyme architectures retain robust POD-mimicking capabilities and demonstrate substantial antibacterial efficacy (Fig. 11C-E) [86]. This methodology establishes a powerful new paradigm for the rational design of customized nanozymes, enabling unparalleled control over atomic composition and seamless integration with biologically functional molecules, opening avenues for highly targeted therapeutic action.

Collectively, metal alloy nanozymes demonstrate a transformative capacity to overcome the inherent constraints of their monometal predecessors. Through the strategic exploitation of multi-component synergy and deliberate structural engineering, they achieve significantly enhanced antibacterial efficacy and superior adaptability to the complex biochemical landscapes of infections. Their cooperative network of multiple active sites effectively mitigates challenges related to catalytic selectivity and oxygen dependency plaguing single-metal systems, enabling efficient combat against formidable drug-resistant biofilms. Looking forward, the evolution of metal alloy nanozymes is poised to focus on several key frontiers. Exploring the vast, largely untapped catalytic diversity offered by high-entropy alloys holds immense promise for discovering novel enzymatic activities. Developing adaptive bio-interfaces that can dynamically sense and respond to specific microenvironmental cues will be crucial for achieving truly context-responsive activity modulation. Furthermore, integrating nanozymes within multimodal therapeutic strategies offers a powerful solution to address persistent catalytic bottlenecks encountered in complex, heterogeneous infections. These converging research directions are set to propel nanozyme technology towards the realization of intelligent, multi-mechanism therapeutic platforms capable of orchestrating precise and effective antimicrobial interventions against even the most challenging resistant pathogens.

Metal oxide nanozymes

Metal oxide nanozymes have emerged as a pivotal class of antimicrobial agents, distinguished by their unique suite of properties encompassing tunable redox chemistry, diverse photoelectric behaviors, and potent intrinsic antibacterial capabilities [87]. This multifaceted nature fuels their intensive investigation as promising alternatives to conventional antibiotics. Representative oxides such as Fe3O4, MnO2, and V2O5 are at the forefront of this exploration, demonstrating significant potential in combating resistant pathogens [88, 89]. For instance, innovative work by Liu and colleagues yielded oleic acid-modified MnO2 (OA-MnO2) nanomaterials synthesized via a one-pot oleic acid nanoemulsion template approach. These OA-MnO2 constructs retain potent OXD-like activity while exhibiting excellent biocompatibility and minimal cytotoxicity. Mechanistic studies confirmed their antibacterial action stems from the generation of ROS imposing toxicity and the subsequent disruption of bacterial membrane integrity, highlighting the fundamental role of redox activity in their efficacy [90].

Moving beyond inherent catalytic functions, biomimetic structural engineering presents a transformative strategy to amplify antibacterial performance. Drawing inspiration from the natural nanocone arrays found on insect wings, researchers designed NiCo2O4 nanozymes featuring self-adaptive hierarchical nanostructures, including nanofibers, nanosheets, and nanoflowers. This bio-inspired design achieves remarkable broad-spectrum activity through synergistic mechano-catalytic coupling. The rigid nanopetal components effectively penetrate bacteria possessing thin peptidoglycan layers. Conversely, the nanozymes dynamically reconfigure into flexible nanofibers to ensnare bacteria characterized by thick, rigid cell walls. This morphology-adaptive targeting capability ensures precise interaction with diverse pathogens. Simultaneously, the intrinsic POD-like activity catalyzes the conversion of H2O2 into highly cytotoxic •OH (Fig. 12A-D) [91]. The potent combination of physical membrane penetration and localized ROS-mediated chemical assault results in an exceptional inhibition rate exceeding 98% against MDR bacteria. This groundbreaking work establishes a novel paradigm for intelligent antibacterial materials rooted in bionic principles.

Cu-based oxide nanozymes further exemplify the versatility of this class, leveraging enzyme-mimicking activities central to antibacterial action. Research consistently demonstrates their GSH-Px-like activity efficiently depletes the critical bacterial antioxidant GSH. Moreover, Cu-derived nanozymes exhibiting inherent OXD and POD activities generate potent oxidizing ROS, synergistically enhancing bactericidal efficacy and membrane damage [92–94]. Building on this foundation, CuCo2O4 nanoflowers with multi-enzyme activities were synthesized via a hydrothermal method, which exhibits multiple enzymatic activities and induces cuproptosis-like bacterial death for treating infected wounds. The unique architecture provides abundant active sites, enabling synergistic POD-like and OXD-like activities for substantial ROS generation, coupled with potent GSH-Px-like activity for glutathione depletion. Molecular-level investigations substantiate the antibacterial mechanism through integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses. Transcriptomics reveals that CuCo2O4 treatment significantly alters the expression of 502 proteins in MRSA, with Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment showing profound disruption in core metabolic pathways including the TCA cycle, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, and oxidative phosphorylation. Concurrent Gene Ontology (GO) analysis demonstrates interference with ribosome function and metabolic processes. Metabolomic profiling further confirms suppression of TCA cycle and oxidative phosphorylation pathways, evidenced by downregulation of key genes (citrate synthase, succinate dehydrogenase) and reduced respiratory chain complex activities. This comprehensive attack on bacterial energy metabolism and protein synthesis, combined with induced lipid peroxidation, effectively mirrors a cuproptosis-like death mechanism in prokaryotic cells. Notably, CuCo2O4 also promotes wound healing by enhancing granulation tissue formation, collagen deposition, angiogenesis, and cell proliferation. These findings establish CuCo2O4 as a promising multi-enzyme mimetic nanozyme for treating wound infections through cuproptosis-like bacterial death coupled with pro-healing effects (Fig. 12E-G) [95].

Metal oxide nanozymes present key advantages for combating resistant pathogens and accelerating wound repair, leveraging tailorable redox properties for precise catalytic control and multifaceted mechanisms including ROS generation, membrane disruption, and antioxidant depletion. Unlocking their full potential requires prioritizing critical advancements: achieving atomic-level precision in catalytic site engineering to boost selectivity and minimize off-target effects; developing intelligent designs responsive to dynamic infection cues for context-appropriate action; and integrating them into multimodal platforms (e.g., combining catalysis with photothermal, photodynamic, or delivery strategies) to overcome bottlenecks in complex infections. Successfully balancing high catalytic potency with physiological biocompatibility remains central to evolving these nanozymes into sophisticated, intelligent systems for precise resistance-evading therapy.

Metal sulfides nanozymes

Building upon the foundation of metal oxide nanozymes for antibacterial mechanism modulation, research focus has broadened to include metal sulfide-based nanozyme systems. Materials such as MoS2, CuS, FeS2 and Ag2S demonstrate compelling advantages for managing complex infections, leveraging their unique electronic structures and dynamic catalytic behaviors. These systems excel in adapting to pathological microenvironments while integrating multifunctional therapeutic actions. A paradigm of this adaptability is pH-responsive Cu2MoS4 (H-CMS) NSs. These engineered structures exhibit pH-gated enzymatic duality, activating OXD- and POD-like functions within acidic biofilm niches to generate localized ROS. Under ultrasound irradiation, this catalytic activity triggers an explosive ROS surge that physically disrupts biofilm matrices. Simultaneously, the nanosheets orchestrate immunomodulation by polarizing macrophages toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype and upregulating cytokine signaling, synergistically eradicating entrenched biofilms. Remarkably, upon biofilm clearance and microenvironment normalization to neutral pH, the intrinsic CAT-like activity suppresses M1 polarization and resolves inflammation, demonstrating intelligent self-regulating functionality (Fig. 13A-C) [96].

Parallel efforts highlight Cu/Co sulfide nanoparticles (CCS NPs) as potent antibacterial agents. These positively charged constructs operate through a dual mechanism, enhancing POD-like activity under acidic conditions while electrostatically targeting negatively charged bacterial membranes. Metal sulfide nanozymes outperform metal oxide nanozymes in dynamic microenvironment adaptation via pH-responsive enzymatic switching and integrated ROS generation, GSH depletion, and immunomodulation. While surpassing noble-metal alloys in cost and functionalization efficiency, their clinical translation necessitates resolving physiological catalytic stability and long-term biosafety. Crucially, their multivalent active sites enable unparalleled functional integration of antibacterial, immunoregulatory, and tissue-repair actions. Future development must prioritize sulfide crystallinity stabilization, defect-engineered active sites, and biocompatible matrix integration to realize these intelligent multifunctional platforms for next-generation antimicrobial therapy.

Single atom nanozymes

Single-atom nanozymes represent a cutting-edge advancement in the field of nanozymes, maximizing atomic utilization and enhancing catalytic efficiency. These nanozymes feature isolated active sites, which significantly improve their enzyme-like activities compared to traditional nanozymes. In the context of treating antibiotic-resistant infections, single-atom nanozymes have exhibited remarkable bactericidal properties with minimal toxicity to host cells. Their unparalleled atomic utilization efficiency, homogeneous active site distribution, and electronically tunable structures collectively unlock precise control over catalytic processes, enabling them to potently mimic various natural enzymes, including superoxide dismutase and catalase [99, 100]. These attributes position SAzymes as transformative agents capable of overcoming intrinsic limitations of conventional antibiotics and clustered nanozymes. Early breakthroughs established the catalytic foundation of SAzymes, demonstrating that Mn-N–C and Cu–N-C systems synergistically integrate POD-like and OXD-like activities [101–103]. This dual enzymatic function drives robust generation of cytotoxic ROS (•OH, •O2−), inflicting oxidative damage on bacterial membranes and inducing rapid cell death. Capitalizing on this mechanism, Fe-SAzymes were engineered through precise nitrogen-carbon coordination. This atomic-level electronic modulation endows Fe-SAzymes with exceptional POD/OXD kinetics, enabling potent disruption of drug-resistant biofilms and achieving > 99.9% eradication rates against multidrug-resistant E. coli and S. aureus (Fig. 14A) [104].

Despite their promise as chemodynamic therapy (CDT) agents, SAzymes can suffer from kinetic constraints in Fenton-like reactions due to suboptimal intermediate adsorption. To circumvent this, heteroatom doping and surface functionalization strategies have emerged. For example, Co-based single-atom catalysts (SACNZs) co-doped with sulfur/phosphorus and surface-polymerized with polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) exhibit enhanced POD-like activity. DFT analyses reveal that asymmetric coordination of electronegative heteroatoms to Co centers optimizes electron transfer, strengthening oxygen-binding capacity and reducing reaction energy barriers—collectively boosting CDT efficacy in S. aureus-infected wounds (Fig. 14B) [105]. Recent innovations leverage SAzymes for synergistic ROS/RNS nanocatalytic therapy. A pioneering example is a bioresponsive L-arginine-loaded Cu SAzyme platform (L-Arg@Cu-SAzymes). Triggered by trace H2O2 in infected microenvironments, this system exploits near-100% atomic utilization of Cu sites to concurrently activate dual CAT-like and OXD-like activities. This catalyzes •O2− generation while simultaneously releasing nitric oxide (NO), which reacts with •O2− to form highly cytotoxic peroxynitrite (ONOO⁻). Crucially, the platform maintains pro-angiogenic NO homeostasis, enabling dynamic regulation of ROS/RNS flux. Simultaneously, it promotes cell proliferation, collagen deposition, and angiogenesis via O2 release, and remodels the infection microenvironment by polarizing macrophages towards the pro-repair M2 phenotype (Fig. 14C-E) [106]. This multi-effect synergistic mechanism provides a novel strategy for on-demand continuous antibacterial treatment and orchestrated regenerative therapy of resistant bacterial infections.

SAzymes redefine antibacterial precision through atomically engineered active sites and multi-enzyme cooperativity, enabling simultaneous pathogen eradication and tissue regeneration. Future advances must prioritize coordination sphere control to fine-tune substrate specificity and reaction kinetics, alongside developing stimuli-responsive systems that autonomously adapt to dynamic infection cues. Comprehensive understanding requires multi-omics interrogation of nanozyme-immune system crosstalk, coupled with rigorous biosafety validation across preclinical models. Cross-scale integration of atomic-scale design with biological functionality will propel SAzymes toward intelligent theranostic platforms that unify antibacterial, antibiofilm, and regenerative actions, ultimately realizing precision therapy for the post-antibiotic era.

Others

Beyond conventional metal-based, oxide, sulfide, and single-atom nanozymes, MOFs and dual-atom nanozymes (DAzymes) represent cutting-edge frontiers in catalytic antibacterial therapy. These systems leverage unique structural and electronic configurations to overcome persistent limitations in infection control. MOFs have emerged as a prominent class of porous crystalline compounds, distinguished by their exceptional structural tunability, high specific surface area, precise porosity control, and robust chemical stability. These properties enable the rational design of microenvironment-responsive antimicrobial agents [107, 108]. A quintessential example is Ni-MOF, which exhibits pH-gated enzymatic duality. Under weakly acidic biofilm conditions, it activates POD-like activity to convert H2O2 into bactericidal •OH. In neutral environments, it switches to CAT-like function to scavenge excess ROS and mitigate inflammatory damage. This dynamic behavior is augmented by intrinsic electrical conductivity and biosafety, collectively accelerating infected wound resolution (Fig. 15A,B) [109]. To address translational challenges, including suboptimal catalytic efficiency, poor targeting specificity, and biocompatibility concerns, advanced biomimetic MOF designs have emerged. Platelet membrane-coated Fe/Zn bimetallic MOFs (PM@MIL-88B-Fe/Zn) exemplify this innovation. Zn doping optimizes Fe(III)/Fe(II) redox cycling kinetics, significantly boosting POD-mimetic activity. The biomimetic coating confers precise targeting against pathogens while suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α), upregulating angiogenic marker CD31, and enabling metabolic clearance (Fig. 15D-F) [110]. This integrated approach resolves key clinical translation barriers spanning catalytic potency, specificity, and biosafety.

While SAzymes offer atomic precision, their efficacy is constrained by high intermediate energy barriers and limited active species diversity inherent to single-metal sites [111]. DAzymes transcend these limitations through heterobimetallic active centers that create collaborative catalytic interfaces. In Fe/Mn-SNC DAzymes, dual-site electron coupling amplifies multienzyme activities, including OXD, POD, SOD, and glutathione oxidase (GSH-Ox) functions. This cascade simultaneously triggers a potent ROS burst and depletes bacterial GSH reserves, achieving synergistic sterilization. Critically, the diatomic strategy reduces reaction energy barriers while expanding dynamic regulatory dimensions for precision therapy [112].

Composite nanozyme systems further transcend the limitations of single-component designs. Studies consistently demonstrate that multi-metal composite nanozymes achieve superior catalytic activity, enabling rapid bacterial eradication [113]. A representative example is the Fe3O4@MOF@Au (FMA) heterostructure fabricated via magnetic core synthesis, porous IRMOF-3 shell growth, and ultrasmall AuNPs incorporation. Three synergistic advantages emerge from this design: enhanced POD-like kinetics through Au and Fe3O4 NPs catalytic synergy, stabilization and improved biocompatibility enabled by the MOF shell, and ultra-efficient antibacterial action requiring only minimal H2O2 concentrations of 0.97 μM (Fig. 15C) [114]. Through its ROS-mediated mechanism, the system achieves broad-spectrum pathogen eradication while maintaining metabolic viability.

Collectively, metal-based nanozymes demonstrate transformative multidimensional evolution progressing from elementary single-metal enzymatic mimics to architecturally complex multi-enzyme systems including alloys, oxides, sulfides, and atomically precise SAzymes/DAzymes (Fig. 16), as systematically cataloged in Table 1. This progression capitalizes on three cornerstone strategic advantages: microenvironment-responsive activity modulation, synergistic integration of bactericidal, antioxidant, and immunomodulatory mechanisms, and programmable functional adaptability. While composite architectures enhance therapeutic efficacy and biosafety through multimodal integration, their clinical translation confronts persistent barriers, including unresolved long-term biodistribution toxicity, compromised catalytic stability within heterogeneous infection microenvironments, and the absence of standardized evaluation frameworks. To advance these catalytic platforms toward clinical viability, future research should prioritize four interconnected domains: constructing intricate multimetal catalytic networks to amplify reaction kinetics, designing intelligent bioresponsive interfaces capable of autonomous adaptation to pathological cues, optimizing scalable ecofabrication processes ensuring manufacturing reproducibility, and deciphering nanozyme-biosystem crosstalk through multi-omics interrogation. Mastery of these dimensions will collectively propel the transition from experimental demonstrations to clinically deployable precision antimicrobials, ultimately redefining infection management paradigms in the post-antibiotic era.

Synergistic antibacterial therapy of metal nanozymes

Metal nanozymes represent a paradigm shift in antibacterial strategy, offering superior catalytic efficiency, broad-spectrum efficacy, and intrinsic resistance-evading properties. To overcome residual limitations in therapeutic potency and biosafety, emerging research strategically integrates these nano-catalysts with external energy sources. This section examines light- and ultrasound-activated systems, analyzing their action mechanisms, therapeutic outcomes, and translational pathways for next-generation antimicrobial intervention.

Combination of metal nanozymes with PTT

Near-infrared photothermal therapy (PTT) provides non-invasive bacterial eradication through localized hyperthermia and offers advantages such as broad-spectrum activity, shortened treatment cycles, and minimal systemic side effects. Yet faces critical constraints including tissue damage risks at therapeutic temperatures and potential heat resistance development [133–135]. The fusion with nanozyme catalysis creates transformative synergistic platforms where photothermal effects permeabilize bacterial membranes while catalytic reactions generate localized ROS. This dual mechanism enables reduced treatment intensity, shortened duration, and prevention of resistance evolution [136].

IONPs exemplify this paradigm through pH-gated POD-like activity and exceptional photothermal conversion. In acidic infection microenvironments, they concurrently catalyze •OH formation from H2O2 while simultaneously utilizing photothermal effects to disrupt bacterial membranes and denature intracellular macromolecules. This combined action achieves near-total pathogen eradication by simultaneously compromising membrane integrity and inducing oxidative stress (Fig. 17A) [137]. Advancing this paradigm, atomically engineered systems like Fe-Cu diatomic nanozymes (FeCu BSNs) demonstrate enhanced therapeutic precision through bimetallic synergy. The strategic incorporation of Cu not only enhances photon absorption and spin–orbit coupling for superior photothermal efficiency but also creates hybridized Fe-Cu active sites that achieve POD activity 2.3-fold higher than Fe-only counterparts. Under NIR irradiation, these diatomic nanozymes deliver coordinated hyperthermia and catalytic ROS generation at ultralow concentrations (10 μg mL−1), simultaneously disrupting membrane architecture and metabolic pathways (Fig. 17B,C) [138].

(B) Synthetic process of FeCu BSNs; (C) Illustration of FeCu BSNs for antibacterial and wound healing. Reproduced from Ref. [123]. (D)Bacteria-responsive programmable hydrogels with a cascade reaction mechanism exhibiting self-switching enzyme-like activity. Reproduced from Ref. [139]

Further elevating this approach, trimetallic AuMnCu nanozyme systems (AMCB) integrate mild phototherapy with sophisticated multi-enzyme activities while providing unprecedented molecular-level insights into antibacterial mechanisms. The NIR-induced mild hyperthermia serves dual functions: significantly boosting the nanozyme’s catalytic activity while triggering controlled release of antimicrobial agents from the supporting hydrogel matrix. Most notably, transcriptomic analysis reveals that this photothermal-nanozyme combination induces comprehensive antibacterial effects at the molecular level, systematically disrupting bacterial energy metabolism, protein synthesis, and lipid pathways. The treatment significantly down-regulates genes governing quorum sensing, flagellar assembly, and biofilm formation, while concurrently depleting glutathione and disrupting redox homeostasis. This multifaceted attack, powered by the synergy between photothermal effects and enzymatic activities, effectively suppresses bacterial virulence and survival through coordinated genetic and metabolic interventions (Fig. 17D) [139].

While photothermal-catalytic synergy demonstrates remarkable antibacterial enhancement, clinical translation requires addressing four fundamental challenges: achieving spatiotemporal coordination of thermal and catalytic processes, mitigating dynamic interference from biological components during activation, ensuring reproducible manufacturing of complex nanostructures, and implementing thermal management protocols to prevent collateral tissue damage. Future development must prioritize stimuli-responsive materials incorporating built-in safety controls such as temperature-feedback modulation and microenvironment-specific activation profiles. The integration of real-time monitoring capabilities with therapeutic functions will further advance these systems toward precision antibacterial applications.

Combination of metal nanozymes with PDT

Photodynamic therapy (PDT) harnesses photosensitizers (PS) to generate ROS under light activation to eradicate pathogens, yet its clinical utility is severely constrained by hypoxic microenvironments prevalent in biofilms and chronic wounds. The integration of metal nanozymes with PDT represents a paradigm-shifting strategy that simultaneously addresses oxygen limitations while amplifying therapeutic outcomes through catalytic microenvironment remodeling [140, 141]. Conventional oxygen-delivery approaches face inherent limitations in penetration efficiency and material stability. The DHTPY-Cu@ZOL system pioneers an oxygen-economized solution by leveraging Cu-based nanozymes with CAT-like activity to catalytically recycle endogenous H2O2 into molecular O2. This continuous in situ oxygenation reactivates the aggregation-induced emission photosensitizer, enabling sustained 1O2 generation even in profound hypoxia. Beyond bactericidal effects, this platform demonstrates intelligent immunomodulation capable of resolving pathological inflammation while simultaneously stimulating tissue regeneration. This transformative functionality redefines PDT from a purely cytotoxic intervention into a regenerative therapeutic modality (Fig. 18A) [142].

(C) Application and potential mechanisms of the Gel-HA-Se@CeO2 hydrogel in wound healing under controllable PDT. Reproduced from Ref. [144]

Beyond addressing hypoxia, achieving targeted delivery while overcoming limitations in catalytic activity and specificity is crucial for treating resistant infections. A breakthrough in targeted delivery is exemplified by a smart nanocarrier system utilizing macrophage cell membrane biomimetics. This system exploits Toll-like receptors on the macrophage membrane surface (activated by pathogens) to specifically recognize molecular patterns of S. aureus, enabling precise guidance of a mesoporous CeO2-meso-tetra(4-carboxyphenyl)porphyrin (TCPP) complex (S-MM@CeO2-TCPP) to the infection site. This nanoplatform integrates three critical functionalities where mesoporous CeO2 delivers POD-mimetic activity while the photosensitizer TCPP enables photodynamic action complemented by a biomimetic membrane coating that facilitates immune evasion. Upon activation photo-enhanced CDT synergizes with targeted 1O2 generation to penetrate drug-resistant intracellular niches, simultaneously reprogramming pro-inflammatory cytokine networks (Fig. 18B) [143]. The critical balance between antimicrobial efficacy and host tissue protection necessitates precision control over ROS dynamics, a challenge addressed by the Gel-HA-Se@CeO₂ hydrogel platform through a four-dimensional regulatory paradigm. Physical sequestration of selenium-doped ceria nanoparticles minimizes direct tissue contact. CeO2 NPs exhibit dual nanozyme functions, provide endogenous ROS scavenging through CAT and SOD activities, complemented by light-triggered photosensitization enabling on-demand 1O2 production. RNA sequencing validation confirms this system orchestrates macrophage polarization toward regenerative M2 phenotypes while maintaining redox homeostasis, representing a breakthrough in tissue-compatible antimicrobial activity (Fig. 18C) [144].

Photodynamic-nanozyme synergy has evolved from hypoxia compensation to intelligent microenvironment reprogramming, offering unprecedented adaptability against resistant infections. Three critical frontiers demand focus for clinical maturation: establishing predictive models for PS-nanozyme electronic coupling efficiency, developing accelerated aging protocols for nanomaterial stability assessment, and creating standardized metrics for spatiotemporal ROS quantification. The next evolutionary leap will emerge from rational design of multimodal nanozymes integrating photocatalytic, immunomodulatory, and diagnostic capabilities within modular biomimetic chassis, ultimately realizing dynamic therapy systems that autonomously adapt to infection progression.

Combination of metal nanozymes with SDT

While PDT and PTT effectively target superficial infections, their dependence on optical wavelengths imposes inherent penetration limitations due to tissue scattering and absorption. Ultrasound activation overcomes this fundamental constraint through superior tissue penetration, millimeter-scale spatial precision, and non-invasive energy delivery, establishing an ideal modality for nanozyme activation in deep-seated infections [145–147]. Antibacterial sonodynamic therapy (aSDT) capitalizes on this advantage by using ultrasound to activate sonosensitizers (SS) for localized ROS generation, yet its efficacy remains constrained by hypoxic microenvironments and rapid oxygen depletion during treatment [148, 149]. Pd@Pt NSs provide a multifaceted solution through their trienzyme-mimetic capabilities (POD-, OXD-, and CAT-like activities) [150, 151]. The engineered ultrasound switchable Pd@Pt-T790 nanozyme system exploits these properties with sophisticated spatial control where T790 molecular modification selectively inhibits CAT-like activity under physiological conditions, preventing off-target oxidative damage, while ultrasound irradiation dynamically restores enzymatic function at infection sites. This on-demand activation catalyzes endogenous H2O2 decomposition into O2 , creating a self-amplifying cycle that enhances sonodynamic ROS production. Validated in myositis models, this spatiotemporally controlled system eradicates deep-tissue MRSA biofilms through synergistic catalytic and acoustic effects while simultaneously promoting microenvironment repair (Fig. 19A-D) [152].

Conventional degradable sonosensitizers face functional decay with material degradation. The two-dimensional metal–organic sonozyme MON(CuMn)-Q addresses this limitation. Copper-manganese bimetallic centers coordinated with quercetin (Que) form dynamic active sites (MnN4/Cu2O8) that generate US-triggered 1O2 while adsorbing excess ROS to modulate inflammation. Crucially, ligand–metal charge transfer (LMCT) during degradation enables continuous release of bioactive copper/manganese ions and quercetin, creating sustained antibacterial and antioxidant effects that persist beyond material disintegration. This functional longevity represents a paradigm shift from transient to self-replenishing therapeutic activity (Fig. 19E-H) [153].

Sonodynamic-nanozyme integration resolves dual limitations of superficial penetration depth and transient efficacy through three complementary strategies: catalytic oxygen regeneration, spatiotemporal activity control, and post-degradation functionality. Future development should prioritize metastable coordination architectures enabling adaptive infection targeting and programmable bioorthogonal catalytic circuits that dynamically couple pathogen elimination with tissue regeneration. This convergence of materials innovation and microbiological insight will drive next-generation solutions for implant-associated biofilms and multidrug-resistant deep-tissue infections.

Combination of metal nanozymes with dual therapy

Conventional PTT and PDT face intrinsic limitations including suboptimal energy conversion efficiency, oxygen-dependent photosensitizer constraints, and localized thermal damage risks that collectively restrict efficacy against deep-seated infections. Strategic integration with metal nanozyme catalysis overcomes these barriers through synergistic mechanisms. A persistent challenge in conventional PTT/PDT combinations, however, is the spectral mismatch between the optimal wavelengths for photothermal conversion and photosensitizer excitation. This incompatibility typically necessitates dual laser sources for independent activation [154, 155].

Recent breakthroughs resolve wavelength conflicts through nanomaterial design innovations. Indocyanine green (ICG)-functionalized Mn3O4 NSs exemplify this approach by combining defective Mn3O4 hexagons with NIR-responsive ICG. This configuration enables concurrent activation of photothermal conversion, photodynamic action, and OXD-like catalysis under single 808 nm laser irradiation. Surface lattice defects critically enhance bacterial membrane adhesion while facilitating photothermally amplified 1O2 penetration. Molecular-level evidence through propidium iodide staining and ATP quantification confirms that this multi-mechanism attack severely compromises MRSA membrane integrity and disrupts cellular energy metabolism, leading to efficient bacterial eradication (Fig. 20A-C) [156]. Parallel innovation emerges in oxygen-deficient molybdenum trioxide nanodots (MoO3−x NDs), where precise photothermal control to 50°C optimally regulates nanozyme kinetics. This temperature-tuned catalysis enables explosive hydroxyl and superoxide radical generation at ultralow H2O2 concentrations, creating a self-amplifying photodynamic-photothermal-enzymatic cascade. Validated in deep-tissue infection models, this single-light-triggered triple mechanism demonstrates broad-spectrum efficacy against E. coli and MRSA (Fig. 20D) [157].

Beyond photonic integration, advanced systems address CDT limitations through microbial metabolism manipulation. Cu/Mo bimetallic sulfides (MCS) loaded with GOX create self-sustaining therapeutic microenvironments. Enzymatic glucose oxidation generates continuous H2O2 supply while acidifying local pH, subsequently activating POD-mimetic nanozyme activity for •OH production. The Mo4+/Mo6+ redox pair further amplifies catalytic efficiency, while adjunct laser irradiation introduces photothermal membrane disruption and photodynamic singlet oxygen generation. This antimicrobial strategy coordinates pathogen elimination with inflammation resolution and tissue regeneration (Fig. 20E) [158].

The evolution toward multimodal nanozyme platforms marks a paradigm shift from single-mechanism interventions to integrated therapeutic ecosystems, where key advances encompass spectral-unified photonic systems eliminating dual-laser requirements and US-responsive nanomaterials enabling dynamic deep-tissue modulation. These innovations transcend traditional efficacy-toxicity tradeoffs through biomimetic targeting and stimulus-activated precision. Future development demands concerted focus on three interconnected frontiers beginning with intelligent material architectures that feature broad-spectrum energy harvesters possessing multivalent catalytic adjustability. Progress equally requires biointerface engineering incorporating pathogen-specific enrichment mechanisms synergized with immunomodulatory functions while advancing therapeutic-regenerative integration to seamlessly couple antibacterial action with tissue rebuilding processes. Accelerating advancement in these domains will propel clinical translation for complex scenarios including orthopedic implant infections and diabetic wound biofilms. The convergence of nanocatalytic precision with synthetic biology principles enables a new era of resistance-evading antimicrobial solutions delivering simultaneous potency, specificity, and tissue-compatible safety profiles.

Conclusion and outlook

Metal nanozymes represent a transformative class of potential antibacterial agents, characterized by their catalytic efficiency, synthetic versatility, cost-effectiveness, and tunable functionalities. Their broad-spectrum activity stems primarily from the generation of ROS, which causes nonspecific oxidative damage to universal bacterial structures such as lipid membranes and DNA. This mechanism enables effective action against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, irrespective of their specific resistance profiles. With their multifunctional capabilities and precise controllability, these engineered systems offer a promising strategic response to the escalating antibiotic resistance crisis and could emerge as viable alternatives to conventional antimicrobials in the future [159, 160]. This comprehensive review has detailed recent advancements in their therapeutic applications, highlighting their core capacity to mimic natural enzymatic activities including POD, OXD, CAT, and SOD functions. Crucially, bactericidal potency is governed by precisely tunable structural parameters, including size, morphology, composition, ions or molecules, and internal and external stimuli, that collectively optimize catalytic performance. The primary mechanism involves catalytic generation of ROS that inflict oxidative damage on essential microbial components, supplemented by secondary pathways like controlled metal ion release and context-dependent ROS scavenging to protect host tissues. Integration with external energy sources such as light and ultrasound further amplifies therapeutic efficacy through synergistic effects.

Despite significant promise, clinical translation faces four interconnected challenges requiring multidisciplinary solutions. First, elucidating complex catalytic mechanisms remains paramount, particularly dynamic processes involving structural transformations, valence state changes, and coordination environment evolution during catalysis. Current reliance on endpoint analyses fails to capture intermediate adsorption–desorption behavior and real-time kinetics, necessitating advanced in situ characterization techniques. Second, optimizing activity within biological environments demands overcoming interference from physiological variables like pH fluctuations, ionic strength variations, and biomolecule adsorption that compromise catalytic efficiency and predictability. Third, and foremost, is the assurance of safety and biocompatibility. As the vast majority of research remains in the preclinical stage, a significant gap exists regarding human clinical trials specifically for antibacterial therapy. As foreign materials, metal nanozymes risk triggering immunogenicity, leading to neutralization by the immune system or long-term toxicity due to accumulation in reticuloendothelial organs. Consequently, surface modification strategies must delicately balance safety enhancements, such as PEGylation to reduce immune recognition, with the preservation of the catalytic core’s activity. Finally, harmonizing multifunctional activities presents particular complexity, as synergistic integration of complementary functions like hypoxia alleviation and bactericidal action requires preventing kinetic competition between coexisting enzymatic pathways (Fig. 21A,B).

(A) Key challenges of metal nanozymes in antibacterial applications. (B) Schematic illustration antibacterial applications of metal nanozymes for disease treatment. (C) Future strategies to address the challenges

Successfully navigating these challenges necessitates a concerted multidisciplinary effort. This includes fundamental research utilizing sophisticated in situ and operando techniques to unravel catalytic pathways, innovative synthetic approaches for enhancing enzymatic activity and material stability, comprehensive long-term evaluations of toxicological and pharmacokinetic profiles, and intelligent designs enabling synergistic therapeutic actions. Further accelerating advancements in nanotechnology, biomaterials science, and analytical characterization methods will significantly advance the performance and real-world applicability of metal nanozymes. Overcoming current limitations is thus essential for translating these powerful nanoscale catalysts into reliable, clinically viable antimicrobial agents capable of substantial impact (Fig. 21C).
