A post hoc analysis of linaclotide in pediatric patients with functional constipation and neurodevelopmental disorders
Authors: Hannibal Person, Jeffrey S Hyams, Rinarani M Sanghavi, Valentina Shakhnovich, Katherine Kosch, Moming Li, Danielle D'Achino, Julie Khlevner, Bruno P Chumpitazi, Miguel Saps
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesFunctional constipation (FC) is common in children with comorbid neurodevelopmental disorders (NDD) and its optimal treatment may be challenged by adherence and medication tolerability issues in this population. We assessed the efficacy and safety of linaclotide in pediatric patients with FC and NDD.MethodsThis post hoc analysis of a Phase 3, randomized, placebo‐controlled study assessed oral linaclotide 72 µg once daily for 12 weeks in patients aged 6–17 years with FC and NDD. The primary endpoint was change from baseline (CFB) in weekly spontaneous bowel movement (SBM) frequency. In efficacy comparisons, an absolute standardized mean difference (SMD) > 0.2 was considered indicative of a treatment difference.ResultsIn this analysis of 57 pediatric patients (linaclotide n = 25; placebo n = 32), 91% had attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder; other NDDs included autism spectrum disorder, tic disorders, and sensory processing disorders. CFB in weekly SBM frequency over 12 weeks was greater with linaclotide versus placebo (2.09 vs. 0.59, respectively; SMD 0.688). Linaclotide was also associated with greater improvements relative to placebo in stool consistency (CFB 1.37 vs. 0.55, respectively) and straining score (CFB –0.98 vs. –0.59). Most patients (≥96%) achieved ≥80% dosing compliance with once‐daily administration of linaclotide or placebo. Linaclotide was well tolerated, with a safety profile consistent with that reported previously.ConclusionsIn children with FC and comorbid NDD, linaclotide 72 µg once daily was associated with improved constipation outcomes, high adherence, and a well‐tolerated safety profile, similar to that seen in the overall study population.Trial RegistrationClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04026113 (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04026113).Keywords: attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, autism spectrum disorder, disorder of gut‐brain interaction

INTRODUCTION

Functional constipation (FC) is common in the pediatric population, affecting an estimated 12.9%–14.1% of children aged 4–18 years in the United States. 1 Children with FC may experience infrequent and difficult bowel movements, with hard or painful stools. 2 As a disorder of gut–brain interaction, the pathophysiology of pediatric FC is influenced by intrinsic biological factors, lifestyle, and psychological and behavioral/sensory factors. 2 , 3 , 4 FC occurs more frequently in children with concomitant psychosocial/neurodevelopmental disorders (NDD) such as anxiety, depression, attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), and autism spectrum disorder (ASD). 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 In addition to associated difficulties in sensory processing and inattention, some children with NDD can struggle to adequately relax their pelvic floor muscles during defecation (dyssynergia), contributing to a cycle of difficult‐to‐pass and infrequent stooling, characteristic of FC. 5 Others, but not all children with NDD, may be more likely to exhibit voluntary stool‐withholding behaviors due to defecation‐related anxiety, thought to be a key trigger in the development of FC. 5 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14

Neurobehavioral disorders tend to co‐exist. For example, ADHD, which is the most commonly reported NDD in children and adolescents, may co‐occur with ASD, tic disorders, and depressive, bipolar, anxiety, conduct, and oppositional defiant disorders; thus, a patient with ADHD may exhibit additional symptoms characteristic of one or more of these NDD comorbidities. 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 While there are a lack of data regarding the management of FC in all NDD conditions, available literature and clinical experience suggest that treatment of FC in children with comorbid NDD, such as ASD and ADHD, may pose unique challenges due to the behavioral aspects associated with these conditions, for example, anxiety or a heightened sensitivity to tastes or smells may result in a refusal to take medications as well as lack of attention to bodily cues, and fidgeting. 8 , 10 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 As such, a personalized treatment plan incorporating pharmacotherapy and behavioral intervention for patients with FC and comorbid NDD is recommended. 22 , 27

Linaclotide, a guanylate cyclase C agonist acting on the luminal surface of the intestinal epithelium, is approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for treatment of FC in children 6 years and older based on the results of a Phase 3 clinical study in children and adolescents (NCT04026113). 28 , 29 , 30 Other medications are available for the treatment of FC, for example, polyethylene glycol (PEG 3350), which can be an effective osmotic laxative for children with FC; however, it is only FDA‐approved for occasional constipation in patients aged 17 years and older and for no more than 7 days. 31 , 32 , 33 Furthermore, studies in pediatric patients have demonstrated that laxatives, such as PEG, particularly when combined with electrolytes, may be associated with poor palatability and, consequently, may have a negative impact on treatment compliance. 34 , 35 , 36 In view of the treatment challenges inherent in children with NDD, we conducted a post hoc analysis using the pivotal Phase 3 study population to evaluate the efficacy and safety of linaclotide in a subgroup of pediatric patients with NDD.

METHODS

Ethics statement

The Phase 3 study, from which data were used for this post hoc analysis, was designed, conducted, and reported in compliance with ethical principles set forth by the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. The research protocol was approved by an institutional review board at each study site, and the study was registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04026113). 30

Study design and participants

This was a descriptive post hoc analysis of a multicenter, Phase 3, randomized, double‐blind, placebo‑controlled study (NCT04026113), which evaluated the efficacy and safety of once‐daily linaclotide in pediatric patients with FC. 30 The date of study registration was July 17, 2019, and participants were randomized between October 1, 2019, and March 21, 2022. 30

This study included pediatric patients aged 6–17 years weighing at least 18 kg who met modified Rome III criteria for child and adolescent FC. Study design, eligibility criteria, and assessments have been published previously. 30

The current analysis was based on a subgroup of 57 children with NDD who received once‐daily oral linaclotide 72 µg in the Phase 3 study population. Eligible patients were required to have received at least one dose of the study intervention and to have NDD defined in this sub‐analysis as ADHD, aggressive behavior, ASD, conduct disorder, disruptive mood dysregulation, oppositional defiant disorder, sensory processing disorder, and/or tic disorders. These disorders were grouped together based on the evidence supporting frequent co‐occurrence and shared behavioral characteristics. 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 Comorbidities were reported by the patient's parent or legal guardian and corroborated by medical records when available. Pediatric patients who had more than one NDD were counted only once in the NDD subgroup. Outside of the USA and Canada, rescue medication (senna, bisacodyl, and sodium picosulfate) was allowed if ≥72 h had passed since the patient's previous bowel movement or if symptoms became intolerable.

Assessments and outcomes

The study comprised eight visits, including screening (Days −49 to −21), pre‐intervention (Days −21 to −14), randomization (Day 1), and visits at Weeks 2, 4, 8, 12, and an end‐of‐study visit (for patients not entering the long‐term safety study). Throughout the treatment period, patients or their caregivers used an eDiary twice daily to report on the frequency of spontaneous bowel movements (SBMs), stool consistency, use of rescue medication, sensation of incomplete evacuation, straining, abdominal symptoms (pain, bloating during the day or night), and fecal incontinence (during the day). Patients and/or their caregivers were trained on the use of the eDiary device at the pre‐intervention visit, and compliance with the eDiary device was verified at each subsequent visit.

The primary efficacy endpoint was the change from baseline (CFB) in weekly SBM frequency over the 12‐week treatment period. The secondary efficacy endpoint was the CFB in stool consistency over the 12‐week treatment period, measured daily using a pediatric (modified) version of the 7‐point ordinal Bristol Stool Form Scale (m‐BSFS; 1 = small hard lumps or balls, like pebbles/rabbit droppings; 7 = watery, no solid pieces/gravy). 37 , 38 Additional efficacy endpoints included: the CFB in straining score over the 12‐week treatment period, evaluated by assessing how hard a patient pushed when defecating on a scale of 0 (not hard at all) to 4 (pushed very hard); the proportion of patients with an SBM within 48 h after receiving the first dose of study intervention; the proportion of overall SBM responders, defined as patients who had an increase of two or more SBMs per week from baseline over the 12‐week treatment period; and the proportion of patients using rescue medication or any other laxative, suppository, or enema, or increasing the frequency of their use versus baseline during the 12‐week treatment period. An increase in rescue medication use was observed when the percentage of days of rescue medication use between any two visits during an analysis period was greater than the percentage of days of use at baseline.

Duration of treatment exposure and treatment (dosing) compliance were also assessed. The percentage thresholds for dosing compliance, defined as the number of capsules taken by a participant during the double‐blind treatment period divided by the number of capsules planned to be taken during this period and multiplied by 100 (i.e., the percentage of doses taken as planned), 39 were <80%, 80%–120%, and >120% in accordance with accepted compliance metrics. 40 Safety assessments were made at each study visit, including the frequency of treatment‐emergent adverse events (TEAEs).

Statistical analysis

Baseline values for efficacy endpoints were derived from the eDiary in the pre‐intervention period. 30 The SBM weekly rate at baseline was derived from the number of SBMs a patient/carer recorded in the eDiary during this period. Participants who discontinued prematurely during the 12‐week treatment period had their eDiary data included up to the morning diary entry following the last treatment dose for all endpoints. As this was a post hoc analysis with no formal hypothesis and an anticipated small sample size, efficacy comparisons between linaclotide and placebo were performed using the standardized mean difference (SMD), a measure of effect size when comparing two groups that is unaffected by sample size. An absolute SMD of >0.2 was considered indicative of a difference between linaclotide and placebo. 41

RESULTS

Demographics and clinical characteristics

In total, 328 pediatric patients with FC received treatment and were included in the Phase 3 study (n = 164 in each of the linaclotide 72 µg and placebo groups). Of these, 57 patients were eligible for inclusion in this post hoc analysis and comprised the NDD subgroup (linaclotide, n = 25; placebo, n = 32). Most of the included patients had ADHD (n = 52 [91.2%]) followed by ASD (n = 8 [14.0%]). Other NDD categories were: tic disorders (n = 3); disorders of disruptive mood dysregulation, oppositional defiant disorder, and sensory processing disorder (n = 2 each); and aggressive behavior or conduct disorder (n = 1 each) (Table 1). The mean (standard deviation [SD]) duration of NDD (from start of medical history until randomization or end of medical history, whichever occurred first) in the NDD subgroup was 4.09 (3.00) years (linaclotide 72 µg 4.04 [3.14]; placebo 4.13 [2.93]). Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were generally balanced between the linaclotide and placebo groups (Table 1) and were comparable with the overall Phase 3 study population. However, in this NDD subgroup, the linaclotide treatment arm comprised a greater proportion of males and fewer patients of Hispanic/Latino ethnicity than in the placebo arm (Table 1).

Efficacy outcomes in patients with comorbid NDD

At baseline, the mean (SD) frequency rate for SBMs was 1.08 (0.78) SBMs per week for patients in the linaclotide arm and 1.46 (0.85) SBMs per week for those in the placebo arm, increasing to 3.17 (2.76) and 2.06 (1.22) SBMs per week over the 12‐week treatment period, respectively. The corresponding mean (SD) CFB in weekly SBM frequency was higher with linaclotide treatment than with placebo (2.09 [2.85] vs. 0.59 [1.14], respectively; SMD = 0.688; Figure 1). Among patients in the NDD subgroup who had a m‐BSFS score at baseline and at the end of the 12‐week treatment period (linaclotide, n = 21; placebo, n = 30), the mean (SD) CFB in stool consistency was 1.37 (1.32) with linaclotide treatment versus 0.55 (0.98) with placebo (SMD = 0.707; Figure 2A). In terms of straining score among those with baseline and end of study data, mean CFB was greater for the NDD subgroup of patients treated with linaclotide (n = 22) compared with placebo (n = 30). The mean (SD) straining score was reduced from 2.35 (1.23) at baseline to 1.37 (0.94) after 12 weeks of linaclotide treatment and from 2.33 (1.05) to 1.75 (0.88) after 12 weeks in those receiving placebo (SMD = −0.402; Figure 2B). In the NDD subgroup, the proportion of patients with an SBM within 48 h after receiving the first dose of study treatment was higher in those who received linaclotide than in those who received placebo (56.0% vs. 43.8%, respectively; SMD = 0.247; Figure 2C). Furthermore, a higher proportion of overall SBM responders (patients with an increase of two or more SBMs per week from baseline over the 12‐week treatment period) was observed in the linaclotide group compared with the placebo group (40.0% vs. 12.5%, respectively; SMD = 0.658; Figure 2D).

Fewer patients treated with linaclotide (n = 9 [36.0%]) used rescue medication (including stimulant laxatives, suppository, or enema) compared with those receiving placebo (n = 19 [59.4%]; SMD = −0.481) during the 12‐week treatment period. Additionally, fewer patients treated with linaclotide than with placebo required an increase in frequency of rescue medicines or any other laxative, suppository, or enema (12.0% [n = 3] vs. 28.1% [n = 9]; SMD –0.411).

Duration of treatment exposure and dosing compliance

Approximately 96% of patients included in this post hoc analysis subgroup completed between 56 and 96 days of treatment with linaclotide or placebo. Two patients discontinued the study early. A patient in the placebo group withdrew consent within 14 days of starting treatment and another in the linaclotide group discontinued between 14 and 28 days of treatment due to an adverse event (described below). The mean (SD) duration of treatment exposure was 83.8 (12.3) days in the linaclotide group and 84.0 (14.3) days in the placebo group. Dosing compliance was high, with ≥96% achieving between 80% and 120% dosing compliance with once‐daily administration of linaclotide or placebo.

Safety of linaclotide in patients with comorbid NDD

The incidence of any TEAE in the NDD subgroup was comparable between linaclotide (n = 6; 24.0%) and placebo (n = 6; 18.8%) groups (Table 2). Diarrhea was reported for one patient (4.0%) treated with linaclotide. Other TEAEs in the linaclotide treatment arm were constipation, fecaloma, hepatic steatosis, impetigo, influenza, and otitis media (all n = 1; 4.0%; Table 2). TEAEs in the placebo group, all occurring in one patient (3.1%), were increased blood thyroid‐stimulating hormone, cough, dysuria, influenza, nasopharyngitis, oropharyngeal pain, pharyngitis streptococcal, pyrexia, and SARS‐CoV‐2 test positive. One patient in the NDD subgroup, treated with linaclotide, had a serious TEAE (fecaloma), which resolved, but was considered by the investigator to be related to linaclotide and led to treatment discontinuation.

DISCUSSION

This was a post hoc analysis in a subgroup of patients with NDD derived from the Phase 3 randomized, double‐blind, placebo‑controlled study, which evaluated the efficacy and safety of linaclotide in pediatric patients aged 6–17 years with FC. We found that in patients with comorbid NDD, treatment with oral linaclotide 72 μg once daily was associated with increased weekly SBM frequency from baseline and softened stool consistency compared with placebo. These findings are in alignment with those reported for the overall Phase 3 study population, where patients receiving linaclotide demonstrated greater improvements from baseline in both weekly SBM frequency and stool consistency compared with patients receiving placebo. 30 In addition, more than half of all patients in the NDD subgroup treated with linaclotide had an SBM within 48 h of the first treatment dose, which was also comparable with observations in the overall Phase 3 population. Furthermore, linaclotide treatment was associated with a reduction in straining compared with placebo. Overall, the data from this post hoc analysis, together with the observed reduction in rescue medication use in this subgroup of pediatric patients, replicate the effectiveness of linaclotide that has been observed in the wider FC pediatric population.

Notably, we observed high medication compliance in our study of children with NDD, with almost all patients complying with their once‐daily oral linaclotide regimen at least 80% of the time, similar to the overall pediatric population participating in this trial. This is pertinent because poor treatment compliance is frequently observed in pediatric patients with FC, often driven by issues such as taste and palatability of orally administered treatments and the emotional impact of constipation. 42 , 43 These issues are also regularly exacerbated in children with FC who are neurodivergent, and our findings are of particular relevance to the families and healthcare providers of such children. 25 Linaclotide capsules are not associated with any known palatability or taste issues; nevertheless, patients in this study were permitted to sprinkle the contents of the linaclotide capsule into bottled water or applesauce (consistent with the US Prescribing Information 28 ), which could have contributed to the high rates of compliance that we observed. Overall, the high treatment adherence rates, coupled with low rates of TEAEs and discontinuation due to TEAEs, suggest linaclotide treatment is well tolerated in pediatric patients with FC and NDD. The safety profile of linaclotide reported in this analysis was similar to that of the overall Phase 3 study population. 30 To our knowledge, this post hoc subgroup analysis of a pivotal Phase 3 pediatric study is the first to examine the efficacy and safety of a pharmacologic agent in children and adolescents (aged 6–17 years) with FC and comorbid NDD. The findings demonstrated that once‐daily 72 µg oral linaclotide improved FC outcomes and was associated with a well‐tolerated safety profile and high levels of adherence in a population that may be considered challenging due to the anxiety, sensory overload, and behavioral issues characteristic of NDD. 8 , 10 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 These findings are potentially noteworthy considering the impact constipation can have on school absenteeism and health‐related quality of life, including psychological complaints such as anxiety/depression and impaired social functioning in pediatric FC. 3 , 5 , 44 , 45 Importantly, no treatment‐related adverse events of psychological events were observed in either treatment arm among patients with NDD in this post hoc analysis.

Strengths of this study include its rigorous multicenter design and comprehensive data collection, which both contributed to the FDA approval of linaclotide for the treatment of FC in children and adolescents. Approval currently extends to children 6–17 years of age in both the United States and Canada. The proportion of study participants with comorbid NDD (17%) is potentially reflective of real‐world practice. 46 Limitations of the analysis are inherently related to its post hoc nature, the relatively small sample size of patients with comorbid NDD within the larger Phase 3 population, and potential imbalances in baseline demographic characteristics between treatment groups in the NDD subgroup.

CONCLUSIONS

This post hoc analysis demonstrated that once‐daily oral treatment with linaclotide was efficacious and well tolerated in pediatric patients with FC and comorbid NDD. Patients experienced improvements in SBM frequency, stool consistency, and straining. These outcomes were consistent with those observed in the pivotal Phase 3 clinical study population. Importantly, high medication compliance was maintained throughout the study, an encouraging finding given the known challenges of treatment adherence in the general pediatric FC population, especially among those with comorbid NDD.
