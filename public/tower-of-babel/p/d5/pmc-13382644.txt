Associations between transdiagnostic dimensions of psychopathology and brain structure and function in youth: What have we learned from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) study?
Authors: Harry R Smolker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe onset of puberty and subsequent transition into adolescence is a formative period for long-term mental health outcomes, yet the neural bases of psychopathology symptom levels during this dynamic developmental stage remain unclear. With its large sample size, breadth of measures, and longitudinal scope, the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) study provides a unique opportunity to uncover the neural bases of psychopathology symptoms and related risk factors in youth. In this review, we survey studies testing for associations between magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) measures and transdiagnostic symptom dimensions within the ABCD dataset. Looking across studies utilizing ABCD data, we find converging evidence that at the transition into adolescence: 1) general psychopathology is stably associated with reduced total brain volume and surface area, as well as alterations in the functional connectivity of the default mode and dorsal attention networks and altered diffusivity of cortico-striatal tracts and tracts innervating the frontal lobe; 2) internalizing psychopathology is specifically associated with alterations in connectivity of the amygdala; 3) internalizing and externalizing symptoms are associated with alterations in fronto-striatal activation during tasks tapping working memory and reward processing; 4) cortical thickness shows relatively few cross-sectional associations with psychopathology symptoms but is associated with change in symptoms over time; and 5) MRI metrics have limited utility in predicting psychopathology symptom levels over and above typical clinical and demographic measures. In addition to highlighting evidence supporting these findings, we suggest important future directions for researchers to pursue within the ABCD dataset.Keywords: Adolescence, Psychopathology, P-factor, Internalizing, Externalizing, Magnetic resonance imaging

Introduction

An era of great promise and personal growth, adolescence is also a period of immense vulnerability. Nearly half of all mental disorders emerge prior to the completion of adolescence while subclinical symptoms during this formative era are predictive of later mental health problems (Bohman et al., 2018, Read et al., 2018, Solmi et al., 2022). As a result, an improved understanding of the neural bases of psychopathology symptoms in youth may have an outsized impact on reducing the burden of mental illness across the lifespan by informing biologically based prevention and intervention techniques. Coupling an unprecedented sample size and longitudinal scope with rich measurement of psychopathology and multimodal imaging data, the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) study has allowed for a wealth of studies linking both structural and functional properties of the brain to psychopathology symptoms in youth. In the current review, we survey studies that have utilized data from the ABCD study to evaluate what we have learned from ABCD thus far regarding the neural correlates of psychopathology symptom dimensions in youth while identifying important topics for future research utilizing this novel dataset.

Developmental models of psychopathology identify adolescence as a sensitive period for the emergence of mental illness, when genetic liability and environmental exposures may alter neurodevelopmental trajectories and increase risk for psychopathology (Cicchetti and Rogosch, 2002, Paus et al., 2008, Sisk and Gee, 2022). During this period, the brain undergoes substantial changes in cortical morphology, white matter organization, and functional network architecture as it solidifies into its adult form (Baum et al., 2022, Casey et al., 2019, Tamnes et al., 2017). Though there is considerable regional variability in the timing and trajectory of neurodevelopment, normative adolescent neurodevelopment is generally characterized by widespread synaptic pruning and myelination of white matter, resulting in decreases in cortical thickness and volume, as well as increases in both white matter volume and integrity as adolescence progresses (Tamnes et al., 2017, Spear, 2013, Sydnor et al., 2021). Critically, youth neurodevelopment coincides with considerable plasticity, with the brain rapidly adapting in accordance with environmental experiences (Whittle et al., 2025). In the case of mental illness, experiences of adversity during youth may lead to neurodevelopment trajectories that confer a susceptibility to mental illness. Although these frameworks have generated important hypotheses regarding adolescent psychopathology, critical questions remain regarding which neural features most consistently confer risk and how environmental experiences may shape developmental trajectories.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has revealed important insights into adolescent neurodevelopment, yet understanding of the neural bases of psychopathology symptoms in youth remains incomplete in part due to methodological and statistical challenges. Evidence from ABCD suggests that much of the previous literature linking neural metrics to psychopathology (as well as other behaviors) has been woefully underpowered, with the true effect size of brain-behavior associations being quite small (r < .1) (Marek et al., 2022). Relatedly, imaging metrics show considerable variability in reliability, both across modalities and across the brain, calling into question the validity of many previous brain-behavior associations (Elliott et al., 2020, Hedges et al., 2022, Holiga et al., 2018, Morey et al., 2010, Noble et al., 2019, Rai et al., 2024, Vollmar et al., 2010). Thus, the ABCD dataset presents a unique opportunity to both make new discoveries regarding the neural bases of mental illness in youth and to also confirm previous findings and theory regarding the neural correlates of mental illness.

In this article, we review studies which utilized the ABCD dataset to investigate the neural correlates of both manifest and factor analytic measures of general psychopathology (often referred to as the “p-factor”), as well as internalizing, and externalizing symptom dimensions in youth. Of particular interest is understanding the degree to which results from ABCD align with smaller, less powered studies, and how these findings compare to what has been observed in adults. To this end, the current review paper identifies consistent themes across ABCD studies, evaluates how these themes align or diverge from previous findings and current theory in youth neurodevelopment and psychopathology, while highlighting novel insights that have been gleaned through the ABCD dataset, including how neural features may link risk and resiliency factors to psychopathology symptom levels.

Review criteria

Psychopathology symptom dimensions

We focus on broad transdiagnostic dimensions because they are more reliably measured, more consistently replicated across studies, and may be particularly relevant during early adolescence when symptoms are often nonspecific (Achenbach et al., 2008, Lahey et al., 2017). Although the p-factor remains debated (Watts et al., 2024), broad symptom dimensions have shown stronger and more consistent associations with neural measures than more specific syndromes. As a result, although it is important to interpret analyses involving p-factor and related dimensions with caution, the p-factor may provide researchers with the best opportunity to identify the brain systems related to psychopathology, particularly in general population youth samples in which there is a relatively low incidence of disorders.

The majority of ABCD studies investigating transdiagnostic symptom dimensions in ABCD have relied on the Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL; Achenbach and Rescorla, 2001) to measure symptom severity. As part of the ABCD protocol, participant caregivers answered CBCL items annually on a three-point scale indicating how frequently their child engaged in 119 problem behaviors, with items divided into distinct subscales, including total, internalizing, and externalizing problems, among others. Despite being commonly used in studies of youth mental health, the CBCL has several notable limitations. First, parent-report has been shown to be unreliable, particularly regarding internalizing symptoms (De Los Reyes et al., 2015, Go et al., 2022). Second, there are demographic and cultural biases in CBCL responses, meaning CBCL scores may not be invariant across potential subsamples within the ABCD dataset (Rescorla et al., 2013). As a result, it is important to interpret findings related to the CBCL with caution yet despite these caveats, the CBCL remains the primary measure utilized to track psychopathology symptom levels in ABCD participants and is thus central to understanding what ABCD has revealed regarding the neural correlates of psychopathology symptoms in youth.

MRI metrics

The current review focuses on studies utilizing MRI data collected from the ABCD study, including structural MRI derived from T1-weighted and T2-weighted scans and diffusion weighted imaging (DWI), as well as functional scans during both rest and task. T1- and T2-weighted images were used to quantify brain morphometry, including measures of volume, thickness, and surface area of the brain at various levels of spatial resolution. These metrics provide broad indices of brain structure and development, with cortical volume and surface area often interpreted as markers of overall brain growth and organization, while cortical thickness is commonly examined as a potential indicator of developmental change across adolescence. DWI was used to assess white matter microstructure through metrics including FA, MD, and neurite density, measures frequently interpreted as reflecting aspects of white matter organization and maturation, although their precise biological underpinnings remain an active area of investigation. Functional MRI (fMRI) scans were collected both at rest and during task performance. Resting-state fMRI measures spontaneous fluctuations in neural activity and is commonly used to characterize functional connectivity between brain regions and large-scale networks. Several networks frequently discussed in this review have been implicated in cognitive and emotional functioning, including the default mode network (DMN), which is associated with internally directed cognition and self-referential processing; the frontoparietal network (FPN), which supports cognitive control and goal-directed behavior; the cingulo-opercular network (CON), which is implicated in maintaining task goals and performance monitoring; and the dorsal attention network (DAN), which supports attentional orienting and allocation of cognitive resources. In addition to resting-state scans, participants completed three task-based fMRI paradigms including the Emotional N-Back task (ENBack; Cohen et al., 2016), Monetary Incentive Delay task (MID; Knutson et al., 2000), and Stop Signal Task (SST; Logan, 1994), tapping control over working memory, reward processing, and inhibitory control, respectively. Together, these tasks provide insight into neural systems frequently implicated in developmental models of psychopathology, including fronto-striatal circuitry involved in cognitive control, motivation, and affective processing.

Despite the great potential of ABCD’s MRI data to make new discoveries regarding adolescent development, there are several limitations to these measures. Many of the studies highlighted in this review only utilized MRI data that passed stringent quality control for head motion during scanning, which can introduce significant errors into MRI image processing. Importantly, head motion is not randomly distributed across the ABCD sample but is instead associated with variables of interest in this review, including psychopathology levels (Hercules et al., 2024), age (Shi et al., 2025), as well as other demographic differences (Ramduny et al., 2025). As a result, exclusions based on head motion may disproportionately impact younger participants with relatively high levels of psychopathology, potentially reducing the reliability and generalizability of findings. While this concern is slightly assuaged by the sheer sample size of ABCD, it is an important point to consider when making inferences from ABCD imaging studies of psychopathology. Another factor to consider when assessing the neural correlates of psychopathology is differing degrees of reliability across imaging modalities. Notably, structural MRI shows better test-retest reliability than functional MRI (Hedges et al., 2022, Holiga et al., 2018, Rai et al., 2024), meaning researchers are generally more likely to detect replicable effects while using structural MRI. Yet even within imaging modalities, different measures show varying degrees of reliability that may also vary spatially across the brain, with larger ROIs generally being more reliable than smaller ones, and cortical volume and area being more reliable than cortical thickness (Hedges et al., 2022, Iscan et al., 2015). These differences in reliability across modalities are likely to influence the degree to which findings replicate across studies and, in the context of MRI, means the current review is most likely to find convergence across structural MRI and less so for functional MRI. Nevertheless, the ABCD dataset represents one of the most powerful resources to both confirm existing theories and make new discoveries about the neurobiological underpinnings of psychopathology across adolescence.

Scope of the current review

This review surveys studies which used the ABCD dataset to link MRI metrics to manifest or latent measures of p-factor, internalizing, and externalizing symptom levels. To identify studies that met criteria for this review, we reviewed 1640 publications identified as using ABCD data on the ABCD publication website as of November 1st of 2025 (https://abcdstudy.org/research-publications/). To determine which studies to include, the research team reviewed the title, abstracts, and keywords for each of these publications, and removed studies that did not appear to test for associations between some MRI metric and measures of p-factor-, internalizing-, or externalizing-like dimensions. Just over 100 studies were identified as meeting criteria for inclusion in this review, with roughly 40% testing for longitudinal associations between MRI metrics and psychopathology. While we are unable to review all the studies that met criteria for inclusion in this review, we emphasize novel findings as well as results that converge across studies.

Due to the staggered release of ABCD data, most studies to date have utilized data from the initial baseline timepoint, when participants were 9–10 years old. At this age, there is considerable variability in youths pubertal status, which has a profound impact on both psychopathology and neurodevelopment (Pfeifer and Allen, 2021). Indeed, analyses investigating pubertal status at baseline timepoint of ABCD suggest that roughly half of the sample had begun puberty, yet these rates significantly differed between the sexes, with 70% of females but only 30% of males having begun puberty by the first ABCD data collection timepoint (Herting et al., 2021). However, as most of the reviewed studies did not test for moderating impacts of sex or pubertal status on associations between the brain and psychopathology symptoms, it is largely unclear the degree to which associations between the brain and psychopathology at the initial timepoint are influenced by puberty or vary by sex. As a result, we conceptualize the baseline timepoint as capturing a transitory period between late childhood and early adolescence, unless a given study provides information suggesting moderating impacts of age or puberty on brain-psychopathology associations.

Central themes across ABCD studies linking MRI to transdiagnostic symptom dimensions

In the following, we present several notable themes across ABCD studies testing for the neural correlates of psychopathology symptom dimensions. We present these themes by symptom dimension, beginning with findings regarding the general symptoms (i.e., p-factor) and then move on to comparing the neural correlates of internalizing and externalizing symptoms. While many of the brain systems implicated in this review have been previously implicated in psychopathology, findings from ABCD provide a level of clarity regarding what specific neural properties of these brain systems are altered in accordance with psychopathology, and the specific dimension of psychopathology these systems are most strongly associated with. We conclude with a summary of key findings and suggest future directions for studies utilizing the ABCD dataset.

General symptoms of psychopathology

Prominent models of psychopathology across the lifespan posit that risk for mental illness often manifests as non-specific symptoms during childhood that begin to differentiate into disorder-specific symptom profiles across adolescence and into adulthood (Lahey et al., 2017). As a result, characterizing the neural bases of general, transdiagnostic symptoms at the transition between childhood and adolescence may provide insight into neurodevelopmental processes that confer broad liability for psychopathology before symptoms differentiate into more specific forms of mental illness. At the initial ABCD timepoint, general psychopathology, as captured by the p-factor, was most strongly characterized by CBCL symptoms related to attention problems, thought problems, social problems, and disruptive behaviors, suggesting that broad psychopathology during late childhood may be driven largely by neurodevelopmental and externalizing symptom domains (Michelini et al., 2019). In this section, we discuss findings from ABCD linking individual differences in general symptoms of psychopathology to MRI metrics.

Low global brain volume and surface area is a marker of increased general symptoms at the transition into adolescence

Across a range of approaches to measuring psychopathology, findings from ABCD consistently suggest that reduced total gray matter volume and surface area at the transition into adolescence are associated with higher levels of general psychopathology (Du et al., 2022, Durham et al., 2021, Kunitoki et al., 2023, Mewton et al., 2022, Modabbernia et al., 2022, Patel et al., 2022, Rakesh et al., 2025, Romer et al., 2023, Wainberg et al., 2022, Wang et al., 2023, Zhang et al., 2022). For instance, cross-sectional analyses using both hierarchical and bifactor models found that higher p-factor scores were associated with lower global gray matter volume and surface area at ages 9–10 years (Durham et al., 2021, Mewton et al., 2022, Romer et al., 2023). One of the major contributions of the ABCD study has been clarifying that associations between psychopathology and regional morphometric measures largely reflect broader differences in global brain organization. Multiple studies found widespread associations between the p-factor and regional volume or surface area, but these effects largely disappeared after accounting for total brain volume or surface area (Durham et al., 2021, Patel et al., 2022, Zhang et al., 2022). After controlling for global morphometry, relatively few region-specific findings remained, including associations of higher p-factor and internalizing symptoms with smaller hippocampal volume and higher p-factor with greater isthmus cingulate volume, two stress-sensitive brain regions jointly implicated in the posterior medial memory system (Durham et al., 2021, Parker et al., 2025, Zhang et al., 2022). Together, these findings suggest that broad dimensions of psychopathology in youth are more strongly related to global aspects of brain structure than to focal neuroanatomical abnormalities.

Longitudinal analyses of ABCD further suggest that global brain volume and area index relatively stable differences in psychopathology risk. Lower total brain volume and surface area at ages 9–10 years were associated with higher psychopathology levels both concurrently and across subsequent follow-ups (Romer et al., 2023, Royer et al., 2024, Wang et al., 2023). However, these measures generally did not predict increases or decreases in symptoms over time (Romer et al., 2023), suggesting that global morphometry may reflect a broad developmental liability for psychopathology established prior to adolescence rather than mechanisms directly driving symptom escalation during this developmental period.

ABCD studies also suggest that global brain morphology may represent a pathway linking diverse risk factors to psychopathology. Genetic studies found that associations between polygenic risk for psychopathology and symptom levels were partially mediated by total gray matter volume (Hughes et al., 2023, Lahey et al., 2024, Teeuw et al., 2023). Similarly, total brain volume and surface area partially mediated associations between psychopathology and multiple environmental factors, including prenatal substance exposure, family and neighborhood characteristics, and cognitive functioning (Gong et al., 2021, Gu et al., 2024, Lees et al., 2020, Maxwell et al., 2023, Patel et al., 2022). Because MRI-derived measures of brain volume are influenced by numerous developmental processes and do not map directly onto specific cellular mechanisms (Natu et al., 2019), lower total brain volume is best interpreted as a marker of broad neurodevelopmental variation rather than evidence of any singular biological process.

Taken together, findings from ABCD identify reduced total brain volume and surface area as some of the most robust neural correlates of general psychopathology during the transition into adolescence. More importantly, ABCD has shifted interpretation away from focal neuroanatomical abnormalities toward broader differences in global brain organization and suggests that these global measures may serve as intermediate markers linking genetic and environmental risk factors to psychopathology.

Alterations in diffusion of cortico-striatal and frontal white matter tracts associated with higher symptoms of psychopathology

White matter pathways support communication between distributed brain regions and undergo substantial maturation throughout childhood and adolescence, including increased myelination and fiber organization (Chang et al., 2017). These changes in white matter allow for improved transmission of information between brain systems and are thought to contribute to improvements in executive functioning, cognitive control, and emotional regulation, leading to the hypothesis that atypical white matter development may contribute to risk for psychopathology (Ladouceur et al., 2012, Paus et al., 2008). Consistent with this framework, previous research in other well-powered youth samples suggested that higher general and externalizing symptom levels are associated with lower levels of global fractional anisotropy (FA), particularly within major tracts linking frontal regions to the rest of the brain (Alnæs et al., 2018, Neumann et al., 2020). Findings from ABCD largely support these observations, while providing greater specificity regarding the white matter systems most consistently associated with psychopathology during the transition into adolescence. Specifically, supporting associations between psychopathology and more global measures of FA, Patel and colleagues (2022) found that higher p-factor scores were associated with increased neurite density across much of the frontal lobe. However, ABCD has also provided an increased degree of specificity, suggesting that general levels of psychopathology are specifically associated with FA of the inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus, which connects the frontal lobe to more posterior cortical areas, as well as the cortico-spinal tract and cortico-striatal pathways, which connect the cortex to the spinal cord and striatum, respectively. For example, Christensen and colleagues (2021) reported associations between FA of the inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus and a neurodevelopmental symptom factor while Cardenas-Iniguez and colleagues (2022) found that FA of the inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus was associated with conduct- and ADHD-related symptom dimensions when controlling for global FA. Together, these studies suggest that the inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus may play an outsized role in ADHD and related neurodevelopmental disorders (Leng et al., 2016). Given the role of the inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus in connecting frontal regions with posterior cortical areas involved in attention and higher-order cognition, these findings are broadly consistent with theories linking disruptions in cognitive control systems to neurodevelopmental psychopathology and attentional problems (Leng et al., 2016).

ABCD studies have also revealed that general symptoms of psychopathology are associated with diffusivity of cortico-spinal and cortico-striatal tracts. Utilizing a correlated factors model of psychopathology symptoms, Christensen and colleagues (2021) observed that higher FA of left cortico-spinal tract was associated with lower levels on four correlated symptom dimensions. Relatedly, using multimodal data fusion, Wang and colleagues (2023) clustered participants into distinct groups based on covariation in neural metrics and found that a group characterized by reduced FA across much of the brain had higher general levels of psychopathology, with the cortico-spinal tract serving as one of the most predictive features of group membership. These findings align with research from smaller studies and general theory suggesting that myelination of white matter tracts enervating the frontal lobe and subcortical structures during youth is central to both normative development and risk for mental illness (Ladouceur et al., 2012, Paus et al., 2008). Findings from ABCD extend this work, suggesting that cortical white matter diffusivity may impact psychopathology symptoms indirectly through executive functioning with Cardenas-Iniguez and colleagues (2022) finding indirect associations of externalizing (i.e. conduct) and ADHD-related dimensions with white matter diffusion via executive function in the superior longitudinal fasciculus, inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus, forceps major, and the body of the corpus callosum. Notably, however, they did not find any direct or indirect effects involving the cortico-spinal and cortico-striatal tracts, suggesting the associations observed by Christensen and colleagues (2021) may reflect another form of neuropsychological vulnerability to mental illness beyond executive functioning.

In summary, findings from the ABCD study suggest that psychopathology symptoms during the transition into adolescence are associated with alterations in both global white matter properties, as well as within white matter pathways linking frontal, cortical, and subcortical systems. Converging evidence implicates diffusivity of the inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus, cortico-spinal tract, and cortico-striatal pathways in psychopathology at the transition to adolescence, supporting developmental models positing that the maturation of structural connections critical to cognitive control and self-regulation is central to risk for psychopathology at this age. ABCD has extended previous work by providing greater specificity regarding the white matter tracts most consistently associated with psychopathology and by demonstrating that some associations between white matter microstructure and psychopathology may operate indirectly through executive functioning.

Alterations in default mode and dorsal attention network resting state connectivity are associated with general psychopathology

Adolescence is characterized by substantial reorganization of large-scale functional brain networks, including changes in the strength and organization of connections both within and between networks (Stevens., 2016). These developmental changes are thought to support increasingly complex cognitive, emotional, and social functioning and have long been hypothesized to contribute to vulnerability for psychopathology. In adults, psychopathology has most consistently been linked to the default mode network (DMN), frontoparietal network (FPN), and salience network (SN). The DMN is broadly associated with internally directed cognition and self-referential thought, the FPN supports cognitive control and goal-directed behavior, and the SN is involved in detecting behaviorally relevant stimuli and coordinating attentional shifts between internal and external modes of processing (Menon, 2011). A key unresolved question has been whether the network-level correlates of psychopathology observed in adulthood are already present at the transition into adolescence or instead emerge later in development. ABCD studies testing for associations between symptom dimensions and resting state functional connectivity confirmed a prominent role of the DMN across various psychopathology symptom dimensions (George et al., 2024, Hong et al., 2023, Karcher et al., 2021, Lees et al., 2021, Modabbernia et al., 2022, Owens et al., 2020, Royer et al., 2024, Sripada et al., 2021, Yang et al., 2022), while finding some evidence of associations of symptoms with FPN connectivity (e.g., George et al., 2024; Pacheco et al., 2025), yet very little evidence of associations with the SN proper. Thus, the ABCD study suggests psychopathology-related connectivity patterns involving the DMN frequently observed in adulthood have already begun to manifest in youth ages 9–10 years old, while associations with FPN connectivity and SN may be just beginning to emerge. However, while there was little evidence suggesting that psychopathology symptoms were associated with SN connectivity in ABCD, multiple studies found associations between symptoms and the cingulo-opercular network (CON; Modabbernia et al., 2022; Sripada et al., 2021), a network closely related to the SN implicated in maintaining task goals and performance monitoring. These findings raise the possibility that the CON may be more strongly implicated in psychopathology during early adolescence than the broader SN, although further work is needed to determine whether this reflects developmental differences or differences in network parcellation approaches.

Whereas previous research emphasized connectivity between the DMN and FPN as being central to psychopathology in adults, there was limited evidence of this in ABCD (however, see George et al., 2024; Sripada et al., 2021). Instead, several studies utilizing ABCD data suggest that psychopathology symptoms are associated with the DMN’s connectivity with the dorsal attention network (DAN) (Lees et al., 2021, Modabbernia et al., 2022, Owens et al., 2020, Sripada et al., 2021, Yang et al., 2022, Hong et al., 2023), a brain network involved in the allocation of attention toward behaviorally relevant information in the external environment and plays a central role in attentional control (Corbetta and Shulman, 2002). For instance, Owens and colleagues (2020) found that less of an anti-correlation between the DMN and DAN was associated with higher internalizing and externalizing symptoms while Modabbernia and colleagues (2022) found that a neurodevelopmental factor largely isomorphic with the p-factor was also associated with DMN-DAN connectivity. Further evidence from ABCD suggests that DAN properties may impact psychopathology independent from its connectivity with DMN, with multiple studies reporting associations of within-network DAN connectivity and psychopathology symptom levels (Lees et al., 2021, Hong et al., 2023, Yang et al., 2022). For instance, Hong and colleagues (2023) predicted psychopathology levels from both within- and between-network connectivity. Though overall predictive accuracy was relatively low (r < .2), within- and between-network connectivity of the DMN and DAN were found to be the most predictive features, suggesting both distinct and interactive links between these functional brain networks and youth mental health. While DMN-DAN connectivity has been previously implicated in adult psychopathology (Kaiser et al., 2015), results from the ABCD study suggest DMN-DAN dysfunction is evident during early adolescence and one of the most strongly associated connectivity-based markers of youth psychopathology symptom levels. This preferential association between psychopathology and the DAN may mirror the predominance of attentional problems during the baseline timepoint of ABCD, as compared to other forms of psychopathology which emerge later in adolescence. Previous theory posits the interactions between the DMN and DAN may contribute to attentional problems commonly implicated in mental illness due to their role in the balance between internally directed cognition (i.e., DMN) and externally directed attention (i.e., DAN). Reduced segregation between these networks may reflect an excessive influence of internal thoughts, emotions, or self-focused cognition on attentional systems responsible for engaging with the external environment and pursuing goals, resulting in increased attention towards internal states and subsequent impairments in ability to maintain attention to the external world.

Leveraging ABCD’s rich data on risk and resiliency factors, several studies found that DMN-related connectivity partially mediated associations between psychopathology symptoms and parenting styles (Davis et al., 2022), poverty (Pacheco et al., 2025), environmental unpredictability (Yang et al., 2024), neighborhood and social threat (Tsomokos et al., 2025, Vargas et al., 2025), and sleep (Mitchell and Nugiel, 2024), amongst others. These broad associations between DMN connectivity and both risk factors and symptoms of psychopathology in ABCD largely mirrors what was observed for total gray matter volume and surface area, raising the possibility that these two neural markers of psychopathology symptoms may be intricately linked, potentially with similar genetic and environmental influences. Indeed, Royer and colleagues (2024) multimodal analyses of latent neural and psychological dimensions within ABCD identified a latent neural component associated with higher p-factor levels that was characterized by both low global brain volume and alterations in DMN connectivity, suggesting that these two neural markers of psychopathology symptoms covary together. Having identified both low global brain volume and altered DMN connectivity as interrelated correlates of psychopathology in youth, future research is needed to further unpack these relations. For instance, it is unknown the degree to which these two neural markers of risk for psychopathology reflect common genetic risk for mental illness and whether they might respond to similar preventive or intervention techniques.

Taken together, ABCD studies largely confirm previous evidence implicating the DMN in transdiagnostic psychopathology while also providing a novel developmental perspective on large-scale network dysfunction. Specifically, ABCD findings suggest that interactions between the DMN and DAN may be more consistently associated with psychopathology during early adolescence than the FPN- and SN-centric models frequently emphasized in adult samples. Furthermore, evidence that DMN-related connectivity mediates associations between multiple environmental risk factors and psychopathology suggests that large-scale network organization may represent an important developmental pathway through which adversity contributes to mental illness risk.

Predictive utility of MRI in predicting symptom levels

One of the promises of MRI is that it will at some point be used as a tool to augment clinical practice, aiding clinicians by providing biomarkers of specific forms of psychopathology. Beyond testing associations between individual neural features and symptom dimensions, the scale and breadth of the ABCD dataset have enabled researchers to apply multivariate approaches that integrate information across multiple imaging features and modalities to evaluate the predictive utility of MRI for psychopathology. Such approaches are particularly important because psychopathology likely reflects distributed patterns of brain organization rather than abnormalities within any single brain region or network. To this end, several ABCD studies have utilized machine learning and multimodal fusion approaches to evaluate the degree to which MRI data improve prediction of psychopathology symptoms. Xia et al., (2025) evaluated the added value of using brain morphometry, structural connectivity, and resting state functional connectivity measures as features in neural network models predicting general psychopathology. While the inclusion of neuroimaging features improved predictive accuracy beyond demographic and clinical variables alone (r = .30 to r = .39), the improvement was relatively modest. Notably, the three modalities all had similar predictive accuracy on their own (rs<.2), but accuracy was slightly improved when combining the three (r = .24), suggesting that each domain has unique predictive power, albeit small. A similar pattern of results was observed when predicting more specific dimensions of psychopathology (i.e., externalizing, psychosis, anxiety, and fear). Relatedly, Chen and colleagues (2022) found that while functional connectivity was moderately predictive of individual differences in cognitive abilities (rs=.2–.5), it was only weakly predictive of a range of psychopathology symptom subdimensions (rs<.2). Similar results were found longitudinally, as Hill and colleagues (2025) found that neuroimaging provided no added value over behavioral and demographic measures when predicting which individuals would have the highest levels of p-factor at the year 4 timepoint, when participants were 14–15 years old.

Despite these apparent limitations of MRI in predicting psychopathology symptom levels within ABCD, there is evidence that neuroimaging may be more useful for identifying clinically meaningful subgroups than predicting continuous symptom dimensions. For instance, Wang and colleagues (2023) used functional connectivity measures to predict ADHD diagnoses and reported classification accuracies approaching 70%, while Gaus et al. (2023) found some predictive utility of structural MRI for identifying ADHD and bipolar disorder status. Similarly, recent work suggests that multimodal neuroimaging may aid in identifying biologically distinct subtypes of psychopathology (Chen et al., 2025). These findings raise the possibility that neuroimaging may ultimately prove more useful for characterizing heterogeneity within psychopathology than for predicting symptom severity per se.

In summary, multivariate studies utilizing ABCD data suggest that psychopathology is associated with distributed patterns of brain organization spanning multiple imaging modalities, but that MRI currently provides only modest improvements in prediction beyond behavioral and demographic measures. One of the most important lessons from ABCD is therefore not the discovery of highly predictive neural biomarkers, but rather the recognition that psychopathology is linked to many small neural effects distributed across multiple systems (e.g., Marek et al., 2022), at least at the transition to adolescence. Future work should continue leveraging multimodal and longitudinal approaches to determine whether patterns of brain development, rather than static neural features, provide more useful markers of psychopathology risk across adolescence.

Comparing internalizing and externalizing symptom dimensions

Comparing the neural correlates of internalizing and externalizing symptoms during youth may provide important insight into the mechanisms underlying heterogeneity in psychopathology. Although these symptom dimensions frequently co-occur and share common risk factors, they differ markedly in their behavioral presentation, developmental trajectories, and long-term outcomes. Importantly, internalizing symptoms generally increase across adolescence, whereas externalizing symptoms often emerge earlier and may decline with age, a pattern that has been replicated within ABCD (e.g., Smolker et al., 2022). Most studies comparing the neural correlates of internalizing and externalizing symptoms within ABCD have utilized data from the baseline timepoint when ABCD participants were 9–11 years old, meaning that levels of internalizing within the sample were relatively low compared to externalizing and attentional problems (Katsantonis, 2024, Roskam, 2019). A critical area of future research is utilizing later timepoints from the ABCD study to investigate the neural correlates of internalizing psychopathology later in adolescence when internalizing symptoms have begun to emerge at a greater rate. Nonetheless, ABCD research comparing the neural correlates of internalizing and externalizing at the baseline timepoint provide an important window into the biological bases of youth mental illness at the transition into adolescence.

Externalizing but not internalizing symptoms are associated with brain volume and area at the transition into adolescence

Across studies utilizing ABCD data, there is strong evidence of associations between gray matter morphometry and externalizing but not internalizing symptoms (Bounoua et al., 2023, Durham et al., 2021, Hughes et al., 2023, Modabbernia et al., 2022, Zhang et al., 2022). There are several possible explanations for the lack of associations with internalizing symptoms, with very different implications. First, the lack of associations with internalizing psychopathology may simply reflect the relatively low rates of internalizing psychopathology in the initial few ABCD timepoints, but particularly at the baseline timepoint, when youth were ages 9–10 years old. The relatively low variance in internalizing symptoms at the baseline timepoint may be compounded by potential reliability issues with parent-report measures of internalizing psychopathology, as externalizing symptoms are observable behaviors that parents can directly witness, whereas internalizing symptoms are largely internal experiences that may be less obvious to an outside observer. As a result, parental reports are generally considered to be more reliable for youths’ externalizing than internalizing symptoms, with previous research suggesting higher parent-youth agreement on reports of externalizing than internalizing symptoms, though cross-informant correlations for both symptom types are moderate at best (Go et al., 2022, De Los Reyes et al., 2015). To better address this issue, future studies utilizing the ABCD dataset can use multi-informant, multi-instrument approaches that integrate both parent- and self-report measures of symptoms, approaches that have begun to gain traction within the ABCD dataset already (e.g., Jacobs et al., 2024). At the same time, findings from other imaging modalities suggest that the relative absence of morphometric associations with internalizing symptoms may not be solely attributable to measurement issues. Several ABCD studies have linked internalizing symptoms to white matter microstructure (Cardenas-Iniguez et al., 2022) and functional connectivity (Brieant et al., 2021), suggesting that the neural correlates of internalizing psychopathology during early adolescence may be more apparent in measures of brain connectivity than in gross anatomical features such as volume and surface area. This interpretation is broadly consistent with developmental models proposing that internalizing psychopathology may emerge through alterations in the functional coordination of distributed neural systems involved in affective processing and emotion regulation rather than through widespread differences in brain morphology.

ABCD findings suggest that associations between gray matter morphometry and psychopathology are not uniform across symptom dimensions. Whereas externalizing symptoms show relatively robust associations with brain volume and surface area during early adolescence, associations between gray matter morphometry and internalizing symptoms are considerably weaker. Whether this reflects differences in developmental timing, measurement reliability, or genuinely distinct neurobiological mechanisms remains an important question for future research as ABCD participants progress through adolescence, when rates of internalizing psychopathology increase substantially. However, as described in the following sections, there is some evidence that internalizing symptoms are associated with other MRI metrics, suggesting distinct neurobiological mechanisms between internalizing and externalizing symptoms.

Cortical thickness at the transition into adolescence is differentially associated with change in internalizing and externalizing symptoms over time

Although MRI-derived measures of cortical thickness are influenced by multiple biological processes and do not map directly onto specific cellular mechanisms, they provide a useful tool for studying individual differences in brain development, with cortical thickness generally decreasing across adolescence, albeit in a non-linear, spatially dependent fashion (Natu et al., 2019, Tamnes et al., 2017, Tau and Peterson, 2010). Because cortical thickness undergoes substantial change throughout childhood and adolescence and has been linked to cognitive abilities, emotional regulation, and behavioral control, it has long been considered a potential marker of neurodevelopmental processes relevant to psychopathology. Interestingly, despite robust cross-sectional associations of reduced total brain volume and surface area with higher levels of general psychopathology, there has been very limited evidence from ABCD of cross-sectional associations between symptom dimensions and cortical thickness, particularly at the baseline timepoint, when participants were 9–10 years old. For instance, across both hierarchical factor model (Mewton et al., 2022, Romer et al., 2023) and manifest measures of symptom dimensions (Wainberg et al., 2022), no significant associations were observed between symptom dimensions and cortical thickness at baseline. Relatedly, cortical thickness across all ROIs only accounted for.5% variance in internalizing symptom dimension levels at the baseline timepoint (Tandberg et al., 2024). Thus, results from the ABCD study suggest that at the transition into adolescence, cortical thickness is not a reliable marker of increased psychopathology symptoms, at least not at that stage of development. However, the absence of cross-sectional associations does not imply that cortical thickness is unrelated to psychopathology. Rather, ABCD findings suggest that cortical thickness is more strongly associated with future symptom trajectories than with contemporaneous symptom levels. This distinction is particularly relevant within developmental psychopathology frameworks, which emphasize that risk for mental illness emerges through developmental processes unfolding over time rather than through static neural abnormalities. Supporting this interpretation, Romer and colleagues (2023) found that lower mean global cortical thickness at ages 9–10 years was associated with a greater decrease in internalizing symptoms across the subsequent two years. When examining regional associations while controlling for global thickness, similar effects were observed within early developing sensory and parietal regions. One possible interpretation is that cortical thickness in these regions captures individual differences in neurodevelopmental timing. Consistent with this possibility, ABCD studies have reported associations between more advanced pubertal development and lower cortical thickness in several cortical regions (Beck et al., 2023, Wiglesworth et al., 2023). Interestingly, associations with externalizing symptoms appear to follow a different pattern. Nakua et al., (2025) found that greater cortical thickness in late-developing lateral prefrontal regions at baseline was associated with larger reductions in externalizing symptoms across the subsequent two years. Although the neurodevelopmental mechanisms underlying this association remain uncertain, the lateral prefrontal cortex has long been implicated in executive functioning and behavioral control (Petrides, 2005). Thus, variation in the developmental status of prefrontal systems at the transition into adolescence may contribute to individual differences in the degree to which externalizing symptoms remit as youth mature.

In addition to linking cortical thickness at baseline to subsequent symptom trajectories, results from ABCD reinforce previous findings suggesting that the emergence of a range of psychiatric conditions is linked to altered trajectories of thinning during youth, particularly in frontal regions (e.g., Bos et al., 2018; Fortea et al., 2024; Oostermeijer et al., 2016). For instance, research utilizing data from ABCD found that change in left hemisphere cortical thickness from ages 9–11–11–13 predicted both parent- and youth-reported internalizing symptoms at age 12–14 (Cohen et al., 2025) while youth who met clinical criteria for an externalizing disorder showed decelerated cortical thinning across the first two imaging timepoints when compared to healthy controls (Yu et al., 2023). Thus, while cortical thickness may not be a reliable predictor of psychopathology symptoms at the transition into adolescence, findings from ABCD support the importance of neurodevelopmental changes in cortical thickness as being linked with the emergence of psychopathology in youth.

When considered together, these findings provide novel insight into the developmental nature of psychopathology risk. Whereas global brain volume and surface area appear to index relatively stable differences in psychopathology risk, cortical thickness appears to be more informative regarding the developmental trajectories of psychopathology across adolescence. Moreover, the distinct associations observed for internalizing and externalizing symptoms suggest that trajectories of psychopathology may be differentially related to the developmental status of early- and late-maturing cortical systems. These findings highlight the importance of considering developmental timing when investigating associations between brain structure and psychopathology, something that the ABCD dataset is specifically tailored to do.

Alterations in amygdala connectivity are associated with internalizing symptoms

The amygdala undergoes substantial functional reorganization throughout adolescence and plays a central role in processing emotionally salient information and coordinating responses to potential threats. Consequently, developmental changes in amygdala connectivity have long been hypothesized to contribute to risk for psychopathology (Cao et al., 2025). However, it remains an open question as to whether alterations in the amygdala are more closely linked to internalizing or externalizing symptoms. Previous findings testing for specificity are mixed, with some studies suggesting properties of the amygdala are associated with both internalizing and externalizing symptoms (e.g., Picci et al., 2022) while others suggesting it is specific to internalizing psychopathology (Neumann 2020).

Because emotional experiences depend not only on activity within the amygdala itself but also on communication between the amygdala and cortical systems involved in attention, emotion regulation, and cognitive control, alterations in amygdala connectivity may provide insight into neural mechanisms underlying vulnerability to psychopathology. Informing this debate, multiple studies utilizing ABCD data suggest that the strength of connectivity between the amygdala and other brain regions is associated with higher levels of internalizing psychopathology and may mediate or moderate associations between internalizing symptoms and related risk factors (Brieant et al., 2021, Gunther et al., 2023, Ip et al., 2022, Thomas et al., 2024) Most notably, Brieant and colleagues (2021) found that a greater number of stressful life events was associated with greater inverse connectivity between the amygdala and the CON, which was in turn associated with internalizing symptoms two years later. Importantly, they found that both cross-sectional and longitudinal associations between amygdala connectivity and psychopathology symptoms were unique to internalizing and not externalizing symptoms, providing compelling evidence that the amygdala connectivity may play a specific role in internalizing psychopathology in youth. Other studies found associations between amygdala connectivity with internalizing, but the degree to which these results were specific to internalizing and not shared across internalizing and externalizing was not directly tested. For instance, Thomas and colleagues (2024) found that multiple internalizing subdimensions were associated with connectivity of the amygdala with the auditory network, FPN, and DAN. Further supporting the idea that amygdala connectivity may be a neural link between adversity exposure and internalizing psychopathology, Ip and colleagues (2024) found that internalizing psychopathology was related to connectivity of the amygdala to the orbitofrontal cortex and that connectivity levels moderated associations between neighborhood deprivation and internalizing symptoms, while Gunther and colleagues (2023) found that amygdala to FPN connectivity moderated associations between inhibitory control and internalizing symptoms. These studies align with models that posit a specific role of the amygdala in internalizing symptoms as compared to externalizing symptoms, yet further research is needed comparing how properties of the amygdala differentially relate to internalizing and externalizing symptoms across adolescence.

Externalizing and internalizing symptoms are associated with alterations in fronto-striatal activity during cognitive control and reward tasks

Fronto-striatal circuitry has long been implicated in models of psychopathology across both youth and adult samples (Cole et al., 2014, Pujara and Koenigs, 2014). These circuits support a range of functions relevant to mental health, including cognitive control, reward processing, motivation, and the regulation of goal-directed behavior. Consequently, alterations in fronto-striatal function have been hypothesized to contribute to both externalizing and internalizing forms of psychopathology. Results from ABCD task-based fMRI studies demonstrate that internalizing and externalizing symptoms may be associated with alterations in partially overlapping fronto-striatal systems, but during distinct psychological operations. Specifically, externalizing symptoms appear most consistently related to fronto-striatal activation during cognitive control, whereas internalizing symptoms may be more strongly linked to fronto-striatal responses during reward processing. Linking scores on a hierarchical factors model with regional activation from the 2- vs 0-back contrast of the ENBack, Lees and colleagues (2021) found that all symptom dimensions were associated with reduced activation in the caudal middle frontal gyrus when cognitive control demands were high, a brain region commonly implicated in executive control. However, when controlling for the other lower-order dimensions, the externalizing factor was uniquely associated with greater activation in multiple regions of the striatum, including the putamen, pallidum, and nucleus accumbens, as well as the anterior cingulate, which shares strong bilateral connections with the striatum. Interestingly, no significant associations were observed with internalizing symptoms when controlling for the other lower-order dimensions, suggesting that alterations in fronto-striatal activation during the ENBack task may be unique to externalizing and not internalizing symptoms. Perlstein et al. (2024) provided a higher degree of specificity to these findings, differentiating distinct externalizing subdimensions and evaluating their associations with activation during the ENBack task. They not only found evidence of distinct regions associating with distinct externalizing subdimensions, but that these associations differed depending on the emotional valence of the facial stimuli. Specifically, when viewing fearful faces as compared to neutral faces, a general externalizing factor was associated with reduced amygdala activation, callous-unemotional traits were associated with reduced activation in the middle frontal gyrus, and attentional problems were associated with greater activation in the nucleus accumbens. However, when the facial stimuli were happy as compared to neutral, a conduct problems dimension was associated with increased activation in the fusiform gyrus, a brain area commonly implicated in face perception. Together, these results not only reinforce findings suggesting alterations in fronto-striatal activity in accordance with externalizing psychopathology but also suggest that these alterations may only manifest when youth must exert control over negatively valenced information.

Whereas results from ABCD suggest externalizing symptoms are associated with alterations in fronto-striatal activation during cognitive control over working memory, other findings from ABCD suggest that internalizing symptoms may also be associated with alterations in fronto-striatal activation, but during reward processing, as indexed by the MID task. Though McNeilly et al. (2022) did not find evidence of cross-sectional associations between internalizing symptoms and fronto-striatal activation during the MID task, longitudinal analyses conducted by Yang et al. (2023) found that greater ventral striatum activity during reward receipt at the baseline timepoint was associated with greater reductions in internalizing psychopathology over the following year. Somewhat paradoxically, they also found that activity levels in the ventral striatum moderated an association between family conflict and internalizing psychopathology such that in youth with high ventral striatum activation during reward receipt, greater family conflict was more strongly associated with increased internalizing symptoms over time whereas the opposite was true in youth with relatively low levels of ventral striatum activation. Together, these findings suggest that even though greater ventral striatum activity during reward receipt is predictive of lower internalizing psychopathology over time, it also suggests that ventral striatum reward sensitivity may be a marker of youth who are particularly susceptible to impacts of family conflict on internalizing psychopathology. It is important to note, however, that Yang and colleagues (2023) did not explicitly test whether these associations between striatal activation during reward receipt were unique to internalizing psychopathology, over and above externalizing psychopathology. This limitation can be easily addressed in future research by running all analyses across multiple forms of psychopathology instead of just focusing on one disorder or symptom dimension. In so doing, researchers can gain further insight into whether associations between the brain and psychopathology in ABCD are constrained to certain forms of psychopathology or instead capture more general aspects of mental illness that are shared across most disorders. Such information may have important implications for understanding heterogeneity in mental illness, and the neural mechanisms through which symptoms of mental illness at the onset of adolescence may differentiate into more specific forms of mental illness as youth age.

One of the unique contributions of ABCD has been demonstrating that task-based fMRI may reveal associations with psychopathology that are less apparent in structural MRI and resting-state analyses. Whereas many structural and resting-state findings implicate broad, distributed markers of psychopathology, task-based paradigms provide greater specificity regarding the psychological processes through which these neural systems may contribute to symptom dimensions. ABCD findings suggest that fronto-striatal dysfunction may manifest differently across internalizing and externalizing symptoms depending on whether youth are engaged in cognitive control or reward-related processing.

Conclusions and future directions

Summary

The current review surveyed studies utilizing the ABCD dataset to characterize associations between MRI measures and transdiagnostic dimensions of psychopathology during the transition from childhood into adolescence. Several broad themes emerge that refine current models of developmental psychopathology.

First, ABCD studies suggest that general psychopathology is associated with broad aspects of brain organization rather than highly localized neural abnormalities. Across studies, higher levels of general psychopathology were consistently associated with reduced total brain volume and surface area, altered connectivity involving the default mode and dorsal attention networks, and differences in white matter pathways linking frontal and subcortical systems. Although many of these findings are consistent with previous reports, ABCD has provided the statistical power necessary to demonstrate that these associations are generally small in magnitude yet reproducible.

Second, ABCD findings suggest that different dimensions of psychopathology show partially distinct neural correlates. Whereas externalizing symptoms were more consistently associated with brain morphology and fronto-striatal activation during cognitive control tasks, internalizing symptoms were more commonly associated with amygdala connectivity and reward-related neural processes. These findings suggest that broad dimensions of psychopathology may emerge through both shared and partially distinct neurodevelopmental pathways.

Third, ABCD findings highlight the importance of developmental timing. While measures such as total brain volume appear to index relatively stable differences in psychopathology risk, cortical thickness showed little evidence of cross-sectional associations with symptoms at ages 9–10 but was repeatedly associated with future symptom trajectories. These findings suggest that some neural features may be more informative regarding developmental change than current symptom burden and underscore the value of longitudinal developmental frameworks for understanding psychopathology.

Finally, ABCD studies increasingly support developmental psychopathology models positing that mental illness risk emerges through interactions among genetic, environmental, and neurodevelopmental factors. Across multiple studies, neural measures partially mediated associations between psychopathology and diverse risk factors including adversity exposure, family environment, neighborhood characteristics, sleep, cognition, and genetic liability. These findings suggest that broad aspects of brain organization may serve as intermediate pathways through which environmental and biological risk factors contribute to psychopathology during adolescence.

Future directions

Although ABCD has begun to provide novel insights into the neurodevelopmental bases of youth psychopathology, several important questions remain unresolved. Most fundamentally, it remains unclear how the neural correlates identified during early adolescence evolve as participants progress through mid- and late-adolescence, when rates of internalizing disorders increase substantially and more youth begin meeting criteria for psychiatric diagnoses. Although ABCD findings suggest that internalizing and externalizing symptoms show partially distinct neural correlates by ages 9–10, it remains unclear whether these differences reflect stable, disorder-specific neurobiological pathways or emerge gradually from shared transdiagnostic liabilities. As additional ABCD timepoints become available, researchers will be able to determine how neural correlates of broad psychopathology evolve across development and whether specific patterns of brain development predict the emergence of specific mental health disorders. For instance, data from later ABCD timepoints can be used to determine what factors may drive different psychopathology trajectories in individuals who show neural markers of general risk at the baseline timepoint, such as lower total brain volume or altered DMN-DAN connectivity. Such information may provide critical insights into the etiology of mental illness, including what factors drive the differentiation from general risk to specific disorders. Relatedly, future work should increasingly utilize multimodal and multivariate approaches to identify biologically meaningful subtypes of psychopathology and understand why similar symptoms can arise through different neurodevelopmental pathways.

A related priority is determining the precise role that neural measures play in linking risk exposure to psychopathology. While many ABCD studies have identified associations among environmental risk factors, brain measures, and mental health outcomes, it often remains unclear whether neural features function as mediators through which risk factors influence psychopathology, moderators that alter individuals' susceptibility to risk, or consequences of psychopathology and adversity themselves. Distinguishing among these possibilities is essential for developing mechanistic models of mental illness and identifying optimal targets for prevention and intervention. The longitudinal design of ABCD provides a unique opportunity to disentangle these pathways by examining how changes in environmental exposures, brain development, and psychopathology unfold over time. Additionally, other techniques for interrogating causality may be particularly useful in ABCD, such as Mendelian randomization analyses.

Future work utilizing ABCD should focus on elucidating not only the impacts of risk factors on brain development and psychopathology, but also the neurobiological bases of resilience. Much of the existing ABCD literature has focused on identifying factors that increase risk for psychopathology, including adversity exposure, neighborhood disadvantage, family conflict, sleep disturbances, and genetic liability. However, many youth exposed to these risk factors do not develop clinically significant symptoms, suggesting that resilience is not merely the absence of risk but may instead reflect distinct developmental processes that buffer against psychopathology. Despite the unique capacity of the ABCD dataset to address this question, relatively little research has examined the neural pathways through which protective factors promote positive mental health outcomes.

The rich phenotyping of ABCD provides an unprecedented opportunity to investigate a broad range of resilience factors, including supportive family relationships, positive peer interactions, extracurricular engagement, physical activity, sleep quality, cognitive functioning, and neighborhood resources. Future studies should move beyond identifying whether these factors are associated with lower symptom levels and instead investigate how they shape neurodevelopmental trajectories. ABCD provides a unique opportunity to shift developmental psychopathology research from a predominantly deficit-based framework toward one focused on adaptive functioning and positive development. Understanding how protective environmental factors, cognitive strengths, and supportive social relationships become embedded in the developing brain may ultimately prove as important as understanding the mechanisms through which risk factors contribute to mental illness. Identifying neural pathways that support resilience could inform prevention and intervention efforts aimed at promoting healthy development before the onset of clinically significant psychopathology.

Finally, ABCD findings suggest that future research should move beyond searching for highly predictive neural biomarkers of symptom severity. Multivariate studies consistently indicate that MRI contributes only modestly to prediction beyond behavioral and demographic measures. Rather than viewing this as a limitation, these findings suggest that the greatest value of neuroimaging may lie in characterizing developmental mechanisms, identifying biologically meaningful subgroups, and understanding how environmental and genetic risk factors become embedded within the developing brain.

Concluding remarks

Even prior to its completion, the ABCD study has had an immense impact on our understanding of the interplay between youth brain and behavior, both confirming and extending previous models of psychopathology during adolescence. As data from additional ABCD timepoints become available and more researchers begin to utilize this unique resource, the impact of the ABCD study will almost certainly grow in both magnitude and nuance. Critically, this dataset has the potential to make important discoveries that inform how we think about adolescent development and how we promote mental health not just during this dynamic developmental stage but across the lifespan. Due to this immense potential, researchers should prioritize using data from the ABCD study whenever possible, with an emphasis on conducting longitudinal analyses linking multimodal imaging data with genetic, environmental, and psychopathology domains to characterize the neurobiological mechanisms driving both typical and atypical adolescent development.
