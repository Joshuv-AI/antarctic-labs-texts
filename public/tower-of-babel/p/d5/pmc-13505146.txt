Molecular detection of pathogens associated with childhood gastroenteritis in Burkina Faso using the FilmArray® GI panel

Background

Diarrhoeal diseases, caused by various pathogens such as bacteria and viruses, are a major public health issue worldwide, responsible for more than 1 million deaths each year [1]. These diseases are among the leading causes of death in children younger than 5 years, particularly in low-income and middle-income countries. The etiology of acute gastroenteritis (AGE) is complex, involving a wide spectrum of viral, bacterial, and parasitic pathogens. Rotavirus, norovirus, adenovirus, and sapovirus are among the most frequently implicated viral agents, while diarrheagenic Escherichia coli, Salmonella, and Campylobacter represent major bacterial contributors. Protozoa such as Giardia lamblia and Cryptosporidium further complicate the epidemiological landscape [2, 3]. Although rotavirus vaccination has reduced severe disease in many regions, viral gastroenteritis continues to pose a significant public health challenge [4, 5].

In low-income countries, such as Burkina Faso, poor collective and individual hygiene conditions and a lack of safe drinking water are key factors influencing the profile of infectious diarrhea [4]. These factors result in recurrent consultations and hospitalizations, with a negative impact on household finances. Conventional diagnostic methods, such as stool culture, are time-consuming (≥ 24 h) and often fail to detect viral pathogens. Recently, syndromic multiplex PCR systems such as the FilmArray® GI panel have enabled simultaneous detection of up to 22 enteropathogens with high sensitivity and specificity [6].

Despite the high burden of diarrhoeal disease, data on the epidemiology of childhood diarrhea in Burkina Faso remain limited, particularly those generated using molecular diagnostic methods. We therefore undertook this study to investigate the etiology of AGE in hospitalized children under five years of age at Bogodogo University Hospital, using the FilmArray® GI panel. The study aimed to document pathogen prevalence, age distribution, and associated clinical signs, thereby providing supplementary information to the scientific community and policymakers to support the implementation of effective surveillance and management strategies for childhood diarrhea in Burkina Faso. To our knowledge, this is the first study reporting the use of the FilmArray® GI panel for pathogen detection in pediatric infectious diarrhea in Burkina Faso.

Methods

Sampling

The study was carried out in Ouagadougou from December 2017 to November 2018. Pediatric ward of the Bogodogo University Hospital (BUH) was used for samples collection. A total of 90 samples (approximately 3 to 5 ml or 3 to 5 g) were obtained of diarrheal stool from children under 5 years of age hospitalized for gastroenteritis.

Inclusion criteria

Children aged 0 to 59 months admitted with diarrhea to the emergency or hospitalized with acute or chronic diarrhea as the main reason for consultation in the pediatric ward of the Bogodogo University Hospital were included. The patient’s parents or guardians provided informed consent.

Exclusion criteria

Children older than 59 months at admission were excluded. Patients hospitalized for more than 24 h in another facility before transfer to Bogodogo University Hospital, as well as those with diarrhea acquired during hospitalization (nosocomial infection), were also excluded.

Clinical sociodemographic data collection

Sociodemographic and clinical information was collected using standardized forms adapted from the rotavirus diarrhea surveillance system. These forms were completed by healthcare staff with verbal consent from the mother or accompanying guardian. Data included patient identity, medical history, vaccination status, parental marital status, living conditions, hospitalization outcome, and laboratory test results. Stool specimens were analyzed using the FilmArray Gastrointestinal (GI) Panel system (BioFire Diagnostics, Salt Lake City, UT, USA), a multiplex PCR assay designed to simultaneously detect twenty-two pathogens associated with acute gastroenteritis. Enteropathogens can be bacteria, viruses, or parasites. The bacterial targets comprised Campylobacter species (C. jejuni, C. coli, C. upsaliensis), Clostridioides difficile (toxin A/B), Plesiomonas shigelloides, Salmonella spp., Yersinia enterocolitica, and Vibrio species (V. parahaemolyticus, V. vulnificus, V. cholerae), as well as Vibrio cholerae specifically, Enteroaggregative E. coli (EAEC), Enteropathogenic E. coli (EPEC), Enterotoxigenic E. coli (ETEC, lt/st), Shiga toxin–producing E. coli (STEC, stx1/stx2), E. coli O157, and Shigella/Enteroinvasive E. coli (EIEC). Viral detection included Adenovirus F40/41, Astrovirus, Norovirus GI/GII, Rotavirus A, and Sapovirus genogroups I, II, IV, and V. Parasitic detection encompassed Cryptosporidium spp., Cyclospora cayetanensis, Entamoeba histolytica, and Giardia lamblia.

Sample processing and detection

The samples were processed using the FilmArray GI panel according to Biofire Diagnostic’s instructions. A 200 µL aliquot of stool was inverted three times with sample buffer (provided with the GI kit) to ensure homogenization and loaded into the FilmArray GI cassette. The cassette was inserted into the FilmArray. Two controls (RNA process control and a PCR control) were included with each cassette run. The system software automatically analyzed and interpreted the results and indicated if a pathogen had been detected or not detected or if the result was invalid.

Statistical analysis

Data were managed using Microsoft Excel 2016. Statistical analyses were performed using R software version 3.6. Sample distribution was explored using a correspondence analysis biplot. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study population and clinical characteristics

A total of 90 children under five years of age were enrolled. The majority 64.4% (58/90) were younger than 12 months, 31.1% (28/90) were aged 13–24 months, and 4.5% (4/90) were aged 25–59 months. Males accounted for 54.4% (49/90) and females for 45.6% (41/90), yielding a sex ratio (F/M) of 0.84 (49/41). (Table 1).

Overall pathogen detection

At least one pathogen was detected in 74.4% (67/90) of children. Single pathogen infections represented 44.7% (30/67) of positive cases, while co-infections accounted for 55.2% (37/67). Fever was the most frequent symptom in children with gastroenteritis (56.6%; 51/90), followed by vomiting (52.2%; 47/90) and dehydration (37.7%; 34/90) (Table 1).

Symptom profiles by pathogen type

Among viral infections, vomiting was the most common symptom, occurring in 78.3% (18/23) of cases, followed by fever in 69.6% (16/23) and dehydration in 43.5% (10/23) (Fig. 1). Similarly, in bacterial infections, vomiting was also the predominant symptom, reported in 75% (9/12) of cases, followed by dehydration in 66.6% (8/12) and fever in 50% (6/12) (Fig. 1). As for the parasitic infections, fever was observed in all cases (100%; 4/4), while vomiting occurred in 50% (2/4) and dehydration in 25% (1/4) of cases (Fig. 1). However, in co-infections, fever and vomiting were the most frequently reported symptoms, each occurring in 75.6% (28/37) of cases, followed by dehydration in 48.6% (18/37) (Fig. 1).

Prevalence of pathogen groups

Viruses and bacteria were equally prevalent, each detected in 58.8% (53/90) of cases, while parasites were identified in 30% (27/90) (Table 2.) Rotavirus was the most common viral agent, detected in 22.2% (20/90), followed by norovirus in 15.5% (14/90), adenovirus in 11.1% (10/90), sapovirus in 7.7% (7/90), and astrovirus in 2.2% (2/90) (Table 2). Among bacterial pathogens, diarrheagenic E. coli (DEC) pathovars predominated, accounting for 48.8% (44/90). Enteroaggregative E. coli (EAEC) was most frequent at 27.7% (25/90), followed by enteropathogenic E. coli (EPEC) at 8.8% (8/90), enterotoxigenic E. coli (ETEC) at 7.7% (7/90), and Shigella/enteroinvasive E. coli (EIEC) at 2.2% (2/90). Rare isolates included E. coli O157:H7 and Shiga toxin-producing E. coli (STEC), each detected in 1.1% (1/90). Other bacterial agents identified were Campylobacter spp. in 6.6% (6/90) and Salmonella spp. in 3.3% (3/90), while Clostridium difficile, Plesiomonas shigelloides, Yersinia enterocolitica, Vibrio parahaemolyticus, and Vibrio cholerae were not detected (Table 2). Parasites were present in 30% (27/90) of samples, with Giardia lamblia most frequent at 16.6% (15/90, p < 0.05), followed by Cryptosporidium spp. at 13.3% (12/90).

Age-specific distribution of pathogens

As shown in Table 2, in children aged 0–12 months, viral infections predominated 55.1%; (32/58), with rotavirus 22.4% (13/58), norovirus 18.9% (11/58), and adenovirus 8.6% (5/58) most frequent. Bacteria were detected in 44.8% (26/58), and parasites in 27.5% (16/58). In children aged 13–24 months, bacterial infections were most common 82.1% (23/28), followed by viruses 67.8% (19/28). DEC was the leading bacterial agent 64.3% (18/28), with EAEC 28.5% (8/28), ETEC 17.8% (5/28), and EPEC 17.8% (5/28) most frequent. Campylobacter spp. and Salmonella spp. were each detected in 7.1% (2/28). In children aged 25–59 months, DEC remained predominant, with EPEC detected in 50% (2/4), EAEC in 25% (1/4), and EIEC in 25% (1/4). Parasites were also common in this age group, with Giardia lamblia detected in 50% (2/4) and Cryptosporidium spp. in 25% (1/4). Viral agents were less frequent; with norovirus and adenovirus each identified in 25% (1/4).

Patterns of single and mixed infections

Single infections were mainly viral (66.6%; 20/30, p < 0.05). Rotavirus was the leading agent (43.3%; 13/30), followed by norovirus (13.3%; 4/30). Bacterial single infections were exclusively DEC, predominantly EAEC (20%; 6/30) and EPEC (6.6%; 2/30). Parasitic single infections were rare, with Cryptosporidium spp. detected in 6.6% (2/30) (Table 2).

Mixed infections were observed in 37.7% (34/90, p < 0.05) of children under 24 months. These included virus–bacteria 8.8% (8/90) virus–bacteria–parasite 7.7% (7/90), and virus–virus or parasite–parasite 2.2% (2/90). EAEC was the most frequent bacterial agent in mixed infections (20%; 18/90, p < 0.05), followed by Giardia lamblia (16.6%; 15/90). Norovirus was the leading viral agent in mixed infections (11.1%; 10/90). Mixed infections with two pathogens occurred in 22.2% (20/90), while three or more pathogens were detected in 17.7% (16/90). The most frequent pathogen combinations were Cryptosporidium–norovirus (11.1%; 10/90, p < 0.05), rotavirus–ETEC (7.7%; 7/90), and Campylobacter–EPEC (6.6%; 6/90) (supplementary Table).

Seasonality of gastroenteritis pathogens

Viral diarrhea cases peaked between December and March, corresponding to the dry and relatively cool season (Fig. 2) Significant seasonal associations were observed for Cryptosporidium (p = 0.0007), rotavirus (p = 0.0012), and sapovirus (p = 0.0099). The correspondence analysis biplot indicated overlapping distributions of pathogens across seasons, with multiple-pathogen infections clustering along dimension 2, reflecting their complex epidemiological patterns (Fig. 3).

Discussion

This study sought to describe the prevalence of pathogens responsible for childhood diarrhea detected by the FilmArray® GI Panel in Burkina Faso. At least one pathogen was identified in 74.4% (67/90) of stool samples, a detection rate slightly higher than the 64.7% reported in Côte d’Ivoire using the Luminex xTAG GPP [7]. Traditional stool-based diagnostic methods, constrained by limited resources, often fail to capture the full spectrum of enteric pathogens, leading to variable accuracy depending on institutional capacity [8]. In contrast, the FilmArray® GI Panel simultaneously identifies 22 enteropathogens, providing comprehensive insights into diarrheal etiologies and supporting targeted public health interventions [9]. However, some authors have noted limitations, as molecular detection may identify non-viable organisms, colonization, or asymptomatic shedding, raising concerns about clinical relevance and potential overtreatment [10–12].

The mean age of children enrolled was 12 months, with infants under 12 months most affected (64.4%). Similar findings were previously reported in Burkina Faso, with enteric pathogens detected in 70.16% of children under five years of age presenting with gastroenteritis at three hospitals in Ouagadougou between November 2011 and September 2012: Bogodogo District Hospital (61 children), Les Tissérins Pediatric Clinic (128 children), and Paul VI Medical Center with Surgical Unit (74 children).) [13]. Factors contributing to this high burden include malnutrition, early weaning, and immature immunity. Weaning reduces maternal antibody protection, and when not accompanied by adequate nutrition, increases susceptibility to gastroenteritis.

Rotavirus was the leading viral pathogen (22.2%) in this study, though lower than the 53.9% reported in the Democratic Republic of Congo [14]. The introduction of the RV5 vaccine into Burkina Faso’s immunization program in 2013 has reduced rotavirus prevalence, from 36% in 2014 to 20% in 2016, though rotavirus remains a major contributor to infant diarrhea [15]. While norovirus also played a significant role (15.5%) in the current study, previous surveillance detected norovirus in 20.9% of children under 5 years of age presenting with gastroenteritis at the Bogodogo District Hospital from November 2011 to September 2012 [13]. Additional active surveillance of AGE conducted at the Bogodogo District Hospital and Yalgado Ouédraogo University Hospital in Ouagadougou, and a rural hospital in Gaoua in the South-West region from December 2012 to February 2017 reported norovirus prevalence of 23% [15]. Similarly, norovirus prevalence of 21% was reported in Bobo-Dioulasso among 453 sampled individuals, including 319 patients with diarrheal symptoms and 134 control subjects without diarrhea [16].

Foodborne bacteria were the most frequently detected pathogens (58.8%). Diarrheagenic Escherichia coli (DEC) was predominant, particularly EAEC (27.7%). Campylobacter spp. (6.6%) and Salmonella spp. (3.3%) were also detected, similar to findings in Trinidad and Tobago [17]. These pathogens are often associated with severe gastroenteritis and remain a major public health concern in low- and middle-income countries [18].

Parasites were detected in 30% of cases, with Giardia lamblia (16.6%) and Cryptosporidium spp. (13.3%) most frequent. Their high prevalence reflects poor hygiene, limited access to safe drinking water, and inadequate sanitation in Burkina Faso.

Mixed infections were common, accounting for 55.2% of positive cases, higher than rates reported in Côte d’Ivoire (45.4%) [7] and the USA (31.5%) [10]. Mixed virus–bacteria infections represented 8%, while virus–virus mixed infections accounted for 2.2%. Similar trends were reported in the United States, with virus-bacteria (7%), virus-virus (4%), and bacteria-bacteria (3%) [19]. These findings highlight the complexity of diarrheal disease and the importance of multiplex molecular diagnostics to distinguish true infections from asymptomatic carriage [20].

Seasonal variation was evident, with viral infections peaking during the dry and cold season (December–March). Rotavirus infections, although present year-round, showed peak prevalence during this period, consistent with reports from Ghana [21]. Similar seasonal trends have been observed in Guinea-Bissau [22]. and other African countries [23]. In the United States, viral pathogens predominate in winter and bacterial pathogens in summer [19].

Limitations and constraints of our study

This molecular detection technique has certain limitations, as it does not allow for the identification of the complete cellular structure of the microorganism, which is essential for further characterization of its properties and for improving patient management. For example, the isolation of pathogens such as Salmonella and Campylobacter would enable antimicrobial susceptibility testing, which is increasingly important for monitoring multidrug resistance.

Similarly, PCR assays are highly sensitive detection methods, which may result in higher detection rates of enteric pathogens associated with asymptomatic infection or prolonged carriage, that are not the causative agents of the acute diarrheal illness, particularly in cases involving three or more pathogens. we strongly recommend that future studies include a control group in order to distinguish asymptomatic carriage from the actual pathogenic potential of the microorganism. Molecular techniques may detect traces of a microorganism even when it is not actively responsible for diarrheal disease at the time of detection.

Thirdly, the FilmArray Gastroenteritis Panel has certain limitations in gene identification accuracy. It does not specify the norovirus genogroup, the Campylobacter species, or the Salmonella pathovar. The test also cannot distinguish between EIEC and Shigella because the target gene ipaH is present in both pathogens. However, identifying circulating serotypes and genogroups is important for improving vaccine formulation and strengthening preventive strategies.

Fourthly, sampling across multiple sites would have allowed for better generalization of our findings and improved data accuracy through a larger sample size. The relatively small sample size may have affected the precision of prevalence estimates, as well as the assessment of infectious risk factors, sociodemographic characteristics, and clinical data. This resulted in a small number of isolates, particularly among parasites, thereby inflating the prevalence estimates in certain groups, especially the 25–59 months age group.

Conclusion

This study highlights key pathogens associated with childhood gastroenteritis in Burkina Faso, notably diarrheagenic Escherichia coli, rotavirus, and Giardia lamblia. The FilmArray® GI Panel proved valuable for rapid, broad-spectrum detection, complementing conventional diagnostics and supporting case management.

Yet, the small sample size, frequent mixed infections, and the high sensitivity of PCR assays which may detect non-causative organisms or asymptomatic carriage require cautious interpretation. Future studies with larger cohorts, inclusion of control groups will be essential to validate these findings, distinguish true causative agents from non-causative organisms or asymptomatic carriage and strengthen surveillance capacity.
