Incidence and predictors of tuberculosis among adult diabetes mellitus patients in western Amhara comprehensive specialized hospitals, northwest Ethiopia. A multicenter retrospective follow-up study
Authors: Jember Ayelgne Beyene, Zemene Mesafint Kassie, Nebiyu Mekonnen Derseh, Solomon Gedlu Nigatu, Fantahun Ayenew Mekonnen, Ayana Dinku Abebe, Yeshiwas Ayale Ferede, Agerie Mengistie Zeleke, Worku Chekol Tassew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTuberculosis (TB) is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, and Ethiopia is among the 30 high TB-burden countries. People with diabetes mellitus (DM) have a 2–3 times higher risk of developing TB than those without DM. However, the incidence and predictors of TB among adults with DM in the western Amhara region remain unclear. This study aimed to determine the incidence rate and predictors of TB among adult DM patients.MethodsA multi-center, institution-based retrospective follow-up study was conducted among 750 adult DM patients. Bivariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards models were used to identify predictors of TB. Variables with a p-value < 0.05 in the multivariate analysis were considered independent predictors..ResultsAmong 750 participants, 67 (8.9%) developed TB, with an incidence rate of 27 per 1,000 person-years of observation (95% CI: 17.8–28.8). Significant predictors of TB included body mass index (BMI) < 18.5 kg/m2 (AHR = 1.87; 95% CI: 1.00–3.47), HIV positivity (AHR = 4.71; 95% CI: 2.68–8.26), cancer (AHR = 4.37; 95% CI: 1.54–12.39), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) (AHR = 2.53; 95% CI: 1.23–5.21), and a history of close contact with TB patients (AHR = 6.53; 95% CI: 3.15–13.51)..ConclusionThe incidence of TB among adult DM patients in western Amhara is high. Low BMI, HIV infection, cancer, COPD, and close contact with TB patients are significant predictors of TB in this population.Keywords: incidence rate, tuberculosis, predictors, diabetes mellitus, Ethiopia

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a group of metabolic diseases characterized by hyperglycemia due to defective insulin secretion and/or action. 1 It is a chronic metabolic disease in which the hormone insulin is not produced in sufficient quantities by the pancreas or is not used effectively by the body to control blood sugar levels. 2 According to the Global Burden of Diabetes Mellitus (2024–2025), 589 million adults between the ages of 20 and 79 have diabetes. 3 853 million by 2050. It is anticipated that 853 million people worldwide will have diabetes by that time. 4 Over 4 in 5 adults (81%) with diabetes live in low- and middle-income countries. 5 An estimated 43% of adults with diabetes (252 million people) are unaware they have the condition. 6 According to the most recent estimates, 24–25 million adults in Africa between the ages of 20 and 79 have diabetes (2024). Lifestyle changes like urbanization, dietary changes, decreased physical activity, and demographic shifts are the main causes of the rising prevalence of diabetes in Africa. 7 By 2050, the number of adults with diabetes in Africa is expected to almost double. 8 According to the International Diabetes Federation (IDF), 2.3 million adults (aged 20–79 years) are living with diabetes in Ethiopia in 2024. 9

Tuberculosis (TB) is a communicable disease that is a major cause of disease and a leading cause of death worldwide. 10 TB is a critical public health issue worldwide, especially in developing countries. TB is among the top 10 causes of death globally, and people living with DM are at increased risk of developing active TB, which remains an important public health concern and a leading cause of morbidity, treatment failure, and death.11,12 It is an airborne infectious disease caused by a microorganism called Mycobacterium tuberculosis. 2 According to the 2020 WHO Global TB Report, an estimated 10.0 million people are ill with TB, with 1.2 million TB deaths among HIV-negative people. 13 The Global Tuberculosis Report estimates that in 2020, there were 1.3 million TB deaths among HIV-negative people (up from 1.2 million in 2019) and another 214,000 in HIV-positive people (up from 209,000 in 2019), with the combined total returning to 2017 levels. 14

Although the association between diabetes mellitus (DM) and tuberculosis (TB) has long been recognized, research conducted in the last ten to fifteen years has demonstrated that DM (both type 1 and type 2) raises the risk of active TB and that patients with dual disease have worse treatment outcomes than those with TB alone. Thus, the rapidly expanding DM epidemic in low- and middle-income nations poses a threat to TB control initiatives and has the potential to undo progress toward the Sustainable Development Goal of eradicating TB by 2030. 2 People with diabetes mellitus (DM) are at greater risk of developing tuberculosis (TB) than people without diabetes mellitus (DM). 15 An estimated 0.37 million incident cases of TB were attributable to DM. 16 Compared with people without diabetes, people with diabetes are more likely to fail treatment and die during treatment. 17 People with DM have a 1.5-fold increased risk of developing TB. 18 Diabetes mellitus (DM) increases the risk of tuberculosis (TB) by 2–3 times and increases the risk of adverse TB treatment outcomes. 14 People with diabetes are 2 to 3 times more likely to develop TB than people without diabetes. 2 People with tuberculosis and coexisting diabetes are more likely to be sputum positive, and people with TB and coexisting diabetes are four times more likely to die during TB treatment and have a greater risk of TB returning after treatment. 2

In a population-based multicenter cohort study carried out in China, patients with diabetes mellitus (DM) had a clinically diagnosed TB incidence rate of 250.6 per 100,000 person-years, whereas the same group had a bacteriologically confirmed TB incidence rate of 47.0 per 100,000 person-years. 19 The overall incidence rate of tuberculosis (TB) among adults with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) was 51.3 per 100,000 person-years, which is significantly higher than in the general population, according to a retrospective cohort study carried out in Shanghai, China. However, this particular study did not report an incidence of 119.85 per 100,000 person-years. 20 A study conducted in an Australian state found that the incidence of TB was 5.8/100,000 and the adjusted RRs were 1.48–2.27. 21 Both TB incidence and TB mortality are impacted by diabetes prevalence; a 2020 WHO report estimated that 370,000 new cases of TB were caused by diabetes. 22 A systematic review and meta-analysis carried out in Asia found that the pooled overall prevalence of tuberculosis in patients with diabetes was 4.16–4.72 percent. 13 The prevalence of tuberculosis ranges from 0.8 to 4.3 percent in different African countries.23,24 Previous research has linked drug resistance and smoking to incident tuberculosis and kidney disease, as well as male sex, high body mass index, treatment success, follow-up, diabetes mellitus (DM), organ transplants, cancer, and indoor air pollution from biomass fuels.25–30 A recent systematic review and meta-analysis in Ethiopia found that the pooled prevalence of TB among people with diabetes mellitus was approximately 4.14 % (≈4140 per 100,000), substantially higher than the general population rate, and highlighted the bidirectional TB-DM burden in the country. 31

Despite the increasing worldwide public burden of DM, data on the incidence of TB among adult people with diabetes mellitus are limited, particularly in Africa and including Ethiopia. A systematic review including different countries worldwide revealed scarce data about the epidemiology of TB and recommended further studies on the incidence of TB.18,26,29,32–36

In Ethiopia, studies have been conducted to assess the prevalence and associated factors of TB among individuals with DM. 33 However, these studies did not adequately measure the incidence of TB among adult people with diabetes mellitus and did not sufficiently investigate predictors, missing several potentially important variables such as history of cancer, chemotherapy, hypertension, heart failure, and renal failure. They were also constrained by limited sample sizes and methodological challenges. Therefore, this study aims to assess the incidence rate of TB and its predictors among adult people with diabetes mellitus, thereby addressing these gaps. The results are expected to inform clearer strategies for earlier TB screening among people with DM, guide clinical and public health interventions, and support policymakers in the development of integrated TB–DM guidelines that promote active case finding and co-management of these dual burdens.

Methods and materials

Study area

The study was conducted in public comprehensive specialized hospitals in the western part of the Amhara region, Northwestern Ethiopia. The Amhara Region is one of the largest regions of Ethiopia; it has 12 zones, three city administrations and 180 districts (139 rural and 41 urban). The Ethiopian Central Statistics Agency estimates that there are 21.5 million people living in the region, with about 80% of them being rural farmers. 37 The region has 80 hospitals (8 comprehensive specialized hospitals, 3 general hospitals and 69 primary hospitals), 847 health centers and 3,342 health posts. There are 5 comprehensive specialized hospitals in the western part of the Amhara region: the University Of Gondar Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (UGCSH), the Felege Hiwot Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (FHCSH), the Debre Tabor Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (DTCSH), the Tibebe Ghion Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (TGCSH) and the Debre Markos Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (DMCSH). The catchment area population of each comprehensive specialized hospital is estimated to be 5–7 million people. The hospitals have both inpatient and outpatient departments for surgery, internal medicine, pediatrics, obstetrics and gynecology, psychiatry, dentistry, and orthopedics. The total number of diabetic patients who received chronic follow-up care from January 1, 2018, to December 31, 2022, were 6000, 5000, 4000 and 3500 at the University of Gondar Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Felege Hiwot Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Debre Tabor Comprehensive Specialized Hospital and Debre Markos Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, respectively.

We acknowledge that including participants from four hospitals with potentially different TB incidence rates could introduce facility-level clustering and influence the estimated hazard ratios.

Baseline comparison by facility: Baseline characteristics of participants (including age, sex, BMI, HIV status, history of TB, comorbidities, smoking status, and alcohol use) were compared across the four hospitals. No substantial or statistically significant differences were observed for the majority of key predictors, suggesting that the study population was broadly comparable across facilities.

Assessment of facility-level effects: Although hospital was not included as a random effect in the primary Cox proportional hazards model, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by including hospital as a stratification variable in the Cox model. The adjusted hazard ratios for the main predictors remained largely unchanged compared with the primary analysis, indicating that facility-level clustering had minimal impact on the study findings.

Objectives

Primary Objective: To determine the incidence rate of tuberculosis (TB) among adult people with diabetes mellitus attending chronic follow-up clinics in comprehensive specialized hospitals of Western Amhara, Northwest Ethiopia.

Secondary Objectives:To identify predictors of tuberculosis among adult people with diabetes mellitus attending chronic follow-up clinics in comprehensive specialized hospitals of Western Amhara, Northwest Ethiopia.

Study design and period

A multicenter, institutional based, retrospective follow-up study was conducted among adult patients with DM who were enrolled at a chronic follow-up clinic from January 1, 2018, to December 31, 2022 in western Amhara Comprehensive Specialized Hospitals, Northwest Ethiopia. The data were retrieved from April 6 to May 28, 2023.

Participants

All adult people with DM who were registered at the chronic follow-up clinic in Western Amhara Comprehensive Specialized Hospitals, Northwest Ethiopia, were the source population, while all adult people with DM who had follow-up visits from Jan. 1, 2018, to December 31, 2022, at the University of Gondar Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (UGCSH), Felege Hiwot Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (FHCSH), Debre Tabor Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (DTCSH) and Debre Markos Comprehensive Specialized Hospital were the study population.

Distinction between baseline and incident TB

To minimize misclassification, individuals with a documented diagnosis of active TB before or at the start of follow-up were excluded. Only adult people with diabetes who were free of active TB at baseline, based on recorded clinical and laboratory information, were included. Incident TB was defined as a new diagnosis of TB.

Handling of latent TB

Because this study relied on secondary (recorded) data, latent TB infection was not routinely screened for or documented in patient charts across the participating hospitals. Consequently, latent TB status could not be extracted or analyzed. Participants without evidence of active TB at baseline were therefore considered TB-free at cohort entry and followed retrospectively for the development of active TB. We acknowledge that undiagnosed latent TB may have progressed to active TB during follow-up. However, this reflects routine clinical practice in the study setting and represents a limitation inherent to retrospective chart-based studies.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All adult people with DM aged 18 years and above and those who were registered for adult chronic clinical follow-up were included in the study, whereas all individuals who developed TB at the first diagnosis of DM and the absence of the date of TB diagnosis was exclude in the study.

Rationale for using more than one method

We used multiple approaches to ensure an adequate and conservative sample size for this retrospective cohort study with time-to-event outcomes. The final sample size was determined by the largest estimate obtained across the methods, consistent with standard epidemiological practice. Therefore, the final sample size with 1.5 design effects was 750, and we used the design effect to consider the clustering effects between the four comprehensive specialized hospitals.

Sampling procedure

Study participants were selected using a simple random sampling technique through a computer-generated random number method in each hospital after proportional allocation of samples. Among the five government comprehensive specialized hospitals found in the Western Amhara Region (University of Gondar, Debre Markos, Felege Hiwot, Tibebe-Ghion, and Debre Tabor Comprehensive Specialized Hospital) of these four comprehensive specialized hospitals (University of Gondar, Felege-Hiwot, Debre Markos and Debre Tabor Comprehensive Specialized Hospitals). The total sample size was then proportionally allocated for each working unit of the selected hospitals. The possible sample size with a 1.5 design effect was 750.

Variables and measurements

The dependent variable was incident tuberculosis (TB) among adult people with diabetes mellitus (DM). TB incidence was defined as the development of a new diagnosis of active TB during the follow-up period (January 1, 2018 to December 31, 2022) among participants who were free of active TB at baseline. Participants with a documented diagnosis of active TB before or at cohort entry were excluded to avoid misclassification. TB occurrence was recorded as yes or no, and the date of diagnosis was used as the event time for survival analysis. Because this was a retrospective follow-up study, latent TB infection was not routinely screened for or documented in medical records. Therefore, individuals without evidence of active TB at baseline were considered TB-free at cohort entry, regardless of latent TB status.

The main independent variables included socio-demographic, behavioral, clinical, and treatment-related factors

Diabetes mellitus was confirmed based on documentation in the chronic follow-up clinic records, including a physician-diagnosed DM status, use of insulin or oral hypoglycemic agents, and/or recorded laboratory evidence consistent with diabetes (fasting plasma glucose ≥126 mg/dL, random plasma glucose ≥200 mg/dl with symptoms, or HbA1c ≥6.5%).

Socio-demographic variables (age, sex, residence, marital status, education level, occupation, and religion) were extracted from patient registration and follow-up records.

Behavioral factors, including alcohol consumption and smoking status, were obtained from clinician-documented patient history at baseline.

Clinical variables such as history of tuberculosis, history of close contact with TB patients, HIV status, hypertension, renal failure, heart failure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), asthma, bronchitis, chronic liver disease, and cancer were determined based on documented clinical diagnoses and/or laboratory results recorded in the medical charts.

Nutritional status was assessed using body mass index (BMI), calculated from baseline height and weight measurements recorded in the patient charts and categorized according to WHO criteria.

Treatment-related variables, including type of antidiabetic medication, history of anti-TB treatment, chemotherapy use, and antiretroviral therapy (ART) use, were extracted from medication and follow-up records.

All variables were measured at baseline, prior to the occurrence of TB, using a standardized data extraction checklist.

Operational definitions of study variables

Survival time: This time period was measured between the DM follow-up start date and the date of TB development or the date of June 30, 2021. 39

Censored: Adult patients with DM who did not develop TB during the follow-up period (those who were transferred, died, or lost to follow-up still did not develop TB at the end of the study). 39 Event: occurrence of TB from the start of DM follow-up to the end of the study. 39 Pulmonary tuberculosis(PTB): Patients who were diagnosed with at least two sputum smear examinations positive for acid-fast bacilli (AFB) or with one initial smear examination positive for AFB and radiographic abnormalities consistent with active TB, as determined by a clinician (PTB) by XpertMTB/RIF assay. 35 Alcohol exposure: Patients who had a history of alcohol consumption for alcohol misuse or alcohol dependency. 29

Smoking exposure was categorized as follows: current smoker, if the patients had smoked for at least one cigarette for the last 12 months; past smoker, if patients stopped smoking just a year before; and never smoker, if patients had never smoked during their whole life. 29

Close contact: People who share air space with an individual with PTB in the household or other indoor setting for >15 hours per week or >180 hours total during an infectious period are defined as the interval from 3 months before collection of the first culture-positive sputum sample or the date of onset of cough through two weeks after the initiation of appropriate anti-TB treatment.40–42 With respect to TB, any person who has been exposed to a person with TB has contact with TB disease. 2

Ascertainment of active TB cases

This study was a retrospective follow-up study based on routinely recorded clinical data. Adult people with diabetes were not systematically screened for TB at predefined intervals. Instead, TB was identified through routine clinical practice, whereby Participants were evaluated for TB when they presented with TB-related symptoms, or TB screening was initiated by clinicians during routine diabetes follow-up visits based on clinical suspicion.

This approach reflects standard care in the participating hospitals.

Consistency of diagnostic criteria across hospitals

All four comprehensive specialized hospitals follow the Ethiopian National Tuberculosis Control Program guidelines, which are aligned with WHO recommendations. Accordingly, the same diagnostic criteria for active TB were applied across hospitals. A diagnosis of active TB was based on one or more of the following, as documented in patient charts or TB registers: Positive sputum smear microscopy, Positive Xpert MTB/RIF assay, Clinical and radiological findings consistent with TB, with a clinician-initiated decision to start anti-TB treatment.

Diagnostic methods used (smear vs xpert)

Due to the retrospective nature of the study and variability in documentation, the exact proportion of TB diagnoses based on sputum smear alone versus sputum smear plus Xpert MTB/RIF could not be consistently extracted for all hospitals. However, Xpert MTB/RIF has been progressively implemented in all participating hospitals during the study period and is routinely used, particularly for people with diabetes and other high-risk groups, in accordance with national guidelines.

Pulmonary vs extrapulmonary TB

Both pulmonary TB (PTB) and extra-pulmonary TB (EPTB) cases were included in the analysis. TB type was identified based on clinician documentation in the medical record or TB register. All confirmed TB cases, regardless of anatomical site, were treated as incident TB events in the survival analysis. Given the limited number of extra-pulmonary TB cases, separate stratified analyses by TB type were not performed; this has now been acknowledged as a limitation.

Data collection tools and procedures

The data collection checklist was adapted from different literature reviews 43 and was used for data collection. The checklist contains information on socio-demographic, behavioral, baseline clinical and treatment-related factors. Forms used for laboratory requests, DM registration logbooks, and patient cards were reviewed. Three BSc nurses for data collection and one health officer for a supervisor were invited to participate in the study. The number of medical records of patients during the data collection period from a logbook was used for the sampling frame to apply a simple random sampling technique. Data were subsequently collected from the medical charts and medical records of the selected patients via the prepared checklists. To prevent additional recollection of the data, a code commonly agreed upon by the data collectors was given after reviewing and collecting the data from each patient’s chart.

Data quality control

The data collection checklist was checked preliminary review for consistency of the tool and completeness of data items or variables of patient charts before the actual data collection started, and these charts were included in the study. Half-day training was given for the data collectors for the description of the checklist and the way they collected data from the patient chart. Each component of the checklist was discussed clearly for the data collectors. The data collection process was monitored closely by the supervisor. The data were cross-checked and clarified for any missing data. Data completeness was assessed during data extraction. Records with missing outcome variables (tuberculosis diagnosis or follow-up time) were excluded from the analysis. For explanatory variables, missing data were minimal (<5% across all covariates); therefore, a complete-case analysis was conducted, and no imputation techniques were applied. Finally, the completeness of the checklist was checked, and corrections were made before the chart was returned.

Data processing and analysis

Before conducting the analysis, the data were checked for consistency and completeness, cleaned, coded and entered into Epi data version 4.6 by the principal investigator and exported to STATA version 14 statistical software for analysis. Continuous patient follow-up data are presented as medians and 25th and 75th percentiles, and categorical data are presented as percentages and frequencies. At the end of follow-up, the outcome of each study participant was dichotomized into censored or event. The TB incidence rate was calculated by dividing the number of new TB cases occurring during the follow-up period by the total person-time observation and is expressed as per 100 person-year observations. Kaplan‒Meier survival curves were used to estimate the time of TB occurrence among adult DM patients, and the log-rank test was used to compare survival curves. The proportional hazard assumption tests were performed graphically (Ph-plot), a global test was performed by using the Shenfield residual test, and a Cox-Snell residual test was used.

Variable selection approach

Initially, candidate variables were screened using bivariable Cox regression, and variables with a p-value ≤ 0.20 were considered for inclusion in the multivariable model. This threshold was chosen deliberately to avoid the premature exclusion of potentially important confounders, as recommended in epidemiologic modeling literature. Importantly, this statistical criterion was supplemented by prior evidence from published studies and biological plausibility. Lastly variables with a p value ≤ 0.2 in the bivariable Cox PH model were entered into the multivariable Cox PH model to control for confounding factors. Variables with a p value < 0.05 in the final model were considered to be significantly associated with TB. Finally, the adjusted hazard ratio with its corresponding 95% confidence interval was reported to indicate the presence of a significant association between the explanatory and outcome variables.

Survival function of different categorical variables among adult people with diabetes mellitus

To determine the survival time of adult people with diabetes mellitus according to different categorical variables, Kaplan‒Meier curves and log rank tests were used. Both test statistics revealed significant differences between the categories of variables. On the basis of these test statistics, nutritional status (BMI), HIV status, cancer status, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) status and TB close contact status were computed to assess differences in survival time among the categories of variables. In this study, adult people with diabetes mellitus who were underweight developed TB more often than normal-weight patients did.

At the end of follow-up, overall survival of underweight and normal patients was 66.88% and 89.66%, respectively. There was significant variation in survival time, with a p value < 0.0001 (Figure 2).

Assessment of the proportional hazard assumption

The proportional hazard assumption of the Cox regression model states that the hazard of tuberculosis in the study subjects must be constant over time and the effect of covariates remains constant over time. The overall full model satisfied the proportional hazard assumption.

The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals for all covariates in the final Cox model. The global test indicated chi-square = 4.32 (p = 0.9597), and individual covariate p-values ranged from 0.3763 to 0.9807, confirming that the proportional hazards assumption was not violated. Predictors of tuberculosis in adult diabetic mellitus patients

In this retrospective follow-up study, the findings of the Bivariable Cox regression analysis showed that sex, residence, nutritional status, smoking status, HIV status, cancer status, COPD status, bronchiectasis status, past history of TB, and history of close contact with TB were significantly associated with tuberculosis. However, after multivariable Cox regression analysis was performed, nutritional status, HIV status, cancer status, COPD status and history of close contact for TB were significantly associated with TB. With other variables held constant, the risk of developing TB among individuals with a BMI less than 18.5 was increased by 1.87 (AHR =1.87; 1.00, 3.47) than those nutritional status in BMI greater than 18.5. In this study, the risk of developing TB among HIV-positive in people with diabetes mellitus was 4.71 times greater (AHR=4.71; CI 2.68, 8.26) than that among HIV-negative in people with diabetes mellitus. In terms of cancer status, the risk of developing TB among people with diabetes mellitus who had cancer was 4.37 times (AHR=4.37; CI 1.54, 12.39) higher than that among people with diabetes mellitus who were cancer negative. Furthermore, the risk of developing TB among people with diabetes mellitus who had COPD was 2.53 times (AHR=2.53; CI 1.23, 5.21) greater than that among people with diabetes mellitus who had no COPD.

Finally, among people with diabetes mellitus who had a history of close contact with TB, the hazard of TB among those with a history of close contact was 6.53 times (AHR=6.53; CI 3.15, 13.51.97) greater than that among those who had no close contact with TB (Table 7).

Discussion

This study investigated the incidence and predictors of TB among people with DM A multicenter retrospective follow-up study at University of Gondar Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (UGCSH),Felege Hiwot Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (FHCSH), Debre Tabor Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (DTCSH) and Debre Markos Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (DMCSH). The current study showed that the overall incidence rate of TB among people with DM was 27 per 1000 PY observation.. This study’s tuberculosis incidence rate is consistent with research conducted in Debre Markos. 38 However, this number is greater than that reported in previous studies, which revealed values of 6.9–7.6 per 100,000 person-years in Iran, 44 16.2 per 100,000 person-years in the UK, 45 47.0/100,000 person-years in China and 119.85 per 100,000 person-years in China,37,38 12.4 per 100 person-years in South Korea, 46 18/10 000 person-years in the Republic of Korea, 26 and 1.18 per 1000 person-years in Taiwan. 25 In China, the discrepancy might be, due to the difference in sampling method, study design and early access to diagnosis, treatment and prevention of TB among people with DM. In UK, the difference might be, due to difference demographic characteristics of study participants, socio-cultural difference, sample size difference, adequate health care coverage, skilled health care provider and early access to screen and treat. In Iran, the lower incidence might be, due to the presence of quality of care delivery for people with DM might contribute for a lower incidence of TB and this difference might be contextual difference between the two countries. These difference might be due to difference in late diagnosis and initiation of follow up of diabetic patients, poor quality in health care systems and lack of strict monitoring of complications in those African countries including Ethiopia may increase the incidence of TB and due to contextual differences present between developing and developed countries.

The incidence, however, is lower than the 0.8/100 per person-years (PY) reported in a study conducted in rural Tanzania. 47 More than half (68%) of the study participants had type 2 diabetes, which is more common in older adults, despite having relatively good insulin secretion for glycemic control. When people with DM had a BMI less than 18.5, their risk of developing tuberculosis (TB) was 1.87 times higher than those who had a BMI greater than 18.5. Studies conducted in China, 47 Addis Ababa, 48 Japan, 49 southern Ethiopia, 50 and Debre Markos 48 all corroborated this conclusion. This could be because underweight individuals are typically regarded as immune-compromised to prevent tuberculosis infection. An additional explanation might be that nutritional status is one of the major variables influencing infection resistance. Poor nutrition and a compromised immune system are well known to be related. 48 The weight loss and nutritional stress caused by infections can lower immune system performance and nutritional status. Additionally, malnutrition decreases cell-mediated immunity and increases susceptibility to infections. 49 Malnutrition can adversely impact several essential immune defense mechanisms, including phagocytic function, cell-mediated immunity, antibody concentration, and cytokine production, in addition to having the potential to cause tuberculosis. 51 Nonetheless, research carried out in Nigeria 52 and Dessie, Northeast Ethiopia, 53 did not reveal any connection between diabetic patients’ TB infection and a body mass index less than 18.5. Variations in sample sizes, study designs, study settings, follow-up periods, and participant socio-demographic may be partly responsible for these discrepancies in results.

Furthermore, people with DM who tested positive for HIV had a greater risk of developing tuberculosis (TB) than patients who tested negative for HIV. The results for Ethiopia, 51 the Gedeo Zone, southern Ethiopia, 52 Arba Minch, 54 and Metema Hospital 55 are all in line with these findings. This could be because tuberculosis (TB) is a common presenting illness for people living with HIV and is a major cause of death. Adults who are HIV positive and malnourished, as well as those who frequently contract opportunistic infections, are especially vulnerable. HIV decreases cellular immune function and CD4+ T lymphocytes, which permits latent tuberculosis bacteria to develop into active tuberculosis. Furthermore, HIV positivity is the most powerful risk factor known for the progression of M. tuberculosis infection to active disease, and it can increase the risk of latent TB reactivation by a factor of 20. 54 Another theory is that HIV destroys immune system cells, which aids in the body’s defense against illnesses and infections and promotes the growth of tuberculosis. 56 An estimated 0.74 million incident cases of TB were attributable to HIV infection. 57

People with diabetes mellitus who tested positive for cancer were more likely to develop tuberculosis than those who tested negative for the disease. Research from Taiwan, Korea, 58 supports this finding. This is because tuberculosis is more common in cancer patients. This is especially true in cases where cancer patients are susceptible to immunosuppression and are more likely to experience PTB after chemotherapy. Additional justification Cancer can cause malnutrition and weakened immune system function directly or indirectly through secondary effects of the disease. One of the main adverse effects of cancer treatment, especially chemotherapy, is immunosuppression. This raises the possibility of latent tuberculosis infections reactivating or developing new TB infections during the clinical course of cancer. 58

Moreover, DM people with COPD are at greater risk of developing tuberculosis (TB) than patients without COPD. This result is in line with research from Taiwan 59 and Sweden. 60 The relationship between COPD and tuberculosis is intricate. A significant portion of tuberculosis patients experience COPD linked to tuberculosis or post tuberculosis airway disease. This relationship is the most frequently reported relationship. The relationship between COPD and tuberculosis is intricate. Tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNFα) and interleukin-1 (IL-1) are critical components of the immune response to bacteria, and COPD reduces their levels to favor infection. Additionally, dysfunctional alveolar macrophages in COPD patients increase the risk of tuberculosis because these cells are critical for the resolution of tuberculosis, even in the absence of steroid use. Patients with COPD are more likely to develop PTB because oral or inhaled steroids have immunosuppressive effects.60,61

Finally, among adult people with DM, those with a history of close contact with tuberculosis had a higher risk of developing active tuberculosis during the follow-up period. Research from the Geodeo Zone, southern Ethiopia, 55 the United States and Canada. 62 This may be because contacts of tuberculosis patients have an increased risk of contracting a tuberculosis infection or illness, depending on a number of factors, including the type of contact infectiousness of the source case and environmental characteristics. Owing to their close proximity, diabetes patients with close ties are more likely to become infected with tuberculosis from the index cases. Close personal contact with someone who has been diagnosed with the disease is another factor contributing to the risk of TB development and transmission among people with DM. In close proximity to patients suffering from infectious tuberculosis, there is an increased risk of contracting mycobacterial tuberculosis infection and illness. 59 According to reports, the prevalence of PTB is highest among people with DM in close contact among several risk groups, including those with dense populations such as the homeless, injectable drug users, and prison population. Close contact is another common risk factor for latent tuberculosis infection becoming an active illness. This is why it is vital to stop the disease from spreading by obtaining a diagnosis as soon as possible and treating it appropriately.61,62

Some predictors, such as BMI, ART use, and COPD, may change during the follow-up period. In this study, these variables were modeled as fixed baseline covariates because repeated measurements were not consistently available in patient charts. Consequently, we could not use time-varying covariate models, which may have led to some underestimation or misclassification of exposure effects. Future prospective studies with repeated measurements are warranted to assess the impact of dynamic changes in these predictors on TB risk.

Although smoking and alcohol consumption are recognized risk factors for tuberculosis in previous primary studies, our study did not find statistically significant associations with these behaviors.63–65 One possible explanation is the difference in study design: previous evidence comes from community-based, prospective cohort studies with direct and repeated measurement of exposures, whereas our study was a retrospective hospital-based cohort relying on medical records. Incomplete recording of smoking and alcohol use may have led to exposure misclassification. In addition, the relatively low prevalence of smokers and alcohol users in our cohort and the presence of stronger predictors, such as HIV infection and history of close TB contact, may have reduced our ability to detect associations. These findings highlight the importance of prospective data collection to accurately assess lifestyle-related TB risk.

Strengths and limitations of the study

Strengths

The data collection tool (checklist) was carefully checked against patient medical records prior to data collection.

Adjustments were made for possible confounders in the analysis, enhancing the validity of the findings.

Limitations

Potential misclassification of exposures and outcomes: Some variables, such as history of alcohol use, smoking, or prior TB exposure, were obtained from patient charts and may be incompletely or inaccurately recorded, potentially leading to misclassification. TB diagnosis relied on hospital records, and diagnostic methods (sputum smear vs. Xpert MTB/RIF) varied across hospitals, which could affect incidence estimates.

Use of secondary data: Due to reliance on chart review, some important predictors, such as chewing khat and other substance use, were not captured, limiting the assessment of all possible confounders.

Retrospective design: The retrospective cohort design limits causal inference and the ability to control for unrecorded confounding factors.

Limited generalizability: The study was conducted in four comprehensive specialized hospitals in Western Amhara, which may not fully represent other regions or healthcare settings.

Small number of TB events relative to predictors: Only 67 incident TB cases were observed, which may limit statistical power and increase the risk of overfitting in multivariable models.

Missing data and potential biases: Some patient charts had incomplete records, which may have introduced information bias or affected the accuracy of exposure and outcome classification.

Conclusions and recommendations

The overall incidence of tuberculosis (TB) among adult people with diabetes mellitus (DM) was high across the four comprehensive specialized hospitals included in this study. Pulmonary TB was identified as the most common form of TB in this population. Independent predictors of incident TB among diabetic patients included under nutrition (BMI < 18.5 kg/m2), HIV infection, a history of cancer, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and close contact with TB cases.. The studyrecommend integrating routine TB screening into diabetes care, especially for patients with low BMI, HIV, cancer, COPD, or TB exposure. Awareness should be raised among patients and communities about the increased TB risk in diabetes. Policymakers should support coordinated TB–DM management and preventive measures. Future research should use prospective designs and consider social, economic, and behavioral factors influencing TB risk.
