The effectiveness of a 3-dimensional printed vaginal dilator in mitigating radiation-induced vaginal stenosis for patients with cervical cancer
Authors: Sirikorn Kongwattanakul, Petcharat Petroch, Kampheang Nimjaroen, Sarinya Bawornpatarapakorn, Kanyanee Laebua, Danupon Nantajit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveVaginal stenosis (VS) is a common adverse effect of pelvic radiotherapy. Current guidelines recommend vaginal dilation to maintain tissue elasticity and prevent VS. Previously, patients were instructed to self-dilate using own fingers. The current study assessed the effectiveness of using a 3-dimensional (3D) printed vaginal dilator (VD) for mitigating vaginal narrowing and shortening in cervical cancer patients within 1 year after radiotherapy.MethodsVDs were developed and validated by radiation therapists using a 3D printer. We enrolled 67 female patients diagnosed with cervical cancer at Chulabhorn Hospital to test the dilators. As a baseline, vaginal diameter and length were measured using the dilator before intracavitary radiotherapy. The patients were instructed on the use of VDs and provided with information guides and logbooks. Physical examinations were conducted at intervals of 1, 3, 6, 9, and 12 months post-radiotherapy.ResultsThe patients’ average age was 51.75 years (standard deviation=13.23). 57% of all patients were diagnosed with stage III squamous cell carcinoma, and 66% underwent concurrent chemoradiation therapy. With the use of dilators, patients exhibited shortened and narrowed vaginal canals at 9 months and 1 month after receiving radiotherapy treatment. Most patients went on to develop VS. However, in most cases patients exhibited grade 1 VS according to the Late Effects in Normal Tissues-Subjective, Objective, Management and Analytic classification, with only 15.79% of patients exhibiting grade 2 VS.ConclusionThe use of a VD could potentially lessen the severity of VS induced by radiotherapy/brachytherapy.Trial RegistrationThai Clinical Trial Registry Identifier: TCTR20250309001Keywords: Cervical Cancer, Pelvic Radiation Therapy, Vaginal Dilator, Vaginal Stenosis

INTRODUCTION

Cervical cancer therapy often involves a combination of external beam radiation therapy (EBRT) and brachytherapy. However, these treatments can lead to side effects such as narrowing of the cervical canal. This narrowing occurs because of tissue fibrosis around the treated area [1,2]. As a result, the cervical canal may become shorter and less flexible, which can cause discomfort during sexual intercourse, often leading to female sexual dysfunction [3]. Previous studies have reported that the occurrence of cervical canal stenosis after radiation therapy is influenced by various factors including patient-specific characteristics, tumor size, radiation dose, treatment duration, concurrent chemotherapy, and patient-related factors (such as age, histopathological cell type, sexual activity, marital status, cultural background, and other side effects) [2,4]. The incidence of cervical canal stenosis reported in a previous review ranges from 1.2% to 88% among patients receiving radiation therapy to the pelvic region; however, recent reports have estimated the incidence to be between 50% and 80% [1].

The use of a vaginal dilator (VD) is currently recommended to prevent vaginal stenosis (VS) [5]. Patients are advised to use dilators 2–3 times per week for 52 weeks or continuously without a specified endpoint [2]. Lubricating gels can be helpful, especially if the cervical canal becomes dry [6]. However, despite the effectiveness of dilators, compliance remains challenging, particularly in the first year after treatment [7,8]. Likewise, the use of VD may have deep psychological and emotional implications that are disturbing for patients [9]. Accessibility and affordability of dilators are another concern because many patients, particularly in Asian countries such as Thailand, may view them as sex toys and are unlikely acquire them for therapeutic use. The availability of sex toys is also very limited as they are often regarded as immoral and prohibited by laws to be sold in store. Further, the patients typically have a significantly lower frequency of sexual activity after the treatment [10,11] which does not aid the condition. Previously, in the clinic, the patients were individually instructed by oncology nurses to use the index and middle fingers to stretch the canal wall for vaginal dilation. However, the compliance was low, and the patients often experienced with signs of VS including narrowing and shortening of the cervix. Therefore, in the current study, we attempted to address this issue by providing patients with 3-dimensional (3D) printed VDs to improve the accessibility of VDs. We then assessed the effectiveness of these 3D printed dilators by measuring vaginal length and diameter. The patients were also asked about their satisfaction regarding the use of the dilators. It was hypothesized that the provision of VDs would yield a small percentage of grade 2 or above VS among the patients as well as adherence to the use of VDs.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Participants, setting, and procedure

Patients with locally advanced cervical cancer treated with chemo-radiation therapy and brachytherapy above the age of 18 were eligible to participate in this study. Vaginal measurements and physical examination results were recorded at baseline and 1, 3, 6, 9, and 12 months after the concurrent chemoradiotherapy. Each patient was provided with a set of VDs with different diameters (2, 2.5, and 3 cm) along with condoms to cover the VD for each use. The VDs were created using a 3D printer, FullScale Max450 (Print3DD, Bangkok, Thailand), and were constructed as white plastic rods. The material used for these dilators is typically polylactic acid, a biodegradable plastic derived from natural sources.

Patient vaginal capacity was defined as the largest VD that could be comfortably inserted into the vaginal canal during pelvic examination. Compliance with VD use was recorded at each visit. The Late Effects in Normal Tissues-Subjective, Objective, Management and Analytic (LENT-SOMA) criteria of stenosis was used for grading VS toxicity.

2. Research procedure

To establish the baseline, patients underwent a vaginal assessment by their attending physicians prior to the start of their final brachytherapy session. The assessment involved using a VD with a centimeter scale to measure the length and conduct an internal examination following the standard brachytherapy procedures to determine the width of the vaginal canal.

The patients were instructed to start using the dilator 1 month after the completion of their treatment. The patients were instructed on the use of the VD by a radiation oncology nurse. The nurse instructed the patients to use the VD three times a week for 3–5 minutes per session. Patients were advised that the VD should be cleaned with soap and water and allowed to dry in the shade. Additionally, they were informed that the VD should not be exposed to sunlight and that it could be reused. Along with the set of VDs, an instruction manual and a logbook for recording the weekly usage over a 12-month period were provided to patients.

3. Data collection

Patients with cervical cancer were evaluated at six different time points: prior to the final brachytherapy session and at 1, 3, 6, 9, and 12 months after the completion of their concurrent chemoradiotherapy.

4. Satisfaction survey

The patients were asked for their satisfaction concerning the VDs in 3 aspects: physical appearance, usability and safety with a rating scale of 1–5 based on the 5-point Likert scale with 1 being very unsatisfying and 5 being very satisfying.

5. Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chulabhorn Research Institute with project number 070/2562. All participants provided written informed consent.

6. Clinical trial registration

The study was registered with the Thai Clinical Trial Registry, ID number TCTR20250309001.

7. Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using Stata MP software (version 18.0; StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA) and visualized using GraphPad Prism version 10.1.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., Boston, MA, USA). A generalized multilevel mixed model with random intercepts was used for the analysis of different factors. The p-values of less than 0.05 were considered to indicate statistical significance.

RESULTS

A total of 67 patients with locally advanced cervical cancer who met the inclusion criteria were enrolled. The patients’ mean age was 51.75±13.23 years old. Squamous cell carcinoma cell type (85.07%) was the most prevalent subtype of tumor, with stage 3 representing the largest group (50.00%). Tumor extended to the upper part of the vagina in 61.19% of patients. Regarding educational attainment, most patients (69.36%) had below undergraduate degrees. Regarding marital status, 67.16% of the participants were married. 82.09% of the patients reported that they actively engage in sexual activity with their partners. Regarding radiotherapy, 98.5% of all patients were treated with EBRT and intracavitary radiation therapy, as shown in Table 1, and 1 patient received EBRT alone. Patients were examined to measure vaginal length and diameter during radiotherapy to provide baseline measures and at follow-up appointments 1, 3, 6, 9, and 12 months post-irradiation. A total of 37 patients attended all of the follow-up examinations. The results showed that despite the reported use of the dilators, patients experienced significantly shortened and narrowed vaginal canals at 9- and 1-month follow-up, respectively (Fig. 1). To identify different factors that could be related to the shortening and narrowing of the vaginal canals as well as the severity of VS, we analyzed the demographic data corresponding to these changes using generalized multilevel mixed model with random intercepts. Regarding vaginal length, tumor involvement with the vagina was the only significant factor (p=0.013). Regarding vaginal diameter, patients’ age (p=0.013), menstruation (p=0.040), and sexual activity (p=0.003) affected the narrowing of the vagina. Menstruation (p=0.003) and sexual activity (p=0.013) were related to the severity of VS (Table 2).

The observed severity of VS among these patients remained relatively low, with grade 2 being the most severe. These results encourage the recommendation for regular use of VD to prevent or lessen the development of VS.

DISCUSSION

In the current study, we provided patients who underwent radiotherapy courses to treat cervical cancer with 3D-printed VDs to make the device accessible to patients. Despite the clinical recommendation that patients should perform insertion approximately 2–3 times per week to reduce the likelihood of stenosis, previous studies have reported low rates of compliance without any interventions [8,13]. Prior to this study, the patients were instructed to use fingers or have sexual intercourse at least 3 times per week as a standard practice for vaginal dilation. For finger dilation, the oncology nurses individually taught the patients for the procedure. However, the compliance for having the finger exercise appeared to be low as they were uncomfortable with such procedure. Moreover, the frequency of having sexual intercourse was lowered. The option of acquiring sex toys for dilation was also unpopular as they are legally banned within the country. The current results indicate that providing instructions with the device improved the adherence with VDs, with patients still using the device months after undergoing radiation treatment. Because VD is a recommended prophylaxis for VS, the study could not be conducted as a randomized controlled trial; therefore, it was not possible to directly assess the effectiveness of the 3D printed devices. Likewise, due to the low compliance of finger dilation practice, there was no previous record of its effects, VS grades, vaginal lengths or widths after radiotherapy. Therefore, we could only compare the results with earlier studies. However, a study comparing the use of fingers and dilators for vaginismus showed that dilators were superior in terms of both the efficacy and drop-out rate [14].

Concerning VS, previous studies using a similar grading classification reported higher percentages of VS incidences of grade 2 or above than the current study [15,16]. Previous studies often reported the incidence of grade 3 or higher vaginal toxicity after radiotherapy on the basis of the CTCAE and Radiation Therapy Oncology Group/European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer criteria [17]. Comparatively, the rate of grade 2 VS among the patients in the current study (15.79%) was relatively low compared with the rates reported in previous studies [18,19,20]. Traditionally, sexual activity is recommended to prevent VS, and 82.9% of the participants were sexually active, which may have aided in maintaining vaginal canal volume.

This study used a set of dilators with diameters of 2, 2.5, and 3 cm. However, insertion length and duration of use vary widely across previous studies [21,22]. Patient satisfaction with the physical appearance of the dilator may affect adherence. Some patients expressed that the VD was overly stiff. Alternative dilator materials, such as plastic, latex, or other medical materials, should be considered for further improvement [22]. Psychological factors and insufficient information have previously been reported as reasons for non-adherence to dilator use [9,23]. Strategies to empower patients and improve adherence, such as psychosocial rehabilitation booklets, could improve patients’ adherence to the use of VD [24].

The current study had several limitations that should be considered, including the impact of the coronavirus disease 2019 pandemic, which prevented consistent in-person vaginal assessments during the study. The lack of frequent in-person follow-up could lessen the adherence to VDs. Varied grading scales also limited direct inter-study comparison. The historical records of VS grades, vaginal lengths and diameters of previous patients based on the finger dilation were also missing, which forbids a direct comparison of the results.

Physical vaginal assessment by radiation oncologists should be a standard practice for follow-ups rather than relying on virtual methods. Follow-up intervals should include physical examinations at 6 months post-treatment and every 3 months up to 1 year. The effectiveness of VDs should be further investigated with longitudinal studies extending beyond 15 months. Future research should focus on developing new dilator models tailored to individual physical needs and preferences. This includes comparing outcomes between patients using novel dilators and those not using any dilators while addressing barriers to adherence.

In conclusion, the current findings indicate that while radiation therapy effectively addresses tumors in cervical cancer patients, it has a lasting impact on VS, affecting both vaginal length and diameter. This highlights the importance of considering patients’ quality of life and the potential side effects of VS, such as procedural pain and discomfort during intercourse. Providing VDs are essential for addressing the physical and societal restrictions. The approach involves patients in their own care and ensuring they are educated on the importance of using VDs while addressing their concerns and difficulties. Radiation oncology teams also play a pivotal role in providing essential support throughout the treatment journey.
