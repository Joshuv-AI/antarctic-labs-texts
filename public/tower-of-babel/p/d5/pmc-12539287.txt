Iron deficiency anemia is associated with depressive symptoms in adolescents with heavy menstrual bleeding
Authors: Ramya Ramakrishnan, Hansa Boddu, Kaleigh Riggs-Harpur, Lakshmi V Srivaths, Asha Davidson, Rebecca M Beyda, Neethu M Menon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHeavy menstrual bleeding (HMB) and associated iron deficiency (ID) with anemia (IDA) or without anemia lead to adverse health outcomes for adolescents.ObjectivesThis descriptive study aimed to assess the association between ID/IDA and depressive symptoms, as measured by Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ)-9 scores, among adolescents with HMB.MethodsThis is a single-center retrospective records review of adolescents with physician-documented HMB and at least 1 PHQ-9 score, evaluated in a specialty clinic. With ID defined as ferritin of <25 ng/dL and IDA as hemoglobin (Hgb) of <12 g/dL, univariate and multivariate statistical models were used to compare ID/IDA with PHQ-9 scores cross-sectionally and longitudinally. We hypothesized that the incidence of PHQ-9 scores of ≥10 would be higher among patients with ID/IDA than that in those without.ResultsAmong 123 adolescents with HMB, 90 had ID and 61 had IDA; 37% of those with ID (33/90) and 51% of those with IDA (31/61) had a PHQ-9 score of ≥10 at baseline (P = .653 and P = .006, respectively). Further, 74 of the 123 had 2 or more PHQ-9 scores across multiple visits during the study period. Among these, PHQ-9 scores improved significantly when Hgb levels improved (P = .04) but did not change when ferritin levels improved (P = .23). PHQ-9 scores also improved with improvement of HMB, regardless of Hgb or ferritin levels (P = .003).ConclusionThis study highlights the importance of addressing and optimally managing depressive symptoms in adolescents with HMB and related IDA.Keywords: iron deficiency, Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9), pictorial blood assessment chart, ferritin, hemoglobin, ethnicity, obesity, bleeding disorder, hormonal therapy, antifibrinolytic therapy, oral iron, intravenous iron

Introduction

Heavy menstrual bleeding (HMB), experienced by up to 37% of menstruating adolescents, is associated with depressive symptoms [[1], [2], [3]]. Studies using adolescent-specific questionnaires have shown that HMB among adolescents leads to impaired health-related quality of life [3]. HMB causes iron deficiency (ID) with or without iron deficiency anemia (IDA), and ID has been associated with depression among otherwise healthy adults, as well as in patients with inflammatory bowel disease and congestive heart failure [[4], [5], [6], [7]]. ID and IDA also increase the risk of depression in children, with females at higher risk [[7], [8], [9]]. There are few studies looking at the overall association among HMB, ID/IDA, and depression in adolescents [10].

The Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ)-9 is a validated screening tool routinely used to alert the provider of the presence of depressive symptoms in patients. It consists of 9 questions each scored from 0 to 3 to get a total PHQ-9 score of 0 to 27. A PHQ-9 score of ≥10 has 88% sensitivity and 88% specificity to diagnose major depression [11,12]. The objective of our study was to describe the presence of ID/IDA in adolescents with HMB and to assess its association with PHQ-9 scores. We hypothesized that the incidence of depressive symptoms based on PHQ-9 scores of ≥10 would be higher among patients with ID/IDA that that among those without. We also anticipated seeing a decrease in PHQ-9 scores with the improvement of ID/IDA.

Methods

This retrospective cohort study was approved by our institutional review board. Electronic medical records of menstruating patients aged 9 to 21 years seen at 3 specialty clinics in our center (adult bleeding disorders clinic, pediatric bleeding disorders clinic, and a multidisciplinary clinic for HMB including pediatric hematologists and adolescent medicine specialists) between July 2019 and June 2023 were screened.

Patients were included in the study if they had at least 1 visit with a diagnosis of HMB and a corresponding patient-completed PHQ-9 questionnaire (these questionnaires are administered routinely in our clinics during scheduled visits that occur every 3-6 months). Patients were recorded as having a diagnosis of HMB if any of the following was documented in the chart: menstrual bleeding >7 days, soaking through a pad/tampon in <2 hours, passage of clots ≥quarter-size, and Pictorial Blood Assessment Chart (PBAC) score of ≥100 [13]. All patients were included in the descriptive and cross-sectional analyses. Patients who had 1 or more additional clinic visits with a recorded PHQ-9 questionnaire were included in the longitudinal analysis. Patients with intellectual disability (unable to independently complete a PHQ-9) and patients with a missing answer to any of the 9 PHQ-9 questions were excluded.

Chart review and data abstraction were performed by a medical student and a clinical fellow using a secure web application (REDCap). A data dictionary specified definitions, and data validation was performed by an attending physician.

For each patient, the PHQ-9 score for every visit during the study period was recorded. Any ferritin value drawn within 30 days and any hemoglobin (Hgb) value drawn within 7 days of the visit were also recorded. Cross-sectional analysis was performed only on the data from the first clinic visit. Longitudinal analysis was performed on all data, including the first visit and all subsequent visits meeting study criteria. Other laboratory values recorded included C-reactive protein (CRP) levels drawn at the same time as the ferritin levels (documented as normal or high based on institutional reference ranges) and thyroid profile laboratories (recorded during any visit across the study period). Additional data collected from the first visit for cross-sectional analyses included age, recorded gender (female vs not female based on a review of clinical notes), race and ethnicity, body mass index with percentile for age, length and regularity of menstrual cycles, type of bleeding disorder if present, and presence or absence of antidepressant medication(s). Medications were classified as antidepressant if they belonged to the pharmacological class of antidepressants and/or documented in a physician’s note as being used for depression [14]. Additional data collected from subsequent visits included the presence or absence of HMB (using the same definition as used for inclusion criteria), PBAC scores as available, medications documented as treatment for HMB (categorized as hormonal, antifibrinolytic, desmopressin, or other), and any iron therapy (categorized as oral, intravenous [IV], or both). The timing and duration of hormonal therapy, antifibrinolytic therapy, and iron therapy were calculated based on chart documentation of the dates these medications were started and stopped.

The answers to each of the questions on the PHQ-9 were recorded individually based on which the total score was calculated. Depressive symptoms were classified as PHQ-9 scores of ≥10 versus ≤9 to serve as the main outcome measure [11,12]. Additionally, PHQ-9 scores as a continuous variable (0-27) served as an outcome measure for the longitudinal portion of the study. ID was defined as ferritin of <25 ng/mL, based on recently recommended thresholds for children and nonpregnant females [15]. As per World Health Organization guidelines, a ferritin value of <15 ng/mL defines low iron stores; hence, the association between PHQ-9 scores and ferritin <15 ng/mL was also studied [16]. IDA was defined as Hgb of <12 g/dL with abnormal iron studies (low ferritin/low serum iron/low transferrin saturation/high total iron binding capacity).

Descriptive statistics were calculated using means for continuous variables and counts with percentages for categorical variables. For cross-sectional analysis, chi-squared tests, Fisher exact tests, and backward-selected multivariate logistic regression models were used to compare PHQ-9 scores of ≥10 versus ≤9 across different categories of demographic, clinical, and laboratory variables as outlined in Table 1.

For longitudinal analysis, repeated-measures correlation coefficient (Rrm) was used first across all visits for all patients and then among patients with improvement in either Hgb (by >0.5 g/dL) or ferritin (by ≥5 ng/mL) to compare PHQ-9 scores during follow up visits to Hgb and ferritin. A backward-selected multivariate logistic regression mixed model was also used in the longitudinal analysis to assess the effect of the presence or absence of HMB, hormonal versus antifibrinolytic therapy for HMB, and oral versus IV iron therapy for ID/IDA, on the PHQ-9 score.

Statistical significance was defined as a P value of <.05. A principal component analysis was performed to ensure answers to 1 or more questions on the PHQ-9 were not primarily driving the results.

Results

One hundred sixty charts were screened. Of these, 123 met study criteria. Table 1 tabulates the demographic, clinical, and laboratory parameters. Study subjects were of a mean age of 14.6 years, with a range of 9 to 21 years.

PHQ-9 of ≥10 was reported in 49 of 123 (40%) subjects. Ferritin values within 30 days of PHQ-9 score and Hgb values within 7 days of PHQ-9 score were available for 114 and 121 patients, respectively, at the first visit within the study period. Among 90 patients with ID (defined as ferritin < 25 ng/mL), 33 (37%) had a PHQ-9 score of ≥10. Among 61 patients with IDA (defined as Hgb < 12 g/dL), 31 (51%) had a PHQ-9 score of ≥10. CRP values were measured and recorded at 28 (22.76%) of the 123 first study visits and at only 40 (20.62%) of the 194 total visits across the study period. Ten of the 28 CRP values (35.71%) recorded at the first visits were high, and 17 of the 40 total CRP values recorded were high. Among 3 subjects with abnormal thyroid stimulating hormone levels at initial testing, 1 had confirmed hypothyroidism on repeat testing and that patient had a PHQ-9 score of 0.

The presence of a bleeding disorder was documented in 38 of 123 (31%) patients, the most common being von Willebrand disease (VWD; n = 21; 55%), followed by platelet function disorders (n = 7; 18%), factor VIII deficiency (n = 6; 16%), factor VII deficiency (n = 2; 5%), hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia plus VWD (n = 1; 2%), and inherited thrombocytopenia (n = 1; 2%).

Mean age of menarche was 11.4 years, with a range of 7 to 16 years. The median of the reported average menstrual cycle length was 7 days, with 13 patients describing cycles lasting more than 14 days. Among 120 patients with documented treatment of HMB, 57 received hormonal therapy alone, 11 received antifibrinolytic therapy alone, and 50 received both. Two patients received intranasal desmopressin—one with antifibrinolytic therapy and the other with both antifibrinolytic and hormonal therapy. Among 114 patients with documented iron therapy, 71 received oral iron alone, 6 received IV iron alone, and 37 received both.

Cross-sectional analysis

Among 114 patients with ferritin values available at first visit, there was no significant association between ID and a PHQ-9 score of ≥10 on univariate analysis (odds ratio [OR], 0.811; 95% CI, 0.324-2.03; P = .653) (Table 1) or in the multiple logistic regression model (OR, 0.372; 95% CI, 0.124-1.113; P = .077) (Table 2). Among 121 patients with Hgb values available at first visit, there was a statistically significant association between the presence of IDA and a PHQ-9 score of ≥10 (OR, 2.84; 95% CI, 1.33-6.08; P = .006) (Table 1), which continued to be significant in a multivariate logistic regression analysis (OR, 2.452; 95% CI, 1.035-5.809; P = .042) (Table 2).

Longitudinal analysis

Longitudinal analysis was performed on data available from 74 patient charts. Fifty-one of these 74 (69%) had documented resolution of HMB during the study period. Repeated-measures correlation analysis across all visits among the 43 patients who had 2 or more PBAC scores showed a statistically significant correlation between PBAC scores and PHQ-9 scores (Rrm, 0.368; 95% CI, 0.129-0.568; P = .003).

Ferritin and/or Hgb values were documented at 2 or more visits in 68 patients. Repeated-measures correlation across all visits among 53 patients with 2 or more ferritin values and 66 patients with 2 or more Hgb values showed no significant correlation of PHQ-9 scores with either ferritin or Hgb. Among patients who showed a ≥5-ng/mL improvement in ferritin (n = 41), PHQ-9 scores did not significantly correlate with ferritin levels (Rrm, −0.151; 95% CI, −0.381 to 0.096; P = .23). Among patients who showed a >0.5-g/dL improvement in hemoglobin (n = 51), PHQ-9 scores decreased significantly (Rrm, −0.236; 95% CI, −0.437 to −0.013; P = .04).

PHQ-9 score improvement from ≥10 to ≤9 was neither influenced by oral versus IV iron therapy nor by hormonal versus antifibrinolytic therapy in a mixed-effects multivariate logistic regression model (Table 3). Of note, there was a significant association between HMB resolution and improvement of PHQ-9 scores in the model including oral/IV iron therapy as well as in the model including hormonal/antifibrinolytic therapy (Table 3). A principal component analysis confirmed the absence of undue influence of any single question in the PHQ-9 form on the overall results.

Discussion

The association between ID and depression has been reported in several studies in the past, but to our knowledge, this is the first descriptive chart review to evaluate the cross-sectional and longitudinal relationship among HMB, ID/IDA, and depressive symptoms in menstruating adolescents.

The association between HMB and depressive symptoms described in previous cross-sectional studies is supported by the statistically significant longitudinal correlation reported in our study; however this correlation is weak (Rrm < 0.4), and larger, more powered cohorts may be needed to confirm this finding [1,2].

The absence of association between a PHQ-9 score of ≥10 and ID in our study is similar to a previous report [10], but the significant association of PHQ-9 score of ≥10 with IDA is a distinct finding. This may be due to differences in patient demographics, study design, or both. In the previous study by McGrath et al. [10], more than 90% of the subjects were non-Hispanic, whereas almost half of our subjects were Hispanic (reflective of the population seen at our center). Of note, Hispanic ethnicity is associated with ID/IDA [17]. Overall, these demographic variations may have impacted the results of our study. Regarding study design, both studies used different definitions for ID and IDA. In our study, the results remained consistent with a ferritin cutoff level of <25 ng/dL versus <15 ng/mL. Further research through prospective studies may help to identify the ferritin thresholds associated with depression in adolescents with HMB.

The significant association between IDA and PHQ-9 score of ≥10 found on cross-sectional analysis was not corroborated by the initial longitudinal analysis using repeated-measures correlation across all visits. This may be because all patients attending our specialty clinics received counseling aimed at improving their depressive symptoms. Hence, the correlation was repeated specifically in subjects showing improvement of ferritin or Hgb values, that time showing significant reduction of PHQ-9 scores with improvement of IDA, but not with ID.

The lack of significant association between ID and PHQ-9 scores by either cross-sectional or longitudinal analyses is similar to another report in adolescents with HMB comparing ferritin levels with fatigue scores [18]. The results may be partly not only due to small sample size but also because ferritin values are known to be elevated due to inflammation. Ten patients in our study had a high CRP at the first visit, although notably, only 28 of the 123 first visits had a documented CRP value. The cross-sectional analysis was first done including all patients regardless of CRP values and then repeated excluding the patients with high CRP. Although our results remained unchanged whether the subjects with high CRP were included or excluded (results not shown for the analysis done after excluding the patients), it is possible that our results were affected by the presence of undocumented inflammation in other patients. This gap may be overcome by future prospective studies that include CRP for all patients or by using additional iron studies, such as transferrin saturation or soluble transferrin receptor levels.

The incidence of bleeding disorders in almost one-third of our study population is similar to a previously published multicenter study of 200 adolescents across the United States [19]. The percentage of patients with VWD among those with bleeding disorders is higher in our study (55%) compared with 25% in the study by Zia et al. [19]. This is likely related to the recent change in the definition of VWD [20]. Of note, the study by Zia et al. [19] reported 57% of patients having low von Willebrand factor levels.

The lack of a significant association between the presence of a bleeding disorder and PHQ-9 scores is similar to previous reports [10]. The low incidence of thyroid dysfunction in our study (0.8%) is also similar to a previous report of 427 adolescents with HMB (0.23%) [21].

A large percentage of our patients (41%) elected to use hormonal therapy with antifibrinolytics, which aligns with previous reports of both modalities combined leading to optimal menstrual control [22]. With the majority of our patients (109/120; 91%) receiving hormonal therapy, the association between individual HMB medications and PHQ-9 scores may not have been accurately assessed. Previous systematic reviews have shown levonorgestrel-releasing intrauterine devices and antifibrinolytics to be the most effective methods to reduce menstrual blood loss, but the evaluation of an association between these methods and PHQ-9 scores was limited in our study by a small sample size [23].

Depleted iron stores secondary to HMB may not be adequately replaced by oral iron, and this has led to increased use of IV iron in patients with HMB [24]. Although one-third of our population received IV iron therapy, it was not significantly associated with improvement of PHQ-9 scores. This is at least partly due to our inability to include patients who received IV iron in the inpatient setting as they did not have a concurrent PHQ-9 score.

The markedly significant association between obesity and depressive symptoms is similar to previous reports [25]. Although all 3 adolescents identifying as not female had a PHQ-9 score of ≥10, this sample size is too small to draw concrete conclusions.

ID itself causes fatigue and is associated with changes in affect and altered emotional behaviors [26]. With questions related to these symptoms forming an integral part of the PHQ-9 form, a principal component analysis was necessary to confirm that the scores reported in our study were due to underlying depression rather than ID. The results of our analysis validated that none of the individual PHQ-9 questions, including the question about fatigue, was the main driving factor toward the final PHQ-9 score, and hence, the PHQ-9 is reliable to diagnose depressive symptoms even in the presence of ID/IDA.

The main limitation of our study is its retrospective nature. Although our study excluded patients with intellectual disability who were unable to complete the PHQ-9 themselves, parental influence during completion of forms in clinic cannot be completely ruled out. Environmental factors such as parental discord, bullying, academic performance, recreational drug use, and sexual activity, which may impact PHQ-9 scores in adolescents, could not be considered due to lack of reliably consistent documentation of these details in a retrospective records review. Finally, although the use of antidepressant medications at baseline was taken into account and comprised only 11.4% of the cohort, the prescription of antidepressant medications from other providers and details of cognitive behavioral therapy and counseling were not reliably available to report and hence were not included in the longitudinal analyses.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates a significant association between IDA and a PHQ-9 score of ≥10 in adolescents with HMB. An improvement of PHQ-9 scores was observed when IDA improved, as well as when HMB improved, regardless of the treatment modalities used for HMB and IDA. Our findings highlight the importance of recognizing and optimally treating IDA and depressive symptoms in all adolescents with HMB.
