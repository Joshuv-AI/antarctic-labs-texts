Replacing soybean meal with distillers dried grains solubles on growth performance and feed utilization in catfish Clarias magur fingerlings
Authors: M. Gokulakrishnan, G. M. Siddaiah, Rakhi Kumari, N. Shamna, S. Ferosekhan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The present study explored the feasibility of the use of distillers dried grains with solubles (DDGS) to replace soybean meal (SBM) in the diet of magur, Clarias magur fingerlings. The experimental feeds were formulated by gradient replacement levels of SBM (0, 25, 50, 75, and 100%) via DDGS. Magur fingerlings (12.75 ± 0.4 g) were randomly stocked in triplicates in fiberglass tanks (10 fish/tank) and fed with their respective diets twice daily at the rate of 3% body weight per day. At the end of the 60-day feeding trial, the growth and feed utilization parameters were not significantly affected by up to 50% replacement level. However, higher replacement levels, such as 75% and 100%, significantly reduced the growth and feed utilization. Antioxidant properties, digestive enzyme activities, and serum and blood biochemistry, responded differently to the different treatments but mostly without any adverse effects at up to 50% replacement, indicating the potential of DDGS as a safe alternative protein source in the magur diet. To conclude, DDGS could be a viable alternative ingredient for the partial replacement of SBM for up to 50% in the magur diet, which could improve the sustainability and profitability of magur aquaculture. In the future, research may be directed toward supplementing limiting nutrients and optimizing processing conditions to further increase the replacement level of SBM by DDGS.

Introduction

Aquaculture has made its record high production of approximately 94 million tons (51% of the total) of fish and aquatic animals in 2022, surpassing that of marine capture fisheries for the first time ever1. The dynamic growth of the global aquaculture sector reversed the effects of stagnant marine capture fisheries and supported the nutritional security of the growing world population. This rapid development of the aquaculture sector through intensified production practices increased the demand for formulated aquaculture feed. Soybean meal (SBM) serves as a major protein ingredient in contemporary aqua feed production, and its sustainability is often disparaged2. Owing to its relatively high demand for aqua feed production, the use of arable land, water, and energy associated with its production has increased, which has ultimately reduced its availability for human consumption3. Alternatively, the valorization and utilization of byproducts from other agro-industrial sectors into fish feed production creates circularity in the aqua feed industry, reduces the dependency on SBM and thus enhances the sustainability of the aquaculture sector4.

Distillers dried grains with solubles (DDGS) is a major byproduct of the alcohol industry that obtained after the fermentation of corn, rice and wheat etc.5,. Ethanol industries use yeasts to convert starch from rice biomass into ethanol, and the remaining unfermentable components, including proteins, lipids and fibers, along with yeast fractions are collected and dried as DDGS meal. The nutritional composition of DDGS depends on the rice production and harvesting conditions and the different processing methods used in distilleries6. However, in general, the resulting DDGS pose high energy and protein values, along with high levels of vitamins and the functional benefits of yeast fraction7,8. The absence of antinutritional factors such as phytate and protease inhibitors adds additional value to DDGS in fish nutrition over other plant protein ingredients9,10. Moreover, its relatively high production volume, low cost and year-round availabilityimply the mass potential of DDGS to replace SBM in global aqua feed production and support a circular economy5,10,11.

Magur, Clarias maguralso called Asian catfish, is an important food fish in South Asian and Southeast Asian countries12. Magur is a highly priced fish on the Indian subcontinent because of its delicacy, nutritional value and less intramuscular bone13. Air breathing ability and the propensity to tolerate and grow at high stocking densities make them potential candidate species for high-density aquaculture practices14. The culture production of magur has steadily increased recently in the Indian subcontinent after the standardization of captive breeding and pond rearing by ICAR – Central Institute of Freshwater Aquaculture, Bhubaneswar, India. In magur culture, feed cost accounts for a major share of the total production cost. Currently, SBM remains a primary protein ingredient in the magur feed, which increases the feed production cost and questions its sustainability. Any reduction in SBM usage in feed results in more sustainable and profitable magur farming. On the other hand, DDGS is considered an excellent alternative protein source for SBM, and its high biological value in terms of fish nutrition over most other plant protein sources is well understood in several fish species. Studies have shown enhanced growth and feed utilization performance with partial replacement of SBM and other plant protein ingredients by DDGS in the diets of many cultured fish species, such as rainbow trout, Oncorhynchus mykiss15,16; channel catfish, Ictalurus punctatus17,18; turbot, Scophthalmus maximus19; Pacu, Piaractus mesopotamicus20; milkfish, Chanos chanos21; European catfish, Silurus glanis11; Nile tilapia, Oreochromis niloticus22; hybrid tilapia, Oreochromis niloticus × O. aureus23; grass carp, Ctenopharyngodon idellus24; gilthead seabream, Sparus aurata25; European sea bass, Dicentrarchus labrax26; and common carp, Cyprinus carpio L.27. Considering the potential of DDGS in fish diets, the present experiment was conducted with the aim of determining the optimal replacement level of SBM using DDGS in the diet of magur fingerlings without compromising growth, feed utilization performances. Additionally, hematological, biochemical indicators and whole-body nutrient compositions were determined to further elucidate the effect of SBM replacement by DDGS in the welfare of magur fingerlings.

Liver antioxidant enzymes

The responses of liver antioxidant enzyme activities to dietary SBM replacement in magur fingerlings are presented in Fig. 2 (A-E). The activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase, glutathione (GSH) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) were significantly affected by different dietary replacement levels (P < 0.05). The SOD and GSH activities in the liver homogenates showed a significant quadratic response trend toward replacement and were estimated to have the lowest values at approximately 24% (48 g/kg of feed) dietary SBM replacement with DDGS. LDH levels also showed a significant quadratic response trend but remained nearly stable up to 50% replacement level, with the highest value at 36%, which decreased at higher replacement levels. Nevertheless, there was no apparent linear or quadratic response trend in the catalase activity. Conversely, the activity of glutamate pyruvate transaminase (GPT) was not affected by dietary replacement (P >0.05).

Serum biochemical parameters

The serum biochemical responses to dietary SBM replacement with DDGS in magur fingerlings are presented in Fig. 3 (A-E). Total serum protein, albumin, globulin and the A: G ratio were significantly affected by the dietary replacement of SBM (P < 0.05). The total serum protein and globulin concentrations initially decreased at 25% replacement level and then gradually increased. The quadratic model effectively explained the response trend and revealed that the lowest total protein and globulin concentrations in the serum were 62% and 56% of replacement respectively. Nevertheless, there was no apparent linear or quadratic response trend observed in the serum albumin levels of the magur fingerlings. Consequently, the A: G ratio increased gradually up to 75% dietary SBM replacement and decreased at 100%. The quadratic response curve revealed the highest serum A.G. ratio at approximately 52% dietary SBM replacement via DDGS. The serum glucose levels were unaffected by dietary replacement of SBM with DDGS in the diet of magur fingerlings (P >0.05).

Hematological response

The effects of dietary SBM replacement on hematology and differential blood cell counts in magur fingerlings are presented in Fig. 4 (A-G). Dietary SBM replacement significantly affected the hemoglobin, red blood cells (RBC) counts, neutrophil and eosinophil counts in magur fingerlings (P < 0.05). The hemoglobin and RBC counts were the highest in 25% replacement level, which presented a significant quadratic response trend, with the highest hemoglobin level and RBC count to be around 27% and 32%, respectively. The number of eosinophils was lower in all treatment groups than in the control group, except for the 100% replacement diet, and the response curve displayed a quadratic trend, with the lowest eosinophil count occurring at approximately 44% SBM replacement. The neutrophil count was greater in the 50% replacement group than in all other treatment groups and did not significantly affect the response trend. Basophils and monocytes were found less than 1000 cells per cu.mm in all treatment groups, irrespective of replacement level. However, the white blood cells (WBC) counts, lymphocyte and platelet counts were not significantly affected by the dietary replacement of SBM (P >0.05).

Discussion

DDGS is a promising protein source for sustainable aqua feed production which is produced as the co-product in the ethanol industry. DDGS utilization in place of conventional plant protein sources such as SBM in aqua feeds has multiple environmental benefits, such as reduced energy, water, and arable land usage5. The present study revealed that despite a negative linear relationship, up to 50% (100 g/kg of feed) SBM could be replaced with DDGS without significantly affecting the growth and feed utilization performance of magur fingerlings. A similar negative linear response has also been observed when DDGS is included in the diet of hybrid tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus × Oreochromis aureus), indicating that up to 30% dietary DDGS inclusion is possible with a partial substitution of SBM (150 g/kg of feed) without significantly affecting growth and feed utilization performance23. Also, similar linear relationship on growth performance and dietary DDGS inclusion has been reported in channel catfish, Ictalurus punctatuswhen replaced with poultry meal18. In general, DDGS is considered a better source of methionine compared to SBM. However, lysine is the most limiting essential amino acid in DDGS compared with SBM, which limits its inclusion in aqua feeds7,25. Lysine is essential for muscle protein synthesis and collagen formation and its deficiency reduces actin and myosin protein production that consequently impairs muscle growth28,29. Hence, the replacement efficiency of DDGS over SBM for a fish species mostly depends on lysine availability in the feed, and to the best of our knowledge, no previous studies have elucidated the precise dietary requirement level of lysine in magur fish.

The potential of DDGS to support the growth of cultured fish is due to its various inherent nutritional qualities. For example, antinutritional factors like phytates are a major concern in the use of plant proteins like SBM as a primary ingredient in fish feed, as they decrease the nutrient availability of the ingested feed and cause growth restriction and water quality deterioration30,31. In addition to fiber, DDGS does not include any antinutritional factors, such as those in most other plant protein sources, which relatively increase the nutritional value of the feed when it is replaced with other plant protein feeds8. This may also indirectly reduce the energy usage of fish in ameliorating suboptimal environmental stress arising from undigested nutrients in the culture environment. Second, the presence of residual spent yeast biomass in DDGS amplifies its nutritional functionality when it is incorporated into the fish diet32estimated that at least 3.9% of DDGS biomass is contributed by residual spent yeast, which accounts for approximately 5.3% of the total protein content of DDGS33reviewed the bioactive potential of spent yeast biomass in promoting growth and other beneficial physiological responses in fish. In general, this residual yeast contains copious levels of bioactive compounds such as pyruvate, amino acids, vitamins, nucleic acids, beta glucans and mannan oligosaccharides that are collectively involved in feed attraction, growth promotion, stress resistance and immune enhancement in fish34. Additionally, supplementing spent yeast cells in the diet has been shown to fortify the autochthonous microflora of the fish gut with essential nutrients35,36, which helps increase the microbial degradation of complex fibers into digestible nutrients, thus increasing the nutritional value of DDGS. The unaffected growth and feed utilization performance of magur fingerlings with up to 50% SBM replacement in the present experiment is possibly because of the aforementioned nutritional qualities of DDGS. Alternatively, better growth and feed utilization were also observed in other fish species when the diet is incorporated with different levels of DDGS in place of SBM and other plant protein compounds. Approximately 30% dietary inclusion of DDGS with partial replacement of SBM (148.5 g/kg of feed) in the diet resulted in increased weight gain and feed efficiency in channel catfish, Ictalurus punctatus17. It is also reported that up to 375 g of total dietary protein kg−1 of feed from SBM and fishmeal could be replaced by high protein DDGS without any significant reduction in growth and feed efficiency in Nile tilapia Oreochromis niloticus22. Conversely, growth performance and feed efficiency were not compromised in gilthead seabream, Sparus aurata and milkfish, Chanos chanoseven when the SBM (200 g/kg of feed; 350 g/kg of feed, respectively) was completely replaced with DDGS21,25. However, the optimal inclusion level of DDGS in magur fingerlings from this experiment was relatively lower than that in other fish species, which might be attributed to various factors, including the amino acid requirements of different fish species, the nature and composition of other protein sources used in feed formulations and the experimental conditions.

The significant reduction in the growth and feed utilization performance of magur fingerlings more than 50% dietary SBM replacement (>100 g/kg of feed) in the present study is probably due to excessive fiber contents, nutrient imbalances and relative deficiency of lysine in the final feed. Certain nutrient imbalances, including lysine deficiency, could downregulate protein synthesis in fish by mediating amino acid sensitive mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway responsible for muscle protein transcription28. Excessive levels of DDGS inclusion in diets relatively increase the insoluble fiber content of the feed and affect the overall protein and energy digestibility of the ingested feed37 reported that higher levels of fibers in feed increase chime viscosity and affect chime movement, digestive enzyme activities, nutrient uptake capacity of microvilli in the fish gut, thus reducing growth. The significant negative response trend of gut protease activities in the present study further explains this construal interpretation. Similarly, a numerical reduction in protease activity was also reported in turbot (Scophthalmus maximus) fed with gradient levels of DDGS in the diet38. Additional supplementation with synthetic lysine and digestive enzymes along with feed might be beneficial in overcoming this problem and possibly increasing the inclusion levels of DDGS in the magur fingerling diet39–41.

The whole-body proximate compositions were not significantly affected in any of the treatment groups in the present study. However, the crude protein levels peaked numerically at the 50% replacement level, indicating an ideal supply of essential amino acids in the 50% replacement diet that might relatively increase their biomass deposition in magur fingerlings. According to42, the maximum N retention in fish is achieved when the dietary amino acid supply in the feed exactly meets the requirements of the fish. Nitrogen retention tends to be reduced when the diet supplies either excess or reduced amounts of essential amino acids to the fish species. In this way, it could be justified that 100 g/kg DDGS incorporated diet plausibly comprises a balanced amino acid profile for supporting growth and protein deposition in magur fingerlings. Similarly, the body composition of fish species such as gilthead seabream, Sparus aurata25, hybrid tilapia, Oreochromis niloticus × Oreochromis aureus23, and Nile tilapia Oreochromis niloticus22 was unaffected when the diet was incorporated with DDGS.

The antioxidant defense mechanism is a crucial physiological process in fish that fights against oxidative stress, especially in confined culture environments. The antioxidant system in fish is backed by a group of antioxidant enzymes, and their levels could be used as health indicators for culturing fish43. SOD is responsible for the conversion of superoxide anions into hydrogen peroxide in mitochondria, where intense energy production takes place. In the present study, liver SOD activity did not considerably differ at up to 75% replacement level, which indicates no difference in oxidative stress compared with that in the control diet. Moreover, the GSH activities decreased up to 50% SBM replacement. Similarly, reduced GSH activity was detected in grass carp, Ctenopharyngodon idellus24, and gilthead seabream, Sparus aurata25, at low concentrations of DDGS in the feed. These enhancements in the antioxidant defense system in magur fingerlings might be attributed to exogenous antioxidant compounds found in yeast fractions, such as vitamin C, free amino acids (glutamine, glutamic acid), nonpolar peptides (Cyclo His-Pro), and minerals33,44–46. However, the mechanism of action for higher SOD and GSH activities at very high replacement levels is unclear in magur fingerlings. LDH plays a crucial role in aerobic glycolysis by catalyzing the conversion of pyruvate into lactic acid. Changes in the liver LDH activities are often associated with altered energy metabolism either due to oxidative stress or dietary imbalances47. The significant reduction in liver LDH activity at 100% SBM replacement with DDGS suggests impaired carbohydrate metabolism due to the high insoluble fiber content of DDGS, which can reduce glucose availability and shift metabolism toward protein or lipid oxidation, which has reflected in the poor growth performances of magur fingerlings. Moreover, lower LDH activities could also elucidated by mitochondrial dysfunction of hepatocytes due to chronic metabolic stress which complemented by the increased levels SOD activities in magur fingerlings at 100% SBM replaced diet48.

The total serum protein, albumin, and globulin levels were significantly lower in the DDGS-incorporated group than in the control group in the present study. This could be attributed to reduced protein digestion as well as digestible protein utilization of magur fingerlings. The relative increase in the A: G ratio indicates that globulins are more prone to be affected by dietary DDGS incorporation than albumins. Our results were in accordance with38, where the serum protein components of the turbot Scophthalmus maximus were reduced significantly when DDGS was incorporated into the diet. In contrast, opposite responses have been reported in the European sea bass Dicentrarchus labrax41. However, no deviation in serum protein levels was observed in gilthead seabream, Sparus aurata, when fed with DDGS-containing diet in place of SBM25. The unaffected serum glucose levels of the magur fingerlings in the present study are in agreement with those of other fish species, such as gilthead seabream, Sparus aurata25; turbot, Scophthalmus maximus38; grass carp, Ctenopharyngodon idellus24,49; and common carp, Cyprinus carpio27, which are fed DDGS-incorporated diets. The significantly increasing trend of blood hemoglobin and RBC counts at lower replacement levels indicates an efficient oxygen supply system to support complex metabolic processes inside the fish body and might play a crucial role in preserving the growth performance of magur fingerlings in the SBM replaced diet. Similar responses were also observed in the European sea bass Dicentrarchus labraxfingerlings upon dietary inclusion of DDGS26,40,41. These increases in hemoglobin and RBC counts might be due to the increased levels of B vitamins and iron in the yeast fractions of DDGS biomass, which are crucial for hematopoiesis in fish50. However, the precise cause for the decrease in eosinophils in the DDGS-incorporated diet, with the exception of D100, is relatively obscure, and to the best of our knowledge, no previous studies have reported on eosinophil counts in fish upon DDGS-incorporated diet.

Conclusion

In the present study, the dietary replacement of SBM with DDGS caused a gradual decrease in the overall growth performance of magur fingerlings. However, up to 100 g/kg of dietary DDGS in place of SBM does not significantly affect the growth and feed utilization of magur. In summary, about 100 g of SBM can be optimally replaced with DDGS in each kilogram of magur fingerling feed, which reduces the overall feed cost and ensures circularity and sustainability in magur farming. In the future, studies may be directed toward the supplementation of lysine and digestive enzymes in magur feeds to further increase DDGS inclusion levels and promote sustainability in magur aquaculture.

Materials and methods

Ethical statement

The present study was conducted with approval from the ethical committee of ICAR – Central Institute of Freshwater Aquaculture, Bhubaneswar, India. All procedures and protocols for handling live fish adhered to the CPCSEA guidelines (Committee for the Purpose of Control and Supervision of Experiments on Animals, Ministry of Environment and Forests, Government of India) and complied with current animal welfare laws in India. The experiment was conducted in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Fish husbandry and feeding trial

The feeding trial was conducted in the catfish hatchery complex of the ICAR – Central Institute of Freshwater Aquaculture, Bhubaneswar, India. Two hundred similar-sized magur fingerlings with an initial body weight of 10–12 g (n = 20) were collected from outdoor concrete tanks and acclimatized in the indoor experimental fiberglass tanks for two weeks. Fingerlings were fed on a control diet (35% CP; 6% CL) during acclimatization to satiety. Acclimatized fish (12.75 ± 0.4 g) were then stocked in triplicate in fifteen randomly assigned circular fiberglass tanks each containing 150 L of water at a stocking density of 10 fish per tank. The fingerlings were fed with their corresponding experimental diets twice daily (09:00 and 16:00) at 3% body weight per day for two months. Total fish biomass from individual tanks were weighed after every two weeks to adjust the feeding quantity. The natural light regime and sufficient aeration supply were maintained throughout the feeding trial. The fecal matter and uneaten feed were siphoned out daily, and nearly 15% of the water was replaced to preserve the water quality for the optimal growth of the experimental fish. The temperature and dissolved oxygen content were measured daily and ranged from 28 to 29.1 °C (HANNA instruments, Belgium) and 4.5–8.5 mg L−1 (HANNA instruments, Belgium), respectively. Other water quality parameters, such as pH (8.1–9.2) (Labman, India), total ammonia nitrogen (TAN) (0.01–0.24 mg L−1) and nitrite (< 0.50 mg L−1), were measured twice a week following standard methods51with a Thermo Scientific Orion AquaMate 8100 UV–Visible spectrophotometer and the values found to be within the optimal range for magur52.

Experimental diets

Five isonitrogenous (35% CP approximate) and isolipidic (6% CL approximate) diets were formulated by replacing graded levels of (0%, 25%, 50%, 75% and 100%) SBM in the diet with DDGS and named as DG0, DG25, DG50, DG75 and DG100, respectively. Feed ingredients such as fishmeal, fish oil, SBM (animal grade), groundnut oil cake (GNOC), maize, deoiled rice bran (DORB), vegetable oil and vita-min premix were procured from the local market, whereas DDGS meal (46.8% CP dry basis) was obtained from a local rice distillery. The proximate compositions of DDGS and SBM used in this experiment were summarized in Table 3. The diets were prepared and stored according to the methods of34 using a mechanical pelletizer with a 2 mm diameter sieve. Collection and preservation of samples

At the end of the feeding trial, the experimental fish were starved for one day before sample collection. The total number and biomass of fish in individual tanks were subsequently determined to compute the growth performance, feed utilization and survival rate of the experimental fish. The fish from respective dietary group were collected using hand net and euthanized using clove oil (eugenol) at a concentration of 150 ppm for 10 min and the euthanized fish were sampled for total length, weight measurement and blood samples were collected from the caudal peduncle (23G needle and 1 ml syringe) and stored into an anticoagulant-coated tubes for hematology. For serum separation, the blood samples collected via an unheparinized syringe were allowed to clot overnight at 4 °C, followed by centrifugation (2000xg for 10 min) and stored immediately at −80 °C for further biochemical analyses. Three euthanized fish per tank were randomly collected for whole-body proximate analysis, and six euthanized fish per tank were selected for serum and blood collection. Among these, three euthanized fish per tank were dissected to obtain the liver and intestine for assaying metabolic and digestive enzymes, respectively. The collected liver and intestine samples were stored immediately at −80 °C until enzyme activities were estimated. However, the hematological analysis of blood samples was carried out on the same day.

Biochemical analysis

Liver and intestine tissue (5%) homogenates were prepared using 0.25 M ice-cold sucrose solution (1:9 w/v) as described by53. After centrifugation at 10,000 × gfor 15 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was collected and stored at − 20 °C until enzymatic activity was measured. The total protein concentration in the intestine and liver extracts was estimated by the method of54, using bovine serum albumin as the standard.

Antioxidant and metabolic enzyme assays

SOD activity was measured following the method of55based on the oxidation of epinephrine to adrenochrome by the enzyme. Catalase activity was determined according to56using a reaction mixture of 2.45 ml of phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7), 50 µl of tissue homogenate, and 1 ml of freshly prepared hydrogen peroxide substrate, with the reduction in absorbance monitored at 240 nm for 3 min. GSH activity was assessed by the method of57using S-2,4-dinitrophenyl glutathione (CDNB) as a substrate. Hepatic GPT activity was measured by quantifying pyruvate release after 60 min of incubation at 37 °C, according to58. LDH activity was determined in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.5) with 0.2 mM NADH, initiated by adding 0.2 mM sodium pyruvate as the substrate, and optical density (OD) changes at 340 nm were recorded based on the method of59.

Serum total protein, albumin, Globulin and the A: G ratio

Serum total protein was quantified using the biuret method60, and albumin was measured via the bromocresol green binding method61, both performed calorimetrically with diagnostic kits (Diatek Diagnostics, West Bengal, India) and a UV-Vis Spectrophotometer (LI-2800, Lasany International, India). Serum globulin levels and albumin‒globulin (AG) ratio were calculated from the measured total protein and albumin values using the following formulae.

Blood glucose

Blood glucose levels were estimated using the Nelson-Somogyi method, as described by62. Briefly, 500 µl of blood was deproteinized with zinc sulfate, followed by the addition of 4.75 ml of barium hydroxide. The mixture was vigorously shaken and filtered, and the resulting supernatant was used for colorimetric glucose estimation at 540 nm, which was measured against a blank.

Hematological analysis

Hemoglobin levels and differential cell counts, such as RBC, WBC, platelet, neutrophil, eosinophil, basophil, monocyte and lymphocyte counts, were determined from the collected blood samples via an automatic blood cell analyzer (HOPE Diagnostics laboratory, Bhubaneswar, India).

Digestive enzyme assay

Intestinal amylase activity was assayed using 1% (w/v) soluble starch as the substrate, following the method of63, with enzyme activity calculated from a maltose standard curve. Protease activity was quantified using the azo-casein digestion method described by64. Intestinal lipase activity was determined using a modified microplate-based method with 20 mM p-nitrophenyl myristate as the substrate, following the protocol of65.

Proximate composition

Standard procedures were employed to analyze the proximate compositions of the experimental feeds and the whole-body fish samples66. The moisture contents were determined by oven drying at 105 °C until a constant weight was reached. The crude protein contents were estimated by the Kjeldahl method with a 6.25 conversion factor (KEL PLUS, Pelican Equipments, Chennai, India). The crude lipid contents were determined by Soxhlet method (SOC PLUS, Pelican Equipments) and acid/alkali digestion was performed to measure the crude fiber contents of the samples (FIBRA PLUS, Pelican Equipments, Chennai, India). To determine the ash content, the samples were incinerated at 600 °C for 12 h in a muffle furnace.

Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were performed in SPSS version 25 (IBM, USA), and all the data are expressed as Mean ± SEM. Firstly, the data set was subjected to normality and homogeneity checks via the Kolmogorov‒Smirnoff test and Levene’s test, respectively. One-way ANOVA was performed with a threshold confidence level of 95% (P = 0.05) followed by orthogonal polynomial contrast to determine the significance response model (linear and quadratic) for all the dependent variables analyzed in the present study. Additionally, the growth and feed utilization parameters were analyzed with Tukey´s HSD to determine the best treatment group that optimally replaced SBM with DDGS.
