Human-bat Competition on Cultivated Fruit Resources Promotes Bat-borne Pathogens Spillover to Humans and Domestic Animals in Bangladesh: An Exploratory Qualitative Study
Authors: A K M Dawlat Khan, Md Arif Khan, Pronesh Dutta, Monjurul Islam, Nabila Nujhat Chowdhury, Shusmita Dutta Choudhury, Tahmina Shirin, Ariful Islam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNipah virus (NiV) outbreaks have recurred nearly annually in Bangladesh since 2001, with a fatality >70%. These outbreaks are primarily linked to the consumption of raw date palm sap (RDPS), which is contaminated by Indian flying fox bats. Due to the rapidly changing ecology, bats increasingly depend on cultivated fruits. To address this, we observed how humans and domestic animals compete and interact with bats for cultivated fruits facilitate pathogen transmission to humans and animals.MethodsWe conducted an exploratory qualitative study in 4 NiV outbreak districts of Bangladesh spanning both the summer (April–June) and winter (December–February) of 2021 and 2022, including 60 ethnographic interviews and 24 observations with fruit orchard owners and RDPS harvesters.ResultsAlmost all participants reported frequent visits of fruit bats to cultivated fruit orchards and RDPS trees. They noted wild fruit trees are scarce in their localities due to deforestation, resulting in bats turning to feed on cultivated fruits and RDPS. Participants also reported consuming bat-bitten dropped fruits and RDPS, occasionally feeding them to domestic animals, which also consume these fruits while grazing in the orchards. Orchard owners often use nets to protect their fruits from bats, and are exposed to bats during removal from the nets. Even some local ethnic people collect the trapped bats for consumption. Although RDPS harvesters use protective measures, bats scratch them to access and consume sap.ConclusionsThe study's findings highlight the significance of increasing human-bat and domestic animal-bat food competition on cultivated fruits. We recommend future studies on ecological and behavioral interventions to prevent bat-borne pathogen spillover to humans and animals.

Bats are natural reservoirs of many emerging infectious diseases like Nipah virus (NiV), Hendra virus (HeV), Ebola, and Marburg virus, and severe acute respiratory syndrome-like coronaviruses [1–3], responsible for significant human morbidity and mortality [4, 5]. Moreover, bat-borne viruses caused fatal infections in some domestic animals; NiV in Malaysia [6, 7], HeV in Australia [8, 9], and HeV in the Philippines [10]. In addition, NiV antibodies in cattle, dogs, and cats were identified in Bangladesh [11].

Humans were infected with bat-borne viruses through contact with contaminated trees by bat urine and feces while resting under the orchard trees, and climbing the fruit trees [12–14]. Pteropus medius, a large frugivorous bat found throughout the world, including Bangladesh and neighboring country India, has been identified as a natural reservoir for NiV [15–17]. The global fatality rate of human NiV cases has been estimated to be ∼61% [18]. However, in Bangladesh, the fatality rate is ∼71%, with nearly annual outbreaks [19, 20] occurring most commonly through the drinking of raw date palm sap (RDPS) or fermented date palm sap (locally referred to as tari) contaminated with bat saliva, urine, and feces [13, 21].

Rapidly changing ecological conditions have made bats increasingly dependent on human-cultivated fruits. Consumption of half-eaten fruits has not been directly linked to human NiV infection, but it was the putative route of NiV transmission from pteropid bats to pigs in Malaysia (bat→fruit→pig→human) [12, 14] and isolation of NiV from partially bat-eaten fruits [22]. There are also hypotheses for NiV outbreak in Kerala (bat→fruit→human) [23]. NiV can survive for several days in sugar-rich solutions like RDPS [24]. Limited research exists on cultivated fruit resources and their relations to bat-borne pathogens spillover to humans and domestic animals throughout the world. Furthermore, South Asia remains a significant geographic gap in our understanding of spillover dynamics, including direct bat-to-human transmission and the consumption of bat-bitten fruits, which underscores the need to explore interactions between humans, domestic animals, and wildlife in pathogen transmission. To address this, we conducted a study to explore how humans and domestic animals compete and interact with bats for cultivated fruits facilitate pathogen transmission to humans and animals.

METHODS

Study Design and Study Sites

We performed an exploratory qualitative study in 4 NiV outbreak districts: Dinajpur, Naogaon, Rajshahi, and Rajbari, in Bangladesh (Figure 1) in the summer and winter seasons of 2021 and 2022 .We selected those districts based on repeated NiV outbreaks reported [20] and known for mango, lychee, and RDPS harvesting areas [25–27]. The study was conducted in the months of April to June on orchard owners when the local cultivated fruits (mango and lychee) are ripe, and bats visited the orchards. Besides, we also observed RDPS harvesting and bat interactions in the months of December to February.

Map showing 4 study sites—Dinajpur, Naogaon, Rajshahi, and Rajbari districts of Bangladesh.

Study Participants and Data Collection

We conducted 60 qualitative interviews with fruit orchard owners, including 15 mango and 14 lychee orchard owners/workers, and 31 RDPS harvesters. All interview participants were selected purposively, focusing on mixed-scaled RDPS harvesters and orchard owners, based on experience and study locations [28]. Through the interviews, we collected data on fruit harvesting and RDPS collection practices, protective measures on trees of their orchards and RDPS trees, and the use of bat-bitten dropped-off fruits. All interviews were conducted in Bangla and recorded on a voice recorder following a semistructured interview guideline (Supplementary 1 and 2).

To complement the interviews, we conducted 24 participant observations in orchards and on RDPS trees. Of these, 10 observations took place in orchards, including 6 in mango orchards and 4 in lychee orchards. Observations were carried out in 3 daily time blocks—5:00 Am to 11:00 Am for documenting human interactions with bats and bats' excreta, 12:00 Pm to 5:00 Pm for recording domestic animals grazing and collection of bat-bitten dropped fruits for consumption by humans, and 6:00 Pm to 11:00 Pm to document animal visits to orchards and fruit trees, and RDPS trees following a guideline (Supplementary 3, 4). We observed 14 RDPS trees managed by 14 different sap collectors, focusing on materials used for sap collection, animal visits to the trees, and protective measures employed to prevent animals, particularly bats, from accessing the collection pots (Supplementary 4). In the afternoon, we documented activities such as tree shaving, cleaning, collection pot placement, and the installation of protective materials. In the evening, we observed bat access to RDPS trees over a period of 3 to 4 hours. All observations were systematically recorded in field notebooks, and significant events were documented through photographs and video recordings.

Data Analysis

The audio-recorded interviews were transcribed verbatim to the greatest extent possible. The interview transcripts were reviewed multiple times to identify emerging concepts and themes, which were developed using the grounded theory approach [29]. Additionally, data analysis followed an inductive approach [30]. Key themes that emerged included the “unavailability of wild fruits,’ “bat dependency on human-cultivated fruits,” “conflicts between bats and fruit growers,” and “conflicts between bats and domestic animals.” Representative quotes were selected to illustrate specific themes and highlight commonalities and differences. Observation data were triangulated with interview findings to enhance the validity [31].

RESULTS

Socio-demographic Characteristics of Study Participants

All participants were male with a median age of 40 years (range: 18–71, IQR: 17.75). Almost half of the participants (45%, 27/60) possessed a primary level of education, and around one-third (28%, 17/60) individuals had not received any formal education. Only 10 participants had attained a secondary level of education, and 6 had education beyond the secondary level. Participants reported that fruit harvesting is their seasonal source of supplemental income in the summer, and RDPS is a source of supplemental income in the winter, not as a primary occupation. Agriculture and/or crop production serve as the primary source of income for the majority, with 75% (45/60) individuals relying on these activities. Other professions included grocers, school teachers, fruit businessmen, and van (rickshaw) paddlers. The average monthly family expenditure for their households amounted to Bangladeshi Taka BDT 14 209 (equivalent to US$130). Wild Fruits’ Unavailability

According to the study participants, fruit trees were fewer in number in the local community; because of this, the local people plant more timber trees than fruit trees for economic purposes. They also reported that people want to get immediate benefits from timber trees when they need them. The people, particularly the poor, sell timber trees, earn money, and use this for their various activities, such as treatments, child education, and other crop cultivation.

The study participants reported, and our observations reveal that the local community plants Indian jujube, guava, sapodilla, mango, and lychee fruit trees in their backyards. However, participants reported that some people cultivate mango and lychee fruits commercially in farming systems. Participants reported that bats feed on these human-cultivated fruits and wild fruits like figs, bur flower trees, red silk cotton, and banyan. They also reported that these wild fruit trees are decreasing in the communities due to less economic value and deforestation. According to the participants’ opinions, hence, bats are heavily dependent on human-cultivated fruit resources and RDPS drinking. They also reported that RDPS is a winter delicacy for humans in Bangladesh. However, RDPS is not a natural food for bats. People make bats habituated in RDPS due to harvesting. In addition, participants reported that the number of fruit items is less in the winter season.

“In the winter, fewer fruits are in nature; bats are attracted to drinking sap.” [45 years old male RDPS collector]

Sap harvesters (87%, 27/31) reported, and our observations (64%, 9/14) also found that Pteropus and non-Pteropus bats and rodents visit the trees, drink RDPS, and contaminate it with their saliva, urine, and feces. Some harvesters protect the sap from these animals by implementing protective measures such as bamboo skirts, jute and plastic bags, and nylon nets to protect bat access. They also reported that in recent years, there has been an increase in bats” visits to RDPS trees and bats have resorted to scratching the protection apparatuses and trunks to access and consume sap.

“Occasionally, I noticed broken and displaced bamboo skirts; I saw bats biting on the skirt and trying to lick the sap.” [55 years RDPS collector]

Conflict Between Bats and Humans

All of our 10 observations in the orchards and all 29 orchard owners reported that bats visit their fruit trees and eat the fruit. They also observed that bats visit their trees, eat fruits, and destroy or drop them on the ground. Most of the orchard owners (89.6%, 26/29), especially lychee fruit growers, use nets and/or create sounds to protect bats visiting their orchards by scaring them. Sometimes, bats entangled in the net and died when they came to the orchard to take cultivated fruits (Figure 2).

Bat trapped on the net of the trees that the orchard owners use to protect fruits (lychee).

“Bats visit my garden at night, eat and damage my fruits. I use nets to protect fruits; sometimes bats are trapped on the net and die.” [40 years orchard owner]

The orchard owners (24%, 7/29) reported that trapped bats make noise for leaving out from the net. The orchard owners use this trapped bat's noise as bait. The other bats do not come to the trees by hearing this noise. On average, 2 to 3 days after being trapped, the bat died. The orchard owners remove the dead bats from the net. They are exposed to bats during removal from the nets. All orchard owners do not use any kind of protection like gloves during the removal of the bats. Few orchard owners (13.8%, 4/29) reported that sometimes, children remove the live bats from the net and play with the bats. Furthermore, they reported that some local ethnic people collect the trapped bats for consumption and medicinal purposes.

All community people do not have fruit trees, or they cannot buy fruits due to a lack of money. According to the study participants, poor adults and their children pick up and eat the dropped-off fruits and bat-bitten fruits beneath the fruit trees. Not only the children of poor households but also rich households pick up and eat the bat-bitten dropped-off fruits in the orchard areas while playing together in the orchard (Figure 3). Some of the orchard owners sometimes pick up the dropped-off fruits to avoid economic loss, particularly in mango.

Children playing and eating bat-bitten dropped fruits while playing in the orchard.

“We are poor, we do not buy fruits, we pick up dropped-off fruits from the garden early in the morning.” [50 years old orchard workers]

Conflict Between Bats and Domestic Animals

Our observations (70%, 7/10) and all the 29 participants reported that domestic animals eat dropped fruit while grazing in orchards (Figure 4). The participants (41.4%, 12/29) also reported that they occasionally feed bat-bitten dropped-off fruits to their household animals, particularly cattle, goats, and pigs (Figure 5).

Domestic animals eating bat-bitten dropped fruit while grazing in the fruit orchard.

Domestic animals (cattle, goat, and pig) feeding on bat-bitten dropped fruits.

“A big-sized mango usually is weighted 500 grams to 1 kg (kilogram), we do not want to leave this. Although we do not sell this, we eat by cutting the damaged parts. We collect dropped-off fruits that we cannot eat (due to much damage) and feed them to our household animals.” [30 years old orchard owner]

Participants’ Knowledge of Bat-borne Diseases

The participants (36.7%, 22/60) identified several illnesses, including diarrhea and vomiting, as potential health risks associated with consuming bat-bitten fruits. A small subset (11.7%, 7/60) demonstrated awareness of NiV transmission through bat saliva and urine, with 2 of these individuals being fruit growers.

DISCUSSION

Our findings show that most of the orchard owners and RDPS harvesters open see fruit bats in their trees. These indicate that bats’ dependency on cultivated fruits is increasing due to the lack of wild fruits, resulting in more frequent human-bat interactions, which may lead to bat-borne virus spillover.

Fruit Seasonality and Deforestation in Bangladesh

Natural forests have been declining due to increasing demands for housing, industrial expansion, infrastructure development, and agricultural expansion. Bangladesh is one of the most densely populated countries in the world, with a population of 165 million living within 147 570 square kilometers [32], which has lower forest coverage than the standard [33]. In Bangladesh, bats primarily feed on natural fruits that mature during the summer and rainy seasons [34, 35]. However, from November to April, natural fruit availability declines [34] when the outbreaks of bat-borne NiV tend to occur during the same period of fruit scarcity [36]. In Bangladesh, natural forests have declined by 8.7% between 2002 and 2023, due to increasing demands for housing, industrial expansion, infrastructure development, and agricultural expansion [33]. In addition, the number of wild fruit trees is decreasing due to ongoing deforestation in our study sites, the northwestern part of Bangladesh [26] Furthermore, fruit tree plantations are less common than timber plantations due to their lower economic returns [37], leading bats to consume cultivated fruits, particularly RDPS, as an alternative food source due to the scarcity of natural wild fruits in forest areas [34, 38]. This dependency highlights the growing interaction between bats and agricultural landscapes, driven by shifts in land use.

Risk of Bat-borne Virus Spillover to Humans

Direct contact with bats during their removal from the nets in fruit trees, as well as the consumption of bats as bushmeat, poses a spillover risk for bat-borne viruses to humans [39, 40]. Furthermore, the interaction between bats and the local ethnic community people increases this risk as trapped bats from cultivated fruit trees are readily available and consumed at no cost [41]. Consuming bat-bitten dropped fruits risks of bat-borne viruses’ spillover to humans, as there is evidence of NiV in bat-bitten fruit samples [22]. Bat-origin viruses are among the most significant emerging zoonotic pathogens that transmit from wildlife to domestic animals and humans, and gaining insights into how these infections navigate ecological systems to ultimately impact human health is crucial for public health. Increased human-bat interactions, driven by land-use changes and seasonal resource scarcity due to habitat loss or climate change, elevate the risk of bat-borne viral spillover to humans and domestic animals as bats seek alternative food sources—human-cultivated fruits [42]. Understanding these ecological and behavioral factors is essential for mitigating public health risks.

Risk of Bat-borne Virus Spillover to Domestic Animals

Bat-bitten dropped-off fruit eating during grazing and feeding is the risk of zoonotic disease transmission to domestic animals. Bat-borne pathogens spillover from bats to domestic animals is evidenced—NiV in Malaysia and Bangladesh, and Hendra in Australia and the Philippines, which have caused morbidity and mortality [6, 10, 43, 44]. Subsequently, infected domestic animals transmit viruses to humans while caring or by contact with them [22, 43]. Furthermore, bat-borne viral infections can lead to significant economic losses for farmers due to the death or culling of domesticated animals [6]. Bangladesh is geographically positioned in a region highly susceptible to the emergence of zoonotic diseases due to its rich wildlife diversity, dense human population, and extensive interactions among humans, domestic animals, and wildlife [45, 46]. Besides, the free grazing land for domestic animals is rapidly decreasing due to human settlement and industrial development. A national study conducted by Openshaw, in 207 villages in Bangladesh, found more illnesses and deaths among the domestic animals that were grazed and fed dropped or bat-bitten fruits [47]. The spillover of bat-borne viruses to domestic animals might increase the risk of zoonotic disease transmission to humans, leading to potential outbreaks and public health threats.

CONCLUSIONS

This study highlights the intricate human-bat and domestic animal-bat interactions driven by fruit seasonality, deforestation, and economic necessity in Bangladesh. The heavy dependency of bats on human-cultivated fruits and RDPS, alongside human handling of bat-bitten fruits and trapped bats, underscores the potential risk of zoonotic virus spillover. Additionally, domestic animals feeding on bat-bitten fruits may act as intermediate hosts, further amplifying the transmission risk. These findings emphasize the urgent need for integrated public health and ecological interventions such as educational outreach programs should be implemented throughout the cultivated fruits and RDPS harvesting districts to increase awareness among the fruit orchard owners and RDPS harvesters’ connection between fruit and sap contamination and NiV transmission, which may increase the adoption of protective measures among the harvesters and other stakeholders. In addition, public awareness programs included the announcement of zoonotic risks of NiV, avoiding consuming half-eaten and dropped fruits, and RDPS through the local authorities, and electronic and print media. Future research should focus on assessing viral spillover and its presence in humans, domestic animals, and bat-bitten fruits, to inform targeted interventions in the one health approach, ultimately reducing the risk of emerging infectious diseases in Bangladesh.
