Outcomes of Successful Versus Failed Stenting in Patients With Unilateral Atherosclerotic Renal Artery Occlusion
Authors: Pengyu Li, Ziguang Yan, Letao Lin, Bihui Zhang, Guochen Niu, Min Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTStenting for renal artery occlusion (RAO) remains a subject of considerable debate. We aim to observe whether stenting can improve the clinical outcomes of patients with RAO. Patients with atherosclerotic unilateral RAO and at least 12 months of follow‐up were included (n = 42) and were divided into the stenting group (n = 30) and the failed‐stenting group (n = 12) based on their surgical outcomes. Blood pressure, serum creatinine, and clinical end point (major adverse cardiovascular or renal events) were recorded. At the last follow‐up, there was no significant difference in BP and medication usage between the two groups of patients. Compared with failed revascularization, successful stenting was associated with reduced risk for renal function deterioration (p = 0.035) and clinical end point (p = 0.009). Kaplan–Meier (K–M) analysis showed a benefit of stenting on event‐free survival (log‐rank p = 0.029) and dialysis‐free survival (log‐rank p = 0.049). In conclusion, stenting plus medical therapy is effective in slowing the deterioration of renal function and preventing clinical events in patients with atherosclerotic unilateral RAO.Keywords: clinical outcome, percutaneous transluminal renal angioplasty, renal artery occlusion, stenting

Introduction

Renal artery stenosis (RAS) is highly prevalent among elderly populations and is particularly common in individuals with underlying coronary or aortic arch artery disease, underscoring the critical need for systematic diagnostic evaluation and a proactive interventional approach in these high‐risk groups to mitigate adverse renal and cardiovascular outcomes [1]. However, based on negative results from three large‐scale randomized controlled trials (RCTs), STAR, ASTRAL, and CORAL, medication was recommended as first‐line treatment rather than revascularization for patients with atherosclerotic RAS [2, 3, 4, 5]. Indications of revascularization for atherosclerotic RAS were becoming more and more restricted. High‐grade RAS was reported to be rapidly progressing to renal artery occlusion (RAO) and leading to severe kidney function impairment [6]. Endovascular treatment of RAO is controversial owing to that kidney dysfunction is often irreversible when patients have severe atrophic kidneys. Patients with bilateral lesions often experience circulatory overload, necessitating timely revascularization or renal‐replacement therapy. Recanalization for unilateral occlusive lesion is more controversial. The aim of this article is to observe whether stenting can improve the clinical outcomes of patients with unilateral atherosclerotic RAO during long‐term follow‐up.

Materials and Methods

Patients

Indications of stenting for RAO patients were as previously described [7]. Inclusion criteria for this study were: (1) patients with atherosclerotic unilateral RAO; (2) stenosis of the contralateral renal artery ≤50%; (3) patients with more than 12 months of follow‐up. Patients were divided into the stenting group and the failed‐stenting group based on their surgical outcomes.

Stenting Procedure and Post‐Operative Care

Stenting procedures were performed as previously described [7]. After stenting, dual antiplatelet therapy of aspirin (100 mg) with clopidogrel (75 mg) was prescribed for at least 3 months, followed by lifelong aspirin or clopidogrel. Anti‐hypertensive and glucose‐ and lipid‐lowering drugs were continued in all patients according to patients’ conditions. Blood pressure, serum creatinine and clinical end point (major adverse cardiovascular or renal events) were recorded. Follow‐up visit was recommended at 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 12 months after discharge and subsequently every 6–12 months thereafter. Routine renal artery ultrasound was performed at each follow‐up. If severe restenosis (>60%) of the stent was indicated, angiography was recommended for patients with blood pressure or creatinine fluctuations. If there were no symptomatic changes, angiography was not recommended even if severe stenosis is indicated. In cases of confirmed severe stenosis, balloon angioplasty was performed.

Outcome Evaluation

The clinical end point was defined the occurrence of major adverse cardiovascular or renal events according to CORAL study [4]: death from cardiovascular or renal causes, myocardial infarction (MI), congestive heart failure, stroke, progressive renal insufficiency, or the need for permanent renal‐replacement therapy (PRRT). Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the Modification of Diet in Renal Disease (MDRD) formula corrected for Chinese [8].

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Version 20 (IBM, Armonk, NY). Results are presented as median and interquartile range. Continuous variables were analyzed using Wilcoxon signed‐rank test. Categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi‐squared test or Fisher exact test, as propriate. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed using the log‐rank statistic to compare time‐to‐event outcomes between the treatment groups. p value < 0.05 were considered to be statistically significant.

Results

From January 2007 to December 2021, angiography was performed in patients who were suspected of atherosclerotic RAS. Fifty patients were confirmed with unilateral RAO with stenosis of the contralateral renal artery ≤ 50%. A total of 42 patients with at least one‐time of follow‐up were included in this study. The median follow‐up time was 28.5 (19.5, 54.3) months. Thirty patients with occluded renal arteries were successfully recanalized, while 12 cases experienced failed revascularization. Primary reasons for failed recanalization include invisible stump of the occluded renal artery and inability to confirm re‐entry into the true lumen. At the last follow‐up, there was no significant difference in BP and medication usage between the two groups of patients (Table 2). In the stenting group, both Systolic BP and Diastolic BP were significantly lower (p < 0.001 and p = 0.002 at follow‐up compared to preoperative levels, with no significant difference in the number of medications taken (p = 0.166). However, there was no significant difference in BP control in the failed‐stenting group compared to before angiography (p = 0.513 for Systolic BP, p = 0.779 for Diastolic BP). Compared with patients who underwent stenting, those who did not receive stenting exhibited a more pronounced elevation in serum creatinine levels and lower eGFR values (p = 0.003 and p = 0.002) at last follow‐up. Dialysis was required in four patients (33.33%) in the failed‐stenting group, but only in one patient (3.33%) in the stenting group (p = 0.029). The dialysis incidence rate of the cohort was 2.59 per 1000 person‐years. The MACE incidence rate of the cohort was 5.49 per 1000 person‐years. Two patients died from MI in the Non‐stenting group, while there were no fatalities in the stenting group (p = 0.077). The mortality rate of the cohort was 1.38 per 1000 person‐years. Compared with medical therapy, stenting was associated with reduced risk for clinical end point (p = 0.009). Kaplan–Meier (K–M) analysis showed a benefit of stenting on event‐free survival (log‐rank p = 0.029, Figure 1) and dialysis‐free survival (log‐rank p = 0.049, Figure 2). Restenosis was indicated in five patients (16.67%) by ultrasound. However, only three patients (10.0%) underwent repeat balloon angioplasty due to blood pressure fluctuations. Cox regression analysis also indicated that stenting might reduce the risk of dialysis (p = 0.084, HR 0.139, 95% CI 0.015–1.307) and MACE (p = 0.088, HR 0.280, 95% CI 0.065–1.209) occurrence, but the results did not reach statistical significance (p > 0.05).

Discussion

The findings in this study indicated that stenting may improve clinical outcomes of patients with unilateral RAO. Severe RAS is prone to RAO, leading to loss of renal mass [6, 9]. However, kidney damage often does not correlate with the degree of narrowing. Maintaining normal renal metabolic function requires only 10% of arterial blood flow for oxygen supply [10]. Besides, the coexistence of hypertension and atherosclerosis may exert a more significant impact on renal parenchymal damage in the pathophysiological process of kidney failure [11]. Patients with RAO are likely to have more extensive coexisting extrarenal vascular disease than those with RAS [12, 13], suggesting a more severe atherosclerotic damage to kidney. According to current guidelines, revascularization indications for RAS are strictly controlled [5]. Occluded kidneys often exhibit pronounced atrophy. Selecting suitable patients with RAO for intervention is of utmost importance. Patients with bilateral lesions are susceptible to pulmonary edema, making them suitable candidates for revascularization. However, whether to revascularize the occluded artery when the contralateral renal artery is patent is highly contentious. Because impaired kidneys with total occluded renal artery are less likely to recover after revascularization. According to previous reports, clinical presentations in RAO patients are not consistent. Due to the compensatory function of the contralateral kidney, some patients maintain stable creatinine levels and blood pressure [14]. However, other studies have indicated that patients with unilateral RAO can also experience significant increases in creatinine levels and refractory hypertension [10, 15, 16]. RAO patients with contralateral insignificant stenotic renal artery (≤50%) were also reported to trend to greater deterioration in renal function [17]. The unilateral RAO patients in this study had higher baseline creatinine levels, but there were no significant differences in BP and the number of medications used, compared to RAS patients [4]. Although the impaired renal function is consistent with previous report, baseline BP of our patients is lower than previous data [17]. Ethnicity might be a contributing factor in this regard. Patients which have severe atrophic kidneys with long disease duration might have low activity of renin‐angiotensin system. Therefore, some patients may exhibit relatively stable BP control. However, it has been reported that some patients with atrophic kidneys still experience persistent activation of the renin‐angiotensin system [15]. Besides, during the chronic stage, as compensatory pressure natriures fails, the disorder can transition from a renin‐dependent to a volume‐dependent state [18]. Based on our experience, there is significant variability in blood pressure control among patients with unilateral RAO.

RAO patients could benefit from stenting according to previous reports. Recanalization had a positive effect on decreasing the plasma renin activity and BP control in RAO patients with resistant hypertension [15]. Endovascular treatment demonstrated great efficacy in patients with acute RAO [19, 20]. Kawarada et al. reported that recurrent congestive heart failure and flash pulmonary edema were observed in a patient with unilateral RAO [21]. The patient's cardiac function significantly improved after stenting in the occluded renal artery. Severely atrophic kidney (length < 7 cm) can also experience volume recovery after revascularization [22]. Scholars have been committed to identifying predictive factors for the improvement of blood pressure and renal function. A multifactorial scoring model incorporating blood pressure, creatinine levels, renal artery status, and overall kidney condition could be useful to predict the probability of blood pressure and renal function improvement following stenting [23]. Manohar et al. from Mayo clinic reported that the presence of collateral perfusion and elevated cortical R2* of blood oxygen level‐dependent magnetic resonance (BOLD‐MR) are suggestive of preserved kidney function [10]. Collateral flow and BOLD‐MR are valuable in evaluating the potential for recovery of split renal function. Previous study indicated that patients with RAS who have high‐risk clinical features, including flash pulmonary edema, rapidly declining kidney function, and refractory hypertension, are more likely to benefit from stenting [24]. Our experiences in treating patients with RAO are similar to that of treating high‐risk patients. However, we recommend revascularization for all patients presenting with edema, rather than limiting it to those with pulmonary edema. Decompensation of the contralateral kidney may lead to volume‐dependent hypertension [18], resulting in edema and increased serum creatinine. Besides, occluded renal artery may lead to sustained activation of the renin‐angiotensin and sympathetic nervous systems. RI might be used as predictors of benefit from revascularization. An elevated RI indicates a higher degree of renal parenchymal fibrosis and arteriosclerosis, as well as a lower response to revascularization [25, 26]. We found that patients with failed interventions had higher RI values in their occluded renal arteries. This is consistent with the notion that elevated RI may indicate more severe arteriosclerosis. Whereas, the sample size in this study is too small to analyze the impact of RI on the clinical outcomes of patients after successful revascularization.

Previous studies have primarily been case reports emphasizing short‐term outcomes. In the largest study involving RAO patients currently available, there are only a few patients who have undergone revascularization treatment [17]. This study aims to include a larger number of patients to summarize the long‐term efficacy of interventional treatment for unilateral RAO patients. To our knowledge, this is the first study comparing the efficacy of stenting versus medication therapy in RAO patients. According to our results, although there was no significant difference in follow‐up blood pressure between the two groups, stented patients experienced a significant reduction in blood pressure postoperatively. On the other hand, stenting decelerate the rate of creatinine elevation. Despite significant impairment of renal function in the occluded side, stenting still demonstrates a renal protective effect. Successful recanalization might mitigate parenchymal damage of the contralateral kidney, which results from hyperperfusion and glomerular hyperfiltration associated with renin‐angiotensin activation [27]. CORAL trial suggested that stenting conferred no significant benefit with respect to the prevention of major cardiovascular or renal events when added to optimal medical therapy in ARAS patients [4]. Whereas, our results indicate that compared to failed revascularization, successful stenting was associated with reduced risk for clinical end points. RAO patients often present with advanced ischemic nephropathy, and renal function on the occluded side was believed to be difficult to restore after revascularization. Large‐scale RCT studies tend to exclude patients with advanced stages of CKD. Our study also suggests that stenting may have advantages in atherosclerotic RAS patients with advanced renal dysfunction. We speculate that the clinical benefit for patients primarily originates from the renal protective effect of revascularization. However, it is important to note that although there is no statistical difference, the baseline creatinine level of patients without stent is still higher than patients treated by stenting, which could potentially contribute to the poorer outcomes in the failed‐stenting group.

This study has several limitations: group assignment was based solely on surgical outcomes, mismatched baseline data between the two groups, insufficient follow‐up duration, and a small sample size.

Conclusion

In conclusion, in this single‐center, retrospective study, renal artery stenting plus medication therapy is effective in slowing the deterioration of renal function and preventing clinical events in patients with atherosclerotic unilateral RAO.
