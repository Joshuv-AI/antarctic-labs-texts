Optimizing student learning of clinical skills
Authors: Sarah Baillie, Rebecca S. V. Parkes, Francois-Xavier Lalèyê
Published: 2025

To the Editor,

We were fortunate to deliver a workshop on ‘Optimizing student learning of clinical skills’ at the Federation of Asian Veterinary Associations (FAVA) conference in Daejeon in Republic of Korea, supported by the American Association of Veterinary Medical Colleges (AAVMC) Council on International Veterinary Medical Education (CIVME). The workshop focused on clinical skills training using models and involved a combination of presentations about the latest developments in clinical skills education and activities such as making suturing models and running a mock exam. We were delighted with the level of attendance, enthusiasm and knowledge exchange.

WHY TEACH CLINICAL SKILLS?

The primary motivation for delivering high-quality clinical skills education is to ensure students are prepared to start work as veterinarians and have the ‘Day One Competences’ required by accrediting organizations [12]. Teaching with models has an important role in complementing the use of animals, aligning with the concept of “never the first time on a live animal” [1]. A well-structured clinical skills program will help students optimize their learning at every stage of the curriculum, can reduce staff workload when teaching in clinics as students are better prepared, and has benefits for animal welfare.

DEVELOPING A CLINICAL SKILLS PROGRAM

It is important to take a proactive and structured approach to building a clinical skills program within the curriculum to ensure students are learning and are assessed on practical animal handling and clinical skills at the right times (Fig. 1). Consulting recent graduates and employers can help to identify skills that should be prioritized [34]. The program should support planned acquisition of skills through a combination of timetabled classes embedded within the curriculum and opportunities for repeated practice to enable mastery of skills [5].

LEARNING WITH MODELS IN A CLINICAL SKILLS LABORATORY

There are an increasingly wide range of models available for veterinary teaching, some of which are commercial, but many can be homemade [6]. When deciding which models to make or purchase considerations should include whether the model adequately supports learning. It should be noted that for novice learners, such as veterinary students, a high level of realism may not be required. Other important considerations are having enough models for all students to practice and the cost of purchase as well as repair and replacement.

Clinical skills laboratories are becoming commonplace on veterinary campuses around the world [7]. The facilities provide excellent hands-on learning in a safe, trial and error environment where mistakes carry no serious consequences, which helps reduce student anxiety [8]. Important features of the facility include having a large teaching area that can be repurposed for different classes, an open-access space dedicated for practice, sufficient storage and a model-making area. Key to the success of a clinical skills program and the associated facilities are the people, including an academic lead and technical support team. It is useful to provide staff training specifically on how to teach skills to ensure students are performing the skills, receiving useful feedback and having a consistent learning experience.

SUPPORTING LEARNING RESOURCES

In addition to a comprehensive set of models, supporting learning resources are crucial. These include booklets with step-by-step instructions [9] and videos [10]. Additionally, a flipped classroom approach, whereby students complete a short online course before attending a practical class, will enhance preparation and allow students to focus in-class time on performing skills. The online content should cover the underpinning knowledge, e.g., images of equipment and short videos depicting the steps of the skill. A standardized flipped classroom design is recommended with defined learning outcomes for the online and in-class components, brief explanatory text to guide the student through the course, interactive content and quizzes [11].

ASSESSING CLINICAL SKILLS

Clinical skills are typically assessed using Objective Structured Clinical Examinations (OSCEs). OSCEs were first introduced in medical education in the 1970s [12] and are increasingly included in veterinary curricula to assess animal handling and clinical skills, e.g., basic medical, diagnostic, anesthesia and surgical techniques. An OSCE is typically organized as a circuit with each station being of the same length, e.g., 5 min for simple skills. Students demonstrate their ability to perform a skill while an examiner marks using a standardized checklist or rubric. OSCEs have a huge educational impact, driving student learning, but require thorough planning, consideration of feasibility/cost (resources and time) and examiner training to ensure reliability [13].

CONCLUSIONS

There has been rapid growth in the use of models and clinical skills laboratories to complement the use of animals in veterinary education around the world, with much innovation and best practice being shared by the community [1415]. The discipline is increasingly supported by research studies, including validation of models and identification of the most important skills for today’s graduates, creating an evidence base on which veterinary clinical skills education will continue to grow and thrive.
