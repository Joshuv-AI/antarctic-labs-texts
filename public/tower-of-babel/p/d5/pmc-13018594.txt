Effects of videos with whole-body movements on young children’s geography learning
Authors: Myrto F. Mavilidi, Fred Paas, Liye Zou, David R. Lubans, Leila Mokhtari, Mirko Schmidt, Juan Cristobal Castro Alonso, Rebecca Ng, Sue Bennett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This video-based study explores the feasibility, acceptability, and efficacy of an online movement-based intervention in young children. The intervention integrated sensorimotor experiences with digital instruction to facilitate knowledge acquisition. Seventy-five children (Mage = 5.61; SD = 1.03) were randomly assigned to either an embodied cognition group, where they watched videos and performed simple full-body movements mimicking animals from different continents, or a control group, where they watched the same videos while remaining seated. Pre- and post-test assessments measured geographical knowledge retention. Children rated their enjoyment of the instructional preference. Results from MANCOVA indicated no significant group differences in knowledge recall. An ANOVA showed that both groups reported high levels of enjoyment related to instructional engagement. The study contributes to the growing body of research on movement-based digital learning, emphasizing the importance of balancing cognitive and motor demands in online educational environments.

Introduction

Early developmental theorists such as Jean Piaget (e.g., role of play, “acting upon objects”, “reflective abstraction)1 and Lev Vygotsky (“make-believe play” and imagination)2 highlighted the importance of sensorimotor learning, engagement, and physical interactions with the external world. More recent developmental theories include the evolutionary upgrade of cognitive load theory3, experiential4, and embodied and experiential5 learning.

To begin with, building on Geary’s work6,7, which emphasized the distinction between biologically primary and secondary knowledge, Paas and Sweller3 have incorporated an evolutionary approach to learning. Specifically, biologically primary knowledge refers to innate information and skills that humans have evolved to acquire naturally, unconsciously, rapidly, and effortlessly (e.g., native language, movement, tool use). This type of knowledge places minimal demands on working memory capacity. In contrast, biologically secondary knowledge consists of culturally significant information, such as mathematics or science, which requires conscious processing, formal instruction, and extrinsic motivation for effective learning3. Unlike primary knowledge, it does not develop intuitively and must be explicitly taught through structured education. Biologically, secondary knowledge relies heavily on cognitive resources, especially working memory capacity.

Paas and Sweller3 proposed that biologically primary knowledge operates with minimal demands on working memory, allowing it to be strategically applied to facilitate the acquisition of biologically secondary knowledge, which requires explicit instruction and greater cognitive effort (see also refs. 8,9). For example, primary school students learned origami skills10 and university students were guided to tie knots or assemble puzzle rings through dynamic representations11. These findings highlight how visual demonstrations of procedural tasks can facilitate learning and skill acquisition.

Physical actions such as gestures, object manipulation, and full-body movement are considered a form of biologically primary knowledge and are believed to serve as “cognitive offloading” mechanisms. These actions have the potential to reduce the cognitive demands of a learning task by modifying its information processing requirements and alleviating the burden on working memory capacity12. Furthermore, cognitive offloading occurs because executing physical actions facilitates the integration of sensorimotor information via multiple modalities (e.g., tactile, motor, visual, auditory). For learning to be effective (i.e., germane cognitive processes leading to schema construction and meaningful learning13, the inherent complexity of learning tasks and the unnecessary distractions or inefficient instructional design (e.g., irrelevant activities) should be minimised14,15. In the present study, these cognitive load concepts informed the design of the digital movement-based activity, but cognitive load itself was not directly measured.

Complementing this line of research, embodied cognition posits that cognition is shaped via sensorimotor experiences in human movements and interactions with the external world4,16. The mind-body interaction with the environment is an integral part of the cognitive system, where information processing occurs through physical actions4,17, improving cognitive functions and facilitating meaningful learning18. For example, when an object is presented and interpreted through movement-based representations (e.g., gestures, simulation, or full-body movements), information about its properties (e.g., size, function), is simultaneously conveyed across multimodal and sensorimotor coordination (e.g., auditory and visual perception), enhancing comprehension and retention19. Further, these multimodal representations may facilitate memory encoding and are positively linked to recall efficiency4. This notion is highly relevant for science, technology, engineering, and mathematics (STEM), facilitating abstract concepts to reach a more tangible form.

Neuroimaging studies indicated that motor engagement during abstract reasoning activates brain areas (i.e., intraparietal sulcus and dorsal premotor cortex) that elicit sensorimotor simulation and predictive coding, functioning as external cues for retrieving visuospatial information20. It is suggested that, during processing of dual motor and cognitive tasks, representational resonance occurs when shared representations allow modality compatibility by sharing/reusing neural circuits21. Interestingly, in dynamic digital environments, the brain is able to anticipate sensory consequences of action and adjust motor output when needed. Variability as a functional signal is essential for understanding adaptive control in real-world behaviours such as human–machine interaction or sports. Sensorimotor prediction, sensory detection, motor execution, and visuomotor adaptation seem to operate as separate processes, in a way that predictive processing in human sensorimotor control is compartmentalized22.

Episodic memory is based on the storage and retrieval of temporally dated, spatially located, and personally experienced events or episodes23. Movements may support episodic memories as encoding of the words relies on the context or situation elements24,25. From another perspective, motor development is embodied (reflecting the current status of the body), embedded (based on the possibilities and constraints of the external environment), enculturated (depending on social and cultural influences), and enabling (where new skills create opportunities for exploration and learning26). Both motor and cognitive development use training practice (physical, mental, or both) as the main mechanism of the learning process for acquiring skills27.

Research suggests that movement-based learning, when relevant to and temporally connected with a goal-directed task, can enhance learning in children28,29. Such movement-based learning practices range from gestures to gross-motor activities (e.g., full-body movement). For example, previous movement-based learning programmes in early childhood education have shown promising effects in STEM (geography, mathematics, science28,30,31). Notably, these examples were conducted in physical environments. A study with similar methodology integrating movements with executive function and numeracy skills in preschool children through videos and a book did not reveal any significant learning outcomes of the intervention group32. Additionally, the level of cognitive demand seems to be a significant moderator in the relationship between physical activity and cognition, with physical activity of higher cognitive demand enhancing cognitive outcomes (e.g., executive function) across the lifespan33. Recent literature has attested that physical activity and executive function in children is mediated through brainwaves (alpha, beta34). Alpha and beta brain waves have shown positive relationships with both physical activity and executive function, while to reinforce cognitive development, it is recommended to use more complicated cognitive activities or set time limitations during the cognitive tasks. For example, a 20-week exercise programme in overweight/obese children has found that physical multi-games, incorporating aerobic and resistance exercise with moderate levels of cognitive demand, may modulate brain activity (i.e., increased brain activation in temporal, occipital and frontal areas as shown in EEG) related to positive changes on children’s working memory and inhibition compared to a sedentary control group35.

Embodied learning has close ties to experiential learning, which places experience as the primary source of learning5, and can be substantially extended and applied to geography learning. Despite the unique characteristics of teaching geography in young children, with information needed to be presented in a fun and game-based manner, digital technologies can support and advance children’s development of spatial cognition36. For example, in geography learning, maps function as tools for learning environments, promoting spatial thinking (e.g., learning about geophysical entities and the Earth’s position37).

A successful example providing geographic education to preschoolers is a TV programme called “Dora the Explorer”38. Dora is a cartoon character of a little girl, interacting with viewers to help her solve problems (e.g., reading a map and finding where she has to go). This programme aimed to improve children’s language development (name/describes places and features), their spatial awareness (sense of direction), and physical skills (e.g., coordination39) using real and virtual environments. Lastly, a recent study used animation to teach geography in early childhood children, as an instructional approach to represent space, movement, and the social-material environment (how people, animals and objects live and move40). The suggested “interaction geography” approach with animation contained gestures and movements between teachers, children and other materials, connecting geography with the physical design of spaces in a naturalistic context (i.e., cultural heritage museum), and promoting learning with enjoyment in this age group.

Nonetheless, advancements in educational technologies have revolutionised the landscape of children’s learning experiences, influencing diverse domains such as mathematics, language skill acquisition, and scientific principles41. It can be concluded that the content and type of use of digital technologies in early childhood is crucial for determining whether cognitive outcomes will be impeded or enhanced42. Research in educational technology, particularly in human–computer interaction involving collaborative gesture-based interfaces, has shown beneficial effects on STEM learning outcomes (e.g., geometry, science, mathematics43,44). Studies using video-delivered embodied learning have been primarily conducted with primary school children45, with very few studies investigating the effects of full-body movements on learning in an online environment in young children.

Collectively, empirical evidence shows the potential of movement-based practices during digital activities for enhancing learning4,46. There is currently a knowledge gap in the existing literature, looking at how movement-based learning (e.g., full-body movement) with supporting technologies can facilitate children’s learning47. In particular, very little is known about how such digitally supported, movement-based activities can foster early geography learning (e.g., linking symbolic representations of continents on a map to locations and salient features such as climate, iconic animals) in young children. To this vein, the aim of the present study was to evaluate the feasibility, acceptability, and effectiveness of a single online movement-based learning session in young children.

We aimed to address the following research question: How does movement-based learning in a digital environment impact knowledge acquisition and engagement in young children compared to traditional, non-movement-based digital learning? To explore this main research question, we proposed the two sub-hypotheses: (1) H1: Children who engage in full-body movement-based learning within a digital environment will demonstrate greater knowledge retention and recall compared to those in a traditional digital learning group; (2) H2: Movement-based digital learning will result in higher levels of engagement and motivation, as indicated by self-reported enjoyment on instructional preferences. Results in the present study will contribute to the growing field of digital movement-based learning, offering insights into its potential to enhance cognitive and motivational aspects of early childhood education.

Discussion

The present study aimed to evaluate the feasibility, acceptability, and effectiveness of a single online movement-based learning session in young children. An intervention program designed for digital environment, including a world map presented in a video with physically executed animal movements, found no significant differences from the control group, who watched the same video without performing movements. Ratings on the enjoyment of the instructional type also did not differ across groups. This study suggests that integrating technology with full-body movement during learning might lead to outcomes comparable to those achieved in the same digital environment when children remain seated. At the same time, these null differences should be interpreted cautiously, as the single brief session, the relatively simple geography content, and group-based administration may all have limited the scope for detecting advantages of the movement condition.

The present study attempted to integrate the theoretical accounts of cognitive load theory and embodied learning, but no measures were included to test the proposed mechanisms (e.g., reduced transient information burden, improved segmentation, or increased cognitive load from dual-task demands). As a result, the mechanistic interpretation remains speculative. This study is in alignment with Nathan’s48 call for developing fun and developmentally appropriate learning activities in education, promoting grounded and embodied learning. Dynamic visualisations (e.g., animations and videos) are commonly used to present complex procedures such as learning a new task, but learners can experience a high cognitive load with a detrimental effect due to the transiency of these visualisations49,50. During the transient-interval process of learning, information can disappear at a fast pace, demanding more cognitive resources in comparison to a permanent format (e.g., static pictures or written text). Also, during learning of complex novel information, it is recommended to use segmentation, which allows to split a complex dynamic visualisation in meaningful segments and enables the learners to process fully one segment before moving on to the next51.

In the present study, the whole-body movements fulfilled an analogous role of “segmentation”, allowing children to consolidate the knowledge they watched and heard. However, considering the null findings, the dual-task performance may have been too complex, causing interference27,52. Zou et al.53 proposed that, when the embodied (physical) actions are too cognitive or coordinatively demanding, not temporally connected or not meaningfully related to the learning task, they can impede learning. In addition, recent eye-tracking evidence indicated that frequent, user-driven content shifts inherent to short-video watching may impair event segmentation, which is imperative for continuous memory encoding54. In contrast, this effect did not occur during personalised videos or static images. In the case of our study, it is possible that the video segmentation occurring by the experimenters, who controlled the pace and timing of each video, obstructed children’s memory encoding. From a cognitive load theory perspective, these possibilities suggest that the embodied condition may have introduced additional dual-task demands (and probably did not manage to reduce extraneous cognitive load), consistent with the absence of an advantage for the movement group.

Surprisingly, this study’s findings are in contrast with evidence on embodied cognition showing promising effects on STEM learning. For instance, a study conducted by Georgiou et al.55 involving primary school children used motion-based technology to teach geometry concepts, such as angles, found that learning gains and emotional engagement were greater for students who participated in a digital embodied intervention compared to those in a non-digital embodied intervention. No significant group difference on cognitive load was observed between the two groups.

Bokosmaty et al.56 indicated that the use of an interactive geometry software in primary school students has been observed to bear the potential to improve comprehension and logical reasoning regarding two-dimensional shapes, which is linked to better mathematical performance. Further, a study focused on the theoretical constructs (i.e., embodied learning) required for an effective instructional design, implementing a digital game-based activity at a Science Discovery Centre (children’s museum activity) to test its effects on computational thinking skills of young children (i.e., deconstruction, pattern identification, abstraction, and algorithmic thinking)57. Lastly, Rudling et al. (2026) examined the effects of embodied learning on high school students’ second language vocabulary58. Results showed that recall was higher after computer-based compared to virtual reality training, but students’ sustained attention may be a significant predictor. It is adamant that research on educational technology and psychology (e.g., virtual reality- learning) deciphers the mechanisms and theoretical constructs required for effective learning through digital technologies.

Further, research has shown that educational technologies may offer valuable contributions to geography education learners. For instance, Tometzova et al.59 used an augmented reality mobile application to teach geography to primary school children. Their findings revealed that this approach significantly boosted students’ motivation to learn, leading to greater engagement in the educational process. In addition, “Dora” is a sustainable, successful example of how digital technology through videos can incentivise preschoolers in learning geography (introducing maps of varying scales)38. Early exposure paves the way for a deeper understanding of maps and geography as children progress, showcasing how to leverage technology to cultivate a strong enthusiasm for geography among students. This study used maps and spatial navigation as the same conceptual and functioning tools for learning, with the control condition being comparable to Dora’s videos. The inclusion of movements was a novelty factor, and hence, we cannot relate it directly to Dora’s instructional approach.

Nevertheless, instruction through video in digital game-based learning environments has already been established in educational settings. Children in this generation are being raised in settings inundated with digital technology, thereby initiating their engagement with technology and interactive media at a very early age60. This trend is confirmed by a large-scale survey with approximately 4000 Australian children, showing that one in three preschoolers (aged 2–5 years old) possesses their own tablet or smartphone, spending nearly 26 h per week in front of screens61. During the COVID-19 pandemic, this situation was exacerbated with children across New South Wales (Australia) reporting increased screen time and decreased physical activity62. Unfortunately, children’s screen-based activities do not necessarily provide appropriate content for learning63. Parent report data from the current study showed that children spent 6 hours during weekdays and 4 h during weekends in front of screens. It is suggested that future research uses objectively measured assessments (e.g., accelerometers) to identify whether parents and/ or guardians under-report their children’s time on screen.

Furthermore, despite not being able to establish the superiority of the digital movement-based learning, the relevance and importance of this research are high, offering a “silver lining” and a way to reconcile calls for reduced screen time with an emphasis on higher-quality, movement and learning-oriented screen time to opposing views of current stakeholders: On one hand, World Health Organisation’s guidelines64 recommend minimising screen time for young children as much as possible. Estimates in preschool children show that 72% of children (three in four) use screen devices above the recommended timeframes, mostly for TV/ programme viewing65. In contrast, education and industry explicitly encourage screen time in the early years for stimulating children’s digital skills66.

Relatedly, Mallawaarachchi et al.42 reported that co-use of screen time was positively associated with cognitive outcomes in young children (0-6 years), whereas programme viewing, background television, and age-inappropriate content were negatively associated with cognitive outcomes. Recently, a taxonomy of content relevance and mental activation has been proposed to categorise sedentary digital activities67. In this direction, mentally active sedentary behaviour (e.g., video gaming) may benefit brain health, acting as a potential mediator in the relationship between sedentary behaviour and brain health outcomes53. In contrast, mentally passive sedentary behaviour (e.g., television viewing of no educational value) not only does not confer such benefits, but it may also elevate risks of disorders later in life68.

Improvements in neuroscience tend to be domain-specific (e.g., strengthened common neural circuits), rather than domain-general (e.g., general functional networks67). For example, an acute study in grade 5 students, who were allocated to mentally passive sedentary behaviour (i.e., video watching), mentally active sedentary behaviour (i.e., numeracy practice), and 5 or 10 min physical activity, found better inverse efficiency scores in a flanker task on the exercise groups compared to the sedentary groups68. Pecukonis et al.69 also conducted a study in young children (3–6 years) using fNRIS to compare brain activation between a live-book reading session and a screen-based story condition. Results showed significant brain activation (i.e., right parietal junction, greater right temporal parietal junction brain responses – inferior and middle frontal gyri, superior and middle temporal gyri- compared to left-laterised regions) was apparent only in the book reading condition.

Lastly, the present study also looked at how children perceived the activity in terms of the type of instruction and enjoyment. Children in both groups provided high ratings for the type of instruction and reported enjoying the task, but the ratings did not differ significantly between groups. It is important to highlight that, regardless of the inclusion of movement, the video with the game-based geography activity was equally novel for both groups, increasing children’s interest and enjoyment and thus their intrinsic motivation70. Research has shown that videos in digital game-based learning environments may result in increased levels of intrinsic motivation, lower intrinsic cognitive load and reduced extraneous cognitive load71.

Notably, the present study did not include a measurement of cognitive load and as such, we cannot make inferences on whether cognitive load in the embodied learning group was induced or alleviated. Previous experience of the research team has shown that children in this age group, when shown a smiley scale, choose the “happy” option, regardless of the question’s content. The most commonly applied self-estimated cognitive load ratings are for primary school children15,72. Future research could explore and validate multiple age-appropriate approaches (e.g., pictorial rating scales, simplified self-report, behavioural indicators, observer-based measures, physiological proxies) for measuring cognitive load in young children.

Finally, looking at children’s demographic characteristics in the present study, their socio-economic status seems to be medium to high: participating children could be considered as an audience interested in STEM, as visitors of a Science Space, where parents invested a paid amount to these extra-curricular activities. Also, 80% of parents/guardians did not have a health care card (as an indication of governmental support), and 65% obtained a university/ postgraduate degree. It would be interesting to investigate whether replicating the study in a low socioeconomic setting and possibly including a non-digital comparison group, would highlight the superiority of the online movement-based learning.

To sum up, the strength of this study was its novel learning task incorporating movements into a geography task, presented in a digital environment. This study was also conducted in ecologically valid conditions (at a children’s museum during their daily visit), highlighting how such approaches could be successfully applied in children’s daily practices. As limitations, we note the lack of direct cognitive load measurement and the single learning session, which restrict the inferences that can be drawn about mechanisms and longer-term learning. Due to practical reasons, including more learning sessions and follow-up assessments, would have resulted in a significant loss of participants, as children irregularly or casually visit children’s museums. In addition, we consider the pre-test as a coarse indicator of prior knowledge and acknowledge that, given the uniformly low scores, its sensitivity to differentiate between levels of baseline understanding was limited. As the maximum possible score that children could get was 12, floor effects could be a plausible explanation. Future studies could include additional or alternative test items to capture a broader range of geography knowledge in this age group. Importantly, while age-appropriate, this task captures only an introductory subset of geography competencies (e.g., it does not address more advanced spatial reasoning or scale) and therefore interpretation of our findings pertains to these basic aspects of geography learning. Future work should consider including more advanced established goals or competencies in geography.

Future research could also explore the effects of similar programmes with longer duration (e.g., 4-6 weeks) during regular routines in early childhood education and care centres, benefiting from the consistency of children’s attendance. Adopting a longitudinal design can potentially show delayed effects on learning from movements (e.g., gestures73), considering that the way information is encoded and recalled over time (through repeated practice allows retrieval from the long-term retention system74–76). Last, research on how digital technologies need to shed light on how to moderate the cognitive demands of physical and learning tasks through systematic approaches, using mixed-methods designs, collating experts' and stakeholders’ opinions, and including empirical testing via randomised controlled trials.

To conclude, current challenges of technological interventions incorporating whole-body movements (i.e., physical activity) to provide productive learning experiences include unbalanced designs of movements and learning, as well as scalability and reliability issues with technology47. Balancing out the cognitive load demands of the motor and learning tasks is important for facilitation of learning outcomes, as too complex motor tasks may impede learning27,52. Consequently, given the increased time children spend in front of screens and the current lack of robust evidence, conducting intervention programmes using online movement-based learning is indispensable, aligned with theoretical considerations from developmental, cognitive, and educational psychology, educational technology, and early childhood education.

Participants

Seventy-five children (intervention = 37; 36 females; Mage = 5.61; SD = 1.03) were recruited from the Science Space at the University of Wollongong during their visit to the children’s museum. Participants were randomly assigned to either the embodied cognition or the control group based (i.e., block allocation) on their time and order of their visit. This randomisation was conducted in blocks of four, with the first block allocated to the intervention group, the next to the control group, and so forth). Ethical approval was obtained from the University of Wollongong Human Ethics Research Committee (Approval No: 2020/458) and both written parental consent form and children's verbal assent were secured prior to participation. Intervention

Upon their visit to the children’s museum “Science Space”, both parent(s) and children were asked by the research staff to attend this study, which was conducted in a quiet room of the museum. Of note, only those children who verbally agreed, along with a written consent form from their parent(s) were finally arranged into two groups. In the intervention group, participating children were asked to watch a video featuring a world map, with animated cartoon animals performing unique movements. Children were asked to verbally repeat the name of the continent associated with the animal while navigating along the map. Additionally, participating children were instructed to engage in simple full-body movements, mimicking the animal movements shown in the video (e.g., jumping like a kangaroo, running on the spot, or stomping their feet on the floor pretending to walk like an elephant, while moving from one continent to the other on the map; see Fig. 1). In the control group, children were arranged to only watch the same videos in a sitting position and then were asked to repeat the names of the continents. In both conditions, each 5-min video was played twice to provide repeated exposure to the content and, in the intervention group, additional opportunities to practise the animal movements78. The learning task focused on three world continents, with children learning to identify each continent. The animals chosen were native and representative of each continent (i.e., kangaroo in Oceania, elephant in Asia, and raccoon in America). Children in both groups completed the same task, watching the videos with the continents appearing in the same order, with a duration of 10–15 min. This duration was chosen to engage children’s attention for a short time without mentally and physically exhausting them. The learning sessions were conducted in small groups with a maximum of four children per session, to ensure engagement and effective participation. As the study occurred in a naturalistic setting, multiple sessions ran over the day with a diverse number of children (e.g., 2 or 4), accommodating participants’ activities in the museum while ensuring their involvement in the research study. For instance, if two children participated in the intervention group, the next set of children arriving at a similar time could be assigned to the control group, depending on the ongoing block, which kept predictability of group assignment low, although still plausible.

The procedure was adapted from Mavilidi et al.30 (learning task and materials) and Mavilidi et al.32 (active videos). Immediately after watching the videos, either with or without performing full-body movements, children in both groups completed the learning assessment about geography. Experimental process.

The order of the experimental process was the following: (1) a parent-based survey and individual child pre-test assessment, (2) learning session across groups - intervention group: children watched the videos and imitated animal movements; control group: children watched the videos while seated, and (3) individual post-test assessment and rating scale on enjoyment of instructional type.

This game-based (geography) activity was designed based on cognitive load theory, embodied and experiential learning principles: Complementary information was presented through several modalities (auditory, visual and motor), without repeating the same information across them. Furthermore, movements served as a means to segment transient information that quickly disappears, such as spoken text or animations49,79. Children could simulate the position and shape of each continent of the digital map in the real physical environment. Learning activities were centred around the world map, allowing for the development of children’s spatial cognition and navigation skills.

Materials

Learning

Children’s geography knowledge (i.e., naming continents and animals living in each continent, continents’ location on the map) on the content previously taught in the videos was assessed both before (pre-test) and immediately after the learning session (post-test). The pre-test was designed to evaluate prior knowledge of participating children, while the post-test was conducted to assess knowledge acquisition80. As in prior work with similar materials81, pre-test scores were expected to be low, reflecting limited prior knowledge of continent names in this age group. Children were asked to name any continent they knew and any animal living in a specific continent, without any prompts or cues provided. Children received 1 point for each correct answer, with a maximum of 6 points. The pre-test lasted 1 min. Post-test: each participating child completed two different tasks: a free-recall test and a cued-recall test: (1) In the free-recall test, children were asked to name the continents and to name the correct animal of each continent (e.g., “What’s the animal of Oceania?”). (2) In the cued-recall test, a printed A3 map (same as presented in the videos) was presented to children along with toy figurines of the animals. They were asked to put the animals on the right place on the map and to match animals and continents with a prompt given (e.g., “Start from America and go to the continent of the elephant”). The total duration of the post-test lasted 3 to 5 min. Children received 1 point for each correct answer, with a maximum of 6 points in each individual sub-test (12 points in total). The reliability coefficient (Cronbach’s alpha) was 0.79 in the present study, aligning with the findings of a previous study by Mavilidi et al.30, which used the same testing materials.

Enjoyment of instructional type

At the end of the learning session, children were asked to answer two questions: how much they liked the type of instruction (“Did you like this game?”), and if they would like to be taught this way in the future (“Would you like to play it again in the future?”). Each question was rated on a 5-point Likert scale with semantic anchors represented by smiley faces82 ranging from 1, “I didn’t like it at all, to 5, “I liked it a lot”, and 1, “Not at all”, to 5, “I would love to”, respectively and was aggregated and summarised into one score. This scale has been used in previous studies29,81.

Screen time use

A parent-based survey was conducted to collect data on activity behaviours (i.e., screen-based activities) of preschool children83. Parents were asked to report about their children’s leisure activities during a typical week including time spent in each activity for weekdays (i.e., Monday-Friday) and weekends (i.e., Saturday-Sunday). Leisure activities included screen time and traditional play: (i) program viewing on traditional devices such as TV/DVD; (ii) use of applications/electronic games on portable handheld devices, laptop/computer, and/ or console games such as tablet, mobile phone, handheld game system (hereafter referred to as apps), PlayStation, Xbox; (iii) internet (excluding games); (iv) quiet play such as Lego, books, train set, craft, doll, board games; (v) imaginary games such as dress-ups, imitating favourite cartoon/TV characters. The time spent on screen time (i, ii, iii) and traditional play (iv, v) for weekdays and weekend days respectively was summed and averaged (see Table 1). This survey was previously reported having high reliability (0.83%83).

Statistical analyses

Data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics v29.0. Descriptive statistics are displayed in Table 2. Regarding learning, a multivariate analysis of covariance (MANCOVA) was run, with pre-test measures as a covariate, while group was an independent variable and learning the outcome variable (i.e., free-recall, cued-recall). This choice was because the two types of recall (free and cued) were highly interrelated. The inclusion of traditional play during the week did not alter the direction and significance of results. Regarding the enjoyment of instructional type, an analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted for the enjoyment of instructional type (dependent variable), with group as the independent variable. Partial eta square (ηp2) is reported and interpreted based on Cohen’s84 definition of small, medium, and large effect sizes (ηp2: 0.01, 0.06, and 0.14, respectively).
