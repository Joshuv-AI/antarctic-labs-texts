Sex-specific risk factors for genital infections associated with SGLT2 inhibitors in type 2 diabetes: a retrospective cohort study and disproportionality analysis using JADER
Authors: Ayami Kajiwara-Morita, Sawa Kugimoto, Kentaro Oniki, Akira Yoshida, Noboru Kurinami, Tomoko Suzuki, Fumio Miyamoto, Kunio Hieshima, Seigo Sugiyama, Keizo Kajiwara, Katsunori Jinnouchi, Hideaki Jinnouchi, Junji Saruwatari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Genital infections are notable adverse events that may lead to nonadherence to or discontinuation of sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors, essential agents in the management of type 2 diabetes (T2D). Although sex differences in incidence rates have been reported, the risk factors for each sex remain poorly understood.Objective:To identify sex-specific risk factors for genital infections associated with SGLT2 inhibitors, with the aim of optimising prevention and intervention strategies.Design:(i) Retrospective cohort study based on real-world clinical data and (ii) disproportionality analysis using the Japanese Adverse Drug Event Report database.Methods:(i) We investigated sex-specific risk factors for genital infections attributable to SGLT2 inhibitors using logistic regression, and further evaluated the discriminatory ability of sex–risk-factor combinations using a Cox proportional hazards model. (ii) As a supplementary analysis, a disproportionality analysis using reporting odds ratios (RORs) was performed for genital infection associated with SGLT2 inhibitors, stratified by the risk factors identified in (i).Results:(i) Among 2202 patients with T2D, 36 (1.6%) individuals developed genital infections after initiation of SGLT2 inhibitors (median 596.5 days). Females had a higher incidence than males (3.6% vs 0.8%, p < 0.001). In females, the combination of older age (⩾65 years) and three or more concomitant antidiabetic medications was identified as a significant contributor to infection risk, whereas elevated body mass index (BMI) was implicated in males. The Cox model demonstrated excellent discrimination (concordance index, 0.83; p < 0.001). (ii) A total of 228,261 cases submitted by diabetes patients were examined. The RORs were higher in older individuals and in those taking a greater number of diabetic medications, only in females. The effects of the combination of these factors and BMI could not be evaluated.Conclusion:Older age and polypharmacy of antidiabetic agents in females were identified as risk factors for SGLT2 inhibitor-associated genital infections.

Introduction

Sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors are a class of antidiabetic agents that extend beyond glucose-lowering effects, offering therapeutic benefits such as weight reduction, cardiovascular protection and renal preservation.1 –3 On the basis of evidence from randomised controlled trials and subsequent meta-analyses, current guidelines strongly recommend the use of SGLT2 inhibitors in patients with type 2 diabetes (T2D) who have chronic kidney disease (CKD), cardiovascular disease or heart failure.4 –6 Therefore, SGLT2 inhibitors are now regarded as key therapeutic agents in the care of T2D, with a pivotal role in preventing complications.

SGLT2 inhibitors exert their pharmacological effect by inhibiting SGLT2 in proximal renal tubules, thereby promoting urinary glucose excretion. The consequent glycosuria provides a microenvironment favourable for microbial proliferation, and genitourinary infections have thus been discussed as a characteristic adverse drug reaction associated with this class of agents. 7 While dapagliflozin has been implicated in a potential association with urinary tract infections, current evidence indicates that SGLT2 inhibitors as a drug class are not associated with an overall increased risk.8 –10 Conversely, treatment with SGLT2 inhibitors has been shown to increase the risk of genital infections by approximately three- to five-fold in patients with T2D, as demonstrated in several meta-analyses of randomised controlled trials.1,9,10 Genital infections can be caused by a variety of pathogens, and SGLT2 inhibitors have been reported to contribute to an increased risk of both mycotic1,8,11 and bacterial forms. 12

Genital infections have been reported as the second most common reason for discontinuation of SGLT2 inhibitors (12.2%), following polyuria (18.9%), in Japanese patients with T2D. 13 The onset of genital infections not only contributes to a decline in quality of life but also leads to medication nonadherence or discontinuation, resulting in poor glycaemic control and increased risks of cardiovascular disease and mortality.14,15 Therefore, elucidating the risk factors for these infections and establishing effective preventive and interventional strategies is warranted.

There appears to be a sex-related difference in the development of genital infections. The common clinical manifestations of genital infections include vulvovaginitis in females and balanitis in males, both typically of fungal origin.9,16 It is well recognised that females with diabetes exhibit a higher susceptibility to genital infections, regardless of SGLT2 inhibitor therapy. 17 Although sex differences in the magnitude of risk elevation of genital mycotic infections associated with SGLT2 inhibitors remain inconclusive, these infections during SGLT2 inhibitor therapy are generally reported more frequently in females.9,11,14 In addition, it has been suggested that patient backgrounds predisposing to genital infections may differ between males and females. 7 However, sex-specific risk factors for these infections during SGLT2 inhibitor therapy remain poorly understood.

We considered that elucidating sex-specific risk factors in real-world settings would facilitate the development of effective and efficient strategies for prevention and intervention of genital infections associated with SGLT2 inhibitors. In particular, we focused on genital infections that lead to discontinuation of SGLT2 inhibitors. Discontinuing these agents can deprive patients of their cardio-renal protective benefits, representing a substantial clinical disadvantage. Furthermore, unlike mild genital infections – which patients may notice but often do not report – severe infections that result in treatment cessation can be objectively identified even in retrospective analyses, thereby improving the accuracy of outcome assessment. Accordingly, the objectives of this study were (i) to identify sex-specific risk factors for genital infections that lead to discontinuation of SGLT2 inhibitors through a retrospective cohort study and (ii) to explore, as a supplementary analysis, whether signals consistent with these findings could be detected in a spontaneous adverse event (AE) reporting database.

Methods

Retrospective cohort study based on real-world clinical data

Study population

We reviewed the consecutive records of 2232 patients with T2D who were newly prescribed an SGLT2 inhibitor at the Jinnouchi Hospital Diabetes Care Centre in Kumamoto, Japan, between August 2014 and March 2020. No additional inclusion or exclusion criteria were applied. Participants were followed until the earliest of discontinuation of SGLT2 inhibitor due to genital infection, discontinuation for other reasons or 31 December 2020.

Measurements and definitions

Patient characteristics and information on AEs occurring during the observation period were obtained from medical records. In this study, genital infection attributable to SGLT2 inhibitor was defined as meeting all the following criteria: (i) the physician judged a high likelihood of causality between SGLT2 inhibitor treatment and either genital infection-like symptoms reported by patient (i.e. pruritus genital, genital discomfort and genital rash) or a diagnosis made by another department (i.e. balanoposthitis and genital candidiasis); (ii) SGLT2 inhibitor was discontinued due to these symptoms and (iii) the symptoms subsequently resolved or attenuated, confirming the causal relationship. T2D was defined according to the Japanese Clinical Practice Guidelines for Diabetes 18 or a patient history of T2D. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in metres. The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the Japanese eGFR-estimating equation, which has relatively little bias across the wide range of GFR values in the Japanese population: eGFR (mL/min/1.73 m2) = 194 × SCr−1.094 × Age−0.287 × 0.739 (if female). 19 According to the Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes guidelines, albuminuria is classified as A1 (normoalbuminuria), A2 (microalbuminuria) or A3 (macroalbuminuria) according to the urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio in an isolated urine sample for values <30, 30–300 or >300 mg/g, respectively. 6 In Japan, the urine protein-to-creatinine ratio (uPCR) is commonly used to classify the severity of CKD after the diagnosis of early-stage diabetic nephropathy in patients with diabetes, and a uPCR of 150–500 mg/g is considered equivalent to A2 albuminuria. Therefore, in the absence of urine albumin data, uPCR was used with cut-offs of <150, 150–500 and >500 mg/g. 20 Positive urine ketones were defined as ⩾1+ on dipstick urinalysis. Patients were considered exposed to high-dose SGLT2 inhibitors if they received at least one prescription exceeding the standard dose indicated in the respective package insert during the follow-up period. Concomitant antidiabetic medications (CAMs) were categorised by drug class (e.g. biguanides, sulfonylureas), and the number of CAMs was defined as the count of antidiabetic classes excluding SGLT2 inhibitors. For combination antidiabetic drugs, both components were considered as being administered.

Statistical analyses

Data are presented as the number of individuals (%) for categorical variables and as the mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables. Group comparisons were performed using Welch’s t-test for continuous variables and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. To explore sex-specific candidate risk factors associated with genital infections induced by SGLT2 inhibitors, we initially conducted univariable logistic regression analyses stratified by sex, focusing on baseline (i.e. the point of SGLT2 inhibitor initiation) characteristics as potential predictors. In this step, interactions between factors were also examined. Variables with statistical significance were subsequently included in a multivariable logistic regression using stepwise forward selection based on the Wald criterion to derive the final model. For continuous variables identified in the final model, optimal cutoff values were derived from receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis using the maximum Youden index criterion. Based on the results of the preceding analyses, survival analysis was conducted for four subgroups defined by sex and the presence or absence of the identified risk factor(s). Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated for each group, and group differences were evaluated using Cox proportional hazards regression. Model discrimination was assessed using the concordance index (C-index), calculated from the linear predictor (Xβ) in the Cox proportional hazards model. To further evaluate the model’s time-dependent discriminative ability, we calculated the area under the curve (AUC) at 3, 6, 9 and 12 months after the initiation of SGLT2 inhibitor, using the cumulative/dynamic AUC method proposed by Heagerty et al. 21 A piecewise Cox proportional hazards model was fitted using a start–stop data structure with an interaction term between risk groups and time interval to account for time-varying effects, with the cut-off time point selected based on observed data patterns (time-dependent ROC peak and early event clustering). Hazard ratios with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for each risk group and time interval were derived from the fitted model coefficients and visualised using forest plots. In addition, to evaluate long-term glycaemic control according to the number of CAMs at baseline, we graphed the changes in mean HbA1c over 48 months (i.e. 24 months before and after the initiation of SGLT2 inhibitors) stratified by the number of CAMs. The impact of the number of CAMs on both the baseline and 48-month mean HbA1c was assessed using a multivariable linear regression model, with unstandardised partial regression coefficient (B) calculations.

Because a proportion of baseline clinical laboratory values were missing in this study, we imputed missing values using the measurement closest to the baseline time point for each variable. To evaluate the potential impact of missing data, variable-specific analyses were conducted using both imputed and complete-case datasets, given that the extent of missing data varied across variables. In addition, sensitivity analyses were conducted for the multivariable models by comparing results from complete-case analysis with those from the imputed dataset.

Two-tailed p-values of <0.05 were considered statistically significant. The C-index and time-dependent AUC were calculated using R software (version 4.5.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), with the survcomp and timeROC packages, respectively. The piecewise Cox proportional hazards model was implemented using the survival package. All other statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 30.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Reporting guidelines

The reporting of this study conforms to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement. 22

Disproportionality analysis based on the JADER database

Data source and preprocessing

We conducted a disproportionality analysis using the Japanese Adverse Drug Event Report (JADER) database provided by the Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency, a Japanese regulatory agency for pharmaceuticals and medical devices, working with the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare. Because disproportionality analysis cannot estimate incidence or establish causality, this supplementary analysis was conducted to assess the consistency of signals with the findings from the retrospective cohort. JADER collated spontaneously reported AEs from healthcare professionals, pharmaceutical manufacturers and patients, encompassing four datasets: DEMO (patient demographics), HIST (medical history), DRUG (drug exposure) and REAC (AE records). 23 The study covered reports submitted from Q1 2004 through Q4 2024. Supplemental Figure 1 illustrates the data processing workflow and case selection criteria in detail. From the complete dataset, we extracted AE reports submitted by adult patients with diabetes. In this study, patients were classified as diabetic if their medical history included the terms “diabetes” or “diabetic.” Cases in which drug involvement was classified as “concomitant” or “interaction” were excluded, and only those labelled as “suspected drug” were retained, as these are considered to have a potential causal relationship with the reported AEs. Duplicate reports were identified using case identifiers (case ID) in combination with the suspected drug and AE. In cases with multiple records for the same combination, duplicates were removed, and a single representative record was retained.

Definition of genital infection

In JADER, AEs are represented using preferred terms (PTs) defined by the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA). Since no Standardised MedDRA Query (SMQ) corresponding to genital infection exists, we developed a custom definition using selected PTs from MedDRA/J version 28.1 (MedDRA MSSO, McLean, VA, USA). PTs were classified as genital infections if their associated higher-level terms (i.e. high-level terms; high-level group terms) included both “reproductive tract” and “infections” (Supplemental Table 1). Given the limited understanding of the specific disease entities and causative pathogens involved in genital infections associated with SGLT2 inhibitors, this study prioritised diagnostic sensitivity and employed a broad definitional framework. However, PTs related to intrauterine infections, pelvic inflammatory disorders and pelvic infections were excluded because of their frequent association with specific clinical contexts, such as the perinatal period or postoperative abdominal conditions.

Signal detection

Signal detection was performed by calculating the reporting odds ratio (ROR) and its 95% CI from a two-by-two contingency table (Supplemental Figure 2). The p-value was obtained using Fisher’s exact test. To visualise the relative signal strength of genital infection associated with the SGLT2 inhibitor among all suspected drugs, a scatter plot (volcano plot) was constructed. The vertical axis represents the negative base-10 logarithm of the p-value (−log10[p-value]), and the horizontal axis shows the natural logarithm of the ROR (ln ROR). To examine whether sex-specific risk factors identified in our retrospective cohort study were similarly associated with genital infection in other populations, stratified analyses were performed for each risk factor. Within each stratum, the ROR and its 95% CI for genital infection associated with SGLT2 inhibitors were calculated. Given the limited number of reports on genital infections and the primary objective of assessing directional trends between groups, no statistical testing was conducted for ROR comparisons; instead, a descriptive evaluation was performed.

Results

Retrospective cohort study based on real-world clinical data

Among the 2232 patients with T2D who were prescribed SGLT2 inhibitors, 2202 (98.7%) were included in the analysis after excluding 30 patients with missing data required for evaluation. SGLT2 inhibitor prescriptions were distributed as follows: luseogliflozin (39.0%), empagliflozin (20.8%), dapagliflozin (15.1%), tofogliflozin (12.9%), ipragliflozin (9.4%) and canagliflozin (2.8%). The baseline characteristics of the study participants are presented in Table 1. Compared with males, females were older and had a higher BMI. Significant differences were also observed in diastolic blood pressure, liver function indicators, lipid profiles and the prevalence of albuminuria. Sex differences in the use of CAMs were observed for dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors and glucagon-like peptidase-1 receptor agonists; however, the total number of CAMs did not differ significantly between males and females (Table 1). The composition of CAM by the number of concomitant drug classes is shown in Supplemental Table 2.

During the follow-up period (median, 596.5 days; interquartile range, 187.75–1108.25 days), 36 (1.6%) individuals developed genital infections. The diagnosis of genital infections was made primarily at Jinnouchi Hospital, a specialised diabetes centre, based on physicians’ clinical judgement informed by patients’ reported symptoms. Among patients who developed genital infections, 30.6% sought evaluation at gynaecology or urology departments at other institutions. Although some patients were microbiologically confirmed in these departments, detailed information, including the causative pathogens, was unavailable in a substantial proportion of cases due to the absence of referral documentation. Among patients with genital infections, pathogens were documented in three cases, including two cases of Candida infection and one case of fungal infections of unspecified aetiology. Although the severity of genital infections could not be assessed, 63.9% of patients required treatment (e.g., antifungal, antibacterial or anti-inflammatory agents) before and/or at the time of discontinuation of SGLT2 inhibitors. Notably, symptoms resolved in all patients following discontinuation of SGLT2 inhibitors.

Females had a significantly higher incidence of genital infections than males (3.6% vs 0.8%, p < 0.001). The distribution of genital infections by individual SGLT2 inhibitor is shown in Supplemental Table 3, with no obvious predominance in any particular agent. According to the univariable logistic regression analyses stratified by sex, higher BMI, AST and ALT, as well as the presence of urine ketones, were significantly associated with the incidence of genital infection in males (Table 2). In the multivariable logistic regression analysis using stepwise forward selection, a higher BMI was the only variable as a potential risk factor for genital infection. The BMI cutoff value for predicting the incidence of genital infection, determined by ROC curve analysis, was 26.88 kg/m2. By contrast, among females, univariable logistic regression analyses revealed that age ⩾65 years, thiazolidinedione use and ⩾3 CAMs were significantly associated with the incidence of genital infection. Given the potential interaction between age ⩾65 years and ⩾3 CAMs, multivariable analysis was performed, including two candidate factors: stratified groups defined by their combinations and thiazolidinedione use. Subsequently, in females, the combination of age ⩾65 years and ⩾3 CAMs was identified as a significant risk factor for genital infection in the final model (Table 2).

Based on the results of the logistic regression analyses, a composite risk variable with four categories – defined by sex and the presence of risk factors – was constructed and incorporated into the survival analysis. Using males with BMI <26.88 kg/m2 as the reference group, the risk of developing genital infection increased progressively among males with BMI ⩾26.88 kg/m2, females aged <65 years and/or receiving <3 CAMs and females aged ⩾65 years receiving ⩾3 CAMs (Figure 1 and Table 3). A predictive model incorporating this composite risk variable demonstrated excellent discrimination, with a C-index of 0.83 (p < 0.001). According to the time-dependent ROC curves, the model’s discriminative ability peaked at 6 months after initiation of the SGLT2 inhibitor, with AUC values of 0.79, 0.85, 0.77 and 0.76 at 3, 6, 9 and 12 months, respectively (Supplemental Figure 3). In females aged ⩾65 years receiving ⩾3 CAMs, identified as the highest-risk cohort, over 80% of patients who developed genital infections experienced onset within 6 months. A piecewise Cox proportional hazards model with a cut point at 6 months showed no statistically significant interaction between risk group and time intervals. Although the point estimates suggested a higher hazard ratio for females aged ⩾65 years receiving ⩾3 CAMs before 6 months than after 6 months, this difference was not statistically significant, and the CIs overlapped (Supplemental Figure 4).

An increased number of CAMs was associated with higher mean HbA1c levels over the 48 months. By contrast, no dose-dependent relationship was observed between the number of CAMs and baseline HbA1c (Supplemental Figure 5 and Supplemental Table 4).

Sensitivity analyses showed consistent results. The direction of the association was consistent across variable-specific analyses using the imputed and complete-case datasets. Furthermore, sensitivity analyses of the multivariable models showed no material differences in effect estimates or statistical significance between complete-case and imputed analyses.

Disproportionality analysis based on the JADER database

A total of 228,261 cases (drug–AE pairs) were extracted from the JADER database. Among them, genital infection was reported in 241 cases, with various SGLT2 inhibitors being frequently identified as suspected drugs (Supplemental Table 5). A volcano plot illustrating the association between genital infection and suspected drugs, based on RORs, is presented in Supplemental Figure 6. In this plot, points in the upper-right area indicate drug–AE pairs with both high RORs and strong statistical significance, characterised by large log-odds ratios on the x-axis and small p-values (−log10[p-value]) on the y-axis. Consequently, several SGLT2 inhibitors (dapagliflozin, empagliflozin, ipragliflozin and luseogliflozin) exhibited strong associations with genital infection, which were statistically significant (Supplemental Figure 6).

In an attempt to determine elevated BMI, polypharmacy of antidiabetic agents, older age – identified in retrospective cohort study as sex-specific risk factors for genital infection – using the JADER database, it was found that BMI could not be evaluated due to the use of broad categorical data for body weight and height (i.e. in 10-kg or 10-cm increments), which made precise calculation impossible. Conversely, the number of antidiabetic drug classes was successfully determined for each patient, based on all drug information registered as suspected, concomitant or interacting agents. Given that age was recorded in 10-year increments, we used 60 years as the cutoff point, comparing individuals aged ⩾60 years with those aged 20–59 years. The effect of the combination of age ⩾60 years and number of antidiabetic medications could not be evaluated due to an insufficient sample size. Table 4 presents the results of stratified disproportionality analyses by ROR for genital infections in the JADER database. The RORs for genital infection were higher in older individuals and in those taking a greater number of diabetic medications in females. By contrast, no such association was observed in males.

Discussion

In our retrospective cohort study, females were found to have a higher risk of developing genital infections associated with SGLT2 inhibitors compared with males. Among females, the combination of older age and polypharmacy of antidiabetic agents was identified as a significant contributor to infection risk, whereas elevated BMI was implicated in males. Furthermore, a supplementary analysis using the JADER database confirmed that females are at higher risk and that older age and polypharmacy of antidiabetic agents may represent female-specific risk determinants for genital infections.

The incidence of genital infection in our cohort was 1.6%, which was lower than previously reported.11,14,15,24 This discrepancy is likely attributable to differences in study design, with the most influential factor being that our study focused specifically on genital infections leading to discontinuation of SGLT2 inhibitors. It is presumed that the incidence would have been higher if mild symptoms that allowed continuation of therapy had also been included. However, by defining genital infections that led to discontinuation as the study outcome, we were able to confirm a causal relationship with SGLT2 inhibitors. This approach also allowed us to identify key risk factors that may inform preventive and interventional strategies to support therapy continuation.

Although sex differences in the incidence of genital infections during SGLT2 inhibitor therapy are well recognised,9,11,14 to our knowledge, no prior study has examined risk factors separately for males and females in detail. All risk factors identified in the present study were mechanistically consistent with previous reports, yet they differed markedly between sexes. The older age and polypharmacy of antidiabetic agents in females, and elevated BMI in males, were simple features that could detect subgroups at significantly increased risk of genital infections with SGLT2 inhibitor therapy.

In this study, the use of a specific antidiabetic agent did not influence the risk of developing genital infections, whereas the number of CAMs was associated with the risk in females. On the other hand, HbA1c was not identified as a significant risk factor, which is consistent with the previous report by McGovern et al. 14 In our cohort, when longitudinal changes in HbA1c were stratified by the baseline number of CAMs, no substantial differences were observed in baseline HbA1c; however, the mean HbA1c over 48 months was dose-dependently related to the number of CAMs. Patients with more CAMs are considered more likely to have difficulty achieving optimal glycaemic control. This may be attributable to underlying conditions such as severe insulin resistance or chronic hyperglycaemia, which cannot be sufficiently assessed by HbA1c at a single time point.

In females, oestrogen is essential for maintaining normal vulvovaginal epithelium, lubrication and a healthy microbiota. 25 Hypo-oestrogenic states lead to pubic hair loss, epithelial atrophy and reduced vaginal secretions, while decreased collagen and adipose tissue result in structural changes of the labia. Furthermore, the reduction of glycogen in the genital epithelium diminishes lactobacilli, thereby promoting a shift towards an alkaline pH. Ageing and oestrogen deficiency therefore induce these physical and physiological alterations in the genital region, contributing to significant morbidity. 25

Additionally, insulin resistance contributes to a series of immune responses that exacerbate the inflammatory state and promote hyperglycaemia. 26 In a state of chronic hyperglycaemia, defects in innate immunity – including dysfunctional neutrophils and macrophages – as well as dysfunction of the adaptive immune response, such as T-cell impairment, are thought to contribute to reduced host defence against invading pathogens. 26 Hine et al. reported that infections, most commonly bacterial, fungal or yeast origin, including genital infections, were more frequent with worse glycaemic control in patients with T2D. 27 Taken together, the hormonal, age-related and metabolic factors likely acted synergistically in females aged ⩾65 years receiving ⩾3 CAMs, markedly increasing the risk of SGLT2 inhibitor-associated genital infections.

In males, elevated BMI was identified as a significant risk factor for genital infections. Although no statistically significant association was observed between BMI categories based on conventional cutoffs and the incidence of genital infections (Table 2), the detailed distribution of the incidence across BMI categories (Supplemental Table 6) suggests a trend towards a higher risk among individuals with obesity. Obesity is associated with secondary phimosis, buried penis and deterioration of local hygiene, all of which are known risk factors for genital infections.28 –30 Diabetes is also a well-recognised contributor to secondary phimosis and poor hygiene. 29 Therefore, males with both obesity and diabetes may have a particularly elevated risk of developing genital infections.

On the other hand, no age-related increase in risk was observed in males. As shown in Supplemental Figure 7, the age distribution by sex in the cohort indicated that males had a higher proportion in their 40s and 50s and a lower proportion in their 70s than females. This pattern may be related to sex differences in the age at diabetes diagnosis and life expectancy.31,32 It cannot be ruled out that this demographic difference contributed to the absence of an age-related risk trend observed in males in the present study. Further investigation of the impact of age and optimal BMI cut-off values is warranted in larger cohorts, particularly those including a sufficient number of male patients with genital infections.

While no clear predominance of genital infections was observed for any individual SGLT2 inhibitor (Supplemental Table 3), the statistical power to detect differences across multiple agents was limited by the sparse events. Therefore, the absence of statistical significance should not be interpreted as evidence of no difference among groups. These findings should be interpreted with caution, as the study may have been underpowered to detect modest differences between agents.

In this study, the predictive performance for incident genital infections based on stratification by sex and sex-specific risk factors was sufficiently high (C-index 0.83). In addition to sex, age, number of CAMs, and BMI at the initiation of SGLT2 inhibitor therapy are all readily obtainable in routine clinical practice and can be used to stratify the risk of genital infections. When initiating SGLT2 inhibitors in patients at high risk, healthcare professionals should provide more proactive and careful guidance on the early signs of genital infections and the importance of maintaining optimal genital hygiene. 17 Furthermore, it should be emphasised that adequate glycaemic control in older females and the achievement of a healthy weight in males are also crucial from the perspective of preventing genital infections.

Continuous follow-up and timely interventions can alleviate patients’ discomfort and concerns, thereby reducing the likelihood of discontinuing SGLT2 inhibitors. 15 Genital infections are generally manageable with pharmacological treatments such as antifungal agents, and in most cases, continuation of SGLT2 inhibitor therapy is feasible. 15 Although temporary discontinuation may be reasonable in cases of severe genital infection, resumption of therapy should be considered once the infection has resolved, given the incremental benefits of SGLT2 inhibitors in preventing renal and cardiovascular complications.15,17 In this study, we found that more than 80% of genital infections among patients at the highest risk (i.e. females aged ⩾65 years receiving ⩾3 CAMs) occurred within the first 6 months of treatment. Despite the absence of a statistically significant interaction between risk group and time intervals, these findings suggest that intensive follow-up during the initial 6 months after initiating SGLT2 inhibitor therapy may be warranted.

The cohort in this study was derived from a specialised diabetes hospital without on-site urologists or gynaecologists. Consequently, specialised diagnostic testing for genital infections (e.g. laboratory analysis of vaginal discharge specimens) was not performed in-house. Diagnoses were based instead on patient-reported symptoms, visual assessments by in-house physicians and information from external specialists. Physicians, nurses and pharmacists primarily conducted hygiene counselling; however, it was not standardised. Management of genital infections, including prescribing, was based on the latest evidence and physicians’ clinical experience, but no explicit protocols were established. These factors cannot be excluded as factors limiting the generalizability of the present findings.

It is particularly noteworthy that the female-specific potential of older age and polypharmacy of antidiabetic agents to increase the risk of genital infections was also observed among diabetic patients in the JADER database. Although the JADER analysis differs substantially from our retrospective cohort study in terms of study population and methodology, the consistency of findings across these distinct settings suggests reproducibility under varying conditions and supports the external validity of the results. However, due to limitations in both the number of relevant cases and the level of data granularity in the JADER database, a sufficient evaluation of the combined effects of older age and polypharmacy of antidiabetic agents, as well as of elevated BMI, was not feasible. These issues consequently remain to be addressed in future research.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, in the retrospective cohort component of this research, no comparison was made with placebo or active control groups. Therefore, we cannot exclude the possibility that the risk factors for genital infections identified in this study may also confer risk on individuals not receiving SGLT2 inhibitors. Nevertheless, because the outcome was defined as genital infections whose symptoms resolved or attenuated after discontinuation of the SGLT2 inhibitor, a causal relationship is reasonably supported. Second, although we collected as much information as possible on factors that could influence the development of genital infections, we cannot exclude the possibility that additional, unmeasured risk factors may have influenced the outcomes. In particular, we were unable to account for recurrent cases or the duration of T2D. In the report by McGovern et al., the tabulated data show a tendency for patients with a more recent history of genital infections and a shorter duration of T2D to have a higher risk. 14 Third, most genital infections identified in the retrospective cohort analysis were defined based on symptoms, and neither formal diagnosis nor causative pathogens (such as Candida species) could be confirmed. The genital infections defined in the JADER analysis also included many PTs for which the causative pathogen was unknown. We were unable to perform a detailed analysis stratified by pathogen while maintaining sufficient statistical power. Fourth, a single-centre retrospective study has inherent limitations in terms of sample size and generalizability. Nevertheless, we could detect similar signals regarding the female-specific risks in the JADER analysis. Finally, although the ROR analysis using JADER is useful for detecting potential drug–AE associations, it does not allow estimation of incidence or absolute risk due to the lack of denominator data and the inherent reporting bias in spontaneous reporting systems. In addition, serious AEs may be overrepresented, and residual duplicate or follow-up reports may remain despite attempts to remove duplicates using case identifiers and drug–event combinations. Potential confounding factors, including concomitant medications and patient characteristics, could not be fully accounted for.

Conclusion

Sex differences were evident in both the incidence and risk factors of genital infections associated with SGLT2 inhibitors. The combination of older age and polypharmacy of antidiabetic agents in females, and elevated BMI in males, represented simple characteristics that identified subgroups with markedly increased risk of genital infections. These findings allow for more efficient stratification of the risk of genital infections during SGLT2 inhibitor therapy. Furthermore, preventive strategies and interventions that take these sex differences into account may help reduce unintended discontinuation of SGLT2 inhibitors due to genital infections.
