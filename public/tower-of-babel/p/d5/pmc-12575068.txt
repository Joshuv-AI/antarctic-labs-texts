Osseointegration properties of niobia-modified yttria-stabilized zirconia implant surface: an in vivo study
Authors: Ji-Hyun Park, Le Na Lau, Young Suk Kang, In-Sung Luke Yeo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPURPOSEThis study aimed to assess the osseointegration effects of niobia-modified yttria-stabilized tetragonal zirconia ((Y,Nb)-TZP) implants, compared to titanium implants and 3 mol% yttria-stabilized tetragonal zirconia (3Y-TZP) implants in a rabbit tibia model.MATERIALS AND METHODSSandblasted (Y,Nb)-TZP implants (YNb), sandblasted and acid-etched titanium implants (SLA), and smooth-surface 3D-printed 3Y-TZP implants (3Y) were prepared. Surface characteristics of the specimens were evaluated using scanning electron microscopy and confocal laser scanning microscopy. Implants were inserted into the tibiae of 16 rabbits, with eight rabbits euthanized at each time point (2 and 6 weeks). To estimate periodic bone remodeling, tetracycline and xylenol orange were administered to the rabbits euthanized at 6 weeks. Undecalcified specimens were prepared to measure the periodic bone remodeling ratio (PBR), bone-to-implant contact (BIC) and bone area (BA). Data analysis was conducted using one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post hoc analysis and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test at a significance level of 0.05.RESULTSAt both the 2-week and 6-week intervals, YNb group exhibited significantly higher BIC values than the SLA and 3Y groups. No significant differences (P > .05) were found in PBR and BA across all comparisons.CONCLUSIONSandblasted (Y,Nb)-TZP implants indicated superior osseointegration in comparison to SLA titanium and smooth-surface 3D-printed 3Y-TZP implants. The enhanced osseointegration observed in this study supports the clinical potential of (Y,Nb)-TZP as a next-generation zirconia implant material.Keywords: Bone regeneration, Dental implants, Niobium pentoxide, Osseointegration, Zirconium oxide

INTRODUCTION

Zirconia stabilized with 3 mol% yttria (3Y-TZP) is the most extensively utilized polycrystalline ceramic in dentistry, effectively applied across a range of prosthetic applications, from single-unit prostheses to complete mouth rehabilitation.1,2,3,4 Due to its biocompatibility, osseointegration capability, and aesthetic qualities, it has also garnered interest as a material for dental implants.5,6,7,8 In the early 2000s, several studies confirmed that 3Y-TZP could achieve osseointegration comparable to sandblasted and acid-etched (SLA) titanium when subjected to identical surface treatments, which facilitated the commercialization of zirconia implants.9,10 Many SLA-treated 3Y-TZP implants with additional modifications, such as hydroxyapatite (HA) coating or irradiation, have been developed based on extensive research.11,12,13 Furthermore, several recent studies have reported the clinical success of zirconia implants.14,15,16 However, inherent brittleness and high surface hardness of 3Y-TZP result in fracture issues, low machinability, and challenges in surface treatment, causing clinicians to hesitate in adopting zirconia implants in practice.17,18,19

Several preclinical investigations have indicated that the surface of 3Y-TZP exhibits comparable or enhanced osseointegration compared to titanium when both surfaces possess identical morphology. 20,21,22 Nonetheless, the application of identical surface treatments to 3Y-TZP results in a surface morphology that markedly differs from that of titanium, due to variations in surface hardness and crystalline structure, consequently leading to differences in bone response.9,23,24 Recent studies have shown that the application of SLA surface treatment to 3Y-TZP does not yield an optimal substrate for eliciting the most favorable bone response, despite its status as the gold standard for titanium. When 3Y-TZP underwent sandblasting and acid etching, there was a reduction in cell reactivity compared to turned or smooth surfaces, and in vivo studies using animal models did not exhibit enhanced osseointegration.12,25,26,27,28 Therefore, improving both the mechanical properties of the material and its surface morphology remains a significant challenge for zirconia implants in achieving clinically superior performance. Various strategies are under investigation, including increasing the yttrium content in 3Y-TZP or incorporating other additives to modify its crystalline phase and structure under clinical conditions, with the aim of improving its mechanical properties and responsiveness to surface treatments. 29,30,31,32

Incorporating niobium oxide as a stabilizer along-side yttrium in zirconia modifies the crystal lattice and grain structure, thereby reducing surface hardness, and enhancing fracture toughness.33,34,35,36 In the study by Lee et al., when 4.75 mol% of Nb2O5 was added to Yttrium-modified TZP followed by sintering and hot pressing, the flexural strength increased to approximately 1 GPa, and the fracture toughness also improved to 7.5 MPa·m1/2, confirming the clinical applicability of niobia-modified Y-TZP ((Y,Nb)-TZP) in the dental field. Furthermore, even after aging at 220 – 1000℃ for 1000 hours, although there was a 15% reduction in strength, the content of m-ZrO2 did not increase, indicating excellent resistance to low-temperature degradation (LTD).37 Other previous researches have indicated that (Y,Nb)-TZP exhibits a diminished risk of fracture and demonstrates mechanical properties suitable for clinical applications in the fabrication of dental prostheses.38,39,40 The addition of niobium oxide to Y-TZP has been shown to result in osteoblastic cell behavior that is similar or superior to that of 3Y-TZP, with an animal study confirming osseointegration effects comparable to those observed with SLA-treated titanium.24,41 Recent investigations have also revealed that sandblasting the surface of (Y,Nb)-TZP enhances surface roughness, which can provide a clinically appropriate removal torque value while sustaining the flexural strength of this material.42

Although various studies support the potential of (Y,Nb)-TZP as an implant material, few studies have compared the osseointegration of sandblasted (Y,Nb)-TZP implants with that of titanium implants or 3Y-TZP implants. This study aimed to compare the osseointegration of sandblasted (Y,Nb)-TZP implants to SLA-treated titanium implants and smooth-surface 3Y-TZP implants in a rabbit tibia model. Two in vivo experiments were conducted. In the first, injection-molded and sandblasted (Y,Nb)-TZP implants were compared to pre-fabricated SLA-treated titanium implants. In the second, (Y,Nb)-TZP implants manufactured in the same manner were compared to 3D-printed and sintered 3Y-TZP implants without surface modification. The surface of each implant was characterized, and bone-to-implant contact (BIC), bone area (BA), and periodic bone remodeling ratio (PBR) were quantitatively analyzed using fluorescent markers administered during healing. The hypothesis underlying this study was that sandblasted (Y,Nb)-TZP implants would exhibit osseointegration comparable to or exceeding that of SLA-treated titanium and smooth-surface 3Y-TZP implants.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A total of 64 implants were prepared for in vivo experiments and surface characterization. For the animal experiments, 16 commercially available SLA titanium implants (Deep implant, Deep Implant System, Seongnam, Korea) with dimensions of Ø3.4 mm × 8 mm were prepared. Molds with the same macroscopic morphology were fabricated to produce 32 (Y,Nb)-TZP implants by pressing and sintering with (Y,Nb)-TZP powder (Vatech MCIS, Suwon, Korea). These implants were sandblasted with 125 µm Al2O3 grit at a pressure of 400 KPa (4 bar). Furthermore, 16 3Y-TZP implants were prepared by 3D printing (CeraFab System S65 Medical, Lithoz, Vienna, Austria) using zirconia powder (LithaCon 3Y 210, Lithoz, Vienna, Austria) without surface modification following the manufacturer’s instructions. For surface characterization, six implants of each type (SLA titanium, (Y,Nb)-TZP, and 3Y-TZP) were prepared using the same methods.

SLA group: computer numerical control-milled grade 4 titanium implants with SLA surface modification

YNb group: pressed and sintered niobia-modified yttria-stabilized tetragonal zirconia polycrystal ((Y,Nb)- TZP) implants with sandblasting

3Y group: 3D-printed 3Y-TZP (3 mol% yttria-stabilized tetragonal zirconia polycrystal) implants without surface modification

YNb′ group: pressed and sintered (Y,Nb)-TZP implants with sandblasting (only for comparison 2 of the in vivo experiment)

For each group, three implants were selected, and three points per implant were observed using field emission-scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) (S-4700, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) to examine the surface microstructure. The chemical composition of the implant surfaces was analyzed using an energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) device (EMAX, Horiba, Northampton, UK). The average value from these three observations was used as the representative value for each specimen. Surface topography was examined using confocal laser scanning microscopy (LSM 800, Carl Zeiss AG, Jena, Germany). Images acquired from CLSM were analyzed with dedicated software (ConfoMap, Carl Zeiss AG) to calculate surface parameters, including the arithmetical mean height (Sa) and the developed interfacial area ratio (Sdr). Six points were measured from three implants per group, and the mean of these measurements was used as the representative value for the specimen.

Sixteen male New Zealand white rabbits (weighing approximately 2.5 kg) were used for this in vivo study, which was approved by the Institutional Animal Research Ethics Committee of Cronex (CRONEX-IACUC: 202309001, Hwaseong, Korea). All procedures were conducted following the Animal Research: Reporting In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines 2.0.43 The experiment was conducted as two separate comparative studies. As only four implants could be placed in each rabbit, complete randomization of three implant types would have necessitated the use of a large number of animals. To minimize animal use, the (Y,Nb)-TZP group was divided into two subgroups (YNb and YNb′), each paired with a different counterpart implant type (SLA and 3Y). The first study compared the SLA group with the YNb group, and the second study compared the 3Y group with the YNb′ group. In each experiment, implants from two groups were alternately placed in the tibiae of four rabbits (two rabbits per one set), which were euthanized at 2 weeks. The same procedure was repeated for another four rabbits, which were sacrificed at 6 weeks (Fig. 1).

Experimental design. Each set comprised two rabbits. For each comparison, two sets (four rabbits; n = 8) were sacrificed at each time point (2 weeks and 6 weeks).

Prior to surgery, general anesthesia was induced via intravenous administration of 5 mg/kg tiletamine hydrochloride and 5 mg/kg zolazepam hydrochloride (Zoletil 50, Virbac S.A., Carros, France), and 4.7 mg/kg xylazine hydrochloride (Rompun, Bayer Korea, Seoul, Korea) was delivered intramuscularly. The surgical sites were shaved and disinfected with 7.5% povidone- iodine (Betadine, Korea Pharma, Seoul, Korea). Local anesthesia was then administered by injecting one ampule of 2% lidocaine hydrochloride with 1 : 100,000 epinephrine (Yuhan Company, Seoul, Korea) into the surgical sites. Incision, which was approximately 30 mm in length, was made along the tibia axis, and the full thickness flaps were elevated to expose the surface of bone. Drilling was performed up to 3.2 mm in diameter for implant placement. To prevent tibial fractures, all implants were inserted manually, ensuring that the macrothreads were completely embedded in cortical bone. The implants were spaced 10 mm apart in each tibia. After implant placement, the muscle and fascia were sutured with polyglactin sutures (Vicryl, Ethicon, Somerville, MA, USA), and the skin was closed with polyamide suture (Blue Nylon, Ailee, Busan, Korea). Each rabbit was isolated and given enrofloxacin (Biotril, Komipharm International, Siheung, Korea) at 4 mg/kg and meloxicam (Metacam, Labiana Life science, Terrassa, Spain) at 0.4 mg/kg for three days for antibiotic prophylaxis and pain control.

For the rabbits scheduled for sacrifice at 6 weeks, 63 mg of tetracycline (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) dissolved in 0.45% phosphate buffered saline (BioPrince, Chuncheon, Korea) at 10 mg/mL was administered intramuscularly at 2 weeks post-operation. At 4 weeks post-surgery, 230 mg of xylenol orange (Sigma-Aldrich) dissolved in a 1% NaHCO3 buffer (Sigma-Aldrich) at 100 mg/mL was injected. Except for two rabbits euthanized early due to tibial fractures, six rabbits at 2 weeks and eight rabbits at 6 weeks were sacrificed under general anesthesia via intramuscular injection of 15 mg/kg of suxamethonium chloride hydrate (Succipharm, Komipharm International). The tibial specimens with integrated implants were dissected from the surrounding soft tissue and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for one week. To prepare tissue slides, the fixed tibial specimens were rinsed under running water for 9 hours, and regions of interest were sectioned. After dehydrated with ethyl alcohol, the specimens were infiltrated with Technovit 7200 Resin (Heraeus Kulzer, Hanau, Germany) and polymerized in a UV embedding system (KULZER EXAKT 520®, Heraeus Kulzer). The resulting resin blocks were ground and polished to a thickness of 40 ± 5 µm using a grinding machine (Kulzer Exakt 400CS®, Heraeus Kulzer) and mounted on acrylic slides. For histomorphometric analysis, the specimens from the 2-week sacrifices were stained with Goldner’s trichrome. The 6-week specimens were first examined under fluorescence labeling and subsequently stained with Goldner’s trichrome.

The unstained slides from the 6-week sacrifices were examined under an optical microscope (Olympus IX71, Olympus Optical, Osaka, Japan) equipped with an ultraviolet light source (U-RFL-T, Olympus Optical) and individual optical bandpass filters (Samwoo Science, Seoul, Korea). Images were captured using a digital camera (DP72, Olympus Optical) attached to the microscope. Image acquisition settings in the software (CellSens, Olympus Optical) were adjusted so that the brightest green (tetracycline- labeled) and red (xylenol orange-labeled) areas reached a gray value of 200, while dark regions without marker expression had a gray value of 0. Marker distribution around the threads was qualitatively evaluated, and periodic bone remodeling ratios were quantitatively measured using an image analysis system (ImageJ 1.60, U.S. National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). The periodic bone remodeling ratio was calculated by dividing the total tetracycline-labeled area (green, representing 2 weeks) of the best three consecutive threads by the total xylenol orange-labeled area (red, representing 4 weeks). From both sides of the implant, the value closest to 1 was selected as the representative ratio. A higher ratio indicated earlier bone formation in the inter-thread space.

The slides stained with Goldner’s trichrome were observed under a light microscope (DM2700M, Leica Microsystems GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany) and photographed using a mounted digital camera (DMC5400, Leica Microsystems GmbH). The images were then analyzed using ImageJ 1.60 (U.S. National Institutes of Health) to measure bone-to-implant contact (BIC) and bone area (BA) within the threads. Each parameter was measured in the best three consecutive threads. Between the two implant sides, the higher value was taken as the representative value for each slide.44

The Shapiro-Wilk test was conducted to assess normality, and all surface parameter data met the assumption of normality (P > .05). Accordingly, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with the Bonferroni post hoc test was employed to compare surface parameter data and descriptive statistics for these data were reported as the mean and standard deviation. On the other hand, the periodic bone remodeling ratio and histomorphometric data were analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test within the same tibia since most of the data did not satisfy the assumption of normality according to the Shapiro-Wilk test (P < .05). Consequently, descriptive statistics for these histological data were reported as the median and interquartile range. All statistical analyses were performed with R version 4.1.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) at the significance level of 0.05.

In the YNb group, grain structures measuring approximately 100 – 200 nm were exposed on the surface. While these surfaces exhibited undulations similar to those observed in the SLA group, the peaks and fossae were relatively blunted. The 3Y group featured grain structures of similar size to those in the YNb group but arranged on a uniform surface, appearing as hemispherical or quarter-spherical forms without additional microstructural features. Bright green and red fluorescence were detected along bone remodeling areas on all slides (Fig. 4). Within the triangular spaces between threads, the green marker was more abundant near the old bone surface and implant surface. In contrast, toward the center of these triangular spaces, the 4-week marker (xylenol orange, red) commonly appeared in a ring-like pattern (Fig. 4A). DISCUSSION

In this study, (Y,Nb)-TZP implant exhibited higher BIC values at both 2 and 6 weeks compared to SLA titanium and 3Y-TZP implants. Therefore, the null hypothesis of this study ‘There is no difference in osseointegration between sandblasted (Y,Nb)-TZP implants and SLA-treated titanium implants, nor between sandblasted (Y,Nb)-TZP implants and smooth-surface 3Y-TZP implants.’ was rejected, suggesting that (Y,Nb)-TZP implants may provide enhanced osseointegration compared to the other implant types. Given that the cortical bone remodeling rate of rabbits is approximately 3 times that of humans, these findings suggest that (Y,Nb)-TZP may achieve stronger osseointegration at 1- and 3-month healing periods in humans.45 Generally, increasing surface roughness through various treatments, as indicated by higher Sa or Sdr values, improves removal torque but does not definitively confirm enhanced cellular response or osseointegration.46 Nevertheless, considering a previous study by Hein et al., which reported osteoblast cell behavior on (Y,Nb)-TZP surfaces comparable to that of 3Y-TZP, the present results in this study imply that sandblasted (Y,Nb)-TZP may serve as a more favorable implant substrate than surfaces derived from 3Y-TZP.41

In this study, three types of implants were separated into two distinct pairwise comparisons, designated as Comparison 1 and Comparison 2. In a rabbit tibia model, where four implants can be placed per subject, arranging all three types simultaneously would result in an incomplete block design. Fully randomizing such a design would require an impractically large number of animals. To comply with the ARRIVE guidelines and minimize animal use, this study adopted the ‘control variable’ concept proposed by Choi et al., dividing the (Y,Nb)-TZP group into two subgroups, YNb and YNb′, each compared with a different counterpart implant type.47 Although YNb and YNb′ showed differing trends in some outcomes, Mann-Whitney tests between the two subgroups revealed no statistically significant differences across any parameter (P > .05), suggesting that the variation was within an acceptable range of experimental error. This variation is presumed to result from inter-animal variability and the fact that all procedures were conducted independently within each comparison.

In Comparison 2 of this study, the osseointegration capability of sandblasted (Y,Nb)-TZP implants was compared with that of smooth-surface 3Y-TZP implants. Recent studies have reported that applying SLA or sandblasting treatments to 3Y-TZP surfaces results in osseointegration and cell behavior that are either similar to or worse than those of untreated surfaces.25,26,27,28 These findings suggest that sandblasting- based surface treatments may not provide an optimal substrate for promoting cell activity and osseointegration in 3Y-TZP implants. In the present study, osseointegration was assessed using histomorphometric methods, rather than removal torque values, which are strongly influenced by surface roughness. Therefore, the control group was selected as untreated smooth-surface 3Y-TZP, which has shown the most favorable histomorphometric results in the previous literatures.25,28

Juvin et al. introduced a quantitative method to estimate bone remodeling by determining the mineral apposition rate (MAR) based on the distance between the centers of each fluorescent band.48 However, the shape and width of the marker bands on slides were inconsistent, along with irregular interband spacing, which is presumed to be attributable to the complex biological influences exerted by both the bone marrow and the periosteal surface, as the implants were placed in close proximity to both structures within the thin tibia of the rabbit. An alternative quantitative method became necessary for the analysis of undecalcified light microscopic slides. In this study, PBR was devised as a novel measure of bone remodeling rate. The expression level of the fluorescent marker was influenced by various factors, including individual differences in the rabbits’ body weight and metabolic rate, variations in slide thickness, storage and UV exposure of the slides, and the settings of the light source and camera. To compensate for these potential sources of error, fluorescence imaging was standardized by normalizing the maximum intensity of each marker using software. Furthermore, by calculating the ratio of the areas of each fluorescent marker’s total area between the threads located in the cortical bone, this approach offsets disparities in new bone formation arising from variations in drilling hole size and minimizes operator bias, thus enabling practical quantitative evaluation of multiple slides. Nonetheless, no significant differences in PBR were found between implant types. This outcome may be attributable to setting the region of interest to encompass the entire inter-thread space, where the 2-week marker often appeared as a green belt around the implant surface, while the 4-week marker formed a red ring centrally. This pattern indicated that early bone formation occurred via both distance osteogenesis, originating from preexisting bone, and contact osteogenesis, beginning at the implant surface.49 Taken together, future studies are needed to re-evaluate the PBR calculation method proposed in this study and to further refine the approach for setting the region of interest.

In this study, (Y,Nb)-TZP implants were produced through injection molding and sintering, while 3Y-TZP implants were fabricated via 3D printing and sintering. Both types were designed to replicate the SLA-treated titanium implants manufactured by milling. This study has a limitation in that the effects of different manufacturing methods for each implant group were not accounted for in the experimental design. While EDS analysis confirmed minimal carbon residue in 3D-printed 3Y-TZP implants, subtle morphological and dimensional variations, such as the rounding of sharp thread edges during zirconia fabrication, may have influenced osseointegration and bone remodeling. Additionally, previous studies by Lee et al. and Kim et al. that evaluated the clinical suitability of the mechanical properties of (Y,Nb)-TZP did not fully comply with the specifications of ISO 13356:2015, which sets the standards for the clinical use of zirconia. 36,37 Therefore, it is necessary to assess mechanical properties including flexural strength, fracture toughness, and resistance to low-temperature degradation in accordance with this standard. In addition, it is necessary to determine the optimal Nb mol% based on mechanical and biological considerations, and to investigate surface treatment methods for (Y,Nb)-TZP in order to identify the most suitable substrate in terms of cell behavior, osseointegration, and removal torque values. Furthermore, clinical evaluations should be conducted to assess the safety, stability, and esthetic outcomes of (Y,Nb)-TZP implants under various clinical conditions to ensure their reliable clinical application.

CONCLUSION

Within the limitations of this study, sandblasted (Y, Nb)-TZP implants revealed superior osseointegration, compared to SLA-treated titanium implants and smooth-surface 3Y-TZP implants. Along with the improved mechanical properties and cellular behavior reported in previous studies,24,33,41 the enhanced osseointegration observed in this study suggests the clinical potential of (Y,Nb)-TZP as a next-generation zirconia implant material.
