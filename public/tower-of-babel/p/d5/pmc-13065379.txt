Effects of 8-week prop Pilates or apparatus Pilates on body composition, blood pressure, mechanical properties of core muscles, mental health, and physical fitness in postmenopausal women
Authors: Kiyoung Lim, Yerin Sun, Jae-Ho Choi, Eunjoo Lee, Hun-Young Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract[Purpose] The present study aimed to verify the effects of an 8-week prop Pilates training program on body composition, blood pressure, core muscle mechanical properties, mental health, and physical fitness in postmenopausal women.[Methods] Twenty-four postmenopausal women were divided into a prop Pilates group (PPG, n = 12) and an apparatus Pilates group (APG, n = 12) via randomized equal sampling. All participants maintained their normal daily lifestyle during the study period and performed the Pilates intervention corresponding to each group for 8 weeks, 3 times per week, 50 min a day. The effectiveness of the two modalities was examined by analyzing pre- and post-intervention changes.[Results] PPG showed a significant decrease in external oblique muscle tone, STAI, and BDI and a significant increase in sit-ups, sit-and-reach, and one-leg stand with eyes closed. The APG showed a significant decrease in systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, mean arterial pressure, external oblique and erector spinae muscle tone, and BDI and a significant increase in grip strength, sit-ups, seated forward bends, and one-leg standing with eyes closed.[Conclusion] The present study confirmed that both prop Pilates and apparatus Pilates were effective in improving the mechanical properties of the core muscles, mental health, and physical fitness of postmenopausal women. Overall, significant time effects were observed in the APG for blood pressure, lumbar erector spinae muscle tone, and grip strength.Keywords: prop Pilates, apparatus Pilates, blood pressure, mechanical properties of core muscles, mental health, physical fitness

INTRODUCTION

Women typically experience changes in their menstrual cycles due to a decline in estrogen and progesterone secreted by the ovaries [1]. In their mid-40s, women go through a 4- to 5-year menopausal transition period, during which menstrual cycles become longer and more irregular, and a 12-month period of continuous amenorrhea after the last menstrual period, referred to as menopause or postmenopause [2].

Postmenopausal women experience vasomotor, urogenital, and musculoskeletal abnormalities and are at increased risk of developing chronic diseases such as cardiovascular disease, stroke, osteoporosis, and obesity, as well as increased mortality. Additionally, they are reported to experience emotional crises such as feelings of inferiority, depression, worthlessness, anxiety, and impatience [3]. Most menopausal women experience more than one menopausal symptom. Despite the complexity and diversity of menopausal symptoms, active management remains limited of these symptoms owing to a lack of awareness and neglect of menopause [4]. Although symptoms experienced during menopause are recognized as natural life events that do not necessarily require specific clinical treatment, menopause represents an unhealthy stage in a woman’s life [5]. Furthermore, this period is a life-changing transition marked by complex psychological changes ranging from developmental crises to shifts in self-identity [6].

During menopause, various clinical problems arise due to various physiological changes. Hormonal changes cause physical property changes, and a rapid decrease in muscle mass and bone mass in the femoral neck leads to osteopenia, osteoporosis, and sarcopenia, which in turn causes various health problems [7]. In addition, the prevalence of cardiovascular disease may be higher in women than in men [8], and decreased estrogen secretion decreases lipolytic activity, leading to an increase in body fat mass and a decrease in lean mass, which has a negative impact on body composition [9]. Changes in the levels of estrogen, a female hormone, have been reported to increase the prevalence of lifestyle-related diseases by increasing triglycerides and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels and decreasing high-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels [10], and causing vasomotor dysfunction, sleep problems, and depressive symptoms [11]. A previous study on the clinical problems caused by menopause in women revealed that obesity, diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, heart disease, osteoporosis, thyroid disease, uterine disease, and depressive symptoms all had high prevalence rates after menopause [4], confirming that physical changes in women after menopause progress in a negative direction.

Exercise interventions are widely used for solving menopausal symptoms [12-15]. Green et al. [13] reported that regular aerobic exercise improved oxygen supply capacity during maximal and submaximal exercise, regardless of hormone replacement therapy in postmenopausal women. Mangione et al. [14] reported that resistance exercise was effective in preventing skeletal muscle degeneration and muscle strength loss due to postmenopausal aging. Thune and Furberg [15] reported that women who performed regular walking exercises of 1 to 1.5 hours per week had a 51% lower incidence of coronary artery disease than women who did not, and that performing long-term walking exercises was more effective in reducing the risk of coronary artery disease than walking quickly. Previous studies have shown that various exercise modalities are effective for improving the health of menopausal women.

Among these, Pilates has recently been actively used to promote health [16]. Pilates has been reported to be very effective in reducing pain and disability by significantly improving lower-extremity muscle strength in women [17], contributing to maintaining spinal and pelvic functions, and decreasing low back pain [18]. It is also effective in strengthening deep muscles, improving flexibility, and rehabilitation [19] and has been reported to improve body mass index (BMI), body fat percentage, and waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) [20]. Additionally, Pilates has been reported to be effective in preventing urinary incontinence in middle-aged women [21]; reducing depression, anxiety, and sleep disorders [22]; and improving balance and proprioception [23]. These effects show that its applications have expanded considerably. Jang et al. [16] reported that a 12-week Pilates apparatus program improved body composition, physical fitness, cardiovascular function, and endothelial function in middle-aged women. Park et al. [24] examined the differences between the effects of an online prop Pilates intervention and a face-to-face Pilates intervention on body composition, muscle mechanical properties, cardiometabolic parameters, mental health, and physical fitness in middle-aged obese women. The results showed no significant differences in body composition or cardiometabolic parameters between the online and face-to-face Pilates sessions. However, both online and face-to-face Pilates sessions showed greater improvements in muscle mechanical properties, cardiometabolic parameters, mental health, and physical fitness than the control group, with the face-to-face Pilates intervention demonstrating greater improvements [24].

Previous studies have confirmed that Pilates is effective in promoting health, regardless of the form or apparatus used. However, studies investigating the effects of two representative Pilates modalities (prop Pilates and apparatus Pilates) on postmenopausal women based on various physiological and clinical parameters are lacking. Therefore, the present study aimed to compare the effects of an 8-week prop Pilates and apparatus Pilates program on body composition, blood pressure, mechanical properties of core muscles, mental health, and physical fitness in postmenopausal women. We hypothesized that 8 weeks of Pilates may improve these parameters and that there will be differences in effectiveness between prop Pilates and apparatus Pilates.

METHODS AND MATERIALS

Participants

The present study included 24 postmenopausal women aged 45-65 years without diagnosed clinical diseases. The participants were randomly allocated (1:1) to either the prop Pilates group (PPG, n = 12) or the apparatus Pilates group (APG, n = 12) using a computer-generated random sequence by a researcher who was not involved in the outcome assessments. Participants were instructed to maintain their usual daily lifestyle throughout the study period. According to their group assignment, participants performed the prescribed Pilates intervention for 8 weeks (3 sessions/week, 50 min/session). However, during the study period, two postmenopausal women dropped out of the PPG (n = 1, withdrawal) and APG (n = 1, withdrawal). Consequently, data from 22 participants were used in the final analysis. The physical characteristics of the participants are presented in Table 1. No differences in physical characteristics were observed between the two groups before the intervention. All participants received detailed information regarding the purpose and procedures of the study, and provided written informed consent prior to participation. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Konkuk University, Korea (7001355-202407-HR-817), and was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study Design

The study design is illustrated in Figure 1, and the two Pilates program modalities (prop Pilates or apparatus Pilates) are shown in Table 2. The Pilates programs for both groups (PPG and APG) were composed of a warm-up, main Pilates session, and cool-down. The warm-up consisted of a bridge (5 reps × 3 sets, 4 min), saw (2 reps × 10 sets, 3 min), and bird dog (6 reps × 6 sets, 3 min) for 10 min, performed at an intensity of 6-9 on the rating of perceived exertion (RPE) scale, corresponding to the Borg 20 scale. The main Pilates session was performed for a total of 30 min and consisted of the following exercises: abdominal/roll down (10 reps × 3 sets, 5 min), bridging (5 reps × 3 sets, 5 min), triceps (8 reps × 3 sets, 4 min), squat/lunge (20 reps × 6 sets, 10 min), and low/hug a tree (10 reps × 3 sets, 6 min). This was performed at an intensity of 9-16 on the RPE scale, with progressive loading applied as follows: RPE 9-11 in weeks 1 and 2, RPE 11-13 in weeks 3-5, and RPE 13-16. The cool-down consisted of dead bugs (6 reps × 3 sets, 3 min), mat cat (5 s × 3 sets, 3 min), mermaid (4 reps × 3 sets, 2 min), and stretching (4 reps × 3 sets, 2 min) for 10 min at an intensity of 4-9 of the RPE scale. All exercises were provided with a 30-sec rest period between movements. The PPG performed the sequence using an elastic band and gym ball, whereas the APG used a reformer and cadillac. To ensure that the participants performed the exercises at the prescribed intensity and with the proper technique, their RPE was continuously monitored during each session. All sessions were supervised by trained exercise specialists who provided real-time instructions and feedback to ensure accurate execution of the exercise protocols and movements.

All participants in both groups performed the exercises for 50 min per session, three times a week for 8 weeks, and changes in body composition, blood pressure, mechanical properties of core muscles, mental health, and physical fitness before and after the intervention were analyzed in postmenopausal women. All interventions and measurements were performed under the same environmental conditions (22 ± 2°C and 50 ± 5%).

Measurements

Body composition and anthropometry parameters—height, weight, BMI, lean body mass, fat mass, percent body fat, waist circumference (WC), hip circumference (HC), and waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) —were measured after fasting for more than 8 h and refraining from strenuous physical activity for 48 h the day before the test. Measurements were obtained using a stadiometer (BSM 330, InBody, Seoul, Korea), bioelectrical impedance analysis device (Inbody 770; Inbody, Seoul, Korea), and anthropometric measuring tape (Balzer 80206F, Hoechstmass, Sulzbach, Germany).

Blood pressure was measured using a manual electronic sphygmomanometer (UM-102; A&D Medical, Tokyo, Japan). The pulse, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), mean arterial pressure (MAP), and pulse pressure (PP) were calculated. The following formulas were used: MAP (mmHg) = 1/3 × SBP (mmHg) + 2/3 × DBP (mmHg); PP (mmHg) = SBP (mmHg) - DBP (mmHg).

The mechanical properties of the core muscles (muscle tone and stiffness) were measured using Myoton PRO (Myoton AS, Tallinn, Estonia), a contact-type soft tissue-measuring device with a built-in 3-axis digital acceleration sensor. Myoton PRO is a device whose reliability and validity have been proven for muscle tone evaluation [25]. The muscle tone and stiffness of the rectus abdominis, external oblique, and erector spinae muscles were measured before and after the intervention using this device.

Mental health was assessed using the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) and Beck Depression Inventory (BDI). The STAI score used in the present study has been used clinically to measure the anxiety state of healthy adults without mental disorders [26]. The STAI consists of 20 questions assessing the respondent’s feelings and emotions or the individual’s innate emotional anxiety characteristics, with responses presented on a 4-point scale. The results are structured such that a minimum score of 20 points and a maximum score of 80 points could be obtained. An experiment was conducted using the BDI to evaluate depression levels [27]. The BDI consists of 21 questions covering the cognitive, emotional, motivational, and physical symptom domains of depression. The criteria for the BDI score are as follows: a score of 0-10 was considered normal, a score of 11-16 indicates a mild mood disorder, a score of 17-20 indicates clinical borderline depressive disorder, a score of 21-30 indicates moderate depression, a score of 31-40 indicates severe depression, and a score of 40 indicates severe depression.

Physical fitness was measured using health-related physical fitness parameters, including muscle strength, muscular endurance, flexibility, balance, and cardiopulmonary endurance. Muscle strength was measured using a grip dynamometer (T.K.K. 5401, TAKEI, Tokyo, Japan) to measure grip strength (kg) on the dominant side. Muscular endurance was measured in sit-ups (n). Flexibility was measured in terms of sit and reach (cm) using a sit-and-reach tester (T.K.K. 5111; Takei, Tokyo, Japan). Balance was measured through a one-leg stand with the eyes closed (sec). Cardiorespiratory endurance was measured using the McCardle protocol for the Queens College Step Test, in which participants performed 22 steps/min for 3 min. After the 3-min test, heart rate (HR) was measured for 15 s after 5 s, and maximal oxygen uptake (VO2max) was calculated using the following formula: VO2max = 65.810 - (0.1847 x HR).

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were performed using SPSS version 29.0 (SPSS institute, IBM corp, Armonk, NY, USA) and data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. The normality of the distribution of all outcome parameters was verified using the Shapiro-Wilk test prior to the parametric tests. To verify the differences between PPG and APG, a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with repeated measures was used to assess the presence of interactions or main effects within the intervention. Partial eta-squared (ηp²) values were calculated as measures of effect size for the analysis of variance. Effect sizes were expressed as partial eta squared (ηp2) and interpreted as small (≈ 0.01), moderate (≈ 0.06), or large (≈ 0.14). When ANOVA revealed a significant interaction or main effect within the intervention, a Bonferroni post-hoc test and paired t-tests were used to identify within-time differences. The level of significance was set a priori at p < 0.05.

DISCUSSION

The present study hypothesized that an 8-week Pilates program may improve body composition, blood pressure, core muscle mechanical properties, mental health, and physical fitness, and that there would be differences in effectiveness between prop Pilates and apparatus Pilates. Consistent with this hypothesis, additional time-related changes were evident in the apparatus Pilates group, particularly for blood pressure, lumbar erector spinae muscle tone, and grip strength.; however, other parameters (the mechanical properties of core muscles, mental health, and physical fitness) showed similar positive effects after the 8-week Pilates intervention in both groups.

Previous studies have reported various results related to changes in body composition based on physiological or clinical conditions following Pilates. Segal et al. [28] reported no changes in body composition after mat Pilates for 2 months in 47 healthy adults. Through a systematic review, Aladro-Gonzalvo et al. [29] verified the effects of Pilates on body composition based on seven studies included in the criteria. However, they reported that it was difficult to accurately determine the effectiveness of Pilates on body composition due to methodological flaws, lack of standardization of the study design and measurement techniques, and lack of control over nutritional status. In contrast, Jung et al. [30] reported that a 12-week mat Pilates program using tubing bands was effective in reducing fat mass and percent body fat in middle-aged obese women. Adigüzel et al. [31] reported that a 10-week combination of Pilates and breathing exercises was effective in improving weight, BMI, and percent body fat in adult women with a normal BMI. In the present study, no significant changes in body composition were observed in either the PPG or the APG, and this is thought to be mainly due to the fact that the postmenopausal women who participated in this study had a relatively high body fat percentage but normal weight based on BMI, and the 8-week Pilates period was relatively short to induce changes in body composition. In future studies, it will be necessary to examine the effectiveness of prop or apparatus Pilates over a longer period, including the control of dietary conditions and daily activity levels.

Several previous studies have investigated the effects of various Pilates modalities on improving blood pressure. Park et al. [24] conducted a clinical trial to examine the effects of mat Pilates with a control group (n = 10), online Pilates group (n = 10), and face-to-face Pilates group (n = 10) on blood pressure and cardiometabolic indices in middle-aged obese women. They reported that no effect was observed on blood pressure and argued that this was because the study participants had normal blood pressure. In contrast, Wong et al. [32] randomly assigned 28 young and obese women with elevated blood pressure to a control group (n = 14) or a Pilates group (n = 14) and examined the effects of a 12-week mat Pilates program on blood pressure and vascular function. The results showed that Pilates was effective in improving systolic blood pressure and arterial stiffness, suggesting that it is an effective exercise modality for early blood pressure management. González-Devesa et al. [33] conducted a systematic review of four randomized clinical trials and seven comparative studies to examine the effects of Pilates on blood pressure in hypertensive patients. As a result, participants who completed the Pilates program reported significant reductions in SBP and DBP, with values of -4.76 mmHg and -3.43 mmHg, respectively. Based on this, the authors explained that Pilates is safe for patients with hypertension and can be part of their rehabilitation. In the present study, participants had blood pressure levels corresponding to elevated blood pressure or stage 1 hypertension. While no statistically significant within-group changes were observed in the PPG, the APG showed significant reductions in SBP (p = 0.008, ηp2 = 0.304), DBP (p = 0.013, ηp2 = 0.272), and MAP (p = 0.001, ηp2 = 0.412); although the group × time interactions were not significant, significant main effects of time were observed for these outcomes, indicating meaningful decreases over the intervention period. However, considering that the change rates of SBP (-3.58% vs. -4.46%), DBP (-4.38% vs. -4.78%), and MAP (-4.63% vs. -4.03%) after 8 weeks in the PPG and APG were similar, the difference between the two Pilates modalities was not considered significant. In the future, it will be necessary to examine the differences in the effectiveness of prop and apparatus Pilates in improving blood pressure through the analysis of biomarkers such as arterial stiffness, vascular endothelial cell response, and nitric oxide in addition to blood pressure.

Although various exercise programs are used to relieve and relax muscle tension caused by aging and unbalanced posture, Pilates is widely used to improve core muscle stability and flexibility [24]. Park et al. [24] examined the effects of online or face-to-face mat Pilates on the mechanical properties of core muscles such as the rectus abdominis, external oblique, and erector spinae in 30 middle-aged obese women and reported that Pilates was effective in improving muscle tone and stiffness in all core muscles. Huang and Park [34] argued that Pilates is effective in improving pain and disability in patients with chronic low back pain by increasing core muscle mechanical stability, based on a systematic analysis and meta-analysis. Conversely, although it was not an intervention study, Wawryszewicz & Targosiński [35] conducted a comparative study on the activity of the transverse abdominis muscle and lumbar stability between 63 adults who had performed Pilates exercises within the past 6 months and a control group and reported that no differences were found between the two groups. In the present study, the PPG showed a significant decrease in external oblique muscle tone, whereas the APG showed a significant decrease in external oblique muscle tone and erector spinae muscle tone. Notably, lumbar erector spinae muscle tone demonstrated a significant group × time interaction (p = 0.022, ηp2 = 0.235), indicating a moderate effect. In addition, external oblique muscle tone showed a significant main effect of time (p = 0.001, ηp2 = 0.415), representing a large time-related change across the intervention. These findings may be explained by the fact that, although both Pilates methods used in this study are relatively effective in improving core muscle tone, the Pilates apparatus can provide a stronger stimulus to the core by maintaining more precise movements. Further research is needed to clearly examine the mechanism by which this occurs through analysis of core muscle activity when performing prop Pilates or apparatus Pilates.

Pilates is recommended not only for improving core muscle stability and physical fitness, but also for improving stress, depression, and anxiety disorders [22]. Rodríguez-Fuent et al. [36] reported that a 12-week Pilates program improved quality of life and mental health in postmenopausal women, and Park et al. [24] reported that a 12-week online or face-to-face mat Pilates program improved autonomic nervous system (ANS) function and reduced anxiety and depressive symptoms in middle-aged obese women. Fleming and Herrin [22] conducted a meta-analysis to examine the effects of Pilates on mental health outcomes related to anxiety and depressive symptoms and confirmed that Pilates significantly improved depressive symptoms, anxiety symptoms, and fatigue. Ju et al. [37] verified the effects of Pilates on female patients with depression and anxiety symptoms through a systematic analysis and meta-analysis of 18 randomized clinical trials, and reported that Pilates improved depression and anxiety levels. In the present study, the PPG showed a significant decrease in STAI and BDI, while the APG showed a significant decrease in BDI. However, considering that the PPG had a relatively high STAI in the pre-intervention period, both Pilates modalities demonstrated similar improvements in mental health. Nevertheless, given the baseline imbalance, the greater reductions observed in the PPG may partly reflect regression to the mean and/or greater room for improvement; therefore, mental health outcomes should be interpreted cautiously. To more accurately determine the effects of prop or apparatus Pilates on mental health, a larger study involving a larger sample of postmenopausal women with impaired ANS function and clinically elevated levels of depression and anxiety symptoms is needed to more clearly elucidate the impact of Pilates on mental health.

Pilates is an effective exercise modality for improving various physical fitness parameters in individuals with different characteristics. Jang et al. [16] reported that a 12-week Pilates exercise program was effective for improving grip strength, sit-ups, sit-and-reach, and VO2max in middle-aged women. Amin et al. [38] reported that Pilates improved core muscle endurance and trunk balance in adults aged 19-25 years. Su et al. [39] divided 45 middle-aged women who had not exercised regularly for the past 3 months into a control group (n = 23) and a Pilates group (n = 22) and treated them with a 12-week mat Pilates program. The authors reported that functional fitness factors, such as flexibility (+3.5%), core strength (+31.5%), lower extremity strength (+13.5%), agility (+7.3%), and balance (+4.2%), improved in the Pilates group compared to the control group. In addition, Mueller et al. [40] examined the effects of mat Pilates or apparatus Pilates on the Senior Fitness Test in older women (65.15 ± 3.0 years) and reported that both modalities improved upper and lower extremity muscle strength, aerobic endurance, upper and lower extremity flexibility, and agility, but no significant differences were observed between both. In the present study, the PPG showed significant improvement in sit-ups, sit-and-reach, and one-leg-stand with eyes closed, whereas the APG showed significant improvement in grip strength, sit-ups, sit-and-reach, and one-leg-stand with eyes closed. In the case of grip strength, a significant improvement was observed only in the APG, which is believed to be because the PPG had a significantly higher grip strength than the APG before the intervention. Interestingly, in our study, the 8-week Pilates program did not improve VO2max, an indicator of cardiorespiratory endurance, in either the PPG or the APG. In this regard, Jung et al. [30] reported that a 12-week Pilates program using tubing bands did not improve VO2max in obese middle-aged women because the exercise intensity was too low (50% of the maximum heart rate). Additionally, Park et al. [24] reported that they did not confirm an improvement in VO2max after a 12-week online or face-to-face Pilates. These results suggest that the two forms of Pilates applied in the present study do not correspond to traditional aerobic exercise modalities, and that the exercise intensity is lower than the anaerobic threshold level that can induce improvement in cardiopulmonary endurance. The present study has several limitations. First, the lack of a non-exercise control group limited the causal inference and could have contributed to the overestimation of the intervention effects. Second, given the relatively small sample size, the present study should be interpreted as preliminary/exploratory, and may have been underpowered to detect modest group × time interactions. Third, lifestyle factors such as physical activity, dietary intake, and medication use were not objectively monitored during the intervention period, which may have influenced the outcomes. Finally, VO2max was estimated using the Queens College step test rather than being directly measured, which may introduce estimation errors, particularly in older or postmenopausal populations.

In conclusion, the present study confirmed that 8 weeks of prop Pilates or apparatus Pilates were effective in improving the mechanical properties of the core muscles, mental health, and physical fitness of postmenopausal women. In addition, additional time-related improvements were observed in the Pilates group, particularly in blood pressure, lumbar erector spinae muscle tone, and grip strength.
