Brain injury reactivates a developmental program driving genesis and integration of transient LGE-class interneurons
Authors: G Nato, M Fogli, N Marichal, V Cerrato, G Turrini, V Proserpio, I Ghia, G Zanotto, I Molineris, M Bergami, S Oliviero, P Peretto, B Berninger, A Buffo, F Luzzati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBrain lesions can unlock latent neurogenic potential in parenchymal astrocytes, but the identity of their neuronal progeny has remained unclear. Here, we show that neurons generated by striatal astrocytes after excitotoxic lesions are transient, yet reach advanced morphological and functional maturation and integrate into cortico-striatal-thalamic circuits. Single-cell RNA-seq mapping onto an embryonic reference revealed these cells are not fated to adult striatal neuron types but belong to the LGE-MEIS2/PAX6 interneuron class. Reanalysis of neuroblasts from cortical and striatal astrocytes after Notch abrogation revealed shared commitment of rostral telencephalic astrocytes to this class. Public spatial transcriptomics datasets revealed these cells are widely distributed throughout the mouse telencephalon during embryonic and postnatal development. Thus, unlike other vertebrates in which adult telencephalic astroglia preserve the potential to generate resident, regionally appropriate neuronal types, homologous mammalian cells converge on a specific transient neuron class, possibly representing a reservoir for circuit plasticity in adult life.Keywords: reactive neurogenesis, circuit integration, dormant stem cells, cell fate specification, lateral ganglionic eminence interneurons, cortico-striatal-thalamic circuits, neuronal identity, neurorepair, astrocyte neural stem cell potential, postnatal development

Introduction

The extraordinary neuronal diversity of the adult brain originates during development from a periventricular germinal layer subdivided into committed progenitor domains (Kriegstein and Alvarez-Buylla 2009). In most adult mammals, astroglial-like cells derived from several telencephalic domains remain neurogenic in two specialized niches: the sub granular zone (SGZ) and the subventricular zone (SVZ) (Obernier and Alvarez-Buylla 2019; Bond et al., 2021). Like their embryonic ancestors, SGZ progenitors in the hippocampus generate dentate gyrus granule cells. By contrast, in the SVZ, the descendants of both cortical and subcortical progenitors converge toward lateral ganglionic eminence (LGE)-class interneurons that differentiate into distinct OB interneurons subtypes (Merkle et al., 2007; Schmitz et al., 2022). Whether other neuron types can be produced in the adult brain remains unclear. Under specific conditions new neurons have been described outside the canonical niches, particularly in the striatum and to a lesser extent in the cortex (Fogli et al., 2026). In the striatum, neurogenesis occurs physiologically in pre-pubertal guinea pigs (Luzzati et al., 2014), adult rabbits (Luzzati et al., 2006), monkeys (Bédard et al., 2002) and humans (Ernst et al., 2014), and under neurodegeneration in mice and rats (Arvidsson et al., 2002; Luzzati et al., 2011a; Nato et al., 2015). These neurons may derive from the SVZ (Liu et al., 2009; Luzzati et al., 2011a; Gordon et al., 2007), but are also consistently generated locally (Luzzati et al., 2006; Luzzati et al., 2011a; Nato et al., 2015; Magnusson et al., 2014), in some cases such as the rabbit caudate nucleus or the mice quinolinic acid (QA)-lesioned striatum, almost exclusively. Indeed, brain lesions can unlock a latent neurogenic potential in cortical and striatal astrocytes (Buffo et al., 2008; Luzzati et al., 2014; Magnusson et al., 2014; Nato et al., 2015). At least for striatal astrocytes the prevalence of neurogenic potential is comparable to that observed in SVZ astrocytes, potentially encompassing the entire population (Fogli et al., 2024). Despite this widespread potential and robust neurogenic responses, the damaged brain fails to regenerate, raising the question of where parenchymal neurogenesis deviates from normal development. In both the neocortex and striatum lesion-induced neurogenesis exerts beneficial roles after stroke (Butti et al., 2012; Liang et al., 2019; Jin et al., 2010), however the identity and integration capacity of the newborn neurons is still unknown.

In the striatum, the generation of medium spiny neurons (MSNs), the principal striatal cell type, was initially proposed (Arvidsson et al., 2002), but not confirmed subsequently (Liu et al., 2009; Luzzati et al., 2011a; Magnusson et al., 2014). By contrast striatal neuroblasts (STRs) have been shown to express the transcription factor Sp8 and the 5HT3A receptor (Luzzati et al., 2011a; Luzzati et al., 2014; Liu et al., 2009; García-González et al., 2020), typical of multiple LGE- and caudal GE (CGE)-class interneurons (Ma et al., 2012; Frazer et al., 2017) which were not reported to contribute to the striatum (Marin et al., 2000). However, the 5HT3A receptor is expressed by numerous medial GE (MGE)-derived striatal interneurons (Muñoz-Manchado et al., 2018), highlighting the need for a more comprehensive molecular profile to ascertain neuronal identity. Moreover, in all models of parenchymal neurogenesis analyzed so far, in physiological or pathological conditions, most if not all newborn neurons are short-lived (Arvidsson et al., 2002; Luzzati et al. 2006, 2014; Luzzati et al., 2011a; Ohira et al., 2010; Gould et al., 2001). Whether this reflects a failure to integrate into pre-existing circuits remains to be determined. In canonical niches, newborn neurons maturation and survival critically depend on electrical activity and circuit integration, with about half of them undergoing negative selection (Petreanu and Alvarez-Buylla 2002; Lin et al., 2010). At the same time, during development, transient neuronal populations exist that integrate only temporarily to support circuit formation and refinement (Molnár et al., 2020), but whether such cells can re-emerge in adulthood is unknown.

Here, taking advantage of the neurogenic response elicited by QA-lesion, a model of Huntington-like excitotoxic striatal degeneration which develops in a highly reproducible spatiotemporal pattern (Fogli et al., 2024), we show that QA-induced (STRs-nbl) mature and integrate into pre-existing circuits. Their transcriptional profile does not match any adult striatal neuronal lineage, but instead corresponds to a GABAergic interneuron class that transiently populates multiple forebrain regions, including the striatum, during development. The adult brain parenchyma thus maintains a latent potential to generate a previously unrecognized facultative neuron type, possibly involved in circuit plasticity during development and in adult life.

Results

STR-nbl live transiently

As previously described (Fogli et al. 2024), QA-lesion induced a strong local neurogenic response in the striatum with numerous neuroblasts dispersing in the ventro-medial caudoputamen (Video S1). To unravel the identity and integration capacity of these neuroblasts, we first assessed their survival and maturation by administering BrdU at 4 weeks post-lesion (wpl), when neurogenesis is already active (Nato et al., 2015) (Figure 1A). At 14 days (14days) post-BrdU injection (n = 6) numerous BrdU+DCX+ cells were present in the striatum but their number dropped by about 80% at 35days (n = 5; Figures 1B and 1C; Figures S1A and S1B). By contrast, the number of BrdU+DCX-cells remained stable (Table S1) indicating a selective and marked loss of BrdU+DCX+ cells. BrdU+ cells never expressed DARPP-32, an MSN marker, at any time points, while at both 14days and 35days a few expressed NeuN, a marker of neuronal maturity and the interneuron markers nNOS and Calretinin (Figure 1D; Figures S1C and S1D; Table S1). However these cells were still largely expressing DCX at 35days (69% ± 3% of the cells; Figures 1E and S1C), indicating that the vast majority of STR-nbl do not acquire a mature striatal phenotype and die within 35days. The apoptotic death of at least part of the neuroblasts was confirmed by the presence of DCX+ cells positive for cleaved caspase-3 (Figure 1F). In conclusion, as in other models of striatal neurogenesis, the great majority of newborn neuroblasts live transiently and do not express characteristic striatal neuron markers while retaining DCX expression for their entire life.

STR-nbl can attain complex morphologies and show dendritic spines

To clarify the degree of maturation reached by STR-nbl and their potential identity, we performed high-resolution serial section 3D reconstructions (voxel size: 0.12 × 0.12 × 2μm) of 161 DCX+RFP+ cells, 14 days after DsRed-encoding retrovirus injection, before the main survival drop (n = 4; Video S2; Figure S1E). Hierarchical clustering based on basic dendritic features, identified four main groups (Clusters A-D; Figures 1G and 1H) spanning a continuum of increasing morphological complexity. Cluster A comprised simple, predominantly unipolar and short cells with low branching and spinosity, whereas Cluster D included highly elaborate neurons with long, branched dendrites and high spine density (Figure S1F). Clusters B and C displayed intermediate features. While some variation may reflect maturation stage, marked differences in overall morphologies suggest distinct subtypes and/or context-dependent constraints on dendritic organization (Figure 1H). Notably, the centroid of Cluster D cells was significantly shifted laterally, toward the lesion (Figure 1I; Table S1). As observed in other models (Luzzati et al., 2014; Luzzati et al., 2011a; Thanou et al., 2025), part of the reconstructed neuroblasts resided in the internal capsule fiber bundles, often entirely confined within them (IC; Figures S1G and S1H, Video S1). IC cells were enriched in cluster A (Figure S1F) and were generally simpler, yet often more complex than typical bipolar migrating cells, and displayed spine densities comparable to cells in gray matter (GM; Table S1). Interestingly, no STR-nbl showed distinguishable axons and most were clearly axonless. Overall, STR-nbl acquire complex and highly heterogeneous morphologies. The presence of spines, the white matter tropism, and absence of an axon clearly distinguish them from typical striatal interneurons.

STR-nbl functionally mature and integrate into pre-existing circuits

To assess the functional maturation and connectivity of STR-nbl, we conducted whole-cell patch-clamp recordings in acute slices at 5 wpl, 14 days after DsRed-encoding retrovirus injection. Labeled cells comprised both neurons and glia, which could be distinguished by their morphology and electrophysiological characteristics (Figure 1J). All recorded cells were located at a clear distance from the ventricular wall, in both the medial and lateral striatum.

In line with the variation in morphological complexity, STR-nbl displayed corresponding differences in functional maturation. Type 1 cells (n = 3) displayed the lowest membrane capacitance (Cm; Figure 1K), highest input resistance (IR; Figure 1L), consistent with a smaller surface area containing less ion channels and transporters (Figure 1N), and did not generate action potentials upon depolarizing current steps (Figure 1O). Type 2 cells (n = 6) had a higher Cm, and could fire a single spikelet when depolarized. Type 3 cells (n = 3) showed the largest Cm and were able to fire repetitive action potentials in response to depolarizing current steps (Figure 1O), thus representing the more mature cells.

To examine lesion-induced neuroblast integration, we recorded spontaneous Excitatory and Inhibitory Post Synaptic Currents (sEPSCs, sIPSCs) in voltage-clamp. While Type 1 cells lacked synaptic activity (Figures 1P and 1Q), 3/6 of Type 2 neuroblasts received sEPSC at a low frequency (Freq = 0.09 Hz; Figure 1P). All Type 3 cells (3/3) displayed sEPSC (Freq = 0.06 Hz) and 2 also exhibited sIPSC (Freq = 0.22 Hz; Figure 1Q). In total 6/12 recorded cells received excitatory inputs. Consistent with this, we detected the expression of the immediate-early gene c-FOS in a subset of DCX+ cells (5% ± 4%, n = 4; Figure S1I).

Overall, QA-lesion induced neuroblasts exhibit electrophysiological features typical of newborn neurons (Carleton et al., 2003). Despite their short lifespan, STR-nbl functionally integrate into brain circuits and preferentially receive excitatory inputs, which in the striatum typically derive from long-range afferents.

Newborn STR-nbl receive long-range afferents

To trace the origin of synaptic inputs to STR-nbl, we used a monosynaptic retrograde tracing method based on sequential injections of modified retro and rabies viruses (Deshpande et al., 2013; Bergami et al., 2015) (Figure 2A). Retrovirus encoding RFP, rabies glycoprotein G, and TVA receptor was injected 3 wpl, followed 10 days later by RABV-GFP, and a 4-day chase. Double-infected cells (RFP+GFP+) were “starter cells” (Figures 2A–2E) spreading RABV-GFP to first-order presynaptic afferents (RFP-GFP+; Figures 2F–2J). To target the entire neurogenic region, we varied the injection sites across specimens (Figures 2B and 2C; Figure S2; Table S2; n = 22). Only in 3 out of 22 specimens the starter cells were exclusively DCX+ STR-nbl (Figures 2B, 2C, 3A, and S2) while all others included SVZ DCX+ neuroblasts (SVZ-nbl; Figure 2D) and/or striatal DCX-cells (STR-glia; Figure 2E). Based on starter composition, specimens were divided into four groups: Glial-only (n = 3) and SVZ-only (n = 3) included exclusively STR-glia and SVZ-nbl, respectively. Animals containing STR-nbl were assigned to either the STR&SVZ-Mixed (n = 7) or STR-only (n = 8) groups, depending on the presence or absence of SVZ-nbl starter cells, respectively (Figures 2B, 2C, 3A, and 3B). STR-only specimens resulted from more lateral injections, had more STR-glia and fewer STR-nbl starter cells (Figures 3A and 3B; Figure S2). These STR-nbl starters were distributed more ventro-laterally (Figure 3C) and, accordingly, displayed longer and more branched dendrites (Figure 3D) and were enriched in morphological classes C and D (STRonly: 4C+5D/14 reconstructed cells; Mixed: 9C+5D/49 reconstructed cells, corresponding to 64% and 29% respectively; Table S1).

Presynaptic neurons were mainly in the neocortex and thalamus, the principal striatal inputs (Hunnicutt et al., 2016) (Figures 2F–2J, 3A, 3B; Figures S3, S4A, and S4B). Notably, only STR-nbl starters formed synaptic-like contacts with GFP+RFP- axons, often with varicosities typical of cortico-striatal inputs (Kincaid et al., 1998) (Figures 2B’’, 2C’’ and 2E). Accordingly, multiple linear regression showed that STR-nbl but not SVZ-nbl or STR-glia predicted presynaptic neuron counts (Figures S4C and S4D; Table S1). Interestingly, the number of presynaptic cells per STR-nbl starter, or connectivity ratio, was higher in STR-only than in STR&SVZ-Mixed specimens (Figure 3E; Table S1) suggesting that lateral cells may receive more inputs, possibly by virtue of their higher morphological complexity. Regression analysis confirmed this hypothesis, revealing that the centroid position of STR-nbl distribution was a strong predictor of presynaptic neuron numbers (Figure S4E; Table S1).

Collectively, these analyses indicate that STR-nbl starters receive long-distance glutamatergic inputs from the neocortex and thalamus that vary in strength in different striatal regions.

Cortical and thalamic innervation of STR-nbl

Cortico-striatal and thalamo-striatal projections are organized into multiple parallel circuits that are topographically organized in the striatum (Hunnicutt et al., 2016; Hintiryan et al., 2016; Lee et al., 2020). These connections divide the striatum in medial and lateral macro-domains involved in goal-directed and habitual behaviors (Kupferschmidt et al., 2017). Most inputs to STR-nbl originated from regions projecting to the medial striatum, where these cells mostly reside, such as the anterior cingulate cortex (ACA, in 11/15 specimens, in which they represent the 35% ± 20% of CX inputs) and secondary motor cortex (MOs, 11/15 specimens, 25% ± 17% of CX inputs; Figures 3F and 3G). By contrast, afferents of the lateral striatum, such as the somatosensory cortex (SS, 6/15 specimens, 11% ± 8% of CX inputs) or primary motor cortex (MOp, 7/15 specimens, 13% ± 7% of CX inputs), were less represented. The medio-lateral position of STR-nbl further correlated with the expected differential representation of areas projecting to the medial and lateral striatum (Figure 3H). Like typical cortico-striatal projections, these inputs originated mainly from layer 5, and to a lesser extent from layer 2/3 (Harris and Shepherd 2015) (Figures 3I and S4B).

Thalamic inputs were found in 10/15 specimens and were always less abundant than cortical inputs (Figures 3G and 3H, S3 and S4). Interestingly, the two major thalamic afferents of striatal MSNs, Pf and VM nuclei (Hunnicutt et al., 2016; Mandelbaum et al., 2019), had limited projections to STR-nbl (in 3/10 specimens for both nuclei, 33% ± 24% and 42% ± 42% of thalamic inputs respectively) that rather received mostly from the MD (8/10 of the specimens, 52% ± 28% of thalamic inputs; Figure 3H), a nucleus strongly interconnected with the prefrontal cortex with which it shares also dense projections to the medial striatum (Hunnicutt et al., 2016).

Collectively, these results indicate that STR-nbl receive afferents that are consistent with their medial position, namely the medial-cortical subnetwork (Hintiryan et al., 2016) and its associated thalamic nuclei.

Transcriptional maturation of parenchymal astrocyte-derived neuroblasts

To better characterize the identity and maturation trajectory of lesion-induced neurons, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) of PSA-NCAM+ neuroblasts lineage-traced from adult Glast+ astrocytes and isolated from QA-lesioned striatum (QA-STR) at 5 wpl. Neuroblasts from the contralateral olfactory bulb (OB) were used as reference (Figures 4A and S5A).

Among sequenced cells, 53 QA-STR and 46 OB cells expressed markers of immature GABAergic neurons (Gad1, Gad2, Slc32a1) while lacking mature neuronal and glial signatures (Figures S5B and S5C). QA-STR and OB neuroblasts clustered together with minimal transcriptional differences, indicating similar lineage identity and maturation states (Figure S5D; Data S1). Label transfer onto a reference SVZ scRNA-seq dataset (Cebrian-Silla et al., 2021) showed that these cells were largely post-mitotic (Figures 4B–4D). QA-STR cells included five proliferating neuroblasts (cluster 15) and were enriched in early post-mitotic states (cluster 6) relative to OB.

To more broadly assess the neuronal fate potential of parenchymal astrocytes, we integrated these data with publicly available scRNA-seq datasets of neurons generated from cortical and striatal astrocytes after conditional Rbpj deletion (Rbpj-CX, Rbpj-STR; (Magnusson et al., 2020; Zamboni et al., 2020). Label transfer onto the SVZ dataset (Figures 4B–4D) indicated that these cells were also largely post-mitotic but enriched in early post-mitotic states (cluster 6), with Rbpj-CX cells representing the least mature population, in line with their isolation shortly after neurogenic activation (see also Method Details).

In the integrated dataset, 1090 cells aligned along a pseudotime consistent with SVZ-predicted identities (Figures 4E and 4F). To assess functional maturation, we computed UCell scores (Andreatta and Carmona 2021) for 11 GO terms related to neuronal communication and clustered cells accordingly (Figures 4G and 4H; Data S2). Clusters arranged along a maturation axis consistent with pseudotime and SVZ identities (Figures 4G, 4I and 4L), revealing sequential activation of functional programs. Presynaptic and synaptic modules emerged early, whereas intrinsic excitability programs were restricted to advanced states. Accordingly, the most mature cluster showed higher scores for action potential membrane depolarization (Figure 4H; Table S1) and accounted for ∼25% of QA-STR cells (Figure 4M), matching electrophysiological estimates. Rbpj-STR and Rbpj-CX neuroblasts showed more limited functional maturation.

Functional progression was accompanied by the increased expression of gene modules linked to neuronal structural maturation, including mitochondrial organization, dendritic spine components and ERBB4 signaling (Figures 4G and 4H). While the overall score for a curated set of structural plasticity genes decreased, selected microtubule-associated genes (e.g., Dcx, Stmn2, Nav3) increased in advanced clusters (Figure 4I), consistent with coordinated morphological and functional maturation.

Astrocyte-generated striatal and cortical neuroblasts belong to the LGE interneurons class

While GABAergic neuron diversity emerges with maturation, major classes can already be distinguished at early post-mitotic stages (Mayer et al., 2018; Schmitz et al., 2022). To determine the lineage identity of parenchymal astrocyte-derived neuroblasts, we performed label transfer onto an E18 reference dataset of cortical and subcortical GABAergic interneurons (Mayer-E18; Figures 5A–5C, S5E, and S5F). Beside proliferating or early post-mitotic cells, more mature QA-STR, OB and Rbpj-derived cells mapped to LGE_MEIS2/PAX6 class interneurons (Figures 5B and 5C). A CGE_NR2F2/PROX1 fraction (15%–17%) was observed in QA-STR and OB, but were rare in Rbpj datasets. Many STR-QA and OB cells mapped to the subclass of LGE-OB_MEIS2/PAX6 subclass, defined based on adult immature OB interneurons (Schmitz et al., 2022) and likely representing more mature states (Figures 5C and S5L). Class assignments were confirmed by marker expression (Figures S5G, S5H, and S6I).

Immunohistochemistry confirmed that lesion-induced striatal DCX+ neuroblasts expressed LGE markers (CTIP2, MEIS2) and interneuron markers (ERBB4, Sp8; Figures 5D, 5E and 5G), while lacking MGE interneurons (LHX6, NKX2.1; Figures S6A, S6B, and S6F), or STR projection neuron markers (FOXP2, DARPP32; Figures S6C, S6D and S6F) supporting their identity as LGE_MEIS2/PAX6 interneurons. CGE assigned cells expressed Prox1 but lacked specific CGE markers such as Nr2f2 (Figures S6E–S6G), suggesting an intermediate phenotype as shown in human neonatal SVZ (Kim et al., 2025).

In the adult OB the LGE_MEIS2/PAX6 class include multiple fate-restricted subtypes (Schmitz et al., 2022). Although poorly resolved transcriptionally at immature stages, the adult SVZ dataset (Cebrian-Silla et al., 2021) distinguishes dorsal and ventral lineages, which among more mature stages segregate into distinct clusters. Label transfer indicated that OB, Rbpj-STR and QA-induced neuroblasts included both identities, with QA-STR biased toward ventral lineages (cluster 1 and 4; Figure 5F; Table S1). By contrast, mature Rbpj-CX neuroblasts were exclusively assigned to the dorsal lineage (cluster 0; Figure 5F) and accordingly broadly expressed Pax6 (Figure S6E).

Notably, this fate restriction parallels that of SVZ astrocytes derived from cortical progenitors. (Merkle et al., 2007). Cells attributed to SVZ mature clusters expressed the main markers of these clusters (Cebrian-Silla et al., 2021)(Figures S6J and S6K).

Immunohistochemistry confirmed that a small fraction of QA-induced neuroblasts in the STR expressed the dorsal marker PAX6 (∼2%), without any evident spatial pattern (Figure S6H). While some cells expressed the mRNA of the ventral marker Foxp2, we could not detect the protein (Figures S6C and S6I). The CGE attributed cells did not show any lineage preference and PROX1 protein, that was expressed in about 20% of the DCX+ cells, did not show a spatial pattern (Figures S6F and S6G).

In conclusion, the neurogenic potential of rostral telencephalic astrocytes, in both parenchyma and SVZ, converges on the generation of LGE_MEIS2/PAX6 interneurons possibly with a shared sub lineage commitment determined by embryonic origin.

LGE-class interneurons transiently populate the embryonic and postnatal forebrain

OB interneurons are the only adult neurons predicted to derive from LGE-MEIS2/PAX6 progenitors (Schmitz et al., 2022), yet why parenchymal astrocytes retain the potential to generate this class remains unclear. The circuit integration of STRs suggests that, as for developmental transient neurons, their limited lifespan may be intrinsically regulated. LGE-derived cells have been described in the SVZ and intermediate zone (IZ) of the developing cortex (Anderson et al., 2001) and identified by single-cell RNA-seq in the developing cortical white matter and striatum of primates, although their fate remains unresolved (Schmitz et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2025). Consistently, a fraction of Mayer-E18 LGE_MEIS2/PAX6 cells were originally isolated from the cortex (Figures 5A and S5I). To define their spatial distribution during mouse development, we projected MGE-, CGE- and LGE-class neurons from the E18-reference dataset (Mayer et al., 2018) onto public spatial transcriptomic datasets from E16.5 (Chen et al., 2022) and P7 (Joglekar et al., 2021) brain sections (Figures 5H and 6A; Figures S7A and S7B; Data S3). As expected, cortical CGE- and MGE-derived interneurons localized to cortical plate and marginal zone while avoiding the striatum (Villar-Cerviño et al., 2015; Marín and Rubenstein 2001). By contrast, LGE-MEIS2/PAX6 interneurons were found both in the cortex, mostly SVZ and IZ at E16.5 and corpus callosum at P7, as well as the striatum.

To validate their presence in the postnatal striatum, we identified Sp8 as a specific marker of LGE-class interneurons in that region, as it is not expressed by striatal projection neurons or interneurons (Schmitz et al., 2022; Muñoz-Manchado et al., 2018) (Figure S5H). In line with spatial transcriptomics observations, at P10 Sp8+ cells were abundant in the corpus callosum and to a lesser extent in the striatum. A few of these cells showed oligodendroglia identity (SOX10+; Figures 6B and 6C), while they were always negative for astrocytic and microglial markers (Figures S7F and S7G). At P28, numerous DCX+Sp8+ cells populated the medial striatum (Figure 6F). At this age, the striatal DCX+ cells resembled morphologically the QA-induced neuroblasts and expressed ERBB4, MEIS2 and CTIP2 in similar proportions (Figures S7C and S7D). Both the P10 SOX10-Sp8+ and the P28 DCX+Sp8+ cells almost disappeared at P90 (Figures 6D, 6E, and 6G–6I; Table S1), confirming the transient presence of immature LGE-class interneurons in the postnatal mouse striatum. Interestingly, in humans Sp8 expression have transient peaks in the prefrontal cortex and the striatum during development (Figure S7H). Overall, these data indicate that lesion-induced STRs correspond to a conserved class of interneurons transiently populating the forebrain during development.

Discussion

Here, we demonstrate that cortical and striatal astrocytes share with their SVZ counterparts the potential to generate LGE_MEIS/PAX6-class interneurons. In addition to OB interneurons, during development this class includes neurons that transiently populate several brain regions including the striatum. These cells can thus be viewed as a facultative cell type that is generated only in specific conditions. Following striatal injury, they re-emerge, mature and transiently integrate into pre-existing circuits.

Neuroblast maturation and heterogeneity

STR-nbl at approximately 14days of age showed considerable variability in shapes and morphological complexity. This heterogeneity can be at least partly explained by maturation differences. Indeed, simpler Type 1 and Type 2 STR-nbl displayed electrophysiological features typical of early and intermediate maturation stages, particularly of SVZ neuroblasts, including reduced membrane capacitance and increased input resistance (Carleton et al., 2003). However, Type 2 cells further showed fast inward currents and spikelet firing, consistent with the presence of voltage-gated Na+ and Ca2+ channels, while Type 3 STR-nbl were capable of firing repetitive action potentials. Transcriptional analyses were consistent with this progression, revealing a gradual activation of gene programs linked first to synaptic responsiveness and later to action potential generation. Of note, Type 2 and Type 3-like features are developed relatively later in SVZ neuroblasts, only after 2 and 3 weeks, respectively (Carleton et al., 2003). STR-nbl faster maturation may represent a specific feature of this population, but could also result from environmental factors such as neural activity, a well-known driver of neuronal maturation (Wallace and Pollen 2024; Lin et al., 2010; Petreanu and Alvarez-Buylla 2002). In line with this interpretation the QA-induced neuroblasts in the ventral and lateral striatum, where resident neurons were largely depleted, displayed longer dendrites and received more synaptic inputs, possibly reflecting a competitive advantage that fosters maturation and integration. Beside maturation differences, transcriptional analyses revealed the presence of subtypes among striatal LGE interneurons that, as for newborn OB interneurons, could be regulated by intrinsic factors (Cebrian-Silla et al., 2021). While more integrated morphological, functional and transcriptional techniques will be required to identify specific subtypes, our results suggest that both intrinsic and local contextual factors shape the huge morphological and functional variability of STR-nbl.

Transient LGE-class interneurons during development

Despite their integration into striatal circuits, most QA-induced STR-nbl live only a few weeks. This limited survival is consistently observed across models of parenchymal neurogenesis, in both physiological and pathological conditions, and contrasts with the long-term integration of transplanted embryonic precursors (Luzzati et al., 2011a; Schellino et al., 2023). Overall, these observations strongly suggest that the short lifespan of STRs is governed by intrinsic mechanisms.

Although electrical activity typically promotes neuronal survival (Duan et al., 2020; Lin et al., 2010; Veyrac et al., 2013), specific neuronal populations are known to transiently integrate into circuits and be subsequently eliminated, such as Cajal–Retzius and subplate neurons during development (Elorriaga et al., 2023; Molnár et al., 2020), and pregnancy-induced interneurons in the adult OB (Chaker et al., 2023). In line with this concept, our in-depth transcriptional analysis supports the identification of STR-nbl as a previously unrecognized transient neuronal population. Both striatal and cortical astrocyte-derived neuroblasts showed a specific LGE_MEIS2/PAX6 class signature. This class is predicted to differentiate exclusively into OB interneurons (Schmitz et al., 2022) as supported also by in utero transplantation of early LGE cells (Wichterle et al., 2001) and BrdU injections at P1 (Ma et al., 2012), when the production of these cells peaks in the cortical SVZ (Zhang et al., 2020; Lee et al., 2024). However, here we showed that LGE_MEIS2/PAX6 cells are broadly distributed in the telencephalon of late embryonic and early postnatal mice and persist in the CC and medial striatum up to the weaning period. According to our observations, LGE_MEIS2/PAX6 cells have been isolated from the developing macaque cortex and striatum (Schmitz et al., 2022). In humans, similar cells, referred to as dorsal LGE immature neurons, were identified by merfish analysis in the cortical white matter, subplate and layer 6b (Wang et al., 2025). These cells are likely part of a late migratory stream of interneurons, particularly developed in gyrencephalic brains, that stem from the arc (Paredes et al., 2016; Kim et al., 2025; Schmitz et al., 2022) an accumulation of immature neuroblasts along the Muratoff bundle, a fiber tract running along the dorso-lateral corner of the LV and carrying numerous cortico-striatal fibers (Bullock et al., 2022). At later stages, they may include SVZ-derived neuroblasts associated with CC fibers connected with the prefrontal cortex in postnatal humans, mice, possibly dolphins and rabbits where neuronal migration continue up to young adult age (Sanai et al., 2011; Luzzati et al., 2003; 2006; Le Magueresse et al., 2011; La Rosa et al., 2018). As the LGE_MEIS2/PAX6 cells do not contribute to adult cortical or striatal cell types they likely represent a previously unrecognized class of transient neurons. This interpretation is further supported by their strong tropism for regions such as developing fiber tracts and the subplate, which are enriched in transient neurons during development (Niquille et al., 2013; Qu et al., 2016; Molnár et al., 2020). Of note, this neuron class may also transiently populate the LOT, where, unlike the CC, only transient glutamatergic neurons have been demonstrated so far (Ruiz-Reig et al., 2017; Tomioka et al., 2000).

A transient neuron class for circuit assembly and plasticity?

The role of LGE_MEIS2/PAX6 neurons during development remains to be established. In the corpus callosum, transient neurons has been shown to act as guideposts, attract growing axons and support their fasciculation (Niquille et al., 2013), while in the subplate, they establish intermediary connections with thalamic fibers that are thought to be important in proper wiring of the neocortex (Molnár et al., 2020). The strong increase of these cells in the dorsal white matter of gyrencephalic brains (centrum semiovale) suggests that they may be involved in the establishment of long-range cortical connectivity. Of note, an early LGE-derived population of corridor cells guide thalamic axons through the MGE (López-Bendito et al., 2006). Whether later striatal LGE interneurons still interact with cortical and thalamic fibers remains to be determined. However, our results suggest that, as in the cortex, the development of striatal circuits may also be supported by transient neurons. Like their developmental counterparts, adult generated striatal LGE interneurons show high tropism for white matter tracts carrying striatal and cortical fibers such as the IC (Luzzati et al., 2014; Thanou et al., 2025; Luzzati et al., 2011a). In addition, unlike adult-generated neuroblasts in canonical niches that initially integrate into local circuits (Panzanelli et al., 2009; Ming and Song 2011), STR-nbl received mainly long-range cortical and thalamic afferents. Whether in adults these cells play similar roles as during development remains to be determined. A peculiar and conserved characteristic of adult generated STR-nbl is that they retain an immature phenotype throughout their lifespan, rarely transitioning to a DCX-negative stage. Rather than a failure to complete maturation, this persistent immaturity may represent a defined functional state. Before becoming fully engaged in neural signal processing, developing neurons play structural and supportive roles that are essential for the maturation and organization of the nervous system (Cossart and Sonia, 2022). These cells exhibit heightened plasticity that, during development, supports circuit assembly and, in adults, may promote circuit remodeling, as observed in adult niches or after interneuron transplantation (Sailor et al., 2017; Dehorter et al., 2017). In this view, immaturity is not merely a transient stage but a specialized operational state, and selection may act to balance the distinct functional demands of these roles in mature circuits.

Brain lesions stimulate plasticity of both neurons and glial cells to support the rearrangement of surviving connections, often reactivating developmental-like mechanisms (Cramer and Chopp 2000; Jones and Adkins 2015). The striatal neurogenic response may thus act in concert with this remodeling process. Consistently, neurogenesis starts two weeks after the QA lesion (Nato et al., 2015; Fogli et al., 2024), in a putative recovery phase, and neuroblasts concentrate at the lesion border, where circuit reorganization is most active (Jones and Adkins 2015). Moreover, newborn neurons have been shown to support functional recovery after cortical injury (Liang et al., 2019). However, unlike development, the striatal neurogenic response persists for months (Nato et al., 2015; Thored et al., 2006) suggesting that these cells may either 1) they fail to fulfill their function, 2) support a prolonged remodeling process in the adult brain or 3) maintain a form of constitutive structural plasticity, analogous to that observed in canonical neurogenic niches. Consistent with this latter possibility, striatal neurogenesis is constitutive in rabbits and possibly in humans (Ernst et al., 2014; Luzzati et al., 2006). Notably, across different models, the spatial distribution of LGE_MEIS2/PAX6 neuroblasts varies markedly (Fogli et al., 2026), suggesting these cells can be selectively recruited to modulate specific circuits. The LGE_MEIS2/PAX6 neuroblasts may thus represent a specialized neuron class that can be deployed on demand to transiently modulate circuit architecture, supporting plasticity in a context- and species-dependent manner.

Concluding remarks

At least in the cortex, the fate switch of radial glial progenitors from glutamatergic neurons to LGE_class interneurons occurs at the beginning of astrogliogenesis (Zhang et al., 2020; Fuentealba et al., 2015; Lee et al., 2024), and new astrocytes may thus keep this genetic program accessible throughout life and reactivate it on demand. In non-mammalian vertebrates, adult astroglial cells maintain the neurogenic commitment of their embryonic ancestors, thereby supporting lifelong neuronal addition and regeneration (Jurisch-Yaksi et al., 2020; Fogli et al., 2026; Wei et al., 2022). Of note, in fish, OB-like interneurons are present in a region ventral to the striatum proper (Tibi et al., 2023) and are constitutively generated during adulthood (Rothenaigner et al., 2011), suggesting that this interneuron class may extend beyond the OB also in other species. The progenitors of these cells however are distinct from those of the striatal and pallial domains. The convergence of astroglial neurogenic potential from both pallial and subpallial origins onto transient LGE-MEIS2/PAX6-class interneurons may thus represent a specific mammalian adaptation. The functions and dysfunctions of this emerging population of transient neurons, during development and in adulthood, await to be clarified. These cells could be harnessed for brain repair, either by exploiting their plastic potential or by reprogramming their progenitors toward earlier developmental programs.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animal procedures

The experimental plan was designed according to the guidelines of the NIH, the European Communities Council (2010/63/EU), and the Italian Law for Care and Use of Experimental Animals (DL26/2014). It was also approved by the Italian Ministry of Health (authorization 327/2020-PR) and the Bioethical Committee of the University of Turin. The study was conducted according to the Arrive guidelines.

Mouse lines

Experiments were performed on 8–12 weeks old C57BL/6J mice and 8–12 weeks old GLAST-CreERT2 mice(Mori et al., 2006) crossed with R26R-YFP mice(Srinivas et al., 2001) to produce Glast CreERT2/R26R-YFP heterozygous for GLAST-CreERT2 and homozygous for R26R-YFP.

Method details

Stereotaxic injections

Mice were anesthetized with a solution containing ketamine (133 mg/kg; Ketavet 100; Gellini, Aprilia LT, Italy) and xylazine (6.7 mg/kg; Rompun 20 mg/mL; Bayer, Wuppertal, Germany), positioned in a stereotaxic apparatus, and injected into the right hemisphere using a picopump (Picospritzer II, General Valve Corporation). QA injection: 1 μL of quinolinic acid (QA) diluted to 120 mM in 0.1 M PB was injected +0.1 mm AP, −2.1 mm ML, and −2.6 mm DV. Retroviral injections for morphological analysis and electrophysiological recordings: double injections of CAG-IRES-DsRedExpress2 or CAG(GW)-Psd95∗YFP-IRES-dsRed retroviruses were performed at the following coordinates: +0.9 mm AP, −1.8 mm ML, and 3.2-3-4 mm DV, and +1.1 mm AP, −1.5 mm ML, and 3.0-3-2 mm DV.

Viral injections for monosynaptic retrograde tracing: Retrovirus: CAG-DsRedExpress2-2A-G-IRES2-TVA; Rabies viruses: EnvA-pseudotyped_SAD_ΔG-eGFP, EnvA-pseudotyped_SAD_ΔG-Gephyrin∗GFP; see Table S2 for injection coordinates.

BrdU pulse labeling

Mice received two intraperitoneal injections of 5-bromo-2-deoxyuridine (BrdU, Sigma-Aldrich; 50 mg/kg in 0.1 M Tris pH 7.4) at 4 weeks after QA injection, 6 h apart, and were sacrificed either 15 days (15days) or 35 days (35days) after the last injection.

Histology

Animals were anesthetized with a ketamine (266 mg/kg) and xylazine (20 mg/kg) solution and perfused with a solution of 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, Sigma-Aldrich) and 2% picric acid (AnalytiCals) in 0.1 M sodium phosphate buffer (PB) pH 7.4. Brains were then post-fixed for 5 h, cryoprotected in 30% sucrose (Sigma-Aldrich) in 0.1M PB pH 7.4, embedded at −80°C in Killik/OCT (Bio-Optica), and cryostat sectioned in series of 50 μm-thick coronal sections.

Immunofluorescence staining and image acquisitions

Sections were incubated for 48 h at 4°C in 0.01 M PBS pH 7.4 containing 2% Triton X-100, 1:100 normal donkey serum, and primary antibodies (Key Resource Table). For BrdU staining, sections were pre-incubated in 2M HCl for 30 min at 37°C and then rinsed in 0.1 M borate buffer pH 8.5. Sections were incubated overnight with appropriate secondary antibodies (Key Resource Table) and coverslipped with antifade mounting medium Mowiol (4–88 reagent, Calbiochem).

All the fluorescent images presented in this study were acquired on a Leica SP5 confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems) equipped with 40x and 63x objectives (HCX PL APO lambda blue: 40×, NA 1.25; 63x, NA 1.4). Most 40x magnification images were taken at a resolution of 0.38 μm/pixel with optical sectioning in Z every 1.5 μm, unless otherwise specified.

Electrophysiology

Mice were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane (Forane, Abbvie), decapitated and the brains were quickly collected into a chilled artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF; Composition in mM: NaCl, 85; Sucrose, 73; KCl, 2.5; NaHCO3, 25; CaCl2, 0.5; MgCl2, 7; NaH2PO4, 1.25 and glucose, 10; pH 7.4) saturated with 95% O2 and 5% CO2 (pH 7.4). Coronal brain slices (250 μm thick) containing the striatum were prepared using a vibratome (VT1200 S, Leica) and transferred to standard ACSF (Composition in mM: NaCl, 125; KCl, 2.5; NaHCO3, 25; CaCl2, 2; MgCl2, 1; NaH2PO4, 1.25 and glucose, 12; pH 7.4). Slices were incubated in standard ACSF at 34C for 1h followed by one additional hour at room temperature (21C ± 2°C). For recordings, individual slices were transferred into a recording chamber mounted on the stage of an upright Zeiss microscope (Axio Imager 2, Germany). Slices were constantly perfused at the rate of 1–2 mL/min with standard ACSF maintained at RT and saturated with 95% O2 and 5% CO2.

Cells were visualized by epifluorescence and infrared DIC videomicroscopy using a 40X (0.8 numerical aperture) water immersion objective. Patch-clamp whole-cell recordings were performed in a total of 24 cells using capillaries (5–10 MΩ) pulled from borosilicate glass (BF150-86-10, Sutter Instruments) in a horizontal puller (P-1000 Micropipette puller, Sutter Instruments) and filled with intracellular solution (Composition in mM: K-gluconate, 125; NaCl, 5; HEPES, 10; EGTA, 10; MgCl2, 2; ATP, 2; Biocytin, 5 at pH 7.4). Only cells in which an evident dsRed signal was observed within the pipet tip were considered. Voltage-clamp and current-clamp recordings were obtained using a Axopatch 200B amplifier (Molecular Devices), digitized (Digidata 1440A, Molecular Devices), and acquired and analyzed with pClamp 10 software (Molecular Devices).

In voltage clamp mode, voltage steps were applied from −70 mV holding potential to levels ranging from −100 to +60 mV in 10 mV steps. To subtract leak currents, we used a P4 protocol provided by Clampex10 that allowed simultaneous storage of raw and leak subtracted data.

In current clamp mode, passive and active membrane properties were recorded by applying a series of hyperpolarizing and depolarizing current steps (2-or 10-pA steps, 500 ms). The RMP was assessed immediately after break-in by reading the voltage value in the absence of current injection (I = 0 configuration). Input resistance (Rin) was calculated from the peak of the voltage response to a −20-pA, 500-ms current step according to Ohm’s law (Vhold = −70 mV). The membrane capacitance (Cm) was obtained with the following equation: membrane time constant (τm) = Rin · Cm. τm was derived from a single exponential fitted to the voltage response to −20-pA, 500-ms current step.

Neuroblast reconstruction for morphological analysis

Confocal microscopy serial section 3D reconstructions were performed as previously described (Fogli et al., 2024; Luzzati et al., 2011b). Briefly, confocal high-resolution images at the level of the striatum were acquired with a 63X objective (N.A. 1.4), at a resolution of 0.12 μm/pixel with optical sectioning in Z every 0.8 μm, to appreciate cellular details. The optical plane sequences of subsequent sections were aligned in TrakEM2. Cells that were entirely comprised in the reconstructed volume were traced with NeuTube (https://www.neutracing.com). During tracing, all the processes shorter than 3 μm were considered as spines and their traces were detached from the main branches. When possible, the border of the IC was identified based on background stain. Renderings were exported from Neutube. Unbiased hierarchical clustering analysis on the reconstructed cells was performed using the R package pheatmap. The same procedure was used to reconstruct the starter cells from selected rabies virus-injected animals.

Single-cell RNA sequencing

For single-cell sequencing analysis (scRNA-seq), QA was injected in GlastCreERT2 x R26R-YFP animals, and tamoxifen was administered 1 week before the lesion to select striatal astrocyte progeny (Nato et al., 2015; Fogli et al., 2024). Five weeks after lesion, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and immediately cutted using a 0.5mm Coronal Brain Matrix (Stainless Steel, Agnthos) in PBS-glucose medium. The peri-lesion part of the striatum was dissected under a Leica Stereomicroscope, ensuring to discard the SVZ. Four biological replicates containing three QA-lesioned striata each were prepared. As a control, we also collected one OB for each animal. The lesioned striatum and OB dissected tissues were then transferred to a 15 mL falcon tube containing HibernateA (Brainbits LLC, #HA). Tissue dissociation was performed with the Miltenyi Neural Tissue Dissociation Kit (130-093-231 T) and dissociated cells were labeled with a PSA-NCAM APC antibody. This surface glycoprotein is expressed by immature and migrating neurons of both the striatum and the OB. Cells that co-expressed both YFP and APC were subsequently FACS-sorted (Cell Sorter BD FACS Aria III) and subjected to SMArtSeq2 (Picelli et al., 2014). This technique is characterized by full-length coverage, high sensitivity (i.e., it detects a high gene number per cell), and high precision in transcript-level quantification. After mRNA conversion into cDNA, individual libraries were prepared by tagmentation using a transposase enzyme. Libraries were sequenced on Illumina NextSeq 500 System. Following quality controls (performed with FastQC v0.11.2 (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc), sequencing reads were processed with Trim Galore! v0.5.0 (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/trim_galore) to perform quality and adapter trimming (parameters:–stringency 3 –q 20). Trimmed reads were next aligned to the mouse reference genome (mm10/GRCm38 Ensembl release 84) using STAR v2.7.1a51 with options:–outFilterMultimapNmax 10–outFilterMultimapScoreRange 1 –outFilterMismatchNmax 999 –outFilterMismatchNoverLmax 0.04. Gene expression levels were quantified with featureCounts v1.6.1 (https://subread.sourceforge.net/, options: -t exon -g gene_name) using the GENCODE Release M23 annotation. Multi-mapped reads were excluded from quantification. Datasets have been deposited on GEO: GSE303816.

scRNA-seq quality control and clustering

Data analysis was performed in Seurat v4.2.1. Cells with less than 500 detected genes and more than 10% of mitochondrial DNA were filtered out. Two batches were merged using the default Seurat integration method based on Canonical correlation analysis (CCA) using default parameters. For integration, we used log-normalized TPM, and reduced the k.weight of the IntegrateData function to 49. After integration, we calculated PCA and clustered cells using the first 10 significant PCs as assessed by the JackStrawPlot function in Seurat. A group of microglial cells clustered separately and were thus excluded from further analyses. Fiducial markers of the main neural cell types confirmed that all the remaining cells corresponded to immature GABAergic neurons. After subsetting, we recalculated the PCA and clustered cells using significant PCs. The analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was performed using FindAllMarkers function on Log-transformed TPM, using the Wilcoxon Rank-Sum test, only genes with an average log2 fold change =>0.5 and expressed by at least 25% of the cluster cells were considered. Cell Cycle scores and NSCs developmental stage scores were calculated using the CellCycleScoring and AddModuleScore functions of Seurat, respectively. The gene lists defining the different NSCs developmental stages were obtained from (Kalamakis et al., 2019).

Public datasets

Mayer E18 dataset, including E18 cortical and subcortical GABAergic cells (Mayer et al., 2018) was obtained from GEO: GSE104156 and imported into Seurat. Cells were filtered and analyzed as described; the first 50 PCs were used for clustering and UMAP plot. Different clustering depths were considered and manually annotated. The neuron class annotations produced by Schmitz et al., (2022) were downloaded from https://dev-inhibitory-neurons.cells.ucsc.edu. Original annotation colors were derived from the original scripts at https://github.com/mtvector/dev-and-evo-of-primate-inhibitory-neurons/tree/main/mouse, for clarity we modified the color of the LGE-OB_Meis2/PAX6 from red to brown.

The Cebrian-Silla SVZ dataset (Cebrian-Silla et al., 2021) was obtained from GEO: GSM5039270 and included the original annotations. The UMAP dimensionality reduction did not include the model, which is required for map transfer, and we thus generated a new UMAP using the first 50 PCs, as reported in the original study.

Striatal and cortical RBPJ mutant cells were downloaded from GEO:GSM4658401 and GEO: GSE139842, respectively (Zamboni et al., 2020; Magnusson et al., 2020). Cells originally annotated as neuroblasts were subsetted, and their neuronal identity was confirmed by the expression of canonical markers and by label transfer onto the Cebrian-Silla SVZ dataset. Cortical cells annotated as neuroblasts included several TAP like cells (cluster 17) that were excluded. Cortical astrocytes and their progeny were isolated 3 weeks after stab wound injury, i.e., around the onset of neurogenesis. By contrast, striatal cells were isolated 5 weeks after AAV-Cre injection. Although the onset of neurogenesis was not reported in this model, previous work from the same group using Cx30-CreERT2 (Magnusson et al., 2014) showed that the first TAP clusters appear 3 weeks after induction, similarly to the QA-induced neurogenic response. However, these cells were isolated solely on the basis of lineage-tracing reporter expression, whereas QA-STR neuroblasts were additionally selected for PSA-NCAM, a marker enriched in more advanced stages. This difference limits direct comparison of the relative distribution of maturation stages across datasets.

Integration of parenchymal neuroblast datasets

Integration of scRNA-seq datasets containing parenchymal neuroblasts (i.e., the one generated in the present study, Rbpj-CX and Rbpj-STR) were integrated as follows. The Seurat objects of each dataset were merged using the Seurat merge() function, followed by RNA data normalization using NormalizeData(normalization.method = “LogNormalize”, scale.factor = 10000) and scaling (ScaleData()). After SCtransformation (Seurat SCTransfom() function) of the RNA assay, we performed RunPCA(Par_int_merged, npcs = 50) and the datasets were integrated using Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA, using Seurat function: IntegrateLayers(object = Par_int_merged, method = CCAIntegration, orig.reduction = “pca”, new.reduction = “integrated.cca”, normalization.method = “SCT”, k.weight = 50)). RunUMAP(reduction = “integrated.cca”, dims = 1:30) was then used for dimensionality reduction of the integrated dataset and clustering was performed (FindNeighbors(reduction = “integrated.cca”, dims = 1:30) %>% FindClusters(resolution = 1.2); the name of this clustering annotation is “SCT_snn_res.1.2”).

Pseudotime trajectories were inferred using the Monocle v3 package (Cao et al., 2019) in R. The integrated Seurat object (Par_int_CCA) was transformed into a cds object (as.cell_data_set(Par_int_CCA, assay = “RNA”, group.by = “SCT_snn_res.1.2”)) in which the same embedding of the Seurat object were imported (reducedDims(cds)$PCA <- Embeddings(Par_int_CCA, reduction = “integrated.cca”)). A principal graph was then learned to capture the developmental trajectory structure using default function learn_graph(). Cells were ordered along the trajectory using the order_cells() function, with the root state defined based on prior clustering of the Seurat object. Default parameters were used for all steps unless otherwise noted.

UCell scores for the expression of functional GO gene sets (related to Figures 4G and 4H; Data S2) were computed as follows. For each GO term, the corresponding gene list was imported into R using the dedicated function from the AnnotationDbi and org.Mm.eg.db R packages. Only genes present in the integrated dataset were included in the lists used for calculating the UCell scores. The UCell scores were calculated with the UCell R package (Andreatta and Carmona, 2021) with default parameters (AddModuleScore_UCell()).

Label transfer to reference datasets

To explore the identity of STR and OB neuroblasts obtained from this study and of striatal and cortical RBPJ mutant cells (query), we projected them on the Mayer E18 or Cebrian-Silla SVZ datasets (reference) using LabelTransfer and MapTransfer functions in Seurat. This analysis has been performed on log-normalized gene counts for both reference and query databases. Label transfer robustness was evaluated by systematically varying the number of principal components (PCs) and the k.weight parameter. Across all tested combinations, the overall repertoire of predicted identities remained stable, with no major identity classes being lost or newly introduced. Furthermore, when using more than 20 PCs, individual cell label assignments showed high consistency, suggesting that the label transfer procedure yielded robust and reproducible identity predictions. We thus ended up using the default parameters for PC 1:30 and an intermediate value for the k.weight.

Identity transfer to spatial transcriptomics datasets

The neurons and their progenitors from embryonic day (E) 18 mouse striatal dataset(Mayer et al., 2018; Schmitz et al., 2022) were aligned with four sagittal mouse brain sections from two E16.5 specimens analyzed with stereo-seq (Chen et al., 2022) and a postnatal day 7 (P7) sagittal section analyzed with Visium (Joglekar et al., 2021). Prior to alignment, cells belonging to the developing forebrain were subsetted based on spatial coordinates. Subsequently, the cells were normalized with the function SCTransform and clustered with FindClusters (k = 0.2) from Seurat v5 (Hao et al., 2024), after that, markers were calculated with FindAllMarkers and inspected to annotate the cells based on the anatomic region they occupied, which was inferred by the overexpression of characteristic markers for their most abundant populations.

To align spatial cells with the Mayer-E18 dataset, an approach based on a loss function of cosine similarity between scRNA-seq and spatial data was performed with the Python package Tangram (Biancalani et al., 2021). Clusters from the Mayer-E18 dataset, following the annotation of Schmitz et al., (2022) were fitted on each forebrain slice with the tangram function map_cells_to_space based on the normalized expression of a set of training genes. Training genes were defined with the Scanpy function tl.rank_genes_groups and the tangram function pp.adatas(Wolf et al., 2018), this process scored each cell in the slices based on their probability of belonging to the neuron classes of the E18 dataset. The Tangram-obtained scores indicate the probability of each Schmitz GABAergic neuron class being present in each spatial spot (i.e., the probability of cells from identity i being present in spot j). The log2 score distribution for all identities displayed a prominent peak at higher values, which we interpreted as a correct attribution of clusters to their respective spots. This peak appeared at similar score values across all identities, indicating a consistent and relatively uniform strength of attribution and suggesting relatively spatially segregated distributions. Interestingly, the score distributions for cortical interneurons exhibited a left-sided tail of lower values, whereas LGE-derived interneurons showed an additional, broader peak at substantially lower values. This second peak likely reflects the high transcriptional similarities between LGE interneurons subtypes and possibly also some spatial overlap. In order to avoid confounding factors, we attributed each spot to the identity with the highest score, filtering out values falling below the local minimum between the Gaussian peaks.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Distribution of reconstructed neuroblasts

A permutation test was performed to assess whether a given cluster exhibited a more lateral position along the X axis than expected by chance. This non-parametric approach does not rely on distributional assumptions and is robust to unequal group sizes. First, the observed median X-coordinate of the cluster of interest was computed. Cluster labels were then randomly permuted across all observations for n = 10000 iterations. At each iteration, the median X-coordinate of the permuted cluster was recalculated to generate a null distribution of median values. The proportion of permuted medians that were as extreme as or more extreme than the observed value was used to estimate the pseudo p-value. A low frequency of such extreme values indicates that the observed lateral position is unlikely to have occurred by chance.

Starter cell and presynaptic neuron analyses

To reconstruct the distribution of starter and presynaptic cells, most brains were imaged during cryostat cutting to serve as a pre-aligned template. One in three sections were labeled for antibodies against RFP, GFP, DCX, and either CTIP2 or DAPI. Whole sections were acquired with a slide scanner Axio Scan (.Z1, Zeiss) equipped with a 10x objective (Plan-Apochromat 10×, NA 0.45 M27). Images of 3 planes per section were acquired at a resolution of 0.65 μm/pixel and subsequently merged into a MAX projection. Regions of higher cell densities, mainly in the striatum, were acquired at the confocal microscope with a 40X objective (voxel size 0.38×0.38 × 1 μm). Images of the different modalities were registered in TrakEM2, where the starter and presynaptic cells were then manually annotated as “ball objects”. Aspecific local infection along the RABV injection track, as previously reported (Beier et al., 2015), was observed in the striatum and occasionally in cortical layer 6b (data not shown). Presynaptic cells in these areas were thus excluded from further analyses. Area attributions were based on CTIP2 and DAPI staining and were further verified by alignments to the Allen Brain Atlas. To this aim, low-resolution TrakEM2 exports of these brains were imported in QuickNII (https://www.nitrc.org/projects/quicknii) (Puchades et al., 2019). Annotations were based on the Allen Brain Atlas hierarchy and nomenclature. Export from TrakEM2 and cell countings per section and per animal were obtained with customized scripts in Python and R. For 3D analysis, using customized scripts, the positions of TrakEM2 ball objects were imported into Blender3D. Surfaces of the whole brain, striatum and lateral ventricle were manually drawn in TrakEM2, exported as.obj files and imported in Blender 3.79 together with the coordinates of the more rostral and caudal ends of the CC and of the anterior commissure. These points and the surfaces were used to manually align the different brains to a common reference space. To analyze the position of the starter cells, these alignments were further refined, focusing solely on the striatum. To better understand the overlap with cortical projection domains, we downloaded maps from anterograde tracer experiments that had already been segmented and registered to the Allen Brain Atlas common reference space http://www.mouseconnectome.org/CorticalMap/page/map/5 (Hintiryan et al., 2016). The segmented sections from each individual experiment were imported into TrakEM2, overlaid onto the corresponding Allen Brain Atlas sections, interpolated, and exported as 3D objects along with selected major brain subdivisions from the Atlas. These exports were then imported into Blender and aligned to the common reference space of the injected brains. Projections from different experiments were grouped into composite 3D objects representing specific cortical areas (SSp, MOp, PTL, ORB, ACA, and PL). QA-induced neuroblast phenotyping

Quantification of the number of cells expressing BrdU, DCX (14days n = 8; 35days n = 6) was performed for each animal on two 50 μm-thick sections (Figure 1B). LGE interneuron analyses during postnatal development

Animals at P10 (n = 3), P29 (n = 4), and P90 (n = 3) were sectioned in 6 series of 50μm-thick sections and individual series were labeled for Sp8 and SOX10 (at P10 and P90) or for DCX and Sp8 in P29 and P90. Labeled sections have been acquired at the confocal microscope (Leica Stellaris5) with a 40x objective at a resolution of 0.28×0.28 × 1.5μm/px, 10 focal planes. The Sp8+ and SOX10-cells have been counted on four equally spaced sections spanning the entire striatum, while the DCX+ cells were automatically counted with Ilastik in all the sections of the series (Figures 6H and 6I). To estimate the total number of cells over the entire striatum, the counted cells have been multiplied by the inverse of the sampling fraction.

Statistical analyses

For mean comparison, we assessed whether the data were normally distributed, using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homoscedastic, using Levene’s test. Data that fulfilled those requirements were compared with the t test in the case of two groups or with the ANOVA in the case of three or more groups. ANOVA analyses that returned significant F values were followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. For comparison of proportions, we used the Fisher’s Exact Test, suitable to compare groups with low numbers of observations.

Simple and multiple linear regression analyses were performed to evaluate the ability of different starter cell populations to predict the number of presynaptic neurons. Model quality was assessed by comparing adjusted R-squared (Adj R2) values, which indicate the proportion of variance in presynaptic neuron numbers explained by the model, as well as the associated significance (p-values). The large variability in presynaptic and starter cell counts led to violations of linear model assumptions, which were resolved by applying a logarithmic transformation. Partial F-tests were used to statistically assess whether the inclusion of additional independent variables significantly improved the amount of explained variance (Adj R2).

Generalized linear mixed effect models were used to compare variables among groups, when we had to consider that multiple observations were obtained from individual specimens and thus the group n was not composed of independent observations (e.g., Figure 3C). Based on data distribution, different types of models with maximum likelihood estimation of model parameters were applied (i.e., Gaussian, Tweedie). Generalized linear mixed-effect models were run with the R packages lmer4 and glmmTMB. Likelihood-ratio tests were performed with the drop1 function of the lme4 package to obtain global statistics.

All the results of statistical tests presented in this study are reported in the Table S1.
