Comprehensive risk mapping for heatwave-sensitive chronic diseases mortality in Argentina: An approximation based on cardiovascular diseases
Authors: Juan D Pinotti, Ximena Porcasi, Sonia A Pou, Camila Niclis, María I Stimolo, Laura R Aballay, Rubén Actis Danna, Sonia E Muñoz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Non-communicable diseases mortality risk linked to heatwaves was studied in Argentina.•Mortality risk map shows a high-risk southwest-northeast diagonal.•High risks are in central-north, while northwest and coastal areas have low ones.•A strong correlation was found between heatwave and cardiovascular mortality risk.•Vulnerability maps support targeted actions to mitigate heatwave-related mortality.

Introduction

Climate change is increasing the frequency and intensity of extreme weather events and, deepening vulnerabilities such as social inequity, ecosystem degradation, and health disparities across diverse socio-ecological systems [1]. In South America, temperatures have consistently risen, with anomalies of 1–2 °C observed in multiple regions in 2021 compared to the 1981–2010 period [2]. The frequency and intensity of heatwaves (HW) in Argentina have increased significantly over recent decades in the central and northern regions, with the 2001–2010 period showing the largest HW count [3]. In Argentina, HW arise from atmospheric blocking and land-atmosphere feedback, where drought-prone regions (such as northeastern Argentina) experience intensified HW [4]. Several studies have quantified HW in the region [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9]], although they were focused on specific cities [[5], [6], [7], [8]] or used extrapolated meteorological data [9]. HW have been linked to health impacts, including cognitive impairment [10], kidney-related conditions [11], preterm births [12], and cardiovascular and respiratory mortality [[13], [14], [15]]. While research on climate impacts on health has traditionally focused on infectious diseases and immediate damage, climate change will exacerbate chronic conditions such as cancer and cardiovascular and respiratory diseases [16]. Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) account for 63.3 % of annual deaths in Argentina [17]. However, the social implications of NCDs are often overlooked, and strategies for their prevention remain underexplored.

High temperatures represent the most extensively studied climate-related hazard associated with NCDs, particularly cardiovascular diseases (CVDs). In South America and Argentina, CVDs remain the leading cause of mortality. A high prevalence of risk factors and rising metabolic risks continue to challenge public health [18]. Higher mortality rates have been observed during periods with long and intense HW [[8], [9], [10]]. This happens through mechanisms such as heat-induced dehydration and systemic inflammation caused by heat stroke, both of which can disrupt key physiological functions [19]. Heat stress can also promote thrombosis [20] and electrolyte imbalances [21].

Argentina has significant disparities across its territory, shaped by age, gender, education, and socioeconomic status [22]. This creates distinct vulnerability profiles to climate change, making it crucial to identify and prioritize areas for effective interventions. Health officials must make well-informed risk management decisions and mitigate climate change impacts, considering these varying profiles. Comparing risks across geographic contexts is only possible with a systematic risk assessment approach. A multi-vulnerability perspective identifies sensitive targets, highlighting the distinct capacities required for prevention and response [23]. Risk maps illustrate the likelihood of a hazard causing harm to a vulnerable population within a given territory. These maps combine hazards and vulnerability to assess risks and are valuable for exploring climate risk and policy planning [24]. This study examines the spatial distribution of mortality risks from heat-sensitive NCDs by integrating climate, environmental, and health data across Argentina. Extreme heat events (HW) were used as a hazard. Adopting a multi-vulnerability approach [23], vulnerabilities were divided into four dimensions: socio-demographic, environmental, pre-existing chronic conditions, and lifestyle factors. The resulting risk maps identify priority areas where overlapping vulnerabilities amplify heat-related health threats, providing a systematic tool for targeted public health action and climate adaptation planning.

Methods

Study design and data sources

Argentina has four main climatic zones: warm, temperate, cold, and arid, and numerous microclimates [25]. The country’s socio-demographic conditions are marked by significant disparities, with differences in population structure and socioeconomic development [26,27]. The central-eastern region is the most populous, urbanized, and economically developed, whereas the northern region faces the highest levels of poverty and inequality [26,28]. This nationwide study uses vulnerability data from 2009–2010 and hazard data from 2006–2010. Public data (national surveys, census, vital statistics, and satellite products) were processed to create georeferenced indicators and risk maps, following the Climate Change Risk Mapping System methodology (SIMARCC) developed by the National Climate Change Cabinet [29]. The 2006–2010 period was selected to synchronize hazard data (HW) with available vulnerability indicators. While more recent mortality data exist, their exclusion here preserves consistency with the latest available socio-environmental variables. Spatial statistics and modeling were used to compare risk score maps with mortality rates for heat-related CVDs (CIE-10 I00-I99, excluding I46 due to frequent misclassification). Mortality data were provided by the Directorate of Statistics and Health Information (DEIS), and population estimates were interpolated using census data. Age-standardized mortality rates (ASMRs) per 100,000 person-years were calculated by sex using the 2010 Argentine population as standard. Hereafter, the rates reported for 2010 represent the 2009–2011 three-year average, calculated to minimize annual fluctuations.

Hazard: heatwaves data

Given the complexities of multiple climate hazards, we focused on heatwaves (HW), the climatic factor most recognized as linked to cardiovascular diseases [30]. Since there is no consensus on the definition of heatwave [31], we adopted the criterion of the National Meteorological Service of Argentina (SMN), which defines a heatwave as a period when daily maximum and minimum temperatures exceed the 90th percentile (calculated from a 30-year baseline) for at least three consecutive days during the warm season [32]. This 30-year window aligns with SMN monitoring standards while capturing contemporary warming trends. To calculate HW, daily maximum and minimum temperatures from 1992–2022 at a ∼9 km resolution were obtained from the ERA5-Land product (www.cds.climate.copernicus.eu) [33]. The 90th percentile (p90) for the period was calculated using R software version 4.3.1 with the ncdf4 (v1.21), raster (v3.6–20), and rgdal (v1.6–5) packages (Figure S1) [[34], [35], [36], [37]]. HW detected were averaged by department, and the number of HW per year, as well as the accumulated HW for 2006–2010, were normalized. Scripts are available at https://github.com/Dargwin/heat_waves_arg.

Vulnerability dimensions

Given the multifactorial nature of NCDs, vulnerabilities were categorized into four dimensions: sociodemographic, environmental, pre-existing chronic conditions, and lifestyle-related factors [23]. For sociodemographic vulnerability, indicators from the 2010 National Population, Household, and Housing Census were used [38], including populations with unsatisfied basic needs, individuals without primary or university education, without health insurance, and aged over 65. Child mortality rates were included from the Ministry of Health's annual vital statistics report. These indicators were expressed as percentages of the department population.

For environmental vulnerability, we used three metrics: tree cover, bare soil, and urban area percentages. Tree cover and bare soil were obtained from MODIS-MOD44B imagery. Urban area per department was determined using the MODIS-MCD12Q1 land cover classification image (2010–01–01). The analysis was conducted using Google Earth Engine and QGIS 3.28.9. The script is available in the mentioned GitHub repository. We used tree cover complementarity, recognizing higher values as indicating greater vulnerability to heat stress.

The vulnerability dimensions of pre-existing chronic conditions and lifestyle-related factors were based on the 2009 National Survey of Chronic Diseases Risk Factors [22]. This survey targets individuals aged 18+ in towns with populations over 5000. For pre-existing conditions, we used indicators for diabetes, hypertension, and obesity. For lifestyle factors, we included alcohol consumption, fruit and vegetable intake, sedentary behavior, and smoking. To align with the vulnerability scale (where higher values indicate greater vulnerability), the original fruit and vegetable consumption variable was inverted. All factors were normalized to a 0–1 scale and averaged to calculate a vulnerability score, which was classified as very–low (0–0.2), low (0.2–0.4), medium (0.4–0.6), high (0.6–0.8), and very-high (0.8–1).

Risk assessment

The total risk (TR) for HW-sensitive CVD mortality was estimated by combining hazard data with the average vulnerability score across four dimensions: sociodemographic (SV), environmental (EV), pre-existing chronic conditions (PCV), and lifestyle-related factors (LV). The formula used was: TR= Hazard × (0.25×SV + 0.25×EV + 0.25×PCV + 0.25×LV). This balanced weighting approach ensures equal contribution from each dimension. Specific risk values for each vulnerability dimension were also calculated by combining the hazard with each dimension separately (i.e., Sociodemographic Risk = Hazard x SV). All data is available on the project’s GitHub page (Table S1).

Spatial analysis

Spatial autocorrelation was evaluated using Moran's I statistic for hazard, vulnerabilities, total risk, and risks by dimension, with spatial lag based on the Queen contiguity method. A mixed-effects negative binomial model, treating provinces as random intercepts, was used to capture spatial variability (departments nested in provinces), with CVD ASMR for 2010, both overall and disaggregated by sex, as the outcome variable, and total risk as the predictor. The model was selected based on its superior fit (AIC). The Moran's I analysis was conducted using GeoDa software v1.20, while the mixed-effects model was performed using Stata software v17.

Discussion

This study presents the first maps that illustrate non-communicable diseases mortality risks linked to climate-related events, specifically HW, across Argentina. It combines demographic and health indicators with climate and remote sensing data to identify factors that increase vulnerability to heat-sensitive NCDs. This methodology permits updates to the risk maps as new hazard or vulnerability data are acquired. A broad diagonal zone, extending from southwest to northeast of Argentina, presents higher mortality risks due to a combination of vulnerabilities and HW, while coastal and high-altitude areas exhibit lower risks, underscoring the complex interaction between environmental factors, regional demographics, and health outcomes.

The variability of HW extent and intensity observed is consistent with global trends [33]. In line with our findings, a recent study also noted an increasing trend in the number of heatwaves along the broad diagonal observed [39]. The low occurrence of heatwaves along the coast may be due to air cycling between sea and land, since large water bodies act as thermal buffers and decrease temperature amplitude [40]. In the northwest, the vertical thermal gradient caused by the Andes Mountains could help mitigate heatwave frequency and intensity by promoting convection and cooler conditions compared to lowland areas [41].

The sociodemographic vulnerability shows higher values in northern Argentina, with intermediate values in northern Patagonia. This pattern, shaped by the country's socioeconomic disparities, has been well-documented in previous studies [26,27] and is reflected in the risk map for this dimension. In contrast, the spatial distribution of environmental vulnerability differs from the risks associated with it. The northwest, characterized by low tree cover and exposed soil, exhibited high environmental vulnerability but a low number of HW during the study period, resulting in a correspondingly low risk. Bare soils of anthropogenic origin have lower albedo values and lower evapotranspiration rates. This increases heat flux and local temperatures [42]. By contrast, natural bare soils can mitigate thermal extremes through higher albedo and nocturnal cooling [43]. Vulnerabilities related to pre-existing health conditions and lifestyle were more prominent along the broad diagonal, with moderate to high vulnerability values that align with their risk maps. Together, these findings suggest that HW are a significant trigger for mortality when adverse contextual factors, such as sociodemographic conditions, combine with unfavorable individual factors, including lifestyle habits and pre-existing health conditions. This study’s multidimensional vulnerability assessment provides a foundation for risk mapping that can be integrated with other hazards and datasets. Developed using Argentina’s Climate Change Risk Mapping System [29], these maps have the potential to be unified with ongoing and future national initiatives, like climate adaptation plans or Pan American Health Organization (PAHO)-supported projects [44].

These vulnerability and risk maps provide valuable insights for defining targeted actions in different regions. For example, the northeast shows high vulnerability across most socio-demographic factors. In contrast, the southwest is particularly vulnerable in terms of health coverage and primary education. Therefore, a strategic action plan for the southwest should focus on addressing these specific socio-demographic challenges. Meanwhile, in the northeast, a more comprehensive development plan would be necessary. Given the strong link between living conditions and health [45], improving quality of life could yield significant benefits, both intrinsic and in terms of reducing deaths from all causes, including heat-sensitive NCDs. Studies in Argentina have shown that during heatwaves, the risk of death from all causes increases by 14 %, with variations across age groups [7,9]. Like many countries in Latin America [46], Argentina continues to face significant challenges in addressing social inequality, which plays a critical role in shaping the health outcomes of its population.

This study empirically links CVD mortality to the risks estimated, strengthening the maps' reliability as a valuable tool for public health responses to climate change. Importantly, NCD integration into climate risk analysis is grounded in a syndemic approach (obesity-malnutrition-climate change synergies), emphasizing the need to identify synergies that promote health and equity [47]. Such synergies can yield benefits through double or triple-duty actions, which are especially crucial from a cost-benefit perspective.

It is important to acknowledge certain limitations in this study. Firstly, some of the data used (factors related to lifestyle and pre-existing conditions) are only available at the provincial level and have demographic limitations (individuals aged 18+ in towns with populations over 5000). Secondly, the aggregation of data on an annual basis may mask seasonal variations in mortality related to extreme events like HW. Thirdly, our analysis prioritizes temporal coherence over recency. Future work should integrate newer datasets once full departmental-level disaggregation is publicly available. Although the use of equal weighting in the proposed risk calculation may be viewed as a limitation, this methodological decision was made to prioritize transparency in this first assessment. In future studies, methods such as the Analytic Hierarchy Process [48] could be used to derive dimension-specific weights. Further studies are needed to identify if some dimensions are more influential, enabling better weighting to refine risk maps. Finally, we chose to employ a climatic definition of heatwave. However, for future studies, it may be beneficial to consider indices that incorporate the influence of human sensitivity to extreme heat events, like the Excess Heat Factor [49] or the Heat Index [50]. These indices would provide a more specific measure for epidemiological studies.

Climate change is transforming patterns of vulnerability, risk, and disease and disproportionately affecting susceptible populations such as the elderly, who face elevated health risks due to heightened sensitivity to climatic shifts [51]. NCDs amplify these climate impacts by acting as comorbidities that exacerbate health risks. Our findings suggest a correlation between the frequency of HW and mortality rates associated with heat-sensitive NCDs. Further research is essential to delve into this connection, leading to the development of strategies to mitigate its impact. Given the spatio-temporal variability of hazards and vulnerability factors, updating the risk map is crucial to ensuring its efficacy as a planning tool. Understanding the diverse spatial and temporal patterns of climate threats, in complex interaction with vulnerability conditions, would provide a clearer insight into the impact of climate change on health, thereby guiding effective health interventions.
