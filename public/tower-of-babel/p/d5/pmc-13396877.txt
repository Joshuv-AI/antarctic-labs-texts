Improvement in the Microbiological Diagnosis of Hepatitis D on the Mediterranean Coast of Southeastern Spain: A Repeated Cross‐Sectional Survey

Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundHepatitis D virus (HDV) remains underdiagnosed, and universal screening of HBsAg‐positive patients is recommended. We assessed changes in HDV diagnostic capacity across public hospitals in the Valencian Community, Spain.MethodsRepeated cross‐sectional surveys were conducted in 2024 and 2025. Analyses included the 19 hospitals participating in both years and evaluated anti‐HDV testing, HDV RNA detection, reflex testing, and clinician notification.ResultsAnti‐HDV testing increased from 84.2% to 94.7%, HDV RNA testing from 68.4% to 84.2%, reflex testing from 43.8% to 77.8% (p = 0.04), and direct clinician communication from 75.0% to 83.3%. Improvements were most marked in hospitals with fewer than 500 beds, where anti‐HDV testing rose from 78.6% to 92.9% and HDV RNA testing from 57.1% to 78.6%.ConclusionsHDV diagnostic capacity improved substantially, particularly in smaller hospitals. Further organizational measures are needed to achieve universal double‐reflex testing and timely communication of positive results.

Introduction

The hepatitis D virus (HDV) is present in 1 in every 22 people with hepatitis B and is implicated in 1 in every 6 cases of cirrhosis and in 1 in every 5 cases of liver cancer in these patients [1]. Due to the diagnostic challenges of the process and the underdiagnosis that exists in many regions, there is still uncertainty regarding the true number of infected individuals worldwide [2].

The Chronic Liver Disease Foundation (CLDF) recommends universal HDV screening for all patients who are Hepatitis B surface antigen‐positive (HBsAg). In these patients, an initial screening with a total antibody detection system (anti‐HDV) is recommended, and in the case of positivity, viral activity should be confirmed by determining the viral load quantitatively (HDV RNA) [3].

Regarding the epidemiology of the virus in Spain, HDV prevalence is estimated to be around 5% among patients with Hepatitis B. This percentage mainly corresponding to immigrants from Eastern European countries, elderly patients, and those with Hepatitis C and HIV coinfection probably associated with past injection drug use, with 62% of cases showing active infection [4].

In the current clinical context, improving HDV diagnosis is especially important because new therapeutic options are now available [5]. Accurate identification of patients with HDV infection is therefore essential, as diagnosis is a prerequisite for treatment evaluation and access [3].

In this study, a survey was conducted on the diagnostic capacity of the Microbiology Laboratories in public hospitals in 2024, and in order to evaluate the diagnostic improvements made, the survey was repeated in 2025.

Materials and Method

Evaluated Population

All public hospitals in the Valencian Community (approximately 5,500,000 inhabitants), located on the Mediterranean coast of southeastern Spain. Hospitals were classified according to the number of beds, using a cutoff of 500 beds.

Data Collection and Statistical Analysis

Two surveys were conducted using Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) in 2024 and Google Forms in 2025. The platform was changed because some participants reported difficulties completing the questionnaire in REDCap, while Google Forms was a more familiar platform. Categorical variables are presented as n/N (%). Prespecified comparisons were performed by survey year and hospital size using the chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. No exploratory subgroup analyses were performed. All tests were two‐sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

In 2024, all contacted hospitals (24 centers, 6 with more than 500 beds—25.0%) responded to the survey. In 2025, the survey was completed by 19 centers, 5 of which had more than 500 beds (26.3%). To ensure data consistency and comparability, only centers participating in both years were included in the analysis (n = 19).

A substantial improvement is observed in all analyzed parameters: determination of anti‐HDV (84.2% [16/19] in 2024 versus 94.7% [18/19] in 2025), detection of viral genome (68.4% [13/19] in 2024 vs. 84.2% [16/19 in 2025]), reflex testing (43.8% [7/16] in 2024 vs. 77.8% [14/18] in 2025) (p = 0.04), direct communication with the clinician (75.0% [12/16] in 2024 vs. 83.3% [15/18] in 2025) (Table 1).

In relation to the commercial systems used for the detection of antibodies and viral genome, there is variability among the centers. In 2024, the most commonly used commercial brands for the detection of the HBsAg were Abbott (50% of the centers), Roche Diagnostics (20.84% of the centers), and Siemens (16.66% of the centers), while 12.5% of the centers used other brands. In 2025, the number of centers using Abbott increased to 60%, followed by Roche Diagnostics (25%), with the remaining centers (15%) using other brands. Regarding the commercial brands used for the detection of antibodies anti‐HDV, the most utilized in 2024 was DiaSorin, implemented in 72.72% of the centers, becoming the only brand used by all centers in 2025. For the determination of the hepatitis D viral genome, the 53.8% of centers (7/13) performed the assay on site rather than outsourcing it in 2024, this proportion increased to 58.7% (11/16) in 2052. Among these centers, 42.9% (3/7) used Altona in 2024, and that percentage increased to 45.4% (5/11) in 2025. Other commercial brands for the detection of viral genome included Roche Diagnostics (14.3%—1/7 in 2024, 27.3%—3/11 in 2025), Euroimmun (14.3%—1/7 in 2024, 9.1%—1/11 in 2025), and Vircell (14.3%—1/7 in 2024, 27.3%—3/11 in 2025).

Discussion

The emergence of new therapies for HDV has highlighted the need to optimize diagnostic strategies in microbiology laboratories. Consistent with a previous nationwide study conducted in Spain [6], our results show that diagnostic capacity continued to improve in 2025, particularly in smaller hospitals. However, important shortcomings in diagnostic work‐flows and result communication remain. The systematic implementation of anti‐HDV testing in all HBsAg‐positive patients resulted in a five‐fold increase in HDV diagnoses [7].

Our findings are consistent with reports from other regions. In France, 84.9% of patients were screened for HDV, with variability depending on screening strategies, and HDV RNA testing was performed in 78.3% of anti‐HDV–positive individuals [8]. In Greece, screening coverage was 53%, with substantial inter‐center variability (14%–100%) [9]. In Spain, a national survey revealed lower diagnostic capacity, with only 63% of centers performing anti‐HDV testing and 28% HDV‐RNA testing; single‐step diagnosis and structured communication of active infection were infrequent [6].

The double reflex diagnostic system has proven to be the most effective approach. However, its implementation has not been homogeneous across hospitals and has largely depended on local laboratory organization and diagnostic work‐flows. This local variability remains one of the main challenges to the widespread adoption of this diagnostic approach [6]. Its universal implementation in Andalusia led to a significant increase in anti‐HDV detection (from 18% to 96%) and HDV RNA assessment (from 77% to 89% of anti‐HDV‐positive patients) [10]. This strategy is also cost‐effective: prior to its introduction, only 7.6% of HBsAg‐positive patients were tested, whereas its implementation reduced liver‐related complications by 35%–38% and generated an estimated €36 million in savings, with a cost of €132 per detected case [11]. The non‐implementation of the process represents a significant health‐care cost. In Spain, it has been estimated that the total annual cost of hidden HDV would be €17.8 million [12].

In conclusion, although diagnostic performance has improved—particularly in hospitals with fewer than 500 beds—organizational efforts remain necessary to ensure universal adoption of the double reflex system and timely communication of positive results. The goal is to improve patient management, facilitate access to newly available therapies, and help reduce health‐care costs.

Ethics Statement

The study was conducted within the framework of a research project with reference number ISABIAL 2023‐0449, which received a favorable assessment from the Comité de Ética para la Investigación con Medicamentos del Departamento de Salud de Alicante—Hospital General (reference number CEIm PI2023‐157). The associated research project had requested an exemption from written informed consent. The survey was addressed to professional participants and participation was entirely voluntary. No patients were involved, no clinical intervention was performed, and no patient‐level clinical data were collected. No separate written informed consent was obtained from the professional participants; consent was considered implied by voluntary completion and submission of the survey. Survey responses were treated confidentially and analyzed in aggregate.
