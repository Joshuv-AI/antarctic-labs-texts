Environmental Determinants of Surgical Site Infections in Abdominal Surgeries: A Scoping Review of Evidence from East Africa
Authors: Alphonsine Imanishimwe, Celestin Banamwana, Georges Bucyibaruta, Christian Nsanzabaganwa, Faustin Ntirenganya, Erigene Rutayisire
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground Surgical site infections (SSIs) remain a major cause of postoperative morbidity and mortality, particularly following abdominal surgeries. Environmental factors are key contributors, yet evidence from East Africa remains fragmented. This scoping review mapped available evidence on environmental determinants of SSIs in abdominal surgeries across East African healthcare settings to inform context-specific interventions.MethodsFollowing the Arksey and O’Malley framework, a comprehensive search of PubMed, Embase, CINAHL, and African Journals Online identified studies published between 2014 and 2024. Eligible studies included quantitative, qualitative, and mixed-method research addressing environmental factors associated with SSIs in abdominal surgeries. Data were thematically analyzed, with findings from Rwanda compared to Uganda, Tanzania, Kenya, and Burundi.ResultsNineteen studies met inclusion criteria (nine cross-sectional, six cohort, one chart review, one quality improvement, one mixed-method, and one surveillance study). Few studies directly examined temperature, ventilation, and humidity, though their influence was indirectly recognized. Poorly implemented cleaning protocols, absence of standardized infection prevention and control (IPC) guidelines, and inadequate IPC training were major contributors to high SSI rates. Rwanda showed weaker SSI surveillance compared to Kenya and Uganda. Klebsiella spp. E. coli, and Staphylococcus aureus were the predominant pathogens, exhibiting high antibiotic resistance. Additionally, inadequate staffing, poor infrastructure, and contamination in operating theaters and wards elevated SSI risk across the region.ConclusionEnvironmental factors substantially influence SSI burden in East Africa. Strengthening IPC training, environmental controls, and surveillance, alongside developing affordable, context-specific interventions, is vital to reduce SSIs and improve surgical outcomes.Keywords: surgical site infections, abdominal surgery, environmental factors, temperature control, ventilation, hospital cleaning, Rwanda, infection prevention

Introduction

Surgical site infections (SSIs) are a significant concern in the realm of abdominal surgeries, representing one of the most common complications associated with surgical procedures.1 SSIs occur within 30 days of surgery at the incision site or within a year if an implant is involved.1,2 These infections can lead to severe implications, including prolonged hospital stays, increased healthcare costs, and heightened morbidity and mortality rates.3

Recent studies indicate that the incidence of SSIs in abdominal surgeries ranges from 10% to over 25%, depending on various factors such as the type of surgery performed, patient demographics, and the presence of comorbidities.1 Emergency surgeries are particularly susceptible to higher infection rates compared to elective procedures. For instance, one study reported an SSI rate of 26% in emergency cases versus 8% in planned surgeries.4,5 This disparity underscores the need for targeted preventive measures tailored to different surgical contexts. Surgical site infections remain a significant concern in East African countries, with reported prevalence rates ranging from 3.48% to 26% across various surgical procedures.6–11 The multifactorial nature of SSIs necessitates a comprehensive understanding of various risk factors, particularly those associated with the surgical environment.

Environmental factors play a crucial role in the development of SSIs. Contamination during surgery can stem from multiple sources, including surgical instruments, operating room air quality, and even the surgical team’s attire.12 Furthermore, factors such as ambient temperature, humidity levels, and cleanliness of the operating room can significantly influence bacterial colonization and subsequent infection rates.13,14 Studies have shown that hypothermia during surgery can impair immune function and increase susceptibility to infections.15,16 Ventilation systems are another key environmental factor influencing SSI rates. Proper air circulation and filtration are essential for reducing airborne contaminants that can lead to infections, and inadequate ventilation can result in the accumulation of pathogens within the operating room, increasing the risk of SSIs.14,17 High humidity levels can promote bacterial growth on surfaces and instruments, while excessively low humidity can lead to dry skin and increased susceptibility to surgical site infections.14 In addition to temperature, ventilation, and humidity, hospital cleaning protocols are critical components of infection control strategies, as effective cleaning practices help eliminate potential sources of contamination within the surgical environment.18

While patient-related (eg, age, comorbidities) and procedure-related (eg, surgery duration, emergency status) risk factors for SSIs are well-documented, the role of the environment is increasingly recognized as a pivotal, yet often overlooked, determinant.17,19 In resource-constrained settings like those in East Africa, challenges such as unreliable infrastructure, intermittent water supply, and limited access to essential sanitation and hygiene (WASH) facilities exacerbate these environmental risks.20 However, the specific impact and interplay of these factors within the East African context remain poorly documented, leading to a gap in evidence-based, context-specific interventions.

In East Africa, emerging studies indicate substantial environmental contamination of operating theaters and wards, limited adherence to IPC protocols, shortages of trained personnel, and inconsistent surveillance systems.11,21,22 For instance, Gram-negative organisms such as Klebsiella spp., Escherichia coli, and Proteus spp. have been consistently linked to SSIs in Rwanda and across the region, often displaying high levels of antimicrobial resistance.6,22–24 Moreover, contextual factors such as overcrowding in surgical wards, interruptions in water supply, and unsafe home environments post-discharge compound the infection risk.25–27

Despite these challenges, there is limited region-specific synthesis of evidence addressing environmental determinants of SSIs in abdominal surgeries. Most available studies either focus on clinical risk factors or provide fragmented insights into environmental aspects, without integrating findings into a comprehensive framework. This gap hinders the development of context-specific interventions and policies tailored to the realities of East African healthcare systems.

This scoping review therefore addresses a critical evidence gap by systematically mapping the literature on environmental risk factors for SSIs in abdominal surgeries within East Africa. By synthesizing evidence across East Africa, the review provides a regional perspective, highlights cross-country variations, and identifies unaddressed determinants such as temperature control, ventilation, and humidity regulation. The findings will highlight the gaps to inform further studies and could guide policymakers, healthcare providers, and researchers in designing low-cost, innovative, and sustainable interventions adapted to resource-constrained settings. Ultimately, strengthening the evidence base on environmental determinants is essential for reducing SSI burden, improving surgical outcomes, and advancing patient safety across East Africa. This is crucial because solutions effective in high-income countries and other regions are often not transferable to LMICs, including East Africa due to vast differences in resources, infrastructure, and environment.

Methods

Study Design

This scoping review was conducted following the framework proposed by Arksey and O’Malley.28 The framework guided the systematic mapping of existing evidence on environmental factors influencing SSIs in abdominal surgeries, focusing the East African countries healthcare context. By incorporating a broad spectrum of studies, the review aimed to identify knowledge gaps, highlight evidence, and provide recommendations to address SSIs in resource-limited settings. This approach also allowed for the synthesis of findings across diverse methodologies and settings, ensuring a comprehensive understanding of the topic.

Search Strategy

A comprehensive and systematic search strategy was developed to identify relevant literature across East African countries, including Rwanda, Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania, and Burundi. Searches were conducted across multiple databases, including PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, CINAHL, Web of Science, and African Journals Online (AJOL). The search incorporated a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH), Emtree terms, and free-text keywords.

The search strategy employed the following Boolean operators to refine the search: AND: used to narrow results by combining terms (eg, “surgical site infection” AND “abdominal surgery”); and OR: used to broaden results by including synonyms or related terms (eg, “SSI” OR “surgical site infection”).

Core concepts included surgical site infections (SSIs), abdominal surgeries, and environmental factors and for example, this search string was used: “surgical site infection” OR “SSI” AND “abdominal surgery” OR “cesarean section” AND “environmental factors” OR “hospital environment” OR “temperature control” OR “humidity” OR “ventilation” OR “cleaning protocols” OR “infection prevention” OR “WASH” AND (“Rwanda” OR “Kenya” OR “Uganda” OR “Tanzania” OR “Burundi” OR East Africa).

The reference lists of all eligible studies were also screened manually to identify additional relevant publications that may have been missed during the database search.

Search Period

The review included studies published between 2014 and 2024, and this 10-year timeframe ensured the inclusion of recent evidence while capturing trends relevant to SSIs in East African countries.

Eligibility Criteria

Inclusion Criteria

To ensure the inclusion of relevant studies, eligibility criteria were defined, and articles were included if they were conducted in East African countries, specifically Rwanda Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania, and Burundi. The comparisons within East African countries in terms of socio-demographic and economic conditions were conducted We included studies that examined SSIs following abdominal surgical procedures among any population group, regardless of age, gender, or comorbidity status. Both elective and emergency surgeries were considered, provided the studies evaluated or reported on environmental factors contributing to SSIs.

Environmental factors of interest included intraoperative or perioperative temperature regulation, ventilation systems, humidity control, hospital or ward cleaning protocols, water and sanitation conditions (WASH), surgical room hygiene, and infection prevention infrastructure. Studies conducted in any clinical setting—whether primary, secondary, or tertiary hospitals—were considered eligible.

We included original empirical studies utilizing quantitative, qualitative, or mixed-methods designs. Eligible study types encompassed observational designs (cross-sectional, cohort, and case-control studies), randomized controlled trials, implementation research, and other peer-reviewed primary studies. We only included articles published in English.

Exclusion Criteria

Studies that were not conducted in the aforementioned East African countries were excluded, as were opinion pieces, commentaries, and editorials unless they provided unique contextual value. Conference abstracts were excluded unless full methodological and results sections were available. Finally, non-English studies were omitted from this review.

Study Selection and Data Extraction

The selection process involved two stages to ensure methodological rigor: (1) We screened the titles and abstracts of retrieved studies. The inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied consistently, and after the de-duplication process, title screening was followed by abstract screening. (2) Full-text screening of studies deemed potentially eligible was performed to confirm their relevance. A PRISMA flow diagram was used to document the selection process.

Data Extraction

A standardized data extraction form was developed, and data included study characteristics, such as title, authors, publication year, sample, and study design. We also recorded factors studied in the article, such as temperature control, ventilation systems, humidity regulation, cleaning protocols, and SSI-related outcomes, including infection rates, associated pathogens, and antibiotic resistance profiles. Finally, the key findings/conclusions were recorded.

Collating, Summarising, and Reporting the Results

The data were collated, compared, and summarised. Then, thematic analysis was conducted using an inductive technique, and descriptive and methodological indicators were used to categorize the data into themes, including temperature control, ventilation systems, humidity regulation, and cleaning protocols. Figures and tables, such as flowcharts of the research selection process and table of the characteristics of the included studies, were used to present the findings.

Results

Included Studies and Their Main Characteristics

The search of electronic databases initially yielded 710 items in total. The number of possibly acceptable articles was lowered to 98 after 161 duplicates were eliminated, and 344 articles were disqualified after their titles were examined. After screening abstracts of 205 articles, 36 remained whose full-text versions were attempted for retrieval. Then, 31 articles remained after 5 full-texts were not successfully retrieved, and 14 more were eliminated following the second round of full-text screening, leaving 17 fully eligible articles. The references of relevant articles were also explored to find 2 more articles that fulfilled the criteria, making 19 articles in total (Figure 1). The included studies were predominantly cross-sectional (9 articles) followed by cohort studies (6 articles) (Table 1).

Prevalence and Incidence of SSIs

The prevalence of SSIs post-abdominal surgeries across healthcare facilities in East African countries varies widely from 14.5% to 26%.,23,26,27,32,36 reflecting the influence of multiple factors across healthcare settings.

Environment-Associated Factors That Influence Surgical Site Infections in Abdominal Surgeries in East Africa

The role of temperature control in preventing SSIs has received limited direct attention in Rwandan healthcare settings, though related factors such as operating room conditions and sterility are frequently discussed.29,31 Studies addressing gaps in infection prevention and control (IPC) measures in Rwandan hospitals underscore the need for improved infrastructure, which inherently includes maintaining optimal temperatures during surgical procedures.18,29,33,34 Inconsistent climate control can create an environment conducive to microbial proliferation, indirectly increasing the risk of SSIs.14,37 This compounds the issue of inadequate infection prevention programs, as documented by Irakiza et al,18 point to gaps in policies, training, and staffing for infection control, highlighting a need for comprehensive assessments and interventions to ensure stable temperature regulation in operating rooms, aligning with global best practices.Ventilation deficiencies are a recurring issue in Rwandan hospitals and pose a significant challenge to infection control. According to Irakiza et al,18 only 24% of surveyed healthcare facilities demonstrated advanced IPC measures, which likely included effective ventilation systems. Inadequate airflow and filtration mechanisms in surgical wards facilitate the spread of airborne pathogens, increasing the risk of SSIs, particularly during emergency procedures where sterility may be compromised.14,38

In East African countries neighboring Rwanda, there is a notable lack of studies directly examining the influence of temperature, humidity, ventilation systems, and IPC protocols on SSIs. Existing research primarily explores other environmental factors contributing to SSIs, which may or may not include these specific elements. Thus, studies highlight critical environmental risk factors for SSIs, with variations in focus between countries. In Uganda, Muhindo et al23 examined operating theater contamination and antibiotic resistance, revealing significant bacterial contamination linked to SSIs. Similarly, in Tanzania, Mawalla et al36 identified poor skin preparation, prolonged operation duration, and high theater occupancy as key contributors to SSIs, while Eriksen27 emphasized the role of preoperative stays and operation classification. Fehr et al22 further underscored Tanzania’s challenges with hygiene practices and inadequate equipment availability in surgical settings. Also in Tanzania, post-discharge risks (home environments and wound care practices) were found to be associated with SSIs, a factor absent in other East African countries’ studies.25

Kenya’s research diverged slightly, with Aiken26 assess systemic issues like staffing shortages and facility limitations. Gakuanyi et al24 specifically evaluated adherence to WHO infection prevention guidelines, revealing gaps in compliance.

Burundi was underrepresented as it was only studies by a multi-country study by Chu et al32 covering other countries, such as DRC, and Sierra Leone. This study collectively highlighted the compounding effects of hospital type and geographical constraints on SSI rates, suggesting broader infrastructural challenges in low-resource settings.

Although specific studies on room humidity in Rwandan healthcare settings are scarce, broader challenges in environmental control suggest that humidity regulation is an overlooked yet critical factor. Improper humidity levels can significantly impact surgical outcomes; high humidity fosters microbial growth, while very low humidity may compromise the sterility of surgical instruments.14,39 One study in Rwanda indirectly highlights these challenges, revealing widespread inadequacies in IPC infrastructure.18

Effective hospital cleaning protocols are fundamental to minimizing SSIs, yet studies reveal persistent gaps in this area across Rwandan healthcare facilities. Munyeshyaka et al29 found that Gram-negative bacteria, particularly E. coli, were prevalent in medical wards and significantly associated with SSIs, indicating the critical need for stringent cleaning and disinfection practices. Similarly, Irakiza et al18 reported that most hospitals lacked dedicated IPC staff and comprehensive policies for managing multidrug-resistant infections, directly impacting the cleanliness of hospital environments. However, successful interventions demonstrate that targeted quality improvement initiatives, including aseptic techniques, enhanced antibiotic prophylaxis, and improved staff coordination, can significantly reduce SSI rates.33 Studies in Rwanda demonstrated that contamination in medical wards, particularly with gram-negative bacteria like E. coli, plays a significant role in surgical wound infections,29 while also highlighting the role of water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) conditions in the postpartum wards and patients’ homes, and finding that interruptions in water supply significantly increased SSI risks.31 Akimana et al8 emphasize the role of environmental factors like preoperative skin preparation and surgical environment sterility in cesarean section outcomes, with showering before the operation, not shaving immediately before the incision, and using a combination of povidone and chlorhexidine for skin preparation associated with reduced risk of post-CS SSI. These findings underscore the importance of standardized, resource-appropriate cleaning protocols tailored to the needs of Rwandan hospitals.

The evidence highlights that while environmental factors and cleaning protocols play a pivotal role in reducing SSIs, the knowledge and practices of healthcare providers are equally critical. Niyomugano et al21 reported that over half of the healthcare professionals demonstrated good knowledge and practices in preventing SSIs, with those receiving training on infection prevention showing better performance.

Bacteria Linked to SSIs in East Africa

The review identified the following pathogens as key contributors to SSIs in abdominal surgeries in Rwanda:

Gram-Negative Bacteria

Klebsiella spp. was highlighted as the most prevalent pathogen (55%) in Rwandan tertiary care hospitals,6 while E. coli was frequently isolated from contaminated hospital environments and surgical wounds (15%).6,29 Proteus spp. was associated with antibiotic-resistant SSIs,6 and Acinetobacter baumannii complex had role in hospital-acquired infections.40

Gram-Positive Bacteria

Staphylococcus aureus was found to be associated with SSIs, particularly in settings with inadequate infection control, and Coagulase-negative staphylococci was identified in postoperative infections.6

Comparatively, other studies from neighboring countries noted similarities and variations in bacterial profiles. In Uganda, Staphylococcus aureus and Pseudomonas aeruginosa were reported alongside Klebsiella spp. and E. coli, indicating a diverse range of bacterial pathogens associated with infections.23 This profile suggests the coexistence of both Gram-positive and Gram-negative organisms in clinical settings, reflecting a multifaceted challenge for infection control and treatment.

In Tanzania, the bacterial profile demonstrated a similar pattern, with E. coli and Staphylococcus aureus being the predominant pathogens.22,36 The predominance of these organisms highlights common regional trends, potentially linked to comparable healthcare settings, environmental conditions, and antibiotic usage patterns. These findings underscore the importance of cross-country surveillance and the potential for shared strategies in antimicrobial resistance containment.

Other Environmental Risk Factors Across East African Countries

In Rwanda, Emergency surgeries and prolonged operation times have been highlighted as significant factors that increase the risk of SSIs.6,34 In addition, multiple patient-related risk factors have also been found to be associated with increased SSI risks, including maternal age ≥35 years in obstetric surgery, obesity, and HIV positively, mainly in rural areas.9,30,35

Hospital contamination emerges as a critical shared concern, with multiple studies from Rwanda,29 Uganda,23 and Tanzania,36 consistently reporting high levels of bacterial contamination in surgical wards and operating theaters. This environmental factor was identified as a major contributor to SSIs throughout the region, suggesting a need for standardized sterilization protocols and improved facility maintenance across borders. Rwanda also presents unique challenges not prominently featured in neighboring countries’ research. Overcrowding in surgical wards was specifically linked to elevated SSI risk in Rwandan healthcare settings,41 a factor that appears particularly acute in this context. Similarly, surveillance system deficiencies were primarily noted in Rwanda,42 indicating potential gaps in infection monitoring that could hinder effective intervention strategies.

Tanzanian studies revealed distinctive environmental risk factors, particularly regarding post-discharge care. Peter & Seif25 uniquely identified poor home hygiene conditions as contributing to post-operative infection risk, an aspect not emphasized in studies from other East African nations. This finding suggests the need for community-based infection prevention programs in Tanzania that extend beyond hospital settings. In Kenya, Magori et al43 documented significant microbial presence on surgical surfaces and patient beds, underscoring the critical role of environmental hygiene in SSI prevention and highlighting potential differences in hospital cleaning protocols between countries. Resource constraints emerged as a pan-regional challenge, with both Rwanda and Kenya reporting medical personnel shortages and inadequate infrastructure as high-risk contributors to SSIs.26,41 However, the Rwandan studies placed particular emphasis on personnel shortages, possibly reflecting more severe staffing challenges in this context. Discussion

The results of this scoping review provide an understanding of the environmental factors influencing surgical site infections (SSIs) in abdominal surgeries within Rwandan healthcare settings based on the available literature. The findings provide evidence similar to other countries but also show contextual differences, emphasizing the need for tailored strategies.

Temperature regulation plays a critical role in infection prevention by maintaining immune function and reducing microbial proliferation during surgery.14,15,37 However, this review identified a lack of direct studies on temperature control in Rwanda, with most references being indirect recommendations for maintaining optimal hospital climates. Comparatively, studies from high-income countries (HICs) like the United States (USA), United Kingdom and China emphasize intraoperative temperature management through warming devices and protocols to maintain normothermia, showing a significant reduction in SSI rates.14,44 In Germany, research showed that warmer temperatures, especially above 20°C, are associated with an increased likelihood of SSIs. The association between temperature and SSIs is stronger for SSIs caused by gram-negative pathogens compared to gram-positive pathogens and for superficial SSIs caused by gram-negative pathogens.45 Research from the USA showed that SSI incidence is highly seasonal, with a 26.5% increase in August compared to January. Higher average monthly temperatures are associated with increased odds of SSI, with a 28.9% increase in odds for temperatures above 90°F compared to below 40°F.46 Rwanda’s resource constraints may explain the absence of such targeted interventions and investigations. This highlights the importance of integrating affordable temperature management technologies, such as passive insulation or low-cost warming systems, into operating room protocols, which were shown to be effective in reducing perioperative SSIs.47 A study conducted in China found that increased ventilation and reduced humidity significantly reduced bacterial growth at 26 °C and 34 °C, and the influence of increased ventilation was greater than decreased humidity. At 34°C, medium and high levels of ventilation reduced bacterial growth, and high temperatures increased bacterial growth, while ventilation could reduce it.14

Ventilation systems are fundamental to controlling airborne pathogens in surgical environments.14 While this review highlights deficiencies in IPC measures and ventilation systems in Rwandan hospitals, no study directly investigated the role of ventilation in SSIs in Rwanda. However, research shows that inadequate airflow and filtration increase SSI risks. For instance, a previous scoping review reported that poorly ventilated operating rooms had significantly higher bacterial loads compared to those with high-efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filters.48 This indicated that enhancing ventilation systems, including proper air exchange rates and filtration, is essential for mitigating these risks and ensuring safer surgical outcomes. Though the relationship between room humidity and SSIs remains underexplored in Rwanda, studies from regions, such as Asia, America and Europe indicate that controlling humidity within an optimal range of 40–60% significantly reduces SSI risks.48–50 It was found that there was a 1.09 odds increase in SSI following discharge for every 10 cm increase in maximum daily precipitation and a 1.03 odds increase in SSI risk following discharge for every g/kg unit rise in specific humidity.49 Addressing room humidity as part of comprehensive environmental control measures could substantially reduce abdominal surgery-related SSI rates in Rwanda. However, the absence of humidity-focused research in Rwanda and Africa reflects a broader gap in understanding this environmental factor in low-income settings. The research in Rwanda and Africa, in general could help develop context-specific guidelines for humidity regulation that would enhance infection control. Similarly, ventilation-focused research should be conducted in Rwandan healthcare facilities with advanced IPC systems to highlight the impact of ventilation on SSIs and inform policies aimed at enhancing infection control, developing effective protocols and abdominal surgery outcomes.

There is a significant gap in East African countries like Rwanda in studies examining the impact of temperature, humidity, ventilation systems, and infection prevention protocols on SSIs. Current research focuses on other environmental factors, suggesting a need for future studies to assess climate-related and infrastructural determinants to improve surgical outcomes in resource-limited settings. This review highlights significant commonalities in environmental factors associated with SSIs across East Africa, particularly concerning hospital contamination and inadequate healthcare infrastructure. However, variations in research focus and health system capacities were also evident, suggesting differences in infection prevention priorities and resource allocation among countries.51 In contrast to Rwanda, countries such as Kenya and Tanzania demonstrate a stronger emphasis on infection prevention measures, including adherence to sterilization guidelines, hand hygiene compliance, and monitoring of antibiotic resistance patterns.52 These differences may reflect more established IPC programs or varying research priorities across the region. The presence of structured IPC initiatives in these countries could serve as a model for Rwanda in strengthening its own infection control frameworks.53 While Rwanda faces unique challenges, many environmental factors influencing SSIs are universally recognized. For example, the role of water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) conditions is emphasized in both Rwandan studies and global literature. In rural India, inadequate WASH facilities were associated with a 2.5-fold increase in SSI risk,54 aligning with our findings reported in postpartum wards in Rwanda. In addition, Rwanda faces distinct challenges with key issues including overcrowding in surgical wards, which was reported as a unique environmental risk factor compared to other East African countries. Moreover, the lack of robust SSI surveillance systems hinders data-driven infection control efforts, making it difficult to assess the true burden of SSIs and evaluate interventions effectively.55 Furthermore, there is limited research focusing on adherence to infection control practices, such as compliance with WHO guidelines on sterilization and antibiotic use, indicating a critical gap in evidence-based infection prevention strategies (World Health Organization (WHO).

This review highlights the critical role of hospital cleaning protocols in reducing SSIs, with successful interventions demonstrating dramatic reductions in infection rates. Globally, similar trends have been observed, and adherence to standardized cleaning protocols has been found to reduce bacterial contamination in operating rooms.56 However, many Rwandan hospitals lack IPC-trained staff and standardized protocols, a challenge highlighted in other low-income countries.57–59 A previous review with articles from Africa, Asia, America, and Australia found that barriers to IPC protocol adherence among healthcare providers include increased workloads and fatigue, a lack of training, a lack of isolation spaces, and underequipped settings.59 Innovative strategies, such as training programs and using affordable, locally available equipment, could improve hygiene practices and SSI outcomes. Moreover, studies indicated associations between specific staff behaviors, such as the number of people in the room and door openings, with air quality or surgical site infection rates, and there was a link between surgeon interruptions/distractions or noise and higher surgical site infection rates.60 Staff behavior can be influenced by knowledge, as our review findings show that only around half of healthcare providers have good knowledge and practice of infection control. This points to the need for a dual approach: addressing environmental factors while simultaneously enhancing the capacity of healthcare providers through targeted training and standardized practices. Such combined efforts can significantly improve adherence to infection prevention protocols, thereby reducing the incidence of SSIs in Rwandan healthcare facilities. This is supported by our finding that higher knowledge and better practices were among those trained in infection control compared to those who were not, aligning with other global studies.61–63

Patient-related risk factors and pathogen profiles highlighted by some studies in Rwanda are intrinsically connected to the environmental factors influencing SSIs, forming a multifaceted network of risk determinants. Conditions such as obesity, advanced age, and HIV positivity often require specialized perioperative care, including tailored environmental controls to mitigate risks. For instance, emergency surgeries, which are associated with higher SSI rates due to limited preparation time, often occur in suboptimal environmental conditions, such as inadequate ventilation or insufficient preoperative sterilization measures.64 Prolonged operation times further increase exposure to potential contaminants within the surgical environment,58 underscoring the need for strict adherence to infection control protocols. The prevalence of antibiotic-resistant pathogens such as Klebsiella spp., E. coli, and Proteus spp. adds another layer of complexity. Inadequate cleaning protocols, poor humidity regulation, and inconsistent temperature control create favorable conditions for the proliferation and transmission of these resistant bacteria within healthcare settings.14 This highlights the interplay between environmental and microbial factors, where lapses in hospital infrastructure and hygiene practices exacerbate the risk posed by patient-related vulnerabilities and resistant pathogens. Addressing these interconnected challenges requires an integrated approach that combines robust environmental controls, patient-centered care, and antibiotic stewardship to effectively minimize SSI risks in Rwanda. The pathogens isolated in Rwandan healthcare facilities were also identified in studies from limited resource countries, mainly Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, where overuse and misuse of antibiotics exacerbate resistance and inadequate stewardship in these countries were reported as one of the factors.65,66 However, antibiotic stewardship programs have successfully curbed resistance through surveillance and regulation.67–69 Though Rwanda is implementing such programs in Rwanda,70 enhancing these programs with integrated environmental control measures could address the dual challenges of resistance and SSIs.

The findings of this review highlighted knowledge gaps and potential research opportunities. Combining environmental interventions with improved clinical practices, including preoperative skin preparation protocols that integrate environmental hygiene measures, such as optimized ventilation and humidity control, could enhance patient safety. There should be research on post-discharge infection prevention programs and the establishment of home-based hygiene education and follow-up visits to reduce SSI rates. Research has shown that patient education effectively reduces SSIs, and integrating mobile health (mHealth) interventions was also effective with high patient satisfaction.71 Another study from the USA also showed that home healthcare significantly reduced SSI rates.72 Rwanda should prioritize strengthening SSI surveillance systems and expanding research on infection control adherence, particularly in relation to WHO guidelines. Cross-country collaborations could facilitate standardized data collection, enabling more accurate comparisons of intervention outcomes across East Africa.73 Additionally, future research should integrate environmental sampling with clinical outcome tracking to establish stronger causal links between environmental factors and SSI incidence. Such an approach would enhance the development of targeted, evidence-based interventions to reduce SSI rates in the region.74 In Pakistan and Kenya, studies demonstrated the feasibility of innovative solutions, such as solar-powered ventilation systems and low-cost disinfectants,75,76 which could be adapted for Rwanda. Moreover, there should be studies exploring the influence of ventilation, temperature, and humidity on the incidence of SSIs in healthcare facilities to bridge available knowledge gaps in this area and inform measures aimed at addressing environmental factors increasing risk of SSIs in Rwanda. To achieve this, multisectoral approaches can be adapted and complemented by training programs focusing on environmental IPC measures, combined with regular audits that could strengthen infection control systems. Since some studies reported on bacterial prevalence without operating room environment factors, targeted studies on bacterial prevalence in operating rooms, focusing on temperature, air quality, humidity, and adherence to infection control protocols, are highly recommended. In addition, integrating environmental sampling and microbiological testing to identify and mitigate environmental contributors to antibiotic resistance is needed to bridge identified gaps. The reliance on traditional antiseptic protocols limits the exploration of newer, potentially more effective strategies for infection control. Thus, there should be studies to evaluate innovative interventions, such as advanced antimicrobial coatings, UV disinfection systems, and AI-based monitoring of infection risks. Finally, expanding multivariate models to include environmental variables such as room humidity, temperature, and microbial load on surgical instruments and surfaces would provide robust evidence that could inform policies to curb SSIs in Rwanda.

Limitations and Strengths

This scoping review has limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the availability of studies directly examining environmental factors influencing SSIs in Rwandan healthcare settings and the selected countries was limited. This indicates that there is a gap and reliance on global studies for comparative analysis, which may not fully capture the unique challenges faced in Rwanda, and East Africa in general. Second, the included studies were heterogeneous in design, population, and focus, making direct comparisons and generalizations challenging. This diversity, while enriching the review, also complicates the synthesis of findings. Additionally, the restriction to studies published in English may have resulted in the exclusion of relevant literature available in other languages, potentially limiting the comprehensiveness of the review. Lastly, the focus on published literature may have overlooked valuable unpublished or inaccessible data, particularly from local Rwandan institutions, which could have provided additional context-specific insights.

Despite these limitations, this review has notable strengths that enhance its contribution to the field. By including studies addressing various environmental factors. Discussing findings while comparing them with global literature provides a holistic understanding of the topic, and its focus on Rwandan healthcare settings also makes the findings particularly valuable in the Rwandan context and for low-resource countries facing similar challenges. This review’s approach enabled the provision of nuanced insights into the interplay between environmental factors and SSIs as well as the identification of critical gaps and practical solutions, emphasising on actionable recommendations tailored to Rwanda, bridging gaps between global best practices and local feasibility.

Conclusion

This scoping review explored the critical role of environmental factors in influencing SSIs in abdominal surgeries within Rwandan healthcare settings. Despite the recognized importance of these factors, significant gaps persist. Comparison with global literature underscores that while challenges in Rwanda are shared by many resource-limited settings, the solutions must be context-specific. While Rwanda faces challenges related to infrastructure, staffing, and resources, evidence from other countries highlights opportunities for improvement. Our findings demonstrates that while certain challenges like hospital contamination and resource limitations are widespread across East Africa, each country faces unique environmental risk profiles for SSIs. Rwanda’s particular struggles with overcrowding and surveillance, Tanzania’s post-discharge hygiene concerns, and Kenya’s surface contamination issues all require tailored intervention strategies. These country-specific differences, occurring within a framework of shared regional challenges, highlight the importance of developing both standardized and localized approaches to SSI prevention in East African healthcare systems. Tailored Interventions for SSI prevention should focus on strengthening infrastructure, improving cleaning protocols, addressing antibiotic resistance, and integrating environmental and clinical practices. Prioritizing affordable technologies for temperature and humidity regulation and basic ventilation improvements is also crucial, along with standardized cleaning procedures, training, and supervision. Comprehensive antibiotic stewardship programs addressing environmental factors within healthcare settings are needed to combat resistant pathogens. Addressing the environmental factors influencing SSIs offers a significant opportunity to enhance surgical outcomes and improve patient safety in Rwanda. By leveraging global best practices, investing in targeted interventions, and fostering collaboration, the Rwandan healthcare system can make meaningful achievements in reducing the burden of SSIs. Critical climate-related and infrastructural determinants of SSIs remain understudied in the region Future research should focus on addressing identified gaps, particularly in studying the impact of temperature control, ventilation, and humidity regulation on SSIs, to develop comprehensive, evidence-based strategies for infection prevention.
