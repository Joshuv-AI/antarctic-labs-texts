Central and peripheral glutamatergic biomarkers delineate heterogeneous clinical phenotypes in olanzapine-treated schizophrenia
Authors: Wirginia Krzyściak, Maciej Pilecki, Beata Bystrowska, Marta Szwajca, Natalia Śmierciak, Aleksander Turek, Paulina Karcz, Amira Bryll, Paulina Mazur, Ewa Gomółka, Tadeusz Popiela
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to identify central and peripheral glutamatergic biomarkers underlying heterogeneous clinical phenotypes in olanzapine-treated patients with schizophrenia, with the goal of informing biomarker-guided personalized therapy. We conducted a cross-sectional observational study of 51 adults with schizophrenia receiving chronic olanzapine treatment (mean duration = 5.6 years), integrating clinical assessments with peripheral neurochemical profiling and magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) of the anterior and posterior cingulate cortices. Partial correlations controlling for age, sex, and treatment duration revealed significant associations: higher serum serotonin correlated with more severe negative symptoms (ρ = 0.32, p = 0.029), and increased cingulate Glx ratios were linked to greater depressive burden (ρ = 0.31, p = 0.031). Elevated serum glutamic acid was associated with overall psychopathology, whereas lower serum and cingulate glutamine levels were related to more severe symptoms and higher olanzapine exposure. Hierarchical clustering on principal components (HCPC) identified three phenotypic subgroups: (1) low-symptom, dose-efficient responders; (2) high-symptom, dose-resistant patients with cingulate hypoglutamatergia; and (3) younger individuals with elevated Glx ratios and moderate exposure, suggestive of a hyperglutamatergic, neurodevelopmental profile. These findings demonstrate that central and peripheral glutamatergic markers delineate distinct clinical phenotypes of olanzapine response in schizophrenia, shaped by both neurochemical and demographic factors. Stratification based on glutamatergic profiles and symptom patterns may contribute to the development of precision psychiatry approaches. To our knowledge, this is the first study to integrate central and peripheral glutamatergic biomarkers with multivariate phenotyping in a naturalistic olanzapine-treated cohort, identifying (i) a low-symptom, low-exposure phenotype, (ii) a high-symptom, high-exposure phenotype characterised by reduced cingulate Glx, and (iii) a younger phenotype with elevated cingulate Glx ratios, consistent with, though not confirmatory of, a neurodevelopmental trajectory. Introduction

Cognitive and affective dysfunctions are core features of schizophrenia and are often resistant to standard pharmacological interventions, particularly in chronic populations1. While second-generation antipsychotics (SGAs) such as olanzapine are widely used due to their favorable side-effect profiles compared to first-generation antipsychotics (FGAs)2, their efficacy in addressing the broader neuropsychiatric burden – including negative symptoms, depressive comorbidity, and cognitive impairment – remains only partially understood3–5. Evidence from adult populations suggests that SGAs may modulate certain cognitive and affective domains, potentially through serotonergic and glutamatergic pathways, yet these effects are often subtle, heterogeneous, and difficult to generalize across patient subgroups6–9.

Recent research has underscored the need for biomarker-guided personalization of antipsychotic therapy, especially in treatment-resistant or partially responsive cases10. Central neurochemical dysregulation, particularly within glutamatergic circuits of the anterior and posterior cingulate cortices, has been implicated in the persistence of negative and affective symptoms, while peripheral markers such as serum serotonin and glutamate-related metabolites may reflect systemic neurochemical imbalances associated with illness severity and pharmacodynamic variability11–13. However, the extent to which these central and peripheral neurochemical profiles are associated with clinical outcomes during long-term olanzapine treatment remains insufficiently characterized. Moreover, although peripheral biomarkers are associated with illness severity and pharmacodynamic variability, their relationship to central neurotransmission remains indirect and incompletely understood. In parallel, alterations in central neurochemical profiles—particularly within glutamatergic circuits of the anterior and posterior cingulate cortices, as assessed by proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy (¹H-MRS)—have been linked to the persistence of negative and affective symptoms.

The current study aims to elucidate the neurochemical underpinnings of clinical heterogeneity in a well-characterized cohort of schizophrenia patients undergoing chronic olanzapine therapy. Specifically, we investigate (1) the demographic, clinical, and neurochemical characteristics of the cohort; (2) partial correlations between peripheral and central neurochemical markers and symptom domains; and (3) data-driven phenotyping using hierarchical clustering of multimodal biomarkers. This multidimensional approach aims to identify distinct neurobiological response profiles to olanzapine and to characterise the neurochemical correlates of clinical heterogeneity, with implications for treatment stratification and precision psychiatry in schizophrenia. It further seeks to identify neurobiological profiles associated with olanzapine exposure, with potential implications for future treatment stratification research.

Results

Characteristics of the studied cohort

The baseline characteristics of a cohort of 51 patients with schizophrenia receiving olanzapine treatment were analyzed to elucidate demographic, clinical, and neurochemical profiles that may inform personalized therapeutic strategies. As summarized in Table 1, the cohort exhibited a balanced sex distribution, with 56.9% male (n = 29) and 43.1% female (n = 22), and a mean age of 27.7 years (SD = 9.4). Olanzapine serum concentrations displayed a median of 4.35 µg/L (interquartile range: 4.22–14.77 µg/L), with maximum values reaching 365 µg/L, indicating significant variability in drug exposure. The mean duration of olanzapine treatment was 5.6 years (SD = 5.9), reflecting a chronic treatment population.

Clinical symptom severity, assessed via the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS), Calgary Depression Scale for Schizophrenia (CDSS), and the Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II), revealed substantial heterogeneity. The mean PANSS negative symptoms score was 21.2 (SD = 6.6, range: 2.0–33.0), with a similar negative subscale sum (mean = 21.4, SD = 6.6, range: 8.0–34.0), indicating moderate to severe negative symptomatology. The total PANSS score averaged 79.9 (SD = 20.9, range: 37.0–126.0), reflecting a broad spectrum of overall psychopathology. Depressive symptoms were moderate, with mean CDSS and BDI-II scores of 8.5 (SD = 6.2, range: 0.0–22.0) and 18.7 (SD = 13.5, range: 0.0–51.0), respectively, revealing variable affective burden.

Peripheral neurochemical markers further highlighted cohort diversity. Mean serum serotonin concentration was 130.1 ng/mL (SD = 21.7, range: 4.0–176.5), while glutamic acid and glutamine concentrations averaged 2,711.4 µg/mL (SD = 1,733.9, range: 537.9–8,871.9) and 342.6 µg/mL (SD = 66.6, range: 87.6–476.8), respectively, indicating significant variability in excitatory neurotransmitter levels. Magnetic Resonance Spectroscopy (MRS) ratios in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) and posterior cingulate cortex (PCC) at echo time (TE) = 30 ms provided insights into central neurochemical profiles. In the ACC, mean ratios included glutamine/(Cr + PCr) at 0.6 (SD = 0.1, range: 0.3–1.0), glutamate/(Cr + PCr) at 1.6 (SD = 0.2, range: 1.2–2.4), myo-inositol/(Cr + PCr) at 0.9 (SD = 0.1, range: 0.7–1.1), and composite glutamate + glutamine (Glx)/(Cr + PCr) at 2.2 (SD = 0.3, range: 1.8–3.0). Similar patterns were observed in the PCC, with glutamine/(Cr + PCr) at 0.6 (SD = 0.1, range: 0.4–1.0), glutamate/(Cr + PCr) at 1.6 (SD = 0.2, range: 1.1–1.8), myo-inositol/(Cr + PCr) at 0.9 (SD = 0.1, range: 0.7–1.1), and (glutamate + glutamine) (Glx)/(Cr + PCr) at 2.2 (SD = 0.2, range: 1.8–2.7). These findings indicate relatively stable central neurochemical ratios despite wide peripheral variability, reflecting significant inter-individual variability in peripheral amino acid concentrations.

Among the findings, a significant positive correlation was observed between the PANSS Negative Subscale (Items N1–N7) score and serum serotonin concentration (partial correlation = 0.32, p = 0.029; 95% CI: 0.04–0.54). This suggests that higher serotonin levels are associated with greater severity of negative symptoms, consistent with the proposed role of serotonergic dysregulation in treatment response. Similarly, the BDI-II total score showed a significant positive correlation with the Glx ratio in the ACC (partial correlation = 0.31, p = 0.031; 95% CI: 0.04–0.54), indicating that elevated ACC Glx levels may be associated with greater depressive symptom burden, in line with prior reports linking glutamatergic dysregulation to affective symptoms. Both associations correspond to medium effect sizes according to Cohen’s (1988) benchmarks14(|ρ| = 0.24–0.36), suggesting that serum serotonin and ACC Glx may each account for approximately 10% of the residual variance in their respective clinical outcomes after controlling for demographic and treatment-related confounders.

The PANSS Total Score were positively correlated with serum glutamic acid (partial r = 0.29, p = 0.049, 95% CI: 0.01–0.52) and negatively correlated with serum glutamine (partial r = − 0.29, p = 0.045, 95% CI: − 0.52 to − 0.02). These findings suggest that higher glutamic acid levels may worsen psychopathology, while lower glutamine levels may indicate impaired glutamate–glutamine cycling, both potentially affecting olanzapine’s efficacy. Moreover, higher serum olanzapine levels correlated negatively with the ACC glutamine/(Cr + PCr) ratio (partial r = − 0.30, p = 0.037, 95% CI: − 0.53 to − 0.03), implying a dose-dependent reduction in ACC glutamine. These findings indicate that higher glutamic acid levels are associated with greater overall psychopathology, whereas lower glutamine levels accompany more severe symptom presentations – a pattern that may reflect altered glutamate–glutamine cycling; however, the cross-sectional design precludes directional inference, and this association is consistent with lower ACC glutamine levels at higher olanzapine exposure. The partial correlations for PANSS Total Score with serum glutamic acid (ρ = 0.29) and serum glutamine (ρ = −0.29) likewise fall within the medium effect size range, as does the olanzapine exposure–ACC glutamine association (ρ = −0.30).

Near-significant associations were observed for several biomarker–clinical relationships. A positive trend was noted between the PANSS Negative Symptoms Score and serum serotonin (partial correlation = 0.25, p = 0.086, 95% CI: −0.03 to 0.49). The PANSS Negative Subscale (Items N1–N7 total score) showed inverse associations with both serum glutamine (partial correlation = − 0.27, p = 0.066, 95% CI: −0.51 to 0.01) and ACC glutamate (partial correlation = − 0.26, p = 0.075, 95% CI: −0.50 to 0.02). Additional trends included associations between the PANSS Total Score and serotonin (partial correlation = 0.26, p = 0.070, 95% CI: −0.01 to 0.50), the BDI-II Total Score and ACC glutamate (partial correlation = 0.28, p = 0.053, 95% CI: −0.01 to 0.52), and the proportion of patients with elevated olanzapine concentrations and serum serotonin (partial correlation = 0.26, p = 0.079, 95% CI: −0.02 to 0.50). These near-significant associations (ρ = 0.25–0.28) fall at the lower boundary of the medium effect size range and are consistent with biologically plausible effects whose statistical detection may be constrained by the present sample size (N = 51).

It is imperative to note that the present partial correlation analysis was explicitly exploratory in design, encompassing 77 individual tests (7 clinical outcomes × 11 biomarker candidates) without a priori directional hypotheses. Application of the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction across all 77 tests yielded adjusted q-values exceeding 0.05 for all reported associations (minimum q = 0.086; Supplementary Table S1). Accordingly, none of the observed correlations attained statistical significance following correction for multiple comparisons. These findings should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating observations that warrant independent replication in adequately powered confirmatory studies. The minimum adjusted q-value observed was 0.550.

Sensitivity analysis: Serum olanzapine concentration as a continuous predictor

To evaluate whether the dichotomisation of olanzapine serum concentrations at the cohort median (4.35 µg/L) may have obscured continuous dose–response relationships, a supplementary sensitivity analysis was conducted by treating olanzapine concentration as a continuous variable. Partial Spearman correlations, controlling for age, sex, and duration of olanzapine treatment, revealed no statistically significant associations between continuous olanzapine concentration and any biomarker candidate (all p > 0.10) or clinical outcome measure (all p > 0.27). The strongest individual association was observed between continuous olanzapine concentration and serum serotonin (partial ρ = 0.24, p = 0.10).

The absence of significant continuous associations may be partly attributable to the heavily right-skewed distribution of olanzapine concentrations in this cohort (median = 4.35, mean = 24.86, SD = 63.04 µg/L, range: 0–365 µg/L), which compresses most observations within a narrow low-concentration range. These results are reported in full in Supplementary Table S2 and indicate that the binary exposure classification, while entailing the well-known information loss inherent to dichotomisation15, captures a clinically relevant threshold distinction that the continuous measure, given the present distributional characteristics, may not adequately resolve.

Cluster-based phenotyping of olanzapine response profiles in schizophrenia

The conducted hierarchical clustering on principal components (HCPC) procedure yielded three clusters: Cluster 1 (n = 11, 21.6% of the cohort), Cluster 2 (n = 22, 43.1%), and Cluster 3 (n = 18, 35.3%). These clusters were characterized by differential patterns across clinical, demographic, and neurochemical domains, revealing heterogeneous responses to olanzapine that may inform biomarker-guided personalization of antipsychotic therapy (see Fig. 1).

As shown in Supplementary Table S3, PANSS negative symptoms and total scores exhibited the strongest associations (η² > 0.53, p < 0.001), followed by composite MRS Glx ratios in the PCC and ACC (η² = 0.41–0.50, p < 0.001). Weaker but significant associations were observed for individual MRS glutamate and glutamine ratios, age, CDSS depression scores, and the binary olanzapine exposure indicator (η² = 0.12–0.34, p < 0.05). These findings underscore the multidimensional nature of olanzapine response profiles, with clinical symptom severity and cingulate Glx dysregulation emerging as the primary discriminants.

Cluster validation results: The three-cluster solution accounted for 21.9% of the total variance (between-cluster/total inertia ratio = 0.219), with the inertia gain at successive partitions exhibiting a diminishing returns pattern (k = 2: 11.8%; k = 3: 10.1%; k = 4: 7.0%), supporting the three-cluster partition as the optimal solution. Bootstrap stability assessment (1,000 resamples) yielded a mean adjusted Rand index (ARI) of 0.42 (95% CI: −0.02 to 0.89), with 50.8% of replicates exceeding ARI = 0.40 (moderate agreement) and 21.2% exceeding ARI = 0.60 (substantial agreement). The average silhouette width was 0.11, which is low by conventional geometric separability standards but expected, given that Ward’s linkage optimizes within-cluster inertia rather than silhouette width, and that clinical phenotypes typically occupy overlapping regions in multivariate space.

Cluster-specific profiles are presented in Supplementary Tables S4–S6, which summarize key metrics for Clusters 1–3 and support the interpretation of phenotype–clinical associations. The clusters delineate heterogeneous patient profiles and highlight distinct links between serum olanzapine levels (above versus below the median of 4.35 µg/L) and key clinical, demographic, and neurochemical variables. These patterns suggest differences in pharmacodynamic efficiency and potential mechanisms underlying variability in olanzapine response, thereby informing dose optimization strategies in schizophrenia treatment. Overall, these cross-sectional patterns delineate phenotypic variation across clinical, demographic, and neurochemical domains and may serve as a basis for generating hypotheses regarding heterogeneity in olanzapine dose–response relationships.

Cluster 1: Low-symptom, low-exposure (dose-efficient) phenotype (n = 11)

This subgroup exemplifies therapeutic efficiency at submedian olanzapine exposure, with only 18% of patients surpassing the 4.35 µg/L threshold (versus 51% overall, v-test = −2.43, p = 0.007) and is strongly associated with attenuated clinical symptomatology. Notably, PANSS negative symptoms (mean 11.91 versus overall 21.22, v-test = −5.26, p < 0.001) and total scores (50.91 versus 79.88, v-test = −5.19, p < 0.001) are markedly lower, alongside diminished depressive indices (CDSS: 3.55 versus 8.54, v-test = −3.01, p = 0.001; BDI-II: 10.09 versus 18.74, v-test = −2.39, p = 0.008). Demographically, these patients tend to be older (mean age 33.18 years versus 27.71, v-test = 2.19, p = 0.014) and predominantly female (27% male versus 57% overall, v-test = −2.22, p = 0.013), patterns that may influence olanzapine pharmacokinetics through altered CYP-mediated metabolism or receptor sensitivity. Peripheral neurochemical concentrations (e.g., serotonin, glutamic acid, glutamine) and individual MRS ratios that did not show significant deviations were comparable to cohort means, indicating that the phenotype’s distinctiveness arises primarily from clinical remission and demographic factors rather than peripheral or neurochemical perturbations. Overall, this phenotype is characterised by low symptom severity at submedian olanzapine exposure, in conjunction with demographic features previously associated with altered olanzapine pharmacokinetics.

Cluster 2: High-symptom, high-exposure, dose-resistant phenotype with cingulate hypoglutamatergia (n = 22)

As the largest subgroup, Cluster 2 represents the modal phenotype and reveals a paradoxical pattern in which supra-median olanzapine exposure predominates (68% above 4.35 µg/L versus 51% overall, v-test = 2.12, p = 0.017), yet does not translate into clinical improvement, suggesting potential pharmacoresistance. Elevated PANSS negative symptoms (24.73 versus 21.22, v-test = 3.30, p < 0.001) and total scores (91.45 versus 79.88, v-test = 3.45, p < 0.001), together with increased depressive severity (CDSS: 11.30 versus 8.54, v-test = 2.76, p = 0.003), co-occur with a male predominance (77% versus 57% overall, v-test = 2.54, p = 0.006), a demographic factor previously associated with slower olanzapine clearance and greater negative symptom burden.

Importantly, this high-exposure subgroup is characterised by cingulate hypoglutamatergia, with reduced composite Glx ratios (ACC: 2.07 versus 2.21, v-test = −3.33, p < 0.001; PCC: 2.07 versus 2.20, v-test = −4.13, p < 0.001), alongside reductions in both glutamate (ACC: 1.53 versus 1.62, v-test = −3.02, p = 0.001; PCC: 1.49 versus 1.56, v-test = −2.70, p = 0.003) and glutamine (p ≤ 0.001).

In contrast, variables such as treatment duration, serum serotonin levels, and other peripheral neurochemical markers did not differ significantly from cohort means, indicating that this phenotype is primarily driven by central glutamatergic alterations and persistent symptomatology rather than by treatment duration or peripheral factors. Such neurochemical attenuation, potentially exacerbated by chronic olanzapine blockade of NMDA–glutamate interactions, may contribute to the persistence of negative and affective symptoms despite adequate dosing, in line with eta-squared values indicating moderate cluster discrimination by Glx metrics (η² = 0.41–0.50). This pattern – defined by reduced cingulate Glx ratios co-occurring with severe psychopathology – may be consistent with pharmacoresistance; however, the cross-sectional design precludes causal or directional inference.

Whether the observed reduction in Glx reflects a consequence of prolonged antipsychotic exposure, a pre-existing neurobiological vulnerability, or a combination of both cannot be determined from the present data. Overall, these findings align with previous literature implicating glutamatergic dysregulation in treatment-resistant schizophrenia.

2.3.3. Cluster 3: Younger hyperglutamatergic phenotype with elevated cingulate Glx and neutral olanzapine exposure (n = 18)

In contrast to the dose extremes observed in other clusters, Cluster 3 is characterised by neutral olanzapine exposure, with no significant deviation from the cohort mean proportion exceeding 4.35 µg/L (η² = 0.14 overall, p = 0.024, although this variable was not discriminatory within this cluster), and is primarily defined by pronounced cingulate glutamatergic hyperactivity that may influence early treatment responsiveness. Elevated Glx ratios prevail (ACC: 2.44 versus 2.21, v-test = 4.49, p < 0.001; PCC: 2.37 versus 2.20, v-test = 4.79, p < 0.001), driven by increases in both glutamate (ACC: 1.78 versus 1.62, v-test = 4.11, p < 0.001; PCC: 1.64 versus 1.56, v-test = 2.86, p = 0.002) and glutamine (ACC: 0.66 versus 0.59, v-test = 2.75, p = 0.003; PCC: 0.74 versus 0.64, v-test = 3.78, p < 0.001), in conjunction with younger age (21.17 years versus 27.71, v-test = −3.69, p < 0.001).

Importantly, clinical symptom scores (e.g., PANSS, CDSS, BDI-II), treatment duration, and peripheral neurochemical concentrations that did not reach statistical significance were comparable to cohort means, indicating that this phenotype reflects a neurodevelopmental glutamatergic imbalance independent of overt psychopathology or dosing variation at the cohort median.

In the absence of overt symptom exacerbation, this hyperglutamatergic state – potentially not fully mitigated by standard olanzapine doses due to incomplete receptor adaptation – suggests a developmental window in which increased excitatory activity within frontocingulate networks precedes full syndromal expression. Olanzapine concentrations at median levels appear insufficient to normalize glutamatergic activity, as reflected by persistently elevated cingulate Glx ratios. This pattern, observed in younger individuals without marked clinical severity, may indicate a neurodevelopmental glutamatergic imbalance; however, the cross-sectional design precludes causal or temporal inference.

Overall, this phenotype is characterised by elevated glutamatergic metabolites co-occurring with younger age, independently of overt psychopathology or variations in olanzapine exposure, with median drug concentrations not associated with reduced Glx levels in this subgroup.

Discussion

Partial correlation analysis of clinical and neurochemical associations in olanzapine-treated schizophrenia

The significant positive correlation between serum serotonin levels and the severity of negative symptoms (PANSS N1–N7) in olanzapine-treated patients suggests that higher peripheral serotonin is associated with more pronounced negative symptoms. This counterintuitive finding may reflect impaired central serotonergic processing, whereby reduced serotonin transporter (SERT) availability or altered monoamine oxidase A (MAO-A) expression results in elevated peripheral serotonin without corresponding effective central neurotransmission16. Such a pattern has been interpreted in the literature as reflecting a dissociation between peripheral and central serotonergic compartments, in which altered SERT availability or MAO-A expression may coexist with increased peripheral serotonin without necessarily indicating functional central serotonergic activity.

Olanzapine, known for its high affinity antagonism at 5-HT₂A and 5-HT₆ receptors, may further modulate serotonergic tone. Positron Emission Tomography (PET) studies have demonstrated that even low doses of olanzapine (e.g., 5 mg/day) achieve substantial 5-HT₂A receptor occupancy in vivo17, which could interfere with serotonin-mediated cortical excitation. Moreover, olanzapine has been shown to enhance extracellular serotonin levels when co-administered with selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) such as fluoxetine or citalopram, possibly via disinhibition of serotonergic neurons through 5-HT₆ receptor antagonism in the dorsal raphe nucleus18. These interactions underscore the complexity of serotonergic modulation under antipsychotic treatment and may explain why higher peripheral serotonin is paradoxically associated with negative symptom severity – an effect potentially driven by central serotonergic dysregulation rather than peripheral hyperfunction. This pattern, while not mechanistically conclusive, is consistent with central serotonergic dysregulation rather than peripheral hyperfunction.

Similarly, the positive correlation between depressive symptom severity (BDI-II total score) and Glx levels in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) supports a growing body of evidence linking glutamatergic hyperactivity with affective symptoms in schizophrenia. Elevated Glx - representing the combined signal of glutamate and glutamine in Proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy (1H-MRS) - has been consistently reported in patients with major depressive disorder, particularly in treatment-resistant subgroups19. The increased Glx levels observed in the present study may reflect either excessive synaptic glutamate or a compensatory increase in glutamine synthesis by astrocytes, aimed at buffering excitotoxicity20. Persistent elevations of both glutamate and glutamine may indicate disruption in the glutamate–glutamine cycle or prolonged activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, both of which have been implicated in the pathophysiology of mood dysregulation. The elevated Glx observed in the present study is consistent with either increased glutamate availability or a compensatory increase in glutamine synthesis, both of which have been associated in prior research with disruption of the glutamate–glutamine cycle and activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, and are hypothesised to contribute to affective dysregulation.

Collectively, the associations of increased peripheral serotonin with negative symptoms and elevated central Glx with depressive symptoms suggest a shared disruption of monoaminergic and glutamatergic pathways in schizophrenia, particularly under chronic olanzapine treatment. These findings emphasize the relevance of biomarker-informed phenotyping, and support further research into neurochemical stratification of patients to optimize treatment approaches. Notably, although none of these associations attained statistical significance after FDR correction (see Sect. 2.2 and Supplementary Table S1), the observed partial correlations (ρ = 0.25–0.32) uniformly fall within the medium effect size range14. Effect sizes of this magnitude are consistent with neurochemical–clinical associations reported in methodologically comparable MRS studies of schizophrenia21,22 and their consistency across multiple independent biomarker–symptom pairs reduces the likelihood that they reflect chance findings alone. The combination of medium effect sizes, theoretically grounded directionality, and accompanying 95% confidence intervals provides a more informative basis for interpreting these exploratory associations than reliance on corrected p-values alone23.

Peripheral biomarker caveat

An important interpretive caveat concerns the indirect nature of peripheral neurochemical markers as indices of central neurotransmission. The blood–brain barrier maintains a steep glutamate concentration gradient (plasma: 50–100 µmol/L vs. brain extracellular fluid: 0.5–2 µmol/L), and its endothelial architecture is configured to expel rather than admit glutamate24. Serum glutamate concentrations are largely determined by peripheral metabolic sources – including hepatic transamination, skeletal muscle catabolism, and dietary protein intake – that do not necessarily reflect synaptic glutamate release or MRS-detectable Glx25. Likewise, serum serotonin originates predominantly from enterochromaffin cells and platelet-dense granules and does not cross the intact blood–brain barrier26. The peripheral–clinical correlations reported herein should therefore not be construed as evidence of central neurochemical mechanisms, but rather as reflecting parallel patterns of dysregulation across compartments that warrant further investigation using paired CSF–serum sampling or PET radioligand approaches. It is important to emphasise that the parallel assessment of both peripheral and MRS-derived metabolites in the present study was intended to evaluate the degree of concordance or discordance between compartments, rather than to treat one as a proxy for the other.

Prior antipsychotic exposure caveat

A further interpretive consideration pertains to the potential influence of prior antipsychotic exposure on the neurochemical profiles observed in this cohort. Although all participants met the criterion of olanzapine monotherapy for ≥ 6 months, the TDM panel detected residual plasma concentrations of other antipsychotics in a majority of patients (Supplementary Table S7), likely reflecting previous treatment exposure. Prior antipsychotic exposure has been demonstrated to exert lasting effects on glutamatergic and serotonergic systems: longitudinal MRS studies have documented persistent alterations in ACC glutamate following antipsychotic switching22 and cumulative antipsychotic exposure has been associated with progressive neurochemical changes that may not fully reverse upon discontinuation27. The absence of detailed antipsychotic treatment histories in the present study precludes a clear distinction between olanzapine-specific neurochemical effects and residual influences of prior pharmacotherapy, and this constitutes a notable limitation.

Multicenter studies using proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy (¹H-MRS) have demonstrated that patients with schizophrenia - particularly in the early stages of the illness or during acute psychotic episodes - exhibit elevated glutamate levels in brain regions such as the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) and the hippocampus28.

An increased concentration of glutamate in serum may also reflect excessive glutamatergic activity in the brain, potentially leading to excitotoxicity, oxidative stress, and worsening of psychopathological symptoms. The observed positive correlation between serum glutamate levels and the PANSS total score suggests that glutamate excess may contribute to the severity of both positive and negative symptoms. The observed positive association between serum glutamate concentration and the PANSS total score is consistent with prior reports linking elevated peripheral glutamate levels to greater psychopathological burden in schizophrenia. However, the cross-sectional nature of the present data precludes determining whether elevated serum glutamate reflects, contributes to, or merely covaries with symptom severity.

In our previous studies, we observed that patients with acute psychotic decompensation showed reduced glutamate and glutamine levels in the anterior cingulate cortex, likely reflecting a downstream consequence of earlier excitotoxic and oxidative processes. These neurochemical alterations, correlated with markers of oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction, supported a model of progressive glutamatergic imbalance in schizophrenia. The current finding of elevated serum glutamate and its association with PANSS total score extends this pattern, suggesting that peripheral glutamate excess may mirror central glutamatergic dysregulation contributing to symptom severity29. This observation raises the possibility that peripheral glutamate alterations may parallel, though not necessarily reflect, central glutamatergic changes associated with symptom severity, and is consistent with prior interpretations implicating excitotoxic and oxidative processes.

Consistent with this interpretation, glutamine plays a buffering role in the glutamate–glutamine cycle, mediating glutamate transport and recycling between neurons and astrocytes. Reduced serum glutamine levels may therefore reflect impaired astrocytic glutamine synthetase activity, leading to disrupted glutamate clearance, enhanced excitatory transmission, and worsening of clinical symptoms30. This interpretation is supported by prior literature suggesting that reduced serum glutamine may reflect compromised astrocytic glutamine synthetase function, which in turn may be associated with altered glutamate clearance dynamics; however, such mechanistic inferences cannot be drawn from the present cross-sectional data.

Glutamatergic modulation and clinical response clusters in olanzapine-treated schizophrenia

The critical involvement of glutamatergic pathways in the pathophysiology of schizophrenia, as well as their potential role in mediating the therapeutic effects of antipsychotic treatment, has been increasingly recognized. Although olanzapine primarily acts as an antagonist of dopaminergic and serotonergic receptors, it also exerts modulatory effects on the glutamatergic system. Preclinical studies suggest that olanzapine can alter glutamate and glutamine concentrations in the brain31.

Therefore, the observed correlations may reflect not only underlying pathophysiological mechanisms in schizophrenia but also represent potential biomarkers of response to olanzapine treatment, with implications for precision psychiatry. The findings of a positive correlation between serum glutamate and PANSS total score, alongside a negative correlation with serum glutamine, align with current scientific understanding. These markers may be considered candidate biomarkers warranting prospective evaluation as predictors of clinical variability under olanzapine treatment, with exploratory relevance for precision psychiatry.

Cluster 1: Low-symptom, low-exposure (dose-efficient) phenotype (n = 11).

This cluster comprised predominantly older female patients who achieved clinical remission at submedian olanzapine exposure. The inverse relationship between low drug levels and symptom remission suggests a “dose-sparing” phenotype, in which peripheral monitoring could support individualized dose reduction to minimize metabolic and sedative side effects without compromising efficacy. Similar findings by Zabala et al. (2017) also described patients maintaining stability at low olanzapine concentrations, reinforcing this low-exposure efficacy profile32. In contrast, Hiemke et al. (2018) emphasized that concentrations below approximately 20 ng/mL are generally associated with diminished therapeutic response, underscoring the importance of individualized interpretation of therapeutic reference ranges33 In the present study, a subgroup of patients exhibited low symptom severity despite submedian olanzapine exposure. The co-occurrence of low drug levels and low symptom burden delineates a “low-exposure, low-symptom” phenotype.

This phenotype is further supported by several case reports and large-scale studies highlighting the potential for clinical efficacy at subtherapeutic or low-dose olanzapine exposure, particularly in older adults and women. Kurozumi et al. described a case of a 70-year-old woman with very-late-onset schizophrenia-like psychosis (VLOSLP), who achieved sustained remission for over two years on only 2.5 mg/day of olanzapine, despite an initial relapse following treatment discontinuation34. The authors emphasized that age-related pharmacokinetic changes, such as reduced clearance and slower metabolism, may result in higher drug exposure at lower doses in elderly patients, especially females.

Real-world data from Kang et al. (2025), analyzing 9,565 olanzapine-treated patients, showed that 17% received doses ≤ 1.25 mg/day, which were associated with a significantly lower risk of metabolic side effects (e.g., diabetes, dyslipidemia) without compromising clinical efficacy in older patients, predominantly women35. These findings support the emerging need for personalized, low-dose strategies and the availability of lower-dose formulations.

Similarly, Graff-Guerrero et al. (2015), using PET imaging, demonstrated that gradual dose reduction in stable patients aged ≥ 50 years lowered D2/3 receptor occupancy to approximately 64%, improving tolerability (e.g., EPS, hyperprolactinemia) without loss of clinical stability36. The authors proposed a lower therapeutic window (50–60%) for D2/3 occupancy in late-life schizophrenia (LLS), reinforcing the rationale for individualized dose optimization.

Pharmacokinetic studies also confirm these trends. Solhaug et al. (2025), in a TDM analysis of 19,926 patients, reported a significant age-related increase in the olanzapine concentration-to-dose (C: D) ratio, most pronounced in elderly women37. Castberg et al. (2017), analyzing over 43,000 TDM samples, showed an exponential rise in C: D with age, with women over 80 reaching plasma levels two to three times higher than younger adults at equivalent doses. Women also exhibited 25–30% higher concentrations than men at the same dose, highlighting increased sensitivity in this group38.

Taken together, these findings strengthen the concept of a “low-exposure efficacy phenotype” and suggest that therapeutic targets for older adults, especially women, may be achieved at substantially lower doses. This underscores the clinical utility of TDM and age- and sex-informed dosing strategies to optimize efficacy while minimizing adverse effects, and is consistent with the low-symptom, low-exposure phenotype identified in the present study. These findings indicate that older adults, particularly women, may achieve comparable clinical outcomes at substantially lower doses.

This phenotype further underscores olanzapine’s ability to promote neuroplasticity at lower plasma thresholds, potentially through enhanced 5-HT₂A receptor antagonism. It supports the need for prospective studies to validate submedian concentrations as reliable predictors of sustained remission. In clinical practice, identifying such low-exposure responders through baseline PANSS assessments combined with therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) could enable early dose de-escalation. This approach not only fosters long-term adherence by minimizing adverse effects but also reduces the clinical and economic burdens associated with polypharmacy, particularly in vulnerable populations such as elderly women. However, whether this low-exposure, low-symptom pattern reflects a pharmacological effect of olanzapine at lower plasma concentrations, pre-existing favourable illness characteristics, or a combination of both cannot be determined from the present cross-sectional design. Prospective longitudinal studies are therefore warranted to evaluate whether submedian concentrations reliably predict sustained clinical stability.

Cluster 2: High-symptom, high-exposure, dose-resistant phenotype with cingulate hypoglutamatergia (n = 22).

Cluster 2 comprised high-symptom, high-exposure, dose-resistant individuals with cingulate hypoglutamatergia despite high olanzapine levels, indicating potential pharmacoresistance and the need for adjunctive glutamatergic modulation or alternative treatment strategies. This pattern is consistent with the conceptual framework proposed by Kantrowitz and Javitt (2010), who highlighted NMDA receptor hypofunction as a potential final common pathway underlying persistent symptoms and treatment resistance in schizophrenia39. The current profile advocates for therapeutic drug monitoring to confirm supra-median levels (e.g., targeting 20–40 µg/L troughs) before adjunctive interventions, such as glutamatergic modulators (e.g., sarcosine) or switches to clozapine, thereby addressing resistance rooted in olanzapine-glutamate decoupling and improving functional outcomes. Routine integration of MRS-based assessments in this subgroup may refine risk stratification, enabling more proactive management aimed at mitigating chronic disability and improving psychosocial outcomes. This phenotype, characterised by high symptom severity, elevated olanzapine exposure, and reduced cingulate Glx, represents a cross-sectional pattern that is consistent with, but not confirmatory of, pharmacoresistance. If replicated in longitudinal cohorts, this profile would warrant evaluation using therapeutic drug monitoring to verify supra-median exposure, as well as prospective investigation of adjunctive glutamatergic interventions (e.g., sarcosine) or clozapine switching strategies. Furthermore, integration of MRS-derived metabolite assessments in future studies may support the development of hypothesis-driven risk stratification approaches.

Cluster 3: Younger hyperglutamatergic phenotype with elevated cingulate Glx and neutral olanzapine exposure (n = 18).

Cluster 3 included younger patients with hyperglutamatergic profiles and neutral olanzapine exposure, possibly reflecting a neurodevelopmental imbalance preceding full symptom manifestation. The modest overall η² for olanzapine (0.14) implies indirect modulation, perhaps via dose-dependent GABA-glutamate balance, warranting escalation in youth to harness olanzapine’s potential effects on metabotropic glutamate receptors. Consistent with this notion, Marsman et al. (2013) demonstrated that antipsychotic treatment can normalize cortical Glx concentrations, supporting an indirect glutamatergic modulation underlying clinical improvement40. Similarly, Egerton et al. (2012) emphasized that changes in glutamatergic tone may reflect downstream effects of dopamine–serotonin receptor interactions, aligning with olanzapine’s receptor profile and its putative impact on neuroplasticity41. As a biomarker ensemble, these Glx elevations, decoupled from dose extremes and clinical severity, signal prodromal risk amenable to preventive dosing, integrating MRS surveillance with pharmacokinetic assays to tailor interventions and forestall progression in adolescent cohorts. In this context, Berk et al. (2008) provided evidence that adjunctive N-acetylcysteine may enhance glutamatergic homeostasis and improve functional outcomes, highlighting the translational potential of early metabolic modulation strategies42. This neutral exposure profile further underscores the need for age-specific TDM thresholds, potentially complemented by serial MRS to monitor glutamatergic changes and guide adjunctive therapies such as N-acetylcysteine for early stabilization. Elevated cingulate Glx levels in this subgroup represent a distinct pattern whose temporal and mechanistic significancewhether reflecting a neurodevelopmental trajectory or a cross-sectional epiphenomenon – remains to be established. This observation warrants prospective investigation of age-stratified dose–response relationships. Importantly, these Glx elevations, observed independently of dose extremes and clinical severity, constitute a candidate biomarker profile that merits prospective evaluation to determine its predictive value for illness progression in younger cohorts. These findings are consistent with reports indicating that antipsychotic treatment may influence cortical Glx concentrations, supporting the hypothesis of an indirect glutamatergic effect related to olanzapine’s receptor binding profile.

Summary

This study underscores the urgent need to bridge the gap between laboratory research and clinical practice in schizophrenia treatment. Current psychiatric care often relies on observable symptoms and standardized clinical scales, yet growing evidence shows that such approaches may overlook key biological underpinnings of treatment response. Our findings reveal distinct neurochemical profiles among patients treated with olanzapine, highlighting that similar drug exposure can result in widely divergent clinical outcomes. Particularly striking is the observation that some individuals, especially younger patients, exhibit significant alterations in brain glutamatergic activity without corresponding changes in symptom severity – suggesting that traditional symptom-based monitoring may fail to detect early neurobiological signs of treatment failure or progression and raising the possibility that symptom-based assessment alone may not fully capture the extent of neurochemical variation among patients.

While therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) for antipsychotics remains largely absent from routine psychiatric practice, several countries – including Germany, Switzerland, and Austria – have already implemented TDM as part of clinical decision-making in psychiatry, acknowledging its value in optimizing efficacy and minimizing side effects. However, even in these systems, TDM typically focuses on serum drug levels without considering parallel changes occurring in the brain.

This research highlights the necessity of expanding psychiatric monitoring beyond the bloodstream. Neuroimaging tools, such as magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS), offer a window into real-time neurochemical dynamics, providing insight into mechanisms that influence both drug response and resistance. Integrating central biomarkers with clinical observation and peripheral measures could transform treatment strategies – particularly for patients under 40, where early neurochemical dysregulation may shape long-term outcomes –offering a complementary dimension to clinical assessment. Future prospective research integrating central biomarkers with clinical observation and peripheral measures may inform the development of stratified treatment approaches where neurochemical variation warrants further longitudinal characterisation.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration when interpreting the present findings. First, the cross-sectional design constitutes the study’s principal methodological constraint: all reported associations are correlational, and neither causal directionality nor temporal precedence can be established. Prospective longitudinal studies are essential to determine whether the identified phenotypic profiles predict differential treatment trajectories. Second, single-centre recruitment from a Polish academic institution may introduce site-specific referral biases and limit external validity; moreover, the ethnic and pharmacogenomic homogeneity of the cohort (particularly regarding CYP1A2 polymorphism distributions, which substantially influence olanzapine metabolism; Soldin & Mattison, 2009 may restrict generalisability to populations with different genetic backgrounds43. Third, the restriction to chronically treated patients (mean treatment duration = 5.6 years) confines the findings to a population with prolonged olanzapine exposure; extrapolation to first-episode, treatment-naïve, or recently switched individuals is unwarranted. Fourth, the modest sample size (N = 51) limits statistical power – as illustrated by the absence of significant findings after FDR correction – and increases susceptibility to overfitting in the multivariate clustering analysis, despite the moderate bootstrap stability observed (mean ARI = 0.42). Fifth, the absence of a healthy control group prevents normative benchmarking of neurochemical measures, precluding determination of whether the observed MRS and peripheral biomarker values represent illness-related deviations or fall within the normal physiological range. Sixth, the Glx signal acquired at 3 T with TE = 30 ms represents an unresolved composite of glutamate and glutamine44, and the absence of tissue-fraction-based partial volume correction for MRS voxels introduces an additional source of inter-individual variability. Finally, dietary intake, smoking status, and body mass index – all of which influence peripheral amino acid concentrations and olanzapine pharmacokinetics – were not systematically recorded and may represent unmeasured confounders. Furthermore, the detection of residual concentrations of other antipsychotics in the majority of patients (Supplementary Table S7), despite the monotherapy inclusion criterion, suggests that prior antipsychotic exposure – known to exert lasting effects on glutamatergic and serotonergic systems22– may have contributed to inter-individual variability in neurochemical profiles and cannot be disentangled from olanzapine-specific effects within the constraints of this cross-sectional design.

These findings underscore the need for psychiatry to move toward precision medicine. By combining clinical, peripheral, and neuroimaging data, clinicians may better tailor interventions, avoid ineffective treatments, and identify at-risk patients before symptoms escalate. However, translating these findings into routine clinical practice remains a long-term objective rather than an immediate clinical imperative. The present findings reinforce the rationale for integrating clinical, peripheral, and neuroimaging data in future prospective studies. Such multimodal approaches hold promise for advancing precision psychiatry, although the cross-sectional and exploratory nature of the current study indicates that these observations should be considered preliminary and not yet indicative of clinically actionable stratification.

Materials and methods

Study Design and Objectives

This cross-sectional observational study was conducted in a naturalistic sample of adult patients with schizophrenia treated with olanzapine. The primary objective was to identify clinical and neurochemical profiles associated with treatment response by integrating symptom ratings, peripheral blood biomarkers, and brain metabolites measured via proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy (¹H-MRS). A secondary objective was to examine the relationship between serum olanzapine concentrations and clinical or neurochemical measures to inform personalized treatment strategies. Furthermore, the study aimed to characterise profiles associated with clinical and neurochemical variation under olanzapine treatment, integrating these multidimensional data to generate hypotheses regarding personalised treatment stratification.

Participants

The study included 51 patients with schizophrenia diagnosed according to ICD-10 criteria (F20), confirmed by a structured clinical interview conducted by a trained psychiatrist. Participants were consecutively recruited from inpatient and outpatient psychiatric services to ensure a clinically representative cohort. Inclusion criteria were: (1) treatment with olanzapine monotherapy for ≥ 6 months, (2) maintenance of a stable dose for ≥ 4 weeks prior to enrollment, and (3) capacity to provide informed consent. Exclusion criteria included: (1) comorbid neurological or major medical illness, (2) substance use disorder within the past 6 months (excluding nicotine), and (3) contraindications to magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). All participants provided written informed consent. The study protocol was approved by the Bioethics Committee of the Jagiellonian University. (reference number 1072.6120.87.2024) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Although inclusion required olanzapine monotherapy confirmed by prescribing records and clinical interview, the LC–MS/MS therapeutic drug monitoring panel employed in this study simultaneously quantified plasma concentrations of additional antipsychotics (haloperidol, aripiprazole, quetiapine, clozapine, risperidone metabolites) and their principal metabolites. Detectable concentrations of these compounds were observed in a substantial proportion of the cohort (see Supplementary Table S7), most likely reflecting residual traces of prior treatment rather than ongoing polypharmacy. However, detailed antipsychotic treatment histories – including the number of prior trials, cumulative defined daily doses, and duration of previous exposure – were not systematically recorded.

Clinical assessments

Psychopathology was assessed using the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS)45, the Calgary Depression Scale for Schizophrenia (CDSS)46, and the Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II)47. The Beck Depression Inventory-II was purchased from the official Polish distributor, the Psychological Test Laboratory of the Polish Psychological Association (Pracownia Testów Psychologicznych Polskiego Towarzystwa Psychologicznego), and was used in accordance with the applicable licensing conditions. The Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) and the Calgary Depression Scale for Schizophrenia (CDSS) are copyrighted instruments licensed through Mapi Research Trust, whereas the Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II) is copyrighted by Pearson Clinical Assessment. All instruments were administered by qualified clinicians for research purposes in accordance with the applicable copyright and licensing requirements.

The PANSS was administered to capture overall symptom severity, with the Negative Subscale (items N1–N7) summed to provide a focused index of negative symptoms. The CDSS was employed as a schizophrenia-specific measure of depression designed to minimize overlap with negative symptoms, whereas the BDI-II, a general self-report scale, was included to facilitate comparability with studies outside the schizophrenia field. All clinician-rated assessments (PANSS, CDSS) were conducted by a board-certified psychiatrist trained in the use of these instruments.

Peripheral biomarker measurements

Peripheral blood samples were collected between 8:00 and 10:00 a.m. after an overnight fast. Serum was separated by centrifugation and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Serum concentrations of serotonin, glutamic acid (glutamate), and glutamine were determined using validated chromatographic methods, as described in our previous papers48,49.

The Liquid Chromatography-Tandem Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), a reference method known for its superior sensitivity, specificity, and minimal susceptibility to matrix effects50. To 100 µL of serum, 4 µL of a deuterated serotonin, glutamate and glutamine internal standard (serotonin-d4, 500 µg/mL in water, glutamate acid-13C5 and glutamine-13C5, both 500ug/ml in water) was added, followed by vortex mixing for 10 s. Samples were deproteinized by adding 100 µL of acetonitrile (1:1, v/v), mixed for 30 s, and centrifuged at 8,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant (100 µL) was transferred into chromatographic vials for analysis.

Chromatographic separation was performed using a Waters ACQUITY UPLC® H-Class system (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA) with a ZIC®-HILIC column (5 μm, 200 Å, 150 × 2.1 mm; Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), maintained at 40 °C. The mobile phases consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (phase A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (phase B), delivered at a flow rate of 0.6 mL/min under a gradient program. The injection volume was 4 µL, and the total run time was 6 min. The retention time for serotonin (and its internal standard) was 2.35 min.

Mass spectrometric detection was conducted on a Xevo TQ-S® triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA) equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source operating in positive ionization mode. Ion source parameters were as follows: ion spray voltage 5500 V, nebulizer gas at 30 psi, turbo gas at 20 psi, source temperature at 550 °C, and curtain gas at 30 psi. Data were acquired in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode, monitoring the transitions m/z 177.0 → 119.0 for serotonin and m/z 182.83 → 119.76 for serotonin-d4; m/z = 148/84 for Glut and 153/89 for Glut-13C5; m/z = 147/84 for GLN and 152/88 for GLN-13C5. Quantification was based on calibration curves constructed by linear regression analysis of peak area versus concentration.

Intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation for serotonin, glutamate, and glutamine determinations were consistently below 10%.

Quantification of olanzapine in serum using LC–MS/MS

Serum olanzapine concentrations were quantified using a fully automated high-performance liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) system, designed to support high-throughput and high-precision therapeutic drug monitoring in a clinical laboratory setting. Serum samples were obtained by standard centrifugation at 4000 × g for 4 min at 4 °C and stored at − 80 °C until analysis.

Sample preparation followed the manufacturer’s instructions included with the commercial LC-MS/MS assay kit (MassTox®, Chromsystems). After equilibrating all reagents to room temperature and gentle mixing, 50 µL of each serum sample was pipetted into 1.5 mL reaction tubes. Next, 25 µL of extraction buffer was added; samples were vortexed briefly and allowed to stand for 2 min at room temperature. Subsequently, 250 µL of internal standard solution (containing isotopically labeled olanzapine) was added, and tubes were vortexed for 30 s. Samples were then centrifuged for 5 min at 15,000 × g. The resulting supernatant was diluted 1:4 using a 1:1 (v/v) mixture of two dilution buffers provided in the kit.

Chromatographic separation was performed on a reverse-phase MassTox® TDM MasterColumn Series A (REF 92110, LOT 2024, SN 259) under gradient elution conditions. The LC-MS/MS system included a cooled autosampler and a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer operating in positive electrospray ionization mode (ESI+). The injection volume was 8 µL, with a flow rate of 0.6 mL/min and a total run time of 3.2 min. Autosampler needle rinsing was performed after each injection using a dedicated rinsing solution. The column temperature was maintained between 20 and 25 °C, and the system was stabilized for at least 10 min before analysis.

Multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) was used to detect olanzapine, with two transitions monitored: m/z 313 → 256 and m/z 313 → 213. The method was calibrated using certified reference standards, and validation against an external LC-MS/MS method (MassTox®) demonstrated excellent agreement across the therapeutic range (R² > 0.98). Assay performance was monitored using quality control samples, with coefficients of variation consistently below 5%.

Analytical sensitivity was established during method optimization. The lower limit of detection (LOD) was calculated as 0.31 µg/L, and the lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) as 1.03 µg/L. The analytical bias was calculated as − 2.7%, indicating high accuracy across the measured range. Hemolyzed samples were excluded due to potential interference.

To facilitate stratified analysis, a binary exposure variable was created by dichotomizing olanzapine serum concentrations at the sample median (4.35 µg/L), distinguishing low- and high-exposure groups.

The decision to employ a median-based dichotomisation, rather than a clinically derived threshold, was motivated by the heavily right-skewed distribution of olanzapine concentrations in this cohort (mean = 24.86, SD = 63.04 µg/L), which violates the assumptions of linearity required for parametric dose–response modelling and limits the statistical power of continuous correlation analyses15. The cohort median of 4.35 µg/L falls below the consensus therapeutic reference range of 20–80 ng/mL recommended by the AGNP/ASCP guidelines33, which may reflect factors such as partial non-adherence, pharmacokinetic variability related to CYP1A2 metaboliser status, or variability in sampling time relative to the dosing interval. A complementary sensitivity analysis treating olanzapine concentration as a continuous variable is reported in Sect. 2.2.1 and Supplementary Table S2.

Magnetic Resonance Spectroscopy (MRS) Acquisition and Processing

Brain metabolite levels were measured using a 3 T MRI scanner (Siemens Prisma, Germany) with a 64-channel head coil. Single-voxel proton MRS (¹H-MRS) spectra were obtained from the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) and posterior cingulate cortex (PCC) using the Point-RESolved Spectroscopy (PRESS) sequence with TE = 30 ms, TR = 2000 ms, and 128 averages. Voxel dimensions were 20 × 20 × 20 mm³. Metabolite quantification was performed using LCModel (version 6.3–1 L) with water scaling, and metabolite concentrations were expressed as ratios to total creatine (Cr + PCr). Quantified ratios included: glutamine/(Cr + PCr), glutamate/(Cr + PCr), (glutamate + glutamine)/(Cr + PCr), and myo-inositol/(Cr + PCr). Only spectra with a full width at half maximum (FWHM) < 0.08 ppm and a signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) > 15 were included in the analysis; spectra not meeting these quality criteria were excluded from subsequent statistical analyses. It is important to note that the Glx signal, acquired at TE = 30 ms using a PRESS sequence at 3 T, represents a composite of glutamate and glutamine that cannot be reliably decomposed into its constituent metabolites under these acquisition parameters44. All references to “Glx” in this manuscript therefore denote this composite measure, and any inferences regarding glutamatergic activity should be interpreted in light of this limitation.

MRS voxel justification.

The ACC and PCC were selected as regions of interest based on convergent evidence from prior MRS studies. The ACC constitutes the most extensively investigated region in spectroscopic studies of schizophrenia, with meta-analytic data confirming glutamatergic alterations that correlate with symptom severity and antipsychotic response21,22. The PCC was included as a complementary node within the default mode network, whose dysconnectivity is considered a hallmark feature of schizophrenia51. Paired ACC–PCC measurements provide a within-subject anterior–posterior contrast capable of revealing regional specificity of neurochemical disturbances. Other regions of theoretical interest, including the hippocampus and thalamus, were not assessed owing to technical challenges associated with single-voxel MRS at 3 T (including susceptibility artefacts and shimming constraints) and limitations in sample size, which precluded a multi-region approach without increasing the burden of multiple comparisons. Metabolite ratios relative to total creatine (Cr + PCr) partially mitigate cerebrospinal fluid dilution effects; however, explicit tissue-fraction-based partial volume correction was not performed and represents a limitation of the present study.

Statistical analysis

Partial correlation analysis of clinical and neurochemical variables in olanzapine-treated schizophrenia

Partial correlation analysis was performed to examine the associations between clinical outcomes and biomarker candidates, controlling for confounding factors to isolate direct relationships relevant to olanzapine response. This approach was justified by the need to account for demographic and treatment-related influences that could bias the observed correlations, thereby providing a more accurate assessment of potential biomarkers for personalized therapeutic strategies in schizophrenia. Clinical outcomes were selected as variables directly reflective of symptom severity and treatment exposure, including the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) Negative Symptoms Score, PANSS Negative Subscale Items N1-N7 Sum, PANSS Total Score, Calgary Depression Scale for Schizophrenia (CDSS) Total Score, Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II) Total Score, and a binary indicator of serum olanzapine concentration exceeding the cohort median. These measures were chosen due to their established clinical relevance in evaluating negative, depressive, and overall psychotic symptoms, as well as drug exposure status, which are central to assessing olanzapine efficacy.

Biomarker candidates encompassed peripheral and central neurochemical markers potentially indicative of biological response mechanisms, including Serum Serotonin Concentration, Glutamic Acid Serum Concentration, Glutamine Serum Concentration, Glutamine/(Cr + PCr) Ratio (MRS, ACC, TE = 30 ms), Glutamate/(Cr + PCr) Ratio (MRS, ACC, TE = 30 ms), myo-Inositol/(Cr + PCr) Ratio (MRS, ACC, TE = 30 ms), (Glutamate + Glutamine)/(Cr + PCr) Ratio (MRS, ACC, TE = 30 ms), Glutamine/(Cr + PCr) Ratio (MRS, PCC, TE = 30 ms), Glutamate/(Cr + PCr) Ratio (MRS, PCC, TE = 30 ms), myo-Inositol/(Cr + PCr) Ratio (MRS, PCC, TE = 30 ms), and (Glutamate + Glutamine)/(Cr + PCr) Ratio (MRS, PCC, TE = 30 ms). These parameters were justified by their role in neurotransmitter systems modulated by olanzapine, such as serotonergic and glutamatergic pathways, and their potential to serve as candidate indicators of clinical and neurochemical variation and treatment response.

Covariates were included to adjust for factors known to influence both clinical outcomes and neurochemical profiles, comprising age (years), sex (male), and duration of olanzapine treatment (years). Age and sex were selected as standard demographic confounders due to their established impact on symptom presentation and pharmacokinetics in schizophrenia. Treatment duration was incorporated to control for chronicity-related effects, such as cumulative drug exposure or disease progression, which could confound the associations.

The Spearman rank correlation method was employed overall to compute partial correlation coefficients, accounting for potential non-linear relationships in the data. This non-parametric approach was appropriate given the variability in clinical and neurochemical measures observed in the cohort. Partial correlations were calculated for each pair of a clinical outcome and a biomarker candidate, adjusting for the three covariates.

P-values were approximated using a t-distribution based on the partial correlation coefficient and degrees of freedom adjusted for the number of covariates and sample size, with a significance threshold of α = 0.05. The 95% confidence interval (CI) for the Spearman rank correlation coefficient (Rho) was estimated via Fisher’s z-transformation, converting the correlation to a z-score, calculating the interval in z-space, and transforming back to the correlation scale, ensuring robust interval estimation for the non-parametric method52,53. Due to the exploratory nature of the correlation analysis, all correlation p-values were reported without additional adjustment for multiple comparisons to facilitate the identification of potential associations for further investigation. The present correlation analysis was explicitly exploratory in design: no a priori directional hypotheses were pre-specified, and the full matrix of 77 pairwise tests (7 clinical outcomes × 11 biomarker candidates) was examined to identify candidate associations for subsequent confirmatory investigation. In keeping with the exploratory intent, uncorrected p-values are reported as the primary results. However, to quantify the risk of Type I error inflation, the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction54 was applied across all 77 tests, and FDR-adjusted q-values are reported in Supplementary Table S1. Readers are advised that none of the reported associations attain significance after FDR correction (minimum q = 0.086), and all findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. (For completeness, the full range of q-values extends up to 0.550.)

Effect size reporting: Partial correlation coefficients were interpreted as standardised effect size measures following Cohen’s (1988)14 benchmarks for correlation magnitudes: small (|ρ| = 0.10–0.23), medium (|ρ| = 0.24–0.36), and large (|ρ| ≥ 0.37). For the cluster analysis, η² values were reported as the proportion of variance in each variable explained by cluster membership. Effect sizes are presented alongside p-values throughout the Results and Discussion to facilitate interpretation independent of sample size23.

Hierarchical clustering on principal components for patient profiling in olanzapine treatment

To characterize distinct patient profiles among 51 individuals with schizophrenia treated with olanzapine, hierarchical clustering on principal components (HCPC) was employed. The analysis integrated a binary indicator of serum olanzapine concentration above the cohort median (4.35 µg/L) with demographic variables (age, sex), clinical symptom measures (Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale [PANSS] negative symptoms, CDSS, BDI-II), peripheral neurochemical markers (serum serotonin, glutamate, glutamine concentrations), and MRS ratios from ACC and PCC (glutamate/glutamine and myo-inositol ratios relative to creatine plus phosphocreatine, measured at TE = 30 ms).

Principal component analysis (PCA) was first conducted to reduce dimensionality and capture variance within this heterogeneous dataset. Subsequently, HCPC was applied to the PCA results using the HCPC function, with a maximum of four clusters to identify distinct patient subgroups55. This approach is well-suited for uncovering complex, multidimensional relationships in clinical and neurochemical data, enabling unsupervised identification of patient profiles associated with olanzapine exposure56.

Cluster validation methods.

The quality of the cluster solution was evaluated using inertia-based criteria appropriate for Ward’s minimum-variance linkage. The ratio of between-cluster to total inertia quantified the proportion of total variance explained by the partition. The optimal number of clusters was determined by inspecting the inertia gain across successive partitions (k = 2, 3, 4), selecting the solution at which the marginal gain exhibited a clear diminishing-returns pattern. Bootstrap stability was assessed by resampling 1,000 datasets with replacement, recomputing the full PCA–Ward–k-cluster pipeline on each replicate, and calculating the adjusted Rand index (ARI)57 between bootstrap and original cluster assignments. The average silhouette width58 was computed as an ancillary metric, noting that Ward’s linkage does not optimise this criterion.

The overall link between the cluster assignment and each variable using analysis of variance (ANOVA). For each variable, the eta-squared (η²) statistic is reported, which quantifies the proportion of variance in the quantitative variable explained by the clusters, analogous to R² in regression models. The associated p-value is derived from the F-test in the ANOVA framework, indicating the statistical significance of the cluster effect on the variable, with a significance threshold set at α = 0.05. Variables are ranked by descending η² to highlight those most discriminative across clusters.

Sensitivity analysis: Olanzapine concentration as a continuous predictor

As a sensitivity analysis complementing the primary dichotomised olanzapine exposure variable, partial Spearman rank correlations were calculated between continuous serum olanzapine concentration and all clinical outcome and biomarker variables, controlling for the same set of covariates (age, sex, and duration of olanzapine treatment). This analysis was conducted to evaluate whether the median-based dichotomisation may have obscured continuous dose–response relationships. Given the marked right-skewness of the olanzapine concentration distribution (median = 4.35 µg/L, mean = 24.86 µg/L, SD = 63.04 µg/L), the non-parametric Spearman approach was retained.
