Anastomotic leakage in colorectal cancer surgery in Syria: risk factors and improvements following guideline implementation – a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Elie Bitar, Dima Al-Karad, Hamsa Sahlieh, Maher Nasr, Sandra Habka, Samer Sara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Anastomotic leakage (AL) is a serious complication following colorectal cancer surgery, associated with increased morbidity, mortality, and impaired oncologic outcomes. Evidence from resource-limited, conflict-affected settings such as Syria is scarce. This study aimed to identify risk factors for AL and evaluate outcomes after implementation of revised institutional preoperative guidelines.Materials and methods:A retrospective cohort study was conducted at Damascus Hospital, Syria. The first cohort (January 2016–February 2024) included patients undergoing elective sigmoid colon or rectal resection. Based on the findings, preoperative preparation guidelines were updated, and a follow-up cohort (April 2024–October 2025) was evaluated. AL was defined according to the International Study Group of Rectal Cancer criteria. Patient-, tumor-, and procedure-related factors were analyzed, and outcomes before and after guideline implementation were compared.Results:The first cohort (n = 100) had an AL incidence of 15% and postoperative mortality of 19%. Significant risk factors included hypoalbuminemia, anemia, perioperative blood transfusion, prolonged corticosteroid use, extra-peritoneal anastomosis, and poor tumor differentiation. Following guideline implementation (n = 19), hypoalbuminemia decreased from 61.3% to 10.5%, perioperative transfusions from 60% to 42.1%, AL incidence from 15% to 10.5%, and mortality from 19% to 15.8%. Comparison with international studies highlighted the high baseline prevalence of hypoalbuminemia and AL, emphasizing the importance of nutritional optimization.Conclusion:AL is strongly associated with modifiable factors, particularly hypoalbuminemia and anemia. Evidence-based guideline implementation targeting these factors significantly improved postoperative outcomes, demonstrating the value of context-specific interventions in resource-limited settings.

Introduction

Anastomotic leakage (AL) has been described using multiple definitions over the years; however, a recent international Delphi consensus recommended adopting the definition of the International Study Group of Rectal Cancer (ISREC), which describes AL as “a defect of the intestinal wall integrity at the colorectal or Colo-anal anastomotic site leading to a communication between the intra- and extra-luminal compartments”[1].

AL is a serious and potentially life-threatening complication of colorectal surgery, associated with increased morbidity, prolonged hospitalization, higher costs, and postoperative mortality rates reported between 0.8% and 27%[2–4]. Furthermore, in patients with colorectal cancer, AL has been linked to higher local recurrence rates and reduced long-term survival[5,6]. Globally, the reported incidence of AL ranges from 3% to 18%, depending on tumor location, anastomotic technique, and patient-related risk factors[7].

This study was conducted in Damascus, Syria, a country that has suffered over a decade of armed conflict, severely affecting its healthcare system. Shortages in medical supplies, staffing, and advanced surgical technologies have resulted in increased postoperative complications, including AL[8,9]. Although substantial literature addresses this problem in high-income settings, there is limited evidence from low-income or conflict-affected countries, where limited infrastructure and disrupted supply prevent both prevention and management. Owing to these limitations, including shortages of surgical supplies, limited access to stapling devices, and inadequate perioperative monitoring, laparoscopic surgery is not routinely available, and open surgery remains the primary approach, even for elective colorectal procedures. Given these conditions, identifying context-specific risk factors is crucial to improving surgical outcomes within the available resources.

Multiple studies have proposed both patient and procedure-related determinants of AL, yet no single risk factor has been universally established as predictive[10–15]. Reported contributors include malnutrition, sepsis, cardiovascular disease, perioperative blood transfusion, steroid use, smoking, and alcohol abuse. Further risk factors, including male sex and obesity, have also been suggested[2]. Among these, hypoalbuminemia has increasingly been recognized as a reliable biochemical predictor of poor healing and postoperative leakage[16,17]. Nevertheless, real data from war-affected and resource-limited regions remain scarce.

The spectrum of AL-related complications extends from minor leakage to diffuse peritoneal infection, multi-organ failure, and fatal septic outcomes. The severity of such complications underscores the necessity for early detection and targeted intervention to reduce morbidity and mortality[18].

Several studies demonstrate that sigmoid colon and rectum resections are associated with the highest incidence of AL (5.1% and 10.7%, respectively)[19,20]. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the key risk factors associated with AL following sigmoid colon and rectal resections performed for colorectal cancer patients and recognize the outcomes after modifying these risk factors.

Methods

This retrospective cohort study was conducted in the Department of General Surgery at Damascus Hospital, Syria, between January 2016 and February 2024 (first study). We studied the outcomes that led to updating the preoperative preparation guidelines, and then we conducted a follow-up study between April 2024 and October 2025 (follow-up study) and compared the outcomes before and after updating the preoperative preparation guidelines. The institution operates under severe resource constraints resulting from prolonged conflict and international sanctions, which have limited access to advanced surgical tools and standardized perioperative care. This study adhered to the STROCSS 2025 guidelines for surgical cohort studies.

All patients who underwent elective resection of the sigmoid colon or rectum for histologically confirmed colorectal adenocarcinoma during the study period were eligible for inclusion. Exclusion criteria included emergency cases, non-neoplastic (non-tumor) indications, and incomplete records. In the first study, a total of 137 patients were initially identified, of whom 37 patients were excluded (15 due to incomplete records, 12 emergency cases, and 10 non-tumor resections), leaving 100 patients for final analysis. In the follow-up cohort, 25 patients were screened, of whom 6 were excluded (2 incomplete records, 3 emergency surgeries, and 1 non-neoplastic indication), yielding a final sample of 19 patients for follow-up analysis (Fig. 1). All operations were performed by board-certified general surgeons with 5–25 years of experience. Surgical procedures were classified by approach (open or laparoscopic), anastomotic method (hand-sewn or stapled), location (intra- or extra-peritoneal), and use of diversion (ileostomy or none). Standard preoperative bowel preparation and antibiotic prophylaxis were implemented when resources allowed.

AL was defined according to the ISREC definition as a defect of the intestinal anastomosis resulting in communication between the intra- and extra-luminal compartments, and all cases were confirmed clinically or radiologically.

Diagnosis required at least one of the following: purulent or feculent discharge through the wound or drain, radiologic evidence of leakage on contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) scan, reoperation confirming dehiscence, or clinical suspicion supported by systemic inflammatory response or sepsis.

Data were extracted from patient charts, operative notes, and structured post-discharge telephone follow-ups using a standardized data abstraction form to minimize inter-observer variability. Data collection and verification were supervised by Prof. Dr. S.S., PhD, ensuring accuracy and consistency.

Collected variables included: demographic characteristics, lifestyle habits, medical history, laboratory values, tumor characteristics, operative details, and postoperative outcomes for both studies.

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS, version 24. Frequencies and percentages were calculated to describe the distribution of patients according to demographic and clinical characteristics and the study variables. The chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test were used, as appropriate, to examine associations and compare categorical variables.

A significant level of 0.05 was adopted for all statistical tests. Accordingly, a P-value greater than 0.05 was considered statistically non-significant, whereas a P-value less than or equal to 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To ensure data reliability, dual data entry was performed independently by two researchers, random audits of 10% of records were conducted, and discrepancies were resolved by consensus review.

Given the retrospective and single-center design, potential limitations include incomplete documentation and the very small number of laparoscopic cases (two cases in the first study and three cases in the follow-up study), which may restrict the generalizability of the findings.

By identifying the key risk factors for AL in colorectal cancer surgery, the initial study sought to improve patient outcomes and provide evidence to inform clinical practice, emphasizing the importance of comprehensive preoperative assessment and surgical technique. Findings from the first cohort prompted revisions to the institutional preoperative preparation guidelines. The follow-up study was designed to evaluate outcomes after implementation of these updated protocols and to assess whether the changes were associated with improved postoperative results.

Results

The first study involved a cohort of 137 individuals, with 37 individuals excluded based on predetermined criteria, resulting in a final sample size of 100 participants. The sample size was considered adequate for conducting rigorous statistical analyses and drawing meaningful conclusions.

In terms of demographics, the gender distribution was relatively even, with 51% of the participants being female and 49% male. This balanced representation is important to ensure that the study findings are not influenced by gender bias. Additionally, over half of the participants (53%) were aged >60 years, highlighting the prevalence of colorectal cancer in older age groups and underscoring the need for early detection and intervention strategies in this population.

Regarding lifestyle habits, most participants (58%) reported being non-smokers, and all participants reported abstaining from alcohol consumption, indicating a low prevalence of alcohol use disorder within the study cohort.

Analysis of medical history data revealed that 53% of the participants did not have hypertension, and a significant majority (71%) did not have diabetes mellitus. These findings provide valuable insights into the comorbidities prevalent among patients with colorectal cancer (CRC), which can inform personalized treatment approaches and risk stratification strategies.

Analysis of tumor and surgical data revealed that rectal tumors accounted for 51% of the cases, while sigmoid colon tumors accounted for the remaining 49%. Among the patients with rectal tumors, 58.8% received radiotherapy.

Furthermore, the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification demonstrated that 62% of participants had a score of more than or equal to 2, indicating the overall poor health status among the study population.

Surgical data indicated that the vast majority of procedures (98%) were performed using an open approach, with laparoscopic procedures accounting for only 2% of the cases. This disparity in surgical techniques may be attributed to various factors such as institutional preferences, surgeon expertise, and equipment availability. Additionally, manual surgical techniques were employed in 69% of the cases, with staplers utilized in the remaining 31%. This distinction in surgical methods highlights the diversity of approaches employed in colorectal cancer surgery, each with its own advantages and limitations.

Perioperative blood transfusion was administered to 60% of the individuals, reflecting the significant blood loss associated with colorectal cancer surgery and the need for blood conservation strategies. Moreover, half of the participants in the study required admission to the intensive care unit after undergoing complex colorectal surgeries, highlighting the potential for significant morbidity associated with these procedures. Postoperative complications were common, with 73% of individuals experiencing issues such as wound infections, leaks in surgical connections, and contamination. These results underscore the importance of careful perioperative care and close monitoring postoperatively to reduce complications and improve patient outcomes. In terms of surgical procedures, the research revealed that 53% of patients did not undergo ileostomy, while 47% did. Additionally, 55.3% of surgeries involved intra-peritoneal closure, and the remaining 44.7% involved extra-peritoneal closure. Histopathological analysis showed that 68% of the individuals had tumors with a differentiation level of less than 3, indicating poorly differentiated or undifferentiated tumors. This feature is associated with aggressive tumor behavior and may require more aggressive treatment strategies. Furthermore, the tumor–node–metastasis (TNM) staging classification indicated that 32% of the cases were grade 2, suggesting an intermediate tumor stage with a potential for metastasis. Additionally, 16% of the patients were on long-term steroid treatment, indicating the presence of comorbidities. Low hemoglobin levels were prevalent, with 49% of patients having levels between 10 and 12 g/dL and 40% having levels below 10 g/dL. Low albumin levels were also common, with 44% of the patients having levels below 3.5 g/dL, indicating malnutrition or systemic inflammation. Surgical complications, such as contamination and AL, were observed in 13% and 15% of patients, respectively, highlighting the challenges associated with colorectal surgery. Ultimately, 81% of patients were discharged following surgery, while 19% died, emphasizing the significant morbidity and mortality risks associated with colorectal cancer surgery. Overall, this study provides valuable insights into the characteristics of colorectal cancer patients, their medical histories, tumor details, and postoperative outcomes. These findings have important implications for treatment decisions, risk assessment, and prognosis in clinical practice, contributing to efforts to improve CRC management of colorectal cancer and enhance patient outcomes.

Importantly, the findings of the initial study informed institutional changes to preoperative preparation and perioperative management guidelines, aiming to reduce the identified modifiable risk factors. Following implementation of these updated protocols, notable improvements were observed in the follow-up cohort. The proportion of laparoscopic resections increased to 15.8%, reflecting enhanced availability of minimally invasive equipment and adoption of advanced surgical techniques. Postoperative complications declined markedly to 10.5%, and perioperative blood transfusion rates decreased to 42.1%, indicating improved intraoperative hemostasis and more conservative transfusion practices. Preoperative optimization, including enhanced perioperative counseling, was reflected in reduced chronic steroid use (15.8%) and higher hemoglobin levels (>12 g/dL in 15.8% of patients). Most critically, hypoalbuminemia (<3.5 g/dL), identified in the first study as a key determinant of AL, has declined to 10.5%, demonstrating the impact of protocolized nutritional optimization, and pre- and post-operative serum albumin measurement. These improvements were associated with reductions in AL (from 15% to 10.5%) and postoperative mortality (from 19% to 15.8%), underscoring the clinical significance of targeted guideline modifications driven by the initial study’s findings. Additionally, we conducted a comparison to evaluate potential differences in demographic, clinical, surgical, and oncological characteristics between rectal and sigmoid colon tumor sites. However, significant differences were identified in selected parameters. Furthermore, TNM stage distribution differed significantly between the two sites (P = 0.023), with a higher proportion of stage 0 disease observed in sigmoid colon tumors (34.5%) compared with rectal tumors (11.5%). Discussion

This study evaluated a cohort of patients who underwent sigmoid colon or rectal resection for colorectal cancer at Damascus Hospital, with the primary aim of assessing postoperative complications, particularly AL, and identifying associated risk factors in a resource-limited, conflict-affected setting. Building upon the findings of the first cohort, this study further examined postoperative outcomes following the implementation of revised institutional guidelines designed to address modifiable risk factors. In contrast to studies conducted in high-income countries, which predominantly report outcomes from laparoscopic or robotic colorectal surgery, our results reflect real-world outcomes of predominantly open surgical procedures performed under constrained resources. As such, this work provides valuable insight into colorectal surgical risk and quality improvement in settings that remain underrepresented in the literature[8].

In the first cohort, the incidence of AL was 15%, which was higher than those reported in other studies[21–23] (Table 3). Following the adoption of updated hospital guidelines, the incidence of AL declined to 10.5%, representing a clinically meaningful improvement. Concurrently, postoperative mortality decreased from 19% to 15.8%, suggesting that improved anastomotic integrity and perioperative management translated into better overall patient outcomes. A large group of risk factors associated with leakage from colorectal anastomoses was studied and divided into two types[2,12,20,24]: risk factors related to the patient and risk factors related to the surgical procedure performed on this patient.

Patient-related risk factors

Female sex was more prevalent among patients who developed AL in the initial cohort, a finding that contrasts with the majority of published studies, which report male sex as a stronger predictor of leakage[11,21,25–30]. Advanced age, particularly over 60 years, remained common among patients with AL, consistent with previous reports attributing increased leakage risk to age-related physiological decline[31]. Smoking was frequently observed among affected patients, while alcohol consumption was notably absent in our cohort, reflecting regional and sociocultural factors[32]. Comorbidities such as hypertension and diabetes mellitus were strongly associated with AL, likely due to impaired microcirculation and delayed wound healing[33,34]. Long-term corticosteroid use was also identified as a significant risk factor, in keeping with its known inhibitory effects on collagen synthesis and immune response. In contrast, the ASA score did not demonstrate a statistically significant association with AL[35].

Hematological and nutritional parameters emerged as critical determinants of anastomotic integrity. Preoperative hemoglobin levels below 10 g/dL were significantly associated with AL, consistent with the role of anemia in tissue hypoxia and impaired healing[36].

Albumin is the most abundant circulating protein in human plasma, accounting for approximately half of the total plasma protein content (3.5–5 g/dL) in healthy individuals. It is synthesized by hepatocytes in the liver and rapidly secreted into the bloodstream at a rate of 10–15 g/day. Given its limited hepatic storage, albumin is efficiently released into the circulation shortly after synthesis. Functionally, serum albumin plays a pivotal role in maintaining plasma oncotic pressure and serves as a key transporter for endogenous and exogenous ligands, including pharmaceuticals. In clinical practice, serum albumin levels are routinely assessed through standard laboratory testing and are frequently utilized as an indicator of a patient’s nutritional status[16]. Albumin serves not only as a crucial plasma protein but also as a therapeutic colloid solution utilized in fluid resuscitation, particularly in cases of hypovolemic shock following trauma or during large-volume paracentesis. Its administration aims to restore intravascular volume and maintain oncotic pressure, thereby supporting hemodynamic stability in critically ill patients. Beyond its role in circulatory physiology, serum albumin is a valuable biomarker in clinical diagnostics, offering insights into the hepatic synthetic function and overall protein biosynthesis, which are essential for maintaining systemic homeostasis. Notably, multiple studies have established a strong correlation between hypoalbuminemia, especially levels below 3.5 g/dL, and an increased risk of AL, underscoring the importance of albumin assessment in perioperative and critical care settings[17]. When compared with other studies, we found that the serum albumin level was identified as the most influential modifiable risk factor. In the initial cohort, hypoalbuminemia (<3.5 g/dL) was present in 61.3% of patients, reflecting a high burden of malnutrition and systemic inflammation. Following implementation of routine pre-operative albumin assessment and nutritional optimization protocols, the prevalence of hypoalbuminemia declined markedly to 10.5%.

When compared with other studies[21–23], our cohort demonstrated a substantially higher prevalence of preoperative hypoalbuminemia. Risk factors related to the surgical procedure and the occurrence of leakage

For the procedure, the rate was 98% for the open procedure, as all our patients who leaked had undergone open surgery[35]. Although stapled anastomoses demonstrated higher leakage rates compared with manual closure, this association did not reach statistical significance. Perioperative blood transfusion and postoperative ICU admission were more frequent among patients who developed AL, likely indicating higher surgical complexity and physiological stress[37]. Notably, after guideline implementation, perioperative blood transfusion rates declined from 60% to 42.1%, reflecting improved intraoperative hemostasis and more restrictive transfusion practices. The use of ileostomy and extra-peritoneal anastomosis was associated with higher leakage rates, particularly in rectal surgery, consistent with previous reports[35]. Tumor-related factors, including poor differentiation and advanced TNM stage, were also associated with increased AL risk, likely due to more extensive resections and compromised tissue quality[2]. Our study showed that AL is highly related to high tumor differentiation.

Given the significant clinical consequences of AL, thorough preoperative risk stratification remains essential. Factors such as advanced age, smoking, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hypoalbuminemia, anemia, prolonged corticosteroid use, perioperative blood transfusion, extra-peritoneal anastomosis, and poor tumor differentiation must be carefully evaluated and optimized whenever possible.

A major strength of this study lies in its direct impact on clinical practice. Following dissemination of the initial findings, Damascus Hospital implemented revised internal guidelines aimed at reducing modifiable risk factors for AL. These included mandatory preoperative nutritional assessment with serum albumin measurement, postponement of elective surgery in patients with albumin levels below 3.5 g/dL, optimization of hemoglobin levels, cautious use of perioperative blood transfusion, and enhanced postoperative monitoring of high-risk patients. Subsequently, improvements were observed across multiple outcomes, including reduced hypoalbuminemia, fewer postoperative complications, lower AL incidence, and decreased postoperative mortality.

These findings highlight the importance of context-specific research in driving meaningful improvements in surgical care, even in settings affected by war, limited resources, and infrastructural constraints.

Limitations

A limitation of this study was the lack of a laparoscopic comparison group, which restricts broader generalizability. However, given the resource-constrained environment of our institution, these findings remain highly relevant to similar settings worldwide, including conflict-affected or low-income regions.

Conclusion

AL remains a significant cause of morbidity and mortality following colorectal cancer surgery, particularly in resource-limited settings. This study identified hypoalbuminemia, anemia, perioperative blood transfusion, and prolonged corticosteroid use as major, modifiable risk factors for AL. Importantly, implementation of revised institutional guidelines targeting these factors was associated with a meaningful reduction in AL, postoperative complications, and mortality. These findings demonstrate that evidence-based risk factor–driven interventions can substantially improve surgical outcomes and should be sustained as standard practice in similar healthcare environments.
