Effect of Left Ventricular Systolic Dysfunction on the Outcome of Mechanical Thrombectomy
Authors: Satoshi Miyamoto, Yoshiro Ito, Shinichiro Numao, Shun Tanaka, Takato Hiramine, Toshihide Takahashi, Sho Okune, Hisayuki Hosoo, Mikito Hayakawa, Aiki Marushima, Hiroshi Yamagami, Eiichi Ishikawa, Yuji Matsumaru
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveLeft ventricular systolic dysfunction has traditionally been considered an unfavorable prognostic factor in stroke. However, chronic hypoperfusion due to this dysfunction may improve cerebral collateral flow, potentially serving as a compensatory mechanism during ischemic stroke. This study aimed to investigate the effects of left ventricular systolic dysfunction on outcomes after mechanical thrombectomy (MT), with a focus on cerebral collateral flow.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 94 consecutive patients with acute ischemic stroke who underwent MT between April 2017 and July 2022. Patients were divided into 2 groups based on their left ventricular ejection fraction (EF): the reduced EF group (EF ≤40%) and the preserved EF group (EF >40%). We evaluated post-treatment stroke volume, clinical outcomes, length of hospital stay, and the relationship between EF and cerebral collateral flow.ResultsThe reduced and preserved EF groups consisted of 11 (12%) and 83 (88%) patients, respectively. No significant differences were observed in post-treatment stroke volume (13 vs. 12 cm3, p = 0.779), hospital stay duration (23 vs. 22 days, p = 0.634), or favorable clinical outcomes at discharge (36% vs. 43%, p = 0.754) between the 2 groups. The odds ratio for favorable outcomes at discharge, adjusted using inverse probability of treatment weighting, was 0.693 (95% confidence interval: 0.176–2.732, p = 0.600) for the reduced EF group compared with the preserved EF group. Cerebral collateral flow developed better in the reduced EF group (56% vs. 13%, p = 0.008).ConclusionLeft ventricular systolic dysfunction did not significantly worsen outcomes after MT. Chronic cerebral hypoperfusion due to left ventricular systolic dysfunction may promote the development of cerebral collaterals, potentially enhancing resistance to ischemic events.

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) has been reported both as a risk factor for ischemic stroke and as an unfavorable prognostic factor for stroke,1–4) as it disrupts physiological homeostasis. This disruption can lead to arterial and venous hemodynamic disturbances, body fluid imbalances, reduced cerebral tissue oxygenation, endothelial dysfunction, and increased proinflammatory and prothrombotic responses.1) The Framingham study highlighted that patients with HF have a 2- to 3-fold greater risk of ischemic stroke.5) Similarly, the Olmsted County cohort study revealed that HF is associated with approximately a 2-fold increase in poststroke mortality.6)

Conversely, cases involving mechanical thrombectomy (MT) suggest that HF may not necessarily worsen clinical outcomes.7) The impact of HF on outcomes in patients undergoing MT remains controversial. This difference in findings is likely attributed to the limited number of relevant studies, differing definitions of HF, and a lack of detailed understanding of how HF affects cerebral blood flow.

Previous reports have mentioned that decreased cardiac function, such as in HF, can reduce cerebral blood flow.8) Therefore, we hypothesized that patients with HF may develop collateral flow due to chronic cerebral hypoperfusion, thus leading to ischemic tolerance. In this study, we aimed to investigate the effects of left ventricular systolic dysfunction on outcomes following MT by comparing the results of MT treatment between patients with and those without HF.

Materials and Methods

Study design

This retrospective observational study was conducted at a single center. Consecutive patients diagnosed with acute ischemic stroke who underwent MT and were admitted to our hospital between April 2017 and July 2022 were included. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) absence of echocardiographic data; (2) subsequent treatment for recurrent stroke episodes; and (3) diagnosis of large-artery atherosclerosis. Based on left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) measured using transthoracic echocardiography within 1 week of admission, patients were divided into 2 groups: the reduced EF group (EF ≤40%) and the preserved EF group (EF >40%). This cutoff value aligns with the definition of HF with reduced EF established by the “2022 American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology Foundation/Heart Failure Society of America Guideline for the Management of Heart Failure.”9)

Reperfusion procedure

MT was performed according to Japanese guidelines.10,11) Device selection, including the use of stent retrievers, aspiration devices, or both, was determined at the discretion of the operator. Patients who met the Japanese guidelines for intravenous thrombolysis received alteplase (0.6 mg/kg of body weight).10,12)

Data collection

Patient data were obtained from hospital records. The following variables were collected: age, sex, body mass index, medication use, past medical history, smoking status, premorbid modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score, transthoracic echocardiogram findings, infarction location (anterior or posterior circulation), baseline Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score or Posterior Circulation Alberta Stroke Programme Early CT Score (as appropriate), National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score at admission, and degree of cerebral collateral flow. The latter was assessed using cerebral angiography during MT and graded according to the American Society of Interventional and Therapeutic Neuroradiology/Society of Interventional Radiology (ASITN/SIR) grading system.13) Additional collected data included administration of tissue plasminogen activator, modified thrombolysis in cerebral infarction grade, onset-to-recanalization time, door-to-recanalization time, and post-MT stroke volume, defined as the volume showing an apparent diffusion coefficient <620 on diffusion-weighted imaging within 24 h after the procedure, measured using RAPID automated software (iSchema View, Menlo Park, CA, USA). Hemorrhagic transformation was classified using the Safe Implementation of Thrombolysis in Stroke-Monitoring Study criteria,14) and symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage was defined as a deterioration of NIHSS by ≥4 points. Functional outcome was assessed by the mRS at discharge, and the length of hospital stay was recorded. A favorable outcome was defined as an mRS score of 0–2 or a score equal to the premorbid status.

Cerebral collateral flow was evaluated using the ASITN/SIR grading system, which is a 5-point angiographic scale with the following grading criteria: 0 = no collaterals visible to the ischemic site; 1 = slow collaterals to the periphery of the ischemic site with persistence of some of the defect; 2 = rapid collaterals to the periphery of the ischemic site with persistence of some of the defect and to only a portion of the ischemic territory; 3 = collaterals with slow but complete angiographic blood flow of the ischemic bed by the late venous phase; and 4 = complete and rapid collateral blood flow to the vascular bed in the entire ischemic territory by retrograde perfusion.13) Scores of 3–4 indicated “good collaterals,” while scores of 0–2 were considered “poor collaterals.” Although usually used to assess anterior circulation, this scoring system has been deemed appropriate for use in posterior circulation strokes as well.15) All angiograms obtained during MT were reviewed by 2 certified neurosurgeons (S.M. and Y.I.). In cases of disagreement, a consensus was reached through discussion among the 2 physicians.

We investigated the background characteristics and clinical outcomes between the reduced and preserved EF groups, based on data from 94 patients. Additionally, we investigated the association between EF and cerebral collateral flow, using data from 77 patients with available ASITN/SIR grade information. Subsequently, subgroup analyses were conducted separately for anterior circulation and posterior circulation stroke.

Statistical analysis

Normality of continuous variables was assessed using graphical distribution and the Shapiro–Wilk test. Data are presented as numbers (percentages), medians (interquartile ranges [IQRs]), or means (standard deviations [SDs]), unless otherwise specified. Parametric variables were analyzed using Student’s t-test, and nonparametric variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U-test. Categorical variables were summarized using frequency descriptive analysis and compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. For the analysis examining the association between EF and the degree of collateral flow, Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was used. Propensity scores were calculated using a multivariate logistic regression model, with the 2 groups as dependent variables and age, sex, body mass index, medical history, smoking status, infarction location, and use of tissue plasminogen activator as independent variables. Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was used for propensity score-matched analysis. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 27.0; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). A 2-tailed p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics

Written informed consent was waived, as clinical information was obtained through routine medical care, and participation consent followed an opt-out approach. This study received approval from the Ethics Institutional Review Board of the University of Tsukuba Hospital (R03-359) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki guidelines. Clinical data were anonymized, prospectively collected, and retrospectively analyzed. The study adhered to Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Results

Population

Between April 2017 and July 2022, a total of 149 patients underwent MT for ischemic stroke at our hospital. Following application of the exclusion criteria, 55 patients were excluded, resulting in 94 patients eligible for analysis. Among these, 11 patients (12%) were categorized into the reduced EF group, and 83 patients (88%) comprised the preserved EF group. ASITN/SIR grading scores could not be evaluated in 17 patients. Therefore, 9 patients in the reduced EF group and 68 patients in the preserved EF group were enrolled in the analysis (Fig. 1). Baseline characteristics of the study population are presented in Table 1, with a summary of reduced EF cases provided in Table 2. The mean age was 76 years (SD = 11.0), and 46% of the participants were female. Median EF was 30.0% in the reduced EF group and 63.0% in the preserved EF group (p <0.001). The median NIHSS score at admission was 17 (IQR, 10.8–24.0). No significant differences were observed in other baseline characteristics between the 2 groups.

Clinical outcomes of the EF groups

Successful reperfusion, defined as modified thrombolysis in cerebral infarction grades 2b–3, was achieved in 85 patients (90%), with no difference between the 2 groups (91% vs. 90%, p = 1.000). Median onset-to-recanalization and door-to-recanalization times were 272 min (IQR, 164–562) and 135 min (IQR, 93–184), respectively, and did not differ significantly between groups (245 vs. 277 min and 116 vs. 135 min). Post-MT stroke volumes were also comparable between the 2 groups (13 vs. 12 cm3, p = 0.779). Favorable outcomes at discharge were achieved in 36% and 43% of patients in the reduced and preserved EF groups, respectively (p = 0.754). Length of hospital stay was similar between the 2 groups (23 vs. 22 days, p = 0.634) (Table 3). The odds ratio for favorable discharge outcomes, adjusted using IPTW, was 0.693 (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.176–2.732, p = 0.600) for the reduced EF group compared with the preserved EF group.

Association between EF and cerebral collateral flow

Both groups showed similar baseline characteristics except for EF (Table 4). Examination of the relationship between EF and cerebral collateral flow revealed that a significantly higher proportion of patients with reduced EF had “good collaterals” (5/9, 56%) compared with those with preserved EF (9/68, 13%) (p = 0.008) (Fig. 2A). In subgroup analysis of anterior circulation stroke, a similar relationship was seen (p = 0.005) (Fig. 2B and 2C). A mild trend was observed between EF and the ASITN/SIR grading score (rs = −0.212, 95% CI: −0.422 to 0.019, p = 0.064) (Fig. 3A). This association reached statistical significance in cases of anterior circulation stroke (rs = −0.245, 95% CI −0.461 to −0.001, p = 0.043) (Fig. 3B) but was not significant in posterior circulation stroke (rs = −0.006, 95% CI −0.721 to 0.714, p = 0.988) (Fig. 3C).

Discussion

The main novel findings of this study are as follows: (1) clinical outcomes after MT did not differ significantly regardless of EF; (2) patients with reduced EF demonstrated greater cerebral collateralization compared with those with preserved EF, and (3) a correlation between EF and cerebral collateral flow was observed in anterior circulation strokes.

Our study showed that patients with reduced EF developed more cerebral collaterals than those with preserved EF. Cerebral collateral flow, mainly through leptomeningeal anastomosis, is an essential compensatory mechanism in chronic intracranial atherosclerotic disease.16–18) Evidence from murine models supports this, demonstrating that decreased cerebral blood flow following internal carotid artery ligation results in enlargement of the leptomeningeal anastomosis and the circle of Willis.19) The development of leptomeningeal anastomosis is driven by hemodynamic changes, particularly alterations in pressure gradients and shear stress across pre-existing arterial connections. These mechanical stimuli activate endothelial cells, leading to the release of cytokines such as vascular endothelial growth factor, which promote vascular remodeling. Consequently, dormant arterial anastomoses are recruited and enlarged through a process known as arteriogenesis, rather than angiogenesis.20) Cerebral perfusion pressure is reduced in patients with HF ≤40% due to impaired cerebral autoregulation.21,22) Cerebrovascular resistance varies depending on the location of the vessels, and cerebral perfusion pressure is particularly low in the watershed zone, where blood flow is difficult to reach.23) Therefore, in patients with HF, peripheral cerebral vessels are prone to fluctuations in cerebral perfusion pressure and shear stress, which may induce structural and functional adaptations in leptomeningeal branches.

Although transient ischemic attack has been reported to lead to cerebral ischemic tolerance,24) no reports indicate that HF leads to cerebral ischemic tolerance. However, chronic premorbid hypoperfusion has been reported to activate preconditioning-like ischemic tolerance in patients with HF.25) Therefore, HF may decrease cerebral perfusion pressure, resulting in resistance to ischemia. No difference in functional prognosis in this study may be due to the development of collateral circulation and tolerance to cerebral ischemia. Further verification is required because the cerebral infarction volume could not be evaluated.

By contrast, a study on MT for large-vessel occlusion in patients with HF reported comparable levels of collateral flow between patients with normal and those with reduced EF. However, that study used a broader definition of reduced EF (<55%) and observed a milder difference in EF between the 2 groups (median EF 44.5% vs. 55%) than the current analysis (median EF 30% vs. 64%), which uses a stricter criterion of EF (≤40%).7) Although several studies have reported a significant relationship between EF and cerebral blood flow, the association between EF and cerebral collateral flow remains controversial and requires further validation.8,21,26,27)

Functional outcome

Reduced EF was not associated with unfavorable outcomes in this study. The IPTW analysis showed similar results. This finding challenges the conventional view that HF is an unfavorable prognostic factor in ischemic stroke.1–4) This discrepancy may be attributable to our focus on patients who underwent MT. A previous study reported results similar to ours,7) confirming the safety and efficacy of MT in patients with HF. The development of cerebral collateral flow in the reduced EF group in this study may have contributed to treatment outcomes that were comparable to those in the preserved EF group. Several studies have established that better cerebral collateral flows are associated with better reperfusion rates, smaller infarct volumes, and better mRS scores after MT.28–30) Therefore, we propose that reduced EF leads to chronic cerebral hypoperfusion, which promotes the development of cerebral collateral flow over time, subsequently enhancing ischemic resistance. This change is less likely to occur with only slightly decreased EF, as cerebral autoregulation may adequately compensate, which might explain the differences in results among previous studies.

To date, no reports have evaluated cerebral collateral flow based on EF and angiographic findings. The strength of this study is the use of cerebral angiographic findings to assess the development of cerebral collateral flow.

Limitations

This study has certain limitations. First, this was a retrospective observational study conducted at a single center. Therefore, prospective studies with larger cohorts would help validate these results. Second, the reduced EF group included a small number of patients, limiting the feasibility of multivariate analysis. Although baseline characteristics did not differ between the 2 groups, further studies involving larger populations are necessary to confirm the obtained results. Third, the absence of pre-MT data on stroke or penumbra volume affects the interpretation of post-MT stroke volume findings. Therefore, our results on stroke volume should be interpreted with caution, although both the Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score and NIHSS scores suggested a similar extent of the affected area before MT between groups. Fourth, cerebral infarction volume was automatically analyzed using RAPID software (iSchema View), which may have introduced discrepancies in actual volume. Fifth, EF data were obtained only once after admission, precluding the exclusion of patients with acute myocardial infarction or transiently reduced EF secondary to stroke. However, the development of collateral flow is gradual, as demonstrated in animal models.18) Therefore, the inclusion of patients with acute HF is unlikely to alter the observed trend of higher ASITN/SIR grading scores in the reduced EF group. In addition, the causes of reduced EF, which may vary and affect the outcomes, could not be assessed.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that reduced EF is associated with enhanced cerebral collateral flow and that MT demonstrates comparable efficacy in patients with reduced and preserved EF. These findings challenge the prevailing notion that HF is a determinant of poor prognosis in patients with ischemic stroke. However, future prospective and large cohort studies are warranted to validate our results because of the limited number of cases included in our study.
