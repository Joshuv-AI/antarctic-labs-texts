The organization principles of P. falciparum translation initiation features and their potential as drug targets
Authors: Rom Barzilay, Anastasia Lev, Davide Fraticelli, Ariel Ogran, Alicia Rojas, Paula Abou Karam, Neta Regev-Rudzki, Rivka Dikstein
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract Plasmodium (P.) falciparum, the deadliest malaria-causing parasite, challenges eradication efforts due to drug resistance. The regulation of mRNA translation as a therapeutic target remains unexplored. Most mRNAs contain unusually long 5′UTR and multiple upstream AUGs. How the parasite overcomes scanning distance and multiple uORF constraints is unknown. We employed the eIF1–eIF4G1 inhibitor i14G1-12, Ribo-seq, TIS-seq, and long-read data on P. falciparum trophozoites. Re-annotation of 5′UTRs, initiation site mapping, and translation efficiency (TE) analysis revealed extended 5′UTR length in hundreds of genes and actively translated uORFs in 81% of mRNAs. Active uORFs were predominantly initiated with AUG lacking specific context, while inactive uORFs were enriched with inhibitory AUG contexts. Notably, initiation within coding sequence through leaky scanning is also highly prevalent. Surprisingly, mRNAs with long 5′UTR and active uORFs exhibit higher TE than those without. We demonstrate that this is facilitated by a specific spacing of active uORFs and peptide length, optimizing scanning distance, ribosome density, and reinitiation. Remarkably, short-term i14G1-12 treatment reduced parasite viability and caused translational repression by enhancing leaky scanning and disturbing the unique arrangement of uORFs. Collectively, our findings uncovered the unusual translation regulatory features of P. falciparum and highlighted the therapeutic potential of targeting these mechanisms.

Introduction

Malaria is one of the deadliest infectious diseases globally, with 249 million cases and over 600 000 deaths in 2022, primarily affecting young children in Africa. Despite recent advances, the spread of drug-resistant Plasmodium spp., the causative agent of malaria, continues to challenge eradication efforts. Plasmodium falciparum, the most lethal malaria parasite species, undergoes a complex life cycle between mosquito and human hosts, with the intra-erythrocytic development cycle (IDC) causing the symptoms of the illness. The infection starts as the mosquito injects sporozoites into the human vasculature during a blood meal. The sporozoites invade the liver and undergo schizogony (asexual reproduction), culminating in the release of daughter merozoites into the bloodstream. The merozoites then invade red blood cells (RBCs), initiating a 48-h asexual stage cycle. This cycle progresses through the ring, trophozoite, and schizont stages, ending in the release of dozens of merozoites, which subsequently invade naïve host RBCs. Some merozoites undergo gametocytogenesis, transforming into sexual forms that are transmitted to mosquitoes, where they mature to produce sporozoites capable of infecting a new human host [1].

Plasmodium spp. employ cytosolic translational machinery for the translation of nuclear-encoded messenger RNAs (mRNAs). Additionally, these parasites rely on protein translation carried out in two organelles: the mitochondrion and a nonphotosynthetic plastid known as the apicoplast [1]. While these organelles have been targeted by drugs [2, 3], the parasite cytosolic translation has not been explored as a therapeutic target. Understanding how the parasite translation machinery and regulatory features differ from the human host is crucial for developing targeted treatments.

Translation initiation is the most regulated stage of protein synthesis [4]. In eukaryotes, most mRNAs initiate translation via a canonical pathway involving the recognition of the mRNA 5′ cap structure (m7G) and formation of the 43S Pre-Initiation Complex (PIC) consisting of the 40S small ribosomal subunit, initiator transfer RNA (tRNA), and eukaryotic initiation factors (eIFs) eIF2, eIF3, eIF1, and eIF1A. The PIC is recruited to the mRNA by eIF4F, the cap-binding complex, which then scans along the mRNA 5′ leader until a start codon is encountered, usually the first AUG triplet. Subsequently, the hydrolysis of eIF2-bound GTP and displacement of eIFs enable the joining of the 60S large ribosomal subunit to form the 80S elongation-competent ribosome.

The eIF4F consists of eIF4E, the cap-binding protein; eIF4G1, a scaffolding protein that binds eIF4E and recruits the 43S; and the helicase eIF4A, which unwinds secondary structures near the cap. The recruitment of the 43S PIC to the mRNA is facilitated by the direct interaction of eIF4G1 with the ribosome-bound and eIF3 and eIF1 [5–7]. The scanning and AUG recognition phases are controlled by eIF1 and eIF1A [8], which bind the 40S subunit and induce “open” or “closed” conformations of the 40S, which are either scanning competent or arrested, respectively [9]. Previous studies from human cells revealed that eIF4G1 interacts with eIF1 in a mutually exclusive manner with eIF4E due to nearby binding sites in eIF4G1 [10]. This interaction is required to promote leaky scanning from cap-proximal AUGs while inhibiting leaky scanning from cap-distal AUGs in weak contexts [11, 12].

Recently, it has become possible to study the role of eIF4G1–eIF1 interaction in cells by two small molecules: i14G1-10 and i14G1-12 [13]. These inhibitors were identified through a high throughput in vitro drug screen and subsequent functional translation assays. i14G1-10 mainly targets eIF4G1 and disrupts the interaction with eIF1, while i14G1-12 equally binds eIF4G1 and eIF1 and exerts an allosteric effect. Both chemically distinct molecules inhibit translation and cell growth via the same regulatory features [13]. The use of translation complex profiling (TCP-seq) and the selective TCP-seq methods upon i14G1-12-mediated inhibition revealed that eIF1–eIF4G1 plays a critical role in inhibiting leaky scanning. Interestingly, the eIF3-scanning ribosome complex was found to be highly vulnerable to eIF4G1–eIF1 inhibition, demonstrating the cooperation of eIF1 and eIF4G1 with eIF3 for the regulation of scanning and restriction of leaky scanning from cap-distal AUGs [12].

P. falciparum exhibits unique features related to translational control. First, it possesses one of the most AT-rich genomes (<80%) sequenced to date [14]. Second, the parasite mRNAs are characterized by exceptionally long 5′ leaders [15]. Additionally, over 90% of the P. falciparum mRNAs are predicted to contain at least one upstream open reading frame (uORF) [16]. In eukaryotes, uORFs are defined as short ORFs whose start codons are contained within the 5′ leader. These sequences typically repress translation initiation of the downstream main ORF by stalling scanning ribosomes or affecting mRNA stability through nonsense-mediated decay [17, 18]. The high number of potential uORFs in P. falciparum could have serious implications for translation, as scanning ribosomes will likely engage with uORFs, which may affect their chance of reaching the downstream coding sequence (CDS). To bypass an uORF, a ribosome can either scan past it through leaky scanning or translate it, remain attached to the mRNA, and then resume scanning to reinitiate translation at a downstream site [19]. The unusual features of P. falciparum mRNAs raise the question of how the ribosome overcomes the constraints of long scanning distance and the large number of potential uORFs in its 5' untranslated region (5′UTR). Furthermore, these features imply potentially greater vulnerability of P. falciparum to inhibition of eIFs that inhibit/promote leaky scanning and/or re-initiation, the main mechanisms of overcoming uORFs. Currently, evidence for the utilization of these mechanisms at a global scale is missing. Given the important role of eIF1–eIF4G1 in regulating scanning and leaky scanning, it is plausible that P. falciparum would be particularly susceptible to perturbations by i14G1s.

In this study, we explored the dynamics of translation initiation and eIF1–eIF4G1 inhibition in the deadly malaria parasite, P. falciparum. Using translation efficiency (TE) measurements, translation initiation site (TIS) mapping, and long-read data, we extended the 5′UTR annotation in hundreds of genes, resulting in an even longer average 5′UTR. We discriminate between active and inactive uORFs and demonstrate that most mRNAs (81%) have actively translated uORFs, starting predominantly with AUG lacking specific context, while inactive uORFs are enriched with an inhibitory AUG context. Remarkably, translation initiation within CDS through leaky scanning is also highly prevalent. Actively translated uORFs are associated with high translational efficiency (TE) of the respective genes. Investigating the underlying basis, we found that this is facilitated by a specific spacing of active uORFs relative to the 5′ end, between consecutive active uORFs and between the last uORF and the CDS start, as well as by the peptide length. These arrangements limit the initial scanning distance and optimize reinitiation. We further demonstrate that 1 hour exposure to the eIF1–eIF4G1 inhibitor i14G1-12 is sufficient to reduce parasite viability and cause translational repression by enhancing leaky scanning and impairing the unique organization of the active uORFs. Our findings uncovered several unique translation regulatory and mechanistic features of P. falciparum, improving our understanding of translation initiation and establishing eIF1–eIF4G1 inhibition as a novel therapeutic approach against the parasite.

Materials and methods

Plasmodium falciparum parasite culture and synchronization

P. falciparum (Pf) parasites (NF54, kindly donated from the MR4 malaria source) were cultured in pooled donor RBCs provided by the Israeli Blood Bank at 4% hematocrit and incubated at 37°C in a gas mixture of 1% O2 and 5% CO2 in N2 as was previously described [20]. Parasites were maintained in RPMI medium, 25 mg/ml HEPES, 50 μg/ml hypoxanthine, 2 mg/ml sodium bicarbonate, 20 μg/ml gentamycin, and 0.5% Albumax II. Growth was monitored using methanol fixed Giemsa-stained (Merck Millipore, #109203) blood smears. Pf cultures were tested for Mycoplasma twice a month using a MycoAlert™ PLUS kit.

Ring-stage parasites were synchronized twice a week for regular maintenance and at least once prior to experiment plating using d-sorbitol [21]. Briefly, Pf media were removed from Pf cultures, and the blood pellet was incubated at 37°C with d-sorbitol 5% solution for 5 min. Immediately after, sorbitol was removed by centrifugation and the blood pellet was washed twice with pre-warmed media.

Puromycin labeling

Synchronized to either the ring or trophozoite stage cultures with 2%–4% parasitemia were used. Five milliliters of infected RBCs were incubated with dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), i14G1-10 (20 μM), or i14G1-12 (10 μM) for 1 h. At this point, 0.5–1 ml of culture from each sample was collected for the parasite growth assay. The remaining culture was incubated for 5 min with puromycin (10 μg/ml), followed by RBC lysis with saponin (0.02% final concentration), leaving the parasite cells intact. After centrifugation (6000 × g, 5 min), the supernatant was removed, and the pellets were washed 3–4 times with pohosphate buffer saline (PBS) and resuspended in 50–80 μl RIPA buffer. Protein concentrations were measured using a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.). Samples were subjected to 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) followed by western blot using anti-puromycin antibody (1:1000) (Millipore).

Polysome profiling

For polysome profiling, synchronized P. falciparum culture was used. 60–100 ml of 4% hematocrit and at least 6% parasitemia culture at 28–32 h development was treated with DMSO, i14G1-10 (20 μM), or i14G1-12 (10 μM) for 1 h followed by 10-min incubation with 100 μg/ml cycloheximide (CHX) to arrest elongating ribosomes. Infected RBCs were pelleted at room temperature, 1500 rpm 5 min, and washed with PBS with 100 μg/ml CHX. RBC pellet was lysed with 2.2 volumes of cold lysis buffer: 1% (v/v) Nonidet P-40, 0.5% (w/v) sodium deoxycholate, 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) in polysome buffer [25 mM potassium-HEPES pH 7.2, 400 mM potassium acetate, 15 mM magnesium acetate, 100 μg/ml CHX, and 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT)]. The lysis step was improved by 10 min of rotation at 4°C. After the clarification by centrifugation at 20 000 × g for 10 min at 4°C, the lysate was layered on 2.5 ml of sucrose cushion (0.5 M in polysome buffer with 40 U/ml RNaseOUT) into open-top polyclear centrifuge tubes (Seton Scientific) and subjected 3:45 h of ultra-centrifugation 38 000 rpm with SW41 rotor at 4°C. The ribosome pellet was resuspended in 550 μl of polysome buffer. After 10 min of incubation, the ribosome suspension was centrifuged at 12 000 × g for 10 min at 4°C, and then layered atop a 10 ml continuous linear 10%–50% sucrose gradient, and centrifuged in an SW41 rotor (Beckman Coulter) for 3 h at 35 000 rpm at 4°C. Absorbance measurement was done at 254 nm using the ISCO absorbance detector UA-6.

Parasite growth assays

Trophozoite stage infected RBCs collected after drug treatment were pelleted by centrifugation at 1500 rpm and washed twice with a warm RPMI medium. After the second wash, infected RBCs were transferred into a 12-well plate with 2 ml of warm RPMI medium and uninfected RBCs for cultivation. The next day, parasitemia was measured by microscopy or by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) as described [22, 23]. Briefly, infected RBCs were stained for 20 min at 37°C with a staining solution containing 2 μM Hoechst-33342 (Invitrogen, #H1399) and 10 ng/ml thiazole orange (Sigma–Aldrich, #390062) in PBS (Biological Industries, #02-0230-1A). Analysis was performed using a ZE5™ flow cytometer (Bio-Rad) equipped with 355 and 488 nm lasers.

Ribo-seq and TIS-seq

For Ribo-seq and TI-seq, 150 ml of culture with 2% hematocrit and at least 8% parasitemia at 28–32 h development were used for sample preparation. The polysome isolation protocol was similar to one described for polysome profiling. For TI-seq, after 1 h treatment with DMSO, i14G1-10 (20 μM), or i14G1-12 (10 μM), infected RBCs were incubated for 5 min with 2 μg/ml of harringtonine (HAR), followed by the addition of 100 μg/ml CHX for 5 min. For Ribo-seq, the culture was treated with 100 μg/ml CHX for 5 min, followed by polysome isolation. Approximately 1 ml of lysate was taken for total RNA purification after infected RBCs lysate clarification. For both TI-seq and Ribo-seq, ribosome pellets resuspended in polysome buffer were treated with 2.88 U/μg Micrococcal nuclease for 1 h at room temperature in the presence of 1× Micrococcal nuclease buffer. To stop the digestion reaction, SUPERase·In™ RNase Inhibitor (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) was added, and the sample was loaded on 1 M sucrose cushion into 13 mm × 56 mm thick-wall polycarbonate tubes. Ultracentrifugation was done in TLA-11 rotor for 4 h at 70 000 rpm at 4°C. RNA was isolated using the TRIzol reagent, and Direct-zol RNA MiniPrep Kit (ZYMO Research) was used for high-throughput library preparation as described [24].

Ribo-seq, TIS-seq, and RNA-seq data analysis

The initial analysis steps followed the protocol described by [24]. Ribosome footprint and mRNA sequencing reads were subjected to quality filtering. Reads containing adapter sequences were retained, and those without adapters were discarded. Adapter-containing reads were trimmed to remove the first base and the adapter sequence using the Cutadapt algorithm [25]. For RNA sequencing reads, Cutadapt was also used to retain only those with a minimum length of 20 nucleotides by specifying the -m (minimum length) parameter. Trimmed reads were filtered to exclude those mapping to noncoding RNA (ncRNA) by aligning them to a ncRNA database using the Bowtie algorithm [26]. The ncRNA database was generated from the P. falciparum genome (version 68) available in the PlasmoDB website [27]. Read length distribution plots were generated using FastQC [28]. Based on these plots, CHX-treated reads of length 28–32 nucleotides and HAR-treated reads of length 29–34 nucleotides were retained. Filtering was performed using the Cutadapt tool by specifying the appropriate values for the -m (minimum length) and -M (maximum length) parameters. The reads were uniquely aligned to the P. falciparum genome using the STAR algorithm [29]. To minimize false splice junction detection, a conservative threshold of 500 base pairs (bp) was applied to the maximum splice junction length using the alignIntronMax parameter [30].

Calculation of RPKM and translation efficiency

RPKM (Reads Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads) values for both mRNA and ribosome footprints were calculated using the edgeR package in R [31]. edgeR normalization factors were applied to account for library size differences. mRNA RPKM values were derived from exon models, while ribosome CHX footprint RPKM values were based on CDS models for each gene. To reduce potential bias from translation initiation at the CDS start site, the first 30 bases of each CDS region were excluded from the gene models. Exon and CDS models were generated using the R GenomicFeatures package [32]. Gene expression filtering was performed by retaining genes with mRNA RPKM values of at least 5 in at least one sample across both treatment groups (DMSO and i14G1-12). For genes that passed this filter, TE was determined by normalizing the average ribosome footprint RPKM by the corresponding mRNA RPKM. This analysis was conducted across the 3681 genes that met the expression threshold.

Lengthening of mRNA 5′ leader regions using long-read sequencing data

Long-read, single-molecule transcriptomic sequencing data generated by Pacific Biosciences (PacBio) for total and nuclear-fractionated RNA in trophozoite-stage P. falciparum parasites [33] were downloaded as FASTQ files for each sequenced sample. The data were processed according to the general steps described in the original study. Raw sequencing reads were aligned to the P. falciparum genome (PlasmoDB version 68) using the minimap2 tool [34] in spliced alignment mode with a maximum intron length of 5 kb (-ax splice -uf -G 5k –secondary = no –junc-bed). Candidate nonredundant isoforms were generated from the aligned reads by using the Transcriptome Annotation by Modular Algorithms (TAMA) tools (Kuo et al., 2020). This process involved collapsing redundant transcript models (TAMA collapse) for each sample using the following parameters: -x capped -rm low_mem -sj sj_priority -lde 5 -sjt 20 -a 100 -z 100 -m 20. Collapsed exon models from individual samples were merged into a single BED-derived file using TAMA merge, guided by PlasmoDB annotations. The following parameters were applied: -a 300 -z 300 -m 20 -d merge_dup. Coding potential and CDS annotations for the candidate nonredundant isoforms were obtained using the long-read transcript characterization tool SQANTI3 [35], with PlasmoDB genome annotations serving as the reference. Coding transcripts that exactly matched existing P. falciparum mRNA annotations (i.e. identical CDS start and end coordinates and the same number and positions of splice junctions) were discarded. Remaining candidate transcripts were compared to reference mRNAs based on the coordinates of their CDS start sites, chromosome, and strand. Transcripts with extended 5′ leader regions (measured from the most upstream coordinate to one base before the CDS start) relative to their matched reference mRNA were selected for further analysis. This selection process was performed using a custom Python script.

Mapping of TIS-seq coverage peaks

Regions enriched with HAR reads across the mRNA landscape were identified using the MACS2 tool [36]. Peak calling was performed without modeling and without filtering PCR duplicates, applying a false discovery rate cutoff of 0.01. CHX reads were used as the control (parameters: –keep-dup all –nomodel –extsize = 1 -q 0.01). The resulting single-base peak summits that mapped to expressed genes were retained. Peak strand information was assigned based on the strand of the overlapping gene. Peaks overlapping genes on opposing strands were discarded, accounting for just under 1.5% of peaks in both treatment groups (DMSO and i14G1-12). To account for P-site offset positioning, the retained peaks were shifted 12 bases downstream according to their strand information.

Analysis of TIS-seq peak sequence contexts

To analyze the sequence context surrounding TIS-seq peaks, the single-base peak summits were extended by six bases upstream and downstream, resulting in 13-base long sequences. Motif enrichment discovery was performed using the MEME tool [37] with the parameters -rna -nmotifs 1 -w 9, specifying the identification of a single motif with a fixed width of 9 bases, using the RNA alphabet.

Identification of actively translated ORFs and analysis of uORF initiation contexts

To identify actively translated ORFs, TIS-seq peaks were mapped to potential ORFs across the genome. Potential ORFs were defined as sequences of at least two amino acids, beginning with a start codon (AUG, or AUG and near-cognate start codons in the human dataset) and terminating at an in-frame stop codon (UAA, UAG, or UGA). Peaks were extended by six bases downstream and eight bases upstream to account for variability in positioning relative to the first base of the start codon. In cases where multiple potential ORFs overlapped the same extended peak, the most upstream ORF was selected to reflect the 5′ to 3′ direction of translation. The mapping was performed while ensuring that the chromosome and gene of the ORF matched those of the peak. Motif discovery of uORF initiation contexts was performed using the STREME algorithm [38]. Active and inactive uORF start codons were extended by six bases upstream and downstream, generating 13-base long sequences centered on the start codon. STREME was run with the parameters -rna -w 12, specifying the identification of motifs with a fixed width of 12 bases, using the RNA alphabet.

Metagene coverage plot analysis

Metagene coverage plots were generated using the R metagene2 package [39]. Genes with CDS regions of at least 100 bases and 5′ leader regions of at least 50 bases were included in the analysis, representing 98% of all genes. Mean coverage across replicate samples was normalized to Reads Per Million (RPM).

Gene ontology enrichment analysis

Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed using R topGO package (Alexa et al., 2006). The analysis focused on the Biological Process (BP) domain, employing the “classic” algorithm in combination with Fisher’s exact test. The top 10 GO terms were selected based on p-values, with terms showing P < 0.05 reported. Gene-to-GO term mapping was conducted using the PlasmoDB version 68 GO terms for P. falciparum.

Reporter gene assay

To examine the effect of positional uORFs on translation, we generated linear DNA constructs encoding Renilla luciferase flanked by AT-rich 5′UTR and 3′UTR sequences with or without position-specific AUG-initiated uORF as shown in Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. S5. The DNA sequences were synthesized (Twist Bioscience; Supplementary Table S2) and amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) with appropriate primers containing T7 promoter sequence and a reverse primer that introduces a polyA tail. Next, mRNA was synthesized using the RiboMAX™ T7 Large Scale RNA Production System (Promega, Cat. No. P1280), followed by 5′-capping of in vitro transcribed RNA, which was performed using the Vaccinia Capping System (New England Biolabs, Cat. No. M2080S) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Capped RNA was purified using the Quick-RNA™ Microprep Kit (Zymo Research, R2062), eluted in 15 μl of RNase-free water, and stored in −80°C. 105 HEK293T cells in a 24-well plate were transiently transfected with the in vitro synthesized mRNA reporters (100 ng k-mer Renilla reporter and 400 ng Firefly normalizing control reporter per well) by the Lipofectamine 2000 protocol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat No. 11668019). Six hours after transfection, cells were lysed with 100 μl reporter lysis buffer, and luminescence was measured. For normalizing transfection efficiency, results were calculated as Renilla activity relative to Firefly activity.

Experimental examination of uORFs organization. Reporter constructs used include a common AT-rich 5′UTR, Renilla enzyme, and 3′UTR background, with either no uORF or 1 or 3 uORFs at the indicated locations as shown in the scheme. One set with the uORF initiation context of CCGAUGA (left) and the second of UAUAUGU (right). Statistical testing

Statistical differences were assessed using Analysis of Variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s HSD test for post-hoc comparisons when assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were met. In cases where these assumptions were violated, the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied, followed by Dunn’s test for post-hoc pairwise comparisons. For the reporter gene assay, a Student’s t-test was applied. All statistically significant differences between groups are indicated by asterisks as follows: P < 0.05 (*), P < 0.01 (**), P < 0.001 (***), andP < 0.0001 (****).

Data availability

The Ribo-seq, TIS-seq, and RNA-seq datasets generated during this study have been deposited in NCBI’s GEO database. The accession number for the P. falciparum data is GSE286083. The accession numbers for the HEK293T RNA-seq and Ribo-seq data are GSE301926 and GSE301928.

Results

The eIF4G1–eIF1 inhibitor i14G1-12 inhibits translation and cell growth in P. falciparum

The presence of an unusually large number of potential uORFs in P. falciparum mRNAs implies that the parasite may be particularly sensitive to eIFs that regulate leaky scanning and re-initiation. We, therefore, set out to investigate the effects of the eIF4G1–eIF1 inhibitors i14G1-10 and i14G1-12 on the P. falciparum parasite at the blood stage. Since translation is reported to be differentially regulated during stage transition in P. falciparum [40–42], we first examined the level of active translation during the ring and trophozoite blood-stage parasites by utilizing the puromycin incorporation assay. Puromycin is a structural analog of aminoacylated-transfer RNA, and its incorporation into nascent peptide chains by elongating ribosomes leads to premature translation termination. The puromycin-labeled nascent peptides can be detected by an anti-puromycin antibody, serving as a marker of active translation [43]. Synchronized cultures of ring and trophozoite stage P. falciparum NF54 strain parasites were labeled with puromycin for 5 min and analyzed by western blotting using the anti-puromycin antibody. The results showed a very low level of nascent polypeptide labeling in the ring stage parasites, while the trophozoites were efficiently labeled, indicating active translation in this stage (Fig. 1A). Based on these findings, we proceeded with further experiments using only the trophozoite stage parasites. Next, we investigated the effects of i14G1-10 and i14G1-12 on P. falciparum translation using the puromycin incorporation assay. Trophozoite stage parasite cultures were treated with DMSO, i14G1-10 (20 μM), and i14G1-12 (10 μM) for 1 h, followed by a pulse of puromycin labeling and analysis by western blotting. Treatment with i14G1-12 revealed a significant loss of nascent polypeptide labeling, while i14G1-10 showed no detectable decrease in polypeptide labeling (Fig. 1B). Dose–response nascent chain labeling experiment revealed an IC50 of 8.213 and 24.210 μM for i14G1-12 and i14G1-10, respectively, indicating that i14G1-12 exerts a stronger inhibitory effect on P. falciparum translation than i14G1-10 (Fig. 1C).

To further explore the effect of i14G1s on P. falciparum translation, we treated trophozoite parasites with DMSO, i14G1-10 (20 μM), and i14G1-12 (10 μM) for 1 h, followed by cell lysis and sucrose density gradient (10%–50%) sedimentation. The polysome profiles of the i14G1-12-treated samples showed an increase in the 80S mono-ribosome fraction and a concomitant decrease in the heavy polysome fractions, confirming a defect in translation initiation (Fig. 1D, right). In contrast, these effects were not observed upon treatment with i14G1-10, leading us to exclude the compound from further experiments and analysis (Fig. 1D, left)

To examine whether the effect of i14G1-12 on translation impacts cell survival, we determined the effect of i14G1-12 on P. falciparum parasitemia levels (proportion of infected cells out of all cells). Trophozoite-stage parasites were treated with DMSO and i14G1-12 (10 μM) for 1 h, followed by measuring parasitemia after schizogony the following day as a parameter of cell survival. The result revealed that similar to its effect in mammalian cells, i14G1-12 also inhibited cell survival in P. falciparum (Fig. 1E). The IC50 determination of the cell viability revealed a value of 8.159 μM, which is strikingly similar to the IC50 determined for the puromycin-incorporation assay, suggesting that the reduction in survival is closely linked to translation inhibition (Fig. 1F).

Ribosome profiling reveals extensive translation within P. falciparum 5′ leaders and global i14G1-12 effects

To obtain a genome-wide, quantitative view of translation in P. falciparum and examine how it is affected by i14G1-12, we performed ribosome profiling (deep sequencing of ribosome-protected RNA fragments) on trophozoite-stage parasites following short-term treatment (1 h) with the compound (Fig. 2A). To assess TE, we used CHX, a translation elongation inhibitor that stalls all translating ribosomes. Measurement of total mRNA abundance from the same samples was used for normalization. While translation regulation during P. falciparum IDC has been previously studied [16, 41], a global mapping of TISs in the parasite is still lacking. To fill this gap, we employed the translation inhibitor harringtonine (HAR) alongside CHX, which was shown to immobilize initiating ribosomes, thereby enabling global mapping of TISs [24]. For library preparation, we initially used the standard protocol with RNase I, but the quality of the libraries was consistently low, perhaps due to the low GC content. We therefore switched to micrococal nuclease (Mnase), as previously reported for P. falciparum [16].

Initial inspection of coverage distributions from the P. falciparum Ribo-seq and TIS-seq libraries revealed a substantial accumulation of reads within annotated 5′ leaders of mRNAs. However, we also observed reads within intergenic regions, ranging from 5% to 8% of total reads, depending on the library and treatment group (Supplementary Fig. S1A). These reads primarily accumulated upstream of the annotated mRNA 5′-end boundaries, suggesting that the 5′ leader is either incompletely annotated or entirely missing. Indeed, analysis of the parasite’s reference annotation dataset [27] shows that 17% of the mRNAs lack a 5′ leader.

To assign the intergenic reads onto their originating mRNAs, we utilized publicly available long-read RNA sequencing data to accurately define the 5′ leader regions (Fig. 2B). By leveraging experimentally verified full-length mRNA sequences, this approach builds upon prior studies, which relied on a fixed leader length or applied statistical modeling to define P. falciparum 5′ leaders for subsequent analyses. By using this strategy, we managed to extend the 5′ leader of 1081 out of 4421 mRNAs with annotated leaders and define a new 5′ leader for 241 out of the 897 mRNAs that previously lacked one (Fig. 2C). Overall, 1322 mRNAs were assigned extended 5′ leaders (25% of the total). Consistent with this, 26%–32% of the reads that were initially mapped to the intergenic regions—varying by library and treatment group—map to the extended 5′ leader regions (Supplementary Fig. S1B). Determination of 5′ leader length distributions after the update reveals a marked increase in the median leader length in the revised dataset, from 497 to 560 nt, highlighting the impact of the revised annotation on mRNA structure. A comparison of the revised dataset to human and mouse transcriptomes shows that P. falciparum mRNAs have remarkably longer 5′ leaders, distinguishing it from typical eukaryotes (Fig. 2D). Figure 2E displays two genes, RPL17 and CBP1, where a substantial portion of reads, initially mapped upstream of the annotated mRNA 5′-end, are now included in the newly annotated extended 5′ leaders. We used the revised annotation for all subsequent analyses.

To quantitatively identify the expressed genes in our sequencing data, we filtered our RNA-seq data based on RPKM (Reads Per Kilobase per Million mapped reads) of the exon model across the samples (see “Materials and methods” section). In the P. falciparum dataset, we detected 3999 expressed genes (75% of all protein-coding genes), which is comparable to previous expression analysis in the parasite [16, 41, 44]. Only genes encoding mRNAs with 5′ leaders were qualified for subsequent analyses, resulting in 3681 genes for P. falciparum (92% of expressed genes). We observed high Pearson correlation values between biological replicates of the same library (Supplementary Fig. S1C).

Analysis of P-site assignment revealed no significant enrichment at frame 0 (Supplementary Fig. S2A). This can be explained by the strong A/T cleavage bias of MNase, ∼30-fold higher for cutting near A/T bases [45, 46], which is likely to compromise precise trimming of ribosome-protected fragments, particularly at the 5′ end, and thus limits accurate P-site inference. A similar analysis of the previously reported Ribo-seq data of P. falciparum [16] also resulted in a lack of P-site inference to frame. These findings highlight a limitation of the MNase (Supplementary Fig. S2B). Other special features of P. falciparum, such as heightened 5′UTR translation and enhanced leaky scanning, can also contribute this result (see below).

As we wanted to compare the P. falciparum translational features to mammals, we also used Ribo-seq and TIS-seq data from human HEK293T cells (using RNase I) that we generated as the control for another project and were processed in the same way. In this dataset, the P-site is clearly apparent at the expected frame 0 (Supplementary Fig. S2C).

We next analyzed the distribution of Ribo-seq reads on the expressed genes. To accurately determine the translation activity across the mRNA length, we designated a “CDS start” region by flanking the CDS start site with 30 bases on each side. The portions of the 5′ leader and CDS remaining after excluding the CDS start were designated as the “5′ leader” and “CDS body” regions, respectively (Fig. 2F). First, we compared the read distribution between P. falciparum and humans. As expected, most Ribo-seq reads in both organisms are mapped to the CDS body. However, a significantly larger proportion of reads in P. falciparum (27% versus 7%) also mapped to 5′ leaders (Fig. 2F), indicating extensive translation activity in this region compared to humans. In line with the polysome profiling and puromycin labeling results, treatment with i14G1-12 reduced the proportion of reads mapping to the CDS body, while increasing the proportion of reads mapping to 5′ leaders (Fig. 2F). This suggests that i14G1-12 enhances ribosomal loading onto mRNAs in P. falciparum, similarly to our previous observations in mammalian cells [11].

Mapping P. falciparum TISs and the effect of i14G1-12 on initiation

To globally identify TISs from our TIS-seq data, we employed MACS2, a widely used tool for coverage peak calling from high-throughput sequencing data [36]. Single-base peak summits were identified from regions enriched with HAR reads, using CHX reads as a control to distinguish true initiation events from the background signal of elongating ribosomes (see “Materials and methods” section). Since the actively translated codon within a ribosome footprint is positioned at the P-site, we shifted the peaks downstream to accurately determine the TIS positions. Examination of peak distribution within the CDS start regions revealed clustering at the CDS start codons following shifting, validating our approach (Supplementary Fig. S3A). Next, we analyzed the distribution of peaks across the mRNAs. A peak was mapped to the CDS start region in the majority of P. falciparum (75%) genes, reflecting translation initiation at the CDS. In line with this observation, analysis of the peak sequence contexts using the MEME motif discovery algorithm [47] revealed significant enrichment of a strong Kozak AUG motif (RNNAUG). Notably, position (-3A) relative to the AUG start codon is the most prevalent in P. falciparum, while both A and G are observed at this position in the human dataset (Fig. 3A). Remarkably, 85% of P. falciparum genes also exhibited peaks in their 5′ leaders, with most of these genes containing multiple peaks. In contrast, only 57% of human genes displayed 5′ leader-mapped peaks, most of which contained just a single one (Fig. 3B). These proportions remained consistent when analyzing only the subset of genes where peaks mapped to the CDS start region, confirming that the higher abundance of 5′ leader-mapped peaks in P. falciparum is not attributable to differences in peak detection efficiency compared to humans. Sequence context analysis of the 5′ leader-mapped peaks in P. falciparum revealed an exclusive selection of AUG codons. In contrast, the results in the human data showed significant enrichment of near-cognate codons (Fig. 3C), aligning with previous reports [11, 48, 49]. A significant proportion of P. falciparum genes (71%) also contained peaks mapping to the CDS body, downstream of the CDS start site (Fig. 3D). Sequence context analysis of these peaks revealed significant enrichment of an AUG with a preference for “A” at position (-3), supporting their validity as true TISs (Fig. 3E). Analysis of these CDS peaks relative to the annotated start position revealed significant accumulation at the beginning of the CDS, even though potential AUGs are uniformly distributed throughout this region (Fig. 3F). This suggests that those peaks resulted from leaky scanning, where ribosomes bypassed the CDS start site and initiated translation at the next downstream AUG, rather than from direct mRNA-binding mechanisms.

Treatment with i14G1-12 resulted in a 17% reduction in the total number of mapped peaks compared to the DMSO group, with lower counts observed across all mRNA regions (Fig. 3G). Interestingly, the largest reduction occurred in peaks mapping to the 5′ leaders (21%), which have the weakest initiation contexts among all regions. This was followed by a 13% decrease in peaks mapping to the CDS body. However, the number of peaks at the CDS start site, which features the strongest initiation contexts, did not significantly differ following treatment with the i14G1-12 (Fig. 3G). These findings show that the inhibitory effect of i14G1-12 on translation initiation depends on the strength of the ORF initiation context, consistent with their ability to enhance leaky scanning in mammalian cells [12].

The presence of multiple 5′-leader-mapped peaks in the majority of P. falciparum genes suggests that the parasite genome produces an unusually high number of actively translated uORFs, highlighting the need for further investigation into their functions and the mechanisms regulating translation of the mORF in the context of multiple uORFs. To achieve this, we utilized the TIS-seq dataset to globally identify actively translated uORFs from the pool of potential ones across the P. falciparum genome. As the sequence context of P. falciparum peaks revealed the typical selection of AUGs, we defined actively translated uORFs as sequences of at least two amino acids that start with an AUG codon positioned near a 5′ leader-mapped peak and terminate with an in-frame stop codon (Supplementary Fig. S3B). Using this approach, we defined a total of 8965 actively translated uORFs out of 39 973 potential ones (22%, Supplementary Table S1). Based on the position of their stop sites relative to the CDS start sites, we classified the uORFs into two categories: nonoverlapping uORFs, with stop sites located upstream of the CDS start sites, and overlapping uORFs, with stop sites located downstream of the CDS start sites, in a different reading frame. Nonoverlapping uORFs constitute the vast majority of all potential uORFs in the P. falciparum (98%) genome and represent a similarly high proportion among the actively translated ones (Fig. 3H and Supplementary Table S2). To compare these findings with humans, we also identified active uORFs among the potential uORFs in the human genome, using near-cognate AUGs as start codons. While most of the active uORFs in this dataset were nonoverlapping, the proportion of overlapping uORFs was significantly higher than in P. falciparum (Fig. 3H). Since the translation of overlapping uORFs typically inhibits CDS translation [18, 50], this observation may reflect differences in the regulatory roles of uORFs between the two organisms. In addition, only 23% of the active uORFs in the human dataset were initiated with AUG start codons, while the remaining used near-cognate start codons. Since AUG codons initiate translation more effectively than near-cognate AUGs, this suggests that initiation events at uORFs are generally frequent in P. falciparum compared to humans. Supplementary Tables S1 and S3 summarize the counts of all potential and actively translated uORFs or the counts of genes with potential and active uORFs, respectively. Overall, active uORFs were identified in 83% of P. falciparum genes that can encode them, with most of the genes containing multiple uORFs, whereas only 53% of human genes with potential uORFs encode actively translated uORFs, with most of the genes containing just a single uORF—mirroring the proportions observed in TISs mapping to 5′ leaders (Fig. 3I and J). Considering the prevalence of TISs within the CDS body of P. falciparum, we used the same approach to annotate them. We identified a large group of active ORFs whose start sites are located downstream of the CDS start but in a different reading frame, resulting in a different stop codon than the CDS. We termed these ORFs downstream ORFs (dORFs). Overall, we identified 4102 dORFs, representing 2% of the potential ones, with 62% of the genes containing these active dORFs (Fig. 3J). Finally, we searched for ORFs that share the same in-frame stop codon as the CDS but have a different start codon, thus providing alternative TISs to the main ORF. In the P. falciparum genome, nearly all of these ORFs (99%) start downstream of the CDS start site (Fig. 3J). Overall, we identified 814 active ORFs with alternative TISs relative to the CDS. These alternative TISs are present in ∼20% of the genes capable of encoding them.

uORFs partition P. falciparum long 5′ UTRs to facilitate reinitiation, ribosome flow, and CDS translation

Considering the exceptionally high number of actively translated uORFs encoded in P. falciparum, we next wished to explore their impact on translation. To this end, we classified genes into two groups—those in which no potential uORFs were actively translated and those with at least one active uORF—and examined their CDS TE. Remarkably, genes with active uORFs exhibited significantly higher TE values than those where no uORFs were translated (Fig. 4A). Furthermore, the TE of genes with actively translated uORFs was comparable to genes lacking any potential uORFs altogether (Fig. 4A). These findings indicate that uORFs do not globally suppress translation in P. falciparum. A positive relationship between uORF and CDS translation was also observed in genes where no dORFs were translated, regardless of the number of active uORFs (Fig. 4B). This rules out the possibility that elevated TE resulted from independent initiation events within the CDS and highlights the parasite’s capacity to maintain efficient CDS translation despite the presence of multiple uORFs. Since nearly all of P. falciparum uORFs are nonoverlapping, we conducted a similar analysis on the human dataset, focusing exclusively on genes where all uORFs are nonoverlapping. In contrast to P. falciparum, translation of uORFs initiating from AUG codon in the human data was negatively associated with lower TE, while genes with non-AUG uORFs exhibited significantly higher TE values, similar to genes with AUG uORFs in the parasite (Fig. 4C). The positive relationship between uORF and CDS translation in P. falciparum was preserved following treatment with i14G1-12, regardless of active dORFs presence or the number of active uORFs per gene (Supplementary Fig. S4).

As our findings suggest opposing regulatory roles for AUG uORFs between P. falciparum and humans, we hypothesized that the positive relationship between uORFs and CDS translation in the parasite reflects an adaptive mechanism in which uORFs partition the long 5′ leaders into smaller segments, mitigating the inhibitory effects that prolonged scanning may impose on ribosome progression. This partitioning can also optimize ribosome distribution across the 5′ leader, regulating their traffic and reducing the risk of collisions that could trigger mRNA [51–53]. To test this hypothesis, we examined the arrangement of active uORFs within 5′ leaders, focusing on three key intervals: the distance from the 5′ leader start site to the first active uORF, the spacing between active uORFs, and the distance from the last uORF to the CDS start site (Fig. 4D). In line with our hypothesis, we found that the first active uORF is positioned at a distance that significantly shortens the effective length of the 5′ leader, regardless of the number of active uORFs encoded by the gene (Fig. 4E). This interval, with a median of 149 nts, closely aligns with the typical 5′ leader length in mammals, suggesting a conserved range for effective scanning upon mRNA recognition by ribosomes. The spacing between active uORFs—calculated as the mean of distances between the uORFs per gene—further partitions the 5′ leader into shorter segments (Fig. 4F). The distance from the last active uORF to the CDS start site closely mirrors the distance range from the 5′ leader start site to the first active uORF (Fig. 4G), suggesting that conserved spacing may optimize ribosome distribution across both ends of the 5′ leader. Collectively, these findings indicate that active uORFs partition P. falciparum long 5′ leaders into shorter intervals, resembling typical mammalian 5′ leaders. This partitioning could facilitate ribosome progression and regulate traffic, allowing the parasite to maintain efficient translation of the CDS. Moreover, the median distance between the last active uORF and the CDS start site is 144 nts, with 75% of the genes having the last uORF positioned at least 80 nts upstream of the CDS—a threshold at which AUG uORFs have been shown to lose their inhibitory effect on downstream translation [54]. Similarly, the median of the spacers between active uORFs was 103 nts, with 65% of the genes showing median spacings >79 nts that facilitate reinitiation.

In addition to their arrangement, uORF length also impacts reinitiation. Short uORFs have been shown to promote reinitiation in eukaryotes, presumably by allowing ribosomes to retain initiation factors during their brief elongation phase, enabling another round of scanning upon uORF termination. Experimental data indicate that reinitiation is efficient after translating uORFs that are 10–12 codons long [19, 55]. We found that the active uORFs in the P. falciparum dataset are very short, having a median length of 9 codons (Fig. 4H). Most of these uORFs (65%) are 12 codons or shorter, with only 17% exceeding 20 codons (Fig. 4H). Together with their spacious arrangement, the short sizes of the uORFs facilitate repeated rounds of reinitiation—both between themselves and ultimately at the CDS.

i14G1-12 inhibits P. falciparum translation by disturbing the unique spacing of active uORFs

As our findings revealed the strongest impact of i14G1-12 on 5′UTR TISs, we analyzed the uORFs arrangement following i14G1-12 treatment. Intriguingly, we found that the distance between the 5′ leader start and the first active uORF is increased (Fig. 4I) and the spacing between active uORFs was also expanded (Fig. 4J). However, no significant difference was observed in the spacing between the last active uORF and the CDS start site (Fig. 4K). As uORFs may also play a role in regulating ribosome flow to prevent congestion, reduced uORF translation may lead to excessive ribosome buildup downstream, increasing the risk of collisions and further impairing CDS translation. Consistent with this, metagene analysis of average ribosomal coverage across 5′ leaders and the CDS regions revealed increased ribosome accumulation at the CDS start site and reduced coverage across the CDS following treatment with i14G1-12 (see Fig. 6B below). Taken together, these findings suggest that by promoting uORF bypassing, i14G1-12 leads to ribosome crowding near the CDS start site, while extended scanning distances may also reduce the total number of ribosomes progressing to the CDS through the 5′ leaders. This highlights inefficient initiation at the CDS start site, alongside potential ribosome dissociation during prolonged scanning, as key mechanisms by which i14G1-12 inhibits translation in P. falciparum.

Discrimination between active and inactive AUG selection involves an inhibitory AUG context

Despite their abundance, the active uORFs in the P. falciparum dataset represent only 22% of the potential ones, suggesting a selective process that governs uORF translation and ensures precise arrangement. Since ORF translation is tightly regulated by the nucleotide sequence surrounding the start codon [19], we used the STREME motif discovery algorithm [38] to examine the initiation contexts of active uORF compared to those of the inactive uORFs. However, no significant enrichment of nucleotides flanking the start codon was found (Fig. 4L, top). The absence of distinguishing initiation motifs in active uORFs led us to hypothesize that uORF translation may instead be regulated by weaker initiation contexts in the inactive uORFs. In line with that, analysis of the inactive uORF initiation contexts revealed a significant enrichment of an exceptionally weak AUG context (UAUAUGU) compared to the active ones (Fig. 4L, bottom). These findings suggest that uORF translation in P. falciparum is regulated, at least in part, by inhibitory initiation contexts that promote the bypassing of uORFs marked for translational repression. Since i14G1-12 promotes the bypassing of uORFs, most likely via leaky scanning [12], we hypothesized that uORFs with stronger initiation contexts would be preferentially translated under treatment with the compound. Consistent with this, analysis of active uORF initiation contexts in the i14G1-12 treatment group revealed an enrichment of a stronger initiation motif, particularly with a preference for an A at position −3 as well as +4, compared to the inactive uORFs (Fig. 4M, top). This suggests that i14G1-12 disrupts the selective translation of uORFs, interfering with fine-tuned regulatory mechanisms that govern translation in P. falciparum. A similar inhibitory initiation context was identified when analyzing the inactive uORFs compared to the active ones in the i14G1-12-treated samples.

AUG-context-dependent effect of uORF positioning in mammalian cells

Since validating reporter assays in P. falciparum is technically very challenging, we used human HEK293T cells to examine how uORF organization within a long 5′UTR, similar to that of the parasite, affects translation. We constructed two sets of Renilla luciferase reporter mRNAs, each preceded by a synthetic A/T-rich 500-nt 5′UTR. These constructs either lacked uORFs or contained AUG-initiated uORFs positioned at various distances from the 5′ cap and the downstream AUG(s) (see Fig. 5 for scheme and Supplementary Fig. S5 for sequences). In the first set, the uORF AUGs are in a weak mammalian initiation context (CCGAUGA), and the second bears the repressive P. falciparum context (UAUAUGU). The constructs were in vitro transcribed to generate capped and polyadenylated mRNAs and then transfected into HEK293T cells. The results showed that 1 or 3 uORFs in the CCGAUGA context progressively reduced TE compared to constructs with no uORFs (Fig. 5, left panel), consistent with observations from human Ribo-seq data (Fig. 4C). When the spacing between the first two uORFs was shortened to a length expected to hinder re-initiation, repression was less pronounced, suggesting that the second inhibitory AUG was not utilized (Fig. 5, left panel). With the weak P. falciparum context, a single uORF inhibited translation, but three properly spaced uORFs led to almost no repression (Fig. 5, right panel), suggesting that with the U-rich initiation context, certain uORF arrangements can facilitate translation of the main ORF also in mammalian cells. Together, these findings highlight a significant difference in reinitiation efficiency, with the human translation machinery showing a markedly weaker capacity compared to the parasite.

Additional regulatory roles of uORFs and i14G1-12 impact on P. falciparum translation

To further investigate the functional implications of uORF translation, we performed GO analysis of biological processes for genes with active uORFs and for genes where no uORFs were translated. Interestingly, genes with active uORFs were significantly enriched for housekeeping functions, while genes without active uORFs were enriched for parasite-specific functions, including antigenic variation, symbiotic interaction with the host, and evasion of host immune response (Tables 1 and 2). Since housekeeping genes are involved in maintaining fundamental cellular activity, their expression must be tightly regulated to prevent fluctuations that could disrupt cell survival [56, 57]. This led us to hypothesize that uORFs may have a role in stabilizing the translation of essential genes. To test this, we examined the variance in CDS TE between biological replicates in our dataset. Consistent with our hypothesis, genes with active uORFs exhibited significantly lower variance in TE compared to genes lacking active uORFs (Fig. 6A), suggesting that uORFs may buffer fluctuations in gene expression, promoting stable and consistent translation of essential genes. This may allow P. falciparum to dynamically control parasite-specific genes while maintaining stable translation of essential genes needed across developmental stages. Importantly, under i14G1-12 treatment, lower uORF translation causes some genes that previously translated uORFs to lose this regulation, reducing the difference in TE variance between genes with and without translated uORFs (Fig. 6A). This increased variability in gene expression may disrupt essential cellular processes, potentially contributing to the inhibitory effect of i14G1-12 on P. falciparum survival.

To further investigate the translational effects of i14G1-12, we conducted a differential expression analysis of CDS TE. This analysis identified 685 downregulated and 619 upregulated genes following treatment (Fig. 6C). GO analysis of the downregulated genes revealed that both parasite-specific and housekeeping genes were affected by i14G1-12 (Table 3), which may explain the compound’s potent inhibitory impact on P. falciparum translation, despite the short treatment duration. Metagene analysis of ribosome coverage across all mRNAs showed a particular reduction of CDS reads (Fig. 6B). A similar analysis of downregulated mRNAs revealed reduced ribosomal density starting at the CDS initiation site and extending throughout the CDS following treatment with i14G1-12 (Fig. 6C, left). This occurred despite comparable levels of ribosome occupancy within the 5′ leaders between the treatment groups, suggesting that while ribosomes effectively scanned through the 5′ leaders in the downregulated genes, fewer initiated successfully at the CDS—potentially due to the enhanced leaky scanning induced by i14G1-12. In contrast, metagene analysis of ribosome coverage across the mRNAs encoded by the upregulated genes revealed increased ribosome accumulation at the 5′UTR and around the CDS initiation site following treatment with i14G1-12 (Fig. 6C, right), which may explain their upregulated TE. However, ribosome footprints declined across the CDS body (Fig. 6C, right), suggesting that most of the genes defined as “upregulated” are, in fact, downregulated.

Discussion

The mRNAs of the deadly malaria parasite P. falciparum have exceptionally long 5′ leaders and unusual numbers of uORFs compared to most other eukaryotes. However, the broad implications of these uORFs on protein synthesis were unclear. Here, we used experimentally validated data to delineate the exact 5′ leader boundaries of mis-annotated genes and identified active initiation sites throughout P. falciparum mRNAs. For the first time, we generated a comprehensive mapping of actively translated uORFs across the parasite’s genome. Contrary to initial expectations, our data revealed that uORFs do not globally inhibit downstream translation but actually facilitate translation. In addition, we uncovered the unique organizational and sequence features allowing the concomitant translation of uORFs and CDSs by the parasite translation machinery (Fig. 6D). We further discovered the widespread presence of active alternative ORFs within CDS regions, highlighting another unique aspect of the P. falciparum translational machinery. Finally, our work demonstrates that the eIF1-eIF4G1 complex inhibitor, i14G1-12, represses translation in the parasite by disrupting these mechanisms (Fig. 6D).

Our data show that the vast majority (81%) of P. falciparum genes encode mRNAs containing actively translated uORFs, making it unique among eukaryotes, where <50% of genes contain potential uORFs. Data from the few previous translatome studies on the parasite were not suitable for systematic detection and analysis of the actively translated uORFs among the numerous potential candidates [16, 41]. While a positive correlation between 5′ leader and CDS TE was found in a Ribo-seq study of P. falciparum IDC [16], this relationship was not attributed to uORFs. Our findings demonstrate that this relationship was found only among mRNAs with the translationally active uORFs that constitute 22% of the potential ones, highlighting the importance of determining the uORF translational status.

The analysis of CDS initiation contexts in P. falciparum genes revealed that they are relatively weaker compared to those observed in high eukaryotes. In fact, robust initiation events at the CDS were detectable in only two-thirds of the genes in our dataset, largely due to particularly stronger initiation contexts. Consistent with that, we observed an exceptionally high presence of initiation sites downstream of the CDS, likely resulting from leaky scanning. These sites, constituting both in-frame alternative TISs and short, out-of-frame ORFs, significantly expand the array of peptides encoded by P. falciparum. Taken together, these observations suggest that the CDS initiation contexts have evolved not only to fine-tune the translation of genes’ main protein products but also to enable the parasite to maintain its relatively compact coding genome while still producing numerous alternative peptides that may be required for its complex life cycle.

Irrespective of CDS initiation contexts, we found that both genes with active uORFs and those lacking them but exhibiting short 5′ leaders displayed significantly higher CDS TE values compared to genes with long 5′ leaders, where no potential uORFs were translated. Long 5′ leaders can generally repress CDS translation by introducing inhibitory sequences, such as secondary structures, or by providing binding sites for trans-acting factors that directly limit translation. While our data do not exclude these mechanisms, it implies that prolonged scanning itself may interfere with translation independently of other features. We showed that active uORFs are arranged in a way that significantly reduces the distance scanning ribosomes must traverse to initiate translation (Figs 4F and 6D). This initial scanning distance is maintained across all genes encoding active uORFs, irrespective of the number of uORFs, and aligns with the range of typical eukaryotic 5′ leader sizes. Consequently, scanning beyond that range represses CDS translation, perhaps through reduced integrity of scanning 43S complexes.

The remarkable ability of P. falciparum to translate the CDS, despite the presence of uORFs, can be achieved through reinitiation or leaky scanning. In some cases, concomitant translation of uORFs and the CDS is seen in genes encoding over four uORFs. Given the prevalence of uORFs across the majority of mRNAs, only a fraction of ribosomes could have reached the CDS, through successive rounds of leaky scanning. This suggests that reinitiation, rather than leaky scanning, serves as the primary mechanism enabling both uORF and CDS translation. The efficiency of reinitiation depends on several features of the uORF, including its initiation context, the duration of its elongation, and the inter-cistronic distance between its stop codon and the start site of the downstream ORF [55]. Our findings show that in P. falciparum, all these features are configured to optimize reinitiation efficiency (Fig. 6D). First, in contrast to CDSs, active uORFs do not exhibit distinctive initiation contexts, allowing scanning ribosomes to translate them without stalling their progression to the CDS. This is consistent with findings from studies in mammalian cells, where uORFs have been suggested to evolve under selective pressure, favoring weak translation [58]. By distinguishing actively translated uORFs from the potential ones, we discovered that in P. falciparum, this mechanism is facilitated through an inhibitory context, which promotes the bypassing of the inactive uORFs by scanning ribosomes via leaky scanning. Additionally, active uORFs and out-of-frame dORFs within CDS regions are very short, increasing the likelihood that elongating ribosomes will retain all the initiation factors required for another round of scanning upon termination of their translation. Of most importance, active uORFs are well-spaced across themselves, and even further apart from the CDS start sites (Figs 4D–F and 6D). This optimal spacing likely provides sufficient time for post-terminating 40S ribosomal subunits to reinitiate at a downstream site.

The high levels of translation of certain uORFs raise the question of what purpose they might serve. Previous Ribo-seq studies have shown that translation of the CDS in mRNAs containing uORFs remained resistant to global translational repression induced under conditions of stress, suggesting that uORFs could act as buffers to stabilize CDS translation during changes in cell state [49, 59]. This is supported by our finding that the variability in translation among active uORFs containing mRNAs is significantly smaller than in those lacking active uORFs (Fig. 6A). This could be particularly relevant for P. falciparum, given the parasite’s dependency on rapid adaptation to extreme shifts in environmental settings, as it alternates between the mosquito and human hosts during its dynamic life cycle. Another possibility is that uORFs, as well as dORFs, encode functional short peptides. Emerging evidence indicates that short peptides play various roles in biological processes [60–65]. Particularly intriguing are recent findings showing that short peptides, presented as self-antigens by MHC I molecules, can derive from nonconventional sources such as UTR regions or alternative ORFs, and play crucial roles in shaping immune responses [60]. This raises the possibility that the short or alternative ORFs may contribute to immune evasion by misleading the host immune system, as part of P. falciparum’s broader evasion mechanisms.

Taken together, our data demonstrate that P. falciparum translational machinery critically depends on the integration of both leaky scanning and reinitiation to enable the translation of the myriad potential ORFs encoded by its genome while still maintaining efficient translation of main ORFs. These characteristics make the parasite highly vulnerable to disruptions in these mechanisms. Indeed, we found that i14G1-12, an eIF1–eIF4G1 inhibitor, causes broad enhancement of leaky scanning in P. falciparum and global inhibition of translation initiation. The translation of the parasite is repressed by i14G1-12 through several mechanisms. First, it directly inhibits CDS translation, with CDSs displaying weak initiation contexts being more susceptible. Consequently, CDS with strong contexts are preferentially selected. However, this seems to promote ribosome queuing at the CDS starts. This phenomenon may even be facilitated by enhanced loading of 43S complexes induced by i14G1-12 due to increased levels of eIF4G1–eIF4E, resulting from inhibition of the eIF4G1–eIF1 interaction [11]. This combined effect could lead to interference with translation initiation at the CDS. In addition, due to its important role in regulating the translation rates of downstream ORFs, disruption of CDS translation may be detrimental to the parasite. The facilitating effect of i14G1-12 on leaky scanning results in an even larger decrease in the translation of uORFs, given their relatively weaker initiation contexts. Besides directly inhibiting the synthesis of peptides that may be functionally important, this may also interfere with translation at a global scale, by disrupting the precise configuration of uORFs designated for translation across 5′ leaders. In particular, we have found that the compound led to a substantial increase in the distance ribosomes have to scan to reach the first initiation site, which could interfere with their progression. By disturbing the innate translation of uORFs, i14G1-12 modifies the spaces between them, thereby indirectly interrupting reinitiation as well (Figs 4 and 6). Finally, it is possible that i14G1-12 may also directly interfere with reinitiation as previously suggested [66–68].

In summary, with our detailed analysis of TISs and TE across the P. falciparum genes, we provide novel insights into the regulatory roles of the 5′ leader and the principles governing uORFs features and organization, which enable the expansion of the coding potential without hampering the translation of main ORFs. These unique features highlight the value of utilizing the i14G1s as research tools in the investigation of translation initiation mechanisms and their potential as novel therapeutic approaches against the deadly parasite.
