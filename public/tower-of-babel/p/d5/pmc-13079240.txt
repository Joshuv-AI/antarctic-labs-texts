Development and validation of a nomogram for predicting progression from multidrug-resistant bacterial colonization to infection in lymphoma patients
Authors: Xin Ran, Tingting Liu, Jing Lv, Huijun Duan, Xia Wang, Yuan Zhang, Han Yan, Yuhong Deng, Wenling Xiong, Xingsu Jing, Jing Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo investigate the risk factors for progression from multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacterial colonization to infection in hospitalized lymphoma patients and to develop a Nomogram predictive model.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on clinical data from 70 MDR-positive lymphoma patients admitted to a tertiary hospital between March 2017 and March 2025. Patients were divided into MDR infection and MDR colonization groups based on the occurrence of MDR infection. Demographic characteristics, lymphoma subtypes, lesion sites, laboratory parameters, and treatment-related factors were collected. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify independent risk factors, and a Nomogram predictive model was constructed.ResultsAmong 70 MDR-positive patients, 48 (68.6%) developed MDR infection and 22 (31.4%) remained as colonization. Multivariate analysis revealed that T/NK-cell lymphoma (OR = 3.82, 95% CI 1.46–9.98, P = 0.006), nasopharyngeal/nasal cavity involvement (OR = 2.94, 95% CI 1.23–7.04, P = 0.015), recent antibiotic use (OR = 2.67, 95% CI 1.15–6.21, P = 0.022), and albumin < 35 g/L (OR = 2.31, 95% CI 1.08–4.93, P = 0.031) were independent risk factors. The Nomogram model based on these factors demonstrated good discrimination (C-index = 0.812) and calibration.ConclusionT/NK-cell origin, nasopharyngeal involvement, recent antibiotic exposure, and hypoalbuminemia are independent risk factors for progression from MDR colonization to infection in lymphoma patients. The Nomogram model can effectively identify high-risk patients and provide evidence for clinical prevention and control strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-04749-z.

Introduction

Multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacterial infection has become a major threat to global public health. According to the World Health Organization report, approximately 1.27 million people worldwide died directly from drug-resistant bacterial infections in 2019, with 4.95 million deaths related to drug resistance [1]. The incidence and mortality rates of MDR infection are significantly increased in immunocompromised populations [2]. Lymphoma, as one of the common malignant tumors in China, is expected to have 97,788 new cases of non-Hodgkin lymphoma and 57,929 deaths in 2022 [3]. Lymphoma patients are at high risk of MDR infection due to immune dysfunction caused by the disease itself and treatment-related factors [4]. Research has shown that the detection rate of MDR in patients with malignant hematological diseases can reach over 90%, of which about 60–70% will progress to clinical infections [5]. The progression of MDR from colonization to infection not only prolongs hospitalization time and increases medical costs, but more importantly, affects the implementation of chemotherapy regimens, leading to poor prognosis [6]. The pathological types of lymphoma are complex, and there are significant differences in the degree of immune dysfunction among lymphomas of different cell sources and anatomical sites [7]. The risk of infection in T/NK cell lymphoma patients is higher than that in B-cell lymphoma, especially in extranodal NK/T cell lymphoma patients who have a higher risk of progressing from colonization to infection due to changes in immune system function and EBV infection [8]. However, current research on the risk of progression of MDR infection in different subtypes of lymphoma is still limited. Existing research has mostly focused on the epidemiological characteristics and resistance mechanisms of MDR, while there are relatively few systematic studies on the progression of MDR from colonization to infection risk factors in lymphoma patients, especially the lack of large-scale studies based on the Chinese population. Establishing effective risk prediction models and early identification of high-risk patients is of great clinical significance for developing personalized prevention and control strategies. The aim of this study is to construct a Nomogram prediction model for the progression of MDR infection in lymphoma patients, and based on this model, to analyze the relative importance and clinical significance of various risk factors in depth, providing scientific basis for precise clinical prevention and control.

Data and methods

Patient characteristics

This study is a single-center retrospective cohort study that included consecutive lymphoma patients admitted to the Hematology Department of a tertiary comprehensive hospital from March 2017 to March 2025. In this retrospective design, no a priori sample-size calculation was performed; the sample size was determined by the total number of eligible MDR-positive lymphoma patients during the study period.

Inclusion criteria: (1) Age ≥ 13 years; (2) Pathologically confirmed lymphoma (tissue biopsy and immunohistochemistry); (3) Hospital stay ≥ 48 h; (4) Detection of MDR bacterial colonization or infection; (5) Complete clinical data (demographics, laboratory tests, microbiological culture results, etc.).

Exclusion criteria: (1) Concurrent other malignancies; (2) Missing key data > 20%.

After screening, 70 MDR-positive patients were included for analysis and classified as MDR infection (n = 48) or MDR colonization (n = 22) based on whether an MDR infection occurred during hospitalization. This study was approved by the hospital ethics committee, complied with the Declaration of Helsinki, and informed consent was waived. All data were extracted from the hospital information system and anonymized for privacy protection.

Method

Data collection and quality control

Retrospective patient data were collected from the electronic medical record system using standardized case-report forms, including: (1) Basic information: age, sex, body mass index (BMI), admission and discharge dates; (2) Disease-related variables: lymphoma pathological subtype (WHO 2022 criteria), lesion location, clinical stage (Ann Arbor); (3) Laboratory variables: white blood cell count, absolute neutrophil count, absolute lymphocyte count (when available), hemoglobin, and albumin within 24 h of admission; additionally, neutropenia grade and the duration of neutropenia during hospitalization were abstracted from serial blood counts when available; (4) Treatment-related variables: chemotherapy regimen (CHOP, R-CHOP, PEGEMOX, etc.), chemotherapy course number, recent (within 90 days) antibiotic exposure, and central venous catheter status; (5) Comorbidities/viral status: diabetes, HIV infection, hepatitis B, and Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) infection status; (6) Prophylaxis during chemotherapy: primary prophylaxis with granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF; long-acting or short-acting) and antimicrobial prophylaxis (e.g., trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole [TMP-SMX] and acyclovir, when applicable); (7) Microbiological data: MDR organism type, specimen source, colonization vs. infection classification, and antimicrobial susceptibility results.

The data collection was independently completed by two trained researchers and cross checked, with a consistency of over 95%. For controversial data, resolve it by consulting the original medical records or clinical physicians. Adopting a dual input method to ensure data accuracy, logical checks and range checks are performed after input, and outliers are verified through original medical records.

Definition of key variables

Terminal event

The primary endpoint was the progression from MDR colonization to infection during hospitalization. MDR status was defined according to international expert consensus [9] as bacterial resistance, whether inherent or acquired, to three or more classes of antimicrobial agents. The targeted MDR pathogens primarily included methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE), vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus (VRE), carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB), and carbapenem-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa (CRPA).

Definition of colonization and infection

MDR colonization was defined as the isolation of MRSA from nasal swab cultures or of other MDR bacteria from anal swab cultures, in the absence of corresponding clinical symptoms or signs of infection. The diagnosis of an MDR infection required the concurrent fulfillment of the following criteria: (1) clinical symptoms and signs of infection (e.g., fever, elevated white blood cell count, localized infection manifestations); (2) positive culture for an MDR pathogen from a corresponding clinical specimen; and (3) exclusion of colonization or contamination. All infection diagnoses were made in accordance with the updated 2024 criteria from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) [10].

Diagnostic work-up for suspected infection

For patients with suspected infection (e.g., new-onset fever or focal symptoms), a standardized diagnostic work-up was initiated. This included: (1) clinical examination and assessment of vital signs; (2) complete blood count and routine biochemistry; (3) measurement of inflammatory biomarkers (C-reactive protein and/or procalcitonin, when available); (4) collection of microbiological samples prior to antibiotic administration whenever feasible—specimens comprised at least two sets of blood cultures, as well as site-directed cultures (e.g., sputum/bronchoalveolar lavage for respiratory symptoms, urine culture for urinary symptoms, wound/skin swabs for soft-tissue lesions, and catheter-tip cultures if catheter-related infection was suspected); (5) performance of imaging studies as clinically indicated (e.g., chest radiography or CT for respiratory symptoms; ultrasound or CT for abdominal symptoms). Final diagnosis of infection and site classification were adjudicated independently by two senior physicians using CDC/National Healthcare Safety Network (NHSN) criteria.

Predictive factors

The following predictive factors were analyzed: (1) Lymphoma type: Categorized according to WHO classification and immunohistochemical results into B-cell derived (including diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, follicular lymphoma, mantle cell lymphoma, etc.) and T/NK cell-derived (including peripheral T-cell lymphoma, NK/T-cell lymphoma, angioimmunoblastic T-cell lymphoma, etc.). (2) Disease site: Classified based on the primary anatomical site of involvement into four categories: nasopharynx/nasal cavity, abdominal cavity/gastrointestinal tract, lymph nodes, and other sites (including bone marrow, central nervous system, skin, etc.). (3) Other covariates: Included age (continuous variable), sex, body mass index (BMI; categorized by WHO standards), comorbidities (diabetes mellitus, HIV infection, hepatitis B, EBV infection), recent antibiotic exposure (within 90 days, detailing antibiotic class and duration of use), chemotherapy status, indwelling catheter presence (including PICC, CVC, etc.), and baseline laboratory parameters (white blood cell count, neutrophil count, hemoglobin, albumin, and absolute lymphocyte count when available; using the first value obtained upon admission). Where documented, the grade and duration of neutropenia during hospitalization were also recorded.

Outcome measurement and quality control

Microbial identification was performed using the VITEK 2 fully automated microbial identification system (bioMérieux, France). Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was conducted in accordance with the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) 2024 guidelines. Quality control strains, such as Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 29,213 and Escherichia coli ATCC 25,922, were included in all testing runs. All microbiological results were reviewed and confirmed by two senior laboratory physicians. For complex or ambiguous cases, internal consultations and multidisciplinary discussions were held to reach a consensus.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

A total of 70 MDR-positive lymphoma patients were included, aged 13–90 years, with a mean age of 54.73 ± 16.21 years and a median age of 55 (interquartile range: 42.25–68.50) years. There were 16 males (22.9%) and 54 females (77.1%). Regarding lymphoma subtype, 45 cases (64.3%) were of B-cell origin and 25 cases (35.7%) were of T/NK-cell origin. Lesion locations included lymph nodes (24 cases, 34.3%), nasopharynx/nasal cavity involvement (18 cases, 25.7%), abdominal cavity/digestive tract (15 cases, 21.4%), and other areas (13 cases, 18.6%). Among the 18 patients with nasopharynx/nasal cavity involvement, 11 cases were pathologically confirmed as nasal-type extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma, while the remaining 7 cases lacked definitive histologic classification but may still clinically overlap with the nasal-type entity. Comorbidities/complications included Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) infection in 13 cases (18.6%), diabetes in 9 (12.9%), hepatitis B in 7 (10.0%), and HIV infection in 3 (4.3%).

With respect to treatment status, 53 patients (75.7%) had recently used antibiotics, 44 (62.9%) had indwelling catheters, and 35 (50.0%) had received at least three courses of chemotherapy, including 27 in the MDR infection group and 8 in the colonized group. Chemotherapy regimens were heterogeneous (e.g., CHOP/R-CHOP and other multi-agent regimens), which may influence the severity of myelosuppression and prophylaxis practices. Among the 70 patients, 38 (54.3%) received anthracycline-based regimens (including CHOP or R-CHOP), 17 (24.3%) received PEG-asparaginase-containing regimens (e.g., SMILE or PEG-EMOX), and 15 (21.4%) received other regimens. Patients treated with anthracycline-based regimens had a higher rate of primary prophylaxis with long-acting G-CSF (42.1%) compared to non-anthracycline regimens (15.6%), supporting risk-adapted prophylactic strategies based on chemotherapy intensity. Laboratory test results showed a white blood cell count of 6.38 ± 4.27 × 10⁹/L, neutrophil count of 4.44 ± 3.69 × 10⁹/L, hemoglobin of 94.01 ± 26.63 g/L, and albumin level of 37.53 ± 14.35 g/L.

Primary prophylaxis during chemotherapy was documented as follows: G-CSF prophylaxis was administered in 36/70 (51.4%) patients, including long-acting G-CSF in 16/70 (22.9%) and short-acting G-CSF in 20/70 (28.6%). Antimicrobial prophylaxis included TMP-SMX in 12/70 (17.1%) patients and acyclovir in 22/70 (31.4%) patients. Antibacterial prophylaxis (agent unspecified) was used in 10/70 (14.3%) patients.

Incidence of MDR infection

Among the 70 MDR-positive patients, 48 (68.6%) developed MDR infection and 22 (31.4%) remained colonized without signs of active infection. MRSA was the most frequently detected MDR organism (54/70, 77.1%), followed by CRE (8/70, 11.4%), CRPA (3/70, 4.3%), VRE (2/70, 2.9%), CRAB (1/70, 1.4%), and mixed MDR organisms (2/70, 2.9%). Specimens were obtained from both colonization screening and clinically suspected infection sites, including sputum/respiratory tract samples (n = 22, 31.4%), nasal swabs (n = 13, 18.6%), wound/skin secretions (n = 10, 14.3%), blood cultures (n = 7, 10.0%), anal swabs (n = 6, 8.6%), and other samples (n = 12, 17.1%). Detailed distributions of MDR pathogens by specimen type and antimicrobial susceptibility profiles are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Among the 48 infected patients, infection sites were categorized as respiratory tract infection/pneumonia (22/48, 45.8%), skin and soft tissue infection (10/48, 20.8%), bloodstream infection (7/48, 14.6%), and other sites (including gastrointestinal tract, catheter-related, and unspecified infections; 9/48, 18.8%). The MDR infection rate was significantly higher in T/NK cell-derived lymphoma patients (84.0%) than in B-cell-derived lymphoma patients (60.0%). In addition, patients with nasopharyngeal/nasal lesions had a higher infection rate (83.3%) than those with lesions in other locations (Table 1).

Univariate Analysis

The comparison between the MDR infection group and the MDR colonization group showed that there were statistically significant or nearly statistically significant differences in lymphoma type (P = 0.005), lesion site (P = 0.030), recent antibiotic use (P = 0.008), albumin level (P = 0.012), neutrophil count (P = 0.045), chemotherapy duration (P = 0.067), and catheter placement (P = 0.089) between the two groups (Table 2).

Multivariate logistic regression analysis

Variables with P < 0.1 in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariate model. The results demonstrated that T/NK cell-derived lymphoma (OR = 3.82, 95% CI 1.46–9.98, P = 0.006), nasopharyngeal/nasal lesion site (OR = 2.94, 95% CI 1.23–7.04, P = 0.015), recent antibiotic use (OR = 2.67, 95% CI 1.15–6.21, P = 0.022), and serum albumin < 35 g/L (OR = 2.31, 95% CI 1.08–4.93, P = 0.031) were independent risk factors for MDR infection (Table 3).

Nomogram construction and validation

Based on the results of the multivariate analysis, a nomogram prediction model was constructed (Fig. 1). The model demonstrated good discriminative ability, with a C-index of 0.812 (95% CI 0.738–0.886). The calibration curve indicated good agreement between predicted probabilities and actual observed probabilities. Internal validation using the bootstrap method yielded a C-index of 0.804. Decision curve analysis showed that the model provided positive net benefit within a threshold probability range of 10% to 70% (Fig. 2).

Discussion

This study found that among MDR-positive lymphoma patients, 68.6% progressed to clinical MDR infection, whereas 31.4% remained colonized without signs of active infection. It is worth noting that the MDR infection rate in T/NK cell-derived lymphoma patients (84.0%) is significantly higher than that in B-cell derived lymphoma patients (60.0%), indicating significant differences in the risk of infection progression among different subtypes of lymphoma.

The mechanism of increased risk of MDR infection in T/NK cell lymphoma patients may involve multiple aspects. Firstly, T/NK cell lymphoma is often accompanied by more severe cellular immune dysfunction [11]. T cells play a central role in antibacterial immunity, and their functional abnormalities directly affect the body’s ability to clear pathogens [12]. Secondly, most NK/T-cell lymphomas are associated with EBV infection, which can further impair immune function and increase the risk of secondary infection [13]. Finally, the treatment regimen for T/NK cell lymphoma often includes high-dose chemotherapy and immunosuppressants, exacerbating the degree of immunosuppression [14].

This study identified the nasopharyngeal/nasal lesion site as an independent risk factor, which may be related to its anatomical characteristics and local microenvironment. As the gateway to the respiratory tract, the nasopharynx is directly exposed to the external environment and is therefore susceptible to pathogen invasion [15]. Extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma occurs more frequently in the nasal cavity and is often accompanied by local tissue destruction and necrosis, leading to impairment of mucosal barrier function [16]. Additionally, dysbiosis of the normal nasopharyngeal flora may facilitate the progression of drug-resistant bacteria from colonization to infection [17].

Recent antibiotic use emerged as a significant risk factor for MDR infection, consistent with previous studies [18]. Antibiotic selection pressure serves as the primary driver for the emergence and spread of drug-resistant bacteria. In this study, 75.7% of patients had received antibiotics within 90 days prior to admission, with penicillin being the most commonly used agent. Long-term or repeated use of broad-spectrum antibiotics not only selects for resistant strains, but also disrupts the colonization resistance of the normal flora, thereby creating conditions that favor the progression from colonization to infection by resistant bacteria [19].

Hypoalbuminemia reflects both malnutrition and disease severity and was identified as an independent risk factor for infection [20]. Albumin not only serves as an indicator of nutritional status, but also performs multiple biological functions, including maintaining plasma colloid osmotic pressure, facilitating transport, and exerting antioxidant activity. Patients with hypoalbuminemia exhibit impaired immune function, delayed wound healing, and an increased risk of infection [21].

The Nomogram model constructed in this study integrates four independent risk factors, with a C-index of 0.812, demonstrating good predictive performance. Compared with previous MDR infection prediction models based on the general tumor population [22], this model improves prediction accuracy by targeting specific factors of lymphoma patients, such as cell origin and lesion location. The model can help clinicians identify high-risk patients early and develop personalized prevention and control strategies.

Because most patients in this cohort received intensive multi-cycle chemotherapy, preventive strategies should be emphasized; importantly, different chemotherapy regimens and intensities may lead to varying degrees and durations of neutropenia, supporting risk-adapted prophylaxis (e.g., long-acting G-CSF and indicated TMP-SMX/acyclovir prophylaxis) in high-risk patients. In lymphoma patients receiving anthracycline-based regimens, vigorous primary prophylaxis against infectious complications (including long-acting G-CSF and appropriate antimicrobial prophylaxis such as TMP-SMX and acyclovir when indicated) has been advocated to reduce febrile neutropenia and infection-related morbidity [23].

In febrile neutropenic patients with documented MDR colonization, empirical antibiotic therapy should be promptly initiated and may be tailored according to prior colonization status and local epidemiology, while maintaining guideline-based broad-spectrum coverage [24].

This approach is strongly supported by studies identifying MDR organisms, such as third-generation cephalosporin-resistant Enterobacteriaceae and other gram-negative bacteria, as leading pathogens in bacteremic episodes among febrile neutropenic cancer patients [25].

For high-risk patients (e.g., T/NK-cell lymphoma, nasopharyngeal/nasal involvement, recent antibiotic exposure, and/or hypoalbuminemia), we recommend: (1) enhanced MDR surveillance with repeated site cultures as appropriate; (2) strict contact precautions and hand hygiene; (3) antimicrobial stewardship to avoid unnecessary broad-spectrum exposure; (4) nutritional assessment and timely correction of hypoalbuminemia; (5) consideration of standardized primary prophylaxis during anthracycline-based chemotherapy, including long-acting G-CSF and indicated TMP-SMX/acyclovir prophylaxis; and (6) for febrile neutropenia, early empirical therapy with attention to prior MDR colonization and local resistance patterns.

This study has several limitations. First, the single-center retrospective design limits external generalizability. Second, the sample size was modest, which may reduce statistical power for subgroup analyses. Third, there is a lack of external validation cohorts, and the model’s generalizability requires confirmation. Fourth, the enrolled population had a wide age range (13–90 years); adolescents may differ from adults in baseline immune status, chemotherapy intensity, and prophylaxis strategies, which may introduce clinical heterogeneity. Fifth, key host/immunologic variables (e.g., lymphocyte count, neutropenia grade, and duration of neutropenia) and detailed chemotherapy regimen intensity could not be uniformly captured for all patients and were therefore not incorporated into the final multivariable model. Future multicenter prospective studies with standardized collection of these variables are warranted. Future prospective studies with multiple centers and large samples are needed to validate and improve predictive models.

Conclusion

The source of T/NK cells, nasopharyngeal/nasal involvement, recent antibiotic use, and hypoalbuminemia were independent risk factors for progression from MDR colonization to infection in hospitalized lymphoma patients. These findings should be interpreted in the context of the wide age range and chemotherapy heterogeneity, and future studies incorporating lymphocyte count and neutropenia severity/duration with external validation are warranted.
