InstaNovo-P: a de novo peptide sequencing model for phosphoproteomics
Authors: Jesper Lauridsen, Vahap Canbay, Rachel Catzel, Pathmanaban Ramasamy, Amandla Mabona, Kevin Eloff, Paul Fullwood, Jennifer Ferguson, Annekatrine Kirketerp-Møller, Ida Sofie Goldschmidt, Tine Claeys, Sam van Puyenbroeck, Nicolas Lopez Carranza, Erwin M Schoof, Lennart Martens, Jeroen Van Goey, Chiara Francavilla, Timothy Patrick Jenkins, Konstantinos Kalogeropoulos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhosphorylation, a crucial post-translational modification (PTM), plays a central role in cellular signaling and disease mechanisms. Mass spectrometry-based phosphoproteomics is widely used for system-wide characterization of phosphorylation events. However, traditional methods struggle with accurate phosphorylated site localization, complex search spaces, and detecting sequences outside the reference database. Advances in de novo peptide sequencing offer opportunities to address these limitations, but have yet to become integrated and adapted for phosphoproteomics datasets. Here, we present InstaNovo-P, a phosphorylation specific version of our transformer-based InstaNovo model, fine-tuned on extensive phosphoproteomics datasets. InstaNovo-P surpasses existing methods in phosphorylated peptide detection and phosphorylated site localization accuracy across multiple datasets, including complex experimental scenarios. Our model robustly identifies peptides with single and multiple phosphorylated sites, effectively localizing phosphorylation events on serine, threonine, and tyrosine residues. We experimentally validate our model predictions by studying FGFR2 signaling, further demonstrating that InstaNovo-P uncovers phosphorylated sites previously missed by traditional database searches. These predictions align with critical biological processes, confirming the model’s capacity to yield valuable biological insights. InstaNovo-P adds value to phosphoproteomics experiments by effectively identifying biologically relevant phosphorylation events without prior information, providing a powerful analytical tool for the dissection of signaling pathways.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Proteomics, Cell growth

Introduction

Phosphorylation is a widespread protein post-translational modification (PTM) and the most abundant PTM in eukaryotes1–3. It plays crucial roles in enzyme activation, signal transduction, and transcription regulation, with aberrant activity linked to various pathological states, including cancer4–6. Consequently, detection of phosphorylation events is imperative for dissecting cellular pathways and describing disease states. Kinases, the enzymes responsible for phosphorylation, are important drug targets and have found considerable success in therapeutic intervention7,8.

Mass spectrometry has become the de facto approach for studying protein phosphorylation across different cell types and tissues under various conditions9–13. Phosphoproteomics, the field dedicated to studying phosphorylated proteins, has seen major advancements in sample preparation, instrumentation, and data analysis for phosphorylated peptide detection over the past decades14–20. The traditional approach for identifying phosphorylated peptides involves database searches based on the mass shift induced by phosphorylation in the sequence search space. However, this method has inherent limitations in modification localization ambiguity, and substantially expands the search space, search time and costs21–23. Additionally, target-decoy methods cannot detect non-canonical protein sequences or protein isoforms that are not included in the database search, restricting analysis to only known reference proteomes.

Recent advances in de novo peptide sequencing using machine learning approaches have shown impressive performance in peptide detection, including peptides with modifications such as N-terminal acetylation and methionine oxidation24–26. These methods have the potential to address the shortcomings of database searches, provide complementary information, and be integrated into hybrid approaches to maximize insights into phosphorylation in investigated proteomes. Despite the abundance of publicly available PTM proteomics datasets, de novo peptide sequencing approaches have not been extensively adapted for PTM identification.

Previous transformer-based methods for de novo peptide sequencing, including InstaNovo24, predominantly utilize auto-regressive architectures, predicting amino acids sequentially. Recently, Xia et al. developed AdaNovo27, incorporating conditional mutual information between spectra and peptides, successfully identifying amino acids carrying PTMs on benchmark datasets, however without including phosphorylation. PrimeNovo28, a model using non-autoregressive prediction, is currently the best model that supports detection of phosphorylated peptides.

Here, we introduce the first application of a de novo peptide sequencing model that is trained on an extensive number of phosphoproteomic datasets and is exclusively adapted for phosphorylated peptide detection. We utilize numerous reprocessed phosphoproteomics experiments available in the PRIDE database29 to make a fine-tuned, phosphorylation-specific version of InstaNovo, achieving substantial improvements in phosphorylated peptide identification accuracy. We validate the performance of our fine-tuned model across multiple datasets, and confirm its predictions with targeted proteomics and biochemical assays. Lastly, we provide first time experimental validation of phoshorylated peptide predictions that went undetected by conventional database searches.

Results

InstaNovo-P is a phosphorylation-specific de novo sequencing model

We utilized our previously developed de novo sequencing model, InstaNovo24, and adapted it to the task of phosphorylated peptide detection. InstaNovo is an autoregressive transformer model with an encoder-decoder architecture, trained on the ProteomeTools dataset30. During inference, InstaNovo interprets an MS/MS spectrum by translating it to the peptide sequence that produced the spectrum. To fine-tune InstaNovo for phosphorylated peptide prediction (i.e., to further train and adapt our model for the specific task of phosphorylated peptide sequencing), we curated a set of 29 IonBot31 reprocessed phosphoproteomics projects from Scop3P32 (see Supplementary Table 1 for complete list of projects). After filtering for high phosphosite localization probability (>0.80), our training dataset consisted of 2,760,939 PSMs mapping to 74,686 unique peptides. Three projects made up more than half of the PSMs with PXD000612 alone containing 837,540 or 31% of the PSMs (Fig. 1A). When accounting for unique peptides per project, the analyzed dataset was also dominated by a few large projects, although the distribution is slightly more even (Figure 1B). The distribution of phosphorylated amino acids is dominated by serine (S), making up 84.5%, while threonine (T) and tyrosine (Y) make up 12.0% and 3.4%, respectively (Figure 1C). To minimize data leakage, the dataset was split using GraphPart33 into a train-valid-test split of 70–10–20%.

We used the InstaNovo model architecture with two modifications. The sequence embedding layer and the linear head of the model had to be extended to accommodate three extra tokens for the possible phosphorylated residues of serine, threonine, and tyrosine (Fig. 1D). To fine-tune, we utilized gradual unfreezing34 of the model layers starting with the head and sequence embedding for 1 epoch. Then, instead of unfreezing the decoder, we gradually unfroze the encoder layers. Finally, the decoder layers were also gradually unfrozen, reducing the learning rate with increasing number of training steps (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Interestingly, the model reached good validation performance after only fine-tuning the head, sequence embedding and encoder, but the decoder is also fine-tuned for the last performance gain (Fig. 1E). This fine-tuning strategy was shown to perform best among the alternatives tested (see Supplementary Table 2).

To assess the model performance on phosphorylated peptides, we used 3 evaluation datasets: the test set made from the split of the curated projects, here referred to as “Test”, a subset of project PXD00944935, here referred to as “21PTM”, and a dataset from an in-house experiment using T47D breast cancer cell line expressing or not Fibroblast Growth Factor Receptor 2 (FGFR2) upon growth factor treatment, here referred to as “FGFR2”. The three evaluation datasets contained different distributions of non-phosphorylated and phosphorylated peptides as well as different residue types (Supplementary Fig. 1B, C). In addition to the evaluation on phosphorylated datasets, we also evaluate the model on 2 datasets of unmodified peptides: The original test set used by the base model, here referred to as “AC-PT”, and the unmodified version of 21PTM, here referred to as “21PTM-unmod”. We use the term unmodified to refer to the portion of peptides that are not phosphorylated in a given dataset.

In summary, we extended the InstaNovo transformer by adding three phosphorylation-specific tokens and fine-tuned on a 2.76 million PSM phosphoproteomics corpus, achieving strong validation performance using an encoder-first gradual unfreezing schedule.

InstaNovo-P assigns phosphorylation events with high accuracy and precision

We compared the performance of our model with the base InstaNovo model (version 0.1.4) on unmodified peptides from the AC-PT dataset (ProteomeTools Parts I-III)24 and the 21PTM dataset35. InstaNovo-P increases peptide recall from 64.0 to 68.1% and from 70.0 to 71.4% for the AC-PT test set and 21PTM dataset (taking into account only unmodified peptides), respectively, compared to the base InstaNovo model (Fig. 2A). This surprising observation indicates that the fine-tuning effort did not constrain the model to the detection of phosphorylated peptides only, but also improved the general performance. In other words, the model did not experience catastrophic forgetting even without mixing training data from target and source domain (i.e., unmodified vs modified peptides).

We then evaluated the performance on phosphorylated peptides of the 21PTM dataset. InstaNovo-P achieved a peptide recall of 81.4% (Supplementary Table 3). In comparison, PrimeNovo28 reported a peptide recall of 68% (Fig. 2B). This increase of 13.4% compared to PrimeNovo indicates an excellent generalization to unseen phosphorylated peptides. Moreover, PrimeNovo was fine-tuned specifically on 21PTM, whereas InstaNovo-P evaluated 21PTM entirely as a holdout dataset.

Next, we evaluated the performance of our model in relation to the number of phosphorylated sites present on phosphorylated peptides. Our evaluation datasets contain unmodified peptides, as well as peptides with 1, 2, or more phosphorylation sites (Supplementary Fig. 2A). As expected, we see decreasing peptide recall with the increased number of phosphorylated sites in each peptide (Fig. 2C). Unsurprisingly, we also observed a correlation of peptide recall with the composition of our training dataset. The highest recall was seen in serine phosphorylation events (Supplementary Table 4), which were also the most abundant events in our training dataset (Fig. 2D). The highest recall was seen for the 21PTM dataset, which contained only phosphorylated tyrosines. We speculate that the reason is due to the high-intensity signal, the nature of the synthetic peptides, and lower sample complexity.

Limiting and estimating the number of false positives in proteomics searches is imperative for utility in biological studies and downstream validations. We therefore evaluated the model confidence threshold needed for different values of false discovery rate (FDR, Supplementary Fig. 2B, see “Methods” for further details on threshold calculation). The confidence range distribution of predictions for all evaluation datasets was similar to the base model, and indicates that the model can inherently distinguish between correct and incorrect assignments (Supplementary Fig. 3). We observed highly diminishing peptide recall at 1% FDR (Fig. 2E). Thus the best threshold that balanced recall and by extension identification performance with a satisfactory FDR was 5%. We also observed the same trend based on residue phosphorylation type. Peptide recall on the FGFR2 dataset by type of phosphorylated residue at 5% FDR was 44.4%, 30.2% and 22.6% for S, T and Y, respectively (Fig. 2F).

To directly compare model performance on an unseen dataset, and to evaluate recall using a state-of-the-art database search engine as a reference, we reprocessed a subset of our FGFR2 dataset (phosphopeptide-enriched samples treated with FGF7 and untreated samples at 40 min) using MSFragger within the FragPipe proteomics pipeline36 (see “Methods” and Supplementary Table 5). This dataset is hereafter referred to as FGFR2 FragPipe. On all peptides in this dataset, InstaNovo-P achieved a recall of 44.1%, compared to 30.7% for PrimeNovo-PTM (Supplementary Fig. 4A). When restricting the analysis to phosphorylated peptides (which constitute the majority of PSMs), InstaNovo-P outperformed PrimeNovo-PTM by 13.5% (Supplementary Fig. 4B). For non-phosphorylated PSMs, InstaNovo-P trailed PrimeNovo by 5% recall, but still improved recall by 10.9% and 13.2% relative to the base InstaNovo and PrimeNovo-PTM models, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 4C). We also evaluated the performance by number of phosphorylation sites. On peptides with a single phosphorylation site, InstaNovo-P achieved a recall of 43.9%, compared to 31.1% for PrimeNovo-PTM. For peptides with two phosphorylation sites, InstaNovo-P reached 33.2%, surpassing PrimeNovo-PTM by 12.7%, and for peptides with three phosphorylation sites, InstaNovo-P recall was 19.9% compared to 9.9% for PrimeNovo-PTM (Supplementary Table 6).

To provide an independent estimate of FDR, we applied our recently developed Winnow framework to our FGFR2 FragPipe dataset37. A calibration model was trained on the labeled portion of the dataset and applied to InstaNovo-P predictions on the unlabeled portion to obtain calibrated confidence scores. The resulting sets of predictions passing 5% and 10% FDR thresholds were compared against the database-grounded predictions at the same thresholds. At 5% FDR, the two methods show partial agreement, with 555 sequences identified by both approaches, 287 exclusively by Winnow, and 1450 exclusively by database grounding (Supplementary Fig. 5A). Agreement substantially increases at 10% FDR (3668 in common) (Supplementary Fig. 5B). The convergence of these two approaches provides meaningful support for the reliability of our predictions. We note that Winnow operates conservatively at this stage, as the calibration model is trained on a limited labeled set. We anticipate that agreement will improve as the calibration model is exposed to larger and more diverse training corpora spanning a broader range of peptide sequences, instrument platforms, and acquisition methods, and as additional spectrum features are incorporated into the framework.

We next assessed the ability of InstaNovo-P to generalize to unseen detector types. To this end, we reprocessed an independent dataset acquired on an Orbitrap Astral instrument using the Astral detector with the same database search workflow. On all peptides, InstaNovo-P achieved a recall of 26.8%, compared to 2.8% for PrimeNovo-PTM, corresponding to a 24.0% absolute increase (Supplementary Fig. 6A). However, only 9.4% of phosphorylated peptides were retrieved by InstaNovo-P in this dataset (Supplementary Fig. 6B). Despite this, InstaNovo-P remained competitive across model variants, trailing the base PrimeNovo model by 5.7% on non-phosphorylated peptides while substantially outperforming both base InstaNovo and PrimeNovo overall (Supplementary Fig. 6C).

Altogether, these data indicate that InstaNovo-P generalizes well to phosphorylated peptides without catastrophic forgetting, while the predicted confidence scores effectively discriminate correct assignments, with a 5% FDR threshold offering the optimal trade-off between recall and reliability.

InstaNovo-P performance is dependent on peptide properties

We next evaluated the recall of peptide predictions on the three different test datasets. As previously observed for our base InstaNovo model, we saw highly variable performance depending on the dataset evaluated (Fig. 3A, B). InstaNovo-P achieved the best performance on 21PTM, which was composed of synthetic peptides. We recorded lower recall in our test and FGFR2 dataset, which originated from other projects with endogenous peptides. As seen in prior works28, we noted that performance was dependent on to peptide length (Fig. 3C, D). InstaNovo-P exhibited lower peptide recall with increasing peptide length, which affected the dataset total recall. Even though the overall recall for 21PTM was higher than that of the test set and FGFR2 dataset, we found that the recall for shorter peptides (under 15 amino acids) was almost identical across datasets (Fig. 3D).

The FGFR2 dataset included both a phospho-enriched and an unmodified version of several peptides. In addition to those two, we also took the phospho-enriched FGFR2 and “de-modified” it (changing the phosphorylated sites to their non-modified version). A prediction would usually contain a phosphorylated site, but by de-modifying it, we could infer the added difficulty of phosphorylation localization alone. We found that InstaNovo-P performed well across all three types, however phosphorylated peptide identification was more difficult than for unmodified peptides (Fig. 3E and Supplementary Table 7). Out of all errors on the phospho-enriched FGFR2 dataset at 5% FDR, 61.57% were due to wrong localization of phospho sites (see Supplementary Table 8). For longer peptides, the recall performance was noticeably higher for the FGFR2 dataset when compared to the two other evaluation datasets, with recall also showing dependence on the number of phosphorylated sites for each peptide, with higher recall rates observed across peptide length for all datasets (Fig. 3F).

To further characterize this improvement, we compared InstaNovo-P and InstaNovo performance on unmodified peptides in the FGFR2 FragPipe dataset. Stratifying by model confidence (Supplementary Fig. 7A), InstaNovo-P has a higher weighted average recall. Within individual bins, InstaNovo achieves comparable or higher recall at lower confidence levels. However, this reflects a confidence distribution difference between the two models rather than superior performance of InstaNovo. InstaNovo assigns a substantially larger proportion of its predictions to the lowest confidence bin (Supplementary Fig. 7B), while InstaNovo-P concentrates more predictions in the high-confidence bins (80–100%), where recall is correspondingly higher. Stratifying by peptide length (Supplementary Fig. 7C, D), InstaNovo-P outperforms InstaNovo across all length bins, indicating that the improvement is not concentrated in shorter peptides. Consistent with these observations, the improvement holds across both FragPipe and MaxQuant search results and across multiple instrument types, suggesting it reflects genuine generalization of the model’s fragmentation pattern representations (Supplementary Figs. 4 and 6). We hypothesize that exposure to diverse phosphorylated peptide contexts during fine-tuning broadens the model’s representational capacity in a way that benefits unmodified peptide identification.

In sum, InstaNovo-P performance varied markedly by dataset and peptide characteristics, while recall also trended downward as the number of phosphorylation sites per peptide increased. Our results suggest that our recall differences between the three evaluated datasets were predominantly due to differences in peptide attributes.

InstaNovo-P is a robust tool for proteome-wide identification of phosphorylation events

We used our FGFR2 dataset to map our model’s predictions to the proteome, providing another validation for our predictions, given the very low likelihood of an incorrect prediction mapping to the proteome. We also compared our results to database search results using MaxQuant. We assessed our false discovery rate using the database search identifications in the labeled space (scans in common with database results) or the full search space (all scans in the dataset). We noticed that InstaNovo-P predicted substantially more phosphorylated peptides at 5% FDR when evaluated in the full search space rather than the labeled space alone, and that the gains are similar across different perturbations and time points in our experiments. Overall, InstaNovo-P detected 97.1% more peptides (Fig. 4A). On average, InstaNovo-P detected 3068 (±495) peptides, which was expanded to 5436 (±770) peptides when expanding our search to the full search space, i.e., also scans that the database did not identify (Fig. 4B). We mapped these peptides to 21.6% more proteins in the proteome compared to the database search in the same dataset (Fig. 4B). The identified peptides mapped to 1331 (±209) proteins on average in the labeled space, which increased to 1544 (±239) proteins in the full search space (Fig. 4D).

The localization accuracy of InstaNovo-P, as assessed by recall, indicated that the model is adept at recognizing and predicting phosphorylation events for peptides containing multiple potential phosphorylation sites. The recall observed, as well as the recall at an estimated 5% FDR, positively correlated with database localization certainty (Fig. 4E). However, peptides with low localization certainty included in the database search results might contain incorrect localization certainty that negatively affects peptide recall. This is substantiated by the trend of database localization probability as a function of correct and incorrect assignments by InstaNovo-P in the same dataset (Fig. 4F).

We then turned our attention to a number of specific phosphorylation sites in proteins that are critical components of the cell cycle. We first investigated phosphorylated peptide predictions for sites in the protein TP53, an important cell cycle checkpoint38. InstaNovo-P predicted 35 phosphorylated sites in total, of which 4 contain phosphorylation sites in TP53 that went undetected in the database search space (Fig. 4G). InstaNovo-P predicted 31 phosphorylation sites in common with the database search, while the search predicted 8 sites that InstaNovo-P did not detect.

We also evaluated predicted peptides and phosphorylation sites not detected by database search in lamin A/C (LMNA), a protein that is known to be phosphorylated during mitosis and is thought to be involved in chromatin stability and regulation39. In the full search space, InstaNovo-P predicted 6 more peptides containing 2 more unique phosphorylated sites that are not present in the labeled space (Supplementary Fig. 8).

Collectively, proteome-wide mapping in the FGFR2 dataset shows that InstaNovo-P not only nearly doubles identified phosphorylated peptides at 5% FDR by extending our search to the full search space, but also retains high localization accuracy that scales with database search localization certainty. Importantly, it uncovers undetected phosphorylation sites, demonstrating potential in revealing additional phosphorylation sites beyond database searches.

InstaNovo-P predicts phosphorylation sites that contribute meaningful biological insights

To evaluate insights provided by our model we used a subset of the FGFR2 dataset and compared T47D cells, either wild type (WT) or CRISPR/Cas9 knockout (KO) of FGFR2, upon treatment with the specific FGFR2 ligand FGF7 (F7)40 for different timepoints (40 min or 72 h). Each of these conditions contained only phospho-enriched samples, eluted in 2 separate fractions per column. We mapped InstaNovo-P predictions from these experimental files in the human proteome, and assessed their presence in the labeled space. InstaNovo-P identified 86.75% more peptides that contained phosphorylated sites compared to the labeled space (Fig. 5A). The distribution of these peptides matched well with the standard peptide distribution of human peptides in MS data24 and the database search space (Fig. 5B). We also explored InstaNovo-P predictions in the labeled and full search space in relation to the database search. At 5% FDR, we predicted more than double the peptides compared to the model predictions that overlap with the database search in the same threshold (Fig. 5C).

When comparing the predictions between F7-treated KO and WT cells at 40 min, InstaNovo-P identified 695 peptides that were unique for WT (Fig. 5D) at 5% FDR. These peptides may represent the core of F7-FGFR2 signaling in our experimental model as they were found in the WT but not in the KO cells. Out of these, 153 were not found in the database search space, with 138 of them found only in this condition and timepoint. Importantly, 10 out of 10 of these top predicted phosphorylated sites, when ranked by number of PSMs, represented known phosphorylated sites in PhosphoSitePlus10. As an example, we identified a phosphorylation site at S402, in the C-terminal peptide of SAC31, a protein predicted to be involved in centrosome duplication and mitotic progression (the most commonly phosphorylated residue in this protein according to experiments integrated in PhosphoSitePlus). This suggests that our model is able to identify known and validated phosphorylated peptides, providing complementary information and substantially improving phosphoproteomics searches.

The comparison between F7-treated vs untreated WT cells revealed 2,340 phosphorylated peptides unique for the treated samples, with 417 identified only by InstaNovo-P, and 368 of these identified only in that condition. Similarly to the WT versus KO comparison, all 10 top ranked, predicted peptides contained phosphorylated sites present in PhosphoSitePlus. Three of these 10 peptides mapped to nucleophosmin (NPM1), and contain the same phosphorylated site at position S125, possibly phosphorylated by the kinases Aurora A or B. This site is the most frequent phosphorylation event in NPM1, and mutations in this position show mitotic defects41. This phosphorylation event causes the protein to shift to a monomeric state instead of a pentameric42, and re-localize from the nucleolus to midbody41. However, as none of these peptides identified on NPM1 are tryptic, they would not have been identified with the standard database search settings. These peptides differed by one residue at the N-terminus (starting with GSGPVHISGQHIVAVEEDAEpSEDEEEEDVK), with several cleavage events downstream of this peptide, hinting at aminopeptidase degradation of the protein in a sequential way. Quantification of these peptides corroborated this observation, with the truncated peptidoforms found in increasing or diminishing abundance (Supplementary Fig. 9). Although caspases −3, −7 and −8 can cleave NPM1 during apoptosis43, no cleavage or aminopeptidase activity has been reported for that protein region. We also observed an unmodified, cleaved product in the acidic region of NPM1 (161–188) with cleavage at position 180. This suggests that InstaNovo-P can detect modified, non-tryptic peptides and provide another layer of information in proteomics searches.

We further scrutinized the unique predictions in these two experimental conditions, by performing gene enrichment analysis for the proteins that the identified phosphorylated peptides map to with Metascape44 and default settings with human background. This analysis revealed expected processes significantly enriched in these conditions such as regulation of the cell cycle and RHO GTPase activity (Fig. 5E). Overall, these results indicate that our model successfully predicted phosphorylated peptides that recapitulate the known biological processes downstream of F7-FGFR2 signaling in T47D cells45–47.

InstaNovo-P predictions of FGFR2-dependent phosphorylation events are confirmed by orthogonal assays

To confirm our data-dependent observations, we validated the predicted phosphorylated serine residue on both LIM-domain only protein 7 (LMO7) at position 926 and kinectin-1 (KTN1) at position 75 with targeted proteomics in newly collected samples for the same conditions (Fig. 6A and Supplementary Fig. 10A). We designed a parallel reaction monitoring (PRM) assay for the phosphorylated peptides “GISpSLPR" of LMO7 and “EIQNGNLHEpSDSESVPR” of KTN1, comprising residues 923 to 929 and residues 66 to 82 in the proteins, respectively (Fig. 6A and Supplementary Fig. 10A). We detected these peptides with our de novo predictions but not with the database search. To verify FGFR2 signaling, we acquired precursor/product transitions for the double-phosphorylated FGFR2 peptide “DINNIDpYpYK" within residues 650 to 658. We monitored several product ions for the precursor ion “DINNIDpYpYK", which included the singly charged y2 and y3 ions that preserved the mass shift of 80 Da, caused by the addition of the phosphate group (Fig. 6B). Our results provide evidence of the phosphorylated events via the neutral loss of the phosphoric acid group within the phosphorylated peptide “EIQNGNLHEpSDSESVPR” at position 75 through mass spectrometry-induced fragmentation (singly charged y8 ion), confirming our de novo predictions for KTN1 (Supplementary Fig. 10B, C). Similarly, we detect the mass shift caused by the neutral loss of phosphoric acid on serine 926 (doubly charged y4 ion) within the phosphorylated peptide “GISpSLPR" (Fig. 6A).

The chromatograms of both endogenous and isotopically-labeled reference peptides for the KTN1 precursor/product transitions—including y8 product ion displaying a −98 Da neutral loss –demonstrate aligned retention times (RT) between the endogenous and reference peptide in WT T47D breast cancer cell line (Supplementary Fig. 10B, C). Quantitative analysis of “GISpSLPR" mass spectra supported our observations of a significantly increased normalized peak area in F7-treated WT T47D breast cancer cell lines compared to untreated cells (Fig. 6C). In contrast, the FGFR2 KO T47D breast cancer cell line showed no significant change after treatment with F7 relative to untreated FGFR2 KO cells (Fig. 6C). Similarly, the double-phosphorylated FGFR2 peptide “DINNIDpYpYK" showed significant change between the F7-treated and untreated WT cells but remained unchanged in the KO cells (Fig. 6D).

We tested for the presence of the predicted phosphorylated serine residue on ribosomal S6 kinase 1 (RSK1) at position 732 by immunoblotting (Fig. 6E). This phosphorylated site was only present when the WT T47D cells were treated with F7, but not in the FGFR2 KO cells (see Data Availability). This change in phosphorylated RSK1 indicates the direct role of the FGFR2-F7 axis upstream of RSK1 phosphorylation (Fig. 6E). Furthermore, RSK1 inhibition with inhibitor BI-D1870, resulted in F7-dependent decrease in RSK1 phosphorylation in WT cells. The phosphorylation site is functionally annotated in PhosphoSitePlus and suggested to be a C-terminal autophosphorylation site for RSK1’s N-terminal kinase domain (NTK)10,48. Within the MAPK signaling cascade, ERK1 binds to RSK1’s C-terminus, phosphorylating T573, which in turn phosphorylates S380, recruiting phosphoinositide-dependent kinase 1 (PDK1)49. PDK1 phosphorylates S221 in the NTK, activating RSK1, whose NTK subsequently autophosphorylates S732, promoting ERK1 release and enabling nuclear translocation of RSK110,48,50. To confirm the functional relationship of F7 and RSK1 in cell proliferation, we performed a cell counting assay after treating the cells with an RSK1 inhibitor (RSK1i) and/or with F7 (Fig. 6F). Our data show that WT, but not FGFR2 KO, cells respond to F7 as shown by an increase in cell number upon 48 h of stimulation with F7. Furthermore, RSK1 inhibition with BI-D1870 resulted in decreased cell number in F7-treated WT cells. The cell number of FGFR2 KO cells decreased in all tested conditions. These results confirm a role for RSK1 phosphorylation downstream of F7-FGFR2b signaling (Fig. 6F).

To further assess the predicted phosphorylated peptides, we conducted another PRM assay, detecting fragment ions for the endogenous phosphorylation-dependent mass shift of 80 Da or the respective neutral loss within the co-eluting precursor peak. Out of 42 predicted modified peptides, we successfully identified 23 predicted phosphorylated peptides derived from T47D breast cancer cells, without the need for selective enrichment of phosphorylated peptides, which is usually performed to enhance the identification of low abundant phosphopeptides51. Analysis of samples with data independent acquisition (DIA) assay allowed us to identify 4 additional phosphorylated peptides, adding up to a total of 27 de novo identified peptides without sample enrichment (Supplementary Table 9). To identify whether InstaNovo-P can predict phosphorylation localization, we measured candidate endogenous peptides “SSpSPAPADIAQTVQEDLR" and “pSSSPAPADIAQTVQEDLR" originating from Ras GTPase-activating protein-binding protein 1 (G3BP1), a Ras GTPase involved in stress granule assembly52–54. Despite having an identical precursor mass of 982.9517 m/z, the database search assigned the phosphorylation to the first serine “pSSSPAPADIAQTVQEDLR", while InstaNovo-P predicted modification of the third serine “SSpSPAPADIAQTVQEDLR". Discriminating between these two endogenous species requires the presence of site-determining ions, specifically the y16[+80] and b3[−98] product ions55 (Fig. 6G). In our PRM data, both site-determining fragment ions were detected exclusively in the peptide “SSpSPAPADIAQTVQEDLR", whereas the alternative variant “pSSSPAPADIAQTVQEDLR" showed no signal in WT cells. We also confirmed the presence of “SSpSPAPADIAQTVQEDLR" in the DIA results, while “pSSSPAPADIAQTVQEDLR" was not identified under the same conditions. Taken together, these results demonstrate that our model correctly assigned the phosphorylation-dependent mass shift to the functionally annotated serine 232 residue on G3BP152–54. In summary, our targeted and DIA proteomics results show that InstaNovo-P predictions represent bona fide phosphorylated sites that database-based search algorithms miss. These residues and their associated tryptic peptides are differentially abundant in F7-treated and untreated WT T47D breast cancer cells, demonstrating changes under these conditions.

Discussion

In this work, we introduce and evaluate the phosphorylation-specific de novo peptide sequencing model, InstaNovo-P. We find InstaNovo-P to increase performance of the base model, InstaNovo, in unmodified peptides across all evaluation datasets. This indicates that catastrophic forgetting during fine-tuning was mitigated as the model not only maintains but improves performance in the source domain. We believe that the training strategy of using the encoder-first gradual unfreezing schedule contributed to this effect. By unfreezing the encoder layers before the decoder, we allowed the model to adapt to phosphorylation-specific features while preserving generalizable representations from the base model. This strategy may have helped avoid catastrophic forgetting, as reflected in improved recall on both phosphorylated and unmodified peptides. Further, our model was not only successful at improving detection of phosphorylated peptides, but also improving general sequencing performance. We speculate that this performance gain stems from the exposure of InstaNovo to additional diverse peptide spectra during fine-tuning and from improved representation learning of fragmentation patterns that generalize beyond phosphorylation to unmodified peptides. This performance improvement through further training is known as transfer learning, a well established training strategy56. We note that in our previous work and in independent benchmarking studies, transformer-based de novo sequencing models consistently outperform earlier non-transformer approaches such as DeepNovo, with typical recall gains of approximately 7–35% depending on the dataset25,57,58.

InstaNovo-P delivered state-of-the-art recall on phosphorylated peptide PSMs. InstaNovo-P was able to detect phosphorylated peptides not detected by database search with high confidence. Importantly, these phosphorylation events have been detected in previous proteomics experiments, validating our approach and findings. However, database search still identifies a substantial number of peptides not detected with InstaNovo-P, at least not as the top prediction. This is potentially due to lack of a complete fragment ion series, low fragment ion signal, chimeric spectra, or single residue errors in the peptide predictions. Overall, we believe that InstaNovo-P can be used in tandem with database searches to increase phosphorylated peptide identification.

We also find a substantial number of InstaNovo-P predictions that map to proteins in the reference proteome while not present in database results, further validating our predictions. Additionally, its confidence scores correlate with true FDR and database localization certainty, offering a powerful complementary layer of site-localization information. We observed several instances of our model disagreeing with database localization assignment, and validating these cases will be part of future work. We believe that tighter integration with database search engines, where InstaNovo-P could rescue ambiguous or unassigned spectra, will maximize phosphoproteome coverage and accelerate biological discovery. A limitation of our database grounded FDR estimation strategy lies in the assumption that the spectra unidentified by database search will fall within the distribution of the labeled spectra. Future work is being performed to develop decoy-free FDR estimations.

InstaNovo-P shows diminished performance in peptides with more than one phosphorylation site. Similarly, we see a correlation between peptide recall and peptide length. Longer sequences tend to result in increased sequence complexity and ambiguity, as well as potential missed cleavages, which make them harder to identify. From a modeling perspective, longer peptides also expand the combinatorial decoding space and increase the probability of error propagation across sequential predictions, particularly when fragment ion ladders are incomplete and effective ion coverage is reduced. Several directions may help address performance decay with peptide length in future work. These include length-stratified sampling during training to reduce the imbalance between short and long peptide representation, hybrid inference strategies that combine de novo predictions with database-guided refinement for longer sequences, modifications to the positional encoding or attention mechanism to better capture long-range dependencies in fragmentation spectra, and ensemble approaches that aggregate beam-search predictions with complementary scoring functions sensitive to full peptide mass coverage.

Unsurprisingly, we observe best performance on phosphorylation events in serine residues and worst performance on tyrosine phosphorylation events, reflecting our highly imbalanced training data composition, and we expect performance across phosphorylation types to equalize with the inclusion of larger phosphorylated threonine- and tyrosine-rich training datasets and targeted sampling strategies.

InstaNovo-P exhibits its highest recall on the 21PTM test set, potentially because it is synthetic peptides closely resembling our training dataset for the base model. Admittedly, it is challenging to compare models trained on different datasets with potential for data leakage. In this work, we have implemented a strict homology-based split to control data leakage.

InstaNovo-P has fixed inference, in contrast to database search were the space increases with addition of modifications. This might be beneficial for computational costs and search times, especially when large reference proteomics and additional modifications are considered. Notably, our training data is predominantly Orbitrap-based, yet we observed that InstaNovo-P can generalize to spectra acquired on Orbitrap Astral instruments. Although both InstaNovo-P and PrimeNovo exhibited substantially lower recall on our reprocessed Astral dataset, the better performance of InstaNovo-P suggests that the broader pre-training of the InstaNovo base model confers greater cross-instrument generalization. However, achieving comparable performance across detector types will likely require dedicated fine-tuning on larger datasets from other mass analyzers.

InstaNovo-P is only compatible with data-dependent acquisition, as it relies on additional information about precursor mass and charge. Adapting the model, or preprocessing DIA spectra to be compatible with the model, will be part of future directions. Similarly, we expect part of future work to be the interpretation of InstaNovo-P weights and attention, that can possibly inform on fragmentation events and ions that enable the model to accurately predict phosphorylated peptides.

Our experimental validations in cell models show that InstaNovo-P is a powerful tool for interrogating signaling biology. We confirmed RSK1 phosphorylation on S732 after a 40-min treatment of breast cancer cells with F7. This site was previously functionally annotated and was suggested to be relevant for nuclear translocation of RSK1 in the nucleus for further downstream activity48,50. We provide evidence that this site was regulated by the FGFR2-F7 axis. As a proof of concept, we also demonstrate that InstaNovo-P precisely assigned the phosphorylation-dependent mass shift to S232 on the GTPase G3BP1, whereas database search incorrectly localized the modification. While S230 lacks known functional annotation, S232 has been implicated in stress granule assembly, a key process with consequences for cellular fate52–54. Further studies are required to evaluate the performance of InstaNovo-P in phosphosite localization at a proteome-wide scale. However, these preliminary results further reinforce its potential as a robust de novo sequencing strategy.

Finally, in this study we describe a reproducible framework providing a blueprint for model fine-tuning for different modifications or application domains with substantial biological utility, thus facilitating broader advancements within proteomics research. We expect that such scalable de novo sequencing strategies will enable discoveries in cell signaling, post-translational regulation, and protein function. We anticipate that this framework will translate well to other modifications defined by fixed chemical mass shifts on specific amino acids, such as ubiquitination and dimethylation. In contrast, more structurally complex and heterogeneous PTMs such as glycosylation may require deeper architectural changes, dedicated model components, or specialization towards defined glycan classes to achieve similar performance.

Methods

Data preprocessing

For a description of the All-Confidence ProteomeTools (AC-PT) dataset used to train the base model, see the InstaNovo24 paper.

The dataset used for fine-tuning is comprised of a collection of reprocessed PRIDE projects in Scop3P32 (For a list of the projects, see Supplementary Table 1). The dataset originally contains 4, 053, 346 PSMs. To only fine-tune on high confidence PSMs, the dataset is filtered at a IonBot localization probability of 0.80, reducing it to 2, 760, 939 PSMs, representing 74, 686 unique peptide sequences. Most of the data is of human origin, except for PXD005366 and PXD000218, which contain a mix of human and mouse. All PSMs that were used to train the model contained at least one phosphorylated site, while 169, 114 PSMs (6%) contained oxidated methionine. The entries were acquired using a mixture of instruments, whereas the AC-PT dataset was acquired using only Orbitrap Fusion.

To partition the fine-tuning dataset into training, validation and test, GraphPart33, an algorithm for homology partitioning, was applied on the set of unique peptide sequences. GraphPart was set to use MMseqs259 with a partitioning threshold of 0.8 and a train-validation-test ratio of 0.7/0.1/0.2. Of the 74, 686 unique sequences, 390 were removed by GraphPart, reducing the total number of PSMs to 2, 691, 117 in a 2008, 923/232, 641/449, 553-split, although during training, a random subset of only 2% of the validation set was used in order to reduce computation.

The 21PTM35 dataset consists of around 5000 synthetic peptides carrying 21 different PTMs as well as their unmodified counterparts. We used the phosphorylated and unmodified subsets. To minimize data leakage, we removed any sequences that overlapped with the fine-tuning training set. We removed sequences that had identical sequences, even if they had different phosphorylation sites. For the phosphorylation subset, we dropped 4094 sequences, going from 45,252 to 41,158. For the unmodified subset, we dropped 2188 sequences, going from 34,823 to 32,635. Even though we removed sequences that overlapped with our phosphorylation training set, 21PTM still contains a substantial overlap with the training set of AC-PT, used to train the base InstaNovo model. Specifically, 56.15% of unmodified sequences in 21PTM overlap with AC-PT, owing to the fact that 21PTM is part of the ProteomeTools project. Of course, none of the phosphorylated sequences overlap. The FGFR2 FragPipe dataset consists of 275,364 PSMs, of which ≈94% is phosphorylated. The Astral dataset consists of around 468394 PSMs of which ≈0.3% is phosphorylated. We exclude sequences containing the residue N-terminal acetylation as this residue was not part of the training dataset. For evaluation of predictions in all datasets, we remapped carbamidomethylated cysteines (C[+57.02]) to “C", and isoleucine (I) to leucine (L).

Fine-tuning

Before fine-tuning, we reinitialized the head and sequence embedding of the model decoder, as they had to contain the extended vocabulary of the 3 phosphorylation tokens. All parameters in the specific layers were randomly initialized, therefore not carrying over any information from previous learning.

To fine-tune the base InstaNovo24 model we implemented an encoder-first gradual unfreezing fine-tuning schedule. This gradual unfreezing34 approach is typically implemented by first freezing the entire model, and then unfreezing layers, starting at the head, then progressing from the top of the decoder to the bottom of the decoder and sequence embedding, then from top to bottom through the encoder. We differ from this approach by first unfreezing the head and sequence embedding, then gradually the encoder, then gradually the decoder. We also explored other methods of fine-tuning, including full-model whereby the entire model is unfrozen from the start, head-only where only the final classifier layer is unfrozen and head-then-full where once the head has converged, the remainder of the model is unfrozen. When applying head-only fine-tuning, we also had to reinitialize the sequence embedding layer due to the different vocabulary sizes of the fine-tuning dataset and the dataset the base model was trained on. The results of the ablation study investigating the performance of these fine-tuning strategies are detailed in Supplementary Table 2. The alternative fine-tuning approach of encoder-first gradual unfreezing was motivated by the fact that the more standard fine-tuning approaches quickly led to overfitting.

We find the slanted triangular learning rate (STLR)34 scheduler to be most performant for this encoder-first gradual unfreezing. The learning rate is warmed up for the first 10% of the STLR period, and annealed for the remainder of the period (See Supplementary Fig. 1A). We applied the STLR scheduler in a cyclical manner, whereby each phase of unfreezing restarts the STLR schedule, thus allowing for all frozen parameters to warmup. This warmup strategy was found to be most performant (see Supplementary Table 10 for hyperparameters and Data Availability for the learning-rate schedule used). We do not use an automatic stopping criteria but instead monitor a variety of metrics (validation loss, validation peptide recall, validation peptide precision, etc.). The final model is chosen manually from an evaluation of these. The best model was found in the final epoch at step 273,725 at a validation loss of 0.3949.

The primary Python packages used for fine-tuning were PyTorch60, Lightning61, and Fine-Tuning Scheduler62 to manage the gradual unfreezing and learning rate scheduling.

Metrics

We evaluated our model using peptide-level metrics: peptide precision, peptide recall and Area-Under-Curve (AUC) of precision-recall curves and recall-coverage curves. We used exact string matching to decide whether two peptides were equal. Recall is defined as the number of correctly predicted PSMs over the total number of PSMs in the dataset, whereas precision is defined as the number of correctly predicted PSMs over the total number of predicted PSMs. Coverage is the proportion of the dataset that predictions are made on. For further details, see the InstaNovo24 paper.

To compute metrics at a 5% FDR threshold, we first calculate a confidence cutoff. This confidence cutoff is calculated by determining the confidence value which corresponds to a peptide precision of 95%, thus ensuring a 5% FDR. We make use of the database labels to determine the precision metrics and corresponding confidence value and refer to this FDR estimation method as database grounding. This confidence threshold is then applied to the de novo predictions to filter results at the specified FDR level. For a step-by-step description of our database grounding approach, see the Winnow37 paper.

Benchmarking

We compare our model performance against InstaNovo and PrimeNovo. To effectively benchmark across the same data, we run inference using the publicly available PrimeNovo and PrimeNovo-PTM checkpoints. The latter checkpoint was trained with the phosphorylation modification and so we can compare the performance of our model and PrimeNovo-PTM on both phosphorylated and unmodified data. We found that PrimeNovo-PTM includes the phosphorylation modification as a stand-alone residue in their model vocabulary. This allows the modification to be predicted alongside amino acids that cannot be phosphorylated. We also found that the phosphorylation modification was predicted prior to, rather than following, the corresponding valid amino acids. We reversed these modifications so that they would be identified as correct when calculating metrics. To reverse the modification position we implemented a simple regex pattern to identify instances of the phosphorylation modification followed by a standard amino acid. After identification, the order of this modification and amino acid was reversed. We found that this improved the peptide recall, for example, on the phosphorylated portion of the FGFR2 FragPipe dataset, the peptide recall improved from 3.42% to 30.54% following the reversal step, confirming that this reversal is necessary in the PrimeNovo-PTM metric calculations. We benchmarked on two datasets, FGFR2 FragPipe search data and Astral data (also processed with FragPipe). The FragPipe search was performed using MSFragger v4.1 with trypsin specificity and up to two missed cleavages. Protein N-terminal acetylation, methionine oxidation, and phosphorylation of serine, threonine, and tyrosine were included as variable modifications, while cysteine carbamidomethylation was set as a fixed modification. All other parameters were kept at default settings. Post-processing was performed using MSBooster (spectral and RT prediction with DIA-NN v1.8.2), Percolator, PTMProphet, and ProteinProphet, all with default settings. For the Astral dataset, searches were performed against the S. cerevisiae reference proteome (UniProt, downloaded 09/09/2024), while the FGFR2 dataset was searched against the human reference proteome (UniProt, downloaded 04/11/2025), using identical search parameters. The size of these datasets is detailed in Supplementary Table 5. We benchmark against InstaNovo and PrimeNovo on the unmodified portions of data as these models were not trained on phosphorylated data. We found the overall performance across the models to drop on the Astral dataset. This is not unexpected as the models were not trained on data from this instrument and so it is out of distribution of the training datasets.

Cell culture and FGFR2 depletion

The human epithelial cell line T47D (HTB-133) was purchased from ATCC and authenticated shortly before the samples were collected through short tandem repeat (STR) analysis of 21 markers by Eurofins Genomics, yielding positive results. The cells were routinely monitored monthly for mycoplasma contamination using a PCR-based detection assay (Venor® GeM-Cambio). T47D cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 Medium, GlutaMAX™ Supplement (Gibco), supplemented with 2 mM L-glutamine, 100 U/ml penicillin, 100 μg/ml streptomycin, and 10% fetal bovine serum. For gene editing, guide RNAs (crRNA) specific to FGFR2 (FGFR2 crRNA 1: GCCCTACCTCAAGGTTCTCA; FGFR2 crRNA 2: ACCTTGAGAACCTTGAGGTA) were synthesized (IDT) and combined with a common trans-activating crRNA (Alt-R® CRISPR-Cas9 tracrRNA, IDT) to form a functional ribonucleoprotein (RNP) duplex. The resulting complexes were pre-incubated with Cas9 nuclease (Alt-R® S.p. Cas9 Nuclease V3, IDT) and transiently transfected into parental T47D cells using Viromer® CRISPR transfection reagent (Cambridge Bioscience), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Colonies were subsequently selected and screened for genomic editing efficiency using the Inference of CRISPR Edits (ICE) analysis tool provided by Synthego. Successful knockout of FGFR2 protein expression was validated by Western blot analysis19. T47D were serum starved overnight and treated with 100 ng/mL of FGF7 from Peprotech for 40 min or 72 h before lysis for MS analysis.

Proteomics sample preparation

T47D samples for proteomics and phosphoproteomics were prepared as follows19: Cells were washed with PBS and lysed in ice-cold 1% Triton lysis buffer supplemented with Pierce protease inhibitor tablets (Life Technologies) and phosphatase inhibitors (5 nM Na3VO4, 5 mM NaF, and 5 mM β-glycerophosphate). Proteins were precipitated overnight at −20 ∘C in a four-fold excess of ice-cold acetone. Acetone-precipitated proteins were then solubilized in a denaturation buffer consisting of 6 M urea and 2 M thiourea in 10 mM HEPES (pH 8). Cysteines were reduced with 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) and alkylated with 5.5 mM chloroacetamide (CAA). Proteins were subsequently digested using endoproteinase Lys-C (Wako, Osaka, Japan) followed by sequencing-grade modified trypsin (Sigma), and the digestion reaction was quenched using 1% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA). Peptides were purified using reversed-phase Sep-Pak C18 cartridges (Waters, Milford, MA) and eluted with 50% ACN.

A small fraction (1%) of the eluted peptides was reserved for proteome analysis. These peptides were evaporated using a speed vacuum, resuspended in 40 μL of 0.1% TFA, 5% ACN, and loaded onto C18 STAGE-tips. For phosphoproteomic analysis, 6 mL of 12% TFA in ACN was added to the remaining peptides, which were then enriched using TiO2 beads (5 μm, GL Sciences Inc., Tokyo, Japan). The beads were suspended in 20 mg/mL 2,5-dihydroxybenzoic acid (DHB), 80% ACN, and 6% TFA, and the samples were incubated in a 1:2 (w/w) sample-to-bead ratio for 15 min with rotation. After centrifugation for 5 min, the supernatant was collected and subjected to a second incubation with a two-fold dilution of the previous bead suspension. Elution of phosphorylated peptides was done with 20 μL of 5% NH3, followed by 20 μL of 10% NH3 in 25% ACN, which were evaporated to a final volume of 5 μL in a speed vacuum. The concentrated phosphorylated peptides were acidified by adding 20 μL of 0.1% TFA and 5% ACN, and loaded onto C18 STAGE-tips. Peptides were eluted from STAGE-tips in 20 μL of 40% ACN, followed by 10 μL each of 60% ACN and 100% ACN, then reduced to 5 μL by SpeedVac. Finally, 5 μL of 0.1% FA and 5% ACN were added. A 1 μL aliquot of the sample (for proteome analysis) or a 3 μL aliquot (for phosphoproteome analysis) was transferred to a 5 μL sample loop and loaded onto the column at a flow rate of 300 nL/min with 5% B for 5 min and 13 min, respectively. After sample loading, the loop was removed from the flow path, and the flow rate was decreased from 300 to 200 nL/min within 1 min, with the mobile phase adjusted to 7% B.

Mass spectrometry data acquisition and analysis

Purified peptides were analyzed by LC-MS/MS using an UltiMate® 3000 Rapid Separation LC system (RSLC, Dionex Corporation, Sunnyvale, CA) coupled to a Q Exactive HF mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Mobile phase A consisted of 0.1% formic acid (FA) in water, and mobile phase B contained 0.1% FA in acetonitrile (ACN). Chromatographic separation was performed using a 75 mm × 250 μm inner diameter, 1.7 mM CSH C18 analytical column (Waters). Peptides were separated using a linear gradient as follows: 7—18% B over 64 min, 18—27% B over 8 min, and 27—60% B in 1 min. The column was subsequently washed at 60% B for 3 min and re-equilibrated for 6.5 min. At 85 min, the flow rate was increased back to 300 nL/min until the end of the run at 90 min. Mass spectrometry data were acquired over 90 min in positive ion mode using data-dependent acquisition. Peptides were automatically selected for fragmentation using a Top8 method (phosphoproteome) or Top12 method (proteome), based on precursors with charge states of 2, 3, or 4 and an m/z range of 300–1750 Th. Dynamic exclusion was set to 15 s. MS1 resolution was 120,000, with an AGC target of 3 × 106 and a maximum fill time of 20 ms. For MS2, resolution was set to 60,000 with an AGC target of 2 × 105 and a maximum fill time of 110 ms (Top12), or 30,000 with the same AGC target and a 45 ms fill time (Top8). The isolation window was set to 1.3 Th, and normalized collision energy was 28.

Raw data were analyzed using the MaxQuant software suite (https://www.maxquant.org; version 1.6.2.6) with the integrated Andromeda search engine. Proteins were identified by searching the HCD-MS/MS peak lists against a target/decoy version of the human UniProt Knowledgebase database, including complete proteome sets and isoforms (version 2019; https://uniprot.org/proteomes/UP000005640_9606), supplemented with commonly observed contaminants such as porcine trypsin and bovine serum proteins. Tandem mass spectra were matched with a mass tolerance of 7 ppm for precursor ions and 0.02 Da (or 20 ppm) for fragment ions. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine was set as a fixed modification. Variable modifications included protein N-terminal acetylation, N-pyro-glutamine formation, oxidized methionine, and phosphorylation of serine, threonine, and tyrosine for phosphoproteome analyses. For proteome datasets, variable modifications included N-terminal acetylation, oxidized methionine, and deamidation of asparagine and glutamine. Site localization probabilities were computed by MaxQuant using the PTM scoring algorithm. Data were filtered by posterior error probability to ensure an FDR below 1% at the levels of peptides, proteins, and modification sites. Only peptides with an Andromeda score greater than 40 were included in the final analysis.

Quantification of InstaNovo-P predictions

Quantification was performed using AUC calculation for the predictions at their measured mass charge (m/z), with a tolerance of ±0.05. PSMs with identical sequences, but a difference in mass of more than ±0.05, were considered different PSMs. For identical PSMs, the RT and m/z from the first identified PSM was used for quantification. Boundaries for the AUC were defined to limit cross-interference as the largest window between local minima within a ±1% RT window of the identification RT. Quantification was performed on MS1 spectra. Noise was calculated for each experiment, as the mean of the lowest 5% intensities from all MS2 spectra in the given file, and then subtracted from each intensity measurement during calculation of AUC. PSMs were grouped across experiment files if mass was within tolerance of ±0.05 and sequences and modifications were identical. Each group was then considered as one peptide. Peptide mass was determined by the average mass of the combined PSMs, and confidence was assigned as the highest confidence of the peptide’s PSMs.

Targeted proteomics validation

Confluent (80%) T47D breast cancer cells were starved in FBS-free medium for 24 h. Subsequently cells were either treated with 100 ng/mL F7 or kept untreated for 40 min. Cells were placed on ice and 100 μL of lysis buffer (4 M GuHCl and 100 mM HEPES (pH 8.0)) was added together with Pierce protease inhibitor tablets (Life Technologies) and cells were scraped off. Scraped cells were transferred into a fresh tube, heated to 95 ∘C for 5 min, and sonicated at 30% for 30 s while kept on ice. Lysed cells were centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 15 min at 4 ∘C and the supernatant was transferred into a fresh tube. The Bradford assay was used to determine protein concentration and 5 μg of protein lysate were used for reduction with Tris-(2-carboxyethyl)-phosphin (TCEP) (5 mM final concentration) and alkylation with CAA both carried out at 400 rpm, 65 ∘C for 30 min. After reduction and alkylation, samples were diluted 8-fold to reduce the GuHCl concentration to 0.5 M using 100 mM HEPES (pH 8.0) and Lys-C (Wako, Osaka, Japan) (1:100) was added for 4 h followed by overnight digestion with sequencing-grade modified trypsin (Sigma) at 37 ∘C. The reaction was quenched using 1% TFA. Peptides were purified using Solaμ plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Purified peptides and phosphorylated peptides were analyzed by LC-MS/MS using a Vanquish LC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) coupled to a Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Mobile phase A consisted of 0.1% formic acid (FA) in water, and mobile phase B contained 0.1% FA in 80% acetonitrile (ACN). Chromatographic separation was performed using a μPAC HPLC analytical column (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Peptides were separated using a gradient as follows: 1–10% B over 4.1 min, 10.1–22.5% B over 19 min, 22.5–45% over 8.4 min, and 45–95% over 6.5 min. The flow rate is kept at 750 nL/min over the initial 4.1 min and changed to 250 nL/min over 33.9 min. An initial data-dependent acquisition (DDA) strategy is used to acquire the spectral library of selected peptides and different charge states. The mass list for the reference heavy peptides is DINNIDYYK (583,284402 m/z), DINNIDYYK (389,192027 m/z), DINNIDYYK (292,145839 m/z), DINNIDY[+80]Y[+80]K (663,250733 m/z), DINNIDY[+80]Y[+80]K (442,502914 m/z), DINNIDY[+80]Y[+80]K (332,129005 m/z), EIQNGNLHESDSESVPR (960,949709 m/z), EIQNGNLHESDSESVPR (640,968898 m/z), EIQNGNLHESDSESVPR (480,978493 m/z), EIQNGNLHES[+80]DSESVPR (1000,932875 m/z), EIQNGNLHES[+80]DSESVPR (667,624342 m/z), EIQNGNLHES[+80]DSESVPR (500,970075 m/z), GISSLPR (370,220455 m/z), GISSLPR (247,149395 m/z), GISSLPR (185,613865 m/z), GISS[+80]LPR (410,20362 m/z), GISS[+80]LPR (273,804839 m/z), GISS[+80]LPR (205,605448 m/z). The scans were unscheduled and a dependent scan was performed on the most intense ion if no target was found. For MS1, resolution was set to 120000 with an AGC target of 300% or 3 × 106 and a maximum fill time of 50 ms over a scan range of 300–1500 m/z. For MS2 resolution was set to 7500, isolation window was set to 1 m/z, normalized collision energy was set to 30% over a scan range of 150–1700 m/z and the AGC target was 1000% or 1 × 106 and a maximum fill time of 10 ms.

Following the DDA scan the peptides were acquired using a unscheduled parallel reaction monitoring (PRM) strategy within the biological matrix to obtain RTs, using the following mass list for endogenous and reference heavy peptides: DINNIDY[+80]Y[+80]K (659.243634 m/z), DINNIDY[+80]Y[+80]K[+8] (663.250733 m/z), DINNIDYYK (579.277303 m/z), DINNIDYYK[+8] (583.284402 m/z), EIQNGNLHES[+80]DSESVPR (995.92874 m/z), EIQNGNLHES[+80]DSESVPR[+10] (1000.932875 m/z), EIQNGNLHESDSESVPR (955.945575 m/z), EIQNGNLHESDSESVPR[+10] (960.949709 m/z), GISSLPR (365.21632 m/z), GISSLPR[+10] (370.220455 m/z), GISS[+80]LPR (405.199486 m/z), and GISS[+80]LPR[+10] (410.20362 m/z). For MS1, resolution was set to 120,000 with an AGC target of 300% or 3 × 106 and a maximum fill time of 246 ms over a scan range of 300–1500 m/z. For MS2 resolution was set to 7500, isolation window was set to 1 m/z, normalized collision energy was set to 27% over a scan range of 150–1700 m/z and the AGC target was 1000% or 1 × 106 and a maximum fill time was set to auto.

Raw data were analyzed via Skyline63 (Version 24.1.0.414, Seattle, WA). The DDA survey run was imported and converted into a spectral library. The spectral library was built using the directed DDA runs, including following modifications, Carbamidomethyl (C), phosphorylation (STY) and isotope modifications at the C-terminus (13C(6)15N(4) (C-term R) and 13C(6)15N(2) (C-term K)). Light and heavy peptides were included and unscheduled runs were used to detect RTs that were exported and used for scheduled PRM runs. MS1 filtering was performed by including a minimum of 20% of the base peak at 10 ppm accuracy. MS2 filtering was set to PRM as acquisition method.

Peptide peaks were automatically determined and manually adjusted. Endogenous (light) raw peak areas were normalized to isotopically labeled (heavy) peaks and the normalized peak ratios were exported. Prism GraphPad was used for statistical analysis and visualization.

To monitor endogenous InstaNovo-P predicted phosphorylated peptides T47D WT breast cancer cells were plated in 6-well plates, starved in FBS-free medium for 24 h and collected for MS-based analysis. In brief, samples were prepared as described previously and purified peptides and phosphorylated peptides were analyzed by LC-MS/MS using a Vanquish LC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) coupled to a Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Mobile phase A consisted of 0.1% formic acid (FA) in water, and mobile phase B contained 0.1% FA in 80% acetonitrile (ACN). Chromatographic separation was performed using a μPAC HPLC analytical column (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Peptides were separated using a gradient as follows: 1–10% B over 4.1 min, 10.1–22.5% B over 19 min, 22.5–45% over 8.4 min, and 45–95% over 6.5 min. The flow rate is kept at 750 nL/min over the initial 4.1 min and changed to 250 nL/min over 38 min.

MS1 resolution was set to 60,000 with an AGC target and a maximum fill time set to auto over a scan range of 200–1500 m/z. For MS2 resolution was set to 120,000, isolation window was set to 1 m/z, normalized collision energy was set to 27% over a scan range of 150–1700 m/z and the AGC target was 100% or 1 × e6 and a maximum fill time was set to 256 ms.

Raw data were analyzed via Skyline63 (Version 25.1.0.237, Seattle, WA). Peptide peaks were automatically determined and manually adjusted. Endogenous (light) raw peak areas were normalized to Total Ion Currents (TIC) and the transition list was exported. In the absence of a spectral library and heavy reference peptides, phosphopeptide candidates from the transition list were filtered to maximize confidence in peak assignment and site localization. Product ions were first required to show elution peaks within a ±1-min RT window. For these co-eluting precursors, at least five shared fragment ions within the same peak and replicate were required. Only peptides fulfilling both requirements and carrying the phosphorylation-dependent modified b- or y-ion value were included for identification. Prism GraphPad was used for statistical analysis and visualization.

The same lysates were used for a DIA assay. Peptides were separated using a gradient as follows: 1–10% B over 7.1 min, 10.1–22.5% B over 42 min, 22.5–45% over 12.4 min, and 45–95% over 6.5 min. The flow rate is kept at 750 nL/min over the initial 4.1 min and changed to 250 nL/min over 68 min. A DIA strategy in positive mode was employed for the acquisition. For MS1, resolution was set to 120,000 with an AGC target of 300% or 3 × 106 and a maximum fill time set to auto ms over a scan range of 400–1000 m/z. For MS2 resolution was set to 60,000, isolation window was set to 6 m/z, over a scan range of 400–600 m/z for the first DIA assay, 600–800 m/z for the second DIA assay and 800–1000 m/z for the last DIA assay. The AGC target was 1000% or 1 × 107 and a maximum fill time set to auto.

Raw data were analyzed using Spectronaut (Biognosys) with the integrated BGS Phospho PTM workflow. All setting were kept at default and LysC/P was used as additional cleavage rule, a semi-specific search was used for peptide identification and the minimum peptide length was set to 6. All other settings were kept at default. Proteins were identified using the human UniProt database, including reviewed (canonical) proteins (version 2019; https://uniprot.org/proteomes/UP000005640).

Cell lysis and SDS-PAGE immunoblotting

Cells from endogenous T47D breast cancer cells (80% confluency) were starved in FBS-free medium for 8 h, and subsequently treated with 100 ng/mL F7 for 40 min or treated with 10 μM (final concentration) RSK1 inhibitor (BI-D1870; Selleck Chemicals) for 40 min or kept untreated. Cells were placed on ice and 100 μL of ice-cold lysis buffer RIPA buffer (1% Triton X 100, 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, and 0.1% SDS) was supplemented with EDTA-free Pierce protease inhibitor tablets and phosphatase inhibitor tablets (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cells were scraped off, transferred into a fresh tube and lysed cells were centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 10 min at 4 ∘C. The supernatant was transferred into a fresh tube. Protein concentration was determined using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 30 μg of protein lysate were used for reduction with 4x Laemmli LDS sample buffer (Invitrogen) and boiled for 5 min at 95 ∘C.

Samples were loaded on 4–12% pre-cast Bis-Tris gels (Invitrogen) and run in MOPS buffer (Invitrogen). The molecular weight marker used for immunoblotting was Precision Plus Protein Dual Color Standards (Bio-Rad Laboratories; 1610374). Proteins were transferred onto 0.2 μM nitrocellulose membranes (BioRad) using the Trans-Blot Turbo System (BioRad) and membranes were blocked for 45 min in 5 % milk in T-PBS (PBS with Tween-20). Subsequently, membranes were incubated with the following primary antibodies: FGFR2 (Cell Signaling Technology, 23328, 1:1000, Monoclonal, Anti-Mouse, RRID:AB_2798862); RSK1 (Cell Signaling Technology, 9333, 1:1000, Polyclonal, Anti-Rabbit, RRID:AB_2181177); pRSK1(Abcam, ab106087, 1:1000, Polyclonal, Anti-Rabbit, RRID:AB_10901045); γ-Tubulin (Sigma Aldrich, T5326, 1:2500, Monoclonal, Anti-Mouse, RRID:AB_532292). Blot were kept in 3 % BSA T-PBS solution overnight at 4 ∘C while shaking. Membranes were washed with T-PBS for 1 min and incubated with IgG-HRP-coupled secondary antibodies for 45 min at RT. After 4 × 5 min T-PBS washes, protein bands were developed using ECL Western Blotting Substrate (BioRad). Chemiluminescence was detected using BioRad’s Chemidoc Imaging Systems and software. Uncropped and unprocessed scans of all the immunoblots in the study are provided together with other supplementary files on Figshare (see “Data availability”).

Cell counting assay

Cells from WT and FGFR2 KO T47D cell lines were plated for 24 h in 6-well plates. Cells were washed with PBS and cultured in FBS-free medium. After 24 h, cells were treated with either 10 μM RSK1 inhibitor (Selleck Chemicals; BI-D1870) for 60 min or with DMSO. Subsequently, cells were treated with 100 ng/mL F7 for 48 h or kept untreated. The F7-treated cells were re-supplemented with 100 ng/mL F7 every 24 h and all dishes were kept in FBS-free medium for the entirety of the treatment.

After 48 h, each well was washed with PBS and cells were detached with trypsin. Trypsin was neutralized with FBS-containing medium and transferred into a fresh tube. Before mixing a 10 μL aliquot of cell suspension together with 10 μL of trypan blue (Invitrogen) each tube was inverted twice. The cell solution was placed in the cell counting chamber slide (Invitrogen) and cells were counted using the Countess III automated cell counter64 (Invitrogen). Cell counts were quantified at treatment initiation and after 48 h. Cell counts were normalized to the median of the respective genotype after 24 h in FBS-free medium. Data were analyzed in GraphPad Prism (Dotmatics). A two-way ANOVA with Holm-Šídák post-hoc test was applied to detect changes between genotypes within the same condition.

Software and packages

Database searches were performed with MaxQuant v1.6.2.6 (with the integrated Andromeda search engine) and with FragPipe v22.0 (MSFragger v4.1, DIA-NN v1.8.2). Targeted proteomics data were analyzed with Skyline v24.1.0.414 and v25.1.0.237, and DIA data with Spectronaut v20.1.250624.92449 (Biognosys, BGS Phospho PTM workflow). IonBot v1.0 was used (via Scop3P) for reprocessing of training data. Homology-based dataset splitting used GraphPart v1.0.2 with MMseqs2 v15. CRISPR editing efficiency was evaluated with Synthego ICE. Statistical analysis and plotting of wet-lab measurements were performed in GraphPad Prism v10.3.0. Gene-set enrichment was performed with Metascape (default settings, human background). Phosphosite annotation was cross-referenced against PhosphoSitePlus.

Model training and inference were implemented in Python v3.12 using PyTorch60 v2.8.0, PyTorch Lightning61 v1.8.6 and Fine-Tuning Scheduler62 v0.3.4. Spectrum files were parsed with spectrum_utils v0.4.1, pyOpenMS v3.1.0. Data wrangling, evaluation, quantification and figure generation used NumPy v1.23.3, pandas v2.0.3, scikit-learn v1.5.0, matplotlib v3.7.2, seaborn v0.12.0. All custom analysis scripts are available in the InstaNovo-P GitHub repository (see Code Availability).
