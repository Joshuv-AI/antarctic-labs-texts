Ramadan fasting–induced modulation of beneficial and pathobiont gut bacteria in a healthy Iranian population
Authors: Abbas Asoudeh-Fard, Mahmood Nejabat, Kamran Bagheri Lankarani, Mohadeseh Asoudeh-Fard, Ahmad Gholami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRamadan fasting induces dietary and metabolic changes that may alter gut microbiota, but species-level effects are not well studied in Middle Eastern populations.MethodsFecal samples were collected from 30 healthy fasting and 30 non-fasting adults in Shiraz, Iran. Species-level bacterial abundance was quantified using culture-based isolation and SYBR Green qRT-PCR with universal and species-specific primers.ResultsBeneficial bacteria—including Bacteroides vulgatus (Ab.352 TH, NCBI PX796484), Bacteroides ovatus (Ab.353 TH, NCBI PX796487), and Odoribacter splanchnicus (Ab.354 TH, NCBI PX796490)—were significantly increased in fasting individuals, while pathobionts such as Prevotella stercorea (Ab.355 TH, NCBI PX796493), Alistipes putredinis (Ab.356 TH, NCBI PX796496), Collinsella aerofaciens (Ab.357 TH, NCBI PX796499), and Enterobacter cloacae (Ab.358 TH, NCBI PX796500) were reduced.ConclusionRamadan fasting promotes a gut microbiota enriched in SCFA-producing beneficial bacteria and depleted of inflammation-associated pathobionts, suggesting species-specific microbial adaptations that may enhance gastrointestinal, immunological, and systemic health.Graphical abstract Workflow and key findings of the study investigating Ramadan fasting–induced modulation of gut microbiota in healthy Iranian adults. Introduction

The human gut microbiota constitutes a highly diverse and dynamic ecosystem that exerts profound effects on host physiology, including modulation of immune responses, energy metabolism, nutrient absorption, and maintenance of intestinal barrier integrity [1, 2]. An optimal balance between beneficial commensals and potentially harmful pathobionts is critical for sustaining health [3]. Beneficial bacteria support anti-inflammatory pathways, produce short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) such as acetate, propionate, and butyrate, and maintain mucosal homeostasis, while overgrowth of pathobionts can promote inflammation, gut barrier dysfunction, and susceptibility to metabolic, gastrointestinal, and systemic diseases [4–6]. Disruption of this balance, known as dysbiosis, has been implicated in a wide range of conditions including obesity, type 2 diabetes, inflammatory bowel disease, and cardiovascular disorders [7–9]. Dietary interventions are among the most potent modulators of gut microbial composition and function [10]. Intermittent fasting, including culturally and religiously motivated practices such as Ramadan fasting, provides a natural model to study the effects of prolonged fasting on the gut microbiota [11–13]. During Ramadan, individuals abstain from food and drink from dawn to sunset for approximately 29–30 days, followed by consumption of nocturnal meals that often include fibers, legumes, fermented dairy, fruits, vegetables, and other nutrient-dense foods. This distinctive feeding pattern—characterized by prolonged fasting, altered meal timing, and changes in macronutrient intake—has the potential to induce significant shifts in gut microbial composition [14–16]. Emerging evidence suggests that intermittent fasting can selectively enhance beneficial taxa, reduce the abundance of pathobionts, and improve metabolic and inflammatory profiles in the host, although data from Middle Eastern populations remain limited [17–21].

The province of Fars in southern Iran represents a unique setting for investigating these effects due to its traditional dietary practices during Ramadan. Locally consumed meals are often rich in plant-based fibers, fermented foods, and nutrient-dense ingredients that may act synergistically with fasting to modulate gut microbiota composition [22, 23]. Understanding species-specific responses to Ramadan fasting in this context is crucial for identifying potential dietary strategies that promote gut health and reduce disease risk [24]. Several gut bacterial species have been identified as key modulators of host health. Among them, Bacteroides vulgatus and Bacteroides ovatus are recognized as beneficial, contributing to polysaccharide fermentation, SCFA production, and maintenance of intestinal barrier function, which collectively support metabolic homeostasis and anti-inflammatory effects [25–27]. Odoribacter splanchnicus produces butyrate and other metabolites that modulate immune responses and promote intestinal integrity [28]. In contrast, pathobionts such as Prevotella stercorea, Alistipes putredinis, Collinsella aerofaciens, and Enterobacter cloacae have been linked to pro-inflammatory pathways, gut dysbiosis, and opportunistic infections. P. stercorea can activate inflammatory signaling cascades, A. putredinis produces metabolites associated with metabolic imbalances, C. aerofaciens may contribute to insulin resistance and lipid dysregulation, and E. cloacae is an opportunistic pathogen with the potential for systemic infections [29–35]. Quantifying shifts in these specific taxa provides critical insight into the microbial mechanisms through which dietary practices like Ramadan fasting may influence host health [17].

Despite its global significance, few studies have systematically investigated the impact of Ramadan fasting on gut microbiota in healthy Middle Eastern populations, and species-level analyses remain scarce [36]. Most existing studies focus on broad taxonomic changes without differentiating between beneficial and pathobiont taxa, leaving an important gap in understanding the functional consequences of fasting [37]. Furthermore, the interplay between traditional dietary patterns and intermittent fasting in modulating gut microbiota composition has not been fully elucidated in Iranian populations. Addressing this gap is essential for identifying region-specific, culturally relevant strategies to enhance gut microbial balance and overall health. In this study, we examined species-level alterations in the gut microbiota of healthy adults residing in Fars Province, Iran, during Ramadan fasting. We compared 30 fasting individuals with 30 non-fasting controls to assess whether this culturally significant dietary intervention differentially modulates beneficial versus potentially harmful bacterial populations. Our primary hypothesis was that Ramadan fasting, through prolonged fasting periods, altered meal timing, and nutrient-rich dietary intake, would increase the abundance of beneficial taxa (B. vulgatus, B. ovatus, O. splanchnicus) while reducing the prevalence of pathobionts (P. stercorea, A. putredinis, C. aerofaciens, E. cloacae), thereby promoting a healthier gut microbial ecosystem. By providing detailed, species-level insights into the gut microbial response to Ramadan fasting, this study aims to advance understanding of the diet–microbiota–host axis in Middle Eastern populations. Our findings may inform culturally appropriate nutritional interventions, support strategies for maintaining microbial balance, and highlight the potential of intermittent fasting as a non-pharmacological approach to improve metabolic and immune health. Moreover, this research underscores the importance of considering regional dietary habits and culturally specific feeding patterns when evaluating the health effects of dietary interventions.

Mterial and Method

Study population and sample collection

This study was conducted at Motahari Clinic, Shiraz, Iran. A total of 60 healthy adults aged 18–50 years participated, including 30 individuals who observed Ramadan fasting and 30 non-fasting controls. All participants were free of chronic diseases, had not used any medications or antibiotics within the prior three months, and provided written informed consent before enrollment. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences, Shiraz, Iran (Ethics Code: IR.SUMS.REC.1399.645), and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant international ethical guidelines for research involving human-derived biological materials. Both male and female subjects were included to ensure a representative sample of the healthy population. Fecal samples were collected under standardized conditions. Fecal samples were collected during the middle of Ramadan (days 14–16) to capture a stable, adapted fasting state, specifically in the evening approximately 2–3 h after Iftar. Non-fasting control samples were collected under matched evening conditions, 2–3 h after dinner, to ensure comparability between groups. Fasting individuals adhered to typical Ramadan dietary patterns: a light meal at Iftar (sunset), a moderate meal at midnight, and a heavier meal at Suhoor (pre-dawn). Non-fasting participants consumed their usual daily diet with regular meal timing. All participants consumed a mixed diet including carbohydrates, proteins, and vegetables, reflecting typical regional dietary patterns. Samples were collected in sterile containers, immediately stored on ice, and processed for microbial analysis [38].

Bacterial culture and isolation

Target bacterial species were isolated from fecal samples using selective culture media appropriate for each strain. Fecal material was collected with sterile swabs, homogenized in buffered saline, and serially diluted. For initial isolation, streak plating was performed to obtain well-isolated colonies, which were then sub-cultured to ensure pure cultures. Bacteroides vulgatus and Bacteroides ovatus: cultured on Bacteroides Bile Esculin (BBE) agar (Anaerobe Systems, Cat. No. AS‑114) under anaerobic conditions at 37 °C. This selective medium supports the growth of Bacteroides species and differentiates them based on esculin hydrolysis. Odoribacter splanchnicus: cultured on Anaerobe Basal Agar (ABA) (Thermo Scientific, Cat. No. CM0972B) under anaerobic incubation at 37 °C, which provides a nutrient-rich environment for fastidious anaerobic gut bacteria. Prevotella stercorea: cultured on Fastidious Anaerobe Agar (FAA) (Thermo Scientific, Cat. No. PB0225A) under anaerobic conditions at 37 °C, suitable for obligate anaerobes. Alistipes putredinis: also cultured on Fastidious Anaerobe Agar (FAA) (Thermo Scientific, Cat. No. PB0225A) under anaerobic incubation at 37 °C. Collinsella aerofaciens: cultured on Reinforced Clostridial Medium (RCM) agar (Thermo Scientific, Cat. No. CM0149B) under anaerobic conditions at 37 °C, which supports growth of anaerobic gut commensals. Enterobacter cloacae: cultured on MacConkey agar (Thermo Scientific, Cat. No. CM0007) under aerobic conditions at 37 °C, a selective medium for Gram-negative facultative anaerobes. All cultures were incubated until visible colonies were obtained under appropriate aerobic or anaerobic conditions. Pure colonies were obtained through sub-culturing, and isolates were confirmed using standard biochemical tests prior to downstream DNA extraction [39].

DNA Extraction and Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total genomic DNA was extracted from fecal samples and pure bacterial cultures using the Yekta Tajhiz Erma DNA Extraction Kit (Cat. No. YT9020, Iran) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentration and purity of the extracted DNA were determined using a Thermo Scientific NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). DNA quality was evaluated based on the A260/A280 and A260/A230 absorbance ratios, with samples showing A260/A280 ratios of 1.8–2.0 and A260/A230 ratios of ≥ 2.0 considered acceptable; only samples meeting these purity criteria were used for subsequent analyses. Following extraction, all DNA samples were submitted to Sinacoloul Company for quality control and further processing. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using Taq DNA Polymerase Master Mix RED Amplicon (Ampliqon, Denmark; Cat. No. A180301) and SYBR® Green Master Mix (Ampliqon, Denmark; Cat. No. A322701) in a 96-well format on a Bio-Rad iQ5 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). Each reaction was carried out in a final volume of 25 µL, containing SYBR® Green Master Mix, 0.4 µM of each forward and reverse primer, 20 ng of template DNA, and nuclease-free water to adjust the final reaction volume. Universal bacterial primers (Table 1) were initially used to quantify total bacterial abundance, followed by species-specific primers (Table 2) for the quantification of individual bacterial taxa. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate. The amplification protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 36 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, primer-specific annealing for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s. A melting curve analysis was performed at the end of each run to verify amplification specificity and exclude non-specific PCR products. Positive controls consisting of genomic DNA extracted from previously confirmed bacterial isolates included in this study were incorporated into each qPCR run to verify amplification performance, while no-template controls (NTCs) containing nuclease-free water were included to monitor reagent contamination and non-specific amplification. Ct values were acquired and analyzed using Bio-Rad iQ5 Optical System Software. The relative abundance of each bacterial species was calculated using the ΔCt method, with the universal bacterial 16 S rRNA gene serving as the internal reference for normalization. This approach enabled accurate species-level quantification of both beneficial bacteria (Bacteroides vulgatus, Bacteroides ovatus, and Oscillibacter splanchnicus) and pathobionts (Parabacteroides stercorea, Alistipes putredinis, Collinsella aerofaciens, and Enterobacter cloacae), thereby providing a comprehensive characterization of gut microbial composition in fasting and non-fasting individuals. Following molecular identification, all confirmed bacterial strains were deposited in the NCBI database to ensure traceability, reproducibility, and public accessibility of the generated sequence data [40].

Statistical analysis

Relative abundance of each bacterial species, measured by qRT-PCR (ΔCT values), was compared between fasting and non-fasting groups. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Independent t-tests were used to compare ΔCT values between the two groups. Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test prior to selecting the independent-samples t-test (Welch’s correction applied where variances were unequal). Exact P-values are reported for all comparisons, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses and graphical plots were performed using SPSS v24 (IBM, USA). Bar charts were used to visualize differences in bacterial abundance between groups.

Results

Registration of isolated bacterial strains in the NCBI database

All isolated and molecularly confirmed bacterial strains obtained from fecal samples were successfully registered in the NCBI GenBank database, ensuring traceability, reproducibility, and future accessibility of the data. The deposited sequences included both beneficial commensals and pathobiont species representative of the human gut microbiota. The registered strains and accession numbers were as follows: Bacteroides ovatus strain Ab.353 TH (PX796486), Odoribacter splanchnicus strain Ab.354 TH (PX796487), Leyella stercorea (Prevotella stercorea) strain Ab.355 TH (PX796490), Alistipes putredinis strain Ab.356 TH (PX796493), Collinsella aerofaciens strain Ab.356 TH (PX796496), Enterobacter cloacae strain Ab.358 TH (PX796500), and Phocaeicola vulgatus (formerly Bacteroides vulgatus) strain Ab.352 TH (PX796484). These data are publicly available through the NCBI database. No additional datasets were generated or analyzed in this study.

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis of gut bacterial composition

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis demonstrated clear and consistent alterations in the gut microbial composition between fasting and non-fasting individuals. Relative abundance of target bacterial species was calculated using the ΔCT method, normalized to total bacterial 16 S rRNA gene levels, and expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Overall, Ramadan fasting was associated with a pronounced enrichment of beneficial anaerobic bacteria alongside a marked reduction in pathobiont and opportunistic species. Among beneficial taxa, Bacteroides vulgatus (Phocaeicola vulgatus) exhibited a substantial increase in the fasting group (2.55 ± 0.12) compared with non-fasting individuals (0.22 ± 0.11) (P < 0.05). This species is a well-recognized producer of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), particularly acetate and propionate, and contributes to gut barrier maintenance and host metabolic regulation. Similarly, Bacteroides ovatus showed significantly higher abundance in fasting participants (3.01 ± 0.11) relative to non-fasting controls (0.82 ± 0.10) (P < 0.01). Given its role in the degradation of complex dietary polysaccharides and modulation of immune responses, the enrichment of B. ovatus likely reflects adaptive microbial restructuring in response to altered meal timing and nutrient flux during Ramadan. In parallel, Odoribacter splanchnicus, a butyrate-producing anaerobe with established immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory properties, was markedly elevated in fasting individuals (2.81 ± 0.20) compared with the non-fasting group (0.62 ± 0.17) (P < 0.01), indicating a shift toward a metabolically favorable and anti-inflammatory gut environment. In contrast, several pathobiont species displayed significantly lower relative abundance in the fasting group. Prevotella stercorea (Leyella stercorea) was markedly reduced in fasting individuals (0.19 ± 0.14) compared with non-fasting participants (2.35 ± 0.14) (P < 0.05). Elevated levels of Prevotella species have been repeatedly associated with intestinal inflammation and dysbiotic states; therefore, its suppression during fasting suggests a protective modulation of the gut ecosystem. Likewise, Alistipes putredinis abundance was significantly lower in the fasting group (0.47 ± 0.11) relative to non-fasting controls (2.33 ± 0.12) (P < 0.01). Given previous links between Alistipes species, inflammatory signaling, and metabolic imbalance, this reduction further supports the beneficial impact of fasting on microbial homeostasis. Collinsella aerofaciens also showed decreased abundance in fasting individuals (0.71 ± 0.15) compared with non-fasting subjects (1.90 ± 0.10) (P < 0.01). As increased Collinsella levels have been associated with enhanced gut permeability and pro-inflammatory metabolic profiles, its reduction may indicate improved intestinal integrity during fasting. Finally, the opportunistic Gram-negative bacterium Enterobacter cloacae was markedly suppressed in the fasting group (0.26 ± 0.23) compared with non-fasting individuals (2.17 ± 0.17) (P < 0.05). Considering its association with endotoxemia and systemic inflammation, the reduction of E. cloacae highlights the potential role of Ramadan fasting in limiting harmful facultative anaerobic populations (Figure. 1). Collectively, these findings indicate that Ramadan fasting induces a distinct and favorable remodeling of the gut microbiota, characterized by enrichment of beneficial SCFA-producing bacteria and suppression of inflammation-associated pathobionts.

Discussion

In the present study, we demonstrate that Ramadan fasting induces a marked and coordinated remodeling of the gut microbiota at the species level, favoring beneficial anaerobic bacteria while suppressing pathobiont and opportunistic taxa. These conclusions are strongly supported by our quantitative real-time PCR findings (Fig. 1), which reveal clear and consistent differences in the relative abundance of target bacterial species between fasting and non-fasting individuals. The magnitude and direction of these changes provide important mechanistic insights into the health-promoting effects of fasting. As shown in (Fig. 1), fasting individuals exhibited a pronounced increase in the relative abundance of Bacteroides vulgatus, Bacteroides ovatus, and Odoribacter splanchnicus, with ΔCT values markedly higher than those observed in non-fasting controls. Specifically, B. vulgatus and B. ovatus displayed more than a tenfold relative increase, while O. splanchnicus showed a similarly robust enrichment. These quantitative shifts are biologically meaningful, as all three species are strict anaerobes involved in complex carbohydrate fermentation and short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production [41, 42]. The enrichment of these taxa during Ramadan fasting likely reflects the altered dietary rhythm and prolonged fasting periods, which favor microbial communities adapted to intermittent nutrient availability and fiber fermentation. SCFAs, particularly acetate, propionate, and butyrate, play central roles in gastrointestinal and immune homeostasis [43–45]. The fasting-associated increase in Bacteroides spp. and O. splanchnicus may enhance epithelial energy supply, reinforce tight junction integrity, and reduce intestinal permeability, thereby limiting microbial translocation and systemic endotoxin exposure [17]. At the immune level, SCFAs regulate dendritic cell maturation, promote regulatory T cell (Treg) differentiation, and suppress pro-inflammatory Th17 responses through histone deacetylase inhibition and activation of G-protein–coupled receptors such as GPR41 and GPR43 ]46–49]. These pathways provide a mechanistic explanation for the improved inflammatory and metabolic profiles reported in fasting individuals. In contrast, (Fig. 1) clearly illustrates a marked reduction in several pathobiont species in the fasting group, including Prevotella stercorea, Alistipes putredinis, Collinsella aerofaciens, and Enterobacter cloacae. The relative abundance of P. stercorea was dramatically lower in fasting individuals, suggesting suppression of microbial signatures previously associated with mucosal inflammation and dysbiosis. Overrepresentation of Prevotella species has been linked to enhanced Th17 polarization, increased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-17, and heightened susceptibility to inflammatory and autoimmune diseases. The reduction observed in our study therefore supports a fasting-induced shift toward immune tolerance. Similarly, the significant decrease in (A) putredinis and C. aerofaciens (Fig. 1) is noteworthy. Elevated levels of Alistipes species have been associated with inflammatory bowel disease and metabolic disorders, while Collinsella has been linked to increased gut permeability, altered bile acid metabolism, and pro-inflammatory lipid profiles. The suppression of these taxa in fasting individuals suggests a reduction in microbial drivers of chronic low-grade inflammation and metabolic dysregulation. Of particular relevance is the pronounced reduction in Enterobacter cloacae abundance observed in fasting participants (Fig. 1). As a facultative anaerobic Gram-negative bacterium, E. cloacae is a significant source of lipopolysaccharide (LPS), which activates Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) signaling and downstream inflammatory pathways, including NF-κB and STAT3 [50, 51]. Chronic activation of these pathways is implicated in insulin resistance, cardiovascular disease, and neuroinflammation [52]. The suppression of E. cloacae during Ramadan fasting therefore provides a plausible microbial mechanism for the reduction of systemic inflammatory tone reported in fasting populations. Beyond gastrointestinal and metabolic outcomes, chronic inflammation and impaired gut barrier function have been broadly linked to a range of adverse health processes in the wider literature [53]. We emphasize, however, that our cross-sectional study in healthy individuals did not assess immune cell populations, apoptosis, tumor markers, or any cancer-related endpoint, and no such claims can or should be drawn from the present data. Any extrapolation beyond gut and metabolic health remains outside the scope of this work. A major strength and novelty of this study lies in its targeted, species-level approach. While previous investigations of Ramadan fasting have reported changes at the phylum or genus level, few studies have examined specific bacterial species with well-defined immunological and metabolic functions. To our knowledge, this is the first study to simultaneously evaluate the effects of Ramadan fasting on (B) vulgatus, B. ovatus, O. splanchnicus, P. stercorea, A. putredinis, (C) aerofaciens, and E. cloacae using quantitative real-time PCR in a Middle Eastern population.

Our findings are consistent with the pilot study of Özkul et al. [54]، who reported a significant increase in Akkermansia muciniphila and the Bacteroides fragilis group following 29 days of Ramadan fasting, supporting our observation of enrichment in beneficial, barrier-supportive Bacteroides species. However, direct comparison with other key Ramadan-specific studies reveals notable discrepancies. Gul et al., in a Pakistani cohort, reported that dietary composition during fasting differentially shaped the gut microbiome and short-chain fatty acid profile, with taxa such as Alistipes showing enrichment rather than the reduction we observed. Similarly, Ali et al. [55]، comparing Pakistani and Chinese cohorts, found that Alistipes was significantly enriched after Ramadan fasting specifically in the Pakistani group, while our Iranian cohort showed the opposite direction of change, underscoring that even the sign of change in this genus is population-dependent. These discrepancies likely reflect differences in baseline dietary composition, as fiber- and legume-rich Iranian Suhoor and Iftar meals may favor a distinct microbial response compared with the diets studied in Turkish, Pakistani, or Chinese cohorts. Population-specific factors, including baseline gut enterotype, ethnicity, and regional food culture, have been repeatedly proposed as major drivers of inter-study variability in Ramadan fasting microbiome research. Methodological differences also likely contribute: the studies of Özkul et al. and Gul et al. relied on 16 S rRNA sequencing or genus-level qPCR to profile broad community-level shifts, whereas our targeted, culture-based qRT-PCR approach enabled species-level quantification of specific taxa but precludes direct comparison of alpha/beta diversity indices or full community composition reported in these prior works. Taken together, these comparisons underscore that the direction and magnitude of fasting-induced microbial shifts are highly context-dependent, shaped by diet, population background, and analytical methodology, rather than reflecting a uniform, generalizable response to Ramadan fasting.

The clear, directionally consistent changes observed in (Fig. 1) underscore the biological relevance of focusing on functionally important bacterial species rather than broader taxonomic categories. In conclusion, our results demonstrate that Ramadan fasting induces a distinct and favorable microbial signature characterized by enrichment of beneficial SCFA-producing bacteria and suppression of inflammation-associated pathobionts. The quantitative differences observed by qRT-PCR (Fig. 1) provide mechanistic insight into how fasting may modulate gastrointestinal health and immune regulation, reinforcing the gut microbiota as a potential mediator of the systemic benefits of fasting.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the present study demonstrates that Ramadan fasting induces a profound and biologically meaningful modulation of the gut microbiota at the species level in a healthy Iranian population. Using a targeted culture-based and quantitative real-time PCR approach, we show that fasting is associated with a coordinated enrichment of beneficial anaerobic bacteria, including Bacteroides vulgatus, Bacteroides ovatus, and Odoribacter splanchnicus, alongside a marked suppression of pathobiont and opportunistic taxa such as Prevotella stercorea, Alistipes putredinis, Collinsella aerofaciens, and Enterobacter cloacae. These microbial shifts reflect a transition toward a gut ecosystem characterized by enhanced metabolic efficiency, improved immune regulation, and reduced inflammatory potential. The observed increase in short-chain fatty acid–producing bacteria suggests that Ramadan fasting promotes gut barrier integrity, supports epithelial energy metabolism, and fosters immune tolerance through SCFA-mediated signaling pathways. Concurrently, the reduction of inflammation-associated and endotoxin-producing bacteria indicates a lowering of pro-inflammatory microbial burden, which may have systemic implications for metabolic health and immune homeostasis. Importantly, these changes were consistently supported by quantitative real-time PCR data, underscoring the robustness and biological relevance of the findings. Collectively, our results provide novel species-level insights into the mechanisms by which Ramadan fasting shapes gut microbial composition and function. By focusing on bacterial taxa that have been largely overlooked in previous fasting studies, this work advances current understanding of the gut–diet–immune axis and highlights fasting as a simple, culturally embedded strategy with potential relevance to gastrointestinal and inflammation-related health outcomes, pending confirmation in studies with functional and clinical endpoints. Future longitudinal and functional studies are warranted to elucidate the long-term clinical consequences of these microbial adaptations and their potential translational applications in disease prevention and management.

Limitations and future directions

Despite the strengths of the present study, including its species-level resolution, targeted quantitative approach, and focus on underexplored gut bacterial taxa, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design and single time-point sampling preclude definitive conclusions regarding temporal dynamics and causality. Although clear differences in microbial abundance were observed between fasting and non-fasting individuals, longitudinal sampling before, during, and after Ramadan would provide deeper insight into the stability, reversibility, and long-term persistence of fasting-induced microbial changes. Second, the study focused on a predefined and targeted panel of bacterial species with established relevance to gut health and inflammation. While this strategy allowed high-resolution and mechanistic interpretation at the species level, it does not capture the full complexity of the gut microbial ecosystem. The integration of untargeted metagenomic or metatranscriptomic approaches in future studies would enable comprehensive characterization of microbial diversity, functional pathways, and microbial–microbial interactions influenced by fasting. Another important limitation is the absence of functional host-level readouts. Although the observed microbial shifts strongly suggest enhanced short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production, improved gut barrier integrity, and attenuation of inflammatory signaling, direct measurement of microbial metabolites (e.g., SCFAs and bile acids), inflammatory biomarkers, immune cell profiles, and intestinal permeability parameters would substantially strengthen causal inference. Likewise, assessment of host gene expression related to immune regulation, oxidative stress, and cancer-associated pathways would further clarify the biological consequences of fasting-induced microbial remodeling. Future research should therefore adopt longitudinal and multi-omics approaches to comprehensively investigate how Ramadan fasting modulates the gut microbiota–host axis over time. Studies integrating microbiome profiling with metabolomics, immune phenotyping, and clinically relevant endpoints will be particularly valuable for elucidating the mechanistic role of specific bacterial species in mediating the health-promoting effects of fasting. Furthermore, extending this research to populations with metabolic disorders, chronic inflammatory diseases, or cancer may reveal whether fasting-induced microbial shifts confer therapeutic benefits or enhance responsiveness to conventional treatments. In summary, while the present study provides novel and mechanistic insights into species-specific modulation of the gut microbiota during Ramadan fasting, addressing these limitations in future investigations will be essential to fully realize the translational potential of fasting as a microbiota-targeted strategy for health promotion and disease prevention.
