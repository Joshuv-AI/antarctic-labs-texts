Development of serum-free cryomedia for slow-freezing of chicken primordial germ cells
Authors: Asma KHATUN, Saori HARADA, Satoru OHIRA, Haruto TAKEUCHI, Haruto SHIRAHASE, Natsumi TAKAHASHI, Wataru FUJII, Sota TAKAMIYA, Kazuhiro RIKIMARU, Masaki KATO, Yoshiaki NAKAMURA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The preservation of chicken genetic resources is important to both backup commercial breeds that support egg and meat production worldwide and conserve the genetic diversity of indigenous breeds, which is essential for managing breeding according to future demands. Because chicken embryos are attached to a large yolk, cryopreservation is technically impossible. In chickens, a unique developmental engineering approach based on the manipulation of primordial germ cells (PGCs), the embryonic precursor cells of gametes, has been developed. Cryobanking of PGCs is an innovative strategy for efficiently maintaining chicken genetic resources without breeding live birds. Cryomedia containing dimethyl sulfoxide or propylene glycol (PG) as permeable cryoprotectants, combined with serum as a non-permeable cryoprotectant, have been widely used for the cryopreservation of chicken PGCs. However, because the serum composition varies greatly, performance differences arise between lots. Consequently, there is a demand for serum-free cryomedia with specific known compositions. Here, we investigated the cryoprotective effects of ovalbumin and sericin, as alternatives to serum, on chicken PGCs. Two cryomedia were designed: PO comprising 7.5% PG and 5% ovalbumin and PS comprising 7.5% PG and 2% sericin. Following the culture of PGCs cryopreserved in these cryomedia, viability and cell doubling times recovered to levels comparable to those of an unfrozen control group at 6 and 2 d post-culture, respectively. When frozen-thawed PGCs were transplanted, their gonadal migration ability was significantly lower immediately after thawing, but recovered to levels comparable to those of the unfrozen control group after 4 d of culture. We successfully revived viable offspring from Hinai-dori, designated as a natural monument of Japan, from PGCs cryopreserved in these cryomedia. In conclusion, we developed two serum-free cryomedia that achieved > 60% recovery of viable PGCs after thawing while maintaining germline competency.

Chickens are raised worldwide for the production of eggs and meat (high-quality sources of protein). Despite the existence of 250–300 chicken breeds worldwide, the poultry industry relies heavily on only a few commercial breeds that have been selectively bred and improved over a long period. To develop a poultry industry that can adapt to future changes in the global environment and consumer needs, crossbreeding with indigenous breeds that possess high genetic diversity may be beneficial. Here, the conservation of chicken genetic resources is important from two perspectives: 1) serving as a backup for commercial breeds developed through long-term selective breeding and 2) preserving the genetic diversity of indigenous breeds, which is indispensable for future breed improvements. Notably, the number of indigenous chicken breeds is steadily declining, raising concerns about the loss of genetic diversity. In recent years, highly pathogenic avian influenza has caused major outbreaks worldwide, resulting in various impacts on the poultry industry, including economic losses and egg supply disruptions, and small-scale flocks of indigenous chicken breeds face risk of extinction. To address these risks, safe long-term conservation of chicken genetic resources is imperative.

Cryopreservation of germ cells, the only cell type capable of transmitting genetic information to the next generation, is one of the most effective approaches for the safe and semi-permanent preservation of animal genetic resources. In mammals, cryopreservation of semen and embryos is used for the conservation of genetic resources in major livestock species and laboratory animals, such as mice and rats. These techniques have also been applied in human fertility treatment. Although the cryopreservation of chicken semen is technically feasible [1, 2], its use in genetic resource conservation has progressed primarily for commercial breeds. However, because chickens have a Z/W sex chromosome system in which males are homozygous (ZZ) and females are heterozygous (ZW), semen cryopreservation cannot preserve genetic information from the female-derived W chromosome and mitochondrial DNA. Furthermore, chicken embryos are attached to a large yolk, making cryopreservation technically impossible. Primordial germ cells (PGCs) are the earliest undifferentiated germ cells that appear during early development. In birds, PGCs arise in the extraembryonic region and transiently circulate in the bloodstream while migrating to the genital ridge [3, 4]. Taking advantage of this unique characteristic, a technique was developed in chickens to isolate PGCs and transplant them into the bloodstream of recipient embryos, inducing gamete production in the recipient gonads [5, 6]. Cryopreserved avian PGCs have been shown to differentiate into functional gametes after transplantation [7,8,9]. Partially inbred lines and rare chicken breeds have been restored by mating recipient chickens transplanted with cryopreserved PGCs [10,11,12]. Therefore, cryopreservation of PGCs is considered the most effective method for semi-permanent conservation of genetic resources in chickens [13].

Cryobanking requires that preserved PGCs maintain their cellular function and are efficiently recovered with a high survival rate after thawing. Cryomedia containing cryoprotectants (CPAs) are used to protect cells from freezing damage. Combining permeable and non-permeable CPAs can enhance cryoprotective effects. Cryomedium containing dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) as a permeable CPA, in conjunction with serum as a non-permeable CPA, has been widely used for the cryopreservation of chicken PGCs [7,8,9,10,11,12, 14,15,16]. Recently, we demonstrated that propylene glycol (PG) has cryoprotective effects on chicken PGCs comparable to those of DMSO, and combining it with serum at an appropriate concentration enhances these effects [12]. However, variation in serum composition among batches can affect the post-thaw recovery and survival rates of chicken PGCs. Furthermore, the use of serum carries a risk of contamination by serum-derived viruses, bacteria, and other pathogens. Unfortunately, the cryoprotective potential of chemically defined biopolymers as serum substitutes for chicken PGC cryopreservation has not been sufficiently investigated. Serum albumin, a major protein in serum, has been reported to exert cryoprotective effects in various cell types, including germ cells [17,18,19,20,21,22,23]. In contrast, the cryoprotective effects of ovalbumin, the major protein in egg white, remain largely unknown. Ovalbumin is commonly added to the serum-free culture medium of chicken PGCs as a serum substitute. Therefore, if ovalbumin can provide cryoprotection for chicken PGCs, it would be an optimal non-permeable CPA. On the other hand, sericin, a major protein in silk, is known to act as a non-permeable CPA [24, 25]. Although sericin is relatively inexpensive and readily available, its cryoprotective effects on chicken PGCs remain unknown. In this study, we investigated the cryoprotective effects of the biopolymers ovalbumin and sericin in the cryopreservation of chicken PGCs, compared with the traditional serum-based strategy. Based on the results, we designed two serum-free cryomedia that achieved >60% recovery rates of viable PGCs while maintaining germline competency after freeze-thawing.

Materials and Methods

Fertilized eggs, chickens, and animal care

White Leghorn (WL) fertilized eggs were purchased from KUI Poultry, Mihara, Japan. Fertilized Hinai-dori eggs were obtained from the Akita Prefectural Livestock Experiment Station, Daisen, Japan. Barred Plymouth Rock (BPR), Ingie, and CAG-eGFP transgenic chickens with ubiquitous eGFP expression were raised on a farm at Hiroshima University, Higashi-Hiroshima, Japan. All animal experiments were approved by the Hiroshima University Animal Research Committee (C22-39-4), and conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines.

Culture media

For the primary culture of chicken PGCs and subculturing of established PGC lines, we used the serum-free culture medium described by Whyte et al. [26], with slight modifications. Briefly, the serum-free culture medium contained avian KnockOut Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM: 250 mOsm/kg, 12.0 mM glucose, and calcium chloride free [26]), B-27 supplement, 2.0 mM GlutaMax, 1× nucleosides, 1× non-essential amino acids, 0.1 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 1.2 mM sodium pyruvate, 0.2% (w/v) ovalbumin (A5503; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), 100 μg/ml sodium heparin (Sigma-Aldrich), 10 μg/ml ovotransferrin (Sigma-Aldrich), 2% (v/v) KnockOut Serum Replacement, 5 ng/ml human fibroblast growth factor 2 (R&D Biosystems, Minneapolis, MN, USA), and 25 ng/ml human activin A (PeproTech, Rocky Hill, NJ, USA). Unless otherwise specified, all the reagents were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA).

Cell lines and culture conditions

A PGC line (WL12) derived from a male WL embryo was used to investigate the cryoprotective effects of ovalbumin and sericin. A transgenic PGC line derived from a male CAG-eGFP chicken embryo was used to analyze cell proliferation and gonadal migration. The PGC lines of one Hinai-dori male (H12) and two females (H32 and H34) were used in germline transmission assays. The established PGC lines were cultured as previously described [26]. The PGCs were maintained at 37°C in an atmosphere containing 5% CO2 in air.

Freezing and thawing

Basal medium was used for cryopreservation, as previously described [12]. Briefly, DMEM (330 mOsm/kg, calcium-chloride-free; 21068028, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was diluted with an amount of distilled water equivalent to 24% of its volume to adjust the osmolarity to 250 mOsm/kg, matching that of Avian KnockOut DMEM.

A basal media containing 7.5% (v/v) PG and either 5% (v/v) chicken serum (16110082; Thermo Fisher Scientific), 0–10% (w/v) ovalbumin (A5503; Sigma-Aldrich), or 0–3% (w/v) sericin (167-22681; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan) were used as cryomedia. Approximately 1 × 104 PGCs were suspended in 100 μl cryomedia for the cell viability assay, while 1–1.4 × 105 PGCs were suspended in 500 μl cryomedia for the cell proliferation, gonadal migration, and germline transmission assays. After transferring the cell suspensions to each cryotube, they were cooled at approximately −1°C/min using a commercial freezing container (Nalgene, Rochester, NY, USA) in a deep freezer at −80°C overnight. Thereafter, cryotubes were transferred to liquid nitrogen (LN2: −196°C) and stored for at least 1 month before use in the experiments.

A basal medium containing 0.1% (w/v) ovalbumin was used as the thawing medium. Cryotubes were placed in a water bath at 37°C until the icy masses disappeared, and the cell suspension was diluted with 900 μl thawing medium.

Cell viability counting

To investigate the cryoprotective effects of ovalbumin and sericin, approximately 1 × 104 PGCs (WL12) were cryopreserved in 100 μl of each CPA solution, including 7.5% (v/v) PG and either 5% (v/v) chicken serum, 0–10% (w/v) ovalbumin, or 0–3% (w/v) sericin. After cryopreservation of the PGCs for 1 month, they were thawed, and the recovery rate and viability were analyzed as previously described [12]. After centrifugation at 300 × g for 4 min, 950 μl supernatant was carefully removed, and the cell suspension (about 50 μl) was diluted with 450 μl basal medium. To calculate the recovery rate and viability of frozen-thawed PGCs, 80 μl of the total volume of cell suspension (about 500 μl) was used for a Trypan blue exclusion test. Briefly, 80 μl cell suspension was mixed with an equal amount of 0.4% Trypan blue solution. After incubation of the mixture for 2 min at 25°C, the PGCs were observed under a phase-contrast microscope (IX73; OLYMPUS, Tokyo, Japan), and the number of blue-stained dead cells and unstained live cells was counted. Since 1 × 104 PGCs were cryopreserved per cryotube, the number of PGCs contained in 80 μl cell suspension was calculated at 1,600. The recovery rate was calculated as a percentage of the recovered cells relative to the expected 1,600 cells in the analyzed aliquot. Viability was expressed as the percentage of live cells relative to the total number of recovered cells. The recovery rate of viable PGCs was expressed as a percentage of the recovered live cells relative to the 1,600 cells.

Cell proliferation assay

The male PGC line (CAG-eGFP) derived from a transgenic embryo ubiquitously expressing eGFP was used for the cell proliferation assay. PGCs in the logarithmic growth phase were harvested; some were immediately passaged as controls, while the remainder were suspended in either optimized cryomedia, which included 7.5% (v/v) PG and 5% (w/v) ovalbumin (PO) or 7.5% PG and 2% (w/v) sericin (PS) then cryopreserved in aliquots (500 μl) of 1–1.4 × 105 PGCs per cryotube at −196°C for > 1 month. Approximately 5 × 104 unfrozen or frozen-thawed PGCs expressing eGFP were seeded and cultured for 10 days. Cultured PGCs were harvested every 2 day to count the number of cells and passaged at a density of < 8×104 eGFP+ PGCs per well. The densities of eGFP+ and eGFP- PGCs were measured using a hemocytometer under the fluorescence and phase-contrast microscope (IX73; OLYMPUS) and photographed using a CMOS camera (DP74; OLYMPUS). Based on our preliminary observation of green fluorescence exclusively in living PGCs, cell viability was expressed as the percentage of eGFP+ PGCs relative to the total PGC count. The following formula was used to calculate the cell doubling time based on the growth between the two points:

Doubling time (Td) = (t2 − t1) × ln(2) / ln(N2 / N1)

where:

∙ N1 = initial number of eGFP+ PGCs

∙ N2 = final number of eGFP+ PGCs

∙ (t2 − t1) = elapsed time (h)

Gonadal migration assay

The transgenic PGC line (CAG-eGFP) ubiquitously expressing eGFP was used to track the gonadal migration of PGCs after transplantation. PGCs in the logarithmic growth phase in vitro were collected; some were immediately used for transplantation (unfrozen control group), and the remaining PGCs (1 × 105 cells/vial) were cryopreserved in either 500 μl PO or PS cryomedia at −196°C for > 1 month. After thawing, some PGCs were immediately used for transplantation (PO0d or PS0d groups), and the remainder were seeded at 5 × 104 eGFP+ PGCs per well and cultured for 4 d (PO4d or PS4d groups). Approximately 500 eGFP+ PGCs in each group were intravascularly injected into the dorsal aorta of BPR embryos at stages 15–16 (according to the developmental staging system of Hamburger and Hamilton [27]) through a ~20 mm window created at the narrow end of the eggshell under a stereomicroscope (M125C; Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). The window in the eggshell was sealed with cling film, and the manipulated embryos were incubated for 6 days. Whole gonads attached to the adjacent mesonephros were collected. The eGFP+ cells observed in both the left and right gonads were counted using a fluorescence stereomicroscope (M205FCA; Leica Microsystems) and photographed using a CCD camera (DFC7000T; Leica Microsystems).

Germline transmission assay

Hinai-dori, designated a Japanese natural monument in 1942, was used as a model of rare chicken breeds. The PGC lines of one male (H12) and two females (H32 and H34) were established from Hinai-dori embryos and cryopreserved in 500 μl PO or PS cryomedia (1 × 105 cells/vial). PGCs were thawed after storage for over 1 year at –196°C and cultured for 4 d. Approximately 1 × 103 PGCs (1:1 mixture of male and female cells) were microinjected into the bloodstream of WL embryos at stages 15–16 and incubated until hatching. The hatched recipient WL chicks were raised to sexual maturity, and a progeny test was conducted. Freshly collected semen from each male WL recipient was artificially inseminated into normal female BPR and Ingie chickens, and the feather color and comb shape of their offspring were examined. Each female WL recipient was also mated with a normal male BPR chicken by artificial insemination, and the feather color and comb shape of their progeny were examined. Donor PGC lines were obtained from Hinai-dori, a breed with black-breasted red (wild-type) plumage in males and wheaten plumage in females, featuring a pea-shaped comb. Recipient embryos were obtained from WL, a breed with white plumage and a single comb (wild type). Hinai-dori carries homozygous recessive alleles for extended black (any combination of dominant wheaten (ewh), wild-type (e+), and buttercup (ebc)) [28], homozygous recessive alleles (i/i) for inhibitor of black pigment, and homozygous dominant alleles (P/P) for pea comb. In contrast, WL carries homozygous dominant alleles (E/E) for extended black [28], homozygous dominant alleles (I/I) for inhibitor of black pigment, and homozygous recessive alleles (p/p) for pea comb. Therefore, when the recipient chickens were mated with normal BPR (genotype: E/E, i/i, p/p [28]), black chicks with pea combs (genotype: E/ewh, e+, or ebc, i/i, P/p) were derived from the donor PGCs, whereas white chicks with small black spots and single combs (genotype: E/E, I/i, p/p) were derived from the recipient PGCs. In contrast, when the recipient roosters were mated with normal Ingie hens (genotype: ewh/ewh, i/i, p/p [28]), brown chicks with pea combs (genotype: ewh /ewh, e+, or ebc, i/i, P/p) were derived from donor PGCs, and white chicks with small black spots and single combs (genotype: E/ewh, I/i, p/p) were derived from recipient PGCs.

Artificial insemination was conducted with two pairs of male and female WL recipients to produce Hinai-dori offspring from frozen–thawed male and female PGCs. The fertilized eggs were incubated until hatching, and the feather color and comb shape of their offspring were examined.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). One-way analysis of variance, followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test, was used to determine differences between group means, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.

Ovalbumin enhanced post-thaw recovery and viability of chicken PGCs

We investigated the cryoprotective effects of ovalbumin, the primary protein in egg whites, in cryopreservation solution containing PG, a permeable CPA, and its potential as a substitute for chicken serum. Chicken PGCs were cryopreserved using cryopreservation solutions containing 7.5% (v/v) PG and different concentrations of ovalbumin (0%, 1%, 5%, 10% (w/v)) or chicken serum (5% (v/v)), and the recovery rate of total and viable PGCs, as well as viability rate, was analyzed after thawing. Post-thawed PGCs maintained normal proliferative capacity

Based on the cryoprotective effects of ovalbumin and sericin, we set the following constituent concentrations for the final cocktails: PO cryomedium: 7.5% (v/v) PG and 5% (w/v) ovalbumin; PS cryomedium: 7.5% (v/v) PG and 2% (w/v) sericin. We evaluated the in vitro proliferation capacity of chicken PGCs after cryopreservation in PO or PS cryomedia. Intracellular GFP is a useful marker to assess cell viability both in vitro and in vivo following freezing injury, with the loss of GFP fluorescence following treatment indicating cell death [29, 30]. Thus, we used the PGC line established from CAG-eGFP transgenic chicken embryos that ubiquitously express eGFP to monitor cell viability during the culture period, using eGFP expression as an indicator. Approximately 5 × 104 living PGCs expressing eGFP, before or after freeze-thawing, were seeded onto incubator plates and cultured for 10 days. In the unfrozen control group, the viability rate of PGCs remained > 93% throughout the 10-d culture period (Fig. 3C). Production of functional gametes from frozen-thawed PGCs

We examined whether PGCs cryopreserved in PO or PS cryomedia maintained their germline competency. One male (H12) and two female (H32 and H34) PGC lines obtained from the rare native Japanese chicken breed Hinai-dori were used for germline transmission assay. The H12 and H32 PGC lines were frozen in PO cryomedium, while the H12 and H34 PGC lines were frozen in PS cryomedium and stored for > 1 year at –196°C. Post-thaw PGCs were cultured for 4 days to allow recovery from the damage caused by freezing injury. Approximately 1 × 103 PGCs, comprising of a 1:1 mixture of male and female cells, were transplanted intravascularly into WL embryos at stages 15–16 and incubated until hatching. Five WL recipients (two males and three females) in the PO group and six WL recipients (two males and three females) in the PS group survived to sexual maturity. For progeny testing, male recipients were mated with normal BPR or Ingie females, whereas female recipients were mated with normal BPR males. Discussion

This study developed two serum-free cryomedia for the efficient cryopreservation of chicken PGCs while maintaining germline competency. This work advances the reliable long-term conservation of commercial and indigenous poultry genetic resources.

In cell cryopreservation, combining permeable with non-permeable CPAs improves post-thaw survival rates. Because permeable CPAs are generally small nonionic molecules (e.g., glycerol, DMSO, ethylene glycol, and PG), they can pass through the membrane and penetrate the cell, where they displace intracellular water and inhibit the formation of ice crystals. In contrast, non-permeable CPAs, including sugars with relatively low molecular weights (e.g., sucrose, trehalose, and raffinose), biological macromolecules such as serum proteins, and polymeric compounds such as polyvinylpyrrolidone, not only interact directly with extracellular water but also promote cell dehydration, thereby inhibiting ice crystal formation both inside and outside the cells. In the cryopreservation of chicken PGCs, it is common to use cryomedium containing DMSO as a permeable CPA in conjunction with either fetal bovine or chicken serum as non-permeable CPA [7,8,9,10,11,12, 14,15,16]. Serum proteins act as a buffer during the freeze-thaw process and alleviate osmotic stress, protect cell membranes, and inhibit ice crystal formation and recrystallization [33]. We previously established that adding chicken serum in combination with DMSO or PG improves both the recovery and survival rates of PGCs after freeze-thaw cycles [12]. However, serum batches show qualitative variations that could affect the recovery and survival rates of post-thaw PGCs. Furthermore, to avoid the risk of contamination with viruses, bacteria, and other serum contaminants, the development of alternative serum replacement components is critical. To eliminate batch-to-batch variation in chicken PGC cryopreservation, we focused on the cryoprotective effect of ovalbumin and sericin, two biological macromolecules, in chicken PGCs and determined the optimal concentrations based on recovery and survival rates following freeze-thaw treatment.

Serum primarily comprises organic molecules such as serum proteins, glucose, and electrolytes. Serum albumin accounts for 50–65% of serum proteins and has been used as a serum substitute in the cryopreservation of various cell types, including germ cells [17,18,19,20,21,22,23]. Serum albumin regulates osmotic pressure during cryopreservation and inhibits intracellular ice crystal formation by promoting the exchange of intracellular water with permeable CPA. It also protects cell membranes by scavenging free radicals generated by oxidative stress [34]. In addition to the blood, albumin is primarily found in egg whites, milk, and various animal and plant tissues. Ovalbumin, a monomeric phosphoglycoprotein with a molecular weight of ~45 kDa, accounts for 54% of egg-white proteins. However, the cryoprotective effects of ovalbumin during cell cryopreservation have not been fully elucidated. The present study demonstrated that adding ovalbumin to PG (7.5% (v/v)), the permeable CPA, improved both the recovery and survival rates of chicken PGCs after freeze-thawing (Fig. 1A and B, P < 0.05). Furthermore, the cryopreservation solution containing 5% (w/v) ovalbumin (PO) yielded the highest recovery rate of viable PGCs, and both the recovery and survival rates were comparable to those of the cryopreservation solution containing 5% (v/v) chicken serum (Figs. 1A and B). In contrast, when adding 10% (w/v) ovalbumin to 7.5% PG cryopreservation solution, post-thaw PGC recovery and viability rates were lower than with 5% (w/v) ovalbumin (Figs. 1A and B, P < 0.05). This may have been caused by an increase in cryomedium osmolarity associated with the addition of high-concentration ovalbumin, leading to excessive cell shrinkage [35]. Therefore, the addition of ovalbumin at an appropriate concentration may provide similar cryoprotection as chicken serum in the cryopreservation of chicken PGCs. Because ovalbumin is a common medium component of serum-free culture systems for chicken PGCs [26], it is also suitable as a CPA in the cryomedium for chicken PGC cryopreservation.

Sericin is a major protein in the silk produced by silkworms. Sericin inhibited cell death and promotes cell proliferation in insect cell cultures [36], and improved embryo development rates in bovine embryo cultures [37]. Sericin has also been used as a non-permeable CPA: it acts as a reducing agent during the cryopreservation of human sperm and bovine embryos, improving post-thaw survival rates by reducing oxidative stress [24, 25]. The present study demonstrated that adding sericin to a 7.5% PG cryopreservation solution improved both the post-thaw recovery and viability rates of chicken PGCs (Figs. 2A and B, P < 0.05). Adding 2% sericin to 7.5% PG cryopreservation solution maximized the recovery rate of live PGCs, and the survival rate was higher than those observed with cryopreservation solutions containing 7.5% PG and 5% chicken serum (Figs. 2A and B, P < 0.05). These findings suggest that sericin at an appropriate concentration can exert a cryoprotective effect that exceeds that of chicken serum in the cryopreservation of chicken PGCs.

To evaluate whether chicken PGCs cryopreserved in PO or PS cryomedia maintained normal function after thawing, we quantitatively analyzed their proliferative capacity in culture and migration ability toward the gonads following transplantation. Both cell culture and transplantation assays were performed using the PGC line established from the CAG-eGFP transgenic chicken embryo, which ubiquitously expresses eGFP and allows the monitoring of viable cells and tracking of cell fate using green fluorescence. Transplantation assays revealed that less than half of the eGFP+ cells settled in the gonads when transplanting PGCs immediately after freeze-thawing than with the same number of unfrozen live PGCs (Figs. 4A and B). The in vitro cell proliferation assay revealed that the number of viable cells increased exponentially when using unfrozen PGCs, whereas it decreased to less than half the original count on day 2 of culture when using frozen-thawed PGCs (Figs. 3A and B). Since early apoptotic cells were detected by annexin V staining of post-thawed chicken PGCs [38], some PGCs surviving freeze-thaw might have sustained irreversible damage due to cryoinjury and may die rapidly due to apoptosis after culture or transplantation.

The in vitro cell proliferation assay revealed that the viability rates and cell doubling times of frozen-thawed PGCs reached levels comparable to those of unfrozen PGCs on day 6 of culture and thereafter and on day 2 and thereafter, respectively (Figs. 3C and D). Therefore, the proliferative capacity of frozen-thawed PGCs is expected to recover to pre-freezing levels by day 4 of culture, regardless of whether they are cryopreserved in PO or PS. The transplantation assay demonstrated that PGCs cultured for 4 days after freeze-thaw regained gonadal migration capacity comparable to that observed before freezing (Figs. 4A and B). These findings suggest that chicken PGCs cryopreserved in PO or PS recover from cryogenic damage after at least 4 days of culture and exhibit normal proliferative and gonadal migration capacities. The migration capacity of PGCs cryopreserved in PO or PS toward the gonads immediately after thawing (Fig. 4) was comparable to that of cells cryopreserved in serum-containing cryomedia [12, 39]. Therefore, ovalbumin and sericin may be useful serum-replacing CPAs both in terms of cell viability and function, specifically in maintaining gonadal migration capacity. Note, the transient decline in chicken PGC survival rate after cryopreservation in PO and PS cryomedia (Fig. 3C) also occurs when using serum-containing cryomedium [40]. Further study is needed to determine whether the culture duration required for the recovery of proliferative activity in frozen-thawed PGCs differs when using serum-free or serum-containing cryomedia.

To further evaluate the germline competency of PGCs cryopreserved with PO or PS cryomedia, progeny tests were performed on recipient chickens. Offspring derived from post-thawed PGCs of Hinai-dori were obtained from both male and female recipient chickens with variable efficiencies (16.1–88.2%), regardless of whether PO or PS was used (Fig. 5C). Since chicken PGCs transplanted into recipient embryos of the opposite sex rarely differentiate into functional gametes [31, 32], our findings suggest that both male (H12) and female (H32 and H34) PGC lines differentiated into functional gametes. All male recipients were confirmed to be germline chimeras, while some female recipients did not produce sired offspring derived from frozen-thawed PGCs (Fig. 5C). Germline transmission rates may vary significantly among different PGC lines [12, 26, 41]. Further improvements in chicken PGC culture systems are required to maintain germline competency. Future experiments should use either chemically or genetically sterilized recipient embryos to increase germline transmission rates following transplantation [42,43,44,45]. We successfully revived progeny derived from frozen-thawed PGCs of Hinai-dori by mating male and female recipients, achieving efficiencies of 42.9% (3/7 chicks) in the PO group and 28.6% (6/21 chicks) in the PS group (Figs. 5D and E). Therefore, both PO and PS cryomedia can maintain the germline competency of chicken PGC lines with high recovery and viability even after long-term storage in LN2 (at least 1 year). The success of the demonstration trial with Hinai-dori, a rare breed of native Japanese chickens, indicated that these cryomedia could be extended to various chicken breeds for cryobanking PGC lines.

In conclusion, we demonstrated that the addition of ovalbumin and sericin in combination with the permeable CPA PG enhanced the post-thaw recovery and viability of chicken PGC lines, making them suitable for use as serum substitutes. Based on these findings, we designed PO, comprising 7.5% (v/v) PG and 5% (w/v) ovalbumin, and PS, comprising 7.5% (v/v) PG and 2% (w/v) sericin cryomedia, which achieved >60% recovery of viable PGCs after freeze-thawing while maintaining their proliferative capacity in vitro and post-transplantation germline competency. To demonstrate the practicality of PO and PS as cryomedia, we used Hinai-dori as a representative rare chicken breed and successfully restored offspring from male and female PGCs that had been cryopreserved for >1 year. The proposed serum-free cryomedia offer a highly reproducible and inexpensive approach to the long-term storage of chicken PGC lines. Their prospective application in PGC-based cryobanking can advance the conservation of chicken genetic diversity and promote sustainability and adaptability to future demands in the poultry industry.
