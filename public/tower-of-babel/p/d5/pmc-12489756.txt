Reduced HBsAg positivity in systemic lupus erythematosus and Sjögren's syndrome: A retrospective comparative study
Authors: Man Li, Qihang Zhu, Zhong Fang, Taihong Huang, Lei Dai, Sen Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSystemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and Sjögren's syndrome (SS) are two common systemic autoimmune diseases. Recent studies have suggested that the prevalence of hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) may be lower in patients with SLE compared to the general population; however, the existing evidence remains limited and controversial. A systematic analysis of HBV infection rates and serological marker distributions in patients with SLE and SS may offer important insights into the interaction between autoimmune and antiviral immunity.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 2421 hospitalized patients with SLE and 2049 with SS at Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital from January 2019 to December 2024. Patients with other autoimmune diseases (including rheumatoid arthritis (RA), ankylosing spondylitis (AS), ulcerative colitis (UC), and Crohn's disease (CD)) and 5927 non-autoimmune hospitalized patients were included as controls. Hepatitis B virus (HBV) serological markers (HBsAg, HBsAb, HBeAg, HBeAb, and HBcAb) and anti-HCV antibodies were tested. We compared the positivity rates of HBV seromarkers across different groups and performed subgroup analyses based on sex, age, and autoantibody profiles.ResultsThe HBsAg positivity rate was significantly lower in SLE and SS patients compared with non-autoimmune controls (1.2 % vs. 5.2 %, P < 0.0001; 1.7 % vs. 5.2 %, P < 0.0001, respectively). No significant differences were observed in patients with RA, AS, UC, or CD. Stratified analyses revealed consistently lower HBsAg positivity in SLE and SS patients across both sexes and all age groups above 20 years. Further analyses indicated that this phenomenon was not attributable to occult HBV infection, inpatient versus outpatient status, or immunosuppressant use. Notably, high ANA titers and the presence of antibodies such as anti-dsDNA, anti-SSA, and anti-ribosomal P were associated with lower HBsAg positivity. No significant differences in HBsAg positivity were found between patients with or without common complications.ConclusionSLE and SS patients exhibit consistently lower HBsAg positivity rates, independent of clinical factors, and linked to autoantibody status. These findings suggest disease-specific immune mechanisms that may promote HBV clearance or reduce susceptibility to infection.Keywords: Systemic lupus erythematosus, Sjögren's syndrome, Autoimmune disease, Chronic hepatitis B

Introduction

Autoimmune diseases are a group of complex disorders characterized by abnormal activation of the immune system, resulting in tissue and organ damage. Among them, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and Sjögren's syndrome (SS) are two representative systemic autoimmune diseases [1,2]. SLE is characterized by multi-organ involvement and the presence of various autoantibodies (such as antinuclear antibodies and anti-double-stranded DNA antibodies), with highly heterogeneous clinical manifestations that can affect the skin, joints, kidneys, and hematological system [3]. In contrast, SS primarily involves lymphocytic infiltration and functional impairment of exocrine glands, typically presenting with dry mouth and dry eyes, and may also involve systemic multi-organ damage [4]. Although the exact pathogenesis of these diseases remains unclear, the imbalance between innate and adaptive immunity, as well as the interaction between genetic susceptibility and environmental factors, are considered key contributors to their development.

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection is a global public health issue. According to the World Health Organization, approximately 254 million people worldwide are chronically infected with HBV [5]. Persistent positivity for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) after HBV infection is a key marker of chronic hepatitis progression, which may further develop into cirrhosis or hepatocellular carcinoma [6]. Notably, the interaction between HBV infection and the immune system is complex. The outcome of infection is not only influenced by the viral replication capacity but also closely related to the host's immune status. In recent years, increasing attention has been paid to the potential association between autoimmune diseases and HBV infection. Several studies have suggested that the HBsAg positivity rate in SLE patients may be lower than that in the general population [[7], [8], [9]]. It was speculated that this might be due to altered immune regulation in SLE patients, which suppresses HBV replication or enhances viral clearance. However, this phenomenon has not been consistently observed across all studies. Some researchers have proposed the opposite view, suggesting that long-term use of immunosuppressive therapy in SLE patients may theoretically increase the risk of HBV reactivation [10,11]. Others have hypothesized that HBV infection might reduce the risk of developing SLE through immune modulation [12], implying a potential "bidirectional protective effect." Compared to SLE, studies on the prevalence of HBV infection in SS are relatively limited.

Although some studies have examined the prevalence of HBV infection in autoimmune diseases, several limitations remain [[7], [8], [9], [10],13]. First, most studies had small sample sizes and focused on a single disease, lacking comparative analysis between SLE and SS [7,8]. Second, the assessment of HBV infection status often relied solely on HBsAg, without integrating other serological markers (such as anti-HBs and anti-HBc), potentially missing cases of past or occult infection. In addition, patients with autoimmune diseases often present with various autoantibodies and complications (such as nephritis or interstitial lung disease), yet it remains unclear whether these factors affect the risk of HBV infection. Therefore, a comprehensive analysis based on large-scale cohorts is urgently needed to clarify the epidemiological features and potential mechanisms of HBV infection in patients with SLE and SS.

This study retrospectively analyzes clinical data from a large cohort of patients with autoimmune diseases to systematically compare HBsAg positivity rates among patients with SLE, SS, the general population, and other autoimmune diseases (such as rheumatoid arthritis). It also comprehensively evaluates the distribution of HBV serological markers and explores the correlation between different autoantibodies, complications, and HBV infection rates. This study is expected to provide evidence-based support for optimizing HBV screening strategies and guiding immunosuppressive therapy in autoimmune disease patients, while offering new insights into the interplay between autoimmunity and antiviral immunity.

Materials and methods

Patients and controls

A total of 2421 patients with SLE, 2049 with SS, 2382 with rheumatoid arthritis (RA), 3739 with ankylosing spondylitis (AS), 380 with ulcerative colitis (UC), and 1440 with Crohn's disease (CD) who were hospitalized at Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital between January 1, 2019, and December 31, 2024 were included in this study. In addition, 5927 hospitalized patients without autoimmune diseases during the same period served as the control group. Detailed baseline characteristics of all patients and controls are summarized in Table 1. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital (Approval No. 2020-093).

Detection methods

All participants were tested for HBV and HCV-related serological markers upon hospital admission. The HBV “two pairs and a half” panel (HBsAg, HBsAb, HBeAg, HBeAb, and HBcAb) and anti-HCV were measured using CLIA. The assays were performed on the HISCL-5000 fully automated analyzer (Sysmex Corporation), with reagents provided by the same manufacturer. The cut-off value for HBsAg positivity was set at ≥0.05 IU/mL.

For SLE and SS patients, ANA were detected using IFA (EUROIMMUN). Other autoantibodies, including anti-dsDNA, anti-Sm, anti-SSA, anti-SSB, and anti-RNP, were tested using the EUROIMMUN immunoblotting method. All procedures followed the manufacturer's standard instructions, and fluorescence images were interpreted by certified laboratory technicians.

Data collection and cleaning process

This study utilized the electronic medical record system and laboratory diagnostic database of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital to collect clinical data of hospitalized patients between January 2019 and December 2024. For patients with SLE and SS, clinical information and comorbidity data were obtained from discharge diagnoses to ensure accuracy and reliability. Patients with RA, AS, UC, CD, as well as non-autoimmune hospitalized patients during the same period, were included as controls, with basic demographic information and virological test results collected. During data cleaning, cases with missing or incorrect diagnostic information were excluded, patients with incomplete HBV/HCV serological test results were removed, and for those with multiple hospitalizations, only the first admission record was retained to ensure data completeness and consistency.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using SPSS version 26 (IBM SPSS Statistics, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages, and differences between groups were assessed using the chi-square (χ2) test. A P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism version 8.3 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California USA).

Results

Distribution of HBV serological markers among different populations

We compared the positivity rates of five HBV-related serological markers (HBsAg, HBsAb, HBeAb, HBeAg, and HBcAb) among patients with six representative autoimmune diseases (SLE, SS, RA, AS, IBD, and CD) and individuals from a healthy control population. HBsAg, as the core indicator of chronic HBV infection, was significantly lower in the SLE (1.2 %) and SS (1.7 %) groups compared to the non-autoimmune disease group (5.2 %, P < 0.0001), with statistical significance. In contrast, the HBsAg positivity rates in RA, CD, AS, and UC patients did not differ significantly from those in the control group (Fig. 1A). Regarding other HBV serological markers, HBsAb, a protective antibody, showed significantly higher positivity rates in SLE, AS, and UC patients compared to controls (Fig. 1B). The positivity rate of HBeAg was significantly lower in RA patients than in the control group (Fig. 1C). For HBeAb and HBcAb, the positivity rates in SLE, CD, and AS patients were significantly lower than those in the control group (Fig. 1D and E). Additionally, we analyzed HCV antibody positivity rates across the autoimmune disease groups as a comparison. The results indicated statistically significant differences in anti-HCV positivity between several autoimmune disease groups and the control group (Fig. 1F).

Distribution of HBsAg positivity by sex and age stratification in SLE and SS patients

To further investigate the population characteristics underlying the decreased HBsAg positivity rates in SLE and SS patients, we performed stratified analyses based on sex and age, comparing the results with those of the healthy control group (Table 2). Regarding sex distribution, both male and female patients with SLE and SS showed significantly lower HBsAg positivity rates than the control group, with statistical significance. This suggests that the reduction in chronic HBV infection in SLE and SS patients is observed in both sexes, with the difference being particularly pronounced among females. In terms of age distribution, HBsAg positivity rates in SLE and SS patients were significantly lower than those in the control group across multiple age groups. Among SLE patients, the 20–30, 40–50, and >50-year age groups all demonstrated significantly lower HBsAg positivity compared to the control population. A similar trend was observed in SS patients, particularly within the 30–40, 40–50, and >50-year age brackets, where the differences were statistically significant. In summary, the decline in HBsAg positivity among SLE and SS patients is evident across both sexes and most age groups, suggesting that this phenomenon is broadly applicable and warrants further investigation.

Impact of HBV DNA load, patient source, and immunosuppressant use on HBsAg positivity

To further clarify the potential effects of HBV DNA load, patient source, and immunosuppressant use on the HBsAg positivity rate in SLE and SS patients, we conducted additional analyses. We evaluated the proportion of “occult HBV infection” (defined as HBsAg-negative but HBV DNA-positive). The results showed that the proportion of occult infection in both SLE and SS patients was approximately 1 %, with no significant difference compared with the control group (Fig. 2A). In addition, to assess the potential impact of patient type (inpatient vs. outpatient) on HBsAg positivity, we compared the positivity rates between contemporaneous inpatient and outpatient SLE and SS patients. No significant difference was observed between the two groups (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, to exclude the effect of immunosuppressant use on HBsAg positivity, patients were stratified according to whether they had used immunosuppressants within one month prior to admission. The analysis revealed no significant differences in HBsAg positivity (Fig. 2C) or HBsAb positivity (Fig. 2D) between the immunosuppressant and non-immunosuppressant groups in either SLE or SS patients.

Comparison of HBsAg positivity among different autoantibody subgroups in SLE and SS patients

To explore whether the reduced rate of chronic HBV infection is associated with specific autoantibody profiles, we compared HBsAg positivity between autoantibody-positive and -negative subgroups among SLE and SS patients (Table 3). The analysis showed that, in both SLE and SS patients, those positive for antinuclear antibodies (ANA) had significantly lower HBsAg positivity rates than ANA-negative individuals. This difference was the most pronounced across all subgroup analyses, suggesting a potential association between ANA positivity and reduced HBV infection. In SLE patients, the HBsAg positivity rates were significantly lower in those positive for anti-dsDNA, anti-Ribosomal P, anti-AMA-M2, and anti-SSA antibodies compared to their respective antibody-negative counterparts. In SS patients, those positive for anti-Ro-52 antibodies also exhibited a lower HBsAg positivity rate than negative individuals. Additionally, HBsAg positivity was significantly higher in anti-Ribosomal P -negative patients compared to anti-Ribosomal P-positive ones, with the difference being especially prominent in the SLE subgroup. For other autoantibodies, no significant differences in HBsAg positivity were observed between positive and negative individuals, indicating a weaker association with HBV infection status. In addition to the types of autoantibodies, we further analyzed the positivity of HBsAg across different titers of ANA and anti-dsDNA. The results showed that in SLE patients, the high ANA-titer group exhibited a decreasing trend in HBsAg positivity. In SS patients, the high ANA-titer group had a significantly lower HBsAg positivity rate compared with the low ANA-titer group (Fig. 3A). By contrast, no significant differences were observed in HBsAg positivity across groups with different anti-dsDNA titers (Fig. 3B).

Analysis of the impact of different complications on HBsAg positivity in SLE and SS patients

Building on the confirmed overall decline in HBsAg positivity among patients with SLE and SS, this study further investigated whether disease-related complications influence HBV infection rates. In SLE patients, we conducted a comparative analysis of HBsAg positivity across common complications, including lupus nephritis, neuropsychiatric lupus, anemia, and hyperlipidemia. For SS patients, interstitial pneumonia and anemia-the two major complications-were included in the evaluation. The results showed no significant differences in HBsAg positivity between patients with or without these complications in either SLE or SS groups, suggesting that changes in HBV infection status are not markedly affected by the presence of complications (Fig. 4). These findings suggest that the alteration of HBV infection status is not significantly influenced by the presence of complications. In conclusion, the decrease in HBsAg positivity observed in SLE and SS patients is not affected by major clinical complications, further supporting the hypothesis that this phenomenon may be related to the overall immune status or antibody profile, rather than the severity of the disease or specific organ involvement.

Discussion

This study focused on the distribution characteristics of HBV infection in patients with SLE and SS, particularly the changes in HBsAg positivity rate, using HCV infection as a control. The aim was to explore the potential interactions and mechanisms between viral infection and autoimmune diseases. Based on large-scale data, we found that the HBsAg positivity rate in SLE patients was significantly lower than that in healthy controls and patients with other autoimmune diseases, whereas HCV infection showed no significant differences across groups. This result is consistent with previous clinical observations and provides further support for the interaction between HBV and autoimmune diseases.

In clinical practice, HBV infection status is typically assessed using five serological markers. Among them, HBsAg is the most direct and important indicator of current HBV infection, with positivity representing chronic infection or a carrier state, while HBsAb positivity generally indicates past exposure with viral clearance or immunity acquired through vaccination [14]. In this study, we found that SLE patients had generally lower positivity rates for other HBV serological markers in addition to HBsAg, including HBeAg and HBcAb. This may suggest a lower prevalence of both chronic HBV carriers and prior HBV exposure among these patients. Meanwhile, the HBsAb positivity rate was significantly higher in SLE patients than in other groups, indicating a higher proportion of individuals with vaccine-induced or naturally acquired immunity in this population. Similarly, SS patients also showed a lower HBsAg positivity rate compared to healthy controls, with serological patterns resembling those of SLE, suggesting that both diseases may share common features in viral susceptibility and immune clearance mechanisms.

To eliminate potential confounding due to demographic structure, we conducted stratified analyses by sex and age. The results showed that the trend of lower HBsAg positivity remained significant across both sexes, suggesting that the observed low infection rate was not driven by sex differences. Our overall findings indicated that HBsAg positivity was consistently lower in SLE and SS patients than in controls across most age groups, with the difference being particularly pronounced in individuals over 30 years old. No significant differences were observed in individuals under 20 years of age, likely due to the very low HBV infection rate in this age group and the limited number of HBsAg-positive cases, resulting in non-significant differences. This phenomenon is closely related to the implementation of the universal HBV vaccination program for newborns in China since 1992, which has greatly reduced HBV infection rates in those under 20 years of age [15]. In individuals over 20 years of age, we observed a trend of increasing HBsAg positivity with age, and this pattern was consistent across SLE, SS, and control groups. This phenomenon mainly due to the lower HBV vaccination coverage in older generations in China, leading to a higher cumulative risk of HBV infection.

The pathogenesis of SLE is closely associated with hyperactivation of the humoral immune system and the widespread production of autoantibodies [16]. In this study, we observed preliminary correlations between the positivity of various autoantibodies and reduced HBsAg positivity. Notably, patients positive for common autoantibodies such as ANA, anti-ribosomal P, and anti-SSA showed lower HBV infection rates. Our titer-based analysis also revealed that higher ANA titers were associated with a lower HBsAg positivity rate, reaching statistical significance in SS patients. This suggests that not only the presence but also the abundance of certain autoantibodies may contribute to the reduced HBV susceptibility in autoimmune diseases. In contrast, different anti-dsDNA titers were not associated with significant differences in HBsAg positivity, indicating that the influence of autoantibody titers may vary by specificity. In SS patients, anti-SSA and anti-SSB antibodies are characteristic features, and their high prevalence may similarly be associated with reduced HBV infection, providing a shared immunological explanation for SLE and SS. Some studies suggest that molecular mimicry may exist between viral antigens and self-antigens, theoretically triggering cross-immune responses that promote viral clearance [17]. Although there is currently no direct evidence supporting a cross-reactivity between HBV antigens and the rich repertoire of autoantibodies found in SLE and SS, this hypothesis provides a direction for further exploration of virus-autoimmunity interactions.

In this study, to minimize the influence of potential selection bias and confounding factors, we analyzed the relationships between HBV DNA load, patient source, and immunosuppressant use with HBsAg positivity in SLE and SS patients. The evaluation of occult HBV infection demonstrated that its prevalence did not differ significantly between patients and healthy controls. This finding suggests that occult HBV infection is not common in this population, consistent with previous reports of its low frequency [18], and that the lower HBsAg positivity rate in SLE and SS patients is not attributable to occult hepatitis B. Moreover, comparison between inpatients and outpatients revealed no significant differences in HBsAg positivity, indicating that patient source had limited influence on the findings and supporting the representativeness of our results. Finally, analysis of immunosuppressant use showed that, although such agents theoretically could promote HBV reactivation or affect viral clearance, no significant differences were found in HBsAg or HBsAb positivity between users and non-users. These findings indicate that the reduced HBsAg positivity rate in SLE and SS patients is unlikely to be explained by immunosuppressant use, but may instead be related to intrinsic immune mechanisms.

In addition to immunological cross-reactivity, other mechanisms may also contribute to the reduced HBV infection rate observed in SLE and SS patients. Elevated levels of IFN-α and IL-6 in SLE may play dual roles in modulating HBV infection [19,20]. On the one hand, persistent IFN-α activation may enhance antiviral signaling pathways and directly suppress HBV replication [21]. On the other hand, high IL-6 expression may promote dendritic cell differentiation and excessive B cell activation, accelerating immune complex formation, which could interfere with HBV antigen detection or block viral entry into hepatocytes [22,23]. Similar cytokine changes have also been found in SS [24,25], which may partly explain why both diseases show reduced HBV susceptibility. Moreover, Mendelian randomization studies have supported a causal association between SLE and HBV, suggesting that genetic or immune backgrounds may indirectly affect HBV susceptibility [26,27]. SLE susceptibility genes may enhance viral clearance capacity while regulating autoimmune responses, this hypothesis may also be partially applicable to SS patients.

Despite the retrospective nature of this study, it offers several strengths. Compared with previous studies that involved small sample sizes or a single disease type, our study included a wide range of representative autoimmune diseases and identified a significantly reduced HBsAg positivity rate specifically in SLE and SS patients. This phenomenon remained statistically significant across different sex and age subgroups. Additionally, we systematically assessed the impact of different autoantibody profiles on HBsAg positivity, highlighting that ANA and several specific autoantibodies may be associated with reduced HBV infection rates. Furthermore, we found that major complications did not significantly influence HBV infection status, which further supports the role of the immune background in modulating HBV susceptibility. These findings provide new insights into the interaction between autoimmune diseases and viral infections and may offer novel perspectives for developing therapeutic strategies for both HBV and SLE.

Conclusion

This study found that the HBsAg positivity rate in SLE and SS patients was significantly lower than that in controls and other autoimmune diseases, and this trend was consistent across different sexes and age groups. This phenomenon was not related to occult HBV infection, the use of immunosuppressants, or complications. Further analysis showed that high ANA titers and multiple autoantibodies were associated with decreased HBsAg positivity. These findings suggest that the reduced HBV infection rate in SLE and SS is mainly attributable to disease-specific immunological mechanisms, highlighting the need for further mechanistic and longitudinal investigations.
