Adherence to Potassium Citrate, Changes in 24-Hour Stone Risk Parameters, and Recurrent Kidney Stone Events
Authors: Joseph J. Crivelli, Mary K. Oerline, Naim M. Maalouf, Ryan S. Hsi, Noah A. Krampe, Kevin B. Smith, Sara L. Best, Brian T. Denton, Vahakn B. Shahinian, John M. Hollingsworth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Visual Abstract

Introduction

Alkali therapy is recommended for patients with recurrent calcium-based stones and low or relatively low urinary citrate, as well as those with uric acid stones and low urine pH.1 Potassium citrate is the preferred alkali in these settings, which has improved urinary parameters in clinical trials.2 However, adherence to preventive pharmacologic therapy (PPT) agents is low,3 and potassium citrate is no exception. Previous studies have identified that PPT nonadherence is associated with a higher risk of recurrent stone events.4 For this reason, prescribers of PPT need robust methods for monitoring medication adherence.

There are several approaches to measure potassium citrate adherence. Self-reported adherence is the most common method, although its validity and precision may be suboptimal,5 creating a need for more objective measures. Clinical practice guidelines1 recommend on treatment follow-up testing for assessing response to therapy, and 24-hour urinary potassium excretion is a follow-up measurement that can reflect potassium citrate adherence.6 Metrics on the basis of pharmacy claims have also been used, such as the percentage of days covered (PDC).7 It is unknown to what extent these measures agree with each other and relate to changes in urinary citrate excretion and pH. It has also not been established which approach is more strongly associated with stone recurrence.

To address these questions, we conducted a retrospective observational study. We identified a cohort of older adult patients with urinary stone disease (USD) who had 24-hour urine collections processed by a commercial lab and available linked medical and pharmacy claims data. We identified the subset with hypocitraturia or low urine pH, a subsequent prescription for potassium citrate, and a follow-up 24-hour urine collection. We then compared the performance of adherence measures on the basis of pharmacy claims (PDC) and on treatment follow-up testing (change in urinary potassium excretion). Finally, we assessed the extent to which these measures of adherence were associated with recurrent stone events.

Methods

Data Source and Study Population

For this study, we analyzed data from the Medicare–Litholink database, the details of which have been published previously.8 In brief, the dataset consists of claims in the Medicare Provider and Analysis Review (MedPAR), Outpatient, Carrier, and part D Event Research Identifiable Files from 181,657 beneficiaries with linked 24-hour urine stone risk profile results from Litholink (Labcorp subsidiary) between 2010 and 2019. We required that the beneficiaries had continuous enrollment in Medicare parts A and B for the 12 months preceding the first 24-hour urine collection to ensure complete capture of inpatient and outpatient health care encounters. We also required continuous part D enrollment 6 months before and 6 months after the first collection to allow assessment of baseline medication use and subsequent prescription fills, including potassium citrate.

Identifying Patients Prescribed Potassium Citrate for Hypocitraturia or Low Urine pH

Of the patients meeting the aforementioned criteria, there were 11,510 patients with a follow-up 24-hour urine collection 6–12 months after the baseline collection. Among these patients, there were 6639 patients with baseline hypocitraturia (24-hour urinary citrate excretion <450 mg/d for males and <550 mg/d for female patients9) or low urine pH (<5.6 for males and female patients), and adequate baseline and follow-up 24-hour urine collections on the basis of creatinine excretion per kilogram of body weight. The final cohort comprised 793 patients meeting these criteria who were prescribed potassium citrate no later than 3 months after the baseline 24-hour urine collection. Exclusion criteria included patients with a history of cystinuria, bariatric surgery, bowel resection/bypass, chronic diarrhea, cystic fibrosis, inflammatory bowel disease, intestinal malabsorption, or medullary sponge kidney coded within 12 months before the baseline 24-hour urine collection (Supplemental Table 1). Patients who filled prescriptions for potassium citrate during the 6 months preceding the baseline 24-hour urine collection were excluded. In addition, patients prescribed other PPT agents (i.e., thiazide diuretics, uric acid lowering agents, or other prescription alkali) or other forms of potassium supplementation (e.g., potassium chloride) during the 6 months preceding the baseline collection through the follow-up collection were excluded. Finally, patients on particularly high doses of potassium citrate (>100 mEq/d), potentially representing implausible doses, were excluded.

Developing Measures of Potassium Citrate Adherence

We constructed two measures of adherence to potassium citrate. The first was PDC calculated through pharmacy claims, defined as the number of days covered by a patient's potassium citrate days' supply divided by the total number of days in the period. PDC was assessed over the 90-day period after the first potassium citrate prescription fill, dichotomized as <80% days covered versus ≥80% days covered.10 The second measure of adherence was the change in urinary potassium excretion between baseline and follow-up collections, divided by the prescribed daily dose (change in urinary potassium excretion divided by dose [ΔK/dose]). We dichotomized this measure as <0.5 versus ≥0.5, reflecting a median split and whether the observed change in urinary potassium was greater than half the prescribed dose. An additional threshold (<1.0 versus ≥1.0) was also evaluated, corresponding to a 1:1 agreement between the dose prescribed and potassium excreted. Daily potassium citrate dose was on the basis of the dose of the first prescription fill. As part of our assessment of the performance of these adherence measures, the change in urinary citrate excretion and the change in urine pH between baseline and follow-up were calculated.

Identifying Recurrent Stone Events

Consistent with previous studies, the primary outcome in this study was recurrent stone events, defined as an emergency department visit, hospitalization, or surgery for USD.11 Emergency department visits and hospitalizations were identified using Revenue Center Codes and the MedPAR file, respectively. Surgeries for USD were identified using International Classification of Diseases codes (MedPAR and Outpatient files) and Current Procedural Terminology codes (part B in Carrier file). Relevant codes are listed in Supplemental Table 2.

Other Study Variables

We report characteristics of the cohort including age at the time of the first prescription fill, sex, race, region of residence, dual eligibility status (i.e., whether the patient was eligible for both Medicare and Medicaid), and comorbidity score (hierarchical condition category12). The baseline 24-hour urine parameters reported, included urinary volume, calcium, oxalate, citrate, pH, uric acid, sodium, potassium, magnesium, phosphorus, ammonium, chloride, sulfate, urea nitrogen, and creatinine. We calculated the change in the ammonium-sulfate difference (ΔNH4-SO4) between baseline and follow-up as an indicator of change in renal acid excretion in response to alkali intake.13

Statistical Analysis

We calculated descriptive statistics, including frequencies for categorical variables and means for continuous variables. We used bivariable statistics (chi-square tests for categorical variables and independent t tests for continuous variables) to examine differences in patient characteristics by the two adherence measures. Next, we assessed differences in mean ΔK/dose by PDC (≥80% versus <80%) using an independent t test. Performance characteristics of ΔK/dose ≥0.5 and ΔK/dose ≥1.0 as a predictor of PDC ≥80% were calculated including sensitivity, specificity, positive likelihood ratio, negative likelihood ratio, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value. In addition, a receiver operating characteristic area under the curve describing ΔK/dose as a predictor of PDC ≥80% was calculated.

We then assessed the association of the two measures of medication adherence with changes in urinary citrate excretion and urinary pH using analysis of covariance. Through this approach, the mean changes in urinary citrate excretion and urinary pH were adjusted for age, sex, medication dose, baseline urinary potassium, and ΔNH4-SO4. We compared the strength of association of the two adherence measures with changes in citrate and pH by using the Wald test. We then used the Kaplan–Meier method to calculate the unadjusted cumulative incidence of a recurrent stone event, comparing patients in both strata for each potassium citrate adherence measure (i.e., <80% versus ≥80% for PDC and <0.5 versus ≥0.5 for ΔK/dose). Finally, we fit Cox proportional hazards models to estimate the hazard of a recurrent stone event as a function of both adherence measures, adjusting for age, sex, medication dose, baseline urinary potassium, and ΔNH4-SO4. Recurrent stone events were assessed over the 3-year period after the follow-up 24-hour urine collection.

Sensitivity analyses were performed by redefining the hypocitraturia inclusion criterion. Specifically, we lowered the urinary citrate excretion threshold to <320 mg/d for both male and female patients, a cutoff used by other vendors (e.g., Quest Diagnostics14). Aforementioned analyses including analysis of covariance, the Wald test, and Cox regression were repeated in the subset of patients meeting this threshold (n=702). All other inclusion and exclusion criteria remained unchanged, including the threshold for low urine pH.

We conducted all statistical analyses using SAS Version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). All statistical tests were two-sided with the type 1 error rate set to 0.05. Patients meeting inclusion and exclusion criteria had complete exposure, covariate, and outcome data. The Institutional Review Board at the University of Michigan Medical School determined that this study was exempt from its oversight.

Results

There were a total of 289 (36.4%) patients classified as not adherent and 504 (63.6%) patients classified as adherent on the basis of PDC ≥80%. On the basis of ΔK/dose ≥0.5, a total of 404 (50.9%) patients were classified as not adherent and 389 (49.1%) patients were classified as adherent. The mean (SD) age was 70.0 (8.8) years, 425 (53.6%) patients were male individuals, and 706 (89.0%) patients were White individuals. The mean (SD) potassium citrate dose was 41.6 (22.0) mEq/d.

As shown in Table 1, there was a statistically significant difference in daily medication dose by medication adherence such that adherent patients on the basis of PDC had a higher daily dose (P = 0.0451) whereas adherent patients on the basis of ΔK/dose had a lower daily dose (P < 0.001). A similar pattern was observed for baseline urinary potassium, such that adherent patients on the basis of PDC had a higher baseline urinary potassium (P = 0.0537) whereas adherent patients on the basis of ΔK/dose had a lower baseline urinary potassium (P < 0.001). The decrease in ΔNH4-SO4 between collections was more pronounced in patients who were adherent to potassium citrate, particularly with ΔK/dose as the adherence metric (P < 0.001). Other baseline urinary parameters differed inconsistently by adherence status and were not identified a priori as potential confounders (e.g., urinary chloride, phosphorus, and sodium). There were also no statistically significant differences in age, sex, race, region, dual eligibility status, or comorbidity score by measures of adherence. Patients who were adherent to medication on the basis of PDC ≥80% had significantly higher mean ΔK/dose compared with those not adherent to medication (+0.67 versus +0.43, P < 0.001). However, performance characteristics of ΔK/dose ≥0.5 and ΔK/dose ≥1.0 for the prediction of PDC ≥80% were weak overall (Table 2 and Supplemental Table 3). The receiver operating characteristic area under the curve corresponding to ΔK/dose prediction of PDC ≥80% was 0.57, also indicating limited discriminative ability.

In an analysis adjusted for age, sex, medication dose, baseline urinary potassium, and ΔNH4-SO4, the adjusted mean increase in urinary citrate and pH among patients who were adherent to medication significantly exceeded that of patients not adherent to medication (Figure 1). Compared with PDC, ΔK/dose had a stronger association with change in urinary citrate (P < 0.001) and pH (P < 0.001). At 3 years, the unadjusted cumulative incidence of a recurrent stone event in the overall cohort was 22.2% (95% confidence interval [CI], 19.0% to 25.8%). The 3-year cumulative incidence of recurrent stone events is compared by adherence strata in Figure 2. For patients with PDC <80% versus ≥80%, the 3-year cumulative incidence of a recurrent stone event was 27.8% (95% CI, 22.2% to 34.5%) versus 18.9% (95% CI, 15.2% to 23.2%), respectively. For patients with ΔK/dose <0.5 versus ≥0.5, the corresponding cumulative incidence was 22.8% (95% CI, 18.3% to 28.2%) versus 21.6% (95% CI, 17.4% to 26.7%), respectively. In Cox proportional hazards models adjusted for the same covariates as above (Figure 3), compared with PDC ≥80%, patients with PDC <80% had a 43.1% greater hazard of a recurrent stone event (hazard ratio [HR], 1.431; 95% CI, 1.049 to 1.952); however, compared with ΔK/dose ≥0.5, patients with ΔK/dose <0.5 did not have a statistically significantly different hazard of a recurrent stone event (HR, 1.032; 95% CI, 0.740 to 1.439).

In the sensitivity analyses performed in a subset of the cohort meeting a more stringent definition of hypocitraturia (<320 mg/d), the adjusted mean increase in urinary citrate and pH among patients who were adherent to medication significantly exceeded that of patients not adherent to medication (Supplemental Figure 1). Compared with PDC, ΔK/dose had a stronger association with change in urinary citrate (P < 0.001) and pH (P < 0.001). In adjusted Cox proportional hazards models (Supplemental Figure 2), compared with PDC ≥80%, patients with PDC <80% had a 39.7% greater hazard of a recurrent stone event (HR, 1.397; 95% CI, 1.001 to 1.950); however, compared with ΔK/dose ≥0.5, patients with ΔK/dose <0.5 did not have a statistically significantly different hazard of a recurrent stone event (HR, 0.989; 95% CI, 0.694 to 1.409). These findings are qualitatively similar to those of the primary analyses, supporting robustness of the observed pattern using an alternate definition of hypocitraturia.

Discussion

In this observational study, we aimed to evaluate associations between measures of potassium citrate adherence, changes in 24-hour urine parameters, and the risk of recurrent stone events. We identified that, compared with PDC, increases in urinary potassium were more strongly associated with concurrent increases in urinary citrate and pH. This could suggest that urinary potassium is a more direct or physiologic marker of adherence. However, paradoxically, we found that the change in urinary potassium was not associated with recurrent stone events, whereas PDC was.

There are several potential explanations and implications of these findings. Changes in urinary potassium are expected to correlate more closely with changes in urinary citrate and pH because these measurements are obtained concurrently. By contrast, PDC reflects adherence over the interval between urine collections and may not capture adherence at the time of follow-up testing. In other words, improvement seen in a follow-up urine collection may only reflect medication adherence in the days immediately preceding the collection and the 24-hour period during which the collection is performed. By contrast, PDC integrates adherence behavior longitudinally. This phenomenon is consistent with the broad concept of measurement reactivity, in which individuals modify behavior in response to being observed or evaluated, often described in adherence research as the Hawthorne effect.15,16 This type of short-term behavior change, also referred to as white coat adherence, has been described in other settings including increased medication adherence in pediatric epilepsy and improved glycemic metrics in adult patients with diabetes in the days preceding clinic visits.17,18 Although we cannot directly measure such behavior in this study, this framework provides a plausible explanation for why urinary potassium may more accurately reflect acute pharmacologic effects, whereas PDC demonstrates stronger prognostic utility.

It is also possible that PDC with potassium citrate correlates positively with adherence to other prescribed treatment strategies for patients with calcium-based stones and hypocitraturia or uric acid stones and low urine pH. These include increasing fluid intake, increasing fruit and vegetable intake, and limiting nondairy animal protein intake.1 Although dietary data were not available in this cohort, we did adjust for the ΔNH4-SO4 as an indicator of the change in dietary acid load.13 Nonetheless, adherence to dietary recommendations in between collections are similarly subjected to the Hawthorne effect. As such, 24-hour urine changes potentially attributable to diet also may not associate with stone recurrence risk in this cohort.

The results of this study should be interpreted in the context of its limitations. The study cohort consisted of Medicare enrollees; consequently, the findings may not be generalizable to other populations such as younger adults who represent a significant portion of patients with recurrent USD requiring long-term therapy. Given the observational nature of the study, omitted variable bias is possible. Residual heterogeneity and unmeasured confounding may contribute to variability in clinical outcomes. In multivariable analyses, we adjusted for covariates identified as plausible confounders of the relationships between medication adherence, changes in urinary citrate and pH, and recurrent stone events. Numerous alkali supplements are available over the counter,19 and it is possible that patients took these in addition to or in place of the prescribed potassium citrate. Although we adjusted for the ammonium-sulfate difference as a measure of change in renal acid excretion in response to dietary changes in alkali intake between collections, this may not completely account for dietary adherence or the introduction of such a supplement. Although over the counter supplements could explain why the change in urinary potassium was more strongly associated with the change in urinary citrate and pH, it would not explain why PDC was significantly associated with recurrent stone events whereas the change in urinary potassium was not. PDC was measured over a 3-month period, which may be considered brief compared with other studies that have used 6-month or 12-month windows. Thus, although an association between PDC and recurrent stone events was identified, the strength of association may be underestimated. We also acknowledge that the 3-month PDC window precedes the longer follow-up period over which stone recurrence was addressed, introducing a temporal disconnect. This design choice reflects a necessary trade-off to align PDC measurement with the interval between medication initiation and follow-up 24-hour urine collection. Extending the PDC window also would have substantially reduced the eligible sample. Accordingly, PDC in this study should be interpreted as an early adherence marker rather than a direct measure of long-term exposure. Although PDC demonstrated stronger prognostic utility in this study, it is not routinely available to clinicians in real-time clinical practice, as it typically requires access to pharmacy claims data. Although this study focused on stone events in the form of health care encounters, we were not able to assess imaging-related outcomes such as appearance of new stones or stone growth. Increases in urinary citrate and pH with alkali therapy may have differing effects depending on stone phenotype. For example, higher pH can favor calcium phosphate stone formation; however, stone composition data were not available to explore this possibility.

Despite these limitations, this study has important clinical implications. To our knowledge, this is the first study to compare two measures of potassium citrate adherence. The study cohort met strict criteria defining a within-patient comparison reflecting initiation of potassium citrate therapy on the basis of collection of a baseline 24-hour urine and a follow-up 24-hour urine 3–6 months later. Although changes in 24-hour urinary potassium after potassium citrate initiation may correlate with concurrent changes in urinary citrate and pH, the value of this adherence measurement may be limited. Other measures of medication adherence, such as PDC on the basis of pharmacy claims, may more accurately reflect adherence between urine collections and the potential longer-term effect of adherence on disease activity. PDC is measured using medication refill activity, an indirect indicator of adherence, which could potentially be monitored through electronic health records.20 Integration of pharmacy benefit data into electronic health records may improve the clinical feasibility of claims-based adherence measures such as PDC.

In conclusion, there are clinically important differences in measures of adherence to potassium citrate among patients with USD. Compared with a pharmacy claims-based adherence measure, the change in urinary potassium was more strongly associated with the change in urinary citrate and pH. However, adherence on the basis of pharmacy claims was significantly associated with recurrent stone events, whereas the change in urinary potassium was not. Future work should focus on developing more direct measures of PPT adherence.
