Lightweight dual-stage feature refinement for black gram leaf disease classification using ConViTSE
Authors: M Anu Kiruthika, Angelin Gladston, H Khanna Nehemiah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBlack gram, also known as urad bean, is an economically crucial crop widely cultivated in India, particularly in the central and southern regions. However, black gram is highly prone to multiple leaf diseases, resulting in considerable crop losses and economic challenges for farmers. Manual disease identification is slow and often unreliable, necessitating the development of automated disease detection methods. In this study, we propose ConViTSE, a lightweight hybrid deep learning architecture specifically designed for black gram leaf disease classification. ConViTSE integrates ConvMixer, Vision Transformer (ViT), and Squeeze and Excitation (SE) blocks to effectively extract and refine both local and global features. The model introduces Local Channel Attention Refinement (LCAR) and Global Channel Attention Refinement (GCAR) modules to enhance feature representation at different hierarchical levels. Extensive studies show that ConViTSE achieves a leading classification accuracy of 99.30% on the black gram dataset, outperforming traditional deep learning models. Furthermore, ConViTSE exhibits robust cross-domain generalization, achieving accuracies of 98.75% for rice, 98.20% for maize, and 95% for wheat, highlighting its potential for widespread adoption in precision agriculture. ConViTSE enhances disease detection accuracy while remaining computationally efficient, making it a practical tool for real-time disease management in diverse agricultural environments.Keywords: Black gram leaf disease, Feature extraction, Transformer, ConvMixer, Squeeze and Excitation (SE), Crop disease diagnosis, Lightweight deep learning, Feature refinementSubject terms: Plant sciences, Engineering, Image processing, Machine learning

Introduction

Black gram is a valuable pulse crop with a very high mineral content of phosphoric acid. This crop has high demand among consumers due to its nutrition, nitrogen fixation in soil, and soil fertility improvement1. Major producing states include Maharashtra, Karnataka, Uttar Pradesh, West Bengal, Madhya Pradesh, Andhra Pradesh, and Punjab Tamil Nadu, in particular, sees widespread black gram cultivation and consumption. Several varieties of black gram have been cultivated, including Pusa-1, T.A.U.-1, UPU-1, G 31, Mash-48, Khargone-3, T 27, H 10, T 77, UPU-2, T 9, T.P.U.-4, 55 and T.A.U-22. Black gram is grown over most of India and is the most widely cultivated and consumed pulse crop in Tamil Nadu. Tamil Nadu saw a great demand for black gram last year3. This crop is affected by several factors. One of the significant factors is leaf diseases, which primarily comprise leaf crinkle, powdery mildew, yellow mosaic, leaf spot, and anthracnose that reduce the crop health and yield of black gram. The spread of bacteria, fungi, and viruses will all have an impact on black gram crop disease. Powdery mildew is one of the most common diseases that harm crop health, turning the green parts of leaves grey. Crop diseases not only hamper growth and lower yields but also create a substantial financial burden for farmers, especially small and marginal ones, by increasing cultivation costs and reducing income from sales4. Crop diseases not only cause growth delays and minimize yields, but they also place a considerable financial burden on farming communities. Even moderate yield reductions result in income instability and greater dependency on chemical inputs, which raises cultivation costs. In Tamil Nadu, where black gram is both a major crop and a source of income, delayed or inaccurate disease detection may harm food security and farmer well-being. These challenges highlight the importance of accurate, real-time, low-cost disease detection technology that can function well under a variety of field circumstances.

Detection of black gram leaf disease is important; farmers moving into the field to detect diseases with their limited expertise is a tedious operation that results in a time-intensive process in the field. Thus, automatic disease detection on leaves is increasingly important today5. Machine learning is important in detecting leaf diseases, and several algorithms are used for classification6. Support Vector Machines (SVMs)7 are used for feature extraction and also for disease classification. These methods mostly depend on specific feature extraction, highlighting characteristics such as color, texture, and shape8. While they are excellent at classifying diseases, their ability to adapt is limited because they rely on certain criteria. Many machine learning algorithms struggle with increasingly diverse and complex datasets, limiting their robustness and scalability9. To overcome these difficulties, learning has become an essential solution for the leaf disease detection.

Deep learning models10, in contrast to conventional methods, are capable of automatically extracting features from raw data through neural networks. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs)11 are particularly effective for image-based tasks because they can capture local patterns in the data using convolutional layers. However, CNNs12,13 often face challenges in understanding the global context or detailed spatial relationships within complex datasets. Additionally, their ability to selectively emphasize important features is limited due to the absence of attention mechanisms. High-resolution image processing with CNNs can also be computationally intensive, raising concerns about efficiency. Transformers, initially designed for natural language processing14, have been adapted for image classification and employ self-attention mechanisms15, which allow them to capture both local and global features effectively. This capability enables the model to distinguish between visually similar classes, such as healthy and diseased leaves, by analyzing spatial relationships across the entire image.

Transformers also have the added benefit of dynamically allocating importance to relevant features during the feature extraction process, which improves their capacity to focus on critical patterns for better classification results. Transformer models like as ViT16 and Swin Transformers17,18 have been used to classify leaf diseases with great effectiveness. These models approach an image as a collection of patches, allowing them to detect both local and global dependencies throughout the image. ViT breaks an image into fixed size patches, which are then linearly embedded, positional encodings are applied, and the sequence is processed through layers of multi-head self-attention. This technique outperformed CNNs, particularly on large-scale datasets, identifying and classifying numerous leaf diseases such as rust, blight, and mildew with more accuracy. Transformer models, despite their outstanding results, are computationally expensive, frequently requiring significant memory and processing capacity, which can limit their application in real-time disease classification. Transformer-based models perform well for leaf disease classification, but they encounter several obstacles.

One of their major drawbacks is their high computational cost, especially in terms of memory utilization and processing time during training19.ViTs require large datasets to train effectively and can be computationally demanding due to their self-attention mechanism, which scales quadratically with input size. This makes them resource-intensive, often requiring powerful hardware and extensive processing time, especially when dealing with high-resolution images. Existing approaches, whether based on CNNs or transformers, struggle with these challenges. These issues are addressed by our proposed ConViTSE model which comprises of ConvMixer, ViT and SE. The study questions identified a process for predicting black gram leaf disease and addressing related issues: (1) How does adding ConvMixer, ViT, and SE blocks improve leaf disease classification against typical CNN models? (2) How do SE blocks improve the model’s ability to detect and classify diseases in leaves with different textures? (3) Can the hybrid ConvMixer + ViT model outperform transformer only models on smaller datasets such as the black gram? (4) What are the trade-offs between model accuracy, complexity, and speed when utilizing ConvMixer + ViT with SE blocks in real-time disease detection systems?

In this study, we introduce a new hybrid ConViTSE model that incorporates the advantages of ConvMixer, ViT, and SE blocks. ConvMixer captures local spatial patterns efficiently, whereas ViT models global relationships through self-attention. SE blocks are used for dual-stage feature refinement, with the Local Channel Attention Refinement (LCAR) module emphasizing channel-wise significance at the ConvMixer stage and the Global Channel Attention Refinement (GCAR) module emphasizing semantically important global features after the ViT stage. This combination enables the model to efficiently focus on disease-relevant regions, enhancing visual class discrimination and allowing for exact black gram leaf disease classification. Extensive testing on diverse datasets shows that the model performs reliably and efficiently, making it a scalable and practical solution for detecting diseases in agriculture. Related works

Automated leaf disease identification has extensively utilized deep learning methods. Earlier approaches relied on handcrafted features such as shape descriptors, color histograms, and texture analysis combined with traditional machine learning classifiers, but they often failed to generalize across plant species, disease types, and environmental conditions19. In recent years, deep learning models have achieved significantly higher accuracy and robustness. Automated plant disease identification has supported crop health monitoring and improved yield production. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) consistently achieve high accuracy in leaf disease detection, often surpassing 95% and even exceeding 99% in some studies across various crops and datasets20. Advanced CNN architectures, including EfficientNet, DenseNet, VGG, and ResNet, have demonstrated robust performance. For instance, ResNet50, DenseNet161, and VGG19, when optimized or combined with modules like Multi-Activation Function, further improve accuracy and generalization21–23. Ensemble and hybrid models, such as combining DenseNet, Inception, and Xception, can also achieve high accuracy, up to 98.46%, making these CNNs the most commonly used and effective models for plant leaf disease detection24,25. Building on these works, recent studies have explored deep convolutional and hybrid CNN-Transformer models to capture both local texture patterns and global leaf structures, leading to the development of the proposed ConViTSE model.

The following section reviews existing approaches for leaf disease prediction using deep convolutional and hybrid deep learning models.

Deep convolutional models

Atila et al.26 used the PlantVillage dataset to develop a transfer learning-based technique for diagnosing plant leaf diseases using the EfficientNet architecture. Their study showed that incorporating both original and augmented datasets led to substantial performance improvements, with EfficientNetB5 and B4 achieving remarkable accuracy levels of 99.91% and 99.97%, respectively. Given that biotic and abiotic diseases can reduce crop yields by up to 40%, this study underscores the potential of deep learning in minimizing such losses. Karlekar et al.27 proposed a computer vision-based framework comprising two main modules: one for isolating the leaf from its background and another for detecting soybean plant diseases using SoyNet, a convolutional neural network-based deep learning model. The approach was evaluated on a plant disease symptom image dataset and outperformed nine other deep learning models, including XceptionNet, ResNet50, VGG19, LeNet, DenseNet121, and GoogleNet, achieving an accuracy of 98.14%. Additionally, their research investigated the use of artificial intelligence in agriculture for early detection and classification of leaf diseases in crops such as tomatoes and grapes, with the aim of improving crop yield and food production.

Paymode et al.28 developed a deep learning approach based on a CNN with a Visual Geometry Group (VGG) backbone to classify leaves as healthy or diseased. They trained and tested the model on a dataset containing multiple crops and reported high classification accuracy, achieving 98.40% for grape leaves and 95.71% for tomato leaves. This work underscores the importance of detecting leaf diseases early in crop development and demonstrates the effectiveness of deep learning for reliable disease identification. In another study, Stephen et al.29 improved rice leaf disease detection by incorporating four pretrained CNN architectures. To boost performance, they introduced a self-attention mechanism into ResNet18 and ResNet34, which substantially enhanced classification results. Among the tested models, ResNet34 with self-attention achieved the best overall accuracy of 98.54%. For specific disease categories, brown spot was detected with 87.26% accuracy, healthy leaves with 93.06%, and hispa with 54.11%. The study also reported a misclassification rate of 18.15% and an overall precision of 81.84%.

Reis, H. C. et al.30 introduced MDSCIRNet, a deep convolutional neural network specifically designed for potato leaf disease classification. The model incorporates multi-head attention mechanisms and depthwise separable convolutions. When combined with SVM, MDSCIRNet achieved a peak accuracy of 99.33%, advancing other deep learning models such as EfficientNetB2, ResNet101, and Xception. Additionally, hybrid techniques integrating MDSCIRNet with ensemble learning methods and traditional machine learning algorithms were explored to enhance classification performance further. To improve dataset quality, image enhancement techniques such as Hypercolumn, CLAHE, and ESRGAN were applied, resulting in more robust findings across multiple datasets. This research emphasizes real-world applications, including camera equipped robotic systems, mobile platforms, and web-based tools, offering a scalable approach for early disease detection and sustainable agricultural practices.

Upadhyay., et al.31 proposed SegLearner, an end-to-end pipeline based on CNN that detects and estimates disease severity in apple and guava leaves. The model starts with a pretrained ResNet-50 to categorize leaves as infected or healthy, then employs a SegFormer-inspired segmentation module to accurately identify infected regions. Although SegFormer uses transformer-based encoders, SegLearner relies mostly on traditional CNN backbones such as ResNet-101 and MobileNetV2, keeping the architecture’s focus on the CNN domain. To aid segmentation training, the authors created a special Mask-Leaf Dataset that includes disease regions manually marked in apple and guava leaves. A semi-supervised technique was applied to improve mask generation, resulting in excellent segmentation with limited ground truth data. Additionally, the model was evaluated on the widely used ADE20K dataset to assess generalizability across several image classes. A pixel-counting technique was used to estimate disease severity and distribution. SegLearner achieved 91.4% mIoU on Mask-LeafDataset and 45.8% on ADE20K, surpassing baseline models like as UNet, MaskRCNN, and SegFormer. This work shows the effectiveness of CNN-based segmentation pipelines, especially when adapted to domain-specific agriculture datasets.

Upadhyay et al.32 present a detailed review of artificial intelligence applications throughout the agricultural lifecycle, covering pre-harvest, harvest, and post-harvest stages. Their study highlights how modern AI techniques particularly deep learning models such as convolutional neural networks (CNNs), computer vision (CV), and the Internet of Things (IoT) have advanced crop monitoring, disease detection, yield forecasting, and post-harvest management. They also discuss the growing importance of approaches like transfer learning, generative adversarial networks (GANs), and few-shot learning for addressing limited data and improving model generalization. Additionally, integrating edge computing and hybrid AI frameworks facilitates real-time data analysis and decision-making, which is especially valuable in remote or resource-constrained agricultural settings. The authors also cite many ongoing problems, such as insufficient dataset diversity, high infrastructure costs, deployment limits in low-resource environments, and ethical concerns about data privacy and labor migration. Their work is unusual in that it provides a structured comparison of traditional versus AI-based procedures across agricultural phases, bringing insights into economic feasibility, adaptation across climatic zones, and the necessity for standardized AI regulatory frameworks. This comprehensive study offers useful guidance for future research aiming at establishing scalable, transparent, and inclusive AI-driven agricultural solutions.

Upadhyay et al.33 proposed an improved ResNeXt-based CNN for detecting fungal infections in apple crops, emphasizing the role of deep learning in early disease identification. Their approach involved preprocessing steps such as Gaussian filtering and image resizing, followed by GrabCut segmentation to isolate affected regions. The enhanced ResNeXt model achieved a classification accuracy of 98.94%, outperforming established architectures like ResNet and Inception-v7. The work shows that typical deep CNN architectures can address class imbalance and improve feature localization via segmentation. However, its use was limited to a single crop apple, and the authors emphasized the importance of greater generalization and future severity estimation integration. Their findings highlight the importance of deep CNN models in crop disease assessment, especially in fungal disease scenarios where early detection is important for ensuring food quality and safety.

Upadhyay et al.34 developed a modified ResNeXt CNN model to detect fungal diseases in guava, apple, and custard apple, addressing variability in disease patterns due to regional and climatic differences. During preprocessing, images were resized and filtered using a Gaussian filter, and fuzzy C-means clustering was applied to accurately isolate affected regions. The model was trained on a benchmark dataset containing 14,408 images across three crop types and ten disease categories, achieving a high classification accuracy of 98.92%, with precision, recall exceeding 99%. Compared to conventional CNN architectures like ResNet and Inception-v7, the proposed model demonstrated superior performance. The study also suggested future enhancements, including evaluating disease severity and exploring its relationship with crop maturity, which could support precision agriculture and informed crop management.

Upadhyay., et al.35 stated a lightweight meta-learning framework for mango crop maturity classification based on few-shot learning to address the issue of small training data. This approach combines DenseNet-121, a convolutional backbone, with a meta-learning classifier optimized for cosine distance and adaptive scaling, allowing for quick generalization from a small number of samples. A custom dataset of 200 mango images, evenly split between ripe and unripe classes, was developed and preprocessed using the Faster R-CNN approach to segment the fruit from the background. Despite the short dataset, the model, which has around 0.70 million trainable parameters, produced excellent results, with a classification accuracy of around 68% with one support sample and 83% with five. These findings show that the model is capable of accurately classifying maturity despite limited data availability.

Upadhyay et al.36 developed a CNN-based model aimed at improving the early and accurate detection of potato leaf diseases, including early and late blight, which significantly impact crop yield and cause economic losses. Their model focuses on automating the diagnosing process, which has traditionally depended on skilled human observation and is frequently time-consuming and subjective. To improve learning from the PlantVillage dataset, the proposed CNN architecture integrates many different layers and extracts rich discriminative features using a fine-tuned VGG19 backbone. Experimental data showed that their model outperformed numerous other deep learning algorithms, with a test accuracy of 99.2%. This work provides farmers with a dependable decision support tool for distinguishing between healthy and diseased leaves, so contributing to better crop management and food security.

CNN-transformer hybrid techniques

Karthik et al.37 proposed a deep framework for grape leaf disease classification that integrates a Swin Transformer with a Group Shuffle Residual DeformNet (GSRDN). Their method employs a triplet attention block to fuse features and enhance cross-dimensional interactions, achieving impressive performance on the PlantVillage dataset with 98.6% accuracy, 98.7% precision, 98.59% recall, and a 98.64% F1-score.

Thakur et al.38 introduced a combined CNN and Vision Transformer framework designed to improve plant disease detection and classification accuracy. By leveraging ViTs for global feature extraction and CNNs for capturing localized features, their approach achieved high accuracy on multiple public datasets, including PlantVillage and Embrapa, outperforming nine contemporary methods. The model is lightweight, with only 0.85 million trainable parameters, making it suitable for IoT-based agricultural applications. However, it suffers from challenges such as high computational demands and difficulty distinguishing between diseases with similar visual patterns. Future research will focus on increasing model efficiency and improving its ability to distinguish between visually similar diseases.

The Feature Relationship Conditional Filtering technique, proposed by Liu et al.39, selectively removes non-essential data from the source domain, creating a focused subset that improves target domain performance. Additionally, a latent semantic masking autoencoder (LSMAE) is included to assist the transformer model in selecting critical patches from the images, hence increasing discriminative feature representation. The technique is validated using public datasets such as IP102, CPB, and PlantVillage, and it surpasses previous methods with excellent accuracy ratings of 74.69%, 76.99%, and 99.93%, respectively. Sharma, V. et al.40 introduced the SoyaTrans model makes a substantial contribution to plant leaf disease classification by merging CNNs with Swin transformers global feature mining. The implementation of a novel random shifting criterion improves classification accuracy while decreasing computing cost. It outperforms ten cutting-edge models across many datasets, including PlantVillage, AI2018, plantdoc, and Embrapa, showing amazing accuracy even in adverse situations. The study also presents a new soybean leaf dataset to address a lack of publicly available resources. LIME, GradCAM, and t-SNE visualizations further improve interpretability, ensuring trust in the model’s predictions.

Kamal, S. et al.41 proposed DVTXAI (Deep ViT with Explainable AI), which employs deep ViT to accurately identify tomato and potato diseases while offering explainability for dependability. The model includes nine disease classes, three for potatoes and six for tomatoes, and reports accuracy rates of 99.9% for potatoes and 93.56% for tomatoes. The model augments transparency of the algorithm with the SHAP algorithm, enabling the farmer to see, understand, and trust the decision-making process. This method further empowers non-experts to make well-informed decisions, helping to minimize crop losses and enhance agricultural productivity. Aboelenin et al.42 designed a hybrid The model integrates CNN and ViT architectures to enhance the detection and classification of plant leaf diseases. First, an ensemble of convolutional neural networks initialized with pre-learned weights including VGG16, Inception-V3, and DenseNet201 is employed to extract strong global features from input images. These global representations are subsequently refined by a Vision Transformer, which focuses on capturing fine-grained local patterns. This combination enables accurate identification and classification of plant leaf diseases by effectively leveraging both global context and local details. When evaluated on apple and corn leaf disease datasets, the model achieved impressive accuracy rates of 99.24% and 98%, surpassing several established methods. The study highlights the model’s practical potential for application in real-world agriculture, while also discussing existing limitations and suggesting directions for future research. Proposed hybrid ConViTSE model for blackgram leaf disease classification

Our study introduces a new hybrid ConViTSE model architecture for predicting and classifying diseases in black gram leaves. This model is designed to address the constraints of CNN-only or transformer-only architectures by combining local and global feature learning with attention-based refinement. Figure 1 illustrates the proposed ConViTSE model pipeline, which begins with the collection of black gram leaf images from the Mendeley data repository, comprising a diverse range of healthy and diseased samples. The dataset is subsequently split into training and testing sets to facilitate accurate model evaluation. The proposed hybrid model employs a hierarchical feature learning technique that incorporates ConvMixer, ViT, and SE modules. Each module targets a distinct representation: ConvMixer captures localized textures, ViT handles global spatial relationships, and SE blocks emphasize critical channels adaptively. The model first uses ConvMixer blocks to efficiently extract local spatial features, such as edges, texture, and other localized patterns. It uses depthwise convolution followed by channel mixing operations to collect fine-grained data required for identifying leaf lesions. These collected features are then fed into a Local Channel Attention Refinement (LCAR) module, where SE blocks adaptively improve the most relevant channel replies. By doing so, the model emphasizes disease-specific areas and disregards irrelevant features. A ViT block then processes the improved characteristics, capturing long-range interdependence and global contextual relationships using self-attention mechanisms. This is particularly significant for detecting signs of disease that are spatially spread throughout multiple leaf regions. To augment this global representation, we develop the Global Channel Attention Refinement (GCAR) module, which uses SE blocks to prioritize semantically relevant channels based on global information. This two-stage attention mechanism LCAR for local-level refinement and GCAR for global-level enhancement enables the model to prioritize the most relevant features across different hierarchical layers. This architecture is novel in that it combines ConvMixer and ViT for hierarchical feature extraction, as well as the use of LCAR and GCAR attention modules to improve spatial and semantic feature relevance. These features are then fed into a global average pooling layer followed by a dense classifier to identify the type of disease. This design ensures that the final classification takes into account both fine-grained and global patterns. The model separates black gram leaves into five types: powdery mildew, healthy, leaf crinkle, anthracnose,, and yellow mosaic. Metrics such as accuracy, precision, and recall are used to evaluate classification performance. Comparative experiments reveal that our model outperforms previous methods by efficiently combining local and global feature extraction with attention-based channel refining while being computationally inexpensive.

Preprocessing

The images are preprocessed to make the model network inputs uniform and reduce duplication of images to be more efficient in training. Each black gram leaf disease images are preprocessed to maintain the same architecture before being input into the ConViTSE model. Each image is initially resized to 224 × 224 × 3 with bilinear interpolation to achieve consistent spatial formatting. Feature extraction

Extracting relevant features is a key step in plant disease classification using deep learning, as it shapes the model’s capability to learn meaningful representations from leaf images. Meaningful characteristics are retrieved from different parts of the leaf during this stage to capture significant visual cues associated with disease symptoms. These features are led to accurate classification. In the proposed approach, hierarchical feature learning is essential for recognizing black gram leaf diseases. This section describes the roles and configurations of the ConvMixer, Vision Transformer (ViT), and SE-based attention modules specifically, Local Channel Attention Refinement (LCAR) and Global Channel Attention Refinement (GCAR) in the feature extraction pipeline. ConvMixer is preferred over typical CNNs because conventional CNNs have a pyramid structure in which the size of the features grows while the resolution decreases as depth increases. This is useful, but it is computationally expensive and can reduce fine-grained spatial features. ConvMixer overcomes these constraints by employing an isotropic architecture with mixed features, embedding patches with convolutional patches, and obtaining local spatial patterns efficiently and with fewer parameters. Furthermore, ViT improves feature extraction by taking into account long-range dependencies, providing global context information that ConvMixer fails to provide on its own. Finally, SE-based modules improve feature representations by adaptively selecting informative channels, ensuring that both local and global features are considered when classifying diseases.

ConvMixer

ConvMixer46 is a convolutional neural network (CNN) that aims to match the performance of Vision Transformers (ViTs) while keeping standard CNNs simple and efficient. It is inspired by the MLP-Mixer architecture and replaces token and channel mixing layers with convolutional operations. The model begins by splitting the input image into non-overlapping patches, similar to ViT’s patch embedding, but taking advantage of convolutions inductive biases. ConvMixer layers consist of two basic components: depthwise convolutions, which record long-range spatial interactions using large kernels such as 9 × 9, and pointwise convolutions, which mix information across channels using 1 × 1 convolutions. These procedures are improved with residual connections and activation function GELU, which stabilize training. ConvMixer outperforms ViTs with fewer parameters by utilizing convolutional computational efficiency and capturing both local and global features. This makes it a simpler and computationally affordable alternative to ViTs, particularly on convolutional optimized hardware, while still performing well on image classification tasks. In our proposed ConViTSE model, ConvMixer extracts rich local features from leaf images. Its capacity to efficiently capture fine-grained spatial patterns improves early-stage feature representation, resulting in higher classification accuracy.

ViT

The Vision Transformer, or ViT47, is a deep learning model that adapts the Transformer architecture, originally developed for language tasks, to computer vision. Instead of processing the entire image at once, ViT divides it into small, fixed-size patches, flattens them, and converts each patch into a feature vector, similar to how words are represented in natural language processing. Positional embeddings are added to preserve spatial information, and the sequence is passed through transformer encoder layers, where multi-head self-attention enables the model to capture relationships between all parts of the image. Unlike convolutional neural networks, which gradually build spatial hierarchies through convolution operations, ViT begins by considering the global context, making it particularly effective for complex visual tasks when trained on large datasets. In the ConViTSE model, ViT captures global features, complementing ConvMixer’s focus on local patterns. This combination allows the model to accurately detect even small disease spots scattered across the leaf.

Transformers have proven highly effective in fields like natural language processing and computer vision, largely thanks to their powerful self-attention mechanism and consistent architectural design. In these models, every network element has the same size and structure, which enables them to capture long-range dependencies across the entire input. However, the self-attention mechanism scales quadratically with the number of input tokens, creating a significant computational bottleneck when dealing with high-resolution images or very large datasets.

Traditional CNNs, on the other hand, use a pyramid-like design in which feature size grows and resolution drops as the network deepens. While effective at extracting features, such structures can be computationally costly and may result in the loss of fine-grained spatial details. To address these constraints, ConvMixer was introduced as an alternative. ConvMixer skips the traditional pyramid design by employing an isotropic architecture based purely on convolutional processes for patch embedding and feature mixing. This architecture not only simplifies the model but also captures local spatial data using normal convolution layers, making ConvMixer ideal for computer vision tasks.

Training process

The ConViTSE model is trained end-to-end to efficiently classify blackgram leaf diseases. The input images are preprocessed through resizing, normalization, and data augmentation techniques such as random rotations, flips, and zooms to enhance model robustness. Initially, ConvMixer blocks extract local spatial features by performing patch-wise mixing and depthwise convolutions. The LCAR module, implemented via SE blocks, refines these local features by capturing channel dependencies. These refined features are then passed to the Vision Transformer (ViT), which leverages multi-head self-attention on embedded patches to capture global contextual information. To further strengthen feature representations, the GCAR module applies SE-based refinement to emphasize globally important channels. The model is trained using the AdamW optimizer, with an initial learning rate of 0.0001, and cross-entropy loss, alongside a cosine annealing scheduler. Early stopping and model checkpointing are employed to mitigate overfitting based on validation loss. The model undergoes training for 50 epochs with a batch size of 32 on an NVIDIA GPU. During training, performance is evaluated continuously using accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score metrics, ensuring the effective learning of both local and global features.

Dataset description

The Black Gram Plant Leaf Disease (BPLD) dataset from Mendeley Data49 is used in this work to classify leaf diseases into five classes: anthracnose, healthy, leaf crinkle, powdery mildew, and yellow mosaic. The images were either collected under natural field situations or sourced from agricultural research collections, ensuring a diverse range of visual variety. Each category captures distinct disease-specific visual patterns under a variety of lighting and environmental circumstances. Images were acquired during field trips in the coastal Andhra region, Nagayalanka, Krishna District, Andhra Pradesh, between January and April 2020 and 2021. The original data collectors used a SONY CYBER-SHOT DSC-H300 camera and Samsung mobile phone cameras to gather high-resolution photographs in natural daylight situations. The repository contains 1,000 real-world leaf images, including 230 images of Anthracnose, 220 healthy leaves, 150 leaf crinkle, 180 powdery mildew, and 220 yellow mosaic samples. The dataset has a total size of about 115 MB.

Standard preprocessing approaches were applied to ensure consistency and improve the model’s generalization ability. The original RGB images had varying dimensions due to the use of different devices. Each image was examined to check for square dimensions. Images that were not square were cropped using a cropping tool to focus on the whole leaf region, ensuring square dimensions. All squared images were rescaled to 512 × 512 pixels to standardize input size.

In this study, 15 data augmentation approaches were used, including rotation, lighting correction, mirror symmetry (flipping), noise injection, and shifting/translation to increase dataset diversity and reduce class imbalance. These preprocessing and augmentation processes ensured that the model could reliably learn disease patterns from a realistic and diverse sample. Finally, the dataset was split into 85% training and 15% testing sets, with 10% of the training set kept as a validation set for hyperparameter tuning and overfitting prevention.

Model interpretability and visualization on test samples

Discussion

The ConViTSE model has shown outstanding performance in black gram leaf disease classification, achieving high accuracy, which is higher than its performance on rice, corn, and wheat. This difference is largely due to the nature of black gram leaf diseases, which typically display clear, well-defined symptoms such as distinct lesions, color changes, and texture variations. These features make it easier for the model to recognize and classify diseases accurately. In contrast, rice, wheat, and corn diseases often exhibit similar visual symptoms across different classes, leading to more frequent misclassifications. Factors such as overlapping leaves, varying lighting conditions, and background noise further contribute to the challenges in these datasets. Additionally, black gram leaves have a more consistent structure, making feature extraction easier, whereas wheat and corn leaves tend to have irregular shapes and complex textures, which can interfere with accurate classification. In comparison with leading deep learning models, ConViTSE demonstrates significant improvements. Traditional CNN-based models like VGG19 and ResNet-50 are good at extracting spatial features, but they struggle with capturing long-range dependencies, making them less effective for disease patterns that spread across the leaf. In contrast, transformer-based models for vision tasks, such as the ViT and its hierarchical variation, may capture global dependencies but frequently struggle to keep fine-grained disease features, which may affect classification accuracy. ConViTSE addresses these challenges by combining the strengths of both approaches. Dual-Stage Feature Refinement module comprising LCAR and GCAR to enhance the model’s focus at different hierarchical levels. This hybrid learning approach ensures that the model remains both accurate and computationally efficient, allowing it to outperform conventional architectures. Moreover, despite the complexities of visual symptoms in rice, wheat, and corn, ConViTSE maintains competitive accuracy, demonstrating its generalization capacity across a wide range of leaf morphologies and diseases. Its scalable form and low computing overhead make it suited for real-world applications, such as mobile and edge-based smart agricultural systems. ConViTSE is lightweight, with only 0.756 million parameters and a tiny model size of 2.89 MB, allowing for quick training and inference. This makes it practical for implementation on mobile devices or edge hardware, allowing farmers and agricultural workers to do real-time disease identification out in the field. As a result, ConViTSE not only outperforms current benchmarks in terms of performance, but also paves the way for scalable and feasible plant disease monitoring solutions.

Conclusion

ConViTSE, a hybrid deep learning model, is proposed for the precise identification of black gram leaf diseases. The combination of ConvMixer, ViT, and the LCAR and GCAR attention modules enables the model to extract detailed local features while preserving global contextual information. ConvMixer captures local patterns like textures and edges, whereas ViT models spatial relationships across the image. The SE based attention modules LCAR and GCAR improve class discrimination by increasing important features at both the local and global levels. The model showed excelling classification performance, obtaining 99.30% accuracy on black gram disease images, as well as high generalization with 98.75%, 98.20%, and 95% accuracy on rice, maize, and wheat datasets, respectively. These findings demonstrate ConViTSE’s robustness and adaptability across a variety of crop types. ConViTSE, with its lightweight architecture and dual-stage attention refinement, provides a potential option for precise and efficient crop disease monitoring, particularly in resource constrained situations like mobile and edge-based smart farming systems. While ConViTSE achieves a good balance between accuracy and computational efficiency, future improvements will focus on making the model easier to deploy in real-world farming conditions and ensuring it can accurately identify new or unseen disease variations.

Limitations and future work

ConViTSE model performs well in identifying black gram leaf diseases, although it has a few drawbacks. Being trained on a fixed set of disease types, the model is unable to recognize diseases outside the training data. Furthermore, class imbalance in the dataset, where some diseases are underrepresented, can result in worse accuracy and bias in predictions for such groups. To address these constraints, future research will concentrate on improving generalization through self-supervised learning and domain adaptation approaches, reducing class imbalance using refined loss functions or targeted augmentation, and integrating ConViTSE into a comprehensive agricultural pipeline. By providing disease identification, location, severity evaluation, and suggested treatments, this pipeline is ideal for real-time precision agriculture. In addition, ConViTSE will be used in a variety of smart farming systems in the future. Its lightweight design makes it suited for mobile apps, allowing farmers to monitor plant diseases in real time via their phones. It can also be integrated into drones or robots for large-scale field surveillance, assisting in the detection and mapping of unhealthy areas. Because of its versatility, ConViTSE is an effective tool for modern, precision agriculture.
