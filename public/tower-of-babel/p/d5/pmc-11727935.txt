Primary treatment failure in acute retinal necrosis – A comprehensive review
Authors: Ankush Kawali, Aayesha Khanum, Sai B Mishra, Srinivasan Sanjay, Padmamalini Mahendradas
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Acute retinal necrosis (ARN) is a sight-threatening ophthalmic emergency that requires aggressive treatment to prevent irreversible vision loss. However, primary treatment failure (PTF), where the initial antiviral therapy is ineffective, is a common challenge in ARN management. This comprehensive review examines PTF in ARN, drawing insights from a systematic literature search spanning 1997 to 2022, which identified 35 relevant cases from 23 reports. The analysis focuses on drug resistance patterns, alternative antiviral agents, combination therapies, and emerging treatments. Potential causes of drug resistance, including host factors, viral mutations, and drug-related considerations, are elucidated. Alternative agents such as foscarnet, cidofovir, and brivudine, as well as combination approaches involving systemic and intravitreal administration, high-dose therapy, judicious use of steroids, and potential treatment-related complications, are discussed. The review also highlights emerging therapies, including vaccines, monoclonal antibodies, and natural compounds, offering insights into future directions for addressing this challenging condition.

Acute retinal necrosis (ARN) is a rare but serious ocular condition necessitating urgent medical attention, with incidences ranging from 0.50 to 0.63 cases per million people according to population-based studies.[12] It manifests as retinal necrosis foci with defined borders in the peripheral retina, rapidly advancing circumferentially, accompanied by retinal vasculitis, optic neuritis, and anterior uveitis.[3] Without prompt and aggressive intervention, irreversible vision loss is likely. ARN is primarily attributed to herpesviruses, such as herpes simplex virus (HSV) and varicella-zoster virus (VZV), although other causative organisms such as Cytomegalovirus (CMV), Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), Toxoplasma, and Syphilis have been reported.[4567] According to Standardized Uveitis Nomenclature II, the use of the term “ARN” is restricted to etiological agents such as HSV and VZV only.[8]

ARN poses a significant challenge in its management, especially when faced with issues of drug resistance or inadequate dosing. Antiviral agents, notably acyclovir, play a pivotal role, offering prompt responses when administered orally or parenterally. However, the emergence of drug-resistant strains complicates treatment efficacy, influenced by various factors such as host factors, viral characteristics, and drug-related issues. While existing literature has explored ARN management strategies, a comprehensive study elucidating specific aspects of drug resistance and inadequate therapy in ARN treatment remains notably absent. This review delves into cases unresponsive to primary treatment, discussing drug resistance patterns, alternative antiviral therapies, combination strategies, and the role of various antiviral agents.

Literature Search

An online literature search for articles published from 1997 to 2022 was conducted on PubMed, Google Scholar, and MEDLINE by using the MESH terms “acute retinal necrosis,” “herpes,” “herpetic,” “acyclovir,” “aciclovir,” “valacyclovir,” “valaciclovir,” “resistance,” ‘”refractory,” “atypical,” “treatment failure,” “varicella zoster,” “Herpes simplex” with interposition of Boolean operators “AND” and “OR.” The references of the articles were searched for missed references. Articles reporting clear clinical descriptions of the case(s) were included. All articles with proven diagnoses of ARN either with molecular diagnostics from ocular fluid, on culture, or with typical clinical features and where therapeutic responses to antivirals were observed were included. All the cases in which primary drug failure was documented by progression of retinitis and subsequent dose modification or change of the antiviral agent were studied. Such cases were defined as “primary treatment failure” (PTF) for this study. Case series or larger studies with insufficient treatment descriptions were excluded. We excluded the reports of ARN where an organism other than VZV and HSV was isolated. Cases of progressive outer retinal necrosis (PORN) were also excluded.

The study parameters included demographics, clinical presentation, initial treatment, and its duration, details of treatment change, and complications.

Results

In this comprehensive study, 35 eligible cases from 23 reports spanning 1997 to 2022 were analyzed [Table 1]. The average age of presentation was 40 years, ranging from 10 to 90 years, with 20% of patients below 18 years. Among the cases, 21 were male and 14 were female, while 14% were immunocompromised. Nine cases exhibited bilateral involvement.

All cases displayed typical morphological features of ARN. Notably, eight cases showed posterior pole involvement with retinitis lesions, 17 had retinal vasculitis (one with central retinal artery occlusion),[28] and 13 presented with papillitis. In eight cases, clinical descriptions were unavailable. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) confirmed the diagnosis in 28 cases, viral isolation on culture was achieved in one,[10] and in the remaining cases, the diagnosis was based on the clinical presentation and therapeutic response to antivirals. VZV-ARN was diagnosed in 19 cases, and HSV-ARN in 11 cases (9 HSV-2).

Primary treatment involved intravenous (IV) acyclovir, followed by oral acyclovir or valacyclovir in all cases except three. Dosages for IV administration ranged from 10 to 15 mg/kg three times per day (TID), administered for 6 days to 2 weeks. Various combinations of IV antivirals were employed. A combination of IV acyclovir and foscarnet was advocated by Chau Tran et al.,[14] whereas IV acyclovir was combined with ganciclovir by Choi et al.[24] IV acyclovir and intravitreal foscarnet or intravitreal ganciclovir were preferred by others.[13212328] The three cases that did not receive IV acyclovir as first-line therapy were treated with oral valacyclovir, famciclovir, or valgancyclovir. The period before considering PTF was mentioned in 25 cases and was around a week (range: 2–20 days) [Table 1].

As a second-line treatment, IV foscarnet was the preferred choice for most authors (15 cases). A combination of IV foscarnet and ganciclovir,[28] or acyclovir,[11] as well as gamma interferon was considered by a few authors.[17] Stryjewski et al.[21] in their case had escalated the foscarnet dose and had given two doses of IV cidofovir (5 mg/kg) 1 week apart. Intravitreal ganciclovir was most commonly used (n = 18), followed by foscarnet (n = 11) along with the systemic medications mentioned above. Intravitreal ganciclovir along with foscarnet simultaneously was also given by a few authors.[2816] Zambarakji et al.[13] advocated an aggressive approach with IV cidofovir, IV ganciclovir, and intravitreal ganciclovir to their patient who showed poor response to IV acyclovir and intravitreal foscarnet. Most of the authors switched over to oral acyclovir, valacyclovir, or famciclovir once the retinitis lesions showed improvement with the above intravenous and/or intravitreal therapy for PTF ARN. Oral antiviral after consideration of PTF was advocated by Figueroa et al.[9] with famciclovir, and by Vij et al.[15] with a novel antiviral drug brivudine.[15] Cases were also managed without change of systemic antiviral drugs but with additional intravitreal injections[11181922282930] [Table 1].

High-dose therapy of certain drugs was given by a few authors. Chau Tran et al.[14] advocated IV ganciclovir 10 mg/kg/day (normal dose: 5 mg/kg) in a patient who failed for combination therapy of IV acyclovir and foscarnet. Senter et al. administered oral valacyclovir 2 g every 5 hours, a total of 10 g/day in a patient who showed progressive disease with IV acyclovir 13–15 mg/kg.[27] Stryjewski et al.[21] treated their patient, who did not respond to IV acyclovir 10 mg/kg TID, with IV foscarnet 60 mg/kg TID for 3 weeks, then once a day for 2 months, followed by life-long famciclovir 500 mg twice a day (BD). Intravitreal high-dose ganciclovir (4 mg twice a week) along with oral famciclovir was given by Mason et al.[30]

Retinal detachment occurred in 16 cases, and drug-related complications were observed in two instances: transient transaminase elevation due to cidofovir,[21] and acute renal failure after a single dose of foscarnet.[19] No ARN relapses were reported, but anterior uveitis relapses were noted in two cases. Some cases had a history of old herpes infections, including herpetic keratitis (n = 2)[2116] and herpetic encephalitis (n = 2),[2528] and a case had a history of fellow eye ARN 8 years ago.[14]

Our study included HSV and VZV ARN, and the possibility of mixed infection with other human herpes viruses remains, which was not investigated by many authors, contributing to the limitation of the study.

The trend of reports of refractory ARN

Antiherpetic treatments are effective; however, the emergence of drug-resistant variants remains a major concern, especially in individuals on long-term therapy. Acyclovir-resistant mutants have been isolated after prolonged treatment with acyclovir,[32] especially in severely immunocompromised patients such as organ transplants, bone marrow transplants as well as human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)-infected individuals.[33] In hematological patients (hematopoietic stem cell transplantation for hematological malignancies), drug-resistant mutant VZV was found in 27% of patients with persistent infection, including patients who progressed to retinal and cerebral complications.[34] Documentation of acyclovir resistance microbiologically or by genomic sequencing is known as true acyclovir resistance, which may not be possible in all cases. Andrei et al.[35] noted that only 30% of clinically resistant (non-ocular) VZV infections have true acyclovir resistance. Hence, the occurrence of persistent lesions or the appearance of new lesions after 10 days of acyclovir therapy is considered as acyclovir-resistant infection.[36] A change of the antiviral drug or increase in the dose of the antiviral agent is generally considered in such situations. Some authors even recommended early consideration of alternative antivirals if the retinitis does not respond promptly (within 3–5 days).[16] Thus, for practical purposes, we studied the published literature on ARN where the treating ophthalmologists considered changing the drug or a change in the dosage of the therapeutic agent. For the present study, we termed such cases as “PTF.” We reviewed 35 reports of PTF in 25 years of timeframe. Comparing 1st half (1997–2010) and the 2nd half (2011–2022) of this timeline, no increase in the number of cases was noted. Tam et al.[37] have also noted that the incidence of acyclovir-resistant HSV strains among immunocompetent as well as in immunocompromised patients has remained low despite widespread use of acyclovir, ranging from 0 to 0.6% and 5%, respectively. Improved management of HIV patients and bone marrow or organ transplant recipients and increased vaccination have greatly reduced long-term dependency on antiherpetic drugs.[38] Despite this, the possibility of development of drug resistance remains high, especially in patients on long-term therapy; hence, great caution should be exercised in those patients, and early second-line drugs should be considered if resistance is suspected.

Clinical features

Peripheral necrotic placoid retinitis lesions rapidly progressing in a circumferential manner associated with moderate-to-severe vitritis in immunocompetent patients is a typical clinical presentation of ARN. Optic nerve involvement, vasculitis, and rarely choroiditis can be seen in ARN.[39] In this review, we found that the atypical clinical presentation was rarely reported, posterior pole involvement with retinitis lesions was mentioned in a few cases (n = 8), and the lesions appeared in due course of the disease. Retinal vasculitis was prevalent in this series. Wang et al.[40] reported a case in which the patient had a subretinal lesion in the affected eye and the fellow eye demonstrated chorioretinal scars along the superior arcade. HSV-2 was isolated, and the patient responded to intravitreal foscarnet and a second course of intravenous acyclovir injections. Systemic involvement in the form of encephalitis with isolation of HSV-2 in the brain biopsy and a case of associated Terson syndrome was also reported by a few authors.[2526]

Etiology of ARN

The diagnosis of ARN has historically been based on the typical morphological characteristics. The identification of causative agents has played a crucial role in understanding the spectrum of pathogens associated with ARN. Traditionally, HSV and VZV have been recognized as primary culprits. However, as diagnostic techniques evolved, a broader range of infectious agents has been implicated in ARN. In addition to HSV and VZV, CMV, Toxoplasma, Treponema pallidum, and EBV have been identified in association with the characteristic morphological presentation of ARN.[6741] The year 2021 saw a significant development in the classification criteria for uveitis with the release of Standardization of Uveitis Nomenclature (SUN II). According to these revised criteria, the isolation of VZV and HSV alone, in the context of the characteristic morphological features of necrotizing retinitis, would meet the criteria for diagnosing ARN.[8] Thus, for the present review, we excluded cases of ARN where an organism other than HSV or VZV was associated with the disease. Practically, it is not possible to perform the PCR tests in all cases, and the possibility of false negative results remains a concern. The clinical judgment and the proven treatment response may still prompt the HSV and VZV etiology. Hence, we also included the reported cases of ARN with PTF where PCR was not done or was negative (n = 6).

Primary treatment

Acyclovir is the antiviral drug of choice in patients with ARN. Intravenous acyclovir (1500 mg/m2/day or 10 mg/kg in three divided doses) for 5–10 days followed by oral acyclovir (800 mg 5 times a day) therapy for several weeks until resolution (generally 2–6 weeks) is the standard-of-care treatment. Acyclovir requires phosphorylation by viral thymidine kinase (TK) as well as cellular enzymes before it can inhibit viral DNA polymerase and its action is specific to certain viruses. HSV and VZV-TK effectively phosphorylate acyclovir, whereas CMV-TK has a low affinity toward acyclovir. Thus, acyclovir is effective against HSV and VZV but not against CMV. Comparing its action against VZV and HSV, it is approximately 10-fold less active against VZV than HSV.[21]

For indolent ARN, oral valacyclovir (1 g TID) or oral famciclovir (500 mg TID) has also shown beneficial results, without initial intravenous acyclovir therapy.[42] Various other reports have also confirmed the efficacy of oral valacyclovir and famcyclovir-only therapy in ARN.[43] Both systemic and intravitreal antiviral agents as primary therapy have also been advocated by a few authors and noted to render better visual outcomes.[43] Yeh et al.[44] noted that this combination therapy may have an advantage over systemic antiviral therapy alone. In our series, almost all the cases received intravenous acyclovir as a first-line drug. Some authors preferred combination therapy with intravenous or intravitreal foscarnet along with IV acyclovir as the primary therapy.[131421]

By definition, the appearance of new lesions after 10 days of acyclovir therapy is considered an acyclovir-resistant infection. As noted in our series, the threshold to consider a change in the primary therapy ranged from 2 days to 20 days, but 8 days on average. Early initiation of the therapy is of paramount importance in ARN cases to prevent the progression of retinitis, decrease the rate of complications, and prevent fellow eye involvement, which is reportedly seen in 3%–15% of cases despite IV acyclovir or oral valacyclovir therapy.[21] Aggressive primary management with combination therapy was advocated by Chau Tran et al.[14] given severe necrotizing retinitis, monocular status, and history of previous prophylactic acyclovir therapy. It is important to note that there is no single guideline for first-line therapy for ARN, and the treatment approach may vary but should be started immediately without waiting for laboratory results.[45]

Overview of Acyclovir Resistance

Despite aggressive primary treatment, the retinitis may progress due to the resistance of the virus. The emergence of acyclovir resistance in VZV infections poses challenges, particularly in immunocompromised hosts, leading to severe and persistent infections. Immunocompromised patients with ARN may require prolonged courses of antiviral treatment. This prolonged exposure to acyclovir can increase the likelihood of resistance development over time. Chronic use of acyclovir has been shown to develop resistant strains of HSV.[46] Thus, in patients with a history of previous herpetic infections and prolonged acyclovir therapy, a high index of drug resistance and treatment failure should be suspected. Despite three decades of acyclovir use, drug resistance has remained low among immunocompetent individuals (0.6%).[3747] However, in acyclovir-treated immunocompromised patients, the prevalence was approximately 5%.[37] Resistance can occur due to mutations in the thymidine kinase (TK) or DNA polymerase genes (UL23 and UL30 genes in HSV, and ORF28 and ORF36 in VZV), with TK mutants being the most frequent.[28] HSV strains resistant to acyclovir due to absent or altered TK were isolated in 4.7% of immunocompromised individuals but none from immunocompetent hosts.[48] In allogeneic bone marrow transplant patients, the resistance rate was as high as 30%, again associated with mutations in viral TK or DNA polymerase.[37] TK mutations are either insertion or deletion (codons 92 and 146 of TK gene) or substitution (codon 176-177, 336 of TK gene), whereas DNA polymerase mutations are located mainly in conserved sites of the enzyme. Gene polymorphism, especially for TK, has also been reported.[49] HSV DNA polymerases have a higher affinity for acyclovir than for penciclovir, whereas HSV TK has a higher affinity for penciclovir than for acyclovir.[50] Genotypical documentation of resistance in ARN cases has been rarely reported.[2028] Dokey et al.[20] in their case, isolated HSV-2 resistant to both acyclovir as well as ganciclovir. Van Velzen et al.[51] in their series of 10 patients with HSV-uveitis (2 ARN cases) showed cross resistance to ganciclovir in three out of four cases. Acyclovir-resistant VZV strains were believed to be less common,[52] although Aguado et al.[10] in their report described an HIV-positive patient with bilateral ARN, which relentlessly progressed to no perception of light. Acyclovir-resistant VZV was isolated from cerebrospinal fluid by virus plaque reduction assay (IDso, 150 µM) in their case. Documentation of acyclovir resistance by using genetic analysis or isolation on culture is not always possible. Clinicians consider switching to a different antiviral agent if the initial response to acyclovir is inadequate. Although the resistance to acyclovir should be considered as resistance to its pro-drugs such as valacyclovir and famciclovir and as cross-resistance to gancyclovir, some authors reported cases that failed to respond to acyclovir but responded to oral famciclovir,[9] high doses of valacyclovir,[27] and ganciclovir.[11]

High-dose therapy

Successful ARN treatment depends not only on the choice of antiviral agent but also on the dosing strategy employed. Suboptimal dosing can result in inadequate drug concentrations at the site of infection and foster the emergence of drug-resistant viral strains.

Intravenous administration of acyclovir (10 mg/kg) achieves vitreous levels of approximately 25% of plasma.[21] Continuous infusion of high-dose acyclovir (30–50 mg/kg/day) in hematopoietic cell transplant patients has been reported.[53] Valacylovir, a pro drug of acyclovir, has good bioavailability after oral administration. It offers an oral bioavailability of 54.2%, which is approximately 4.5 times higher than oral acyclovir.[54] Valacyclovir 1 g TID dose can produce an area under the curve (AUC) similar to IV aciclovir at the dosage of 5 mg/kg TID. Pilot clinical studies demonstrated that a higher dosage of oral valaciclovir (2 g four times daily) could produce the daily AUC of 109 μg h, which was close to the one obtained by IV aciclovir at the dosage of 10 mg/kg TID (AUC of 107 µg h).[54] Same was supported by Weller et al. as well.[55] Other clinical studies have used the dose of 2 g TID and shown favorable clinical outcomes.[565758] Senter et al.[27] reported that a case failed to respond to IV acyclovir (15 mg/kg) and responded to oral valacyclovir 2 g given every 5 hours, a total of 10 g/day without any adverse effects. However, high dose of valacyclovir or acyclovir can cause serious side effects. Apart from gasto-intestinal disturbance, nephro and neurotoxicity are of concern. Moreover, acyclovir-induced neuropsychiatric symptoms can result from high serum concentrations of acyclovir as well as its metabolite 9-carboxymethoxymethylguanine.[59] High-dose oral valacyclovir may be considered in cases of non-responsive ARN to the standard acyclovir therapy, but patients should be monitored closely.

Alternative Antiviral Agents

Recognizing the limitations of acyclovir/valacyclovir in the treatment of herpetic infections, alternative antiviral agents have been explored. These alternative medications are typically reserved for cases where standard antiviral therapy proves insufficient and should be considered if the retinitis does not respond in a timely manner within 3–5 days.[16] The selection of alternative systemic medications in ARN should be individualized based on clinical circumstances, including the causative viral agent, drug tolerability, and potential resistance patterns.

Pencyclovir is phosphorylated to its monophosphate form by TK present in cells infected with HSV-1, HSV-2, and VZV. Furthermore, the penciclovir triphosphate converted by the host cellular enzyme inhibits the herpes virus DNA polymerase terminating viral replication. Penciclovir has a higher affinity for the viral TK and a lower affinity for the HSV DNA polymerase than acyclovir.[60] It suppresses VZV infection more effectively compared to acyclovir. It is also unable to be phosphorylated in uninfected cells, thus offering a selective action toward viral infected cells. Although penciclovir resembles acyclovir in its chemical nature, mechanism of action, and spectrum of antiviral activity, due to its poor absorption, a prodrug famciclovir is used which is converted to penciclovir in the liver. Experimental studies suggested that famciclovir is better than valacyclovir in terminating ganglionic HSV infection.[60] After oral administration of famciclovir, the mean intravitreal concentration of penciclovir was found to be more than that required to inhibit growth in cell culture by 50% for sensitive HSV-1, HSV-2, and VZV.[58] The absolute bioavailability for famciclovir is 77% compared to 54% and 21% of valacyclovir and acyclovir, respectively. Although famciclovir and valacyclovir were equally effective in limiting the virulence and spread of HSV-1, in animal model HSV reactivation was noted only in 56% of famciclovir-treated mice, compared with 70% of valacyclovir-treated mice.[61] Figueroa et al.[9] reported a case of ARN that initially showed response to IV acyclovir but worsened on the 15th day of oral acyclovir. The patient showed improvement within a week of oral famciclovir 500 mg TID. In our experience, we have also seen a case of VZV-ARN that showed poor response to IV acyclovir followed by oral valacyclovir. The patient developed other eye involvement while on valacyclovir 1 g TID but showed prompt response to high-dose oral famciclovir (500 mg 2 TID).

Foscarnet, a DNA polymerase inhibitor, is not dependent on TK for its action, offers effectiveness against resistant strains, and has demonstrated success in treating various herpes viruses infections, particularly HSV and VZV. Animal models of IV foscarnet at 120 mg/kg have been shown to achieve 50% inhibitory concentration in the vitreous.[61] Intravenous foscarnet achieves vitreous levels which are 140% of plasma levels, much higher compared to acyclovir which achieves vitreous levels of 25% of plasma.[21] Intravenous foscarnet also results in 10-fold higher concentrations in the retina than in the vitreous. A comparative study reported better visual outcomes with IV foscarnet compared to IV acyclovir in immunocompromised patients with PORN.[62] In adults, IV foscarnet is given at a dose of 40–60 mg/kg TID and has been used successfully not only in HSV-1 and 2-resistant ARN but also in ARN due to EBV.[463] It is recommended to administer 750–1000 mL of normal saline or 5% dextrose solution before foscarnet infusion to avoid nephrotoxicity.[64] Most of the cases in this series received IV foscarnet after considering PTF. Duration varied from case to case and in some situations lasted for 6–8 weeks.[2116] No serious side effects related to the treatment were seen, except in one patient who developed acute renal failure after a single dose of IV foscarnet 9 g/day.[19] No risk factors were identified, but the patient had also received IV acyclovir 750 mg/day for 7 days prior to foscarnet injection. A renal patient was safely administered foscarnet at a dose of 45 mg/kg after hemodialysis for 3 weeks.[21] The use of foscarnet requires careful consideration due to potential nephrotoxicity and electrolyte imbalances associated with its administration. Foscarnet has been used as salvage therapy for acyclovir-resistant VZV infections, but mutations in the VZV DNA polymerase associated with foscarnet resistance have also been reported.[21] Rarely ARN can be resistant to both acyclovir and foscarnet but are usually still susceptible to other antiviral agents such as cidofovir.

Cidofovir is an acyclic nucleoside phosphonate that has a broad-spectrum activity against a variety of DNA viruses, including herpes viruses. Like foscarnet, it acts by inhibiting viral DNA polymerase and is independent of thymidine kinase. It can be used against foscarnet-resistant herpes viruses with a mutation in DNA polymerase. It is given in weekly doses for induction therapy and biweekly dosing for maintenance therapy at 5 mg/kg body weight.[13] It is nephrotoxic and should be given with probenecid and with adequate hydration. Its efficacy in refractory ARN has been reported.[2113] Stryjewski et al.[21] described a case of recurrent HSV-ARN; initial attacks responded to IV acyclovir and valacyclovir and was then put on antiviral prophylaxis for 6 years with oral valacyclovir and famciclovir. Patient’s 3rd attack showed no response to acyclovir as well as to foscarnet but responded to two doses of intravenous cidofovir. Similarly, Zambarakji et al.’s[13] case also showed poor response to acyclovir and foscarnet therapy but responded to intravenous cidofovir (5 mg/kg weekly) and ganciclovir (5 mg/kg twice daily) therapy. IV Cidofovir was continued fortnightly for a further 2 months. No adverse effects were noted in Zambarakji et al.’s case, whereas in Stryjewski et al.’s case,[21] transient transaminase elevation was noted, which made the authors to restrict the treatment to two doses only. Although the use of cidofovir can be an effective alternative to foscarnet in the treatment of resistant ARN, one must remember that cidofovir itself is also known to cause uveitis and hypotony.[65]

Brivudine (500 mg/day orally), a thymidine analog, inhibits viral DNA polymerases, curbing viral replication. It is vastly superior to acyclovir against VZV, with notably higher oral bioavailability. Tolerability matches acyclovir, though occasional GI issues may occur. Vij et al. demonstrated its efficacy in VZV ARN cases unresponsive to IV acyclovir.[15] However, caution is warranted due to the potential lethal interaction with 5-fluorouracil (FU).[35]

Sorivudine (40 mg/day orally), another VZV antagonist, offers once-daily dosing convenience and outperforms acyclovir in cutaneous herpes infections.[66] Both successful as well as unsuccessful use of sorivudine has been reported in the treatment of PORN.[6768] Despite its promise, its association with toxic 5-FU plasma levels led to fatalities in Japan, limiting its usage.[69]

Amenamevir (400 mg/day orally), a helicase-primase inhibitor, exhibits broad-spectrum activity against VZV and HSV with once-daily dosing.[70] Despite its discontinuation in the US due to toxicity concerns, Japanese studies confirm its efficacy and tolerability in herpes zoster.[71] Helicase-primase inhibitors hold the potential for managing resistant VZV strains.

Promising new antivirals such as valnivudine, valomaciclovir, and brincidofovir are in development. FV-100, a valnivudine pro-drug, showed efficacy in reducing VZV infection and pain relief.[71] Valomaciclovir, effective against EBV, demonstrated comparable efficacy to valacyclovir in VZV infection.[71] Research on valnivudine hydrochloride and valomaciclovir underscores their effectiveness in herpes zoster treatment. Brincidofovir shows promise against resistant VZV strains, particularly in transplant recipients, addressing drug resistance and enhancing outcomes in ARN treatment.

Combination Therapies (Intravitreal and Systemic Antivirals)

Recent studies highlight that combination therapy enhances visual acuity and reduces retinal detachment risk. This approach, utilized by various authors, was compared in a meta-analysis.[43] Even though the results were statistically insignificant, a preference for combination therapy was noted in severe cases. Typically, intravitreal ganciclovir or foscarnet, along with systemic acyclovir or valacyclovir, are common choices, although there are reports of intravitreal acyclovir lavage.[72] Intravitreal foscarnet and ganciclovir usage is supported in resistant cases, as seen in Luu et al.’s study.[11] The synergistic effect of this combination makes it possible to simultaneously inject both drugs into the vitreous cavity to impede the progression of retinitis.[73] A comparative study by Yeh et al.[44] involved 12 patients receiving combination therapy (systemic antiviral and intravitreal foscarnet) and 12 receiving systemic therapy alone. The conclusion suggested improved visual outcomes and decreased retinal detachment risks with the combination therapy. Similar outcomes were reported by Flaxel et al.[74] Yet another study concluded that the combination of intravitreal foscarnet and systemic antiviral therapy may have greater therapeutic efficacy than systemic therapy alone, especially in resistant herpes virus strains.[75] Although intravitreal foscarnet and ganciclovir, in combination with systemic antiviral therapy, appear promising, PTF with such combination therapy has been reported. In our series, most of the authors initially opted for systemic antiviral therapy and later added intravitreal drugs when PTF was suspected. Chau Tran et al.[14] in one of their patients (a 13-year-old girl) reported favorable outcomes with aggressive combined primary treatment with IV foscarnet and IV acyclovir along with intravitreal ganciclovir, and the resolution was achieved on day 4, whereas others reported progression of retinitis even with combined therapy.[132130] Stryjewski et al.[21] described a case where intravitreal foscarnet was given along with IV acyclovir as the primary treatment but progression of retinitis was noted. Switching over to IV foscarnet showed rapid resolution. Similarly, Zambarakji et al.[13] reported a case of PTF with IV acyclovir and intravitreal foscarnet which responded only to IV cidofovir, ganciclovir, and intravitreal ganciclovir. Manson et al.[30] reported two cases of HSV-ARN in young immunocompetent individuals. The first case was treated with a combination of high-dose valacyclovir (2 g three times daily) and intravitreal ganciclovir (2 mg twice weekly), while the second case received oral famciclovir (500 mg three times daily) along with high-dose intravitreal ganciclovir (4 mg twice weekly). In both cases, retinitis progressed despite the combination therapy. Authors suspected a resistant variant of HSV-2 that responded to additional intravitreal foscarnet therapy.

Although ganciclovir has been used as combination therapy in the treatment of ARN, there are few reports where oral valganciclovir was initiated as the primary treatment for VZV-ARN. Savant et al. reported favorable outcomes, whereas Vasudevan et al. noted treatment failure in their case.[3176]

Judicious Use of Steroids in ARN

The role of steroids in managing ARN is crucial and must be carefully administered. Shantha et al.[61] reported improved visual outcomes in HSV-associated ARN patients who received corticosteroids within 72 hours of systemic antiviral treatment, compared to delayed or no administration. Wong et al.[41] noted better preservation of visual acuity at 6 months with combined corticosteroids and antiviral agents, contrasting with antiviral therapy alone. Conversely, Dorman and Donaldson[77] found no significant difference in retinal detachment or severe vision loss risk between patients receiving systemic corticosteroids and those who did not. While steroids mitigate inflammation and immune response, caution is necessary due to risks such as exacerbating viral replication, especially with drug-resistant strains. Misdiagnosis and early steroid use may lead to PTF. High-dose and long-acting intravitreal steroids should be avoided initially; instead, short-acting intravitreal dexamethasone alongside antiviral injections can be given. In addition, intravitreal triamcinolone, administered under antiviral cover, may complement systemic steroids in ARN treatment, ideally injected 1–2 weeks post initiation of antiviral therapy.[58]

Future Perspectives and Emerging Therapies

In the realm of future perspectives and emerging therapies, researchers and clinicians are exploring innovative strategies to address the challenges posed by severe viral retinal infections. Vaccination stands out as a pivotal tool for both treatment and prevention, particularly against VZV-induced herpes infections, offering efficacy in reducing their incidence and enhancing cell-mediated immunity.[7879] However, rare adverse effects, acyclovir resistance, and cases of VZV vaccine strains causing ARN underscore the need for vigilance and further research.[7879]

While vaccines play a significant role, the demand for antiviral drugs remains, highlighting the necessity of a comprehensive therapeutic approach. Monoclonal antibody therapy targeting HSV glycoprotein-B shows promise, particularly for drug-resistant HSV infections.[80] Varicella-zoster immune globulin serves as post-exposure prophylaxis and treatment and can be recommended for high-risk individuals.[81] Meanwhile, researchers are exploring novel treatments using natural substances and innovative strategies such as lethal mutagenesis and Janus-type nucleosides, offering potential new avenues for combating herpes viruses.[82]

In conclusion, the management of ARN requires a comprehensive and prompt approach, with immediate treatment without waiting for laboratory results. The choice of antiviral agent and dosing strategy are crucial. This review suggests an aggressive initial approach, including combination therapy and high-dose antiviral therapy to avoid PTF. In resource-limited settings, increasing the doses of available drugs may be beneficial. Alternative antiviral agents should be explored when standard therapy proves insufficient, although there is no single guideline for the choice of alternative drugs. The role of steroids is critical but must be administered carefully to avoid complications. Continued research into emerging therapies and protocol refinement is essential for effectively managing this sight-threatening condition.
