ZIF-8-based chemiresistive CO2 sensor: fabrication, performance evaluation, and comparison with NDIR and colorimetric methods
Authors: Natraj Athreya A. S., S. Fouziya Sulthana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Carbon dioxide (CO2) is a gas prevalent in the environment, but at higher concentrations can be highly toxic or even lethal. This work studies the fabrication and evaluation of chemiresistive sensing of carbon dioxide using ZIF-8 and compares it against a conventional NDIR-based SCD40 sensor and a ZIF-8-based colorimetric sensor. ZIF-8 was chosen due to its high porosity, and selective adsorption of CO2 at room temperature, while the conventional chemiresistive sensors used metal oxides, which require higher temperatures for accurate sensing. ZIF-8 was fabricated using the solvothermal method, drop-cast on a custom etched ITO glass substrate, connecting the two portions of the ITO with the ZIF-8 layer electrically and the sensor was calibrated using the NDIR-based SCD40 sensor (400–1600 ppm) with varying CO2 values. The calibrated sensor was evaluated for sensing behaviour against SCD40 and the colorimetric sensor across various CO2 values and environments. The measured resistance response was subjected to curve fitting using calibration data, and the resulting model was implemented on the ESP32 microcontroller to convert resistance values into corresponding CO2 concentrations (ppm). This work provides a practical, scalable, energy-efficient alternative for environmental monitoring, industrial safety, and healthcare applications.

Introduction

The need for precise and low-cost carbon dioxide (CO2) sensing has increased exponentially due to its profound implications for environmental regulation, industrial safety, and human health, especially within indoor environments.1–3 Accurate detection of CO2 levels is vital for monitoring indoor air quality, given its impact on inhabitants' health and the pervasive need for environmental oversight.4 The research trend on advanced CO2 sensors is crucial across various sectors, including food packaging, emissions monitoring, and chemical safety, highlighting a significant market demand.5 This makes the development of low-cost, high-fidelity CO2 sensors that overcome the limitations of existing technologies, such as arduous fabrication processes and high-power consumption, necessary.6 While the conventional CO2 sensing methods, such as non-dispersive infrared and electrochemical sensors, have seen significant advancements, they frequently encounter limitations regarding sensitivity, selectivity, and susceptibility to environmental interference.7

Gas sensors play a pivotal role in detecting hazardous gases like carbon dioxide, essential for preventing life-threatening situations and ensuring environmental conservation by monitoring emissions.8 The significance of CO2 sensors extends beyond mere detection, enabling precise quantification crucial for effective carbon emission reduction strategies and human safety in enclosed spaces.9 Consequently, there is a rapidly escalating demand for innovative sensing materials to enhance the performance of chemical gas sensors across diverse applications, including environmental pollution monitoring and clinical analysis.10 This demand is further driven by the need for robust and reliable CO2 sensing under varying environmental conditions, prompting the exploration of novel materials and approaches.5,11

Zeolitic Imidazolate Frameworks, particularly ZIF-8, have emerged as promising candidates for gas-sensing applications due to their unique structural properties and tunable characteristics.10 Specifically, ZIF-8, composed of zinc ions and imidazolate ligands, possesses 3.4 Å six-member pore apertures and 11.6 Å pore cavities that are highly accessible for CO2 adsorption.1 This unique architecture, characterized by a large surface area and modifiable pore chemistry, positions ZIF-8 as an ideal material for developing highly sensitive and selective gas sensors for CO2 detection at low temperatures.12,13 Furthermore, the intrinsic properties of ZIF-8, such as its thermal stability and chemical robustness, enable its integration into chemiresistive sensing platforms, offering advantages over conventional materials.14,15 Chemiresistive gas sensors, in particular, capitalize on the change in electrical resistance upon gas adsorption, providing a low-power, cost-effective, and highly sensitive means for detecting target analytes.16 However, achieving optimal selectivity and rapid response/recovery times remains a critical challenge in chemiresistive sensor development, necessitating further exploration into material design and sensor architectures.17 Such metal–organic frameworks, and especially their derivatives, offer significant potential for enhancing chemiresistive gas sensors due to their high specific surface areas, structural tunability, and diverse surface architectures, which collectively amplify resistance changes upon analyte interaction.18 The high surface area and coordinated pore structure of these emerging porous metal–organic framework materials, including ZIF-8, enable them to significantly improve the selectivity of sensors.19

Furthermore, integrating MOFs with conductive materials like graphene oxide can overcome the inherent limitation of low electrical conductivity in MOFs, improving their sensing performance.20,21 Specifically, the pyrolysis of ZIF-8 layers grown on graphene oxide can create porous carbon frameworks decorated with catalytic nanoparticles, leading to increased reaction sites for target gases and improved sensor response.22 This strategic integration of ZIF-8 with conductive substrates thus not only addresses the inherent electrical conductivity challenges of MOFs but also significantly boosts the chemiresistive sensor's performance, particularly in terms of sensitivity, selectivity, and recovery kinetics. This approach leverages the synergetic effects of ZIF-8's selective adsorption capabilities and the high electrical conductivity of carbon-based nanomaterials, ultimately leading to a more robust and efficient CO2 sensing platform.23

This study specifically aims to elucidate the intricate relationship between ZIF-8's structural attributes and its gas-sensing performance, providing a nuanced understanding of its efficacy in practical applications. It details the fabrication process of the ZIF-8 chemiresistive sensor and meticulously compares its performance characteristics, such as response time, recovery time, and sensitivity, against established non-dispersive infrared and colorimetric detection methods. This comparative analysis provides crucial insights into the advantages and potential limitations of ZIF-8-based chemiresistive sensors, thereby advancing the development of next-generation gas detection technologies. This comprehensive evaluation further highlights the potential for ZIF-8 to address long-standing challenges in chemiresistive gas sensing, particularly concerning selectivity and stability under varying environmental conditions.24,25 Recent increase in the research on the development of chemiresistive gas sensors due to the porous nanostructured materials and their tunable analyte–surface interactions. Metal Organic Framework (MOF) derived composites have demonstrated exceptional sensitivity in electrochemical sensing. Iron/titanium bimetallic oxide doped porous carbon derived from MIL-125 has been used for ultrasensitive detection of trace analytes,26 while nanoplate structured zirconium phosphate/carboxylated multiwalled carbon nanotube composites have enabled voltammetric quantification at sub-micromolar concentrations.27 Complementing MOF-derived materials, covalent organic framework (COF) nanoplatforms with precision-engineered crystalline architectures have been shown to substantially advance sensing capabilities in analyte-selective environments.28 These works collectively establish that porous, high-surface-area frameworks with functional inner pore walls are essential for achieving selectivity and sensitivity in chemical sensing.

Transduction mechanisms in low-power gas sensing platforms provide direct precedent for chemiresistive CO2 detection using ZIF-8. Ultra-low-power graphene-based impedance sensors with planar architectures have validated the viability of resistance-modulation sensing in miniaturized formats,29 while optical nanoplasmonic platforms—such as the Au–PVA–Au nanohole sandwich structure studied for humidity detection—demonstrate that analyte-induced changes in a porous host's dielectric properties translate measurably into sensor output signals.30 The chemical basis for CO2–framework interactions is further informed by nanoscale studies of CO_x reactivity, which elucidate how carbon oxides adsorb and interact with surface-active sites at the nanoscale.31 Together, these findings motivate the use of ZIF-8 as a chemiresistive CO2 sensing layer, wherein selective CO2 adsorption within its sodalite-type pores modulates the electrical resistance of an underlying transducer.

Materials and methods

In this work, ZIF-8 based chemiresistive sensor and ZIF-8 based colorimetric sensor, were fabricated to test them in the fabricated gas sensor test setup.

Synthesis of ZIF-8

The prominent material of study, ZIF-8 was synthesized using solvothermal method by dissolving zinc nitrate hexahydrate in N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) solution. Another solution was made by dissolving 2-methylimidazole (Hmim) in DMF. The latter was rapidly added to the former solution under vigorous stirring to create a homogeneous mixture, which was then transferred into a Teflon-lined stainless steel autoclave. It was then heated in an oven at 140 °C for 24 hours to induce crystallization under autogenous pressure. This precipitate was collected by centrifugation, washed with fresh DMF and methanol to remove impurities and dried in an oven at 60 °C to obtain the pure ZIF-8 powder.32–34

Fabrication of custom-etched ITO-coated glass substrate

Initially, the ITO-coated glass substrate of dimension 75 mm (length) x 25 mm (width) was cleaned with acetone, isopropyl alcohol (IPA) and deionized (DI) water. Then it was dried under a stream of nitrogen. A commercial adhesive tape was applied manually to mask the ITO layer for desired electrode regions and a narrow strip 75 mm (length) x 5 mm (width) was left exposed. The exposed ITO areas were etched off by immersing the slides into a 10% solution of HCl at room temperature. The slide was removed and quenched in DI water. The tape was removed and the slide was cleaned off using isopropanol to remove residues. This resulted in two regions of conducting ITO electrodes physically and electrically separated by a narrow gap, structured by etching.

Fabrication of ZIF-8 based chemiresistive sensor

The synthesized ZIF-8 powder was made into a paste with isopropyl alcohol and drop cast onto the patterned substrate to electrically bridge the ITO coated layers as seen on Fig. 1. Fig. 2 shows the exploded view of the layers on the glass substrate. Then the fabricated sensor was dried on a hotplate at 100 °C for an hour. Leads were connected to the ITO sections to enable electrical extension and connected to ESP32 for digital output.

Fabrication of ZIF-8 based colorimetric sensor

The ZIF-8-based colorimetric sensor was fabricated through a dye-precursor synthesis approach. Zinc nitrate hexahydrate and 2-methylimidazole were separately dissolved in methanol, and phenol red was added directly to both precursor solutions. After sonication, the imidazolate–dye solution was poured into the zinc–dye solution and allowed to react at room temperature for 48 h without stirring to form phenol red–incorporated ZIF-8 crystals. The resulting crystals were separated from the supernatant and subsequently blended with a 2% ethylenediamine solution in methanol to obtain the final sensing material, which was then drop-cast onto cellulose filter paper for colorimetric CO2 sensing.35 The prepared paper strips were positioned against RGB sensor as seen on Fig. 3 and connected to ESP32 for identifying the colour change to the atmospheric CO2 concentration. It was then tested in the gas sensor test setup with curated amounts of CO2 gas passed through the test chamber.

Sensing mechanism

The chemiresistive CO2 sensing mechanism of the ZIF-8 layer at room temperature can be comprehensively explained by a humidity-mediated surface reaction model operating on an n-type semiconducting metal–organic framework (MOF) architecture. ZIF-8 naturally exhibits n-type semiconductor characteristics due to coordination defects or structural vacancies, meaning its baseline electrical conductivity is primarily governed by free electrons (e−) within its conduction band. The overall sensing performance relies on the synergistic interplay between the electronic band structure of the framework, surface chemical adsorption equilibria, and subsequent interfacial charge transfer dynamics.

Band structure and electron depletion layer

The electronic structure of ZIF-8 consists of a Valence Band Maximum (VBM) dominated by the π-orbitals of the 2-methylimidazole ligands and a Conduction Band Minimum (CBM) formed primarily by the s- and p-orbitals of the Zn2+ coordination centres. When the sensor is exposed to ambient atmospheric conditions (∼400 ppm CO2, 45% RH), electronegative oxygen molecules (O2) from the air physisorb onto the ZIF-8 surface. These molecules extract free electrons from the CBM of the framework, trapping them as chemisorbed oxygen species (O2− or O−). This localized electron extraction creates an electron depletion layer (EDL) at the surface boundaries of the ZIF-8 grains. The formation of the EDL induces an upward bending of the energy bands, establishing an initial potential barrier that restricts free electron flow and defines the baseline resistance (Ra = 8.2 MΩ).

Surface adsorption reactions

Because CO2 is a relatively inert, non-polar molecule, its direct electronic interaction with the ZIF-8 framework at room temperature is negligible. Instead, the sensing mechanism is driven by a humidity-mediated chemical reaction on the surface. ZIF-8 possesses a porous sodalite topology with a pore window of approximately 3.4 Å, which acts as a molecular sieve to efficiently capture CO2 molecules (∼3.3 Å). Concurrently, the abundant basic nitrogen atoms within the 2-methylimidazole ligands serve as high-affinity Lewis base sites that attract the weakly acidic CO2 molecules. Under ambient humidity (45–55% RH), a thin layer of moisture physisorbs onto the porous framework. The captured CO2 molecules dissolve into this surface moisture layer, triggering a reversible cascade of chemical equilibria that yields carbonic acid (H2CO3), which subsequently dissociates into bicarbonate (HCO3−) and protons (H+) according to the following reactions:CO2(g) ⇋ CO2(ad)CO2(ad) + H2O(ad) ⇋ H2CO3(ad)H2CO3(ad) ⇋ HCO3−(ad) + H+(ad)

Interfacial charge transfer

The generation and accumulation of these ionic species fundamentally alter the charge carrier density of the underlying ZIF-8 film through two distinct electrical pathways. First, the strongly electronegative bicarbonate ions (HCO3−) accumulating at the framework interface exert an electrostatic trapping effect, withdrawing additional free electrons from the ZIF-8 bulk conduction band to balance the surface charge. This further widens the electron depletion layer and increases the upward bending of the energy bands, raising the potential barrier at the grain boundaries. This severe constriction of the electron conduction channels causes the measured sensor resistance (Rg) to rise monotonically from 8.2 MΩ up to 13.7 MΩ as the CO2 concentration increases from 400 to 1600 ppm. Second, the bulkier bicarbonate ions coordinate with the imidazole nodes and disrupt the continuous hydrogen-bonded water networks on the surface, which partially obstructs the alternative Grotthuss proton-hopping (H+ transport) pathway, contributing further to the resistance increase. When the target gas is purged, the chemical equilibrium shifts backward, releasing the trapped electrons back to the conduction band and returning the sensor to its baseline resistance.

Selectivity of the chemiresistive sensor

The chemiresistive sensor was evaluated for the cross-gas sensitivity profile at 25 °C, as seen on Fig. 15 against a benchmark concentration of 1000 ppm for all tested analytes. The sensor demonstrates an exceptional, dominant response (Ra/Rg) of 58.7 exclusively toward the target CO2 gas, establishing a substantial performance margin over common environmental and industrial interferents. Crucially, background atmospheric gases exhibit the lowest overall reactivity across the entire matrix, with recorded response values of just 0.9 for oxygen (O2), 2.0 for nitrogen (N2), and 2.5 for methane (CH4). This near-zero baseline response to primary air components indicates that the active layer can operate reliably in standard ambient conditions without suffering from background inflation or false-positive signal drift. Moderate-tier interference is limited to volatile organic compounds and polar vapours, specifically methanol (13.1) and ethanol (12.5), though the absolute sensor response to CO2 remains 4.5 to 7.6 times greater than these species.

Gas-by-gas response comparison (Ra/Rg) of selectivity behaviour of fabricated ZIF-8 based chemiresistive sensor.

This pronounced chemical selectivity is driven by the synergistic combination of molecular sieving and localized surface affinity dynamics within the metal–organic framework. The structural pore aperture window of pristine ZIF-8 stands at approximately 3.4 Å, which perfectly accommodates the small kinetic diameter of CO2 (3.3 Å) for rapid, unhindered mass transport into the internal framework cavities. In contrast, bulkier hydrocarbon analytes such as propane (C3H8, response of 6.2) and ethane (C2H6, response of 4.9) experience steric hindrance that severely restricts their internal diffusion. Beyond simple size exclusion, the deprotonated 2-methylimidazolate linkers coordinating with the Zn2+ nodes generate localized, polar electrostatic environments within the cages. These sites exhibit a highly preferential thermodynamic binding affinity for the strong quadrupole moment of incoming CO2 molecules over non-polar or weakly polar gases like N2 and CH4, ensuring highly selective chemiresistive signal transduction.

Humidity dependant sensing studies

To isolate the influence of ambient moisture (RH) on the sensing material's electrical properties, a validation study was performed within the same test chamber used during the initial calibration. A reference baseline of 1000 ppm CO2 was continuously delivered to the chamber at a fixed volumetric flow from a calibrated canister. To modulate moisture levels without altering the target analyte volume, dry nitrogen (N2) gas was introduced proportionately through a water bubbler. The volumetric ratio was adjusted to achieve stable relative humidity (RH) setpoints on increments of 10% from 30% to 80% while maintaining constant temperature using a localized thermostat stage.

The output parameters logged during the dynamic humidity sweep are detailed in Table 4. The results reveal a highly linear correlation between ambient moisture and observed framework resistance (Rg), yielding an empirical baseline drift coefficient of kH = 0.05414 MΩ/%RH (R2 = 0.9999). This progressive resistance swelling is highly reproducible and occurs independently of the locked target gas concentration.

Sensor characteristics

The fabricated ZIF-8 based chemiresistive sensor tested under the same conditions – room temperature (25 deg. Celsius) under the lab conditions in the fabricated gas sensor test setup for each month with 800 ppm CO2 gas mixture. The dynamic sensing performance of the fabricated sensor was evaluated through repeated gas exposure cycles, as shown in the Fig. 16. The sensor exhibits a consistent and reversible response, with a rapid increase in resistance upon gas introduction and a prompt return toward baseline upon gas removal. Notably, the response remains nearly identical across successive ON/OFF cycles, indicating excellent repeatability, stability, and negligible signal drift. Furthermore, the transient response analysis reveals that the sensor achieves approximately 90% of its maximum response within ∼0.8 s, demonstrating a fast response time, while the recovery to near-baseline levels occurs within ∼1.2 s, indicating efficient desorption kinetics. The slightly longer recovery time compared to the response is characteristic of gas sensing systems and reflects the intrinsic adsorption and desorption dynamics. The Fig. 17 shows the long-term stability of the sensor over time. It shows that the sensor's accuracy and stability with the output data holding up across time, but there was a significant dip post the 4th month due to the flaking off of the applied layer, reducing the adsorption sites and thus the adsorption capacity of the sensing layer.

Sensing principles – comparison

Other electrochemical methods had the advantage of being very sensitive while the optical methods were all-round best sensors while they can be affected by moisture and small impurities. These sensors were highly accurate and sensitive but were bulkier and expensive to be used in affordable and portable systems. The chemiresistive sensors have the advantage of being portable, low-cost with better sensitivity and faster response times to obtain real-time measurements. The complexity of fabricating the chemiresistive sensors were very low and the relative cost of the sensor is relatively very low due to the very minimal material requirements and fabrication process. The most expensive methods were optical in nature. They usually are bulky and require high precision machining processes to get a workable sensor, thus becoming expensive and less portable as well. The electrochemical, fluorescence and gravimetric methods took the middle ground between the less expensive – simpler chemiresistive and more expensive and time-consuming methods involving optical methodologies.

Conclusion

The carbon dioxide sensing properties were studied and tested for the chemiresistive ZIF-8 material against the colorimetric ZIF-8. These sensors were calibrated and tested against industry standard SCD40 for their sensing behavior. The fabricated ZIF-8 based chemiresistive sensor showed significant sensing behavior when tested under laboratory and real-time conditions. The porous nature and the selective adsorption of carbon dioxide by ZIF-8 enhanced the sensing properties of the fabricated sensor. Further study needs to be done to study the range of the sensor using benchmarks with range outside of the SCD40, to make this sensor a significant potential in low-cost, low-energy, high-accuracy market for real-time data capture.
