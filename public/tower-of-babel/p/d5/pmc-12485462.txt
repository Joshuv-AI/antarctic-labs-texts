Exploring the antinociceptive activity and toxicological profile of Zn(valp)2Bipy: a promising molecule in pain research
Authors: Jozi Godoy Figueiredo, Carla Peron, Paulo Henrique Ancilaggo, Paulo Roberto dos Santos, Vanessa Leal Scarabelot, Leandro Tasso, Sidnei Moura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAntiepileptic drugs have shown promise in treating acute nociceptive pain. Bioisosterism is a crucial strategy in analgesic development, enabling molecular modifications that improve therapeutic efficacy and safety. This study aims to develop and evaluate new compounds based on the concept of bioisosterism, synthesizing organocomplexes derived from compounds with established analgesic properties.MethodsA novel prototype, Zn(valp)2Bipy was synthesized, characterized, and tested for antinociceptive and toxicological effects in mice. The compound was administered orally at different doses to evaluate inhibition of acetic acid-induced abdominal constrictions and both phases of the formalin test. Additional evaluation included hot plate and tail immersion assays for central antinociception, the open field test for motor coordination, and a 14-day regimen for subacute toxicity.ResultsZn(valp)2Bipy (0.1, 1, and 10 mg/kg) significantly reduced abdominal constrictions and licking time in both phases of the formalin test. In the hot plate and tail immersion tests, this treatment significantly increased the latency period, indicating enhanced pain tolerance. Notably, the analgesic effect observed in the hot plate test was reversed by naloxone, suggesting an opioid-like action. Furthermore, in the open field test, the treatment did not affect the animals’ motor function. When administered daily at a dose of 1 mg/kg for 14 days, the compound exhibited no observable toxicity, underscoring its safety profile.ConclusionsZn(valp)2Bipy demonstrated significant antinociceptive activity through central and peripheral mechanisms without detectable toxicity. This study provides the first evidence of analgesic potential for this complex, highlighting it as a promising drug prototype for effective pain management therapies.

INTRODUCTION

Pain is one of the most prevalent conditions that limit productivity and diminish the quality of life for patients [1]. It can be classified as either acute or chronic, depending on the duration of the pain experienced. Acute pain, which arises from tissue damage, is a normal physiological response that serves a protective function. In contrast, chronic pain is an abnormal sensation that often occurs following direct injury or damage to a nerve. In developed nations such as the United States, chronic pain represents a substantial public health burden, affecting an estimated 40% of the adult population and significantly impacting quality of life. Individuals suffering from chronic pain may experience a combination of inflammatory and neuropathic pain [2,3].

Similar pathophysiological and biochemical reactions have been observed in neuropathic pain syndromes and epilepsy [4]. Therefore, antiepileptic drugs may demonstrate efficacy in managing these conditions by alleviating pain with fewer undesirable effects following acute administration (e.g., respiratory depression, constipation, sedation, dizziness, nausea). However, prolonged use can lead to complications such as tolerance, dependence, and potential for abuse, which limit their clinical utility [5,6].

Valproic acid (VA, also known as 2-propylvaleric acid, 2-propylpentanoic acid, or n-dipropylacetic acid) is a short-chain fatty acid (Fig. 1) that was synthesized in 1882. It is a major antiepileptic drug with several therapeutic indications, including the treatment of convulsions, mood stabilization, bipolar disorder, migraine management, as well as exhibiting antinociceptive and anti-inflammatory effects, as demonstrated in various animal seizure models [7,8]. Previous studies have established the antinociceptive properties of VA [8,9]. Building on this foundation, this research aimed to investigate the therapeutic efficacy of Zn(valp)2Bipy and evaluate whether this novel derivative offers advantages over its parent compound.

Valproate increases the concentration of γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) in the brain, functioning as an indirect agonist of this inhibitory neurotransmitter. Unlike other medications that elevate GABA availability solely through indirect agonism (e.g., tiagabine and vigabatrin), valproate acts through multiple pharmacological mechanisms. It can inhibit the degradation of GABA, enhance its synthesis, and reduce its turnover. The acute anticonvulsant effects of valproate are associated with increased activity of glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD), although high doses may inhibit GAD and result in decreased GABA levels [9].

Bioisosterism, a strategic approach in drug development, involves the systematic modification of known drugs to generate enhanced therapeutic effects [10]. This methodology, when combined with metal coordination chemistry, enables the creation of novel molecular classes with improved biological activities. The development of transition metal coordination compounds represents a particularly promising avenue for designing bioactive molecules. Indeed, numerous metal-containing compounds have been successfully synthesized for pharmaceutical applications, addressing both therapeutic and diagnostic needs [11].

In this context, Zn(valp)2Bipy (Fig. 1), derived from VA, exhibits beneficial anticonvulsant effects and low cytotoxicity against Artemia salina, with its synthesis and structural characterization already documented in the literature [12,13]. This investigation aims to develop and identify novel therapeutic agents, with particular emphasis on evaluating both the antinociceptive potential and toxicological profile of Zn(valp)2Bipy through comprehensive mouse model studies.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Chemicals

Acetic acid was obtained from Sigma Chemical. Morphine hydrochloride was sourced from Cristália, while formaldehyde and hematoxylin/eosin were acquired from Reagen Quimibrás Ind. Química. The enzymatic kits used for evaluating the systemic toxicity of the compounds were provided by LABTEST® Diagnostic Tests (Diagnostic Tests-Brazil). All drugs and compounds were dissolved in sterile 0.9% (w/v) sodium chloride (saline).

2. Synthesis of Zn(valp)2Bipy

The synthesis of Zn(valp)2Bipy was carried out following the methods described by dos Santos et al. [13] and Sylla-Iyarreta Veitía et al. [14]. The chemical structures of the synthesized compounds (Fig. 1) were characterized using ultraviolet-visible spectroscopy, Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy, High-Resolution Mass Spectrometry with Electrospray Ionization, Nuclear Magnetic Resonance for both 1H and 13C, and X-ray diffraction. Metal concentrations were determined using Flame Atomic Absorption Spectrophotometry.

3. Animals

Male Swiss lineage mice (Mus musculus), weighing approximately 30 g, were utilized for the study. The animals were housed in polypropylene cages specifically designed for rodents, lined with pine shavings. They were maintained in a well-ventilated environment with air exhaust, controlled temperature (22ºC ± 2ºC), humidity (55% ± 15%), and a 12-hour light-dark cycle. Mice had ad libitum access to filtered water through drinking fountains and were fed as needed. All procedures adhered to the Ethical Principles of Animal Experimentation established by the Brazilian College of Animal Experimentation and the guidelines set forth by the Ethics Committee on Animal Use at the University of Caxias do Sul, with project approval under protocol number 018/2016. To facilitate identification during experimentation, mice from each treatment group were marked with different colored tags on their tails according to their assigned group. The authors’ experimental design included groups of seven animals each, aimed at evaluating various responses: chemical responses using acetic acid and formalin, thermal responses through tail immersion and hot plate tests, and locomotor responses via the open field test. To facilitate the comprehensive analyses required for subacute systemic investigations and enzymatic assays, groups of eight animals were used. This setup ensured sufficient data for the study’s multiple analyses. Zn(valp)2Bipy was administered orally according to the specific timing and dosage requirements detailed in each methodological section. Group sizes for all experimental conditions are indicated in parentheses within the figure descriptions.

Individual animals were used only once throughout the study, with group sizes maintained at the minimum necessary for statistical validity. Group sizes (n) are specified in parentheses in all figure legends. Sample size determination was based on power calculations derived from the authors’ previous studies and published literature [15]. To ensure experimental rigor, animals were randomly allocated to groups, and all analyses were conducted by an experimenter blinded to the treatment conditions.

4. Acetic acid-induced writhing test

Mice were injected intraperitoneally (i.p.) with 0.6% acetic acid at a dosage of 10 mL/kg body weight. After a 10-minute interval, the number of writhing episodes was recorded over a 20-minute observation period [16]. The animals were treated orally via (o.v.) with sterile saline (0.1 mL) or with compound at doses of 0.1, 1, and 10 mg/kg, administered 30 minutes prior to the test observations. The group morphine (5 mg/kg) was administered subcutaneously (s.c.) 30 minutes before the acetic acid injection as a reference treatment.

5. Formalin test

Twenty microliters of 1.2% formalin were administered subcutaneously (s.c.) into the right paw of each mouse, and the duration of licking was recorded for two phases: from 0 to 5 minutes (Phase 1, neurogenic) and from 20 to 25 minutes (Phase 2, inflammatory) after formalin administration [17]. Animals received treatments of sterile saline (0.1 mL), morphine (5 mg/kg, s.c.), or the test compound (1 mg/kg, o.v.) 30 minutes prior to the formalin injection.

6. Hot plate test

The reaction time, measured in seconds from the moment the animals were placed on a surface heated to 51.0°C ± 0.5°C until they either licked their hind paws or jumped off the surface, was recorded as the response latency on a hot plate (model DS 37; Ugo Basile) [18]. Mice were treated orally via (o.v.) with the compound (1 mg/kg; 0.1 mL) 30 minutes prior to the test, while the control group received the same volume of sterile saline. Morphine (5 mg/kg, s.c.) was administered 30 minutes before the test as a reference. Measurements were taken at baseline (0 minutes), and at 30, 60, and 90 minutes after treatment, with a cut-off time of 45 seconds to prevent paw injuries. Mice with baseline latencies exceeding 20 seconds were excluded from the study.

7. Tail immersion test

The tail withdrawal response was assessed by immersing the lower 3.5 cm of the animal’s tail into a cup filled with water at a constant temperature of 50°C until the typical withdrawal response was observed. A cut-off time of 20 seconds was implemented to prevent heat-induced tail damage. Changes in tail-flick latency (Δt, in seconds) were calculated for each animal using the formula: Δt (second) = post-drug latency – pre-drug latency [19,20]. The lower 3.5-cm portion of the tail was marked, and the animals were administered the compound (1 mg/kg) or saline (0.1 mL) orally via (o.v.) 30 minutes before the test. Morphine (5 mg/kg, s.c.) was injected 30 minutes prior to the test as a reference.

8. Open field test

This test, described by Hall [21], aims to assess the behavior of animals when introduced to an unfamiliar enclosed environment. Mice were treated with the compound (1 mg/kg, o.v.) 30 minutes prior to the test, while the control group received the same volume of sterile saline. Diazepam (5 mg/kg, i.p.) was administered 30 minutes before the test as a reference. Each mouse was allowed a 1-minute acclimatization period in the arena before being observed for a duration of 4 minutes [22].

9. Evaluation of systemic effects

Mice were administered daily injections of either saline (0.1 mL) or the compound (1 mg/kg) via the oral route for 14 consecutive days. The animals’ weights were recorded at both the beginning and the end of the treatment period. Peripheral blood samples were collected from the retro-orbital plexus for leukogram and biochemical analysis. An aliquot of blood was mixed with ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid, and total and differential leukocyte counts were performed using standard manual procedures and light microscopy [23]. Standardized diagnostic kits from LABTEST® were utilized for the spectrophotometric determination of aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, creatinine, and urea levels in serum samples obtained after centrifugation of whole blood (without anticoagulants) at 2,500 rpm for 15 minutes. Following the mouse being euthanized, the liver, kidneys, and heart were excised, weighed (wet weight expressed as grams per 20 g of body mass), and subjected to macroscopic analysis. Any ulcerative lesions or hemorrhages were quantified and measured. The stomach was opened via a longitudinal incision along the greater curvature to assess the number and severity of gastric mucosal lesions [24].

10. Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 5 software (GraphPad Software, Inc.). Results are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean or median, as appropriate for the variables under investigation. For statistical analysis, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed, followed by Student–Newman–Keuls multiple comparison tests to examine the thermal and chemical tests, as well as the associated locomotor antinociceptive activities. To assess systemic effects, the authors used both ANOVA and Student’s t-test analyses. The threshold for statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 for all comparisons, ensuring robust and reliable results.

RESULTS

1. Acetic acid-induced writhing test

Oral administration of Zn(valp)2Bipy and Zn(Diclof)₂Nic to mice 30 minutes prior to acetic acid injection significantly inhibited the abdominal constrictions induced by the stimulus. The inhibition percentages for Zn(valp)2Bipy were 35%, 63%, and 80% at doses of 0.1, 1, and 10 mg/kg, respectively, compared to the control group. For Zn(Diclof)₂Nic, the inhibition percentages were 35%, 63%, and 70% at doses of 0.01, 0.1, and 1 mg/kg, respectively. The positive control group, which received morphine (5 mg/kg, s.c.), showed a substantial reduction in writhing, with an inhibition of approximately 91% (Fig. 2).

2. Formalin test

The compounds were administered orally via (o.v.) 30 minutes prior to the formalin injection, demonstrating an antinociceptive effect as indicated by a reduction in licking time during both the first (neurogenic) and second (inflammatory) phases of the test. The percentage reductions in licking time were 58% in the first phase and 78% in the second phase compared to the control group. As expected, morphine significantly attenuated the formalin response in both phases, with inhibitory effects ranging from a 79% reduction in licking time in the first phase to an 88% reduction in the second phase (Table 1).

3. Hot plate test

The results presented in Table 2 demonstrate that Zn(valp)2Bipy (1 mg/kg), administered orally via (o.v.) 30 minutes prior to exposure to thermal stimuli, significantly increased reaction times at 30, 60, and 90 minutes compared to the control group. Specifically, the increases were 58% at 30 minutes, 124% at 60 minutes, and 97% at 90 minutes. As anticipated, morphine administered subcutaneously (s.c.) also significantly enhanced the animals’ latency to respond to thermal stimuli, with increases of 148% at 30 minutes, 178% at 60 minutes, 122% at 90 minutes, and 88% at 120 minutes. Notably, the antinociceptive effects of both Zn(valp)2Bipy (1 mg/kg) and morphine were completely blocked by naloxone.

4. Tail immersion test

Table 3 shows that the administration of the drug at a dose of 1 mg/kg significantly increased the reaction time at both 30 and 60 minutes compared to the control group. Additionally, morphine (5 mg/kg) resulted in a significant enhancement of the withdrawal response in the animals’ tails when immersed in hot water.

5. Open field test

Treatment with Zn(valp)2Bipy (1 mg/kg) did not significantly alter the behavioral responses, specifically the number of crossings, in the open field test compared to the control group (Fig. 3). In contrast, treatment with diazepam (5 mg/kg) resulted in a decrease in the evaluated responses, as reported in the literature.

6. Evaluation of systemic effects (sub-acute toxicity)

Administration of the compound in a single-dose regimen (1 mg/kg, o.v.; the same dose that exhibited antinociceptive effects) over 14 consecutive days showed no signs of toxicity. The treatment did not impact the animals’ body weight, or the wet weights of the liver, kidneys, and heart compared to controls injected with sterile saline. All evaluated organs appeared normal, with no signs of edema throughout the entire treatment period.

Additionally, macroscopic examination of the stomach revealed intact mucosa with no visible lesions in both groups (Zn(valp)2Bipy and saline-treated). The values obtained for urea and creatinine, used as indicators of renal function, did not differ from those of the control group. Furthermore, hepatic function, assessed through the kinetics of the liver enzymes alanine aminotransferase and aspartate aminotransferase, as well as the count of circulating leukocytes, remained unchanged following compound treatment (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

The search for new, safer, and more effective pain management drugs has garnered significant attention from researchers and the pharmaceutical industry. Pain management remains a major challenge in modern medicine, prompting numerous studies to explore new compounds as therapeutic alternatives. Recent research on metal-organic compounds as potential drugs has demonstrated promising potential. The dosing regimen employed in this study was established based on previous investigations with VA [7,8]. The rationale for combining bipyridine with VA stems from bipyridine’s documented antinociceptive properties, suggesting potential synergistic enhancement of analgesic effects through their conjugation.

In the present study, Zn(valp)2Bipy exhibited an antinociceptive effect against nociceptive stimuli induced by acetic acid and formalin. Furthermore, the complex showed effectiveness in models of nociception induced by hot plate, tail immersion, and open field tests. Results from the open field test revealed no changes in locomotor activity or behavioral parameters, suggesting that the observed effects occur independently of central nervous system depression or motor function impairment.

Responses to noxious chemical stimuli were assessed using the acetic acid-induced abdominal constriction assay, a classical, simple, and sensitive model for measuring antinociception induced by centrally or peripherally acting analgesics [25]. This preliminary test indicated that Zn(valp)2Bipy could serve as a promising antinociceptive agent. However, the acetic acid-induced writhing test is somewhat nonspecific, as intraperitoneal administration of the chemical activates nociceptors and stimulates the release of various pain and inflammatory mediators, including histamine, bradykinin, serotonin, glutamate, noradrenaline, substance P, nitric oxide, and prostaglandins [26]. Consequently, it is challenging to delineate the specific nociceptive pathways affected by the molecule.

To gain further insight, the formalin-induced nociception test was conducted. In the first phase (0–5 minutes) of the formalin test, nociceptive neurons are activated, while the second phase (15–30 minutes) reflects the inflammatory response to tissue injury, resulting in the release of histamine, serotonin, prostaglandins, and excitatory amino acids [27]. Centrally acting drugs (e.g., opioids, narcotic analgesics) inhibit both phases, whereas steroids and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs primarily inhibit the second phase. In the formalin test in mice, the organocomplex (1 mg/kg, o.v.) inhibited both phases, with more pronounced effects observed in the second phase (inflammatory) compared to the first phase (neurogenic).

The results for Zn(valp)2Bipy were lower than those of the control group but still promising. Similar findings were reported by Ximenes et al. [8], who observed that valproate administered in comparable or larger doses via the same route yielded similar effects. The data presented here establish that the coordination of zinc (Zn) with bipyridine molecules leads to a marked potentiation of the antinociceptive activity.

In the hot plate test, administration of Zn(valp)2Bipy at a dose of 1 mg/kg (o.v.) significantly increased the latency time to jump or lick, demonstrating a gradual rise at 30, 60, and 90 minutes post-administration compared to the morphine control group (Table 2). The data indicate that Zn(valp)2Bipy possesses both central and peripheral antinociceptive properties. Moreover, the compound’s enhanced efficacy in reducing nociception during the second phase of testing strongly suggests an underlying anti-inflammatory mechanism contributing to its analgesic effects.

The involvement of opioid receptors in modulating nociception has been well-documented [28]. Kunder et al. [29], reported similar data, indicating that sodium valproate (200 mg/kg) increased analgesic activity; however, the compound used in the present study demonstrated efficacy at lower doses. Mesdjian et al. [30] also reported that sodium valproate possessed analgesic properties when used alone, and the authors propose that its conjugation with metals enhances this effect.

The central analgesic action was further confirmed by naloxone’s inhibitory effect, a specific antagonist of morphinomimetic receptors [31]. Previous studies involving sodium valproate suggest its analgesic activity is linked to its impact on GABA neurotransmission, with sodium valproate increasing GABA levels and thereby producing analgesia [8,30].

The tail immersion test evaluates centrally acting antinociceptive effects and is selective for detecting central analgesia, as peripherally acting agents do not influence thermal stimuli [32]. The compound prolonged latency in the tail immersion test, indicating an increase in nociceptive threshold. Consequently, the results of this test corroborate the effects actively observed in the hot plate test.

Many analgesic drugs that act on the central nervous system can lead to reduced motor coordination in animals. Such locomotor impairment can manifest as prolonged immobility or “freezing” between episodes of pain [33]. Pain elicits various autonomic and behavioral responses, and the open field test allows for easy observation of these effects [34]. A behavioral assessment was conducted to determine whether the antinociceptive effects of Zn(valp)2Bipy resulted in any disturbances in the central nervous system. In evaluating the neurobiological basis of anxiety and screening novel pharmacological agents, the open field test serves as a widely validated assessment tool [35]. The present findings reveal that Zn(valp)2Bipy administration at the tested dose achieved therapeutic efficacy while maintaining normal locomotor activity, suggesting preservation of central nervous system function without inducing sedative or motor side effects [36].

Sub-acute toxicity tests assess the potential cumulative adverse effects of a compound and any biochemical, physiological, or pathological changes following multiple oral administrations [37]. In this study, sub-acute administration of the compound (over 14 days) did not result in mortality or behavioral changes in the animals. The treatment did not affect body mass or the wet weights of vital organs such as the liver, heart, or kidneys. Macroscopic examinations of these organs, including the stomach, appeared normal. Moreover, renal and hepatic functions, as well as the number of circulating leukocytes, were unaffected by the compound treatment. Histological analysis revealed no significant morphological or structural differences between control and Zn(valp)2Bipy-treated groups. This preservation of normal tissue architecture indicates that the compound does not adversely affect lymphoid tissue integrity or immune cell populations, supporting its favorable safety profile.

The development of analgesic compounds has progressed substantially in recent decades, with particular success in enhancing existing drugs through strategic structural modifications. The present investigation reveals significant antinociceptive properties of a Zn complex comprising VA conjugated with Zn and bipyridine. Although these results are encouraging, further mechanistic studies using specific modulators are necessary to elucidate the precise mode of action. Additionally, evaluation in chronic pain models will be crucial for determining the compound’s potential therapeutic value in clinical settings.

Recent decades have witnessed significant advances in analgesic drug development, particularly through structural modifications of existing medications to enhance their therapeutic efficacy. Pain management remains a significant challenge in modern medicine, driving the continuous search for new therapeutic alternatives. Metal-organic compounds have recently emerged as promising candidates for analgesic development.

In conclusion, Zn(valp)2Bipy demonstrated significant antinociceptive effects across multiple nociceptive models, including acetic acid-induced writhing and formalin tests, as well as hot plate and tail immersion tests. The authors’ findings indicate that its mechanism of action involves both central and peripheral pathways. Importantly, this organocomplex demonstrated a favorable safety profile in subacute toxicity tests, showing no adverse effects on body weight, organ weight, or behavioral parameters. Unlike many conventional analgesics, it maintained normal motor coordination, positioning it as a promising prototype for future analgesic development.

Further investigation is warranted to fully elucidate the compound’s mechanism of action. Future studies should include comprehensive modulatory analyses at these doses, detailed mechanistic investigations, and correlation studies with the constituent chemical compounds. This additional research will be crucial for understanding the specific substances responsible for the observed antinociceptive effects and their precise mechanisms of action.
