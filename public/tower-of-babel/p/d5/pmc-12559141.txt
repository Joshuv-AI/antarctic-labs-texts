Learning curve and perioperative outcomes of robotic-assisted distal pancreatectomy: a single-center study
Authors: Yizhang Zhu, Jiayue Zou, Daobin Wang, Danyang Shen, Xiaofeng Xue, Weigang Zhang, Lei Qin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo examine the learning curve and perioperative outcomes of robotic-assisted distal pancreatectomy (RDP) conducted at a single center and to contextualize these findings against existing literature. A single expert surgical team conducted RDP on 106 patients at the Department of Hepatobiliary and Pancreatic Surgery, The First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University, from January 2021 to December 2024. To explore the learning pattern of operative duration, cumulative sum (CUSUM) analysis was utilized. Breakpoints in textbook outcomes (TBO) for RDP were subsequently identified through the application of a two-segment regression model. Perioperative indicators before and after the breakpoints were compared. The cohort showed a median operative time of 230 min, with 74.53% (n = 79) achieving TBO. The cubic regression model (R² = 0.958) provided the best fit for the operative-time learning curve, revealing two discrete operative phases: a premier learning phase (cases 1 − 45) together with a succeeding proficiency phase (cases 46 − 106). The TBO breakpoint occurred at case 85, corresponding to the mastery phase. The proficiency phase yielded marked improvements over the learning phase, including shorter operative times (258 min vs. 200 min), lower intraoperative blood loss (100 mL vs. 75 mL), and a reduced rate of major complications (Clavien-Dindo grade ≥ III: 13.33% vs. 1.64%) (all P < 0.05). These phase transitions were consistent with benchmark thresholds reported in previous studies. Operative efficiency in RDP improved significantly after 45 cases, suggesting that technical safety and feasibility can be achieved relatively early. However, the attainment of consistent clinical success, as defined by TBO, required approximately 85 cases. Future initiatives should prioritize shortening RDP’s learning curve, minimizing early complications, and systematically enhancing surgical quality to ensure safer adoption.Keywords: Robotic-assisted distal pancreatectomy, Learning curve, Textbook outcomes, Perioperative outcomes, Spleen preservation

Introduction

Distal pancreatectomy (DP) constitutes an extensively applied surgical intervention aiming at managing lesions in the pancreatic body and tail, whether benign, premalignant, or malignant. Minimally invasive pancreatic surgery has become more common in parallel with the growing adoption of enhanced recovery protocols in pancreatic surgery [1, 2]. Minimally invasive distal pancreatectomy (MIDP) offers reduced operative trauma and enhanced postoperative recovery, long-term outcomes comparable to the open distal pancreatectomy (ODP) [3–5]. As part of the evolution of MIDP, robotic-assisted distal pancreatectomy (RDP) was pioneered by Professor W.S. Melvin in 2003. He anticipated that robotics would expand the potential of laparoscopic surgery, with its clinical value ultimately defined through accumulated surgical experience [6].

The da Vinci surgical system improves surgical precision, visualization, and dexterity, thereby addressing many of the limitations associated with traditional minimally invasive techniques [7, 8]. However, the widespread broader application of complex pancreatic procedures remains constrained by surgeons’ learning curves. Owing to its distinct technical characteristics, robotic surgery constitutes an independent learning process that differs significantly from laparoscopic technology [9, 10].

Most previous studies evaluating the learning curve of RDP have concentrated on isolated intraoperative parameters, with operative time frequently used as the main outcome metric [11, 12]. Nonetheless, increasing attention has been directed toward quality assurance as a fundamental measure of surgical competence. Reliance on a single indicator cannot adequately represent surgical proficiency. The concept of textbook outcomes (TBO), as a multidimensional metric reflecting optimal perioperative results and procedural consistency, has gained recognition as a quality benchmark. Achieving the plateau phase of TBO often requires a larger case volume, indicating advanced technical proficiency or mastery [13, 14]. Currently, few studies have analyzed the RDP learning curves using a structured “learning-proficiency-mastery” framework.

In this study, we analyzed 106 consecutive RDP cases performed by the same surgical team at our center. The learning curve was assessed by use of cumulative sum (CUSUM) analysis and two-phase modeling methods. Additionally, technical characteristics and early clinical outcomes were reviewed to investigate procedural safety and feasibility.

Materials and methods

Perioperative data collection

This study included patients with benign or malignant lesions in the pancreatic body or tail who underwent RDP from January 2021 to December 2024 at the Department of Hepatobiliary and Pancreatic Surgery, First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University. All procedures were implemented by the same surgical team. Only cases with complete perioperative data were analyzed; those with incomplete clinical information were excluded. Clinical information was extracted from the hospital’s electronic medical record system and categorized as preoperative variables [age, sex, body mass index (BMI), American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification, tumor location (body or tail of the pancreas), history of abdominal surgery, smoking and alcohol consumption history, and comorbidities like diabetes or hypertension], intraoperative variables (operative time, estimated blood loss, blood transfusion, conversion to open surgery, and splenic preservation), postoperative variables [intra-abdominal infection, postoperative hemorrhage, chylous leakage, postoperative pancreatic fistula (POPF) (Grade B/C), length of postoperative hospital stay, Clavien-Dindo complications, need for ultrasound-guided percutaneous drainage, reoperation, readmission within 90 days, and 90-day mortality], and pathological variables (diagnosis and maximum tumor diameter). The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University (No.2025829).

Definitions

TBO was defined according to established criteria in previous studies [13, 15] and was considered achieved if all of the following conditions were met: (1) Absence of grade B/C POPF [International Study Group on Pancreatic Surgery (ISGPS)]; (2) absence of grade B/C post-pancreatectomy hemorrhage (PPH) (ISGPS); (3) absence of major complications (Clavien-Dindo grade ≥ III); (4) no readmission within 90 days; (5) no in-hospital or 30-day mortality. POPF [16] and PPH [17] were classified according to ISGPS definitions, and all postoperative complications were classified using the Clavien-Dindo classification [18].

RDP technique

RDP was performed by use of the da Vinci Xi surgical system (Intuitive Surgical Inc., Sunnyvale, CA, USA). All 106 procedures were completed by a single surgical team with extensive experience in both open and laparoscopic pancreatic surgery. Before initiating the robotic program, the team had completed more than 1,000 cumulative open pancreatic resections and laparoscopic distal pancreatectomies (LDPs). The transition to robotic surgery followed a structured, competency-based training pathway that included vendor-certified didactic courses, simulator modules, dry-lab exercises (suturing, stapling, and vessel sealing), and wet-lab or cadaveric workshops. All team members gained bedside-assist experience before performing console procedures. The lead surgeon and two primary assistants obtained institutional credentialing for da Vinci robotic surgery and were formally privileged for console operation. Since 2021, our institution has performed over 208 pancreatic resections annually, including approximately 110 distal pancreatectomies. Among minimally invasive pancreatic procedures, approximately 100 are laparoscopic and 45 are robotic pancreatic resections each year, with an average RDP caseload of 25 per year. Subsequent to general anesthesia, a nasogastric tube was placed in all subjects. Administration of prophylactic antibiotics (ceftriaxone) was finished 30 min before the incision, with moxifloxacin as an alternative for patients allergic to penicillins. The operating room layout and trocar positions are shown in Figs. 1 and 2. The standard RDP technique at our center involves the following steps: (1) Patient positioning: A 30° reverse Trendelenburg and a 15° right tilt were employed to optimize lower splenic pole exposure and facilitate dissection. (2) Pneumoperitoneum and port placement: CO₂ insufflation was performed at the umbilicus using the Hasson approach, achieving and sustaining an intra-abdominal pressure of 12 mmHg. Docking was performed from the right. Port placement was as follows: Arm 1, 5 cm below the right costal margin along the anterior axillary line; Arm 2, positioned at the umbilical transverse line intersecting the right midclavicular line; Arm 3, below the umbilicus; Arm 4, 3 cm beneath the left costal margin at the anterior axillary line; and the assistant port, at the umbilical transverse line intersecting the left midclavicular line. Inter-port spacing ranged from 5 cm to 7 cm and was adjusted according to the patient’s body habitus. (3) Instrument allocation: Arm configuration consisted of Maryland bipolar forceps (Arm 1), fenestrated bipolar forceps (Arm 2), an endoscope (Arm 3), and an ultrasonic scalpel with needle driver (Arm 4). (4) Docking and procedure: The procedure began with abdominal exploration after robotic docking, followed by gastrocolic ligament division to expose the pancreas. According to tumor characteristics and location, dissection was implemented along the superior and inferior margins of the pancreatic neck or body. This maneuver created a posterior tunnel, through which a sling was passed to elevate the pancreas. Pancreatic neck or body transection was then implemented utilizing a linear stapler. In cases of malignant or borderline tumors, DP with splenectomy was performed directly. For benign or low-grade lesions, dissection was extended distally until the distal splenic vein (SpV) and splenic artery (SpA) were exposed. When technically feasible, spleen-preserving distal pancreatectomy (SPDP) (Kimura technique) was attempted. If vessel preservation is not technically feasible, the Warshaw technique was employed. If dissection of the splenic hilum proves challenging or if the spleen shows impaired perfusion following vessel ligation, concurrent splenectomy was performed.

Statistical methods

Clinical data were compiled using Microsoft Excel. Statistical analyses and visualizations were implemented by use of SPSS Statistics for Windows (version 27.0, IBM Corporation) and R (version 4.4.3, R Foundation for Statistical Computing). For quantitative measures, mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range, IQR) was applied, depending on the distribution assessed by tests of normality and homogeneity of variance. When these assumptions were met, between-group differences were assessed utilizing independent samples t-tests or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Assumption violations were addressed by applying the Mann–Whitney U test for nonparametric comparisons. Frequencies and percentages [N (%)] were used to describe categorical variables, with group comparisons conducted by use of Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. P < 0.05 denotes a difference attaining statistical significance.

CUSUM learning curve and two-phase model

The CUSUM method [19–21] was used to plot the learning curve, with operative time selected as the primary indicator of surgical performance. CUSUM values were calculated for each case using the formula: , where xi and µ separately stand for the operative time for the ith case and the mean operative time for the entire cohort. CUSUM values for all 106 cases were plotted to visualize temporal performance trends. Regression models of increasing order—linear (first), quadratic (second), and cubic (third)—were used to implement curve fitting. The regression model demonstrating the maximum coefficient of determination (R²) was regarded as the best fit after model comparison, with R² values approaching 1 indicating superior explanatory power. P < 0.05 denoted a difference of statistical significance. The peak of the optimal CUSUM curve was interpreted as the learning breakpoint, indicating the lowest number of cases mandated to surpass the initial learning phase in RDP.

To evaluate TBO, a two-phase segmented regression model was applied following the method described by Papachristofi et al. [22]. The first phase (learning phase) was characterized by progressive improvement in TBO rates, reflecting performance gains with accumulating surgical experience. The second phase (plateau phase) began when TBO rates stabilized along a horizontal trend, indicating a steady state of clinical performance. The breakpoint (τ) between phases corresponded to the case number at which procedural proficiency was achieved.

Results

Patient characteristics and pathological data

Baseline demographic, clinical, and pathological profiles of the 106 study patients are summarized in Table 1. The median patient age was 58 years, and the cohort comprised roughly two-thirds females. The mean BMI was 23.76 ± 3.28 kg/m². Common comorbidities included hypertension and diabetes mellitus. Approximately one-quarter of patients had a history of prior abdominal surgery. Lesions were located predominantly in the pancreatic body (≈ 60%), with the remainder in the tail. As per the ASA classification, most patients were classified as Grade II. The principal pathological diagnoses were pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, mucinous cystic neoplasm, and solid pseudopapillary tumor, together accounting for more than two-thirds of cases. The mean maximum tumor diameter was 4.03 ± 2.09 cm.

Perioperative outcomes

Perioperative outcomes are presented in Table 2. The median operative time was 230 min, with 100 mL median intraoperative blood loss. The rates of conversion to open surgery (2.83%) and transfusion (1.89%) were low, indicating technical feasibility. Splenectomy was performed in 57.55% of patients. The overall complication profile was acceptable: clinically relevant POPF (Grade B/C) occurred in 23.58% of cases, while major complications (Clavien–Dindo grade ≥ III) occurred in 6.60%. The median postoperative hospital stay was 7 days. TBO was achieved in 74.53% of patients.

Analysis of operative time in learning curve evaluation

To evaluate the learning curve in RDP, operative times for all 106 cases were analyzed (Fig. 3). CUSUM analysis, using operative time as the key performance metric, identified a breakpoint at case 45 (Fig. 4). Based on this breakpoint, the cohort was stratified into a learning phase (cases 1–45, n = 45) and a proficiency phase (cases 46–106, n = 61). As summarized in Table 3, significant declines were noted in the median operative time in the proficiency phase relative to the learning phase (200 min [IQR: 170–225] vs. 258 min [IQR: 245–275]; P < 0.05). Similarly, drops were also noted in the estimated intraoperative blood loss (75 mL [IQR: 50–100] vs. 100 mL [IQR: 50–230]; P < 0.05), along with significant reductions in the incidence of principal complications (Clavien–Dindo grade ≥ III) (13.33% vs. 1.64%; P < 0.05). However, the intraoperative transfusion, conversion to open surgery, spleen preservation, postoperative intra-abdominal infection, postoperative hemorrhage, POPF, reoperation, or length of hospital stay exhibited no statistically significant differences between the two phases.

Comparison between TBO phases

Based on the two-phase segmented regression model, a breakpoint at case 85 was identified as the threshold for achieving consistent TBO (Fig. 5). This breakpoint delineated two distinct performance periods: the pre-TBO phase (cases 1–85) and the mastery phase (cases > 85). As shown in Table 4, operative efficiency improved significantly after this threshold. The median operative time decreased from 235 min (IQR: 205–263) prior to case 85 to 197 min (IQR: 170–230) thereafter (P < 0.05). Similarly, estimated intraoperative blood loss dropped from 100 mL (IQR: 50–200) to 50 mL (IQR: 50–100) (P < 0.05). With respect to postoperative hospital stay, incidence of major complications, or the rate of clinically relevant POPF, differences did not reach statistical significance between the two periods (P > 0.05).

Discussion

In this single-center series of 106 consecutive RDPs, two thresholds were identified: case 45 on the CUSUM analysis of operative time and case 85 on the two-segment regression model of TBO, which plateaued at approximately 80%. Accordingly, we delineated three sequential learning phases: the learning phase (cases 1–45), during which operative time, blood loss, and major complication rates progressively declined; the proficiency phase (cases 46–106), characterized by procedural consistency and refinement of perioperative management; and the mastery stage (> 85 cases), characterized by sustained reductions in operative time and blood loss and the attainment of stable TBO performance. These findings align closely with the recent multicenter international collaborative analysis that proposed a harmonized three-phase framework for RDP learning curves, defining thresholds for competency (operative time), proficiency (major complications), and mastery (TBO) at approximately 46, 63, and 73 cases, respectively [23]. Collectively, these findings indicate that early technical competency in RDP can be achieved after a relatively small number of procedures, but consistent, textbook-level outcomes require additional experience and team-level coordination.

Since the introduction of RDP at our center, the majority of procedures have been performed by a three-member primary team, with the lead surgeon operating the robotic console in all 106 cases. Each procedure also involved two assistants: one sterile assistant at the operating table and another at the console, who participated in pre-dissection, specimen retrieval, and, in some cases, parts of the resection. Therefore, the present findings characterize a collective team-based learning curve rather than an individual trajectory. Importantly, our phase-specific outcomes are comparable to the international reference benchmarks for distal pancreatectomy [24], reaffirming the safety and feasibility of RDP implementation at our center.

Earlier studies on the RDP learning curve predominantly focused on operative time as a single outcome metric. More recent investigations have expanded to include learning curves for specific techniques, such as robotic Warshaw procedures or assistant performance [20, 25]. However, many reports remain limited by small sample sizes, single-center designs, and narrow outcome metrics (operative time, blood loss, or conversion rates). For example, Professor Murtaza Shakir reported a 40-case learning curve [26], whereas the European Consortium on Minimally Invasive Pancreatic Surgery (E-MIPS) emphasized that, although early technical improvements are often observed, consistent performance in MIDP requires extensive experience [13]. Our findings support this multidimensional view. By incorporating both operative time and TBO, we provide a more comprehensive assessment of the learning process—from initial technical competence to the attainment of high-quality, reproducible clinical results. This is consistent with the E-MIPS conclusion that surgical outcomes depend not only on individual expertise but also on institutional and team-level proficiency.

In our cohort, the spleen preservation rate was 42.45%. While spleen preservation is preferred, particularly for benign or borderline lesions, it remains technically demanding given the tight anatomical association of the pancreatic tail with the splenic vessels. Overwhelming post-splenectomy infection (OPSI) and portal vein thrombosis represent serious adverse events that may occur following splenectomy [27, 28]. Two spleen-preserving techniques are commonly employed: the Kimura technique, which preserves both the SpA and SpV [29]; and the Warshaw technique, which sacrifices both vessels but relies on the short gastric and left gastroepiploic vessels for collateral perfusion [30]. While both techniques show comparable overall safety and short-term perioperative outcomes, the Kimura technique demonstrates superior results regarding splenic infarction, gastric/gastroepiploic collateral varices, and other complications related to splenic blood supply [31, 32]. At our center, the Kimura method is preferred for benign or low-grade lesions, while the Warshaw technique is deployed in scenarios where vessel preservation proves technically attainable. In cases where hilar dissection proves too complex or splenic perfusion is compromised following vessel ligation, splenectomy is performed. In one early case, intraoperative bleeding from splenic vessels led to conversion to open surgery, underscoring the challenges of dissection in this region. Regardless of the chosen approach, successful SPDP depends on meticulous dissection of the pancreatic tail vasculature.

In 2020, Dr. Sungho Kim introduced a combined CUSUM and risk-adjusted CUSUM (RA-CUSUM) model for assessing the learning curve of laparoscopic pancreaticoduodenectomy performed by a single surgeon [33]. This model addressed several limitations of conventional methods. However, RA-CUSUM has not yet been widely applied to RDP learning curves. Future studies should incorporate additional variables, such as pancreatic transection method, perioperative laboratory values, and pathological changes before and after the learning curve, to more comprehensively assess how patient selection interacts with the learning process.

The retrospective single-center design and potential influence of institutional practices on case selection and perioperative care constitute the principal limitation of this work. Broader validation should be sought through multicenter studies.

Conclusion

In this cohort of 106 consecutive patients who underwent RDP, we identified a clear progression through three distinct phases: initial learning, procedural proficiency, and consistent achievement of high-quality outcomes. The learning curve observed reflects not only the technical development of the lead surgeon but also the collective learning process of the entire surgical team. To promote the safe and effective adoption of RDP, future efforts should focus on shortening the learning curve, reducing complication rates during the early phase, and systematically improving surgical quality. These findings support broader integration of robotic technology into pancreatic surgery.
