hPL-conditioned MSC-derived extracellular vesicles exhibit enhanced anticancer effects on MCF7 and A549 cells
Authors: Nidaa A Ababneh, Razan AlDiqs, Sura Nashwan, Mohammad A Ismail, Raghda Barham, Fairouz Nairat, Enas Alwohoush, Shaymaa A Hussein, Tareq Saleh, Abdalla Awidi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) have therapeutic potential due to their differentiation and immune-regulatory capabilities, mainly through the secretion of extracellular vesicles (EVs) which can exhibit pro- and anti-tumor effects.AimsThis study aimed to investigate the effects of EVs derived from Whorton’s jelly derived MSCs (WJMSCs) cultured in fetal bovine serum (FBS) or human platelet lysate (hPL), as xeno-free alternative, on breast (MCF7) and lung (A549) cancer cells.MethodsEVs were isolated from WJMSCs cultured in either FBS or hPL and then applied to cancer cells. Following characterization of EVs, the effects on cancer cell proliferation, apoptosis, senescence, and migration were also assessed.ResultsBoth EV types reduced proliferation in MCF7 and A549 cells at 24 hours, with a sustained effect on MCF7 at 48 hours. Senescence marked by upregulation of the Senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) was induced in both cell lines, indicating non-apoptotic growth inhibition. WJMSC-hPL-EVs enhanced migration in MCF7 cells despite reduced proliferation, whereas A549 cell migration was time-dependent, as demonstrated by the wound scratch assay.ConclusionWhile the findings show minimal differences between FBS and hPL, the results nonetheless support the potential use of hPL as a viable xeno-free alternative to FBS. Further research is needed to the therapeutic implications.Keywords: Mesenchymal stem cells (MSC), Wharton’s jelly-derived MSCs (WJMSCs), cell-free therapy, extracellular vesicles (EVs), breast cancer, lung cancer

Introduction

Worldwide, breast and lungs cancers remain the most diagnosed malignancies [1,2]. Although that cancer treatment and management have improved significantly with strategies such as chemotherapy, radiation and personalized therapy, including chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy (CAR-T), antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs), and bispecific T-cell engagers (BiTEs) [3], both cancers continue to spread globally, highlighting the need for the development of new therapeutic strategies.

Stem cells are undifferentiated cells capable of self-renewal and differentiation into specialized tissues [4,5]. In adults, mesenchymal stromal/stem cells (MSCs), found in tissues like bone marrow (BM), umbilical cord (UC), adipose tissue (AT), and Wharton’s jelly (WJ), are widely studied for their regenerative and immunomodulatory properties [6,7]. MSCs can differentiate into osteogenic, chondrogenic, and adipogenic lineages [8,9]. According to 2006 International Society for Cellular Therapy (ISCT) guidelines, MSCs must exhibit self-renewal, multipotency, and express CD105, CD73, and CD90, while lacking CD45, CD34, and other hematopoietic markers, along with plastic adherence and in vitro expansion capabilities [7,10].

In MSC culture, fetal bovine serum (FBS), collected from unborn calves at slaughterhouses, is commonly used as a supplement due to its availability and cost-effectiveness, and packed with nutrients and proteins that aid in cell adhesion, growth and proliferation [11–13]. However, substitutes for FBS are required due to zoonotic contamination hazards, batch variability, and ethical considerations [13,14].

Platelet lysate (PL), derived from the rupture of platelets in platelet-rich plasma (PRP), gained interest recently for its application in cell culture studies [14]. Human platelet lysate (hPL) is suggested as an alternative to animal serum for MSCs expansion, containing growth factors like platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), transforming growth factor (TGF), and epidermal growth factor (EGF), which enhance MSC proliferation while maintain their differentiation potential [15].

One of the key mechanisms through which MSCs exert their therapeutic effects is via the secretion of extracellular vesicles (EVs) [16,17]. EVs are a diverse group of membrane-bound particles that transport proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids from their cells of origin, facilitating communication and influencing the behavior of neighboring cells [18,19]. EVs include three subtypes based on their size and biogenesis: exosomes (Exos), microvesicles (MVs), and apoptotic bodies (Apo BDs) [20]. Exosomes and microvesicles (MVs) are smaller EV subtypes released by viable cells and identified as two key subpopulations of EVs [21]. Exosomes have a more distinct and well-defined composition, and they carry specific components from intracellular organelles, including the endoplasmic reticulum, mitochondria, and Golgi apparatus [22].

Recently, exosomes have attracted scientists’ attention in cancer research due to their role as biomarkers for early cancer diagnosis and their application in targeted therapy [23,24]. Additionally, recent evidence suggests that MSC-derived EVs (MSC-EVs), especially exosomes, play a pivotal role in cancer biology. Studies indicate that MSC-EVs possess both pro-tumor and anti-tumor capabilities depending on their cargo, such as microRNAs, proteins, and lipids, which can either promote tumor growth or suppress it by affecting angiogenesis, immune evasion, and cell proliferation [25,26]. The potential of MSC-derived EVs as a cell-free therapeutic strategy for cancer treatment has gained significant interest, primarily due to their tumor-targeting properties and their capacity to modulate the tumor microenvironment [27]. The conflicting results regarding the effects of MSC-derived EVs on cancer may be attributed to several factors, including the heterogeneity of MSC sources, variations in EV isolation methods, and the use of diverse tumor models in experimental studies [28].

The composition of the culture medium for MSCs plays a significant role in shaping the biological activity of their secreted EVs. For instance, differences between using fetal bovine serum (FBS) and human platelet lysate (hPL) in MSC culture can impact EVs content, thereby influencing their effects on cancer cells [13]. Consequently, it is essential to understand how culture conditions affect the functional properties of MSC-derived EVs to effectively harness their therapeutic potential. This study investigated the effects of EVs derived from WJMSCs cultured in either FBS-based or hPL-based media on the MCF7 and A549 cancer cell lines, particularly on their cell proliferation rates, ability to undergo apoptosis and senescence, and their migratory potential. It is noteworthy that hPL represents a safer, xeno-free alternative to FBS for producing MSC-derived EVs suitable for therapeutic applications. Switching to hPL, which is clinically more acceptable, addresses major safety and regulatory concerns associated with the xenogeneic nature of FBS, such as immunogenicity and pathogen transmission. Importantly, we observed minimal functional differences in the antitumor effects of EVs derived from MSCs cultured in hPL versus FBS, suggesting that replacing FBS with hPL does not compromise the therapeutic potential of these EVs. Testing the effects of EVs derived from hPL-cultured MSCs is therefore crucial to ensure their safety and suitability as a replacement for FBS in clinical applications. Our findings contribute to ongoing efforts exploring MSC-derived EVs as a cell-free therapeutic strategy for cancer and also lay the groundwork for future studies examining EV cargo (e.g., miRNAs, proteins, lipids) and in vivo efficacy. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to explore how the choice of FBS or hPL during Whorton’s jelly-derived mesenchymal stem cells (WJMSCs) culture impacts the biological effects of WJMSC-derived EVs (WJMSC-EVs) on cancer cells.

Materials and methods

WJMSCs were isolated verbal informed consent obtained from participants [29], following ethical guidelines. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the Cell Therapy Center (The University of Jordan) (IRB Number: IRB/7/2019). Human platelet lysate (hPL) preparation was also conducted as previously described [30]. WJMSCs at passage three (p3) were cultured as two groups in Essential Medium Eagle-Alpha Modification (Alpha MEM, Gibco), first group supplemented with 10% FBS (HyClone FBS, Invitrogen, USA), the second with 5% hPL, 1% of 100X Glutamax (Gibco, USA), and 1% of 100X antibiotic-antimycotic mixture (Gibco, USA). Breast cancer cell line (MCF7-HTB-22) and alveolar basal epithelial adenocarcinoma (A549, CCL-185) cell line were commercially obtained from (ATCC, USA). MCF7 and A549 were cultured in RPMI (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% of 100X Glutamax, and 1% of 100X antibiotic antimycotic. An overview of the methodology is summarized in Supplementary Figure 1.

Osteogenic differentiation

For osteogenic differentiation, WJMSC-hPL and WJMSC-FBS were seeded in triplicate at a density of 200,000 cells per well in 6-well plates and cultured in complete culture medium (CCM) until reaching 50% confluency. Then, the medium was replaced with osteogenic differentiation medium, consisting of Minimum Essential Medium Eagle-Alpha Modification (Alpha MEM, Gibco), supplemented with 15% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Hyclone), 1% 100X Glutamax (Gibco, USA), 1% 100X antibiotic-antimycotic solution (Gibco, USA), 10 mM dexamethasone, 50 μg/ml ascorbic acid 2-phosphate, and 10 mM β-glycerophosphate (Carbosynth, USA) [31]. Then the cells were cultured for 21–28 days or until calcium deposits were observed. Once mineral deposits were detected, one well per sample was stained with Alizarin Red to visualize the calcium deposits, and imaging was performed using the EVOS XL Core Imaging System.

Adipogenic differentiation

For adipogenic differentiation, WJMSC-hPL and WJMSC-FBS were cultured in triplicate at a density of 200,000 cells per well in 6-well plates in CCM and then switched to adipogenic differentiation medium composed of Alpha MEM supplemented with 15% FBS, 1% 100X Glutamax, 1% antibiotic-antimycotic solution, 10 mM dexamethasone, 500 μM 3-isobutyl-1-methylxanthine (IBMX), 0.2 mM indomethacin, and 10 μg/ml insulin [31]. Media changes were made every two days, and all cells were incubated under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 21% O2, and 5% CO2). The cells were maintained for 14–21 days until fat vacuoles appeared, after which they were stained with Oil Red-O and imaged. Control cells for both differentiation processes were kept in CCM, with medium changes every 2–3 days.

Characterization of WJMSC-FBS and WJMSC-hPL by flow cytometry

Wharton’s jelly mesenchymal stem cells cultured in FBS (WJMSC-FBS) and hPL (WJMSC-hPL) were characterized by assessing typical MSC surface markers [32]. Briefly, cells at p3 were harvested, washed with 1X PBS, and resuspended in 800 μl of 1% BSA (Abcam, USA) to block nonspecific binding of surface markers. WJMSC-FBS and WJMSC-hPL were then incubated in the dark for 30 minutes with monoclonal antibodies against CD90, CD105, CD73, and CD44, or with isotype control antibodies (all from Bioscience, USA). Following incubation, the cells were washed to remove any unbound antibodies. Surface markers were detected using a BD FACS Canto II flow cytometer and analyzed using BD FACSDiva software.

EVs collection

EVs were isolated from the serum-free conditioned medium (CM) collected 48 hours after exchanging the medium and once the cultures reached 70–80% confluency. Briefly, the CM were centrifuged at 300 x g for 10 minutes at 4 °C, followed by a second centrifugation at 2000 × g for 20 minutes at 4 °C. The supernatants were then filtered through 0.22 μm filter units to eliminate cell debris. Finally, EVs were collected via ultracentrifugation at 110,000 × g for 2 hours at 4 °C using the CS-FNX Micro Ultracentrifuge (Ibaraki, Japan) [33]. After purification, the resulting pellets were resuspended in 500 μl of filtered PBS (using a 0.22 μm filter), and their protein concentration was determined using the Micro BCA™ Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The samples concentration was adjusted to 1 mg/mL, divided into aliquots, resuspended in filtered PBS, and subsequently stored at −80 °C for further analysis

Flow cytometry analysis of EVs

For flow cytometry analysis, 3 μl of 4% w/v aldehyde sulfate-latex beads (4 μm; Invitrogen, USA) were incubated with the isolated EVs for 15 minutes at room temperature. The mixture was then gently shaken (270 rpm) and incubated overnight at 4 °C after adding 10 μl of filtered 1X PBS. To block nonspecific binding, the EVs-coated beads were incubated with 10 μl of 1M glycine for 30 minutes at room temperature. Subsequently, the exo-beads were incubated for 40 minutes at 37 °C with antibodies against EVs surface markers (A5488 anti-CD9, AF647 anti-CD81, and APC anti-CD63) or their corresponding isotype controls (all from Biolegend). After incubation, 150 µl of filtered PBS was added to the samples, and the presence of surface markers was analyzed using the BD FACS Canto II and processed with BD FACSDiva software [34,35].

EVs morphology

To validate the morphology of EVs, a 1:1 mixture of pure EVs and 2% paraformaldehyde (PFA) was prepared and applied to Formvar-carbon-coated electron microscopy (EM) grids. The grids were allowed to dry for 20 minutes at room temperature. Subsequently, they were washed and immersed in drops of 1% glutaraldehyde for 5 minutes. After this, the grids were rinsed seven times with distilled water, with each rinse lasting 2 minutes. To enhance contrast, the grids were treated with drops of uranyl-oxalate for 5 minutes, followed by a transfer to methyl cellulose-uranyl acetate (UA) for 10 minutes on ice. Finally, the grids were air-dried for 10 minutes before being analyzed using a Versa 3D FEI transmission electron microscope (TEM) at an acceleration voltage of 30 volts [36].

Size distribution analysis of EVs

The hydrodynamic size of the isolated EVs was assessed using dynamic light scattering (DLS) with a nanosizer instrument (ZetaView, Malvern Nano ZS, Worcestershire, UK). EVs samples were diluted in filtered PBS, and three measurements were performed for each sample, consisting of 10 runs with a duration of 60 seconds per run. The recording temperature was maintained at 24 °C and the results were analyzed using Zetasizer software version 7.11 (Malvern Instruments, UK) [36].

Cellular internalization of EVs

EVs isolated from both cell groups were labeled with 1,1′-dioctadecyl-3,3,3′,3′-tetramethylindocarbocyanine perchlorate fluorescent dye (DiI; Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol [37,38]. The labeled EVs were then collected in filtered 1X PBS and subjected to ultracentrifugation at 110,000 × g for 1.5 hours at 4 °C. After ultracentrifugation, the DiI-labeled WJMSC-FBS-EVs or WJMSC-hPL-EVs were resuspended in filtered 1X PBS and incubated for 16 hours with 1.5 × 105 MCF7 or A549 cells per well in serum-free RPMI. Following incubation, the cells were washed and stained with 5-chloromethylfluorescein diacetate dye (CMFDA; Invitrogen, USA) for 30 minutes at 37 °C to label the cell bodies. The cells were then fixed with 4% PFA, stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Invitrogen, USA), and mounted using anti-fade mounting medium (Abcam). Images were captured using a time-lapse microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany)

Cell proliferation assays

MCF7 and A549 cells were seeded in three 96-well plates at a density of 8 × 10³ cells per well in 100 μl of RPMI medium. After 24 hours, the culture medium was replaced with serum-free medium, and the cells were treated with 50 µg/ml of either WJMSC-FBS-EVs or WJMSC-hPL-EVs, with serum-free medium as a negative control. After 24 and 48 hours, 10 μl of MTT (Thiazolyl Blue Tetrazolium Bromide, ATCC, USA) reagent was added, followed by incubation for 3 hours at 37 °C. Subsequently, 100 μl of solubilization stop solution was added to each well, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C for an additional 30 minutes. The absorbance was then measured at 570 nm using a Biotek Cytation 5 and analyzed with BioTek Gen 5 data analysis software (BioTek, USA) [33,39].

Apoptotic analysis by flow cytometry

Annexin V-FITC/PI double labeling was used to evaluate apoptosis in MCF-7 and A549 cell lines, each seeded at 3 × 105 cells per well. The cells were cultured in serum-free RPMI and treated with 50 µg/ml of either WJMSC-FBS-EVs or WJMSC-hPL-EVs. After 48 hours of treatment, the cells were harvested by trypsinization and stained with FITC-conjugated Annexin V and propidium iodide (PI), following the manufacturer’s instructions (eBioscience™). The samples were analyzed using a BD FACS Canto II flow cytometer, and data were processed with BD FACSDiva software [33,39].

Wound healing assay

Approximately 3 × 105 MCF7 or A549 cells per well were seeded in 6-well plates. Once the cultures reached 90% confluency, a scratch was made in the monolayer using a sterile 200 µm pipette tip. The cells were then washed three times with 1X PBS to remove debris and floating cells. Following this, the cultures were treated with 50 µg/ml of either WJMSC-FBS-EVs or WJMSC-hPL-EVs, with serum-free medium (SFM) used as a negative control. Images of the cells were captured at 0-, 9-, 20-, and 47-hours post-treatment. The distance of cell migration was quantified using ImageJ software for analysis [40].

Senescence assay

Senescence detection was performed using a senescence detection kit (Abcam, cat #ab65351) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, MCF7 or A549 cells were seeded at a density of 3 × 105 cells per well. The following day, medium was replaced with serum-free RPMI containing 50 μg/ml of either WJMSC-FBS-EVs or WJMSC-hPL-EVs and after 48 hours of treatment, cells were washed once with 1X PBS and fixed with a fixative solution and incubated for 15 minutes at room temperature. The cells were then stained with a staining solution mixture and incubated at 37 °C for 12 hours. Senescent cells, identified by their blue color, were counted under a microscope and compared to untreated control cells [41,42].

Gene expression

Cancer cells were treated with 50 μg/ml of EVs derived from WJMSCs cultured in either FBS or PL media for 48 hours. Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy® Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany), and reverse transcription was performed using the PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix (Takara, Japan). Quantitative real-time PCR was conducted using TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (Takara) on a CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, USA). Reactions were run in triplicate with GAPDH as housekeeping gene. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. Primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.2.3 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). Statistical comparisons were made using unpaired Student’s t-tests or two-way ANOVA, with Bonferroni post-hoc tests applied when appropriate. Statistical significance was set at a p-value of ≤ 0.05, with significance levels indicated as *≤ 0.05, **≤ 0.01, ***≤ 0.001, and ****≤ 0.0001. All experiments were conducted in triplicate (n = 3).

Results

Both WJMSC-FBS and WJMSC-hPL showed characteristics of MSCs with no significant differences

Both WJMSC types cultured with either FBS and hPL exhibited high levels of MSC surface markers, including CD105, CD90, CD73, and CD44, while showing the absence of negative MSC markers cocktail and Tra-1-60 and Nanog, which are pluripotency-specific markers (Supplementary Figure 2(A,B)). No significant differences in MSC surface marker expression were observed between the two group of cells. Moreover, Alizarin Red staining, which specifically stains calcium deposits in osteocytes, demonstrated the presence of mineralized extracellular matrix in both WJMSC-FBS and WJMSC-hPL cultures, confirming their osteogenic differentiation potential (Supplementary Figure 1(C)). To assess the cells’ ability to differentiate into adipocytes, Oil Red O staining was performed to visualize fat vacuoles in the adipocytes. The results showed that both WJMSC-hPL and WJMSC-FBS successfully differentiated into adipocytes as evident by lipid droplet accumulation in both groups, confirming their adipogenic differentiation potential (Supplementary Figure 1(C)).

TEM imaging demonstrated the characteristic round morphology, a typical feature of exosomes, in both groups (Supplementary Figure 1(D)). Size distribution measurements showed a heterogeneous population of WJMSC-FBS-derived EVs with two distinct size groups (30 nm and 137 nm) and an average size of 141 nm for WJMSC-hPL-derived EVs (Supplementary Figure 1(E)). The sizes of EVs from both WJMSC-hPL (WJMSC-hPL-EVs) and WJMSC-FBS (WJMSC-FBS-EVs) were within the typical exosomal size range of 30–150 nm, confirming their identity as small extracellular vesicles. Additionally, the isolated EVs from both groups were successfully characterized by the presence of typical EV surface markers (CD9, CD81, and CD63), with no significant differences observed in their expression levels between the two groups. For WJMSC-FBS-EVs, the expression percentages were as follows: 90% CD9, 80% CD81, and 61% CD63. Similarly, WJMSC-hPL- EVs showed expression levels of 85% CD9, 79% CD81, and 56% CD63, with no statistically significant differences between the two groups (Supplementary Figure 1(F,G)). These results confirm the successful isolation of EVs from WJMSCs cultured under both FBS- and hPL-based conditions. The isolated vesicles are likely enriched in small, exosome-like EVs; however, the potential co-isolation of other EV subtypes cannot be entirely ruled out.

EVs uptake successfully verified in MCF7 and A549 cancer cells

The isolated EVs were evaluated for cellular uptake in MCF7, A549, and fibroblast cells. The EVs were labeled with DiI-O, a fluorescent dye that emits an orange-red color, while the cytoplasm of the cancer cells was stained with CMFDA (green) and their nuclei with DAPI (blue). After a 12-hour incubation with DiI-O-labeled EVs derived from either WJMSC-hPL or WJMSC-FBS, the MCF7 and A549 cells were fixed and analyzed using a fluorescence microscope. The results confirmed the successful internalization of both types of EVs into MCF7 (Figure 1(A)), A549 (Figure 1(B)), and fibroblasts and HUVEC’s cells (Supplementary Figure 3(A,B)).

Uptake assay of WJMSC-derived EVs in MCF7 and A549 cells. (A) Successful internalization of Dil-O-labeled EVs (red) into MCF7 cells. (B) Successful internalization of Dil-O-labeled EVs into A549 cells. The cytoplasm of cancer cells was stained with CMFDA (green), and nuclei were stained with DAPI (blue).

WJMSC-derived EVs reduce MCF7 proliferation and exhibit a transient antitumor effect in A549 cells

After 24 hours of treatment with either WJMSC-FBS-EVs or WJMSC-hPL-EVs, a significant decrease in cell proliferation was observed in both MCF7 and A549 cells compared to the untreated controls. However, at 48 hours, the decrease in proliferation was maintained in MCF7 cells treated with both type of EVs, whereas in A549 cells, the proliferation rate showed no significant difference compared to controls for either type of EVs (Figure 2(A,B)). These results indicate different effects on proliferation depending on the type of cancer cell line used. Furthermore, the examination of EVs effect on fibroblast proliferation, which served as control cells, revealed no significant impact (Figure 2(C)). This suggests that the effects of EVs could be limited to cancer cells. Surprisingly, in MCF7 cells, although a reduced proliferation rate was observed at 48 hours of treatment with either set of EVs, there was no significant increase in apoptotic cell numbers compared to the control group (Figure 2(D)). Indicating that the reduction in proliferation did not correlate with increased apoptosis in this cell line. This suggests that an alternative mechanism underlying the antiproliferative effects of WJMSC-derived EVs on cancer cells. However, treatment with either WJMSC-FBS-EVs or WJMSC-hPL-EVs in A549 cells did not result in a significant change in apoptotic cell numbers at 48 hours compared to the control group (Figure 2(E)). However, the treatment significantly reduced A549 cell proliferation at 24 hours, though this effect was transient and did not persist at 48 hours. This suggests that the effects of WJMSC-derived EVs on A549 cells may not involve alterations in apoptosis, further highlighting the differences in response to EVs treatment between the two cancer cell lines.

MTT assay results showed a reduced proliferation in (A) MCF7 cells at 24 and 48 hours, and in (B) A549 cells at 24 hours and no significant effect in (C) fibroblasts. After treatment with either WJMSC-FBS-Exos or WJMSC-hPL-Exos, Annexin V/PI staining demonstrated a significant increase in live cells and a reduction in necrotic cells following WJMSC-hPL-Exos treatment in (D) MCF7, while no significant effect was observed in (E) A549 cells after 48 hours of treatment. The quadrants represent: Q1 (necrotic cells), Q2 (late apoptotic cells), Q3 (live cells), and Q4 (early apoptotic cells). Data are presented as mean ± SD. **p ≤ 0.01, ***p ≤ 0.001, ****p ≤ 0.0001.

EVs treatment results in increased senescent cell population in MCF7 and A549 cells

EVs treatment exhibited significant increase in the percentage of Senescence-associated β-galactosidase-positive (SA-β-gal) cells compared to untreated control with WJMSC-PL-EVs showing significantly higher percentage of positive cells compared to WJMSC-FBS-EVs (Figure 3(A,B)). The SA-β-gal upregulation in MCF7 cells by EVs treatment indicates that EVs may promote tumor suppression by inducing growth arrest. Similarly, in A549 cells, treatment with both WJMSC-FBS-EVs and WJMSC-hPL-EVs resulted in a significant increase in SA-β-gal positive cells (Figure 3(C,D)). These findings indicate that both EV groups promote senescence-like phenotype, contributing to tumor suppression in MCF7 and A549 cells.

(B) and (D) Graphs demonstrate (B) elevated senescence in MCF7 cells and (D) reduced senescence in A549 cells after treatment with both WJMSC-hPL-EVs and WJMSC-FBS-EVs. WJMSC-PL-EV treatment moderately enhances migration in MCF7 and exerts a time-dependent effect on migration in A549 cells

MCF7 cells demonstrated a consistent increase in migration at the 9-, 20-, and 47-hour time points after treatment with WJMSC-hPL-EVs, compared to both the untreated control and WJMSC-FBS-EVs. No significant difference was observed between the untreated control and WJMSC-FBS-EVs (Figure 4(A,B)). Conversely, A549 cells displayed varying effects at different time points. There was no notable change in migration rate in A549 cells at approximately 9 hours post-treatment; however, at around 20 hours, WJMSC-hPL-EVs significantly enhanced migration compared to both the control and WJMSC-FBS-EVs. However, at approximately 47 hours post-scratch, the migration rate showed a completely opposite trend, with enhanced migration observed in the presence of WJMSC-FBS-EVs and reduced migration in the presence of WJMSC-hPL-EVs (Figure 4(C,D)). These results are intriguing, as they appear to contradict the observed decrease in proliferation and reveal inconsistent, time-dependent outcomes in A549 cells. This suggests that EVs may differentially regulate proliferation and migration in these cancer cell line.

To further investigate these observations, gene expression analysis of migration-associated genes (vimentin (VIM), matrix metalloproteinase-2 (MMP2), and C-X-C chemokine receptor type 4 (CXCR4)) was conducted by qRT-PCR. These genes were selected because they play key roles in cancer cell migration: VIM is involved in epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) and motility [43], MMP2 mediates extracellular matrix degradation [44], and CXCR4 regulates chemotactic migration [45]. In MCF7 cells, WJMSC-PL-EV treatment modestly upregulated these genes compared with both the untreated control and WJMSC-FBS-EVs, aligning with the enhanced migration phenotype (Figure 4(E)). In contrast, A549 cells showed minimal changes in the expression of migration-related genes across both treatment conditions. The fold changes for VIM, MMP2, and CXCR4 remained close to 1 in both the WJMSC-FBS- and WJMSC-PL-EVs groups, indicating a lack of significant transcriptional response and suggesting that the observed variations in migration are unlikely to be driven by changes at the gene expression level (Figure 4(F)).

Discussion

Stem cells are essential in tissue repair and regenerative medicine due to their abilities to self-renew and differentiation potential [46]. Among various stem cell types, MSCs are particularly important for their multipotency and regenerative potential [8]. Traditionally, FBS has been used as a standard culture supplement; however, ethical concerns and scientific limitations associated with FBS have prompted the investigation of alternatives such as hPL [13,14].

In this study, we compared the effects of EVs derived from WJMSC cultured in hPL versus FBS on cancer cell lines, specifically MCF7 (breast cancer) and A549 (lung cancer) cells. Our findings demonstrate how the cell culture medium composition influences EVs functionality and their subsequent impact on cancer cell behavior.

First, our study confirmed that both WJMSC-FBS and WJMSC-hPL meet the International 2006 ISCT minimal criteria for mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) [47]. Both sources exhibited typical MSC characteristics, including the expression of human MSC surface markers (CD90, CD105, CD73, and CD44), differentiation potential into adipogenic and osteogenic lineages, fibroblast-like morphology, and the ability to adhere to plastic surfaces. Importantly, no significant differences were observed between WJMSCs cultured in FBS and those cultured in hPL, indicating that the choice of culture medium does not substantially affect the fundamental characteristics of MSCs.

The characterization of EVs derived from WJMSCs cultured in FBS and hPL demonstrated consistent expression of EV surface markers CD9, CD81, and CD63 across both culture conditions. In terms of size distribution, DLS revealed a slightly more heterogeneous population of WJMSC-FBS-EVs, which exhibited two distinct size groups, whereas WJMSC-hPL-EVs maintained a more uniform size profile. Both sets of EVs fell within the typical size range of exosomes (30–150 nm), suggesting they are enriched in exosome-like small EVs. Additionally, TEM images confirmed the characteristic round morphology commonly associated with exosomes in both culture conditions [48]. These findings suggest that while culture media may influence the distribution of vesicle sizes, the fundamental features typically attributed to exosomes, such as marker expression and morphology, remain consistent across different culture conditions. This consistency supports the robustness of the EVs isolation method and emphasizes that both FBS and hPL can serve as viable media options for producing small EVs enriched in exosome-like populations in WJMSC cultures.

The proliferation assays revealed differential effects of EVs on MCF7 and A549 cells. Notably, treatment with both types of EVs led to decreased proliferation in MCF7 and A549 cells at 24 hours. However, this effect was sustained at 48 hours only in MCF7 cells, while it ceased in A549 cells. The differential proliferative effects of EVs on MCF7 and A549 cells are not surprising, given the conflicting findings regarding MSC-derived EVs in different tumor cell models [25,49,50]. These variations may arise from multiple factors, including the tumor model used, the inherent heterogeneity of MSCs derived from different sources, and differences in MSC isolation, processing, and experimental methodologies [28,51]. For example, Lin et al. [52] demonstrated that exosomes derived from adipose tissue MSCs (ADMSC-Exosomes) promote MCF7 cell migration through the WNT-mediated pathway. In contrast, Ali et al. [53] found that ADMSC-Exosomes suppress MCF7 metastasis progression via exosomal miRNA. Furthermore, Dong et al. [54] reported that extracellular vesicles derived from human umbilical cord MSCs promote lung adenocarcinoma progression, whereas Yan et al. [55] observed that exosomes from bone marrow-derived MSCs (BMSC-Exosomes) reduce A549 cell progression. These findings highlight the conflicting effects of MSC-derived EVs/exosomes on cancer.

The observed reduction in cell proliferation in MCF7 cells at 48 hours, without corresponding increases in apoptosis, may suggest an alternative pathway of growth inhibition, such as the induction of cellular senescence [56]. Indeed, quantification of Senescence-associated β-galactosidase showed an increase in senescent MCF7 and A549 cells at 48 hours post-treatment. This suggests that the induced growth inhibition in MCF7 and A549 cells could primarily occur through a senescence-like growth arrest.

One of the most intriguing findings in this study was the impact of WJMSC-derived EVs on cell migration. In MCF7 cells, WJMSC-hPL-EVs significantly enhanced migration compared to both the control and WJMSC-FBS-EVs, indicating a more potent pro-migratory effect of EVs derived from hPL. This effect was consistent with the upregulation of migration-related genes, including MMP2, VIM, and CXCR4, as assessed by qRT-PCR at 48 hours post-treatment. This pro-migratory effect was observed despite a reduction in proliferation, which contrasts the common assumption that migration and proliferation are positively correlated [57]. Notably, previous studies have also reported similar results, where increased migration did not always coincide with increased proliferation, suggesting that these processes may be regulated independently in cancer cells [58–61].

In A549 cells, migration showed a time-dependent response. While WJMSC-PL-EVs promoted migration at 20 hours post-treatment, this effect was reversed at 47 hours, where WJMSC-PL-EVs inhibited migration and WJMSC-FBS-EVs enhanced it. This suggests that the effect of EVs on migration is dynamic and may depend on the timing and duration of treatment. However, gene expression analysis of migration-related markers (MMP2, VIM, and CXCR4) by qRT-PCR demonstrated a contrasting pattern, with marginally increased expression in the PL-EVs group and downregulation in the FBS-EVs group. However, the fold changes observed in both groups were close to 1, suggesting these differences are unlikely to be biologically significant.

Moreover, building on the findings of Qi et al. [38], which include a time-dependent uptake of EVs derived from BMSCs by both non-senescent and senescent BMSCs, it is crucial to note that they observed differences in the uptake mechanisms between these cell types. These findings shed light on potential differences in the uptake mechanisms of EVs depending on the cell type [38]. Such differences highlight the need for further studies to investigate how EVs uptake influences the outcomes of MSC-EVs treatments. Overall, the differential and time-dependent regulation of migration in A549 cells underscores the complexity of EVs-mediated effects on cancer cell migration. Interestingly, several studies linked increasing in senescence in cancer cells to more aggressive and migratory behaviors along to decreased proliferation in cancer [62–64]. Suggesting that the increase migratory potential observed in cancer cells in this study may be due to increase senescence after treatment with WJMSC-EVs. However, these results in MCF7 and A549 post-treatment encourage further investigation into the complex relationship between proliferation and migration in cancer biology and suggest that different regulatory mechanisms may govern these processes.

While MCF7 and A549 cells showed similar reductions in proliferation and increased senescence-like features following WJMSC-EVs treatment, their migration behavior assessed by scratch assay was different. Since the scratch assay can be influenced by both migration and proliferation [65], it has limitations in accurately measuring true cell motility. This could partly explain the discrepancy between the scratch assay results and the qRT-PCR data, where migration-related gene expression showed only marginal changes, suggesting limited biological impact. To gain clearer insights into the effects of EVs on cancer cell motility, more specific assays such as Transwell migration and invasion assays are recommended. The controversial effects of MSC-derived EVs on cancer may be due to differences in EVs cargo and cell-type specific responses [28]. Future studies employing more precise functional assays and in vivo models are essential to elucidate the mechanisms underlying these observations.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the effects of EVs derived from MSCs cultured in FBS- or PL-based medium on cancer cells. Our results demonstrated minimal differences between EVs from WJMSCs cultured in PL or FBS. Similarly, Seo et al. [66] reported minimal differences in MSC characteristics when cultured in allogeneic PL- or FBS-based medium, whereas Becherucci et al. [67] showed that hPL enhances MSC proliferation and induces morphological changes. A systematic review by Palombella et al. [68] further confirmed that PL increases MSC proliferation compared to FBS, with no apparent effects on other key MSC features. However, they emphasized the need for the optimization of PL preparation protocols, as their effects on MSCs remain unclear [68]. These findings suggest that the minimal differences observed in WJMSC-derived EVs may be attributed to the limited impact of PL and FBS on MSC characteristics. However, this study did not analyze EVs content (e.g., miRNAs, proteins, lipids), which would be crucial to fully understanding whether significant differences arise from the choice of culture medium. Further studies need to be conducted to analyze EVs content, including miRNAs, proteins, and lipids, to determine whether significant differences arise from the choice of culture medium. Additionally, the effects of PL dose and preparation protocols on MSCs warrant further investigation.

Conclusions

Overall, this study suggests that WJMSC-derived EVs, regardless of the culture medium, have distinct effects on cancer cell lines, influencing proliferation, migration, and senescence of cancer cells. The differing responses observed between MCF7 and A549 cells underscore the importance of cancer cell type and culture conditions in shaping the biological effects of MSC-derived EVs. Although minimal functional differences were observed between EVs derived from cells cultured in FBS versus hPL, the use of hPL offers a clear advantage as a xeno-free alternative. This supports its preference in clinical-grade EV production. Future studies should aim to elucidate the molecular mechanisms and signaling pathways underlying the migration–proliferation paradox observed in these models. A deeper understanding of these pathways may reveal novel therapeutic opportunities for MSC-derived EVs in cancer treatment.
