Trisomy 18 presenting with complete bilateral radial aplasia: a rare case report
Authors: Mahdi W. Suboh, Mones H. Atatre, Noor Shwieki, Aseel A. Nassar, Motee Abu Awwad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTrisomy 18 (Edwards syndrome) is the second most common autosomal trisomy and is associated with severe multisystem congenital anomalies and high neonatal mortality. Typical limb findings include clenched fists with overlapping fingers; however, radial ray defects are also recognized within its phenotypic spectrum.Case presentationWe report a preterm male infant born at 33 + 6 weeks’ gestation who presented with severe respiratory distress, complex congenital heart disease, profound symmetrical growth restriction, and complete bilateral radial aplasia with absent thumbs. Antenatal ultrasonography had demonstrated intrauterine growth restriction, congenital heart disease, and upper limb anomalies, raising suspicion of an underlying syndromic condition. Postnatal peripheral blood karyotyping, sent within the first days of life, confirmed a uniform 47, XY, + 18 karyotype. The infant required prolonged mechanical ventilation and intensive supportive care but remained ventilator dependent. Despite ongoing management, he developed progressive cardiorespiratory failure and died at 61 days of life.ConclusionThis case highlights a severe expression of radial ray involvement within the phenotypic spectrum of Trisomy 18 and underscores the importance of comprehensive prenatal evaluation, cytogenetic confirmation, and careful multidisciplinary counseling when significant limb reduction defects are identified.

Background

Trisomy 18 is a chromosomal condition occurs on by an extra copy of chromosome 18 and is a the second most common autosomal trisomy after trisomy 21 [1]. It is associated with multiple severe congenital anomalies involving the cardiovascular, musculoskeletal, nervous, digestive, and genitourinary systems, as well as orofacial clefts and abdominal wall defects such as omphalocele, and carries a high rate of fetal and neonatal mortality [2].

The prognosis of trisomy 18 is poor. Approximately 70% of affected pregnancies result in spontaneous fetal loss. Among pregnancies that progress to delivery, the incidence of preterm birth is high, and 40% of fetuses do not survive labor [2]. Only about 13.5% of affected infants are born alive, with a reported median survival ranging from 3 to 14.5 days and only one in 10 newborns reach the first year of life [2]. Currently, diagnosis is typically made prenatal period based on screening by maternal age or maternal serum marker screening and amniocentesis, followed by pregnancy termination in a significant percentage of cases [2].

Among liveborn infants with Trisomy 18, prognostic factors such as congenital heart disease, respiratory fragility, and quality of life strongly influence family decision-making. Congenital heart disease is present in over 90% of cases, with 63% of pediatric cardiologists considering surgery in selected infants [3]. Respiratory complications further impact outcomes, and many families are offered redirection to comfort-focused care. Contemporary evidence emphasizes individualized counseling and shared decision-making, providing parents with information on both palliative care and medically indicated interventions [4].

We report a rare case of a preterm male infant with trisomy 18. The infant presented with completely absent thumbs and total bilateral absence of the radius, a severe and less frequently described bilateral expression of radial ray involvement within the phenotypic spectrum of Trisomy 18. Accurate diagnosis, appropriate prenatal counseling, and comprehensive understanding of the full clinical spectrum of trisomy 18 are essential for recognizing atypical phenotypic features.

Clinical Timeline of the Patient

The infant was born to a 35-year-old mother in a non-consanguineous marriage with no significant family history of congenital or genetic disorders. The antenatal course was uneventful, and antenatal corticosteroids were administered two weeks prior to delivery.

Antenatal ultrasonography revealed multiple fetal abnormalities, including intrauterine growth restriction, polyhydramnios, and congenital heart disease characterized by a ventricular septal defect and an overriding aorta. Additionally, upper limb anomalies were observed, such as bilateral hand deformities and absence of the radius, along with a small stomach, raising suspicion of an underlying syndromic condition. Prenatal genetic counseling was provided, and the family was informed about the suspected chromosomal abnormality, potential prognosis, and possible clinical outcomes.

The infant was delivered via cesarean section due to a prior cesarean delivery. At birth, the patient was flaccid, cyanotic, and apneic, necessitating immediate resuscitation. Initial drying and stimulation proved ineffective, and bag-mask ventilation did not yield clinical improvements. The neonate developed secondary apnea accompanied by bradycardia and was successfully intubated at 4.5 min of life. The infant had Apgar scores of 5, 7, and 8 at 1, 5, and 10 min, respectively. The patient was subsequently transferred to the NICU and given supplemental oxygen.

At birth, the infant exhibited severe symmetrical growth restriction, with all anthropometric measurements falling below the 3rd percentile: a birth weight of 1.37 kg, a length of 37 cm, and a head circumference of 29 cm.

During the initial hours of life, the neonate was found to be hypoactive and intubated upon physical examination. Several dysmorphic features were noted, such as bilateral radial aplasia with absent thumbs, rudimentary upper limbs, and a low-set ear (Fig. 1). Bilateral upper limb radiographs confirmed complete absence of the radius on both sides (Fig. 2). The cardiovascular examination revealed a systolic murmur and palpable femoral pulses, while the other systems appeared unremarkable and appropriate for gestational age.

At one day of life, echocardiography identified complex congenital heart disease, including severe aortic valve stenosis, a large subaortic ventricular septal defect, an overriding aorta, mild pulmonary stenosis, tricuspid regurgitation, a patent ductus arteriosus (3 mm), and a patent foramen ovale (3 mm). Due to recurrent desaturation episodes, a prostaglandin E1 infusion was initiated on the third day of life at a standard neonatal dose and continued as part of supportive management.

Transfontanel ultrasonography performed on one day of life and repeated during hospitalization demonstrated no evidence of intraventricular hemorrhage, ventricular dilatation, or periventricular leukomalacia. Renal ultrasonography performed on day 3 of life revealed mild bilateral hydronephrosis, with an anteroposterior diameter of the pelvicalyceal system of approximately 5 mm, which remained unchanged on follow-up imaging.

Given the presence of severe intrauterine growth restriction, complex congenital heart disease, and multiple skeletal anomalies, a genetic evaluation was performed. At 14 days of life, peripheral blood karyotyping revealed a uniform abnormal male karyotype: 47, XY, + 18, with all analyzed cells demonstrating trisomy 18, thereby confirming the diagnosis of trisomy 18 (Fig. 3). Throughout hospitalization, the infant remained dependent on mechanical ventilation, with multiple unsuccessful extubation attempts attributed to airway abnormalities necessitating the use of a small-caliber endotracheal tube, a finding that is commonly associated with trisomy 18. The respiratory course was further complicated by respiratory distress syndrome and persistent hypercapnia. Escalation to prolonged mechanical ventilation was required due to persistent respiratory failure and repeated failed extubation attempts.

Despite comprehensive supportive and intensive care, the infant’s condition progressively worsened as a result of severe multisystem involvement related to trisomy 18, particularly complex congenital heart disease and respiratory failure. The patient remained ventilator-dependent and developed refractory cardiorespiratory compromise, leading to death during the course of hospitalization.

Discussion

Trisomy 18 is the second most common autosomal trisomy after trisomy 21, occurring in approximately 1 in 3,600 to 10,000 live births [1]. It is associated with severe, often fatal, multisystem congenital anomalies, including cardiac defects, orofacial clefts, abdominal wall defects, tracheoesophageal malformations, genitourinary anomalies, limb abnormalities, and central nervous system defects [1]. The condition shows a female predominance (3:2), with higher fetal loss in males, and only 5% to 10% of affected infants survive beyond the first year [1]. Typical limb findings include clenched fists with overlapping fingers (second and fifth digits overriding the third and fourth) and rocker-bottom feet with prominent calcanei [5].

The presence of complete bilateral radial aplasia in this case represents a severe and less frequently described bilateral presentation within the known spectrum of radial ray involvement in Trisomy 18. Although this finding is not part of the classic limb phenotype in trisomy 18, radial abnormalities, including hypoplasia or aplasia, have been reported in a small number of cases, reflecting phenotypic variability [6, 7]. This finding highlights phenotypic variability in trisomy 18 and may cause diagnostic uncertainty, especially prenatally. In radial ray deficiencies in general, unilateral involvement is more frequently reported than bilateral forms, which may explain why complete bilateral presentations are less commonly emphasized in the literature.

Radial ray defects arise from disruption of upper limb bud development during the fourth to seventh weeks of gestation. During this critical embryonic period, the apical ectodermal ridge and zone of polarizing activity coordinate proximodistal and anteroposterior limb patterning through complex molecular signaling pathways involving sonic hedgehog (SHH) and fibroblast growth factor (FGF) [8]. The extra genetic material from chromosome 18 in trisomy 18 disrupts normal embryonic development [5, 6], though the specific mechanisms linking trisomy 18 to radial aplasia remain incompletely understood and warrant further investigation.

Radial ray defects are more commonly associated with other syndromes such as TAR, Fanconi anemia, and VACTERL, highlighting the need for comprehensive multisystem evaluation and genetic assessment when encountered in trisomy 18 [8]. Fanconi anemia is characterized by radial defects in approximately 40% of cases, combined with progressive bone marrow failure, café-au-lait spots, and chromosomal instability [9]. Thrombocytopenia-absent radius (TAR) syndrome presents with absent radii but characteristically preserved thumbs (a key distinguishing feature), along with neonatal thrombocytopenia that develops in utero or within the first months of life [10]. VACTERL association (vertebral defects, anal atresia, cardiac defects, tracheoesophageal fistula, renal anomalies, and limb abnormalities) represents another important differential, though radial defects in VACTERL are often associated with absent thumbs [11]. These overlapping phenotypes underscore the critical importance of comprehensive cytogenetic testing, as reliance on isolated limb anomalies may result in misdiagnosis.

From a prenatal perspective, major limb reduction defects such as radial aplasia can be detected by second-trimester ultrasonography, with a reported sensitivity of 76% for limb reduction defects involving the long bones [12]. Prenatal ultrasonographic examination of fetal forearms can detect preaxial limb reduction, including radial aplasia, and may facilitate the diagnosis of trisomy 18 when suspected on imaging. Ultrasonographic detection of preaxial upper limb reduction has been documented in multiple cases and underscores the importance of evaluating limb structures carefully during fetal anatomy scans [13]. Early detection allows for appropriate genetic counseling and informed parental decision-making.

Prognosis in trisomy 18 is generally poor, especially in individuals with complex congenital heart anomalies, with most affected infants not surviving past their first year of life [1]. While bilateral radial aplasia does not directly impact survival, such severe limb malformations may reflect extensive embryologic disruption during critical developmental periods and potentially coexist with significant internal organ involvement. The presence of this rare skeletal anomaly in the current case, combined with other typical trisomy 18 features, reinforces the need for comprehensive multisystem evaluation.

As a single case observation, generalizability is limited, and the absence of molecular analyses beyond standard karyotyping restricts mechanistic interpretation. Nevertheless, reporting rare phenotypic associations such as bilateral radial aplasia in trisomy 18 contributes to improved diagnostic awareness, more accurate prenatal counseling, and future refinement of genotype-phenotype correlations in trisomy 18. Continued documentation of atypical presentations will enhance our understanding of the full clinical spectrum of this chromosomal disorder and assist clinicians in recognizing the diverse manifestations that may be encountered.

Although radial ray defects are well recognized in Trisomy 18, complete bilateral absence of the radius appears less frequently described compared with unilateral involvement. Emphasizing this distinction is important to avoid diagnostic confusion with other radial ray syndromes, particularly in the prenatal setting.

Historically, Trisomy 18 was considered a “lethal” condition, with early reports describing median survival of only days to months and less than 10% survival beyond the first year [14, 15]. However, recent evidence shows that prognosis differs significantly when active intervention is pursued. A 2023 study reported a 33% one-year survival rate among infants receiving intensive care compared to 0% in those without intensive management, with half of the intensively treated infants discharged alive [14].

Congenital heart disease remains a key determinant of mortality. A 2025 meta-analysis demonstrated that cardiac surgery significantly reduced in-hospital mortality (OR 0.12) and increased survival to 12 months (OR 19.77) compared with palliative care [16]. Although long-term survival is uncommon, a population-based cohort study reported 8.6% survival to 10 years, with markedly higher survival in mosaic or partial trisomy (43.8%) compared to full trisomy (6.6%), and rare survival into adolescence has been documented [15].

Conclusion

This case highlights a severe bilateral skeletal manifestation within the recognized phenotypic spectrum of Trisomy 18 in the form of complete bilateral radial aplasia with absent thumbs. Although radial ray defects are well documented in Trisomy 18, complete bilateral involvement represents a less commonly emphasized presentation compared with the classic clenched hand pattern or unilateral forms. Recognition of this presentation is important, particularly in the prenatal setting, to avoid diagnostic confusion with other radial ray syndromes and to facilitate accurate genetic counseling. Careful documentation of the full phenotypic spectrum of Trisomy 18 contributes to improved diagnostic awareness, prognostic assessment, and informed multidisciplinary decision-making for clinicians and families.
