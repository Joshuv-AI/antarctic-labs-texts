Multiscale Structuring of Mycorrhizal Fungal Communities of Tropical Epiphytic Orchids
Authors: Juan Pablo Suárez, Stefania Cevallos, Paulo Herrera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Symbiotic interactions with root-associated fungi are essential for orchid germination, nutrient acquisition and survival, yet the factors structuring these symbioses in tropical epiphytic orchids remain poorly understood. Tropical montane forests provide an ideal system for examining these interactions because steep environmental gradients occur over short geographic distances. We investigated how elevation, variation among populations within elevational belts and host identity shape root-associated fungal communities in the southern Ecuadorian Andes, a region of exceptional epiphytic orchid diversity. Root samples from 699 individuals representing 11 orchid species were analyzed using ITS2 amplicon sequencing. A total of 4,697 operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were recovered, including 271 putative orchid mycorrhizal fungi. Fungal richness peaked at mid elevation and declined at higher elevations. Community composition varied significantly among elevational belts, sites and host species, with differences among elevations driven primarily by species turnover rather than nestedness. Despite this turnover, mycorrhizal assemblages maintained a persistent core across elevations, while site-level heterogeneity contributed to fine-scale differentiation. These results indicate that fungal community assembly in epiphytic orchids is structured across multiple spatial scales by elevational gradients, local environmental conditions and host identity. The mid-elevation diversity peak and stronger filtering at higher elevations indicate that orchid–fungus symbioses are highly sensitive to environmental gradients, with potential consequences for their stability under ongoing environmental change in tropical montane forests.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00248-026-02830-2.

Introduction

Orchidaceae is among the largest angiosperm families, with around 30,000 species distributed across almost every biome [1]. Most species produce dust‑like, achlorophyllous seeds that contain virtually no nutritive reserves, so seed germination and early seedling establishment depend entirely on symbiotic fungi [2]. Orchidaceae is globally widespread; however, species richness is particularly high in the Neotropics [3] and epiphytic taxa, growing on tree trunks or branches rather than in soil, account for roughly 75% of all orchid species [4]. Ecuador provides an exceptional case with about 4,000 orchid species, more than 40% of which are endemic [5].

The association between orchids and their mycorrhizal partners is mediated by specialized structures into the root cortex called pelotons, which form the interface for nutrient exchange between the two partners [6]. Fungal symbionts are essential for orchid growth and survival, especially in nutrient-poor environments, as in the case of epiphytic orchids, where plants are exposed to harsh conditions [7]. The most frequent orchid mycorrhizal fungi (OMF) belong to the Basidiomycota, including members of Tulasnellaceae, Ceratobasidiaceae, Serendipitaceae [8], and Atractiellales [9]. Mycorrhizal specificity varies among orchids and ranges from specialist relationships, where one or a few fungal lineages suffice [10], to generalist associations involving multiple taxa [11].

In addition to OMF, orchid roots host a rich assemblage of non-mycorrhizal fungi [12] commonly referred to as root‑associated fungal endophytes (RAFE). Unlike OMF, RAFE colonize root tissues without forming peloton‑like coils; however, they do not elicit pathogenic symptoms in the host [13, 14]. The ecological interaction between orchids and their endophytic partners spans a continuum of commensalism – where the fungus benefits without harming the plant – to mutualism and, under stress or genetic change, pathogenicity [13, 15]. RAFE are taxonomically diverse, with over 110 fungal genera documented, predominantly Ascomycetes, with frequent representatives from Chaetothyriales, Hypocreales, Helotiales, and Capnodiales [12]. The most frequent fungal isolates from epiphytic orchid roots in the Neotropics belonged to Hypocreales, Helotiales, and Xylariales [16, 17]. RAFE can secrete growth hormones improving nutrient availability and plant growth [13].

Elucidating the diversity and community structure of OMF and RAFE requires to consider both abiotic and biotic drivers. Soil properties such as pH, carbon‑to‑nitrogen ratio, and available phosphorus strongly influence the diversity and structure of root‑associated fungi in terrestrial orchids. For epiphytic orchids, whose substrates include bark and accumulated detritus, bark pH and water‑holding capacity appear to influence epiphytic orchid establishment and associated fungal communities [18]. Different fungal taxa have varying preferences for specific environmental conditions, resulting in unique assemblages in different habitats. Understanding these patterns across environmental gradients can help predict fungal responses to environmental change. In particular, elevation gradients can influence orchid mycorrhizal and endophytic communities in terrestrial orchids [19], although consistent evidence for similar patterns in OMF or RAFE communities of epiphytic orchids remains scarce [20, 21]. Beyond abiotic filters, orchid host identity itself can determine fungal assemblages. Specific orchid genera or species often show preferences for mycorrhizal lineages, such as Serendipita or Tulasnella, while others associate with a broad spectrum of fungi [12].

Despite increased interest in orchid–fungus interactions, most research has focused on terrestrial species from temperate regions. Tropical epiphytic orchids constitute most of the worldwide orchid diversity and face unique challenges of water and nutrient limitation; however, they have received comparatively little attention. Ecuador, a hotspot of orchid diversity and endemism [22], offers an ideal setting to explore these associations. Yet the composition of root‑associated fungal communities across elevation gradients and among host species in epiphytic orchids is poorly known. Our study seeks to answer three questions: (i) do root‑associated fungal communities vary significantly among elevational belts?; (ii) how does the relative abundance of root-associated fungal taxa vary among sites within the same elevational belt?; and (iii) to what extent does orchid species identity influence fungal community composition? Addressing these questions clarifies how environmental gradients and host identity jointly structure root-associated fungal communities in tropical epiphytic orchids, with implications for their resilience under changing climatic conditions.

Methods

Study Site and Sampling

This study was conducted as part of the interdisciplinary project RESPECT [23] at three sites in Podocarpus National Park, southern Ecuador (Zamora-Chinchipe and Loja), representing low, mid and high elevational belts (Fig. 1). The low-elevation site (Bombuscaro; 1,020–1,537 m a.s.l.) is characterized by evergreen foothill forest, with mean annual temperature of 20 °C and an average annual rainfall of 2,000 mm. The mid-elevation site (San Francisco Biological Reserve; 1,988–2,266 m a.s.l.) comprises montane evergreen forest with high humidity (96.3%), mean temperature of 15 °C and average annual rainfall of 2,130 mm. At each elevational belt, four epiphytic orchid species, among the most frequent orchids at the study sites, were sampled across three populations. Different orchid species were represented at each belt, except for Pleurothallis lilijae, which occurred at two elevational belts, resulting in a total of 11 species (Table 1). Within each population, approximately 20 flowering individuals per species were selected, yielding 699 sampled individuals. Orchid identification was based on morphological characters, taxonomic keys and comparison with herbarium specimens. Representative voucher specimens of each orchid species were deposited in the Herbarium UTPL, Loja, Ecuador (HUTPL). Sampling was conducted between 2016 and 2019 and proceeded species by species within each population until the target number of flowering individuals was reached. Because flowering periods differed among orchid species, sampling spanned multiple years, although most collections occurred during comparable seasonal periods. For each individual, three or more root segments were collected using non-destructive sampling, preserved in absolute ethanol and stored at 4 °C until processing as a single sample. DNA Extraction, PCR and Sequencing

Fungal colonization was assessed by optical microscopy, and colonized fragments were surface-sterilized, following Suárez et al. [25]. The velamen was then removed, and cortical tissue was transferred to sterile 1.5 ml microcentrifuge tubes with a drop of sterile water and stored at 4 °C. The tissue was subsequently macerated using a TissueLyser II (Qiagen), and total genomic DNA was extracted using a DNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Extracted DNA was stored at − 20 °C.

The internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region of nuclear ribosomal DNA (nrDNA) was amplified using a nested PCR strategy. Initially, two parallel PCR reactions were conducted using the primer pairs ITS1/TW14 and ITS1/ITS4. The resulting ~ 1200 and ~ 750 bp products, respectively, were pooled and quantified using a NanoDrop 2000c (Thermo Scientific, Wilmington, NC, USA). Two subsequent nested PCRs targeted the ITS2 region: one using ITS86F and ITS4, and a second using ITS3 and ITS4OF (see Supporting Information for detailed PCR amplification conditions, Table S3). The resulting ~ 300 bp amplicons were pooled, purified and quantified.

All PCRs were performed using Phusion High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix (Finnzymes, Espoo, Finland). Amplicons were sequenced at BioSequence Laboratories (Quito, Ecuador) on an Illumina MiSeq platform (250 bp reads) using primer pairs ITS86F/ITS4 and ITS3/ITS4OF. Raw sequences are available in GenBank (BioProject PRJNA1368801).

Delimitation of Operative Taxonomic Units (OTUs)

Sequence quality was assessed using FastQC v0.11.5 and reads with quality scores below Q25 were trimmed using Trimmomatic v0.39 [26]. OTUs were delimited following Herrera et al. [21]. During clustering, singletons, duplicate sequences and chimeras were removed. OTUs were defined at ≥ 97% sequence similarity, a threshold commonly used for ITS2-based fungal community analyses. Taxonomic assignment was performed against the NCBI nucleotide database using BLAST [27].

Sampling Completeness and RAFE OTU Richness Estimation

Sampling completeness and potential RAFE richness were estimated using EstimateS v9.1.0 [28]. Individual-based accumulation curves were generated for orchid species, populations and elevational belts. Asymptotic richness and 95% confidence intervals were estimated using Clench models in Statistica v7.0.61.0 [29]. Significant differences in OTU richness among orchid species, populations, or elevational belts were indicated by non-overlapping 95% confidence intervals of their accumulation curves [30]. To characterize community structure, rare OTUs potentially representing sequencing artefacts were excluded. Following Johnson et al. [31], only OTUs represented by > 1,000 reads were retained, resulting in a dataset of 1,239 RAFE OTUs used for all downstream analyses. Read counts were normalized using cumulative sum scaling (CSS) in the metagenomeSeq package [32]. Relative abundances were summarized at the order level and visualized using ggplot2 in R v4.3.1 [33].

Community Composition and Structure Across Elevation, Sites, and Host Species

Compositional similarity among elevational belts and sites was assessed using incidence-based Chao-Jaccard and Chao-Sørensen indices [34] calculated in EstimateS v9.1.0. Fungal community structure was further examined as in Herrera et al. [21] using non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarities in the vegan package.

Differences in OTUs composition among sites and orchid species were tested using ANOSIM (999 permutations). The independent and combined effects of site and host species were evaluated using PERMANOVA with 999 permutations using the adonis function in the Vegan package [35] in R, followed by pairwise Mann–Whitney tests with Bonferroni correction (p < 0.05) [31].

OTU abundance from the 1,239-OTU dataset was Hellinger-transformed. Homogeneity of multivariate dispersion among elevational belts was tested using betadisper in vegan. Beta diversity was partitioned into turnover and nestedness components following Baselga [36], using the Jaccard index in betapart. Global and pairwise PERMANOVA tests (999 permutations) were conducted using adonis2 in vegan. All analyses were performed in R v4.4.2.

Identification and Analysis of Orchid Mycorrhizal Fungi (OMF)

OTUs assigned to Serendipitaceae, Tulasnellaceae, Ceratobasidiaceae and Atractiellales were selected as putative orchid mycorrhizal fungi [12]. Representative sequences were aligned with GenBank references using MAFFT v7, edited in PhyDE v0.9.6, and analyzed using BioNJ in PAUP* v4.0a169. Phylogenetic trees were visualized in FigTree v1.4.4. OTUs confirmed within these groups were retained as OMF.

OMF communities were analyzed separately using the same diversity, similarity and multivariate approaches applied to the 1,239-OTU dataset, enabling direct comparison between overall fungal communities and their mycorrhizal subset. Core OMF OTUs were defined based on occurrence frequency (proportion of samples in which each OTU was detected), with the core community defined as OTUs at or above the 75th percentile of the frequency distribution [37]. Core richness and mean core frequency were calculated for each elevational belt. All analyses were performed in R v4.4.2.

Results

In total, 699 samples from 11 epiphytic orchid species were collected across three elevational belts (Table 1). Most species were abundant enough to sample up to 60 individuals per belt; the exception was Masdevallia rosea, for which only 40 specimens were available at the high‑elevation belt (Table 1). Only P. lilijae occurred in more than one belt—it was found at both mid‑ and high‑elevation. Mycorrhizal colonization was present in all collected samples. The sequencing of the root samples yielded a total of 24,588,718 good-quality filtered sequences, with a length of ≥ 240 bp. Singletons (1,163,587) and chimeras (10,916) were eliminated.

OTU Richness of RAFE Across Orchid Species, Populations, and Elevational Belts

The studied epiphytic orchids hosted a remarkably high richness of RAFE OTUs. In total, 4,697 OTUs were delimited based on 97% sequence similarity. Among these, 1,962 OTUs belonged to Ascomycota and 1,570 to Basidiomycota. Other fungal phyla occurred in smaller proportions, including 69 OTUs of Mucoromycota, 21 of Chytridiomycota, and 4 of Glomeromycota. Additionally, 1,071 OTUs, 22.8% of the total, remained unassigned to any phylum (incertae sedis).

According to the non-overlapping 95% confidence intervals of the accumulation curves the richness of RAFE OTUs was significantly different between the orchid species in the low and middle elevational belts (Table 2; Fig. 2). There were no differences between the species P. lilijae, Epidendrum hemiscleria and Pleurothallis coriacardia in the higher elevational belt (Fig. 2). Masdevallia rosea at the highest altitude and Pleurothallis cardiostola at the lowest altitude; both exhibited the lowest RAFE OTU richness (857 and 895 OTUs, respectively) (Fig. 2). The orchid species with the highest RAFE OTU richness was Stelis superbiens (2,017 OTUs), found at the mid-elevation belt, closely followed by Maxillaria alpestris (1,760 OTUs) at the low elevation belt. However, the accumulation curves (Figure S1) of RAFE OTUs in 11 orchid species were asymptotic and indicated an inventory completeness of 62% on average (Fig. 2). RAFE OTU richness varied significantly across the three elevational belts (Figure S2). The mid-elevation belt exhibited the highest OTU richness, while the high-altitude site displayed the lowest (Figure S2). Significant variation in RAFE OTU richness was also detected among populations within each elevational belt (Figure S3). At low elevation, population 1 exhibited the highest richness, with 1,789 OTUs. At mid elevation, population 2 showed the greatest richness (1,962 OTUs), and a similar pattern was observed at high elevation, where population 2 also had the highest richness (1,752 OTUs). In contrast, the lowest richness across all belts occurred in population 1 at high elevation, with 1,085 OTUs (Figure S3).

Taxonomic assignment resolved 3,111 of the 4,697 OTUs at the order level, representing 115 fungal orders. The top 10 most abundant orders identified were: Pleosporales, Polyporales, Sebacinales, Helotiales, Agaricales, Chaetothyriales, Hypocreales, Xylariales, Cantharellales, and Mycosphaerellales (Figure S4).

Variation of RAFE Communities Composition Across Orchid Species, Populations, and Elevational Belts

After filtering rare OTUs, we observed that 26.38% of the OTUs (1,239 OTUs) had more than 1,000 sequences (counts), and 73.62% (3,458 OTUs) had less than 1,000 sequences. The relative abundance of filtered OTUs within the RAFE community indicated that the predominant fungal orders were Sebacinales, Helotiales, Pleosporales, Cantharellales, Xylariales, Polyporales, Eurotiales, and Agaricales. Based on the 1,239 RAFE OTUs, Chao-Jaccard and Chao-Sørensen indices showed high similarity in the composition of OTUs between populations in the three elevational belts (Table 2). Similarity indices ranged from 0.65 and 0.79 to 0.92 and 0.96 for Chao-Jaccard and Chao-Sørensen, respectively.

Non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) of RAFE OTUs revealed distinct clustering patterns among populations within the elevation belts (Low, stress = 0.08; Middle, stress = 0.12; High, stress = 0.14). ANOSIM confirmed a significant effect of population identity on the composition of fungal OTU communities at the low elevation (R = 0.012, p = 0.002). Similar population-level differences were detected at the mid- and high-elevation belts (Middle: R = 0.05, p = 0.001; High: R = 0.03, p = 0.005). ANOSIM also confirmed a significant effect of orchid identity on the composition of fungal OTU communities (Low: R = 0.03, p = 0.001; Middle: R = 0.10, p = 0.001; High: R = 0.02, p = 0.007).

PERMANOVA analyses further indicated significant effects of both population and orchid host species at the low-elevation belt (F = 2.27, p = 0.0001; F = 3.86, p = 0.0001, respectively). Significant effects of population and orchid host were also observed at the mid-elevation (F = 3.08, p = 0.0001; F = 5.71, p = 0.0001) and high-elevation sites (F = 4.05, p = 0.0001; F = 2.92, p = 0.0001).

Beta diversity analyses showed that elevational differences were driven primarily by OTU turnover rather than nestedness (Table S1). Indeed, communities at different elevations were composed of distinct OTU assemblages rather than subsets of richer communities, consistent with species replacement rather than progressive OTU loss. Multivariate dispersion of the RAFE OTUs decreased at high elevation (betadisper, F = 3.45, p = 0.044).

Variation of OMF Communities Across Orchid Species, Populations, and Elevational Belts

A total of 271 OTUs were identified as putative OMF, belonging to the families Serendipitaceae, Tulasnellaceae, Ceratobasidiaceae, and the order Atractiellales. Their distribution was as follows: 149 OTUs in Serendipitaceae, 80 in Tulasnellaceae, 30 in Ceratobasidiaceae, and 12 in Atractiellales.

We observed varying patterns in the OTU richness among orchid species across each elevations (Fig. 4; Figure S5). Overall, S. superbiens exhibited the highest richness (168 OMF OTUs), while M. rosea had the lowest (Fig. 4). At the lower elevation, M. alpestris displayed significantly greater richness compared to the other three species investigated. Stelis superbiens showed the highest richness at the middle elevation, and both P. lilijae and E. hemiscleria exhibited significantly higher richness at the higher elevation (Fig. 4). OMF community composition differed significantly among altitudinal levels (PERMANOVA, F = 16.52, p = 0.001). Multivariate dispersion also varied among altitudes (betadisper, F = 4.15, p = 0.022), with lower dispersion at high elevation, consistent with more environmentally restrictive conditions. Beta‐diversity decomposition showed that altitudinal differences were driven primarily by OTU turnover rather than nestedness (Table S2). Pairwise comparisons confirmed significant turnover across all altitudinal contrasts, with the strongest effect between mid‐ and high‐elevation sites, while nestedness was consistently absent. Analysis of core OMF OTUs revealed a persistent core shared across the altitudinal gradient. A total of 71 core OTUs were identified, of which 62 occurred at low elevation, all 71 at mid elevation, and 58 at high elevation. Mean core frequency was similar across elevational belts, indicating comparable prevalence of core taxa despite environmental differences.

Discussion

High Richness and Taxonomic Structure of Root-Associated Fungal Communities

Our comprehensive sampling of 699 individuals representing 11 epiphytic orchid species across three elevational belts revealed an exceptionally rich assemblage of RAFE, totaling 4,697 OTUs. As in other orchid-associated fungal studies, Ascomycota and Basidiomycota dominated both richness and relative abundance, reflecting their broad ecological roles as endophytes, saprotrophs, and symbionts [12]. Within the subset of 1,239 abundant RAFE OTUs, Sebacinales, Helotiales, Pleosporales, Cantharellales, Xylariales, Polyporales, Eurotiales, and Agaricales were the predominant orders. These groups are frequently reported in epiphytic and terrestrial orchids as well as in other forest ecosystems, where they occur as root endophytes with diverse functional capacities [16, 17, 38, 39].

Among the total RAFE diversity, 271 OTUs were identified as putative orchid mycorrhizal fungi (OMF). This represents an exceptionally high record of epiphytic orchid mycorrhizal richness. For comparison, Martos et al. [40], using Sanger sequencing of 452 orchid individuals representing 77 species, detected 58 Tulasnellaceae, 23 Sebacinales, and 14 Ceratobasidiaceae OTUs. Similarly, Wong et al. [41], analyzing 193 root samples from three Vanilla species with Illumina sequencing, reported 32 Tulasnellaceae, 22 Ceratobasidiaceae, 16 Serendipitaceae, and four Atractiellales OTUs.

The high OMF richness observed here likely reflects both the extensive sampling effort and the exceptional diversity of epiphytic orchids coexisting in the study region [42, 43]. In addition, the substantial proportion of OTUs unassigned at the phylum or order level (22.8%) underscores the magnitude of undescribed fungal diversity in tropical montane forests and highlights the power of high-throughput sequencing to uncover cryptic endophytic communities [44]. Despite extensive sampling, inventory completeness remained moderate, suggesting that the overall real RAFE diversity is likely even greater. To our knowledge, this study provides the first report of root-associated fungal communities for M. alpestris, P. cardiostola, P. flexuosa, P. revoluta, E. hemiscleria, and M. rosea, and represents the most comprehensive survey of RAFE in epiphytic orchids to date in terms of sampling depth and observed taxonomic richness.

Host Species Identity and Fungal Specificity

Host identity had a strong influence on both RAFE and OMF community composition across all elevations. Stelis superbiens supported the richest RAFE community (2,017 OTUs) at mid-elevation, whereas the lowest richness values were detected in P. cardiostola at low elevation (857 OTUs) and M. rosea at high elevation (895 OTUs). Host phylogeny may partially contribute to these patterns, as closely related orchids often share similar OMF assemblages [40, 45], although they can differ substantially in their communities of non-mycorrhizal endophytes [13].

Host-specific fungal associations likely arises because mycorrhizal fungi differ in their enzymatic capacities and nutrient acquisition strategies [12, 14]. These functional differences enable certain OMF lineages to mobilize organic nitrogen and phosphorus more efficiently, influencing which fungi are selected by a given orchid species [8, 46]. Consistent with this view, co-occurring epiphytic orchids often associate with partially distinct sets of mycorrhizal fungi, suggesting host-specific fungal preferences and niche differentiation that may facilitate species coexistence [47].

Nonetheless, many orchids are mycorrhizal generalists; in our study, several OTUs of Serendipitaceae were shared across host species, suggesting functional redundancy among these widespread OMF families [8]. Non-mycorrhizal endophytes, including Chaetothyriales, Hypocreales, Helotiales, and Capnodiales, likely contribute to host-specific differentiation by enhancing growth, stress tolerance, or secondary metabolite production [13].

Elevational Gradients and Site-Level Heterogeneity Jointly Structure RAFE and OMF Diversity

RAFE richness exhibited a clear elevational pattern, peaking at mid elevation and declining sharply at high elevation. This trend was mirrored by orchid mycorrhizal fungi (OMF), indicating that elevation consistently constrains both total endophytic diversity and functionally specialized mycorrhizal partners.

High-elevation orchids consistently supported fewer OTUs than low- or mid-elevation species. In the case of M. rosea, however, this pattern may partly reflect reduced sampling effort (40 vs. 60 individuals). In contrast, S. superbiens at mid elevation exhibited the highest fungal richness, potentially reflecting environmental conditions commonly associated with Andean montane forests, including persistent humidity, frequent cloud formation, and moderate temperatures, which have been linked to elevated epiphyte diversity in previous studies [48]. Previous studies have reported weak elevational effects on orchid-associated fungi [20, 21], but our results suggest that elevation becomes a strong structuring factor when gradients encompass pronounced climatic transitions. Similarly, Haug et al. [49] found the highest arbuscular mycorrhizal richness around 2,000 m a.s.l. in the same Andean region, with a sharp decline above 3,000 m a.s.l. Within elevational belts, significant variation in OTU richness and relative abundance was observed among populations despite high compositional similarity. NMDS and ANOSIM analyses confirmed strong site effects across all elevations, indicating that fine-scale habitat heterogeneity modulates fungal community structure independently of elevation. Similar spatial heterogeneity has been reported for Neotropical epiphytic orchids [50]. Alternatively, because most orchid species were restricted to a single elevational belt, host species turnover may partially confound elevational effects, suggesting that some observed patterns could also reflect host replacement across elevations. Differences in phorophyte bark properties, including moisture retention, pH, and texture, influence both orchid establishment and fungal colonization [19, 51], generating site-specific assemblages even within the same elevational belt. Our results confirm that the elevation can be a key driver of RAFE diversity when the gradient spans a wide climatic range. Together, these findings demonstrate that fungal community assembly in epiphytic orchids operates at multiple spatial scales, shaped jointly by altitudinal belts and site-level environmental conditions.

Elevational Turnover and Persistence of a Core OMF Community

Elevational differentiation was driven primarily by compositional turnover rather than nestedness, indicating that environmental filtering operates through species replacement rather than progressive OTU loss. The pronounced turnover between mid- and high-elevation sites further supports strong elevational structuring, consistent with patterns reported for mountain mycorrhizal systems [52, 53].

Despite this turnover, similarity indices were consistently higher for OMF than for total RAFE communities within elevational belts (Table 3), pointing to a widespread pool of core mycorrhizal taxa shared across sites and hosts [54]. These dominant OMF lineages—Serendipitaceae, Tulasnellaceae, Ceratobasidiaceae, and Atractiellales—likely exhibit broad ecological amplitudes that facilitate persistence across contrasting environments [15]. However, orchid distributions may remain strongly constrained by symbiont availability, highlighting the importance of incorporating biotic interactions into elevational community interpretations.

In contrast, non-mycorrhizal endophytes appear more responsive to microenvironmental heterogeneity and may assemble through more stochastic colonization processes, thereby contributing to fine-scale differentiation of orchid root-associated fungal communities [15].

In line with the guild-specific elevational responses reported by Barbi et al. [55], the reduced multivariate dispersion observed at high elevation suggests that environmental filtering intensifies under harsher conditions, leading to increasingly constrained community assembly.

Ecological and Conservation Implications

Together, our results demonstrate that orchid–fungus interactions in tropical montane forests are structured by interacting effects of elevation, microhabitat heterogeneity, and host identity. Mid-elevation cloud forests emerge as key reservoirs of fungal diversity, whereas high elevations harbor compositionally distinct fungal communities associated with elevational variation in environmental conditions. The dominance of turnover over nestedness suggests that environmental change could alter patterns of fungal community composition and orchid–fungus associations. Such shifts may potentially influence orchid nutrition, stress tolerance, and survival.

Conservation strategies should therefore prioritize the protection of mid-elevation forests and maintain elevational connectivity to allow both orchids and their fungal partners to track shifting climatic conditions.
