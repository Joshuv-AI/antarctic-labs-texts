Identifying Risk Factors for Fall‐Related Injuries Among Inpatients at a Tertiary Hospital in China: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Wei‐Feng Bian, Xiao‐Juan Liu, Lian‐Ying Wang, Wei Zhao, Hong Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimAlthough extensive research has identified many risk factors for falls, less attention has been given to those influencing fall‐related injuries after a fall occurs. This retrospective study aimed to bridge this gap by identifying modifiable risk factors, with the goal of mitigating fall‐related injuries.DesignA retrospective cohort study.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed data from 145 inpatient falls reported to the hospital's electronic adverse event reporting system between January 2020 and December 2022 at a tertiary hospital in China. This study examined intrinsic (patient‐related) and extrinsic (environmental) factors associated with fall occurrence. Multiple logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify factors associated with fall‐related injuries specific to fall events.ResultsThe peak times for falls ranged from 6:00–8:00, 16:00–18:00, and 22:00–24:00. Patients aged over 65 years, those in bathroom settings, and those with independent mobility demonstrated higher incidences of both falls and moderate‐to‐severe fall‐related injuries. Analysis of fall causation revealed that toilet‐associated activities accounted for the highest proportion of incidents (35.43%), followed by ambulation (20.47%). Multiple logistic regression analysis revealed three reduced risk factors for fall‐related injuries: appropriate footwear use (adjusted ORs = 0.38, 95% CI 0.14–0.99), self‐care ability (adjusted ORs = 0.97 per 1‐point increase, 95% CI 0.95–0.99), and no (adjusted ORs = 0.09, 95% CI 0.01–0.74) or partial mobility (adjusted ORs = 0.23, 95% CI 0.07–0.74).ConclusionInpatient falls were clustered in the early morning, late afternoon, and night and occurred most frequently during toileting and ambulation. These findings support targeted prevention during identified peak periods and high‐risk activities, prioritizing appropriate footwear, the preservation or improvement of functional independence, and context‐sensitive supervision. Future studies could further elucidate targeted interventions, including rounding during high‐risk periods, supervised mobilization and toileting, and the provision of appropriate footwear, to determine their efficacy in mitigating fall‐related injuries.Patient or Public ContributionAlthough patients were not actively involved in study design due to its retrospective nature, their clinical records provided critical insights into fall‐related injuries.

Background

Falls in hospitals are a significant concern for patient safety, particularly among the vulnerable populations of older adults and infants (Cameron et al. 2018; Dolan et al. 2022; Shaw et al. 2020, 2021), and are the leading cause of injury‐related death in persons aged 65 years or older (Colón‐Emeric et al. 2024). These incidents not only lead to physical injuries but also contribute to increased length of hospital stay, higher healthcare costs, and potential liability issues for medical institutions (Francis‐Coad et al. 2020; Hassan 2024; Mohammed et al. 2024). The Joint Commission International and the national three‐tier hospital evaluation standards have identified the prevention and reduction of patient falls as key goals in patient safety management. One study reported on a nonrandomized controlled trial that assessed the effectiveness of a nurse‐led, patient‐centred tool kit for fall prevention (Dykes et al. 2020). The study revealed that the implementation of this tool kit was associated with a statistically significant reduction in overall numbers of inpatient falls and injurious falls. This highlights the importance of patient engagement in fall prevention, which aligns with the emphasis of the JCI on patient‐centred care.

However, despite these efforts, many hospitals continue to struggle with high fall rates, which range from 0.4–9 falls per 1000 patient days in acute care (Heikkilä et al. 2023). A study examining 60 general adult hospital units revealed substantial variation in the implementation of fall prevention practices and strategies across different units, with some practices being more consistently used than others (Morris et al. 2022). The variation in fall prevention practices is significant because it impacts the effectiveness of fall prevention efforts. For example, keeping a patient's bed in a locked position was found to be more consistently used than scheduled toileting (Turner et al. 2022). This suggests that less resource‐intensive practices may be more readily adopted than those that require more significant investment or changes to hospital protocols. The study also revealed variation in implementation strategies, with quality management strategies such as publicly posting fall rates being more consistently used than having a multidisciplinary huddle after a fall event.

It should be noted that understanding fall‐related injuries in hospitalized patients is essential, as falls remain one of the most frequent and preventable adverse events in acute care, contributing substantially to both clinical harm and economic burden. Substantial evidence underscores that inpatient falls markedly exacerbate the duration of hospitalization and the associated economic burden. A recent large‐scale study indicated that any fall during hospitalization is associated with an additional cost of $14,600 in net avoided costs per 1000 patient‐days (Dykes et al. 2023). Even when no injury occurs, falls remain a significant predictor of delayed discharge; research suggests that the occurrence of a fall significantly reduces the daily probability of discharge, with the economic and operational impact being comparable across various hospital settings (Florence et al. 2018). These findings emphasize that falls, regardless of severity, represent a critical barrier to healthcare efficiency and resource optimization. Although severe injuries constitute a smaller proportion of inpatient falls, they are strongly linked to older age and increased healthcare utilization, often necessitating surgical management or extended inpatient care (Kmetz‐Cutrone et al. 2025). These findings underscore the critical need to investigate the determinants of fall‐related injury severity to better inform targeted prevention strategies, optimize resource use, and ultimately improve patient safety in hospital settings.

Therefore, investigating injury patterns and their underlying mechanisms is not merely an extension of fall prevention but also a crucial step toward mitigating the most severe physical and economic burdens of falls. On this basis, by examining fall incidents between 2020 and 2022, this retrospective analysis of the present study aims to identify critical patterns and risk factors, thereby illuminating potential avenues for reducing both fall frequency and injury severity. Such an analysis can help identify patterns, risk factors, and potential areas for intervention. This may involve strategies such as staff education, environmental modifications, and the use of assistive devices, which have been shown to be effective at reducing falls in hospitals (Morris et al. 2022). Furthermore, the development of targeted programs based on a systematic clinical assessment of modifiable risk factors can help reduce fall rates and fall‐related injuries among high‐risk patients (Colón‐Emeric et al. 2024). By understanding the variation in fall prevention practices and implementation strategies, hospitals can better tailor their approaches to improve patient safety and reduce the incidence of falls. This study is designed to address this gap by specifically analysing the factors associated with fall‐related injuries severity to inform targeted prevention strategies.

Materials and Methods

General Data

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First People's Hospital of Chuzhou City, Anhui Province, China (permission number: 2024‐No. 30). This retrospective study included 145 fall cases reported from nursing safety (adverse) event reports at a tertiary hospital from 2020 to 2022, with 127 adults (87.59%) and 18 children (12.41%). The study population included a hospital‐wide sample of both adult and paediatric patients to provide a comprehensive overview of in‐hospital falls. Among the adults, 67 were male and 60 were female, with an average age of 70.7 ± 13.65 years. For children, the ratio was 14 males to 4 females, with the majority of children being under 3 years of age.

Information Collection

Data were retrieved from the standardized injury reporting forms within the hospital's nursing safety event reporting system, including department, name, age, gender, location, time of falls, fall injury, accompanying state during the falls, presence of a caregiver, and on‐duty nurse. Access to patient medical records for this retrospective analysis was granted by the institutional ethics committee, ensuring that all data confidentiality protocols were maintained throughout the study.

Inclusion Criteria

All inpatients who experienced falls were included in this analysis. Data from paediatric patients, while included for completeness and to avoid selection bias, are presented with the caveat that their small numbers limit separate statistical conclusions. Data were collected through the completion and submission of nursing safety (adverse) event reports, with each fall incident per patient being documented. Medical records were consulted using the medical record number to supplement missing information, including general patient demographic information, patient disease profiles and medication use, an overview of fall‐related injuries, injury severity grading, circumstances at the time of falling, and analysis of fall causes.

Evaluation Indicators

On the basis of the internationally adopted clinical fall classification system (Advances in Patient Safety 2008), Grade 0 (None) indicates that the patient sustained no injury from the fall; Grade 1 (Minor) includes injuries requiring simple intervention; Grade 2 (Moderate) encompasses those needing sutures or splints; Grade 3 (Major) denotes injuries requiring surgery, casting, or further extensive examination; and Grade 4 (Death) refers to a fatal outcome directly resulting from the injuries sustained in the fall. Thus, participants in this study were divided into two groups on the basis of the fall injury grade: those with a grade of 0 constituted the No Injury Group and those with a grade exceeding 0 constituted the With injury Group.

Statistical Analysis

The data were processed and analysed using SPSS 25.0 software. Continuous data that did not satisfy the normality assumption are presented as median with interquartile range (IQR). The Z statistic is reported from Mann–Whitney u tests for between‐group comparisons. Categorical data are presented as frequencies (%), and the chi‐square test was used to compare categorical variables (e.g., gender, fall location, medication use) between groups with and without post‐fall injuries. Independent samples t–tests were used to compare normally distributed continuous variables (e.g., age and body mass index) between the two outcome groups. To further identify risk factors for post‐fall injury, we conducted multivariable regression analysis. The dependent variable was dichotomized as ‘with injury’ (fall injury grade > 0) and ‘without injury’ (fall injury grade = 0). Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to quantify the strength of the associations. A two‐tailed p value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Extrinsic Factors of Fall Injuries

Comparative analyses of extrinsic or environmental factors see (Table 3) revealed one statistically significant association (footwear‐related factors) between the injury group and non‐injury groups (p < 0.05), whereas the other factors were not significantly different. Injuries occurred most frequently in the bathrooms (69.20% injury) and outdoor settings (67.90%), whereas corridor falls were associated with the lowest injury rate (50.00% injury). No significant association was observed between caregiver availability and injury status (χ 2 = 0.027, p = 0.869). Patients without caregivers had a slightly higher injury rate (60.70% vs 62.40% with caregivers). Similarly, caregiver presence at the time of the fall had no significant effect (χ 2 = 0.040; p = 0.841), with injury rates of 62.80% (caregiver absent) and 61.20% (caregiver present). Patients who reported wearing improper footwear had a significantly higher injury rate (68.10% injury) than did those without footwear issues (51.00% injury; χ 2 = 4.109, p = 0.043). Notably, 70.60% of patients with unilateral bed rails sustained injuries, whereas the percentage was 61.50% when the bed rails were bilateral.

Causes of Falls

Furthermore, the reasons for the occurrence of falls among the patients included in this study are listed in Table 4. Analysis of fall circumstances revealed that toileting‐related activities accounted for the highest proportion of falls (35.43%), followed by ambulation (20.47%) and transfers in/out of beds or wheelchairs (11.81%). Notably, instability during sitting (7.87%) and standing (5.51%) collectively contributed to 13.38% of the incidents. Less frequent but clinically relevant scenarios included walking in slippers (3.94%) and functional exercises for disabled patients (3.94%). Rare causes such as dizziness (1.57%) and elevator use (1.57%) were also documented, highlighting the diverse contexts in which falls occur. These findings underscore the need for targeted interventions in high‐risk activities (e.g., toileting assistance and mobility training) while addressing environmental hazards such as unstable seating.

Factors Associated With Injury Outcomes After Falls

The forest plot (Figure 3) presents adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) from a multiple logistic regression analysis examining factors associated with fall‐related injuries. Given the limited sample size, univariable tests had limited power; therefore, to reduce omitted‐variable bias and calculate adjusted ORs, we included clinically important covariates even when univariable p > 0.05, provided that they improved model specification or materially influenced adjusted estimates. On the basis of these findings, in addition to the footwear‐related variables that were significantly different in the univariate screening, we also included other important factors that may influence fall‐related injuries, such as gender, age, fall location, and mobility. Model fitness was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test (p = 0.775 > 0.05), indicating good fit.

Forest plot of factors associated with fall‐related injuries. The horizontal axis displays adjusted ORs and 95% CIs, with vertical lines indicating the null effect (OR = 1). *p < 0.05; ORs: odds ratios; CI: confidence intervals; ADL: activities of daily living scale.

The reference categories in the multiple logistic regression model were female (gender), yes (medication use, footwear‐related, and mobility), > 65 years (age), and outside (fall location), and the ADL score was analysed as a continuous variable. A regression model revealed that footwear‐related interventions (e.g., appropriate use of footwear) were significantly associated with a reduced risk of fall‐related injuries (OR = 0.38, 95% CI 0.14–0.99). Additionally, compared with patients who retained full mobility, those with partial mobility had substantially lower odds of injury (OR = 0.23, 95% CI 0.07–0.74), and patients with no mobility experienced a reduced risk (OR = 0.09, 95% CI 0.01–0.74). In addition, each 1‐point increase in the ADL score was associated with a 3% reduction in the odds of injury (OR = 0.97 per 1‐point increase, 95% CI 0.95–0.99). In contrast, several covariates showed point estimates that did not reach statistical significance: gender, medication use, fall location and age.

Discussion

On the basis of the 3‐year period of 2020–2022, this study aimed to identify the critical periods and locations of inpatient falls, along with the intrinsic and extrinsic factors contributing to fall‐related injuries. The duration of falls showed 3 peaks during the day, namely, 6:00–8:00 in the morning, 16:00–18:00 in the afternoon, and 22:00–24:00 at night, and the duration of falls showed 2 high places of falls: bedside and the bathroom, which may be related to the fact that the inpatients were not yet fully conscious when they woke up in the morning, washed themselves, went to the toilet, and got up at night during this period. Syncope, respiratory symptoms, and urinary tract infections were also identified as key risk factors for falls and fall‐related injuries among elderly and critically ill patients (Khani et al. 2026). These can be prevented by adhering to the Four and a Half principles, which are designed to maximize the avoidance or reduction of fall‐related injuries (wake up and lie down with eyes open for half a minute until fully awake; sit up in bed for half a minute; sit up at the bedside for half a minute; get out of bed and stand up for half a minute).

During morning and night shifts, nurses are busy with blood draws, medications, etc., and cannot visit the ward in time to detect the potential risk of patients falling or to meet their needs (Magota et al. 2017). Moreover, the risk of patient falls is compounded by insufficient nurse staffing during morning and night shifts and a lack of supervisory oversight. Nurses' capacity for timely rounding and patient education is often limited during night shifts because of high workloads from other therapeutic duties, compounded by variable levels of vigilance and communication effectiveness. The peak in afternoon falls can be attributed to two main factors: first, a period of increased patient activity following the completion of infusions; and second, a critical lapse in the implementation of necessary safety measures by either the patients or their attendants. It is recommended that nursing managers pay attention to the analysis of fall data, increase the allocation of human resources to the peak period of falls, increase standardized fall prevention training for accompanying staff, and emphasize accompanying assistance in performing activities of daily living for patients at high risk of falling, including toileting (Jiang et al. 2021), getting in and out of bed, and managing the symptoms of taking hypertension medication (Ngune et al. 2023). More importantly, communication and education with patients should be strengthened to increase their awareness of fall prevention (Russell et al. 2017), to teach patients to correctly assess their ability to engage in self‐care, to show weakness rather than strength, and to improve their compliance with fall prevention.

Based on the analysis of circumstances accompanying falls in adults, the highest incidence occurred in relation to toilet use, with 45 cases (35.43%) occurring before or after visiting the toilet. This was followed by falls during walking (20.47%). Falls related to toileting and walking can be attributed to several factors aligned with the temporal and spatial distribution of incidents. These include polyuria due to infusion, frequent toilet visits caused by conditions such as pelvic floor muscle relaxation in older women or osteoporotic vertebral fractures (Stanghelle et al. 2020) and prostatic hyperplasia in older men (Kim et al. 2024), as well as the lack of proper nonslip footwear. Inappropriate footwear is a significant, modifiable risk factor for fall‐related injuries, which is consistent with the findings of previous research (López‐Soto et al. 2016; Montero‐Odasso et al. 2021). It compromises stability by impairing gait, balance, and proprioception while also increasing slip risk through poor traction. This combination increases both the likelihood of a fall and the potential for severe injury, particularly fractures, which most frequently affect the head (36%), followed by the spine (29%) and the chest (23%) (Mekkodathil et al. 2020).

Additionally, a lack of risk awareness during bed transfers appears to be another cause of bedside falls among patients who are largely self‐caring (Dabkowski et al. 2025). These individuals often initiate the movement by pressing on the edge of the bed with their hands and immediately attempt to get in or out of bed without first assessing whether they have a secure hold on the bed edge, resulting in a fall before any conscious judgment can be made. For patients with partial self‐care capacity, falls may stem from caregivers' insufficient understanding of the associated risks (Ang et al. 2019). These patients are frequently unable to sit safely in a chair for prolonged periods or require additional protective measures. However, caregivers may fail to exercise adequate judgment when assisting them into a chair, which constitutes a primary cause of falls in this population. Given that toileting‐related activities are a high‐risk scenario for falls, we recommend that nursing staff enhance protection and provide targeted education to high‐risk patients and their companions before and after toilet use, integrating awareness of fall prevention into this critical period.

Notably, for full self‐care patients, we should reinforce the safety education of accompanying nurses to ensure the safety of patients. For patients with functional disorders, balance training and assessment should be carried out by nursing staff before their functional exercise, and according to the assessment results, exercise methods should be selected within their ability, and appropriate assistive devices should be selected to prevent falls (Siegrist et al. 2016). Studies have shown that patients can spontaneously recall less than 25% of the information presented (Sandberg et al. 2012), and half of the information retained may be incorrect or even the opposite of what the medical staff meant. Therefore, nurses should carry out follow‐up and evaluation of the impact after the intervention and not only remain at the level of passing on information, especially for patients and family members with poor adherence; they should also provide examples to repeat the intervention, explain the safety risks, guide in multiple ways and through multiple channels, improve communication skills and mobilize the subjective initiative of patients and family members to participate in the management of the self‐prevention of falls (Bongers et al. 2018; Walsh et al. 2019).

Falls in hospitalized patients are complex issues resulting from the interplay of various factors rather than a single cause (Morris and O'Riordan 2017; Najafpour et al. 2019). Adaptive strategies for fall prevention in hospitalized patients were developed based on the results of the above data analysis. First, staff training was strengthened. Nurses throughout the hospital will be trained and assessed in fall risk assessment, prevention, consequence management and other related knowledge in batches and shifts to provide homogeneous assessment, standardized prevention and standardized consequence management for patients at risk of falling. Second, information construction should be strengthened. The warning signs for patients at risk of falling should be improved, as should the reminder function for the use of medications at high risk of falling. Third, there is diversified health promotion. Produce an inpatient Falls Prevention Knowledge Manual and pictures with illustrations and simple language, which are easy to understand and can be used for preaching, training and reference. High‐risk fall prevention video screens should be produced on TV in outpatient clinics and wards to improve targeted education of patients and caregivers. Cases of patients with moderate and severe injuries caused by previous falls should be compiled into a booklet, with a focus on patients who cannot correctly assess their own mobility, who do not recognize their own risk of falling and who have poor compliance. The monitoring of the process of health education for patients and their families should be strengthened, and feedback on the effects should be provided to improve the compliance of patients and their families with fall prevention measures. Slippers should be worn for high‐risk patients, and the use of bed rails for all patients should be promoted (Cockayne et al. 2021; Howard et al. 2024).

More importantly, the safety management of environmental facilities should be strengthened, including the following: First, the access floor should be kept clean, dry, and free of obstacles; nonslip mats should be used in toilets/washrooms; second, eye‐catching signs, logos, wristbands, and fall assessment and disposal procedures should be made; third, adequate lighting should be maintained, and patients should be reminded to use bedside lamps; fourth, paediatric wards should use beds with closed‐bed rails and strengthen safety education for parents; and sixth, corridor/bathroom handrails should be fully covered, and alarm devices should be installed in bathrooms. Finally, the management of nursing staff should involve increased staffing in key departments and during peak risk periods, along with close supervision of elderly patients (especially those with comorbidities or cancer) to ensure the implementation of fall prevention measures during critical activities such as toileting and bed transfers and following the administration of high‐fall‐risk drugs.

Limitations

The study presents several limitations that are important to consider when interpreting the results. First, given the retrospective cohort design of this study, the sample size was determined by the hospital's nursing safety event reporting system. A post hoc power analysis revealed a power of 0.528, which falls below the conventional threshold of 0.80 (Das et al. 2016). This limited statistical power may have reduced our ability to detect statistically significant associations, particularly for variables with small to moderate effect sizes. Consequently, in the multiple logistic regression, variables that were not significant in univariate screening but could have important effects on the outcome were included in the regression equation. Second, the data were collected from a single tertiary hospital in China, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other hospitals or regions. Future research could involve multiple centers across diverse settings to validate these findings and improve their general applicability. Third, the study relied on nursing safety event reports, which may have led to an underestimation of the incidence of falls if not all adverse events related to nursing were reported. Finally, although this study attempted to identify factors associated with the risk of fall‐related injuries, there may be other unmeasured or uncontrolled confounding factors (e.g., specific cognitive impairments or physical conditions). Therefore, future prospective studies designed to control for these potential confounders are recommended to validate our findings.

Conclusion

This study identifies critical periods, locations, and modifiable factors influencing injury risk after inpatient falls. Falls predominantly occurred during early morning, late bedtime, and afternoon hours, with older adults and high‐risk areas such as bedside and bathroom settings requiring prioritized attention. Furthermore, the findings of the present study emphasize that factors associated with reduced risk could focus on three critical elements, namely, appropriate footwear use, enhanced self‐care training, and enhanced supervision, especially for patients with full or partial mobility, given their elevated risk of sustaining more severe injuries following a fall. Future studies could explore whether mobile patients experience an ‘ability‐confidence gap’, where preserved motor function outpaces diminished risk perception and postural control, leading to unassisted and riskier mobility. Notably, caregiver presence showed no significant protective effect, emphasizing the need for proactive environmental and behavioural interventions rather than passive supervision. In clinical practice, patients with full or partial mobility should not be overlooked, as should the use of appropriate footwear for patients who are able to move around, to minimize the occurrence of post‐fall injuries. Integrating these insights into clinical training and facility design could transform fall management from reactive monitoring to proactive injury prevention.
