Blood Clock Correlation Distance (BloodCCD) as a novel marker to detect circadian rhythm disruption in cancer survivors with insomnia
Authors: Brian J Altman, Po-Ju Lin, Lindsey J Mattick, Elliot H Outland, Javier Bautista, Chin-Shang Li, Umang Gada, Kristina M Morris, Amelia M Knudsen-Clark, Daniel Mwangi, Rachel E DeRollo, Amber S Kleckner, Ian R Kleckner, Nikesha J Gilmore, Benjamin T Esparaz, Amarinthia Curtis, Alison Conlin, Gianni Monaco, Jacob J Hughey, Karen M Mustian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInsomnia is a toxicity of cancer and treatment for survivors without an objective biochemical measure. Circadian rhythms are 24-h cycles that influence physiologic processes including sleep, and disrupted rhythms may contribute to insomnia. Here, we use BloodCCD to assess circadian rhythms from RNA-sequencing of blood from cancer survivors with insomnia from the YOCAS-II trial. YOCAS-II aimed to determine whether YOCAS©® yoga or cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) improved insomnia in survivors compared with a behavioral placebo. We hypothesized that circadian rhythms are disrupted in survivors, and that insomnia severity correlates with the degree of circadian disruption.MethodsBloodCCD was developed to biochemically assess circadian rhythms in blood. It was adapted from the previously published Clock Correlation Distance (CCD) and uses a correlation matrix of 42 genes known to oscillate throughout the day in blood.ResultsCancer survivors had higher (worse) BloodCCD scores, indicating disrupted circadian clock, compared to healthy individuals. Furthermore, insomnia severity correlated with worse BloodCCD, and those in the YOCAS and behavioral placebo arm showed significant correlation between BloodCCD score and insomnia.ConclusionsBloodCCD shows promise as a biomarker to biochemically detect disrupted circadian rhythms in cancer survivors, and as an indicator for insomnia severity.Clinical Trial IdentifierNCT02613364.

Background

Insomnia is a significant toxicity of cancer with no well-defined objective biomarkers. It affects up to 90% of cancer survivors and can independently increase cancer-related mortality and reduce survivors’ quality of life [1–5]. Thus, screening and diagnosis of insomnia followed with appropriate treatment to mitigate insomnia can ultimately improve survivors’ quality of life and symptom management. Insomnia is frequently measured by patient-reported questionnaires, yet patients frequently underreport their insomnia symptoms [6, 7]. An objective biochemical biomarker of insomnia that can aid in diagnosis, monitoring, and prediction of treatment efficacy would greatly help clinicians prescribe effective supportive cancer care plans for their patients. This biomarker could also reflect the underlying biological causes that contribute to insomnia severity and persistence in survivors, which can help in the optimization of current treatment strategies and the development of novel ones.

Sleep is deeply connected to the circadian clock, which is a molecular system that synchronizes all cells and organ systems in the body to the 24-h day/night cycle [8, 9]. The circadian clock is a series of interconnected networks in the human body that control day/night cycles of sleep and wakefulness, activity, hunger, metabolism, and general well-being [8]. The main synchronizing stimulus of the circadian clock is light, which daily resets the central circadian clock system in the suprachiasmatic nucleus of the hypothalamus in the brain. The central clock controls rhythms throughout the body in part by several signals, including directing behavior [8]. However, other stimuli, such as exercise, may also reset the clock, and exercise has been studied as an intervention in those with disrupted circadian rhythms [10–16]. Circadian rhythms result in most cells in the body adopting a 24-h cycle of gene expression, metabolism, and other processes, with up to 80% of protein-coding genes being rhythmic in some cell types [17, 18]. Importantly, disrupted circadian rhythms are predictive of poor outcomes in patients with localized and advanced cancer, and survivors of breast, lung, and colorectal cancer [19–27].

Insomnia and other sleep disruptions are strongly associated with disrupted circadian rhythms [28–31], and thus, a direct and continuous measure of the circadian clock of patients represents an opportunity to objectively monitor insomnia in patients. However, continuous measures of circadian disruption in patients have historically been difficult to ascertain. Biological samples such as blood are often collected one time at each assessment or patient visit and do not represent the complete circadian cycle. Studies with multiple blood sample collections often have samples collected at different stages of treatment/intervention trajectory. Recently, Shilts et al. developed the Clock Correlation Distance (CCD) method, using transcriptomic training data from pre-established time-series circadian analyses (i.e., data collected over the course of 1-2 days) to establish a reference correlation of the relationship between 12 molecular clock genes and provided a quantifiable method to measure the organization and order of the molecular clock regardless of tissue sample time point [32]. The molecular clock is present in all cells in the body and is composed of at least 12 genes whose protein products directly regulate each other’s activity and expression to oscillate throughout the day with an approximately 24-h period [8, 9, 33]. Circadian clock genes regulate each other as a system. Thus, the mRNAs that encode the key circadian proteins each have a predictable relationship that is consistent when they are considered as a group rather than individually [33]. To examine circadian disruption in tumors, CCD was tested in over 9000 patient tumor samples representing 12 types of cancer and 20 cohorts including breast, lung, and prostate cancer. The Authors found that CCD from tumor samples was statistically significantly higher compared with CCD from healthy tissue, with average DeltaCCD (difference in CCD between tumor and healthy tissue) of 1.01 (p ≤ 0.022), demonstrating that CCD is a valid biomarker to assess circadian disruption and lack of synchronicity in human tumors [32].

Based on the known links between circadian disruption and insomnia, we hypothesized that cancer survivors with insomnia also suffer from disruption of their molecular circadian rhythms, and this could be measured from RNA sequencing of blood samples. While CCD was designed to be a universal biomarker used across multiple tissue and tumor types, it was noted that the method did not accurately capture rhythmicity from whole blood. We thus modified CCD by re-training it on blood samples from healthy individuals with normal sleep, using timepoints from the full circadian period throughout the day and night. We used these data create a new reference correlation we call Blood Clock Correlation Distance (BloodCCD), which is intended as a biomarker to assess circadian disruption from human blood samples. We used BloodCCD to assess circadian disruption in a nationwide phase III clinical trial, which tested the effects of yoga, cognitive behavioral therapy, and a rigorous behavioral placebo intervention on insomnia among cancer survivors. Our BloodCCD results suggest that cancer survivors, in general, have disrupted rhythms compared to the healthy individuals with normal sleep and that the BloodCCD score (with a higher score indicating more disrupted rhythms) significantly correlated with patient-reported insomnia severity. We further discovered that BloodCCD directly correlated insomnia improvement in two of the study arms, while a third trended towards significance in the direction. Overall, these data suggest that monitoring molecular circadian rhythms from tissue samples such as blood may be clinically useful biomarkers for insomnia in cancer survivors.

Methods

Selection of patient samples from YOCAS-II

For each participant in the randomized controlled trial (RCT), blood was drawn at 5 time points: baseline (T1), mid-intervention (T2), post-intervention (T3), 3-month follow-up (T4), and 6-month follow-up (T5). The blood samples were drawn from participants between 6:45 AM and 5:45 PM, local time, with a median draw time of 9 AM. Due to available budget, we limited our analysis to T1, T3, and T4 samples only. While ISI score improvement continued from mid-intervention to post intervention in all arms, little additional improvement was seen between 3-month follow-up and 6-month follow-up [34]. We tested samples from the SHE and CBTI arms at T1 and T3, but also collected data on the YOCAS arm T4, since the YOCAS arm demonstrated continued improvement in ISI score from T3 to T4 [34].

We sequenced 1170 blood samples from 498 participants. Selection criteria is illustrated in a flowchart in Supplemental Fig. 1. We used the following criterion to select the subset: (1) Participant withdrew at baseline, (2) T1 blood samples or ISI scores were missing, (3) Participant had an ISI < 10 at baseline, which is not clinically significant insomnia [34, 35]. These survivors likely had a higher ISI score during screening, but their insomnia improved naturally before the trial began. (4) Participant has incomplete T2-T3 (for SHE and CBT-I) or T2-T4 (for YOCAS) ISI data, or had missing or insufficient blood samples for these time points.

RNA isolation

Blood from participants was originally stored in PAXgene® tubes (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). Tubes were retrieved from freezers at the URCC Research Base by the Human Biophysiology Shared Resource and delivered to the Genomics Shared Resource, both at the Wilmot Cancer Institute at the University of Rochester Medical Center (URMC). RNA was isolated using the Qiagen PAXgene Blood RNA Kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD, USA), and globin RNAs were depleted using the GLOBINclear™ Human Kit (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Before and after globin depletion, RNA quality was assessed using the BioAnalyzer platform (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA). RNA-sequencing libraries were prepared using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA).

RNA-sequencing and data processing

RNA-sequencing was performed by the URMC Genomics Shared Resource on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform as 100 base single-end sequencing. Data were formatted using the Illumina bcl2fastq program (v2.19.0), and fastq files were further processed using FASTP (v0.20.0) to trim adaptors and remove reads that were of low quality [36]. This processing yielded an average of 31 million filtered reads per sample. Processed reads were mapped to transcripts using Salmon (v0.13.1) in mapping-based mode using a decoy-aware library built from Gencode v36 GRCh38 primary assembly genome and v36 transcriptome [37]. The resulting transcripts were collapsed to genes using Tximport 1.12.3 [38] using the Gencode v26 transcripoome, and the Ensembl GRCh38.102 transcriptome for annotation. Tximport yielded read-normalized Transcripts Per Million (TPM) as output, which were used for further analysis.

Construction of BloodCCD correlation

Blood samples were downloaded from three different publicly available experiments: GEO GSE39445, GSE48113, and GSE56931 [39–41] (Fig. 1). These experiments were performed by microarray. Clock Correlation Distance (CCD) was designed to be robust across both microarray and RNA-sequencing due to the fact that comparisons are made with Spearman correlations, which are non-parametric and rank genes rather than comparing their direct expression values [32]. The original CCD reference correlation was built with mouse microarray data and validated against human microarray and RNA-sequencing data, demonstrating its utility across multiple platforms. BloodCCD also uses Spearman correlations and thus shares the same strengths with CCD with regards to cross-platform utility.

Determination of oscillating genes from blood in healthy individuals.

Sequencing data from three publicly available studies (GSE39445, GSE48113, and GSE56931) were analyzed with ZeitZeiger and the elastic net (GLMNet) to identify oscillating genes in blood. Seven example oscillating genes and how their expression varies over time in each of the 3 studies are shown in the lower panel.

As previously published, blood samples from GSE39445, GSE48113, and GSE56931 were drawn from healthy volunteers every 3-4 h around the clock [39–41]. We used metapredict to map probe sets in each dataset to Entrez Gene IDs [42]. To construct the merged dataset that would serve as the basis for the reference correlations, we used only the samples from the control conditions. We used ComBat to normalize expression values between individuals within each dataset and to normalize expression values between datasets [43]. The merged dataset consisted of 17,404 genes measured in 498 samples from 60 individuals.

Similar to previous work [42, 44, 45], we used the elastic net in multivariable regression, with time of day decomposed into sine and cosine components. Both ZeitZeiger and the elastic net perform feature selection through L1 regularization, which shrinks coefficients for uninformative genes to zero. We ran each method with various values of the respective hyperparameters (sumabsv and nSPC for ZeitZeiger; lambda for the elastic net) in ten-fold cross-validation on the merged dataset (Supplemental Fig. 2a, b). All samples from a given individual were assigned to the same fold. For the elastic net, we kept alpha (the mixture between L1 and L2 regularization) at 0.9. For each method, we calculated the mean absolute error (in hours) on cross-validation as a function of the hyperparameters (Supplemental Fig. 2a, b).

For a given number of selected genes, predictions from the elastic net tended to be more accurate than those from ZeitZeiger (Supplemental Fig. 2c). Thus, for two values of lambda corresponding to 21 and 42 genes, we trained the elastic net on the full merged dataset (Supplemental Fig. 2c). Both resulting gene sets contained the clock genes NR1D1, NR1D2, PER1, PER2, and PER3, but also many genes not in the original 12-gene multi-tissue set (Fig. 2). Based on these findings, we chose to construct a new reference correlation based on the 42 genes identified by the elastic net.

For data presented in Supplemental Fig. 3a, median TPM values of each of the 42 BloodCCD genes are presented across 29 immune cell types as originally published in [46], scaled to within a range of -4 to 4.

Dimensionality reduction using UMAP was performed using the 42 BloodCCD genes, with default parameters [47]. Plots were generated with the R package ggplot2.

Statistical analysis

For Table 1, all statistical comparisons used a chi-square test except for the following exceptions: ANOVA F test was used for age, months since first treatment, and ISI scores, and Kruskal-Wallis test was used for education. BloodCCD P values and delta BloodCCD p values (comparison between conditions) were performed as previously described [32]; briefly, BloodCCD p values were determined by comparing the 42-gene BloodCCD correlation to 42 randomly-selected genes 1000 times, and delta BloodCCD was determined by permuting the sample labels 1000 times. In the original CCD paper, a sample group size as small as n = 12 was sufficient to produce interpretable CCD scores with a significant p value [32]. P value in Supplemental Table 1 comparing simulation of severe vs. a subset of subthreshold was determined by Welch’s corrected Student’s t test (two-sided). Pairwise difference of study arms in Fig. 4 was performed using least-square means and the Tukey adjustment for multiple testing. ISI vs. BloodCCD was performed by simple linear regression with 95% confidence intervals.

Results

YOCAS-II as a platform to determine connections between the molecular circadian clock and insomnia

Blood samples used for the BloodCCD measure were collected from a nationwide phase III RCT comparing the effectiveness of YOCAS©® yoga (a mind-body practice and form of exercise) [35], survivorship health education, and cognitive behavioral therapy for treating insomnia among cancer survivors (NCT02613364) [34]. 740 eligible cancer survivors who had completed primary treatment (e.g., surgery, chemotherapy, and/or radiation therapy) within the past 2 months to 5 years and experienced insomnia defined as meeting DSM-V (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders) criteria and scored ≥10 on the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI) were enrolled [48]. Eligible participants were randomized to receive: (1) Yoga for Cancer Survivors (YOCAS©®, which combines breathing, physical alignment postures, and mindfulness), (2) cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), or (3) ASCO-recommended survivorship health education as behavioral placebo control (SHE) [35, 49–52]. Compared to the behavioral placebo, YOCAS participants and CBT-I participants demonstrated significantly greater improvements in insomnia post-intervention [34]. Since circadian dysfunction is thought to be a direct cause of insomnia [53–55], we sought an objective biomarker that connects biochemical circadian dysfunction to patient-reported insomnia. Blood samples were collected as part of YOCAS-II over the course of the study, so a subset of blood samples collected from this RCT at baseline (T1), post-intervention (T3), and 3-month follow-up (T4) was selected for RNA-sequencing (see Supplemental Fig. 1 and Methods for selection criteria of samples). Table 1 demonstrates the demographics and clinical characteristics of the subset cohort with BloodCCD measures. No significant between-group differences in demographics, cancer type, treatment type, or other clinical characteristics were observed, ensuring an unbiased comparison by RNA-sequencing of blood samples between groups.

BloodCCD as a tool to assess molecular circadian clock progression in blood samples

Clock Correlation Distance (CCD) was developed as a computational method to compare the molecular circadian clock in tumor and normal samples, by measuring the strength and progression of the molecular clock regardless of tissue sample time point [32]. CCD uses the correlation between 12 core molecular circadian clock genes, with data drawn from animal time-series experiments and validated in human samples, to create a “reference” correlation that test samples are compared to. Samples from experimental conditions are compared to the reference correlation using Euclidean distance, to calculate a CCD score. The farther away from the test sample, the higher the CCD score, which indicates disorder and loss of circadian function. A weakness of the original CCD reference is that the expression of several of the 12 circadian genes in CCD are only weakly rhythmic in blood, and CCD thus gave worse baseline scores to healthy blood samples [32]. Thus, we sought to identify a set of genes whose co-expression in human blood could better indicate the progression of daily rhythms.

We first assembled three publicly available datasets of genome-wide gene expression in human blood [39–41]. Each dataset consisted of samples taken at different times of day from multiple individuals. To identify candidate gene sets for the blood CCD, we used the supervised learning methods ZeitZeiger and the elastic net (GLMNet) to identify gene sets predictive of the time of day at which a sample was collected (Fig. 1, seven example genes are shown), resulting in a new reference correlation of 42 genes that oscillate in blood [42, 44, 45] (Fig. 2a). This reference correlation can be used to compare test samples to, and will be referred to as Blood Clock Correlation Distance, or BloodCCD See Methods and Supplemental Fig. 2 for additional details.

We performed several quality controls to determine if this set of 42 oscillating blood genes fully represented the diversity of the entire circadian cycle and the diversity of different blood cell types. When the 42 genes were plotted by peak phase, we observed that they peaked across the day (Fig. 2b). This suggested that the 42 genes selected for the new reference correlation represented the entire range of the circadian cycle. We next asked if these 42 genes represented the range of leukocytes found in blood or were overrepresented in certain cell types. Using RNA-sequencing values calculated from 29 sorted leukocyte cell types, we found that the 42 genes represented the entire diversity of circulating leukocytes and were not over-represented in a single cell type (Supplemental Fig. 3a).

BloodCCD suggests disruption of circadian rhythms in cancer survivors

We performed RNA-sequencing on blood samples from the YOCAS-II trial. Before using the new BloodCCD correlation, we sought to ensure that batch effects in the data would not skew results. Therefore, we used uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) to identify possible batch effects in the RNA-sequencing data, focusing our UMAP analysis on the 42 genes in the reference correlation [47]. We tested the following metrics: assessment, NCORP site, blood draw time, primary cancer type, biological sex, race, insomnia severity, and study arm. As shown in Supplemental Fig. 3b, the 42 BloodCCD genes did not cluster according to any of these metrics, suggesting that these metrics would not introduce batch effects into subsequent analyses.

Our first analysis was to determine the degree to which cancer survivors exhibited disrupted or disordered circadian rhythms, as measured by BloodCCD, compared to healthy good sleepers. To test this, we directly compared BloodCCD between a cohort of healthy good sleepers (from GSE56931) and the entire cohort of samples taken from YOCAS-II. Since GSE56931 was used to build the original BloodCCD reference correlation, we constructed a new “leave-one-out” Blood CCD reference correlation from the other two healthy good sleeper studies, GSE39445 and GSE48113. The BloodCCD results from this analysis revealed that the group of participants with insomnia recruited from the phase III RCT, regardless of intervention assignment, had a significantly higher BloodCCD score (8.27) compared to healthy individuals with normal sleep ((5.51, p = 0.001)) (Fig. 3). This is a far greater difference than that observed between human tumors and normal tissues (2.76 for BloodCCD between survivors and healthy good sleepers, 1.01 average between tumor and normal tissue [32]), indicating the score is a meaningful difference. This suggests that cancer survivors have a disordered circadian clocks in their circulating leukocytes compared to healthy people. The original BloodCCD reference correlation built from all three studies is used in all subsequent analysis in this paper.

BloodCCD tracks insomnia severity at baseline in cancer survivors

Having determined that BloodCCD shows disruption of circadian rhythms in cancer survivors compared to healthy individuals with normal sleep and that batch effects would not hinder analysis of YOCAS-II blood samples, we sought to determine if BloodCCD accurately tracks insomnia severity in cancer survivors, and improvement in the treatment arms of YOCAS-II. Insomnia severity was measured in YOCAS-II using the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI), the current gold-standard method to assess insomnia in cancer patients and survivors, with an ISI score ≥ 10 being considered clinically relevant insomnia in YOCAS-II [48]. Our first analysis was to correlate insomnia severity, as measured by ISI, with BloodCCD score. For this and subsequent analyses, only T1 and T3 data were used, to balance the three treatment arms. Samples regardless of treatment arm and timepoint were divided into the following previously defined groups: ISI = 10–14 is subthreshold, ISI 15–21 is moderate, and ISI greater than 22 is severe [48, 56–58].

We first asked the degree to which BloodCCD score tracked with insomnia severity at baseline, i.e., before participants were randomized to therapeutic or control arms. BloodCCD analysis revealed that those with severe insomnia at baseline had the highest BloodCCD score (9.00), those with moderate insomnia had a lower BloodCCD score (8.24), and those with subthreshold insomnia had the lowest BloodCCD score (7.94) (Table 2). Average ISI scores for each of the groups were near the median of the ranges identified above. BloodCCD calculations can be independently influenced by the sample size. The three groups were uneven, with 49 participants exhibiting severe insomnia and 196 exhibiting subthreshold insomnia at baseline. To ensure that different sample sizes did not influence our findings, we ran a simulation where we compared the 49 patients with severe insomnia to a randomized sampling of 49 patients with subthreshold insomnia 1000 times. In this simulation, the severe group had a significantly higher (worse) average BloodCCD score than subthreshold (Supplemental Table 1), indicating that differing sample sizes did not influence the findings. Together, these data suggested a potential correlation between BloodCCD score (and by extension, circadian dysfunction) and insomnia severity in cancer survivors.

BloodCCD correlates with insomnia severity and improvement in cancer survivors

We next expanded our analysis to include all three arms of the study at baseline and post-intervention. To group participants for BloodCCD analysis, we divided participants by study arm, timepoint (baseline or post-intervention), and insomnia severity. This resulted in 18 groups of samples (a table with complete results of this analysis is available in Supplemental Table 2). We first compared the difference in BloodCCD scores, without regards to accompanying ISI score, using a Least-Square Means regression and multiple testing correction of each study arm. YOCAS participants showed different Blood CCD regression compared to CBT-I (p = 0.14, Fig. 4a), potentially indicating a larger change over time for YOCAS compared with CBT-I participants. Other between-arm comparisons did not show as much of a difference. This suggested that the BloodCCD score of those in the YOCAS was potentially different from that of those in the CBT-I arm.

We next directly compared BloodCCD scores and ISI scores from each group to interrogate if BloodCCD, an objective molecular biomarker, and ISI, a patient-reported score, correlated with each other across the two timepoints (baseline T1 and post-intervention T3). When compared by a simple linear regression model, we discovered that BloodCCD score and ISI score had a significant positive correlation across all 18 groups (p = 0.0015, Fig. 4b). These findings suggested that BloodCCD score has a direct association with patient-reported insomnia severity.

YOCAS-II had three arms for survivors with insomnia, and so we next compared the degree to which BloodCCD score and insomnia severity correlated within each arm of the trial. The correlation analysis revealed a significant positive correlation between BloodCCD and ISI among YOCAS and behavioral placebo (SHE) participants (Fig. 4c). This intriguingly suggests that improvements in insomnia seen in both YOCAS©® and behavioral placebo (SHE) were associated with better circadian rhythms assessed via BloodCCD. However, while BloodCCD trended towards being significantly associated with insomnia scores in the CBT-I arm, it did not reach significance (Fig. 4c). Together, our findings show that BloodCCD, as a measure of circadian strength and order, is a promising objective biomarker corresponding with a patient-reported insomnia outcome.

Discussion

In this study, we developed BloodCCD as a tool to assess the correlation with insomnia in cancer survivors experiencing insomnia. BloodCCD was modified from an existing and validated biomarker for molecular circadian rhythms, CCD [32], and developed from 42 oscillating genes detected in healthy good sleepers. These 42 genes represented a diversity of immune cell types and times of the day. BloodCCD was then used to assess circadian rhythms in cancer survivors from a nationwide phase III RCT, where survivors with clinically relevant insomnia were randomized to receive YOCAS©® yoga, CBT-I, or a behavioral placebo, survivorship health education (SHE). We used BloodCCD to assess the correlation between circadian rhythms and insomnia. BloodCCD analysis demonstrated that cancer survivors had significantly disrupted rhythms as compared to healthy individuals with normal sleep. More importantly, the BloodCCD score (where a higher score indicates greater circadian disruption) was significantly and positively correlated with patient-reported severity of insomnia (insomnia severity index score) in survivors. This was true without regard to the study arm. While there was a significant positive correlation between BloodCCD score (circadian disruption) and insomnia severity in the YOCAS©® and behavioral placebo (SHE) arms, the CBT-I arm trended towards a positive correlation but did not reach significance. These results for the first time show a direct link between the strength of the molecular circadian clock, as measured by blood RNA-sequencing, and patient-reported severity of insomnia in cancer survivors, and may point towards therapies to directly address circadian dysfunction.

Circadian rhythms are known to connect to sleep quality, and this relationship is likely bi-directional: disrupted circadian rhythms in a person can negatively impact sleep quality, and poor sleep quality can, in turn, disrupt circadian rhythms [28–31, 59]. This connection is likely multimodal, but there is not a single agreed-upon method to measure circadian rhythms in humans. Actigraphy (monitoring of activity via a smartwatch or hip monitor) is commonly used to measure sleep quality, and we and others have shown that actigraphy is a consistent and reliable objective measure for sleep disruptions [35, 60, 61]. Actigraphy may also be effective in measuring the disruption of circadian rhythms in cancer patients and survivors, and this disruption correlates with insomnia and other toxicities [19, 62, 63]. Actigraphy is a measure of activity rhythms, which can be an output of circadian rhythms but do not fully represent the molecular underpinnings of the molecular circadian clock. Our method, BloodCCD instead leveraged blood samples that are commonly collected as part of research studies and routine doctor visits to assess circadian rhythm strength as a function of the relationship between known oscillating genes in blood. Using training data from healthy human blood collected over the 24-h circadian cycle, the technique allows researchers to compare the organization and order of test samples to an idealized healthy reference control.

There has been intense interest in developing RNA-sequencing-based techniques to measure circadian rhythms from human biological samples, and these techniques vary widely in methodology and what results they report. We chose to adapt CCD to BloodCCD because CCD was previously shown to be feasible for use in blood samples and measures organization and order of the molecular clock (which serve as proxies for circadian clock strength). Additionally, it was practical to adopt a new reference correlation for blood based on previous published works [32, 39–41]. Other methods that infer the organization and amplitude of the molecular clock in tumors have been developed and show changes in circadian rhythmicity in tumors compared to normal tissue, including CYCLOPS, nCV, LTM, and TimeTeller [32, 64–67], but we determined that these techniques were less practical to adopt for use in human blood than CCD due to requirements for wider coverage around the day and night. Other techniques have been developed to infer the circadian phase (i.e., time of day) from blood samples [68–72], and show great promise in diagnosing circadian misalignment or sleep phase disorders; however we did not hypothesize that cancer survivors would suffer from chronic misalignment. It will be important in the future to determine the extent to which other RNA-sequencing-based techniques to measure circadian rhythms can inform on rhythms from blood samples of cancer patients and survivors.

Insomnia has long been recognized as a major toxicity of cancer and its treatments and can drastically negatively affect patients’ and survivors’ quality of life and symptom outcomes; however, the molecular mechanisms underlying this insomnia remain unclear. There has long been a suspected link between systemic inflammation and insomnia in cancer patients and survivors [73, 74]. This has been borne out by molecular studies that linked inflammatory genes to insomnia in those with insomnia, and studies from our own group have shown alterations in inflammatory biomarkers in cancer patients and survivors with insomnia [75–77]. It was previously shown in animal models that chronic circadian disruption can lead to systemic inflammation [78, 79]. Because BloodCCD is measured from the RNA of circulating immune cells, we speculate that the disruption of circadian rhythms we observe by BloodCCD in cancer survivors may be linked to, or even perhaps upstream, of inflammation that stems from the activity of these immune cells. Future work should better determine potential mechanistic links between circadian disruption and inflammation in cancer survivors.

Insomnia is strongly correlated with and frequently co-occurs alongside other common symptomatic toxicities associated with cancer and its treatment, such as cancer-related cognitive impairment (CRCI) and cancer-related fatigue (CRF) [80–83]. Sleep disturbances not only contribute to the onset and persistence of cognitive issues following a cancer diagnosis but are also a significant risk factor for CRF [84–86]. This connection is thought to arise, at least in part, from this shared hypothesized etiology involving systemic inflammation [87–92], though the exact pathways driving these symptomatic toxicities remain poorly understood. Separate mediation analyses within studies of YOCAS®© revealed that yoga effectively reduced both memory difficulty and CRF, with improvements in sleep accounting for 26% of the observed reductions in memory difficulty and 22–37% of the observed reductions in CRF [93, 94]. These findings have been supported by several subsequent studies [95–97]. Consequently, intervening in the underlying causes of sleep disturbances in patients with cancer and survivors enhances the quality of life directly but may also lead to indirect improvements via reductions in other related symptomatic toxicities (i.e., CRF and CRCI).

We observed that BloodCCD score improved with patient-reported ISI scores in the YOCAS and the behavioral placebo (SHE), while in the CBT-I arm this trend was present but did not reach statistical significance. YOCAS is a yoga intervention designed specifically for cancer survivors that combines breathing exercises, physical alignment postures, and mindfulness components [34, 98, 99]. Exercise in particular is known to be safe and effective in cancer patients and survivors to address a variety of toxicities including insomnia [100, 101]. It has been previously proposed that exercise in humans can re-entrain (re-synchronize) the circadian sleep-wake cycle [14–16], and exercise may thus have direct effects on molecular circadian rhythms as well. Survivors receiving ASCO Survivorship Health Education (SHE) [51] also had improved insomnia, and the improved perceived severity of insomnia was correlated with improved (reduced) BloodCCD score. ASCO Survivorship Health Education is a multimodal course that teaches many healthy-living strategies, and so while it is difficult to speculate which of these might contribute to improvement in circadian function, it does suggest that further study using ASCO Survivorship Health Education as a treatment for insomnia is warranted. In contrast, the CBT-I arm trended towards an association between BloodCCD and insomnia severity but did not reach statistical significance, despite CBT-I leading to the biggest improvement in perceived insomnia among cancer survivors [34]. This was a surprising finding considering that CBT-I is recommended as a gold standard treatment for patients with insomnia stemming from circadian misalignment, and re-entrainment of circadian rhythms is currently proposed as a mechanism of CBT-I efficacy [30, 102]. A potential technical explanation for our finding is that there are a few extreme outliers in gene expression among the CBT-I arm that prevent a full analysis of BloodCCD score compared to the patient-reported ISI score. A potential scientific explanation is that CBT-I decouples sleep and circadian rhythms in a way that the other two arms did not. Further study of the effects of CBT-I and its successor techniques on circadian rhythms, properly powered to ask these questions, should serve to clarify our findings. In closing, our findings for the first time connect insomnia in cancer survivors to molecular disruption of circadian rhythms, and suggest that specific interventions may improve this molecular disruption through mechanisms that remain to be uncovered.

Limitations of the study

CCD and BloodCCD, by virtue of their design, are group-level analyses, not single-patient analyses. They have proven useful in determining the degree to which circadian rhythms are disrupted in tumors and in the blood of cancer survivors, but they are unable to provide single-patient resolution of circadian rhythm state, which limit their diagnostic potential in the clinic. Future computational methods should focus on assessing the order of the circadian system or amplitude from single patient samples. In the future, it will also be important to assay additional blood sequencing samples from healthy volunteers to understand how BloodCCD scores may vary across different healthy cohorts. Another limitation is we were unable to compare BloodCCD scores of cancer survivors with and without insomnia, since an inclusion criterion of the YOCAS-II trial was clinically significant insomnia. Future studies should disentangle the extent to which circadian disruption is a generalizable feature of cancer survivorship compared to the direct correlation of circadian disruption and insomnia.
