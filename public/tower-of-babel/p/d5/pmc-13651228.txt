Delayed traumatic aortic arch pseudoaneurysm with aorto-innominate fistula: a hybrid repair
Authors: Niraj Jung Thapa, Kristi Thapa, Pravakar Dawadi, Brikshya Gurung, Dipesh Poudel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPseudoaneurysms of the aortic arch with aorto-innominate fistulas are very rare, especially when they present long after the initial trauma. We report a case of a silent traumatic fragmentation injury that manifested 4 years later as a high-flow fistula.Case ReportThe 33-year-old active-duty soldier presented with complaints of dyspnea during exertion, as well as finding of a palpable thrill located in the left infra-clavicular area. He sustained a shrapnel wound to the chest approximately four years ago. A large pseudoaneurysm was found located at the aortic arch and originated from just distal to the left subclavian artery (LSA). A pseudoaneurysm was also identified that created a fistulous communication between the aortic arch pseudoaneurysm and the left brachiocephalic vein. Due to the complex nature of this case a hybrid surgical technique was used. The patient underwent a left carotid-subclavian bypass and a left subclavian artery (LSA) ligation. The patient then underwent TEVAR. During the TEVAR procedure, a stent-graft was placed in Zone 2. The postoperative images confirmed that there was no further blood flow into the excluded pseudoaneurysm and ceased the fistulous flow.ConclusionLow velocity fragmentation injuries can result in containable vascular damage that may remain asymptomatic for many years. One of the most important clues to diagnose these types of injuries is the presence of a palpable “thrill.” Hybrid supra-aortic debranching using TEVAR provides a safe and durable solution to complex arch pathology, thereby eliminating the morbidity associated with open repair.Keywords: Aorto-innominate fistula, Combat trauma, Hybrid TEVAR, Pseudoaneurysm, Traumatic aortic injury, Zone 2 debranching

Introduction

Penetrating thoracic aortic injuries (PTAI), represent one of the most lethal forms of trauma in both military and civilian contexts. A review of recent large national studies indicates that PTAI has a much higher mortality rate prior to hospital admission than does blunt trauma, with approximately 30% of victims arriving at emergency departments without signs of life [1]. While most deaths occur immediately due to severe hemorrhagic shock, a small number of survivors survive the immediate injury. This small group of survivors experiences a temporary containment of blood flow around the aorta as a result of the adventitia or surrounding mediastinal tissue acting as a tamponade effect, leading to the eventual formation of a chronic traumatic pseudoaneurysm (PSA) [1, 2].

The mechanism of injury of PTAs in combat is quite different from that observed in civilian trauma. The majority of vascular injuries sustained in civilian trauma are caused by low-velocity handgun wounds or knife injuries. In contrast, a large body of literature exists on the frequency and types of vascular injuries associated with explosive forces during conflict in Iraq and Afghanistan, which indicates that fragmentation injuries account for nearly 75% of all vascular trauma [3]. Fragmentation injuries can produce contained injuries that may not immediately become apparent.

Another less common complication associated with PTAs is the late development of an aorto-venous fistula (AVF). An AVF is an extremely rare clinical condition characterised by a high-flow connection between the aortic arch and the innominate (brachiocephalic) vein. To date, there have been no reported series of such lesions; rather, they have typically appeared in isolated case reports [4]. Lesions such as AVFs are deceptive, as many patients will be asymptomatic for a prolonged latent period, during which time the fistula will gradually enlarge, and it may be several months or even years before symptoms develop secondary to the increasing hemodynamic burden resulting in heart failure [4].

The occurrence of chronic post traumatic pseudoaneurysm of thoracic aorta due to its injury is rare. A comprehensive literature review found only 37 cases presented within 22 different peer-reviewed publications across the span of approximately 40 years. In addition, the time frame between the original traumatic event and the first clinical diagnosis varied widely among these cases [5], which ranged from one month to five decades. The evidence describing post penetrating or fragmentation trauma that developed at long intervals after the original injury, especially after being complicated by an additional aorto-innominate arteriovenous fistula are limited to isolated case reports only. ([4, 6–7]) The present case represents the longest reported interval between a fragmentation mechanism along with the combined presentation of aortic arch pseudoaneurysm with aorto-innominate arteriovenous fistula.

This is a case report describing the presentation and treatment of a 33-year-old soldier who developed a large aortic arch pseudoaneurysm with an aorto-innominate fistula four years following a fragment wound to his chest. We wish to emphasise the difficulty in diagnosing this progressive disease process, especially its radiographic appearance, which may resemble a mediastinal tumour. Additionally, we will present our rationale for employing a hybrid repair approach to treat this lesion and the necessity of left subclavian artery revascularisation based upon the SVS’s evidence-based recommendations to prevent stroke [8].

Case report

A 33-year-old soldier presented to a tertiary care facility with symptoms of intermittent irregular palpitations and shortness of breath (dyspnea) during exercise. Additionally, he described a palpable vibratory sensation in his left upper chest area. Although the initial symptoms were mild, he said they worsened over time to the point that they significantly affected his daily functioning. He had a history of a traumatic injury that resulted in penetrating trauma to his chest; specifically, a suprasternal penetrating trauma caused by multiple metal fragments, which occurred approximately 4 years ago.

At the time of the initial injury, the patient was evaluated at a peripheral hospital and was diagnosed with a hemopneumothorax on the left side and underwent tube thoracostomy. However, there was no vascular intervention at the time of the primary injury. The patient’s vital signs upon admission were as follows: blood pressure = 130/60 mmHg and heart rate = 72 beats per minute.

Additionally, the physical examination revealed a clear and palpable thrill in the left infraclavicular region. Upon auscultation, a loud systolic murmur was heard in the left upper chest, which extended to the neck. The patient’s peripheral pulses were symmetrical and palpable. Lastly, there was no evidence of congestive heart failure, such as elevated jugular venous pressure or pedal oedema.

Laboratory results, including cardiac biomarkers, were all within normal limits. The patient’s ECG revealed a normal sinus rhythm. Chest X-ray results revealed a well-circumscribed opacity in the left upper quadrant. The opacity appeared to be a mediastinal mass, due to its relationship with the mediastinum, forming an obtuse angle with the lung tissue (Fig. 1).

Chest radiograph showing well-defined, soft tissue density radio-opacity in the left upper zone, which was broad-based to the mediastinum and made an obtuse angle with the lung parenchyma, suggestive of a mediastinal mass

Contrast-enhanced CECT of the chest and CT Angiography provided confirmation of a large pseudoaneurysm (71 × 42 × 42 mm) arising from the aortic arch, located approximately 6 mm distally to the origin of the left subclavian artery. The pseudoaneurysm had a small neck of 6 mm in diameter and 5 mm in length, extending posterosuperiorly (Figs. 2 and 3). Importantly, the volume-rendered three-dimensional reconstruction revealed a high-flow fistula between the pseudoaneurysm and the left brachiocephalic (innominate) vein with an aorto-innominate arteriovenous fistula (AVF) (Fig. 4). It is essential to note that the fistula comprises the innominate vein, not the innominate artery.

Therefore, because of the anatomical location of the pseudoaneurysm (involving the ostium of the left subclavian artery) and the fistulous connection, an appropriate method for addressing this complex aortic pathology, a hybrid approach was utilized, aiming to minimize morbidity. Prior to performing the TEVAR portion of the repair, a supra-aortic single-vessel debranching was performed. The left carotid-subclavian bypass was performed using a retrojugular e-PTFE ringed graft via a neck incision. Simultaneously, the ostium of the left subclavian artery was occluded using hemoclips to eliminate the potential for a type II endoleak. Subsequently, the TEVAR portion of the repair was performed, utilizing a stent-graft to exclude the pseudoaneurysm and seal the aorto-innominate fistula in Zone 2 of the aortic arch. A self expanding thoracic endoprosthesis was placed at the site using fluoroscopy. A 10–15% oversized stent was used based on the size of the normal aortic diameter determined by the preoperative CT Angiogram. The proximal landing zone was placed just distal to the left common carotid artery (Zone II), while the distal landing zone was located in the proximal descending thoracic aorta. The placement effectively excluded all blood flow into the pseudoaneurysmal sac and eliminated all inflow into the aorto innominate fistula.

Post-repair angiography and 3D reconstruction confirmed the patency of the bypass grafts and demonstrated complete exclusion of the pseudoaneurysm without residual flow into the innominate vein (Fig. 5). The remainder of the patient’s postoperative course was uneventful. He was subsequently discharged home in a stable condition. After a period of sick leave, the patient was symptom-free and able to return to duty and report back to his unit. The patient was monitored through follow-up visits for six months after surgery. Contrast-Enhanced Computed Tomographic Angiography (CTA) was not performed in the outpatient setting after discharge. Six months after his last clinical visit, he had returned to a state of full health without symptoms, had palpable bilateral upper extremity arterial pulses and showed no signs of venous insufficiency, Superior Vena Cava Syndrome (SVCS), or congestive heart failure.

Discussion

The diagnostic work-up in this patient illustrates the manner in which chronic vascular injuries could resemble neoplastic diseases. On his initial chest x-ray, there was an area of soft tissue density forming an obtuse angle with the adjacent lung parenchyma. As discussed in the radiologic literature, an obtuse angle formed by a lesion and lung parenchyma suggests a pleural or mediastinal site of origin, while intraparenchymal lung lesions form acute angles because they arise from within the lung parenchyma [9].

While the imaging characteristics were suggestive of a mediastinal mass, such as lymphoma or thymoma, the physical exam was decisive in excluding a tumour. While a mediastinal tumour could cause venous compression, a mediastinal tumour cannot create a palpable thrill or a loud systolic bruit. These latter two physical exam findings represent high velocity turbulent blood flow through a fistulous tract and thus indicate the necessity of vascular imaging instead of biopsy [4].

The patient progressed from being asymptomatic to having exertional dyspnea, which can be attributed to a left-to-right shunt. The fistula created a low-resistance pathway for oxygenated blood to bypass the systemic circulation and flow back into the venous system, thereby increasing the right-sided cardiac preload. Similar presentations have been documented in other patients with traumatic great-vessel fistulas that resulted in high-output cardiac failure and pulmonary hypertension if left untreated [4]. The four-year time frame between the trauma and presentation is believed to reflect the time needed for the fistula to grow from a microscopic defect into a sufficiently sized channel to create measurable hemodynamic effects based on the continuous pulsatile pressure of the aorta.

Repairing aortic arch disease that involves the great vessels is technically challenging. An entirely open repair of an aortic arch would necessitate a median sternotomy, Cardiopulmonary Bypass (CPB) and Deep Hypothermic Circulatory Arrest (DHCA). Open repairs are associated with higher rates of complications and longer hospital stays [10]. Because of the above considerations, we elected to perform a hybrid thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) procedure.

In this case, the pseudoaneurysm arose from the origin of the left subclavian artery (LSA), requiring a proximal landing zone in the second zone (Ishimaru Zone 2) [11]. In order to achieve a secure and reliable seal, the stent graft had to extend across the origin of the LSA. Therefore, a left carotid-subclavian bypass was performed prior to deploying the stent. The decision to perform pre-emptive revascularisation of the LSA is consistent with the Society for Vascular Surgery (SVS) Clinical Practice Guidelines that suggest routine preoperative LSA revascularisation should be performed in all elective Zone 2 TEVARs to minimise the risk of postoperative stroke and spinal cord ischemia [8].

The revascularisation of the LSA represented both an anatomical and neuroprotective concern. The LSA provides the primary arterial supply to the left vertebral artery (LVA), which is a major contributor to the posterior cerebral circulation. Coverage of the LSA without prior revascularisation greatly increases the likelihood of a stroke of the posterior circulation. In a large study that analysed > 800 TEVAR procedures from the NSQIP database, Chung et al. demonstrated that coverage of the LSA without revascularisation significantly increased the risk of stroke (odds ratio 2.17) during the perioperative period compared to those with either preserved or bypassed LSA [12].

Additionally, we placed a clip at the proximal portion of the LSA. The placement of clips at the proximal LSA is essential in hybrid repairs to prevent a type II endoleak. A type II endoleak occurs when retrograde flow from the vertebral artery replenishes the aneurysm sac, thereby preventing thrombosis and maintaining the risk of rupture [10].

The mechanism of injury for this patient, a metallic fragment, explains his survival and the delay until he presented for care. High-velocity bullets (> 2000 ft/sec) transfer immense amounts of kinetic energy, which results in a temporary cavitation effect destroying vessels several cm distant to the trajectory of the bullet [13]. In contrast, irregular metallic fragments generally travel at slower velocities over greater distances and may damage a vessel wall without immediately producing a catastrophic rupture. This ballistic profile is typical in contemporary warfare [3], allowing the development of a pseudoaneurysm that is clinically silent for years, illustrating the need for long-term follow-up in military veterans who have suffered wounds containing metallic fragments near major arteries.

The importance of follow-up care after TEVAR cannot be overstated since it may identify potential complications early on in the process of recovery (such as an endoleak, migration of a stent-graft, and recurrence of a fistula). As per the Society for Vascular Surgery (SVS), postoperative follow-up for standard follow-up care includes one-month, twelve-months and yearly postoperative imaging by means of Contrast-Enhanced Computed Tomographic Angiography (C.T.A.) [14]. Due to clinical and logistical reasons that were patient related, no outpatient imaging studies (via C.T.A.) were conducted following discharge from the hospital. However, the patient was followed closely in an outpatient setting for six months. While clearly there are significant limitations associated with the lack of objective postoperative imaging studies, the patient’s completely resolved symptoms (exertional dyspnea); bilateral upper extremity pulses; and the absence of venous congestion, Superior Vena Cava Syndrome (SVCS), or Congestive Heart Failure (CHF), support functionally intact blood flow through both the Carotid-Subclavian Bypass Graft; and successful obliteration of the High Flow Aorto-Innominate Fistula. It is still strongly suggested that all patients who have had young patients with metallic foreign bodies remaining near large vessels should continue to receive lifetime clinical follow-up; and when possible imaging follow-up as metallic fragments may migrate or complications to previously placed vascular grafts may develop many years postoperatively.

Conclusions

This case illustrates that traumatic pseudoaneurysm (TPA) can mimic a tumour of the mediastinum on chest X-rays and occur many years after the initial trauma. A significant clinical feature of TPA is the physical sensation of a pulsating thrill, which aids in distinguishing this vascular lesion from an actual neoplasm. Hybrid TEVAR with supra-aortic debranching represents an effective method to treat complex aortic arch disease, particularly when it affects the great vessels. Further research will need to include long-term follow-up and surveillance of asymptomatic vascular injury due to fragment retention in combat veterans.
