Identification of an age-related monocyte-derived macrophages characteristic genes-based signature for high-grade diffuse glioma
Authors: Wenjuan Chen, Yuanyuan Xie, Huifang Pan, Xuewen Li, Tianyu Qin, Boxin Zhang, Shuanghui Guo, Zhanxiang Wang, Hongda Li, Fanghui Lu, Shaoxuan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Monocyte-derived macrophages (MDMs) have been associated with increased infiltration proportion and shorter overall survival (OS) in diffuse gliomas (DGs), particularly contributing to poor prognosis in elderly patients. However, the criteria for predicting the suitability and efficacy of MDM-targeted therapy in cancer treatment remain unclear. This study utilized TCGA and CGGA high-grade diffuse glioma cohorts (IDH-wildtype glioblastoma or IDH-mutant astrocytoma, CNS WHO grade 4) with complete mRNA expression profiles and clinical information. The MDM intersect gene set was obtained through differential gene analysis and single cell RNA-sequencing analysis, followed by the application of least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) algorithm to identify core genes for risk score (RS) modeling. Using the TCGA dataset as a training set and CGGA dataset as a validation set, we verified that RS, including G0S2, PLAUR, and SPAG4 gene expressions, is a reliable indicator of poor prognosis in high-grade DGs patients. Our findings demonstrate that RS can serve as an infiltration index for MDMs enabling accurate prediction of high-grade DGs patient prognosis. Furthermore, we found that in glioblastoma (GBM), a more malignant subtype of diffuse glioma, monitoring this index and targeting MDMs is crucial for improving overall survival, especially in older female patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-40485-8.

Introduction

Diffuse gliomas (DGs) are the most common primary brain tumors in adults, accounting for more than 80% of all gliomas. Among them, the high-grade subtype—particularly glioblastoma (GBM)—is the most malignant, with a median overall survival of approximately 15 months and a median age at diagnosis of 64 years1–4. Notably, younger patients are correlated with better overall prognoses5,6. According to the 2021 World Health Organization Classification of Tumors of the Central Nervous System, adult-type diffuse gliomas are classified based on integrated histological and molecular features, with isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) mutation status serving as a critical determinant7. Under this classification, glioblastoma is defined exclusively as an IDH-wildtype tumor, whereas gliomas harboring IDH mutations are classified as astrocytoma, IDH-mutant, with grades ranging from 2 to 4. IDH mutation status is a well-established prognostic factor in diffuse gliomas, with IDH-mutant tumors generally exhibiting more favorable overall survival compared to IDH-wildtype tumors8,9.

Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), comprising 30%-50% of the DGs tumor microenvironment, can be categorized as central nervous system-resident microglia and MDM10,11. They facilitate tumor development through various mechanisms including modulation of the immune microenvironment, promotion of angiogenesis, and enhancement of tumor cell growth and migration11–16. Previous studies have demonstrated that IDH-wildtype DGs exhibit significantly higher infiltration of monocyte-derived macrophages (MDMs) than IDH-mutant DGs, and that increased MDM infiltration is closely associated with an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and adverse clinical outcomes. Importantly, within the molecular context of IDH-wildtype DGs, elevated MDM infiltration is considered a key contributor to tumor immune evasion and progression, thereby potentially leading to further reductions in overall survival. Our previous research has also unveiled noteworthy disparities in MDM infiltration across various age cohorts of IDH-wildtype patients, highlighting their pivotal role in TAM-mediated immunosuppression and contribution to unfavorable prognosis in elderly patients17. Targeted interventions for MDM population may ameliorate OS rates for these individuals10.

Therefore, research focusing on MDMs holds great potential for exploring therapeutic strategies for high-grade DGs, particularly the more aggressive IDH-wildtype subtype. The detection of MDM infiltration assumes paramount importance in prognosticating unfavorable outcomes among patients. Although performing single-cell analysis on each patient may not be practical, it is plausible to identify relevant genes that indirectly reflect the levels of MDM infiltration through their expression patterns in high-grade DGs. In this study, we focused on adult-type diffuse high-grade diffuse gliomas (specifically IDH-wildtype glioblastoma and IDH-mutant astrocytoma, grade 4) to investigate the role of MDM-derived gene signatures in prognosis and the tumor immune microenvironment. Using advanced bioinformatics techniques and statistical methods, we analysed bulk-seq and single-cell data from public databases. We established a RS model that accurately reflects the levels of MDM infiltration and predicts overall patient survival. This model can serve as a valuable tool for guiding clinical decision-making.

Results

Enrichment of age-related MDM characteristic genes and MDM core genes screening in high-grade DGs

Age is a negative prognostic indicator (Supplementary 1A-D), and prior researches have evinced that MDM infiltration varies significantly among high-grade DGs patients of disparate ages, concomitantly correlating with patient OS7. To address the objective of our study, the TCGA database was initially divided into two groups based on an age threshold of 50. Each group consisted of randomly selected 10 samples. Boxplot statistics revealed a significant decrease in OS for the older group compared to the younger group (Supplementary 1E). Subsequently, we conducted a gene expression difference analysis to identify any discernible differences in gene expression between these two groups. Based on the threshold criteria of fold change > 1.5 and p value < 0.05, we successfully identified 1,795 up-regulated genes and 2,627 down-regulated genes (Fig. 1A). The up-regulated genes discovered in this study can be regarded as up-regulated genes in high-grade DGs patients of advanced age. Gene ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway analyses have illuminated that these genes are enriched for pathways related to cytokine secretion and macrophage activity, indicating a potential involvement of these processes in the pathogenesis of high-grade DGs among elderly individuals. Furthermore, our findings suggest that there is increasing macrophage-associated gene expression in high-grade DGs tumors at aged group, which may reflect distinct characteristics of tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells (Supplementary 2). By analyzing the intersection of up-regulated genes in aged high-grade DGs patients, MDM-specific high-expression genes obtained from previous single-cell analysis, and the high-expression genes of age-related MDM-B subgroup, we have successfully identified 97 genes that represent MDM characteristics with age-related expression change. (Fig. 1B). An interaction network analysis was conducted on these 97 genes, revealing that most of them are interacted intricately, except a few marginal genes (Supplementary 3 A). Moreover, some of these genes exhibit intricate regulatory relationships with each other. These findings suggest a potential synergistic role of these genes in the function of MDM.

Even if these genes were to represent the age related MDM population, their use alone would still result in a redundancy and potential collinearity that could interfere with one another. To address this issue and eliminate collinearity in complex models, we employed the LASSO algorithm, utilizing gene expression data from TCGA database as a training set for LASSO dimensionality reduction algorithm (Supplementary 3B). Through cross-validation, we obtained two optimal lambda values from our training model (Fig. 1C). However, one of these lambda values resulted in a gene count of zero (as indicated by the dashed line on the right side of Fig. 1C). As such, we ultimately opted for the other lambda value (represented by the dashed line on the left side of Fig. 1C), which corresponded to three specific genes: G0S2, PLAUR and SPAG4. In order to validate that these three genes are truly representative, we conducted a regression analysis on their expression levels (Fig. 1D). The findings evinced that the expression levels of these genes escalated with age, which was in line with the observed trend in MDM alterations over time. Single-cell analysis divulged the disparate expression of G0S2, PLAUR and SPAG4 across distinct cell populations (Fig. 1E). Notably, elevated levels of these genes were observed in MDMs and their precursor monocytes while tumor cells exhibited relatively lower expression. G0S2 is G0/G1 switch gene 2, which plays an important role in cell proliferation18, apoptosis19, oxidative phosphorylation20 and other biological processes21, and is also involved in many cancers including glioma19,22,23. The OS of patients with high G0S2 expression in DGs is significantly lower than that of patients with low G0S2 expression22. PLAUR has been reported to regulate plasminogen activation and extracellular matrix degradation in a variety of malignant tumors24,25, and its expression can be used as a potential marker to predict macrophage infiltration and immune microenvironment status in glioma patients26. SPAG4, a component of the nuclear skeleton and cytoskeleton complex27,28, has been implicated in the progression of glioblastoma and holds promise as a biomarker29. Real-time qPCR results further confirmed the expression of G0S2 gene were significant upregulated in beyond 50 years old high-grade DGs samples than these under 50 years old samples (Fig. 1F). The staining results revealed the absence of G0S2, a characteristic gene of MDM, in microglia (Supplementary 4). Moreover, statistical analysis demonstrated a significant reduction in G0S2 expression among patients with IDH mutations (Fig. 1G and H).

Can these three genes exhibit a correlation with patient survival and represent a MDM infiltration difference? We partitioned the TCGA dataset into four equal segments based on the expression levels of these three genes, subsequently selecting the highest and lowest quartiles for analysis of their correlation with OS (Fig. 2A). The findings suggest that the differential expression of these three genes exerts a significant impact on the survival duration of high-grade DGs patients. Moreover, uni-variable COX regression analyses for each gene reveal their status as risk factors for unfavorable prognosis in high-grade DGs patients (Fig. 2B). To comprehensively assess the three genes and simplify their function mechanism, we utilized a multiple variable regression COX model to ascertain their respective coefficients and amalgamate them into the ensuing formula.

Through survival analysis, we have successfully demonstrated that RS possess a robust power in distinguishing between favorable and unfavorable patient prognosis within the TCGA dataset (Fig. 2C). However, further validation is necessary to confirm the functionality of RS. COX regression analysis was performed on common clinical indicators and risk factors for high-grade DGs (Fig. 2D), revealing that age and RSs are significant determinants of survival in high-grade DGs patients, while TP53 and IDH mutations exert a protective effect to some extent30. Other factors failed to exert a significant impact on patient survival. However, when these variables were incorporated into the same model for multi-variable COX regression analysis (Fig. 2E), the originally significant protective factors TP53 mutation and IDH mutation lost P values independent of other variables in the model. Nevertheless, age and RS retained their significance. This implies that RS can act as an autonomous predictor of survival in the TCGA database, and suggests a correlation between TP53, IDH mutations, etc. and MDM infiltration. Additionally, we have drawn a nomogram utilizing three core genes as indicators to prognosticate 1-year, 3-year-survival probability, and median survival (Fig. 2F). The nomograms showcase an extended scale of 10–20 months and are congruent with high-grade DGs’s typical range for median survival time. The calibration curve vividly illustrates the nomogram remarkable predictive performance, which is in great alignment with actual outcomes (Fig. 2G). Moreover, a ROC curve was meticulously constructed to evaluate the prediction efficacy of our RS model (Fig. 2H). The AUC value of 0.77 unequivocally demonstrates that our established RS model possesses a certain degree of prognostic power for patient survival. These compelling findings undeniably validate the significance and utility of our proposed RS model in predicting survival outcomes.

Revalidating the predictive power of RS

The TCGA dataset was employed as the training set for model construction; however, relying solely on this data is insufficient to support the generalizability of the RS model. Therefore, the CGGA dataset was utilized as an independent validation cohort to replicate the findings obtained from the TCGA training set. Samples in the CGGA dataset were stratified into four groups based on the expression levels of the three genes, and comparisons were performed between the highest and lowest quartiles (Fig. 3A). While SPAG4 showed limited statistical significance, both G0S2 and PLAUR exhibited significant correlations with overall survival (OS) and expression levels. Consistently, Cox regression analysis indicated that G0S2 and PLAUR were associated with increased survival risk, whereas SPAG4 demonstrated a relatively weaker effect (Fig. 3B).

In addition, age-related expression analyses of these three genes in the CGGA dataset yielded results consistent with those observed in the TCGA cohort (Fig. 3C), showing an overall trend of increasing gene expression with advancing age.

Furthermore, the RS formula was applied to the CGGA dataset for survival analysis (Fig. 3D). The results demonstrated that RS could effectively stratify patients into high- and low-risk groups with significantly different survival outcomes, indicating its robustness as a risk stratification indicator. To further assess its clinical relevance, several commonly used clinical variables from the CGGA dataset were incorporated into univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses, and time-dependent ROC curves were generated to evaluate prognostic performance (Fig. 3E and G).

Although the area under the ROC curve (AUC) for RS in the CGGA cohort was relatively modest (AUC = 0.59), suggesting limited discriminative power as a standalone predictive model, RS remained a statistically significant factor in univariate analysis and showed independent prognostic relevance alongside established clinical variables such as age and IDH mutation status in multivariate analysis. These findings suggest that RS is not intended to function as a high-accuracy binary prediction tool, but rather as a continuous risk stratification metric that captures biologically meaningful heterogeneity.

Overall, validation in the CGGA dataset yielded results largely consistent with those from the TCGA cohort, supporting the reproducibility of RS across independent datasets and its potential utility as a biologically informed prognostic indicator in diffuse glioma samples.

RS correlated factor analysis

There were significant variations in the extent of MDM infiltration observed among patients with different high-grade DGs. Previous research has demonstrated that IDH mutations are associated with reduced MDM infiltration. Through conducting Cox multiple regression analysis, we have discovered that IDH mutation is linked to improved prognosis and RS expression. In order to further identify the factors influencing RS values, a correlation analysis was conducted.

Age exhibits a positive correlation with RS due to its linear association with the modeling genes of such factors (Fig. 4A). The frequency of gene mutation can be statistically analyzed to determine the correlation between IDH mutation and other mutations (Fig. 4B). As depicted in the figure, there exists a significant negative correlation between RS and IDH, TP53, ATRX, and MGMT mutation frequency. Conversely, PTEN mutation appears to exhibit a modest positive correlation. Further scrutiny was conducted on IDH, RS, age, and MGMT methylation status separately (Fig. 4C-E), which consistently revealed a significant association between RS, IDH status and MGMT methylation. The negative correlation between IDH mutation and RS may be partially attributed to the relatively young age of patients with IDH mutation, but it cannot be excluded that IDH mutation itself also affects MDM infiltration. Moreover, RS did not exhibit a significant association with common high-grade DGs highly heterotypic gene copy number variants (Supplementary 5). Furthermore, RS has the ability to distinguish between elderly patients with varying OS outcomes, as depicted in Fig. 4F and G. In general, RS demonstrates a robust and independent predictive capacity and may serve as a more precise alternative to age when predicting OS in most cases. However, it is important to note that certain tumor types such as IDH mutated, TP53 mutated and MGMT promoter methylated subtypes may not be suitable for RS detection or targeted MDM therapy.

RS correlated with macrophage infiltration level and the characteristics of pro-tumor immune microenvironment

To explore RS’s impact on the immune microenvironment we conducted an analysis of cell subpopulation infiltration fractions using the TCGA dataset (Fig. 5A), which revealed a significant upregulation trend in M0, M1, and M2 macrophages, while leukomonocytes exhibited no significant change trend. The correlation p value between CD4 + T-cell, CD8 + T cell and B cell ratio scores and RS was found to be well below the threshold of significance, indicating no association. This approach was replicated in the CGGA dataset with similar results (Supplementary 6 A). Our findings from this dataset suggest an even more robust positive correlation between RS and macrophage infiltration proportion, while no such relationship was observed with other immune cell types.

Furthermore, we employed the ssGSEA methodology to evaluate the expression scores of prevalent immune cell populations within the dataset (Fig. 5B). The results revealed distinct positive and negative correlations between different cell types as RS increasing from low to high. Within this group of factors lies a positive correlation between regulatory T cells, mast cells, mononuclear-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), macrophages and other contributors (as indicated by the red box in the diagram) which all play a role in promoting tumor growth and worsening prognosis within its microenvironment. The cell populations exhibiting negative correlation consist of activated CD4 + T cells, memory B cells, eosinophils, NK T cell, NK cells with low CD56 expression and Th17 T-cell (as depicted in the blue box). Among these, the activated B-cell, NK T-cell and CD4 + T-cell are all immune-active types that can effectively induce anti-tumor response and promote pre- and post-operative recovery. Furthermore, there is no apparent pattern observed in certain groups such as γδT and immature B cells, leaving the relationship between these groups and RS ambiguous.

In addition to conducting ssGSEA analysis on the TCGA dataset, we also validated our findings using the CGGA dataset (Supplementary 6B). Our results unequivocally demonstrated that the cell types identified within the red box were largely consistent with those observed in the TCGA dataset and exhibited similar trends. Remarkably, infiltration n scores for mast cells, MDSCs, and macrophages exhibited an overall increase as RS escalated. The immune activation groups enclosed in the blue box bear resemblance to those observed in the TCGA dataset, encompassing Th17 cells, NKT cells and effector memory CD4 + T cells. These groups exhibit a declining trend in terms of RS, albeit not as pronounced as that observed in the TCGA dataset. Overall, both the TCGA and CGGA datasets evince that MDM infiltration levels are mirrored by the RS model and exert an impact on patient outcomes via modulation of the immune microenvironment.

RS and its constituent genes have different effects in patients of different genders

As RS comprises three representative genes, we conducted a comprehensive literature review to determine the MDM subgroup each gene represents. Previous researches have suggested that IL-1B which potentially interacts with PLAUR (Supplementary 3 A) plays a role in gender determinated MDM subtype infiltration differences, impacting the prognosis of female patients significantly31. Therefore, we stratified TCGA dataset samples by sex and performed survival analysis for these three genes (Fig. 6). The results revealed a notable discrepancy in survival rates based on the expression level of G0S2 among male subjects, while no such distinction was observed among their female counterparts. Conversely, PLAUR and SPAG4 showed no significant difference in survival rates within male cohorts but exhibited one within female cohorts. We performed a comparable analysis utilizing the CGGA dataset (Supplementary 7) and discovered that, except for SPAG4 expression not being significantly associated with survival in females, PLAUR was on the brink of achieving statistical significance in this population. However, only G0S2 manifested a significant difference in survival among male patients, while the other two genes did not. Significant survival differentiating effects were observed for RS in both TCGA and CGGA datasets, indicating that RS integrated subtype characteristics represented by the three core genes.

Upon further analysis of macrophage infiltration levels, we divided the data by gender and found a positive correlation between RS and macrophage infiltration levels (Supplementary 8 A and B). Additionally, the immune infiltration characteristics were similar across genders (Supplementary 8 C).

Collectively, these findings indicate that RS retains robust prognostic predictive ability in both male and female glioblastoma patients across the TCGA and CGGA cohorts, supporting its stability as an immune-related risk model. Although immune infiltration analyses were performed in the overall population, the consistent survival-discriminating power of RS in both genders suggests that it captures a conserved macrophage-enriched, pro-tumor immune microenvironment irrespective of sex. Notably, sex-specific differences were observed in the prognostic relevance of RS constituent genes, with G0S2 primarily contributing to risk stratification in males, while PLAUR and SPAG4 exerted stronger prognostic effects in females. These findings imply that RS reflects a shared macrophage-driven immunosuppressive phenotype across genders, whereas the underlying molecular pathways contributing to this phenotype may differ between male and female patients.

Discussion

High-grade DGs is an extremely malignant tumor with a poor prognosis. Despite previous beliefs that brain tumors lacked immune cells, recent research has revealed the efficacy of immunotherapy against high-grade DGs and the infiltration of MDMs32,33. Among these MDMs, IDH WT patients exhibit the most significant alteration in infiltration rate, while IDH MT patients show more resident microglial activations34,35. Reducing TAM infiltration can alleviate immunosuppression36. In particular, decreasing MDM levels has been demonstrated to significantly prolong OS in patients with IDH WT33. Therefore, the identification of MDM infiltration is crucial for targeted therapy of high-grade DGs.

In this study, the integration of bulk-seq and scRNA-seq has led to the identification of three representative genes, and a RS model has been established to prognosticate the impact of MDM infiltration on patient prognosis. The model demonstrates significant associations with age, macrophage infiltration, and several gene mutations. Targeting-MDM therapy may offer greater benefits to elderly patients with IDH WT, particularly in women with high RS. Patients with IDH mutation are less responsive to MDM strategies, possibly due to a mechanism that regulates the reduction of MDM infiltration. Understanding this mechanism could help develop effective techniques for inhibiting MDM infiltration and improving tumor immune response. The RS presented herein not only reflect age-related differences in MDM infiltration but also have the remarkable capability to independently predict OS. Moreover, even among individuals belonging to the same age group, there might exist diverse levels of heterogeneity concerning MDM infiltration. Therefore, by converting an individual’s chronological age into corresponding RS model outcomes, we can achieve a more accurate prognosis regarding their actual OS rate regardless of their respective ages.

For RS consisting of three genes, there is a dearth of research on their correlation with high-grade DGs. Prior studies have evinced that suppression of G0S2 expression can curtail cell secretion of CCL2, which serves as a prototypical ligand for recruiting peripheral macrophages37. Targeted therapy against G0S2 may diminish the infiltration amount in the MDM population and thus specifically reduce the MDM population, particularly in males, with potentially greater benefits. However, the low expression of PLAUR and SPGA4 in female patients may confer greater benefits. PLAUR and other candidate MDM characteristic genes are enriched for screening interaction networks, suggesting that targeting PLAUR could regulate the entire network and improve patient outcomes.

The elevated expression of RS is linked to immunosuppression within the tumor microenvironment, suggesting that these genes or targeted MDMs may regulate tumor growth by influencing the immune microenvironment. This study highlights the heterogeneity of MDM infiltration in high-grade DGs patients and establishes RS as a characteristic for predicting patients’ MDM-related OS. Targeted therapy for MDMs will be particularly beneficial for elderly IDH WT patients, especially women with high RS.

Methods

Data preparation

Data for patients with high-grade DGs (IDH-wildtype glioblastoma or IDH-mutant astrocytoma, CNS WHO grade 4) were obtained from TCGA and CGGA databases. For clarity and consistency, this patient cohort is hereafter referred to as ‘high-grade DGs’ in the text. TCGA DGs bulk-RNA sequencing count matrices were downloaded from GDC (Genomic Data Commons). Single Cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets has described in previous study17, which were derived from GSE117891, GSE131928, CPTAC-3, GSE163120, GSE89567, and SCP503. Clinical information and FPKM matrices were acquired by using R package cgdsr (ver 1.3.0). CGGA DGs patients’ clinical information and FPKM matrices were downloaded from mRNAseq_693 and mRNAseq_325 in CGGA websites. Both IDH-mutant and IDH-wildtype diffuse glioma samples were included in these datasets. After being downloading, all recurrent DGs samples were removed. CGGA samples are integrated and batch effect were removed.

Single cell RNA-sequencing analysis

We constructed an atlas of cells in high-grade DGs by integrating six public Single Cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets generated by various studies. R package Seurat (ver 4.1.1) was used to integrated these datasets and next analysis. To find the subpopulation of every cell type, we needed to perform clustering on the two-dimensional coordinates of cells. Therefore, we built a model that can predict the sub-clusters using a python package Scikit-learn. This model allows for automatic estimation of the number of subpopulations, since the main components of the model are the Dirichlet process.

After obtaining the expression matrix corrected for batch effects, we constructed a new Seurat object, and then calculated its differential genes according to the FindMarkers function of the Seurat package. Violin plots are visualized by the VlnPlot function of the Seurat package. Besides, in order to merge different violin plots, we drew the stacked violin plots based on our code.

Differential gene analysis

We using bulk-seq count matrix as the input files. By R package DESeq2 (ver 1.32.0), we found out differential genes between aged and adult group. The result was displayed by volcano using ggplot2 package (ver 3.4.0) and related packages, including ggpubr (ver 0.4.0) and ggrepel (ver 0.9.1).

Survival analysis

Survival analysis was performed by Rstudio (R version 4.1.2). The R packages survival (ver 3.3.1) and survminer (ver 0.4.9) were used to calculate and plot the Kaplan-Meier curve. For legacy data, median levels were used to segregate cancer patients according to OS outcome, gene expression and age difference. P value is adjusted with the Benjamini-Hochberg correction method.

Cox regression model was also established using survival package. OS status and time were transformed into “surv” object. Unicox use only a variable to perform cox regression, while multicox using more than one variables. Forest plots were plotting by R package forestplot (ver 3.1.0).

LASSO algorithm

We used R package glnmet to perform LASSO algorithm for screening core genes. Aged group upregulated gene expression matrix was the input data, with corresponding survival data. By setting alpha equaling to 1 and “family”=”cox”, lasso dimensionality reduction was conducted and through cross validation, the most moderate model was established. Core genes were determined, too.

Enrichment analysis

Three different pathway enrichment methods were used in the article. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment were enforced by R package clusterprofiler (ver 4.0.5). Gene Sets Enrichment assay (GSEA) enrichments was enforced by GSEA software (ver 4.1.0). GSEA gmt files were from MSigDB (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb). Single-sample GSEA (ssGSEA) was performed by R through R packages GSEABase (ver 1.54.0) and GSVA(ver 1.40.1). Enrichment dataset was downloaded from http://cis.hku.hk/TISIDB/data/download/CellReports.txt. Gene FPKM matrices were from TCGA and CGGA dataset. The result was plotted by R package pheatmap (ver 1.0.12), ranking by RS.

Nomogram and calibration curve

Nomogram was drawn by R package survival and rms (ver 6.5.0). Using COX regression to caculate the effect of core genes to 1-3year survival of patients. Then a survival function was established and the mean survival time was worked out. After that, a nomogram and a three-point corresponding calibration curve were drawn.

RS and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve

RS model was established as a linear model. Using gene expression multiplied by respective coefficients. Then a ROC curve was plot using R package survivalROC (ver 1.0.3) and plotROC (ver 2.3.0). ROC of two timepoints were plotted in the same plot and AUCmax was calculated.

VENN and other plots

We use R package VennDiagram (ver 1.7.3) to plot three-dimension venn plot. Besides plots mentioned above, other plots, like scatter plots, boxplots were all made by R package ggplot2.

Immunofluorescent staining

Human samples were obtained with informed consent from GBM patients undergoing craniotomy at the First Affiliated Hospital of Xiamen University. All experimental processes were conducted with the support and approved of the Medical Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xiamen University (XMYY-2023KY113). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Human brain sections were deparaffinized and fixed slides with 2% PFA at room temperature. Blocked with PBT at room temperature for 1 h. Brain sections incubated with primary antibody overnight at 65℃, followed by secondary antibodies for 6 h at room temperature. Then washed in PBT for 10 min and imaged with Leica confocal microscope TCS SP8 DLS.

RNA extraction and real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis

Tissue total RNA was isolated with TRI reagent (SIGMA) and reverse-transcribed the RevertAid RT kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Real-time qPCR reaction was performed with FastStart Universal SYBR Green Master (ROX) (Roche), and the expression levels were quantified by method -ΔΔCt.18S rRNA was used as the internal control. Primer sequences for real-time qPCR were used as follows:

G0S2:5’- ACTTCAGAGAAACCGCTGACAT-3’(forward)

5’- CAGCAGCAAAACTCAATCCCAA-3’(reverse);

18S: 5’-GTCTGTGATGCCCTTAGATG-3’(forward)

5’-AGCTTATGACCCGCACTTAC-3’(reverse).

Contribution to the field

TAMs play a crucial role in tumorigenesis and development. However, due to the heterogeneity of macrophages, it is necessary to discuss their classification more comprehensively and utilize appropriate systematic and molecular markers as pointers for detection. This study emphasizes that the infiltration of MDMs, rather than microglia, exhibits significant variation among different high-grade DGs patients and is associated with unfavorable patient outcomes due to its detrimental impact on the immune microenvironment. Moreover, we have identified three characteristic genes of MDMs (G0S2, PLAUR, SPAG4) to construct a risk score that correlates with age and sex as prognostic indicators for OS in high-grade DGs patients. Our findings also elucidate the characteristics of high-risk populations, providing a foundation for targeted therapeutic intervention in the future.
