Comment on “Burden of hypertension-related diseases in Eastern Mediterranean region”
Authors: Sangameshwar Manikya, Varshini Vadhithala, Rajnish Kumar, Pankaj Nainwal
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with interest the population-level cost-of-illness analysis by Ahmed et al., which quantifies premature mortality, years of life lost, and productivity losses attributable to hypertension across the Eastern Mediterranean Region.1 By integrating population attributable fractions with a human capital framework, the authors provide a regionally comparable estimate that underscores the macroeconomic footprint of uncontrolled blood pressure in adults aged 30–70 years.

First, the analytical reliance on Levin's population attributable fraction using a single hazard ratio per outcome across heterogeneous health systems warrants closer consideration. Although pragmatic, this approach assumes transportability of relative risks from Golestan and Asia–Pacific cohorts to settings with distinct cardiometabolic profiles, treatment coverage, and competing risks. In populations with lower treatment adherence or delayed detection, the gradient between hypertension and ischemic heart disease or stroke may be steeper than estimated, whereas in higher-income settings with widespread pharmacotherapy it may be attenuated.2 This structural assumption can shift both attributable deaths and downstream cost projections. Region-specific pooled cohort analyses or Bayesian recalibration using country-level blood pressure control rates may yield more context-sensitive estimates and refine prioritization strategies.3

Second, the decision to value productivity losses exclusively through gross domestic product per capita within the human capital method frames economic loss as average macro-output rather than labor-force–weighted contribution. In countries with youthful age structures or high informal employment, the marginal productivity of individuals aged 30–49 years may differ substantially from national per capita output. Because nearly half of attributable deaths occurred before age 60, small changes in age-specific earnings assumptions could meaningfully alter present value of lifetime earnings estimates and the reported 0.49 percent gross domestic product impact. Complementary friction-cost modeling or age- and sex-specific labor participation adjustments would permit sensitivity analyses aligned with labor-market realities in conflict-affected or oil-dependent economies.4

Third, although the study reports years of life lost, the interpretation of burden would benefit from explicit linkage to clinical control gaps. The population attributable fractions of approximately 41 percent for stroke and 25 percent for ischemic heart disease imply that even modest improvements in systolic blood pressure distribution could yield disproportionate reductions in cerebrovascular mortality. However, without modeling shifts in mean systolic blood pressure or treatment coverage, the counterfactual reduction scenarios remain prevalence-based rather than outcome-based.5 Integrating distributional risk models, similar to those used in comparative risk assessment frameworks, would allow policymakers to translate a five-point prevalence reduction into projected declines in stroke incidence, hospitalization demand, and dialysis initiation, thereby aligning epidemiologic metrics with service planning.

Notwithstanding these considerations, the analysis provides an important regional benchmark at a pre-pandemic time point and situates hypertension as a leading driver of avoidable economic loss in the Eastern Mediterranean Region. Strengthening surveillance systems capable of generating country-specific risk estimates and labor-adjusted economic valuations may enhance precision, while coupling burden estimates with modeled gains from intensified primary care control strategies could bridge epidemiology and implementation. Such refinements would further advance the policy relevance of this timely contribution.
