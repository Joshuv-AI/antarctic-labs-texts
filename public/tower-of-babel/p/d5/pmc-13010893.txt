Research note: Stage-associated differences in hygiene indicator bacteria from pre-chlorinated water immersion chilling (CWIC) carcasses through CWIC to packaged quail products✰
Authors: Hyunsook Kim, Hye-Young Youn, Hyeon-Jin Kim, Yong-Seok Jang, Jungwhan Chon, Kwang-Young Song, Kun-Ho Seo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCommercial processing of quails has been less studied than broiler processing, despite the food safety relevance of hygienic control in poultry slaughter operations. This study characterized hygiene indicator bacteria in quail carcasses pre- and post-chlorinated water immersion chilling (CWIC), in finished packaged products, and in selected processing-environment matrices across three independent trials in a commercial facility. In each trial, whole-carcass rinse samples were collected from pre-CWIC (n = 5) and post-CWIC (n = 5) carcasses, with technical triplicate plating for each carcass. The finished packaged products (n = 15 per trial) and environmental samples (gloves, precooling chamber floor, aspiration inlet opening, wash water, and CWIC water) were also evaluated. Aerobic plate counts (APC), coliforms, and Escherichia coli were enumerated and expressed as log colony forming units (CFU)/mL. Qualitative screening for Salmonella was performed using enrichment. Across trials, CWIC reduced APC; however, the magnitude varied (Trial 1: 2.75–2.28 log CFU/mL; Trial 2: 4.60–2.35 log CFU/mL; and Trial 3: 3.21–3.08 log CFU/mL). Coliforms and E. coli were not detected in Trial 1 carcass-stage samples. Trial 2 showed decreased coliforms post-CWIC, with E. coli not detected in the post-CWIC group. Trial 3 showed detectable coliforms and E. coli both pre- and post-CWIC. In all trials, packaged finished products exhibited higher indicator levels than post-CWIC carcasses, including higher APC (4.05–4.88 log CFU/mL across trials). Gloves consistently showed the highest indicator counts among the environmental matrices, whereas wash water and CWIC water were negative in Trial 1 but showed detectable APC in Trials 2 and 3, indicating variability in process hygiene. Salmonella was not detected in any of the carcass stages, packaged products, or environmental samples tested. Overall, the data identified post-wash handling points and water/disinfectant management as practical targets for verification to improve hygiene consistency during commercial quail processing.Keywords: Quail processing, Immersion chilling, Carcass rinse, Aerobic plate count, Escherichia coli

Introduction

Consumption of quail meat is increasing, although it is less widely consumed than chicken and turkey. As production expands and quail products diversify in retail markets, hygienic control during slaughter and processing is becoming increasingly important. However, processing-focused microbiological evidence for quails remains limited compared to that for broiler systems. Most quail research has emphasized production performance, nutrition, and meat quality/composition, rather than processing-stage microbiology (Minvielle, 2004).

Available microbiological studies on quail meat have largely focused on retail-level quality rather than on processing-stage dynamics. A retail survey of fresh quail meat reported substantial indicator loads (mesophiles and Enterobacteriaceae) and detected hazards, including Listeria monocytogenes, highlighting the need for improved hygiene control and source identification across the quail processing continuum (Martinez-Laorden et al., 2023).

In broiler processing, immersion chilling remains a common step; however, in many regions, the antimicrobial used in chiller water has shifted from chlorine toward peroxyacetic acid in recent years (Micciche et al., 2018; Leone et al., 2024). Nevertheless, chlorinated water immersion chilling (CWIC) is still practiced in some plants and served as the focus of this study. Chlorination of immersion chiller water can reduce aerobic plate counts (APCs) and Enterobacteriaceae/Escherichia coli on poultry carcasses (James et al., 1992). However, immersion systems can also facilitate bacterial redistribution and cross-contamination when antimicrobial efficacy is compromised by organic load and water management, emphasizing the need for process verification rather than assuming uniform reductions (Micciche et al., 2018; Xiao et al., 2019).

From a process-control perspective, stage-associated monitoring, from pre-intervention to post-intervention carcasses and finally packaged products, can clarify whether the observed changes reflect true decontamination, cross-contamination during immersion and handling, or subsequent increases driven by post-chill contact events and cold-tolerant flora that may persist and become more prominent during downstream steps. Such an interpretation is particularly relevant for quails, where commercial slaughter lines may differ in scale and equipment compared to broilers, and published stage-to-stage datasets are limited.

Indicator organisms such as APC, coliforms, and E. coli are widely used to assess process hygiene and verify sanitation and process control in poultry processing (EFSA, 2014). Beyond carcass testing, targeted environmental sampling (e.g., gloves, floors, equipment openings, and water/disinfectant points) supports bio-mapping to identify contamination reservoirs and cross-contamination vectors within a facility (Shi et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2020). To the best of our knowledge, stage-associated changes in hygiene indicator bacteria during commercial quail processing, from pre-CWIC carcasses (after the final spray rinse) through CWIC to packaged products, have not yet been systematically characterized.

Therefore, the objectives of this study were to (i) characterize hygiene indicator bacteria levels in quail carcasses before and after CWIC and (ii) evaluate hygiene indicator bacteria in finished packaged products and selected processing-environment matrices across three independent trials in a commercial quail slaughter facility.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

From July to September 2020, sampling was conducted across three independent trials (Trials 1–3) at a commercial quail slaughter facility in Kyeonggi-do, South Korea (approximately > 10,000 quails/day). Each trial included: (1) pre-CWIC carcasses collected immediately after the final spray rinse and just before entry into the chlorinated water immersion chiller (n = 5); (2) post-CWIC carcasses collected immediately after exit from the immersion chiller and drip-draining, before further handling (n = 5); (3) packaged finished products defined as ready-to-retail whole quail carcasses collected after routine post-CWIC handling and packaging (one carcass per retail package; n = 15); and (4) selected environmental matrices, including gloves worn by line workers handling carcasses (n = 3), the precooling chamber floor (n = 3), the aspiration inlet opening ("inhaler hole", n = 3), final spray-rinse wash water collected immediately before CWIC (n = 3), and CWIC water (n = 3). All samples were individually wrapped, stored in an ice chest, and transported to the laboratory within 2 h for immediate processing.

Sample preparation

Whole-carcass rinse samples were collected at two processing stages: pre-CWIC (immediately before entry into CWIC, after the final spray rinse) and post-CWIC (immediately after exit from CWIC and drip-draining, before downstream handling or packaging). For each carcass, the sample weight was recorded, the carcass was placed in a sterile bag, and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Oxoid, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Basingstoke, UK) was added at a 1:9 (sample:PBS, w/v) ratio. The bags were vigorously mixed to obtain a rinse suspension, which was serially diluted 10-fold in PBS (10⁻¹–10⁻⁵) for bacterial enumeration. Packaged finished products (ready-to-retail whole carcasses) were analyzed using the same whole-carcass rinsing procedure.

Final carcass wash water (final spray-rinse water collected immediately before CWIC) and chlorinated immersion chiller water used for CWIC were aseptically collected in sterile 50-mL conical tubes. The CWIC water had a target free available chlorine concentration of 50 ppm at the initiation of chilling, and plant personnel monitored and adjusted the concentration during operation according to facility practice. Each liquid sample was serially diluted 10-fold in PBS (10⁻¹–10⁻⁵) for subsequent enumeration on 3M™ Petrifilm™ plates (3 M, St. Paul, MN, USA). Surface samples (gloves worn by line workers handling carcasses, the precooling chamber floor, and the aspiration inlet opening ["inhaler hole"]) were collected from defined areas (10 × 10 cm) using a 3M™ Pipette Swab Plus kit (3 M), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The swab suspensions were serially diluted 10-fold in PBS (10⁻¹–10⁻⁵) for subsequent enumeration on 3M™ Petrifilm™ plates.

Enumeration of hygiene indicator bacteria (APC, colicforms, and E. coli)

Hygiene indicator bacteria (APC, coliforms, and E. coli) were enumerated using 3M™ Petrifilm™ plates according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Sample suspensions (whole-carcass rinses, processing solutions, and surface swab eluates) were serially diluted 10-fold in PBS (10⁻¹–10⁻⁵). For each specimen, 1 mL aliquots of appropriate dilution(s) were inoculated onto Petrifilm™ Aerobic Count (AC), Coliform Count (CC), and E. coli/Coliform Count (EC) plates in technical triplicates (three plates per dilution). The inoculated plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24–48 h.

Colonies were interpreted as follows: red colonies on Petrifilm™ AC plates were counted as APC, red colonies with associated gas bubbles on Petrifilm™ CC plates were counted as coliforms, and blue colonies with associated gas bubbles on Petrifilm™ EC plates were counted as E. coli. Plate counts were averaged across technical triplicates to yield one value per specimen and expressed as log colony forming units (CFU)/mL.

Detection of Salmonella in quail carcasses, packaged products, and environmental samples

Qualitative detection of Salmonella in carcass-stage samples (pre-CWIC, post-CWIC, and packaged finished products) and environmental matrices (wash water, chlorinated immersion chiller water, and swab suspensions) was performed using a two-step enrichment procedure, followed by plating on selective agar, as described below.

Primary enrichment: For carcass-stage and packaged-product samples, Salmonella screening was performed using the whole-carcass rinse suspension after PBS rinsing for indicator enumeration. Briefly, after the carcass was removed from the bag, a 25-mL aliquot of the rinse suspension was transferred to 225 mL buffered peptone water (BPW; Oxoid) and incubated at 37 °C for 18–24 h. For liquid samples (final wash water and CWIC water) and swab suspensions, a 25-mL aliquot of each sample (or swab eluate) was mixed with 225 mL BPW and incubated under the same conditions.

Secondary selective enrichment: Following primary enrichment, 1 mL of BPW culture was transferred to 10 mL tetrathionate (TT; Oxoid) broth and incubated at 37 °C for 20–24 h. In parallel, 0.1 mL of BPW culture was inoculated into 10 mL Rappaport–Vassiliadis (RV; Oxoid) broth and incubated at 42 °C for 20–24 h.

Isolation on selective agar: After selective enrichment, cultures from TT and RV broths were streaked onto xylose lysine deoxycholate agar (Oxoid) and incubated at 37 °C for 20–24 h. No presumptive Salmonella colonies were recovered from any of the samples; therefore, confirmatory identification was not performed.

Statistical analysis

The carcass was used as the experimental unit. For each trial, pairwise comparisons were performed between pre- and post-CWIC carcasses and between post-CWIC carcasses and packaged finished products using Welch’s two-sample t-test (two-sided) with α = 0.05. Technical triplicate plates per carcass were used to derive a single value per carcass and were not treated as independent observations. Environmental matrices were summarized descriptively without inferential statistics.

Results and discussion

Trial 1

In Trial 1, APC in carcass rinse samples decreased from 2.75 ± 0.10 (pre-CWIC) to 2.28 ± 0.07 log CFU/mL (post-CWIC), representing a reduction of 0.47 log CFU/mL (Welch’s t-test, p < 0.001; Table 1). Coliforms and E. coli were not detected in carcass rinse samples either pre- or post-CWIC. In contrast, packaged finished products showed a higher APC (4.05 ± 0.31 log CFU/mL), exceeding the post-CWIC level by 1.77 log CFU/mL (p < 0.001; Table 1). Environmental monitoring showed the highest indicator levels on gloves, whereas no indicators were detected in the final wash water or CWIC water (Table 2).

Trial 2

In Trial 2, APC decreased from 4.60 ± 0.08 (pre-CWIC) to 2.35 ± 0.04 log CFU/mL (post-CWIC), a reduction of 2.25 log CFU/mL (p < 0.001; Table 1). Coliforms were detected in the pre-CWIC group (3.48 log CFU/mL; single positive value) and post-CWIC group (0.49 ± 0.85 log CFU/mL), whereas E. coli was not detected in carcass-stage samples. Packaged products exhibited a higher APC than post-CWIC carcasses (4.11 ± 0.36 log CFU/mL; +1.76 log CFU/mL, p < 0.001). Coliform levels did not differ between post-CWIC carcasses and packaged products (p = NS; Table 1). The final wash water (1.66 ± 1.10 log CFU/mL) and CWIC water (3.11 ± 0.06 log CFU/mL) had detectable APCs, and gloves again exhibited high indicator counts (Table 2).

Trial 3

In Trial 3, APC decreased slightly from 3.21 ± 0.08 (pre-CWIC) to 3.08 ± 0.06 log CFU/mL (post-CWIC), a reduction of 0.13 log CFU/mL (p < 0.05; Table 1). Coliforms and E. coli were detected at both stages. Coliform counts increased numerically from 0.97 ± 0.06 to 1.26 ± 0.24 log CFU/mL (p = NS), whereas E. coli counts did not differ between stages (p = NS; Table 1). Packaged products showed higher indicator levels than post-CWIC carcasses, including APC (4.88 ± 0.17 log CFU/mL; +1.80 log CFU/mL, p < 0.001), coliforms (+1.24 log CFU/mL, p < 0.001), and E. coli (+1.70 log CFU/mL, p < 0.01). APC was detected in the final wash water and CWIC water, and gloves again carried high indicator levels (Table 2).

Across all carcass-stage samples (pre-CWIC, post-CWIC, and packaged finished products) and environmental matrices tested, no presumptive Salmonella colonies were recovered following qualitative enrichment and selective plating; therefore, Salmonella was not detected in this study.

Across three independent trials, CWIC was associated with reductions in carcass APC; however, the magnitude ranged from minimal (Trial 3) to substantial (Trial 2), indicating variable hygienic performance under commercial conditions. Such variability is consistent with the known dependence of immersion-chiller efficacy on organic load and water management (Micciche et al., 2018; Xiao et al., 2019).

A consistent finding was that packaged finished products exhibited higher indicator levels than post-CWIC carcasses, including higher APC in all trials and higher coliforms and E. coli in Trial 3. These increases suggest additional contamination opportunities during post-CWIC handling and packaging and highlight the value of stage-associated monitoring to distinguish decontamination during CWIC from downstream recontamination.

Environmental monitoring supported this interpretation: gloves worn by line workers consistently had the highest indicator counts among matrices tested, and APC was detected in final spray-rinse water and/or CWIC water in Trials 2 and 3. Together, these results identify post-CWIC handling points and water/disinfectant management as practical targets for verification to improve hygiene consistency in commercial quail processing.

Salmonella was not detected in any carcass-stage samples, packaged products, or environmental matrices. However, considering the limited number of carcasses and sampling days per trial, larger and more temporally representative sampling would be required to estimate pathogen prevalence and intervention performance against enteric pathogens.

Overall, this Research Note provides stage-associated hygiene indicator data for commercial quail processing, showing that CWIC can reduce carcass bacterial loads but that end-product indicator levels may increase during downstream handling. Routine verification focused on personnel contact surfaces and CWIC water control may help stabilize hygiene outcomes.
