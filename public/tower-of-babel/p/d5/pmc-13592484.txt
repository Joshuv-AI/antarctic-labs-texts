Modeling temporal lobe epilepsy with hippocampal sclerosis in rats using the selective neurotoxin stable substance P‐saporin
Authors: Srijal Gupta, Mitchell T Kesler, Morris H Scantlebury, Robert S Sloviter, G Campbell Teskey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTemporal lobe epilepsy with hippocampal sclerosis (TLE‐HS+) is a common and often refractory form of human epilepsy. In the hippocampus, the neurotoxin stable substance P‐saporin (SSP‐SAP) targets hilar inhibitory interneurons and has been used to determine whether hilar neuron loss in an otherwise normal brain is sufficient to initiate the epileptogenic process. We determined the time course of SSP‐SAP‐induced epileptogenesis, as well as the loss of principal cells and astrogliosis, two defining features of TLE‐HS+.MethodsWe started with 70‐day‐old male and female Sprague Dawley rats and injected SSP‐SAP into four unilateral sites along the longitudinal axis of the rat dentate gyrus. We determined how long SSP‐SAP is detectably present in inhibitory interneurons and then analyzed the electroencephalographic characteristics and behavioral expression of the “reactive” seizures that developed several days after SSP‐SAP injection. We then quantified the number of self‐generated 7‐Hz epileptiform events over 3 months. Finally, we counted the numbers of principal cells and astrocytes up to 2 months postinjection.ResultsSSP‐SAP was internalized within 2 h after injection and was immunocytochemically undetectable by 5 days. Rats exhibited spontaneous electrographic and behavioral reactive seizures between days 4 and 6 after SSP‐SAP injection, with most seizures having a Racine score of either 1 or 2. After the early seizures had abated, the number and duration of self‐generated 7‐Hz epileptiform events, which were associated with behavioral arrest, progressively increased over the 3‐month observation period, indicating epileptogenesis. We also observed the progressive loss of CA1 and CA3 neurons and the progressive increase in astrocytes over time, two defining features of HS, a common pathological observation in TLE patients.SignificanceThis study confirms that the SSP‐SAP model reproduces the defining features of human TLE and that a primary, selective, and longitudinally extensive γ‐aminobutyric acidergic defect is sufficient to trigger epileptogenesis that results in TLE‐HS+.Keywords: animal models, epileptogenesis, hippocampal sclerosis, inhibitory neuron ablation, seizures, temporal lobe epilepsy

INTRODUCTION

Temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) is a common seizure disorder affecting approximately 50 million people worldwide. 1 For decades, most animal models of TLE have relied upon chemoconvulsants to induce convulsive status epilepticus (SE), which is defined as prolonged and continuous seizure activity. When not fatal, prolonged SE results in widespread brain damage and a chronic epileptic state. 2 However, prolonged convulsive SE is rarely the cause of TLE in patients, and its use in nonhuman animal studies provides little information about the endogenous defect that underlies epileptogenesis. The imbalance between excitation and inhibition has been proposed as a driver for the development of epilepsy, 3 but the role of γ‐aminobutyric acidergic (GABAergic) dysfunction in this imbalance 4 has remained poorly understood. 5 , 6 , 7 Early nonhuman animal studies indicated a role for GABAergic dysfunction in the occurrence of acute drug‐induced seizures, but not for the development of unprovoked TLE, 8 , 9 , 10 and a more recent study addressed this question using chemogenetics to either ablate or silence hippocampal inhibitory neurons. 11 In 2019, Chun et al., 12 reported that selectively ablating dentate hilar GABA interneurons with stable substance P‐saporin (SSP‐SAP) along the longitudinal axis of the dentate gyrus resulted in self‐generated epileptic seizures.

SSP‐SAP is a conjugate of the protein synthesis inhibitor saporin and a form of substance P resistant to enzymatic degradation. 11 , 12 It is internalized via the binding of substance P receptors, also known as neurokinin‐1 (NK1) receptors, which within the rodent hippocampus are exclusively expressed by virtually all inhibitory interneurons. 12 Once internalized as a “Trojan horse” toxin, saporin kills cells by inhibiting protein synthesis. 13

Hippocampal sclerosis (HS) is a common pathological finding in individuals with TLE and is characterized by principal cell loss, astrogliosis, and pathology in related structures. 13 Chun and colleagues previously showed extensive principal cell loss and hippocampal shrinkage several months following SSP‐SAP injection and attributed it to the early nonconvulsive seizures caused by SSP‐SAP injection. 3 , 12 The present study focused on the early changes during the first 3 months following unilateral administration of SSP‐SAP. Here, we determined the time it takes for SSP‐SAP to be internalized and how long it is detectable within inhibitory interneurons. Next, we quantified the electroencephalographic (EEG) characteristics and seizure behavior associated with the earliest “reactive” seizures. 14 We then quantified the progressive increase in the frequency and duration of self‐generated 7‐Hz epileptiform events and the numbers of principal cells and astrocytes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Rats

Adult male and female Sprague Dawley rats (N = 47) weighing 350–450 g were obtained from Charles River. Males were used for SSP‐SAP internalization and endogenous kindling experiments, whereas both sexes were used for the presence and progression of HS experiments. Rats were individually housed in clear cages in a temperature‐controlled room and were maintained on a 12/12‐h light/dark cycle with lights on at 7:00 a.m. All experiments and protocols were carried out in the light phase and were approved by the University of Calgary Health Sciences Animal Care Committee (AC24‐0194) and in accordance with the Canadian Council for Animal Care guidelines. Experiments were designed to adhere to the three R's of animal research: replacement, refinement, and reduction.

Surgery

Rats were anesthetized using isoflurane (5% induction, 2%–3% maintenance) combined with 100% oxygen. Following induction, heads were shaved and secured in a stereotaxic apparatus and injected with the analgesic Metacam (2 mg/kg, subcutaneously) and the antibiotic Baytril (10 mg/kg, subcutaneously). The scalp was cleaned using betadine and 70% ethanol, and lidocaine (.2 mL) was injected under the scalp as a local anesthetic. A midline anterior–posterior scalp incision was made, bregma was identified, and the four coordinates for injection were marked. The coordinates for unilateral (right side) injection were relative to bregma and were as follows: (1) anterior/posterior (A/P): −3.5, medial/lateral (M/L): 2.5, dorsal/ventral (D/V): 3.6; (2) A/P: −4.0, M/L: 2.0, D/V: 3.6; (3) A/P: −4.5, M/L: 3.0, D/V: 3.6; and (4) A/P: −5.0, M/L: 2.5, D/V: 3.6. Rats in the histology experiment did not receive electrode implantation to keep the hippocampus as intact and lesion‐free as possible, given that astrogliosis is upregulated after electrode implantation. Injection site holes were drilled, and SSP‐SAP (IT‐11; Advanced Targeting Systems) was injected with a Nanoject III nanoinjector (Drummond Scientific). One hundred fifty nanoliters of SSP‐SAP (.04 ng/nL) was injected at a rate of 1 nL/s at the four sites targeting the hilus of dentate gyrus. The glass pipette of the nanoinjector remained at the injection site for 5 min following injection to allow for diffusion of SSP‐SAP. After SSP‐SAP delivery, the incision site was sutured, and rats were placed in a heated recovery cage for 2–3 h. Control rats for the histological experiment received artificial cerebrospinal fluid (3525, 103A; Torcis Bioscience) instead of SSP‐SAP.

The same surgical procedure was followed for rats undergoing 24‐h video‐EEG recordings except that following SSP‐SAP administration, a recording electrode was implanted in the ipsilateral hippocampus at the second SSP‐SAP injection site (MS333/3; P1 Technologies). An additional skull hole was drilled on the left side for a ground screw. Once placed, the electrode was secured using dental adhesive (Parkell, C&B Metabond), which was applied to the surface of the skull surrounding the implant. The electrode was then secured with dental acrylic, and the animal was removed from the stereotaxic apparatus and placed in a heated recovery cage for 2–3 h.

Twenty‐four‐hour video‐EEG recordings and analysis

Following recovery, rats were placed in cages for 24‐h video‐EEG monitoring (1‐kHz sampling rate, MP150; BIOPAC Systems). One group of rats (n = 5) was monitored for reactive seizures by continuous recording for 14 days following SSP‐SAP injection. The number of reactive seizures for each animal was counted, and each seizure was scored on the Racine scale. 15 A “reactive” seizure was defined as trains of mixed frequency hypersynchronous activity having an amplitude that was at least 3 times greater than baseline, lasted more than 10 s, and had a Racine score of 1 or greater.

Rats kept for long‐term EEG analysis were monitored on days 7, 35, 63, and 91 following SSP‐SAP administration, and the number of self‐generated 7‐Hz epileptiform events on those days was recorded. These epileptiform events were defined as 7‐Hz hypersynchronous activity (spikes) having an amplitude that was 3 times greater than baseline and lasting more than 2 s.

Histology

Rats were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane, transcardially perfused with .01 mol·L−1 phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) and 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), and the brains were extracted and postfixed for 24 h in 4% PFA. After postfixation, brains were cryoprotected in 30% sucrose, cryosectioned (HM550 Cryostat; Thermo Fisher Scientific), and mounted on 1% gelatin‐coated slides.

To determine the time course for SSP‐SAP internalization (n = 4), rats were perfused at 2 h and 1, 3, and 5 days following SSP‐SAP administration. Following coronal cryosectioning of the hippocampus at 40 μm, sections were placed in individual wells and washed three times for 10 min each with .01 mol·L−1 PBS, after which they were placed in a 500‐μL solution of 1:350 Saporin Goat Polyclonal, affinity‐purified Alexa 488 (AB‐15AP‐FLA; Advanced Targeting Systems), and .01 mol·L−1 PBS at room temperature overnight. The sections were washed again three times for 10 min each with .01 mol·L−1 PBS. The sections were then placed in a 250‐μL solution of 1:1000 4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (DAPI) and PBS for 30 min and washed three times more with .01 mol·L−1 PBS for 5 min. Sections were inspected and imaged at 40× with 4× digital zoom using an Olympus Fluoview FV3000 confocal microscope. We only examined neurons from the hilar regions of both hemispheres, as only inhibitory neurons have NK1 receptors that are required for SSP‐SAP internalization. 16

To determine principal cell number, rats were perfused at 2 weeks (n control = 6, n SSP‐SAP = 7), 1 month (n control = 5, n SSP‐SAP = 6), or 2 months (n control = 6, n SSP‐SAP = 3) postinfusion. Following coronal cryosectioning of the hippocampus into 12 series (consisting of 14 sections each) of 30 μm, one series was stained for NeuN, a marker for neuronal nuclei, and DAPI, a marker of cell nuclei. Thirty micrometers were chosen as the thickness for the slices, because the soma of pyramidal cells in the hippocampus of the rat have a diameter that ranges from 10 to 20 μm, making it unlikely that cells would be miscounted with two‐dimensional fluorescence imaging. All 14 sections of the series were placed together in test tubes and washed three times for 10 min each with .01 mol·L−1 PBS, after which they were placed in a 1‐mL solution of 1:500 Anti‐NeuN Antibody, clone A60, Alexa Fluor 555 Conjugate (MAB377A5; Sigma‐Aldrich) and diluent (3% bovine serum albumin [BSA] serum and .3% Triton X‐100 in .01 mol·L−1 PBS) at room temperature overnight. The sections were washed again three times for 10 min each with .01 mol·L−1 PBS. The sections were then placed in a 1‐mL solution of 1:1000 DAPI and PBS for 30 min and washed three times with .01 mol·L−1 PBS for 5 min. Sections were imaged at 20× using an Olympus VS110/S100 slide scanner.

To determine astrocyte number, the same brains described above were used. A separate series of sections was stained for the astrocyte marker glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) and with DAPI. All 14 sections of the series were collectively placed in test tubes and washed three times for 10 min each with .1% Triton X‐100 in .01 mol·L−1 PBS, after which they were placed in a 1‐mL solution of 1:500 Anti‐Glial Fibrillary Acidic Protein (GFAP)−Cy3 antibody, mouse monoclonal antibody (C9025; Sigma‐Aldrich), and diluent (100 mg BSA, 50 mg sodium azide, 40 mg sodium EDTA, and .1 mL Triton X‐100 in 100 mL of .01 mol·L−1 PBS) at room temperature overnight. The sections were washed again three times for 10 min each with .1% Triton X‐100 in .01 mol·L−1 PBS. They were then placed in a 1‐mL solution of 1:500 Goat Anti‐Mouse IgG (H + L) Cross‐Adsorbed Secondary Antibody, Alexa Fluor™ 488 (A‐11001; Thermo Fisher Scientific) with the same diluent at room temperature overnight. The sections were washed three times for 10 min each with .1% Triton X‐100 in .01 mol·L−1 PBS, after which they were placed in a 1‐mL solution of 1:1000 DAPI and PBS for 30 min and washed three times more with .01 mol·L−1 PBS for 5 min. Sections were imaged at 20× using an Olympus VS110/S100 slide scanner.

Cell counting analysis

For quantification purposes, three hippocampal slices, equidistant from anterior to posterior (A/P: −2.80, −3.60, −4.40) in sections without damage from the nanoinjector, were analyzed for number of neurons or number of astrocytes, depending on the stain. Each slice was segmented into hippocampal subregions (CA1, CA2, CA3, and dentate gyrus) manually in QuPath (version 5.2) based upon the Waxholm Space atlas of the Sprague Dawley rat brain. 17 Cell counts were made using QuPath (version 5.2), and the same quantification parameters were used for each subfield based on the type of stain (GFAP or NeuN) between hippocampal sections. Mean cell counts for both neurons and astrocytes were recorded on the contralateral and ipsilateral injection sides for both control and SSP‐SAP rats for each coronal section at the three timepoints.

To count the number of neurons, DAPI‐ and NeuN‐positive cells were identified. The parameters on QuPath that were adjusted to identify DAPI‐positive cells were pixel size, background radius, mean filter radius, sigma, maximum and minimum area, intensity threshold, and cell expansion. To determine the values of the parameters, trials were run across samples from both the control and SSP‐SAP groups to identify the numerical values that yielded the most accurate counts. Once DAPI‐positive cells were identified, a single NeuN intensity threshold value was used to identify which of the DAPI‐positive cells were also NeuN‐positive. This threshold value, like before, was determined through running trials across multiple samples in both the control and SSP‐SAP groups. To count the number of astrocytes, DAPI‐ and GFAP‐positive cells were identified. The same parameters used to identify DAPI‐positive cells in NeuN‐stained sections were used for GFAP‐stained sections too. The same method to determine the single NeuN intensity threshold was used to establish the GFAP intensity threshold. The cell counting parameters remained the same across all sections to determine cell counts.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were done with GraphPad Prism software (version 10.3.1). For the 24‐h video‐EEG analysis, a linear regression model was used to analyze the correlation between both number of self‐generated 7‐Hz epileptiform events and time, and duration of self‐generated 7‐Hz epileptiform events and time. For statistical comparison of the cell quantification data, the number of neurons and astrocytes was summed across the three A/P planes. Two‐way repeated measures analyses of variance were used to compare differences in mean number of neurons and astrocytes between contralateral and ipsilateral sides for both control and SSP‐SAP groups, followed by Fisher least significant difference post hoc test for multiple comparisons where appropriate. Additionally, a linear regression model was used to compare the differences in the cell numbers (principal cells and astrocytes) between the contralateral and ipsilateral sides of the SSP‐SAP group, compared across time (2 weeks, 1 month, and 2 months). Significance threshold was accepted at p < .05, and statistical design is outlined in each figure caption.

RESULTS

Internalization of SSP‐SAP in the hilar region

SSP‐SAP is rapidly internalized within 2 h (not shown in figure) after administration and begins to exert its cytotoxic effects within 24 h, indicated by the abnormal shape of neuronal nuclei (Figure 1). SSP‐SAP internalization was detected on day 1, was minimally detectable on day 3, and by day 5, there was no evidence of SSP‐SAP in hilar neurons of the dentate gyrus or surrounding regions. There was also no evidence of SSP‐SAP or its internalization in the hilar region on the contralateral side at any timepoint.

(B) Neuron exhibiting internalized SSP‐SAP and evidence of cytotoxicity as revealed by abnormal nuclear shape; scale bar = 5 μm. GCL, granule cell layer.

Early “reactive” seizures are observed between days 4 and 6

Early “reactive” electrographic (Figure 2A) and behavioral seizures were present 4–6 days following SSP‐SAP infusion. A power spectrum analysis of five randomly selected reactive behavioral seizures showed the most power between 2 and 14 Hz with a peak at 7 Hz (Figure 2B). Sixty‐two reactive behavioral seizures were observed across five rats between days 4 and 6 with most having a Racine score of 1 (Figure 2C). 15

(B) Power spectrum analysis of five randomly picked electrographic and behavioral reactive seizures showing a peak at 7 Hz with a range of 2–49 Hz. (C) Mean number of behavioral reactive seizures for each Racine score observed between days 4 and 6 following SSP‐SAP administration (n = 5). (G) Mean epileptiform event duration (in seconds) on days 7, 35, 63, and 91 following SSP‐SAP administration, showing an increase in the duration of such events. Linear regression analysis showed a positive and significant (r = .64, p = .01) relationship between day and duration of epileptiform events; n = 3–5.

Self‐generated 7‐Hz epileptiform events are observed between days 7 and 91, and they increase in frequency and duration over time

Video‐EEG recordings on days 7, 35, 63, and 91 displayed 7‐Hz epileptiform events and had no obvious Racine scores but were accompanied by behavioral arrest (Figure 2D). A power spectrum analysis of five randomly selected epileptiform events showed peak oscillations at 7 Hz with harmonics at 14 and 21 Hz (Figure 2E). The number and duration of self‐generated 7‐Hz epileptiform events increased significantly during the 3‐month period (Figure 2F,G).

Pyramidal and granule cells and astrocytes in dorsal hippocampus

Histological staining revealed high‐quality labeling in both ipsilateral and contralateral neurons and astrocytes at the three timepoints. There was obviously less NeuN labeling on the injected side and an obvious increase in GFAP labeling on the injected side (Figure 3).

Increased number of astrocytes in all hippocampal subfields

In the control group, the numbers of astrocytes in the ipsi‐ and contralateral groups were not significantly different across time and cell type, with one exception in area CA1. At 2 months, the ipsilateral group had significantly more GFAP‐positive astrocytes than the contralateral group (Figure 5). There were significantly more astrocytes in the SSP‐SAP‐injected ipsilateral group compared with the contralateral group across all times and cell types as well as compared with the ipsilateral control group. There were significantly more astrocytes in the contralateral SSP‐SAP group at 2 months compared with the contralateral control group at 2 months in all CA regions, but not in the dentate region.

DISCUSSION

Chun and colleagues reported a novel model of TLE based on the use of the selective neurotoxin SSP‐SAP to produce a highly localized GABAergic defect along the longitudinal axis of the dentate gyrus. 12 Notably, the GABAergic defect triggered epileptogenesis without involving any external epileptogenic injury such as convulsive SE or traumatic brain injury (TBI). Four to six days after SSP‐SAP injection, rats exhibited Racine stages 1–4: chewing, head nodding, forepaw clonus, and rearing. These reactive seizures were followed by a behavioral seizure‐free “latent period” characterized by focal electrographic activity without obvious behavioral correlates for 3 months, and then the same rats displayed self‐generated, recurrent behavioral seizures and hippocampal lesions. 12 In the current study, reactive electrographic and behavioral seizures were observed from days 4–6 with self‐generated 7‐Hz epileptiform events during the “latent period.” This is the first report with the SSP‐SAP technique of the progressive increase in the number and duration of self‐generated 7‐Hz epileptiform events during the “latent period” with progressive pyramidal and granule cell loss and astrogliosis, indicative of HS during the first months following SSP‐SAP infusion.

In this study, we observed rapid internalization of SSP‐SAP into hilar interneurons by 2 h postinjection. Intracellular SSP‐SAP was detectable at 3 days but not at 5 days postinjection. These findings provide evidence that the direct action of SSP‐SAP preceded the onset of reactive behavioral seizures, supporting the interpretation that these seizures are a result of GABA neuron loss and not an acute pharmacological effect of SSP‐SAP itself. We confirmed by EEG recording that the reactive seizure behaviors observed on days 4–6 were driven by hypersynchronous neuronal activity. Following the period of reactive seizures, and during the so called “latent period,” we observed self‐generated 7‐Hz epileptiform events that were distinguishable from reactive seizures both behaviourally and electrographically. Clinically, the latent period has been defined as the interval between the epileptogenic insult and the first self‐generated and clinically obvious behavioral seizures. 18 , 19 Multiple preclinical studies in rodents using SE and posttraumatic epilepsy models have shown the presence of epileptiform activity, including self‐generated electrographic seizures, that eventually develop into unprovoked clinically obvious behavioral seizures during the “latent period.” 20 , 21 , 22 In this study the “latent period” is not seizure‐free, but rather a period of progressive increase in number and duration of electrographic events, consistent with the process of “endogenous kindling.” 3 , 23 These self‐generated 7‐Hz epileptiform events we observed are similar to the electrographic discharges observed in genetic models of absence epilepsy such as GAERS and WAG/Rij rats. 24 , 25

Pyramidal and hilar cell loss is a core pathological characteristic of HS in TLE and is widely thought to be the result of excitotoxicity. 26 , 27 Here, we observed bilateral pyramidal cell loss as early as 2 weeks following unilateral SSP‐SAP administration across all hippocampal subfields. Given that SSP‐SAP selectively ablates GABAergic interneurons, the pyramidal cell loss in this model is likely the result of excitotoxicity secondary to GABAergic disinhibition. The contralateral pyramidal cell loss is presumably a result of excitotoxic discharges propagating to the contralateral side via the hippocampal commissural pathway. 28 , 29 However, because the SSP‐SAP is infused unilaterally, the contralateral inhibitory interneurons are still present, likely reducing the degree of contralateral pyramidal cell loss. We also observed that pyramidal cell loss worsened over time in areas CA1 and CA3 but not in area CA2. Pyramidal neurons of the CA2 are known to be uniquely resistant to injury not just in TLE but with stroke and TBI as well. 30 This is likely due to multiple factors, including stronger inhibitory interneuron input compared to CA1 and CA3 pyramidal neurons and unique calcium buffering properties, making CA2 pyramidal cells more resistant to excitotoxicity. 31 , 32 Models of epilepsy using kainate or pilocarpine have also shown pyramidal cell loss, but most hippocampal pyramidal neurons are quickly lost hours to days following SE induction, and the cell loss does not progress significantly as self‐generated seizures continue. 33 , 34

Granule cell loss is a characteristic of type 1 HS 13 and is also observed in the SSP‐SAP model. However, in our study, it does not progress over the 2‐month observation period. This is likely because of the low intrinsic excitability of mature granule cells, which is known to be mediated by multiple factors, including a hyperpolarized membrane potential, relatively low input resistance, and preferential expression of KV4.1 channels. 35 , 36 , 37 Importantly, there is greater granule cell loss in the planes located between the SSP‐SAP injection sites compared to the plane that was anterior to the injection sites, suggesting that granule cell loss is more spatially confined compared to pyramidal cell loss. In contrast, whereas the kainate model shows extensive hilar and pyramidal cell loss, granule cells are relatively resistant, regardless of systemic or intrahippocampal administration. 34 , 37

Increased numbers of astrocytes are another anatomical characteristic of HS that is found in all subtypes. 14 The number of astrocytes can increase from either migration of astrocytes into the region, or local astrocyte proliferation as a part of reactive astrogliosis. 38 Astrocyte migration in the adult hippocampus is generally minimal, as mature astrocytes establish stable, nonoverlapping domains and show little long‐range movement except following severe tissue disruption such as penetrating injury or stroke. 39 In contrast, neuronal loss is known to trigger reactive astrogliosis and increases astrocyte number through proliferation of local astrocytes, rather than migration. We observed an increase in the number of astrocytes on the ipsilateral side in the SSP‐SAP group as early as 2 weeks across all hippocampal subfields, with astrocyte numbers continuing to increase over time. By comparing the contralateral sides between the control and SSP‐SAP groups, we also found a greater number of astrocytes on the contralateral side in the SSP‐SAP group by 2 months in all CA regions. Given that there was principal cell loss on the contralateral side in the SSP‐SAP group as early as 2 weeks, this provides evidence that the increase in the number of astrocytes likely follows principal cell loss. This aligns with the clinical literature that shows astrogliosis is a consequence of principal cell loss in TLE. 40 Other preclinical animal models of TLE using kainic acid or pilocarpine have shown astrogliosis as well, although evidence of the temporal progression of astrogliosis remains limited. 41 One study by Sharma et al. 42 examined the time course of principal cell loss and astrogliosis in the kainate model of TLE and found similar results to the present study, with neuron loss preceding astrogliosis, but both processes continued progressively throughout the observation period.

GABAergic dysfunction has long been proposed as a likely driver of epileptogenesis in human TLE. 3 , 4 , 43 However, early attempts to eliminate GABA neurons selectively failed to show that GABAergic dysfunction alone could cause TLE with HS. 44 , 45 , 46 Additionally, although other models of epilepsy using chemoconvulsants have shown the clinical phenotypes of TLE, the seizures elicited in these models are not the result of primary GABAergic dysfunction but instead are the presumed result of excitotoxicity. Recently a study by Dusing et al. 11 used a chemogenetic approach to determine the impact of interneuron loss on epileptogenesis. They used diphtheria toxin to bilaterally delete Vgat‐expressing interneurons from dorsal and ventral hippocampus as well as in DREADDs (designer receptors exclusively activated by designer drug) to silence the same interneuron population in a separate group of mice. Interneuron ablation produced dramatic seizure clusters and persistent epileptiform activity, but 1 week later seizure activity declined precipitously, and persistent epileptiform activity was no longer observed, with only occasional seizures observed to the end of the 4‐week experiment. Transient silencing on the other hand produced large numbers of interictal spikes, a significant but modest increase in seizure occurrence, and changes in EEG frequency band power. 11 Their results, using methods that produced incomplete interneuron loss, like those of earlier studies, indicate that the hippocampus regains relative homeostasis in the face of GABAergic targeting. 11 In contrast, we confirm that unilateral disruption of hippocampal GABAergic inhibition at several sites along the longitudinal axis alone, in the absence of convulsive SE or TBI, is sufficient to trigger the process that results in TLE with HS.

CONCLUSIONS

Taken together with previous studies using SSP‐SAP to selectively eliminate hippocampal inhibitory neurons, 3 , 12 the present results reinforce the conclusion that only an extensive loss of inhibitory neurons produces an initial episode of nonconvulsive status epilepticus, followed by hippocampal sclerosis and a chronic epileptic state. Numerous studies designed to ablate or silence inhibitory neurons, but affecting only a limited number of inhibitory neurons, or that avoided the dentate gyrus as part of the experimental design, have consistently failed to produce HS or chronic epilepsy. 11 , 44 , 45 , 46 , 47 In this regard, SSP‐SAP, when delivered at multiple sites along the longitudinal axis, is uniquely effective in ablating inhibitory neurons and, as a result, reproducing the defining features of TLE with HS.
