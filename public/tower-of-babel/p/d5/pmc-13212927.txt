The effect of breast milk and vanilla odor on duration of transition from gavage to oral feeding in premature infants
Authors: Neda Jamalimoghadam, Mitra Soltanian, Maryam Paran, Parvin Ghaemmaghami, Farzaneh Tavakolian, Zahra Hashemi, Amirmohammad Jaladat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPremature infants are at risk of various physical complications if they do not receive oral feeding and weight gain, which can affect their health and well-being. This study aimed to compare the effect of the smell of breast milk and vanilla extract on the duration of transition from gavage feeding to oral feeding in premature infants in the neonatal intensive care units of hospitals affiliated with Shiraz University of Medical Sciences. This clinical trial was conducted on 60 premature infants who were randomly assigned to three groups: breast milk smell stimulation during gavage feeding, vanilla extract smell stimulation during gavage feeding, and control group without smell stimulation during gavage feeding. Data were collected using a demographic information form, and the variables of duration of transition from gavage feeding to oral feeding were examined. The data were analyzed using SPSS software version 24 and analyzed using analysis of variance and post hoc Tukey test. The results of this study showed a significant difference in the mean duration of transition from gavage feeding to oral feeding between the groups (P < 0.001). However, there was no significant difference in the mean duration of transition from gavage feeding to oral feeding between the vanilla group and the control group (P = 0.114). Aromatherapy with breast milk reduces the time required for the transition from gavage to oral feeding and also shortens the length of hospital stay. Therefore, this simple and low-cost method can be used to accelerate infants’ achievement of nutritional competence.Trial registration https//irct.behdasht.gov.ir/trial/69,014.Keywords: Breast milk, Vanilla, Aromatherapy, Feeding, Infant prematureSubject terms: Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Premature infants are defined as infants born before 37 weeks of gestation and require long-term hospitalization in neonatal intensive care units. Hospitalization of infants is associated with several complications that can cause stress and disruption in the expected mutual interaction skills in parents and their infants1,2. Approximately 15 million premature infants are born worldwide annually3. It is estimated that 1 out of every 10 infants is born prematurely worldwide4. The survival of premature infants in long-term hospitalization is associated with very high costs, accounting for 50% of the total costs of hospitalized infants2. Premature infants born before 37 weeks of gestation are at risk of aspiration due to the lack of coordination between sucking, swallowing, and breathing; therefore, their nutrition should initially be provided through gavage feeding5. One of the therapeutic goals in neonatal intensive care units is to enhance feeding skills in premature infants2. The transition from tube to oral feeding represents one of the most critical developmental milestones in the care of premature infants. Achieving efficient and safe oral feeding not only promotes physiological maturation but also directly influences the length of hospitalization and readiness for discharge6.

Olfactory stimulation has gained increasing attention as a non-invasive developmental care strategy to shorten the time required for weaning from tube feeding and to enhance feeding coordination. The odor of human breast milk, rich in familiar biological compounds, is believed to promote feeding readiness and improve physiological stability during oral feeding attempts. A 2023 meta-analysis of six randomized controlled trials reported that olfactory stimulation with breast milk odor in premature infants significantly reduced the time to achieve full oral feeding and the duration of parenteral nutrition, although it did not significantly affect the length of hospital stay7. Earlier clinical studies have also reported that simultaneous exposure to maternal milk odor and non-nutritive sucking can accelerate the transition to independent oral feeding in preterm infants, particularly when paired with maternal contact and routine caregiving8.

In this regard, the odor of breast milk plays a crucial role. As a biologically familiar sensory cue, it helps activate the sucking reflex and reinforces this innate behavior by stimulating facial nerves, ultimately supporting the development of oral feeding skills in infants. Evidence indicates that olfactory stimulation with breast milk odor can reduce the duration of feeding transition and the length of hospitalization in premature infants9–13. On the other hand, evidence suggests that the use of vanilla extract not only plays an effective role in facilitating the transition from gavage to oral feeding, but also reduces the duration of hospitalization14. More recently, attention has turned to the use of vanilla odor, a soft and recognizable scent shown to influence both emotional regulation and feeding behaviors. Its familiarity and gentle sensory profile may help calm preterm infants while stimulating olfactory pathways associated with feeding readiness. A randomized controlled trial involving 207 preterm infants reported that exposure to vanilla odor improved the cumulative volume of independently consumed milk during early hospitalization and tended to shorten the length of stay in infants born before 32 weeks of gestation15.

Complementary findings indicate that both vanilla and breast milk odors exert calming effects, reducing apnea frequency and supporting physiological self-regulation in preterm neonates16. Likewise, multimodal stimulation approaches combining smell and taste exposure during tube feeding have demonstrated enhanced growth trajectories and improved feeding outcomes6.

Together, these data highlight olfactory stimulation—particularly with biologically meaningful scents such as breast milk and vanilla extract—as a promising adjunctive strategy in neonatal care. Comparative evaluation of these odors may reveal subtle yet clinically relevant differences in how each contributes to feeding transition, behavioral regulation, and discharge readiness among premature infants. This growing body of evidence underscores the need for continued controlled research exploring the differential efficacy and safety of these natural olfactory cues in supporting the neurobehavioral development of preterm neonates.

However, no study has been conducted in Iran on the effect of vanilla smell, and these two smells have not been compared. Therefore, there is a need for further research in this area, and researchers are encouraged to compare the effect of the smell of breast milk and vanilla extract on the duration of transition from gavage to oral feeding in premature infants.

Materials and methods

This study was a three-group randomized clinical trial conducted in the neonatal intensive care units of affiliated hospitals of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences from early April to mid-July 2023. The study population included all premature infants who met the inclusion criteria: gestational age of 27–33 weeks with feeding difficulties, birth weight of at least 1 kg, Apgar score greater than 6 at the first and fifth minutes, absence of significant congenital abnormalities, no underlying or acquired diseases other than prematurity, and receiving breast milk as nutrition. Exclusion criteria were occurrence of severe complications during the study, intolerance to the intervention, transfer to another hospital before completion of the study, or parental withdrawal of consent.

Research hypotheses

Null hypothesis (H0): There is no significant difference in the duration of transition from gavage feeding to oral feeding among the breast milk smell stimulation group, the vanilla extract smell stimulation group, and the control group.

Null hypothesis (H0): There is no significant difference in the duration of transition from gavage feeding to oral feeding among the breast milk smell stimulation group, the vanilla extract smell stimulation group, and the control group.

Research hypothesis (H1): There is a significant difference in the duration of transition from gavage feeding to oral feeding among the three groups.

Research hypothesis (H1): There is a significant difference in the duration of transition from gavage feeding to oral feeding among the three groups.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated using G*Power software with a power of 90%, α (type I error) of 0.05, and based on information from a previous similar article (49). The total sample size was determined to be 51 infants, considering a 20% dropout rate, resulting in a final sample size of 60 infants. Therefore, 20 infants were recruited in each group.

Randomization

Individuals were assigned to three groups using a permutation block method, as follows: the intervention group with vanilla scent was designated as group A, the breast milk scent group was designated as group B, and the control group was designated as group C. Different permutations of the ABC sequence were written and assigned codes as follows: ABC, ACB, BCA, BAC, CAB, CBA. In each allocation, a number between 1 and 6 was randomly selected. By selecting each number, the pattern of letter placement determined the order of individuals’ allocation to the groups. Similarly, a number between 1 and 6 was randomly selected for each group to determine the final sample size.

Interventions

After obtaining permission to implement the project from the vice president of research, the ethics committee of the university and the national ethics committee (ethics code: IR.SUMS.NUMIMG.REC.1401.112 (http://www.irct.ir/)) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, obtaining the clinical trial code number IRCT20160228026817N4, obtaining an introduction letter from the vice president of research of the University of Medical Sciences and the faculty Midwifery Nursing, Shiraz, ensured that informed consent was obtained and giving the right to choose to participate or not to participate in the study was given to each of the participants in order to withdraw from the study at each stage of the research. The procedure was performed in all three groups, with breast milk gavage administered four times a day at 9 am, 12 pm, 3 pm, and 6 pm by the researcher under the supervision of the assigned nurse.

Breast milk group

Infants in the breast milk group received the intervention simultaneously with the start of gavage feeding on four occasions throughout the day (9 a.m., 12 noon, 3 p.m., and 6 p.m.). This intervention aimed to examine the calming effect of maternal odor on the duration of the transition from gavage to oral feeding. A small amount (1 mL) of expressed breast milk from the infant’s mother was placed on a sterile gauze pad and positioned approximately 2 cm from the infant’s nostrils for 5 min before gavage feeding. Care was taken to ensure the pad did not come into direct contact with the infant’s skin to maintain proper hygiene.

Vanilla odor group

Infants in the vanilla odor group were exposed to the intervention simultaneously with the start of gavage feeding on four scheduled occasions (9 a.m., 12 noon, 3 p.m., and 6 p.m.), to assess the soothing effect of a familiar scent in comparison with maternal odor. A cotton ball soaked in a 6 mg/100 mL vanilla solution was placed approximately 2 cm from the infant’s nostrils for 5 min prior to routine procedures. The intervention was applied at a consistent distance and duration for all infants to ensure uniformity across the group.

Data collection tool

The data collection tool was a checklist obtained from a similar study by Yildiz et al. in 201121. Based on this checklist, demographic information including infant’s gender, birth weight, weight day 1 of the study, gestational age at birth, age day 1 of the study, and infant’s position in terms of parity and maternal delivery, Apgar score at the first and fifth minutes, and type of delivery were extracted from the medical records. The main variable of the study was the duration of transition from gavage to oral feeding. The study flowchart is illustrated in Fig. 1. As shown in Fig. 1, randomization and attrition data were organized according to CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) guidelines. None of the samples were excluded from the study.

Ethical considerations and consent

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences (Ethics code: IR.SUMS.NUMIMG.REC.1401.112). The trial was registered in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT20160228026817N4) on 29/04/2023, and the first patient was enrolled on 29/05/2023. Before enrollment, the parents or legal guardians of all infants were provided with detailed information about the study procedures, potential benefits, and risks, and were informed of their right to withdraw at any stage. Both verbal and written informed consent were obtained from all participants’ parents or legal guardians prior to the initiation of the interventions.

Statistical analysis

Using appropriate statistical tests, the collected data was categorized and analyzed. Data description was done through the use of appropriate descriptive statistics including tables, central indices, and dispersion indices. Finally, the hypotheses of interest were tested. In examining the relationship between the target variable (quantitative variable) and demographic characteristics for comparing means between two-level variable levels, independent two-sample t-test was used, and for comparing means between multi-level variables, analysis of variance along with pairwise comparisons by post hoc test was used. The analyses were performed using software (version 24 SPSS).

Results

Initially, 85 premature infants were candidates to participate in the study, among whom 19 parents did not consent to their infant’s participation in the study and were excluded at the beginning. Then, 66 infants were examined for entry criteria into the study, of which 6 cases, for various reasons including infant’s deterioration, cardiovascular disorders, respiratory disorders, and mother’s withdrawal from the study, did not enter the study. Finally, the remaining 60 infants were randomly assigned to three olfactory stimulation groups: breast milk odor stimulation group, vanilla extract odor stimulation group, and control group without receiving olfactory stimulation. After 10 weeks of intervention, the duration of transition from gavage to oral feeding in infants in all three groups was examined and compared.

The average gestational age at birth of newborns in the breast milk group (31.60 ± 1.35), vanilla group (31.10 ± 1.65), and control group (31.25 ± 1.37) weeks, and birth weight in the breast milk group (1615.50 ± 434.88), vanilla group (1742.25 ± 458.79), and control group (1895.35 ± 475.19) grams were presented. Most of the newborns were boys. The demographic characteristics of the participating infants in the study are presented in Table 1. The overall analysis using ANOVA indicated a significant difference in the transition time among the three groups (p < 0.001). Pairwise comparisons using the Bonferroni test showed that the duration of transition in the breast milk stimulation group was significantly shorter than in the control group (p = 0.006) and the vanilla group (p < 0.001), suggesting a stronger effect of breast milk aroma on improving infants’ nutritional transition. In contrast, no significant difference was observed between the vanilla and control groups (p = 0.114).

Discussion

The results of this study confirm that therapeutic aroma using mother’s milk is a superior intervention for reducing the average duration of transition from tube feeding to oral feeding compared with both vanilla aroma and standard care. Infants in the mother’s milk group initiated oral feeding significantly earlier than those in the vanilla and control groups. These findings underscore the crucial role of biologically meaningful olfactory cues in promoting feeding maturation, distinguishing them from generalized soothing scents.

Our findings are strongly supported by the literature advocating for the use of the infant’s own milk odor. Zarrifian et al. (2018) and Ahmed (2019) both previously documented shorter transition times using milk odor versus control, aligning with our observation of milk’s dominant positive effect. Furthermore, meta-analyses confirm the general efficacy of olfactory input17,18. A recent review by Li et al. (2024) found that olfactory and gustatory stimulation significantly shortened the time to reach full oral feeds, reinforcing the validity of this sensory approach19. Similarly, another meta-analysis found that olfactory and gustatory stimulation generally reduced the time to full enteral feeds compared to usual care20.

Davidson et al. (2019) also observed benefits for infants under 31 weeks gestation receiving maternal breast milk odor. These studies collectively suggest that the specific, complex matrix of volatile organic compounds present in maternal milk provides a potent, hardwired stimulus for feeding consolidation that synthetic or generalized odors cannot replicate21.

A necessary point of discussion involves the comparison with vanilla. While Schriever et al. (2018) reported a benefit for vanilla over rose and control in their cohort, our study indicates that vanilla provided no statistically significant advantage over our control group and performed markedly worse than the milk odor. This heterogeneity is echoed in the literature22. Anne et al. (2024) found that while vanilla showed some secondary benefits in certain subgroups (like cumulative food intake), it did not significantly impact the primary outcome of gastric tube weaning across all infants. This inconsistency highlights the heterogeneity in study populations and protocols, suggesting that the developmental age of the infant may moderate the response to different scents23.

In broader context, interventions that promote oral feeding, like olfactory stimulation, are paramount for improving neurocognitive outcomes and preventing complications associated with prolonged tube feeding, such as necrotizing enterocolitis24. The trend toward cue-based transition strategies, which incorporates sensory input, is supported by clinical guides25. While systematic reviews caution about the low certainty of evidence for some secondary outcomes like length of hospitalization20, the consistent positive signal for accelerated oral feeding remains the most robust finding across the literature, and one that our study powerfully confirms for the mother’s milk scent. Given these findings, the clinical recommendation must prioritize the most effective, biologically relevant stimulus.

Conclusion

The results of the present study showed that therapeutic aroma with the smell of mother’s milk and vanilla was effective in reducing the average duration of transition from tube feeding to oral feeding, and the mother’s milk group had better performance in reducing the average duration of transition from tube feeding to oral feeding compared to the vanilla aroma group. Therefore, it is recommended that staff in the special care unit make use of such interventions based on the conditions and needs of infants, and the findings of this study can serve as a basis for future studies.

Conclusion

The present study successfully investigated the comparative efficacy of two distinct olfactory interventions (maternal breast milk odor and vanilla aroma) on the critical transition from tube feeding to full oral feeding in preterm infants admitted to the special care unit. Our primary finding indicates that therapeutic aroma with the smell of mother’s milk was significantly more effective in reducing the average duration of this transition compared to the vanilla aroma group. This outcome underscores the inherent, biologically potent nature of familiar maternal olfactory cues in promoting essential feeding reflexes and fostering physiological stability in the vulnerable preterm neonate. The superior performance of the mother’s milk group confirms that, while non-specific pleasant odors like vanilla can offer some benefit (as suggested by prior literature), the complex chemical signature of human milk provides a richer, more powerful stimulus for oral feeding acquisition. This finding strongly suggests that early and consistent exposure to breast milk odor, even when direct oral feeding is not yet possible, remains a cornerstone of evidence-based developmental care in the NICU.

Recommendations for practice

Based on these results, it is strongly recommended that staff in the special care unit prioritize the implementation of maternal breast milk odor exposure as a routine intervention for tube-fed infants, adjusting the frequency and duration according to the individual infant’s condition and tolerance. Vanilla aroma should be considered a viable, accessible alternative for infants whose mothers cannot provide milk samples or when specialized scent administration is preferred.

Future research

The findings of this study serve as a solid basis for future research. Further studies should aim to: 1- investigate the impact of these odors on long-term feeding success and growth trajectories; 2- explore the optimal concentration and duration of exposure for each scent; and 3- examine the effect of combining olfactory stimuli with other developmental care practices, such as skin-to-skin contact or swaddling.

Ultimately, this research reaffirms the profound impact of sensory ecology on neonatal development, offering concrete, low-risk, and high-impact strategies to optimize feeding outcomes for our most fragile patients.

Limitations

Among the limiting factors were the presence of doctors for infant visits during the intervention, infant’s poor condition, or transfer of the infant to other centers.
