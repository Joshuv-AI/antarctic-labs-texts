A thermosensitive hydrogel delivery system incorporating wogonin-treated mesenchymal stromal cell (MSC)-derived exosomes alleviates osteoarthritis
Authors: Kewei Zhu, Minzhi Mao, Wenzhao Li, Cheng Tao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOsteoarthritis (OA) is a prevalent degenerative joint disease affecting major joints, causing pain and functional impairment due to cartilage degeneration and osteophyte formation. Current treatments have significant limitations. Mesenchymal stromal cell (MSC)-based therapy is advantageous for its regenerative and immunomodulatory properties, with the infrapatellar fat pad (IPFP) being a reliable MSC source involved in knee osteoarthritis (KOA) progression. MSC-derived exosomes (MSCs-Exo) are key for therapeutic efficacy through paracrine actions but face rapid clearance by the reticuloendothelial system. Sustained release drug delivery systems based on biodegradable biomaterials could enhance MSCs-Exo retention and control their release, promoting tissue repair. Previous studies indicate that wogonin, a natural flavonoid from Scutellaria baicalensis, can stimulate MSCs and be associated with enhanced therapeutic efficacy of MSCs-Exo. This study develops a thermosensitive hydrogel delivery system for intra-articular injection, incorporating IFP-MSCs-Exo (Gel@MSCIPFP-Exo) and wogonin (Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo). Thermosensitive gelation aligned with pathological joint hyperthermia enabled intelligent, sustained exosome delivery. Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo significantly enhanced chondrocyte proliferation and migration while inhibiting inflammatory apoptosis. In vivo, the hydrogel prolonged localized retention to 28 days, doubling the <14-day clearance of free exosomes, resulting in statistically superior cartilage repair and lower OARSI scores. Furthermore, the system profoundly modulated the synovial microenvironment by significantly downregulating iNOS/IL-1β and upregulating Arg-1. These findings highlight the potential of wogonin-stimulated MSC-derived exosomes within a thermosensitive hydrogel to improve OA treatment outcomes; while this study validates the improved therapeutic outcomes of this composite system, the mechanisms underlying cartilage degeneration and inflammation are primarily inferred from prior mechanistic studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13036-026-00709-x.Keywords: Osteoarthritis (OA), Mesenchymal stromal cell (MSC), Exosomes, Infrapatellar fat pad (IPFP), Hydrogel, Sustained release, Wogonin

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) stands as the most prevalent degenerative joint disorder, predominantly targeting vital joints like the knees, hands, spine, and hips [1]. The characteristic features of OA include cartilage deterioration and osteophyte proliferation, which lead to discomfort and impaired functionality [2]. Despite extensive research, the exact pathogenesis of OA remains poorly understood. With its avascularity, the inherent ability of articular cartilage to self-repair is notably limited. [3, 4]. Consequently, the damaged cartilage and the development of OA require clinical intervention. Nevertheless, most treatment modalities (microfracture, joint replacement, cellular treatment, pharmacological therapies, etc.) have their limitations. For example, joint replacement surgeries are invasive and carry risks of complications and long recovery periods [5]. Microfracture techniques often result in fibrocartilage formation, which is biomechanically inferior to native cartilage [6]. Cellular therapies face challenges such as limited cell retention and potential immune responses, while pharmacological treatments primarily provide symptomatic relief without addressing the underlying disease progression [7, 8]. Therefore, delivering the drugs directly to the affected joint tissues might be a promising solution to overcome these challenges by improving drug retention, reducing systemic side effects, and promoting cartilage regeneration.

Among various innovative strategies, mesenchymal stromal cell (MSC)-based therapy is advantageous against OA due to its regenerative and immunomodulatory properties [9, 10]. The infrapatellar fat pad (IPFP), a local adipose tissue located beneath the patella, is key in knee osteoarthritis (KOA) progression via interacting with periarticular tissues (synovium, subchondral bone, cartilage, etc.) [11]. Additionally, studies have identified the IPFP as one of the most reliable and accessible sources of MSCs, with a high proliferation rate in vitro [12, 13]. However, MSC-based therapies face limitations such as potential immune rejection, tumorigenic risks, and ethical concerns. MSCs’ paracrine effects are primarily responsible for their therapeutic efficacy, where they secrete exosomes (MSCs-Exo) that transfer proteins, cytokines, and nucleic acids to recipient cells, facilitating crucial cell-to-cell communication in various biological and pathological processes [12, 13]. Despite their potential, the reticuloendothelial system can absorb and then clean the purified exosomes away from the body [14]. To address this, biodegradable biomaterials not only enhance MSCs-Exo’s retention time but also control their release for tissue repair [15]. Additionally, exosomes with biodegradable hydrogels could target specific or nearby tissue sites, facilitating the localized delivery of therapeutic molecules at high concentrations [16]. Therefore, the design and functionality of the biomaterials used are crucial in exosomes’ therapeutic efficacy. This approach aims to maximize MSCs-Exo’s therapeutic efficacy via ensuring sustained-release delivery to the affected tissues, thereby enhancing the overall treatment efficacy for OA.

As a natural flavonoid in the root of traditional Chinese medicine Scutellaria baicalensis, Wogonin (5,7-dihydroxy-8-methoxyflavanone) has multiple biological functions, such as bioactivities against inflammation and cancer [17, 18]. Notably, wogonin shows no remarkable toxic effects on normal tissues [19]. Wogonin has been shown to protect against oxidative stress and inflammatory response, as well as matrix degradation by downregulating key proteases [matrix metalloproteinase (MMP)-3, MMP-9, MMP-13, etc.], while also upregulating chondrogenic biomarkers (Collagen II, Aggrecan, etc.) [20]. Our previous research found that wogonin pretreatment significantly enhances the ability of MSCIPFP-Exo to promote cartilage defect repair via upregulating SOX9 and downregulating MMP13 and ADAMTS5 [21]. However, the application of exosomes has still encountered several challenges, including low retention and instability [22]. To overcome these challenges, hydrogels have emerged as reliable exosome carriers due to their excellent biocompatibility and tunable properties [23]. Inflammation greatly contributes to OA pathophysiology, with the involvement of synovial membrane and inflammation-related cytokines; the release of these cytokines drives extracellular matrix-degrading enzyme production by chondrocytes, hindering tissue repair and regeneration [24]. This inflammation-associated process results in increased blood flow, which manifests clinically as redness, heat, joint swelling, and pain [25]. The elevated temperature observed in arthritic joints, even without external heat sources, suggests the potential of thermo-responsive nanomaterials in treating arthritis. Infrared thermography has highlighted that joint temperature is a key physical indicator of the inflammatory process, suggesting that measuring this component could facilitate more targeted OA treatments [26–28].

While our previous work established the enhanced chondroprotective efficacy of wogonin-pretreated exosomes (Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo) in vitro [21], their rapid clearance in vivo following direct intra-articular injection remains a critical barrier to clinical translation. Given these challenges, our current study aims to develop a thermosensitive hydrogel delivery system for the sustained intra-articular delivery of Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo. This novel approach is designed to extend the retention time of therapeutic agents within the knee joint, leveraging the thermo-responsive properties of the hydrogel to achieve sustained-release to inflamed joint areas more effectively and overcome the limitations of direct exosome injection.

Materials and methods

MSC isolation from IPFP and identification

IPFP tissues were collected from trauma patients undergoing amputation surgery. The informed consent form was signed by each patient involved. All procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Xiangya Hospital, Central South University. IPFP tissues were thoroughly rinsed using phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and then finely minced using surgical scissors. The minced tissues underwent detachment (2 hours, 37°C) with 0.1% Type I collagenase with gentle agitation. Those tissue fragments that failed to digest were removed after filtering the obtained cell suspension through a cell filter (40 μm). Subsequently, the filtered cells were subjected to centrifugation (700 g, 10 minutes). The cell pellet underwent resuspension in MSC growth medium and was seeded in a culture plate and kept in a humidified environment (37 °C, 5% CO2). The non-adherent cells were discarded after 24 h. The medium is refreshed every other day. To mitigate the confounding variable of replicative senescence, which can negatively impact exosomal cargo and therapeutic efficacy, MSCs between passages 3 and 5 (P3–P5) were utilized for all subsequent experiments and exosome isolations. Furthermore, cellular senescence was directly assessed using a senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) staining kit (C0602, Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, comparing P3–P5 MSCs against higher-passage (P10) MSCs (Fig. S1A–B).

To confirm the MSC phenotype, flow cytometry (FCM) was performed. Cells were labeled with antibodies against surface markers CD29, CD44, and CD90 (positive markers, >70% positive expression) and CD45, CD31, and CD117 (negative markers, <5% positive expression). Additionally, the differentiation potential of MSCs was assessed by culturing them under conditions promoting osteogenic, adipogenic, and chondrogenic differentiation to verify their multipotency (Fig. S1C-D). Consistent with established characterization, the isolated MSCs represent a heterogeneous population containing multipotent stem cells, progenitors, and differentiated cells.

Chondrocyte isolation and identification

Isolating primary chondrocytes from human cartilage specimens (obtained from trauma patients undergoing amputation surgery) was achieved by thoroughly washing the samples with PBS and finely chopping them into small pieces. These tissue fragments were then digested overnight at 37 °C using high-glucose DMEM containing 0.2% type II collagenase and 1% P/S, followed by filtering the cell suspension with a cell strainer (40 μm) for undigested material removal. Cells were collected and then centrifuged (400 g, 5 minutes). Next, cells were resuspended in high-glucose DMEM containing 10% FBS and 1% P/S and cultured with medium changed every other day. The cells were used at passages 2–5.

For identification, chondrocyte morphology was observed using a microscope with a bright field. Cartilage-specific proteoglycans were identified using toluidine blue staining, and immunofluorescence (IF) staining for Collagen II was conducted to verify the expression of this key chondrocyte marker (Fig. S1E–G).

Exosome isolation and characterization

MSCs from IPFP (MSCsIPFP) were inoculated in 10 cm dishes, and the cell culture media were replaced with Exo-specific serum-free media (41210ES50, Yeasen, Shanghai, China). The cell supernatant of MSCIPFP was collected upon 24-h incubation with the addition of wogonin (25 μM, MCE, China) after continuing the culture for 2 days, when the cell adherence growth reached about 70%. The cell suspension was subjected to a 10-min centrifugation at 300 × g (4°C) to pellet and remove whole intact cells. The supernatant was harvested, transferred to a sterilized centrifuge tube, and subjected to a 10-min centrifugation at 2000 × g (4°C) to eliminate dead cells and apoptotic bodies. Then, the resulting supernatants were subjected to a 30-min centrifugation at 10,000 × g (4°C) to discard cell debris and larger extracellular vesicles (such as microvesicles). Finally, the clarified supernatants were subjected to a 90-min ultracentrifugation at 100,000 × g (4°C) to precipitate the exosomes. The supernatant was discarded, and the precipitates (isolated Exos) were re-suspended in cooled PBS and subjected to 90-min ultracentrifugation (100000 g, 4℃) again. Finally, precipitates (isolated Exos) were resuspended in cooled PBS and kept at −80 °C for further experiments. Exos (2 μg) on the basis of protein contents determined using Pierce™ BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) were used for incubation with 2 × 105 chondrocytes for 24 h.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

TEM was used to visualize the isolated MSCIPFP-Exo and Wogonin- MSCIPFP-Exo morphology. A 15 µL drop of the fixed exosome suspension was placed on a copper grid coated with a formvar-carbon film, allowing the exosomes to adhere for 20 minutes. The filter paper was utilized to blot excess liquid. The grid floated on a drop of PBS for 2 minutes for washing. The exosomes were then contrasted with a 2% uranyl acetate solution for 1 minute, after which the grid was blotted to remove excess stain and air-dried. The stained grids were examined using a TEM (80 kV accelerating voltage). A digital camera system attached to the microscope was employed for image capturing.

Immunoblotting

Following three washes with PBS, the cell and Exo proteins were lysed using an ice-cold lysis buffer (Beyotime). After lysate centrifugation (12,000 rpm, 10 minutes, 4°C), cell debris was removed. A BCA kit was utilized for protein concentration measurement. Protein samples (20–50 μg) were subjected to SDS-PAGE on 10% gels for separation, followed by transfer to PVDF membranes. After membrane blocking (30 minutes, room temperature) with 5% blocking buffer (Beyotime), primary antibodies targeting markers for exosomal surface (CD81, CD63, and CD9) and apoptosis (cleaved-Caspase 3, Bcl-2, and Bax), as well as anabolic/catabolic markers (Aggrecan, Collagen II, ADAMTS5, and MMP13), as well as GAPDH to serve as a loading control, were added for overnight incubation (4°C). Next, after 3 TBST washes, the membranes underwent incubation (2 hours, room temperature) with secondary antibodies (1:15,000), followed by three times TBST washes. The automatic chemiluminescence imaging system (Tanon-5200, Shanghai, China) was employed for protein band visualization and quantification, and the relative protein levels were assessed. The antibodies’ information was listed in Table S1.

Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA)

MSCIPFP-Exo and Wogonin- MSCIPFP-Exo (isolated from MSCs or released from hydrogel) size distribution and concentration were assessed using an NTA system (NanoSight NS300, Malvern). Exosome specimens were diluted in PBS at ratios of 1:10 to 1:100, with a sample initial concentration-dependent manner. The following software settings were applied for analysis: detection threshold set to 5, room temperature maintained throughout, with a total of 30 frames captured per measurement over a duration of 30 seconds. Each measurement consisted of five individual runs to validate the results’ accuracy and reliability, with the mean values reported for both size distribution and particle concentration.

Hydrogel preparation

To prepare the thermosensitive hydrogel, 2.0 g of methylcellulose (MC) was dissolved in deionized (DI) water at 75 °C with continuous stirring at 750 rpm. After complete dissolution, the solution was mixed with butyl glycidyl ether (BGE), with 5 minutes of stirring (75°C) with the same stirring rate, and the pH was adjusted to 7. The reaction mixture was then cooled (4 °C, 30 minutes) in a refrigerator. The cooled samples were injected into an acetone solution (1000 rpm/min) and collected for dialysis over 72 hours, followed by lyophilization. Subsequently, 200 μL of MSCIPFP-Exo and Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo (1011 vesicles/mL) were mixed with 800 μL of the thermosensitive hydrogel solution to construct the final Gel@MSCIPFP-Exo and Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo hydrogels. This stock concentration was selected based on a previous study which indicates that the effective therapeutic dose for OA ranges from 1 × 108 to 1010 exosomes [29]. At this mixing ratio, a standard 10 μL intra-articular injection of the composite hydrogel delivers a final dose of approximately 2 × 108 vesicles directly to the joint space, ensuring therapeutic efficacy.

Morphological characterization of the hydrogels

Hydrogel microstructural morphology underwent a thorough examination via scanning electron microscopy (SEM) employing a Tescan MIRA3 FEG-SEM microscope (Tescan USA, Inc., Warrendale, PA, USA) for analysis. Cylindrical sample (6 mm diameter × 10 mm thick) preparation was performed as per the previous methods. The samples were freeze-dried, sectioned, and coated with a refined layer of gold-palladium using sputter coating techniques. The SEM microscope was employed for SEM image capturing.

Exosome release assessment

The release of MSCIPFP-Exo and Wogonin- MSCIPFP-Exo from the hydrogels was monitored from day 0 to 28 using the Bradford Protein Assay under different temperature conditions to evaluate temperature-dependent kinetics. The hydrogels (Gel@MSCIPFP-Exo and Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo) were placed in PBS in 24-well plates and incubated at either 25 °C or the physiological temperature of 37 °C. At day 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24 and 28, the liquid on the surface of the hydrogel was collected and the PBS was replaced by fresh PBS. The release was calculated as a percentage.

Rheological properties

The rheological properties of Gel@MSCIPFP-Exo and Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo were analyzed with temperature changes using a rheometer (HAAKE MARS60, Germany). The hydrogel’s thermosensitive behavior was evaluated by measuring its mechanical properties [storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G′′)]. The gelation temperature of the hydrogels was determined through rheological testing, showing the transformation from a sol to a gel state. Additionally, the moduli were monitored at various temperatures to examine the viscoelastic properties of the thermosensitive hydrogels, particularly focusing on the changes near physiological temperature (37°C).

In vitro uptake of exosomes by chondrocytes

To assess the integrity and uptake of released exosomes, DiR-labeled Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo and Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo incorporated Gel were positioned in a transwell chamber and co-cultured with chondrocytes in the lower chamber. After 48 hours, the cells were washed with PBS and fixed in 4% formalin. The cytoskeleton was stained with Phalloidin FITC (Sigma, CA, USA). The nuclei were stained with DAPI. Then, the images were captured by a fluorescence microscope (Olympus). The presence of red fluorescent signals in chondrocytes indicated successful uptake of the DiR-labeled exosomes.

Hydrogel-conditioned culture medium preparation

As for conditioned culture medium preparation, Gel, Gel@MSCIPFP-Exo and Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo were incubated in culture medium (1:10) for one week, respectively. Then the culture medium was collected as conditioned culture medium for chondrocyte culture.

Cell grouping

Chondrocytes treated with IL-1β and different conditioned media were named the following groups: Control, Gel (without loading exosomes), Gel@MSCIPFP-Exos, Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo, IL-1β, IL-1β + Gel, IL-1β + Gel@MSCIPFP-Exos, and IL-1β + Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo.

CCK-8 assay

To measure chondrocyte proliferation, a CCK-8 assay was performed. Chondrocytes were treated with IL-1β and different conditioned media for 24 and 48 h. Then, CCK-8 solution was added to the culture medium (1:10) and further incubated for 2 h. Finally, the OD value of each well was determined at 450 nm by a microreader (Bio-Rad, USA).

EdU incorporation assay

EdU incorporation assay was used to further evaluate chondrocyte proliferative ability. Chondrocytes were treated with IL-1β and different conditioned media for 24 and 48 h. Then, following incubation with EdU solution, the cells were fixed and stained as per the manufacturer’s instructions (Beyotime). Finally, fluorescence microscope analysis was conducted for proliferation rate determination (red fluorescent cells indicated proliferated cells).

Transwell migration assay

Chondrocyte migration was analyzed using a Transwell migration assay. Chondrocytes were cultured with IL-1β and different conditioned media and seeded in the upper chamber. Next, cells migrating through the membrane underwent fixing, staining, and counting.

Scratch wound assay

Chondrocyte migration capacity was evaluated using the scratch wound assay. A confluent monolayer of chondrocytes was scratched using a pipette tip, followed by cell treatment with IL-1β and conditioned medium from the different groups. The wound closure was observed and imaged at 0 and 24 h to assess cell migration.

FCM

FCM was applied to assess chondrocyte apoptosis using the Annexin V-FITC/propidium iodide (PI) Apoptosis Detection Kit (Beyotime). In short, after being cultured in 6-well plates, chondrocytes were subjected to IL-1β and conditioned medium from the different groups. After 24 hours, cells were harvested and underwent re-suspension in 500 μL of binding buffer. Subsequently, the cell suspension was added with 5 μL FITC-conjugated Annexin V and 10 μL PI for incubation (15 minutes, room temperature), avoiding the light. Next, a flow cytometer (Novocyte, CA, USA) was employed for sample analysis. Early apoptotic cells were positive for Annexin V and negative for PI, while late apoptotic cells were positive for Annexin V and PI. The apoptotic chondrocyte percentage was the sum of early and late apoptotic cells.

IF staining

IF staining was used to evaluate Collagen II and MMP13 expressions. Chondrocytes were fixed and permeabilized, followed by incubation with primary antibodies against Collagen II and MMP13. After cell rinsing, a FITC-labeled secondary antibody was added for incubation. Imaging was conducted using a fluorescence microscope.

To quantify the inflammatory state of the joint microenvironment, synovial tissues were harvested from the knee joints of mice in each group at the endpoint of the study. The tissues were fixed, embedded, and sectioned. To evaluate macrophage polarization and inflammatory cytokine levels, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies against iNOS, IL-1β (pro-inflammatory markers), and Arg-1 (an anti-inflammatory marker), followed by incubation with appropriate fluorescent secondary antibodies. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and images were captured using a fluorescence microscope. To ensure precise comparability, all images were captured using identical exposure times and camera settings across all experimental groups. The subsequent quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity was performed independently by two investigators who were blinded to the group assignments, using ImageJ software (NIH, MD, USA). The primary antibody information was listed in Table S1.

Animal model

C57BL/6 male mice (wild type, 10 weeks) purchased from SLAC Laboratory Animal Co., ltd. (Changsha, China) were placed in a pathogen-free environment and subjected to a 12 h-12 h light-dark cycle, ensuring free access to food and water. OA mouse model was induced with destabilization of the medial meniscus (DMM). Briefly, mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection with ketamine and xylazine. Mice were positioned supine, the joint capsule located on the medial side of the right knee patellar tendon was incised, and the medial meniscus-tibial ligament (MTL) was dissected using surgical scissors under the microscope. Subsequently, the joint capsule, the medial vastus muscle, and the connective tissues were sequentially sutured with absorbable sutures, and finally, the skin of the operative area was sutured with nonabsorbable sutures, and the right knee was sterilized with iodophor. Postoperatively, mice were given ampicillin to prevent infection. For the sham mice, only the right knee capsule was opened, and the medial MTL was not dissected.

Mice were grouped at random into the Sham-operated, PBS, unloaded Gel, Gel@MSCIPFP-Exo, and Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo groups (n = 6). Four weeks post-surgery, the sham-operated mice received no further treatment and served as the healthy control group, while OA-induced mice received intra-articular injections of 10 μL PBS, unloaded hydrogel, hydrogel with MSCIPFP-derived exosomes (Gel@MSCIPFP-Exo), or hydrogel with wogonin-treated MSCIPFP-derived exosomes (Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo) into the knee joint every two weeks for a total of four injections. Eight weeks after the first Gel administration, mice were treated with an over-dose of sodium pentobarbital for euthanasia, with their synovial tissues and right knee joints harvested for the subsequent study. Samples collected included serum and bone joint tissues for comprehensive analysis.

To compare the therapeutic effect of free Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo and Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo, OA mice were randomly divided into the PBS, free Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo and Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo groups. OA mice received intra-articular injections of 10 μL PBS, 10 μL free Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo (2 × 108 vesicles), or 10 μL Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo into the knee joint every two weeks for a total of four injections. At the end of experiments, mice were euthanized, and the right knee joints were harvested for subsequent study.

In vivo retention of exosomes

To evaluate the retention ability of the delivery system in vivo, Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo were labeled with DiR fluorescent dye according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The DiR-labeled free exosomes or Gel incorporating DiR-labeled exosomes (Gel@DiR-Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo) were intra-articularly injected into the right knee joint cavities of normal C57BL/6J mice. The fluorescence signals were monitored and captured using an in vivo imaging system (IVIS Spectrum, PerkinElmer, USA) at days 0, 3, 7, 14, and 28 post-injection.

Infrared thermography

To evaluate local joint temperature changes, three mice underwent DMM surgery on the left knee joint. Four weeks post-surgery, the local skin surface temperature of the knee joint was recorded using an infrared thermal camera (FLIR Systems) and analyzed to confirm localized temperature elevation associated with OA progression prior to hydrogel administration.

Histological analysis

Following mouse knee joint decalcification with a 10% EDTA solution (Beyotime), paraffin block fixing and slicing (5 μm) with a microtome (Thermo) were conducted. Slices underwent hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) and Safranin O/Fast Green stainings for evaluating cartilage injury and proteoglycan content. The Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) scoring system was utilized for OA severity assessment in a blinded manner as previously described [30].

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Mouse knee joint tissues sections were deparaffinized and hydrated, followed by blocking (1 hour, room temperature) with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 3% H2O2 (10 min, room temperature) and then overnight incubation (4°C) with primary antibodies against Collagen II and MMP13. HRP-labeled secondary antibody goat anti-rabbit IgG (#BL003A, Biosharp, Shanghai, China) was then added for 1-hour incubation (room temperature). An ultra-sensitive DAB Kit (#1205250, Typing, Nanjing, China) was employed for IHC staining visualization. All bright-field images were acquired under identical illumination and microscopic settings to maintain consistency. The average optical density (AOD) of IHC staining within mouse cartilage was determined by Image J by two independent investigators blinded to the experimental groupings.

Statistical analysis

The GraphPad Prism software (version 8.0) was employed for data analysis. Quantitative data is derived from a minimum of three separate trials. Whether data followed a normal distribution was assessed utilizing the Shapiro-Wilk test, while the homogeneity of variance was evaluated using the Levene method. A paired or unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was employed for comparing the mean values from two groups. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied for comparisons among multiple groups with a single independent variable, while two-way ANOVA was utilized for multifactorial experiments (such as assessing treatments across different time points or temperatures), followed by Tukey’s post-hoc tests. The data were reported as the mean value ± standard deviation (SD). p < 0.05 was the threshold of a statistically significant difference.

Results

Characterization of MSCIPFP-Exo and Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo

The isolation procedure was shown in Fig. 1A. Prior to exosome isolation, the replicative senescence of the source MSCs was evaluated. SA-β-gal staining confirmed that the P3–P5 MSCs used for therapy exhibited normal morphology with a negligible proportion of positively stained cells. In contrast, P10 MSCs displayed a flattened morphology and a high proportion of blue-stained senescent cells (Fig. S1B). This confirms that the exosomes utilized in this study were derived from healthy, highly active MSCs rather than a senescent population. The characterization of exosomes was conducted. TEM images confirmed the typical cup-shaped morphology of exosomes in both the MSCIPFP-Exo and Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo groups, indicating successful isolation of exosomes (Fig. 1B). Exosomal surface biomarker (CD81, CD63, and CD9) levels were determined in MSC, MSCIPFP-Exo, and Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo; MSCIPFP-Exo and Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo groups showed notably higher CD81, CD63, and CD9 levels than the MSC group (Fig. 1C). NTA results showed that exosome size distribution was 40 to 100 nm, consistent with the expected size range for exosomes [31] (Fig. D).

(B) representative transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images showing the morphology of the extracted exosomes. (C) Western blot analysis confirming the expression of exosome-specific surface markers (CD81, CD63, and CD9) and the absence of the negative marker calnexin. (D) Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) illustrates the size distribution and concentration of the exosomes. (n = 3 independent experiments)

Characterization of Gel, Gel@MSCIPFP-Exo, and Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo

Next, the hydrogels were prepared, and the characterization of the hydrogels was performed. SEM images revealed the morphology of exosomes within the hydrogel matrix, showing that the exosomes were uniformly distributed in Gel, Gel@MSCIPFP-Exo, and Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo (Fig. 2A). The thermal responsiveness of the hydrogels was demonstrated by the capacity to transform from a sol into a gel state upon heating (Fig. 2B). Rheological analysis showed significant alterations in storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G′′) of Gel@MSCIPFP-Exo and Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo with temperature variation, confirming their thermosensitive behavior (Fig. 2C). To validate the rationale for employing a temperature-responsive delivery system, local temperature changes on the skin surface of the knee joints were measured in vivo. Infrared thermography revealed a localized increase in surface temperature in the osteoarthritis-induced joints (DMM model) compared to the contralateral or normal joints, consistent with the inflammatory process in OA (Fig. S2). This elevated local temperature supports the application of our thermosensitive hydrogel, which is designed to respond to the physiological and inflammatory temperature of the joint, transitioning into a stable gel to provide sustained, localized release of therapeutic exosomes.

(B) thermal responsiveness of the hydrogels is depicted through images demonstrating the phase transition upon heating. (C) rheological properties of Gel@MSCIPFP-Exo and Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo, analyzed with a rheometer (HAAKE MARS60, Germany), including the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G′′) as a function of temperature. To evaluate the temperature-dependent release kinetics of the system, exosome release was monitored at both 25 °C and 37°C. The BCA protein assay demonstrated that at 25 °C, the hydrogels exhibited a rapid burst release, with the vast majority of the loaded exosomes released into the medium within the first 24 hours as the hydrogel transitioned toward a liquid state. Conversely, at physiological temperature (37°C), the hydrogels facilitated a consistent and sustained exosome release from day 0 to 28, with no significant difference between the Gel@MSCIPFP-Exo and Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo groups (Fig. 2D). NTA measurements confirmed that the size distribution of exosomes released from Gel@MSCIPFP-Exo and Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo remained within the expected range of 40 to 100 nm, similar to the initial exosome preparations (Fig. 2E). Finally, fluorescence microscopy images showed successful uptake of DiR-labeled exosomes by chondrocytes after 48 hours of co-culture with the hydrogels, confirming that the released exosomes were intact and bioactive (Fig. 2F).

Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo promotes chondrocyte proliferation and migration

The impact of Gel@MSCIPFP-Exo and Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo on chondrocyte proliferation and migration was evaluated. Gel@MSCIPFP-Exo and Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo were incubated for one week for obtaining the conditioned culture medium, and chondrocytes were cultured with conditioned medium for 24 or 48 h (Fig. 3A). Regarding the cytotoxicity, without IL-1β stimulation, conditioned culture medium from Gel, Gel@MSCIPFP-Exo, or Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo caused no significant changes in cell viability of chondrocytes (Fig. 3B). IL-1β-stimulated groups showed remarkably inhibited chondrocyte cell viability relative to the non-stimulated groups; under IL-1β stimulation, chondrocytes treated with conditioned medium from Gel@MSCIPFP-Exo or Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo exhibited significantly higher cell viability compared to those treated with IL-1β alone or cultured with medium from Gel, with Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo showing a more pronounced improvement in cell viability (Fig. 3B). Next, chondrocytes were cultured with conditioned culture medium from Gel, Gel@MSCIPFP-Exo, or Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo, stimulated with IL-1β, and examined for cell phenotypes. Under IL-1β stimulation, the EdU incorporation assay showed that conditioned medium from Gel did not affect cell DNA synthesis. While conditioned medium from Gel@MSCIPFP-Exo or Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo promoted the DNA synthesis, the Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo group had the highest EdU positive rate (Fig. 3C). Regarding cell migration, Transwell migration and scratch wound assays showed that chondrocytes exposed to conditioned medium from Gel@MSCIPFP-Exo or Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo showed a remarkably elevated migration rate relative to those treated with IL-1β alone or cultured with medium from Gel, with Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo promoting cell migration more (Fig. 3D–E).

Gel@MSCIPFP-Exo and Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo ameliorate cartilage damage in the OA mouse model

Following determining the effects of Gel@MSCIPFP-Exo and Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo on chondrocytes in vitro, an OA mouse model was constructed, and the in vivo effects of Gel@MSCIPFP-Exo and Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo were determined. The procedure of intra-articular injection of Gel, Gel@MSCIPFP-Exo, or Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo into the knee joint of mice was shown in Fig. 5A. Histological analysis using H&E staining demonstrated varying degrees of cartilage damage across the different treatment groups (Fig. 5B). The sham-operated group showed no significant cartilage damage, while the PBS and blank Gel groups exhibited severe cartilage degradation. Mice treated with Gel@MSCIPFP-Exo or Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo showed reduced cartilage damage, with the Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo group displaying the least damage among the treatment groups (Fig. 5B). Proteoglycan content and cartilage integrity were evaluated through Safranin O/Fast Green staining (Fig. 5C). The staining results indicated that the sham-operated group maintained high proteoglycan content and intact cartilage structure, whereas the PBS and blank Gel groups showed significant loss of proteoglycan and cartilage degradation. Treatment with Gel@MSCIPFP-Exo and Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo improved cartilage integrity and proteoglycan retention compared to the PBS and Gel groups, with Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo providing the most pronounced protection (Fig. 5C). The OARSI scoring system was employed for quantifying cartilage degradation (Fig. 5D). The OARSI scores indicated severe cartilage damage in the PBS and the Gel groups, while the Gel@MSCIPFP-Exo and Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo groups showed progressively lower scores. Notably, the Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo group had the lowest OARSI score, suggesting the most effective cartilage protection and repair among the tested treatments (Fig. 5D).

Gel@MSCIPFP-Exo and Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo promote cartilage repair in OA

Next, the effects of Gel@MSCIPFP-Exo and Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo on cartilage repair in OA were further investigated. Collagen II and MMP13 levels and distribution in cartilage sections were detected. As revealed by IHC results, the Gel@MSCIPFP-Exo and Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo groups showed dramatically higher Collagen II expression than the PBS and Gel groups, with Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo elevating Collagen II levels more, indicating enhanced cartilage repair. Conversely, the catabolic markers ADAMTS5 and MMP13 were significantly lower in the Gel@MSCIPFP-Exo and Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo groups, further supporting the protective effects of this treatment on cartilage (Fig. 6C). Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo caused more obvious changes in Sox-9, Collagen II, Aggrecan, ADAMTS5, and MMP13 levels (Fig. 6C). Immunoblotting detecting apoptotic markers showed that the Gel@MSCIPFP-Exo and Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo groups had higher anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 level and lower pro-apoptotic Bax and cleaved-Caspase-3 levels compared to the PBS and Gel groups, with Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo causing more obvious changes (Fig. 6D).

Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo profoundly modulates the inflammatory microenvironment in OA joints

Given that our thermosensitive sustained-release strategy relies on the localized heat generated by inflamed tissues, it was essential to verify and quantify the inflammatory state of the joint microenvironment. As shown by the IF staining of synovial tissues (Fig. 7), the DMM-induced OA joints (PBS and unloaded Gel groups) exhibited severe inflammation. This was characterized by highly elevated levels of the pro-inflammatory markers iNOS and IL-1β, alongside suppressed levels of the anti-inflammatory marker Arg-1, compared to the healthy Sham group. Treatment with Gel@MSCIPFP-Exo partially mitigated this inflammatory profile. Notably, the Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo group demonstrated the most potent immunomodulatory efficacy. It significantly downregulated the levels of iNOS and IL-1β while markedly upregulating Arg-1, effectively rescuing the severe inflammatory state of the OA joint.

Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo exhibits prolonged intra-articular retention and superior therapeutic efficacy compared to free exosomes

To directly validate the advantage of the hydrogel delivery system over traditional exosome direct injection, we compared the in vivo retention and therapeutic outcomes of free Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo versus Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo (Fig. 8). In vivo fluorescence imaging revealed that directly injected free exosomes were rapidly cleared from the joint, with signals diminishing significantly by day 7. In contrast, the Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo group exhibited a strong, sustained fluorescence signal localized to the knee joint for up to 28 days, confirming the prolonged retention enabled by the hydrogel (Fig. 8A). Following the 8-week treatment protocol in the DMM OA model (Fig. 8B), histological evaluations via H&E (Fig. 8C) and Safranin O/Fast Green staining (Fig. 8D) demonstrated that while free exosomes provided moderate cartilage protection compared to PBS, the Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo treatment yielded significantly superior preservation of cartilage integrity and proteoglycan content. This was quantitatively supported by the OARSI scores, where the hydrogel group scored significantly lower than the free exosome group (Fig. 8E). Furthermore, IHC analysis confirmed that Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo promoted significantly higher Collagen II deposition and a more profound reduction in MMP13 expression compared to direct exosome injection (Fig. 8F–G).

Discussion

In this study, TEM and exosomal markers (CD81/CD63/CD9) confirmed successful isolation of MSCIPFP-Exo and Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo. The hydrogels demonstrated uniform exosome distribution within the matrix and exhibited thermosensitive behavior, with controlled exosome release. The rheological analysis highlighted significant changes in storage and loss moduli with temperature variation. In vitro studies revealed that Gel@ MSCIPFP-Exo and Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo significantly increased chondrocyte proliferation, migration, and ECM deposition, as well as decreased IL-1β-induced apoptosis, with Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo showing more pronounced effects. In vivo, Gel@ MSCIPFP-Exo and Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo ameliorated cartilage damage in an OA mouse model, maintaining proteoglycan content and cartilage integrity, and reducing cartilage degradation, with Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo showing more pronounced effects. These results suggest that Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo effectively promotes cartilage repair and protects against OA progression.

As aforementioned, MSCs secrete extracellular vesicles (exosomes, microvesicles, etc.) that not only facilitate intercellular communication but replicate several therapeutic properties of MSCs. EVs, in comparison with cell-mediated therapy, circumvent potential hazards such as tumor growth [32]. Recent studies have highlighted that MSC exosomes are promising cell-free methods for therapy, demonstrating a remarkable preclinical role in encouraging cartilage restoration and renewal [33, 34]. MSC exosomes could facilitate chondrocytes’ migrating and proliferating ability, and matrix synthesis while inhibiting OA chondrocytes’ inflammation and apoptosis [35, 36]. In the present study, exosomes were successfully isolated from MSCIPFP and Wogonin-stimulated MSCIPFP, as evidenced by TEM images showing typical cup-shaped morphology and elevated levels of exosomal surface markers CD81, CD63, and CD9. These findings confirm that exosomes could be effectively isolated from IFP-derived MSCs, which are essential for cell-cell communication and exhibit therapeutic potential in various regenerative applications.

The utilization of biomaterial scaffolds for cartilage grafts has the potential to revolutionize the treatment of OA. However, its application has been restricted due to concerns over toxicity and the need for invasive surgical procedures. Thermal-responsive hydrogels have garnered significant attention in tissue engineering, particularly for OA therapy, given their ability to be injected into the body without intrusive procedures and their capacity to conform to irregular defect forms, in addition to their response to changes in temperature in lesion sites [26–28]. Additionally, they provide a permeable water-based environment to promote the proliferation of cells and regenerate injured tissues [37, 38]. Our previous study has confirmed the therapeutic effect of MSCIPFP-Exo and Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo intra-articular injection [21]. Flavonoids like wogonin exhibit concentration-dependent hormetic behavior, acting as protective antioxidants at lower doses but becoming cytotoxic at higher levels. Based on our prior viability assays [21], the 25 μM concentration used here falls safely within this antioxidant range, ensuring the observed cartilage protection stems from optimal therapeutic preconditioning rather than pro-oxidant cytotoxicity. However, exosomes rapid clearance and short half-life limit clinical translation [39], especially as OA requires repeated injections. The principal innovation of the current study lies in overcoming this specific translational barrier. In this study, thermal-responsive hydrogels loaded with exosomes from MSCIPFP and Wogonin-MSCIPFP were prepared and characterized. The hydrogels demonstrated uniform exosome distribution within the matrix, as shown by SEM images, and exhibited thermosensitive behavior, allowing the hydrogels to transform from a sol to a gel state upon heating, ensuring that the exosomes remain localized at the lesion sites and are released in a controlled manner. In vivo, our hydrogel system transformed free Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo (retained < 14 days) into a sustained-release depot, prolonging joint retention to 28 days. This enhanced retention significantly outperformed free exosomes in preventing cartilage degradation, preserving proteoglycans, and maintaining articular anabolic/catabolic balance. To validate the physiological basis for our temperature-responsive sustained release, we first quantified the joint microenvironment, confirming a severe inflammatory state in the DMM model characterized by high iNOS/IL-1β and low Arg-1 levels. This intense local inflammation directly drives pathological hyperthermia. Importantly, our in vivo infrared thermography confirmed an elevated local skin surface temperature in OA-induced joints compared to normal joints (a 3.8 °C increase). This localized hyperthermia is a well-documented clinical and preclinical hallmark of active joint inflammation [40]. Crucially, the phase transition temperature of our methylcellulose-based hydrogel is specifically engineered to align with this pathological range. The hydrogel undergoes a rapid sol-to-gel transition near 37 °C, directly matching the hyperthermic state of the arthritic joint. This precise alignment provides a direct physiological basis for our intelligent temperature-responsive strategy. Furthermore, our in vitro release profiles demonstrated a rapid burst release at 25 °C (sol state), contrasted by a stable, sustained release over 28 days at 37 °C (gel state). The controlled release of exosomes at different time points was also confirmed, while rheological analysis highlighted significant changes in storage and loss moduli with temperature variation. This thermosensitive behavior is particularly advantageous for intra-articular delivery, as the hydrogel rapidly solidifies upon exposure to the elevated physiological temperatures of the arthritic joint, thereby enhancing treatment efficacy by maintaining the localized presence and continuous stable release of exosomes at lesion sites. It is also important to consider the intrinsic properties of the hydrogel matrix itself. Throughout our in vitro and in vivo experiments, the blank hydrogel (Gel group) demonstrated outcomes comparable to the PBS control, showing no significant standalone therapeutic efficacy. This indicates that the methylcellulose-based hydrogel is biologically inert; it does not provoke any significant beneficial or adverse immunological, physicochemical, or mechanical responses on its own within the joint cavity. Therefore, its function is strictly that of a biocompatible, temperature-responsive scaffold. This biological inertness is a distinct advantage, as it definitively supports our conclusion that the pronounced cartilage protection and repair observed in the treatment groups are driven primarily by the biological activity of the loaded cargo: specifically, the MSCIPFP-Exo and the differences in therapeutic factors associated with wogonin pretreatment as previously reported. Furthermore, the delivered dose of approximately 2 × 108 vesicles per injection was strategically selected; while suboptimal doses might fail to achieve sufficient local concentrations to stimulate tissue repair, this optimized dosage provides a robust therapeutic response without the potential saturation or clearance issues associated with excessively high concentrations.

Cartilage, an avascular tissue, has constrained regenerative capacity. There are striking signs of chondrocyte mortality in the presence of degenerative joint diseases, showing profound implications for the maintenance and function of the tissue. OA cartilage exhibits apoptosis, which is intimately linked to the degradation of the matrix and the process of calcification, suggesting that chondrocyte apoptosis/survival participates in OA pathogenesis [41]. Herein, in vitro studies revealed that hydrogel loaded with MSCIPFP-Exo or Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo -derived conditioned culture medium significantly increased chondrocyte proliferating and migrating behaviors, and facilitated DNA synthesis, as well as inhibited apoptosis under IL-1β stimulation, with Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo showing more pronounced effects. These findings suggest that the hydrogels might ameliorate OA by promoting chondrocyte survival and activity and suppressing chondrocyte apoptosis. Furthermore, in chondrocytes cultured with Gel@MSCIPFP-Exo- or Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo-derived conditioned culture medium, the increased expression of aggrecan and Collagen II suggests an enhanced synthesis of cartilage matrix components, critical for cartilage repair [42, 43]. Conversely, decreased expression of ADAMTS5, MMP13, and apoptotic markers (Bax, cleaved-Caspase-3) indicates a degradation in cartilage and cell death [41, 44]. The elevated anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 level further underscores that these hydrogels protect against chondrocyte apoptosis, thereby contributing to overall cartilage health [41]. In vivo, the application of Gel@MSCIPFP-Exo and Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo in an OA mouse model resulted in substantial improvements in cartilage repair, as evidenced by increased proteoglycan content and cartilage integrity, which is essential for the structural and functional properties of cartilage [45]. The reduction in cartilage degradation by Gel@MSCIPFP-Exo or Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo was further quantified by the OARSI scoring system, a standardized method for assessing the severity of OA-related cartilage damage. These findings indicate that Gel@MSCIPFP-Exo or Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo facilitates chondrocyte survival and suppresses cell apoptosis, consequently improving chondrocyte functions and ameliorating OA in the mouse model.

Moreover, among the treatment groups, Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo showed the most pronounced effects in vitro and in vivo, indicating superior cartilage protection and repair. Our previous study has reported that wogonin pre-treated MSCIPFP exhibited increased exosome biogenesis and release. Moreover, we previously demonstrated that several chondroprotective miRNAs were enriched in Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo [21]. Notably, the present study does not provide direct mechanistic evidence or test whether previously reported molecular differences underlie the observed effects. Ultimately, while the current study robustly validates the improved therapeutic efficacy of this composite hydrogel system in an OA model, the direct causal mechanistic explanations remain largely inferred from these earlier investigations. However, a limitation of our study is that our in vitro OA model relied solely on IL‑1β stimulation to mimic the inflammatory environment, whereas the in vivo inflammatory microenvironment is considerably more complex, involving multiple cytokines such as TNF‑α and IL‑6. Consequently, cellular responses in vivo may differ from those observed in our simplified system, and future studies incorporating multi‑cytokine stimulation will be necessary to better recapitulate the joint environment during OA progression.

In conclusion, the present study shows that Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo within a thermosensitive hydrogel matrix demonstrates improved cartilage repair and reduced OA progression. The findings suggest that Gel@Wogonin-MSCIPFP-Exo is a promising option for clinical application in OA therapy, offering a novel approach to target the underlying mechanisms of cartilage degeneration and inflammation.
