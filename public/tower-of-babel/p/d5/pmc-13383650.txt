Mapping the Patient Journey of Oral Microbiota Transplantation During Radiotherapy: A Qualitative Study
Authors: Xue Liu, Xinyao Yang, Yufei Li, Like Zhang, Lei Cui, Ailin Zhang, Hao Li, Lili Hou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with head and neck cancer frequently develop severe oral mucositis (OM) during radiotherapy. Oral microbiota transplantation (OMT) has emerged as a promising microbial therapy for the prevention and management of radiation-induced OM. However, the multidimensional experiences of patients undergoing this innovative treatment remain inadequately understood.MethodsA longitudinal qualitative study was conducted in Chinese mainland from July to December 2025. Seventeen patients who underwent oral microbiota transplantation for radiotherapy of head and neck tumors were invited to participate in semi-structured interviews at four time points to explore their diagnostic and therapeutic behaviors, contact points, thoughts, emotions. Using content analysis, we developed a novel visual patient journal map that integrates time and task latitude to illustrate the dynamic care process.ResultsA total of 65 interviews were conducted with 17 patients, generated 52 initial codes, which were integrated into 15 sub-topics. Ultimately integrated as four dynamic nonlinear stages: Pre-OMT, Early-stage OMT, Mid-to-late-stage OMT, Post-OMT.ConclusionPatients with head and neck cancer undergoing OMT experience a complex, multidimensional psychosocial adaptation process. This qualitative journey map provides a new patient-centered framework. By identifying unaddressed pain points and unmet needs, this map provides an actionable perspective for designing personalized, nurse-led intervention measures and optimizing the entire treatment and care process of OMT.Keywords: experience and journey, oral microbiota transplantation, radiotherapy, head and neck cancer, qualitative study

Introduction

Head and neck tumors represent the sixth most common type of cancer worldwide,1 characterized by their unique anatomical location and complex treatment approaches.2 Radiation therapy utilizes ionizing radiation to damage the DNA structure of tumor cells, thereby inhibiting their growth for disease treatment and control.3 It has become a primary treatment modality for head and neck tumors, particularly nasopharyngeal carcinoma,4 with approximately 80% of patients requiring postoperative combined radiation therapy to ensure therapeutic efficacy.5

The oral mucosa, classified as an acute reaction tissue, exhibits high sensitivity to radiation. During radiotherapy, 65%–70% of patients will suffer severe oral mucositis,6 defined as World Health Organization grades 3 or 4. Severe oral mucositis is associated with considerable pain, quality of life impairment, infection potential, hospitalization, and treatment breaks that can decrease oncologic efficacy.7 Beyond the physical burden, head and neck cancer survivors face substantial psychosocial challenges. Studies show that 71% of post-radiotherapy patients report unmet mental health needs, and 15% screen positive for depression.8 Despite advancements in supportive care, its effective prevention and management remain a critical challenge in the field of oncology nursing and supportive therapy.

The oral microbiota represents the second largest microbial community in the human body.9 Radiotherapy can significantly reduce the diversity and richness of oral microbiota, subsequently causing microbial dysbiosis.10 Radiation disrupts the normal renewal of oral mucosal epithelial cells, impairing the mechanical protective barrier of the mucosa. This results in mucosal damage, erosion, or even necrosis, providing abundant nutrients for bacterial growth and metabolism.11

Against this backdrop, oral microbiota transplantation (OMT)—a novel and promising microbial therapy—refers to the transfer of microbial communities from the oral cavity of healthy individuals to that of patients with the aim of restoring a healthy oral microbiome.12 Early preclinical models13 and clinical case studies14 have demonstrated its significant potential in preventing and treating radiation-induced oral mucositis in patients with head and neck cancer.

Despite this promise, the clinical translation of OMT faces substantial challenges. First, sampling and technical variability remain a concern. The lack of longitudinal sampling and variability in collection methods across different oral niches complicate data interpretation and cross-study comparison.15 Second, intervention variability is inherently high, as key parameters—including donor selection, transplantation protocols, and long-term safety—remain poorly defined.16 Third, contamination concerns from tumor surfaces or laboratory kits can introduce exogenous microbial DNA, skewing results and necessitating rigorous controls and standardized protocols.17 These variabilities mean that patients undergoing OMT face not only the physical burden of mucositis but also the uncertainty of receiving an experimental therapy.

The integration of such emerging microbiota-targeted therapies into standard oncology treatment must be grounded in a profound, nuanced understanding of patient experiences. Contemporary cancer care is undergoing a paradigm shift toward “patient-centered” model,18 beyond merely assessing toxicity grades to comprehensively grasp the multidimensional lived experiences of patients undergoing novel therapies.

This study employs an adapted version of the patient journey map, a visual and narrative tool from the field of service design.19 Unlike static thematic analysis, the journey map systematically captures and visualizes patient experiences as a longitudinal process.20 It effectively identifies key touchpoints, pain points, and emotional lows throughout the care continuum,21 precisely highlights critical moments for nursing interventions. Patient journey mapping has emerged as a valuable research methodology in healthcare,22 providing a comprehensive, patient-centric approach to understanding the patients’ experiences of receiving OMT and radiotherapy simultaneously within the healthcare system.

This study aims to systematically explore and characterize the experiences of patients with head and neck cancer undergoing OMT through qualitative longitudinal research. By constructing detailed patient journey maps, we not only seek to document this experience but also strive to identify critical moments for nursing interventions. This will serve as a foundation to develop patient-centered, standardized care protocols, facilitating the safe and effective integration of OMT into care pathways for patients with head and neck cancer undergoing radiotherapy.

Methods

Study Design

We employed a longitudinal qualitative study design, guided by the principles of patient journey mapping. Patients with OMT admitted to the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Head and Neck Tumor Radiotherapy of a tertiary grade A hospital in Shanghai, China were recruited as the research participants. The department is well-known for a key clinical specialties in China, attracting diverse cases from a wide range of regions, which could enhance the depth of our results and the regional representation of samples. The research design followed the guidelines of the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research(COREQ).23

Participants

To ensure the representativeness and diversity of the sample, we adopted purposeful sampling.24 From July to December 2025, we held semi-structured interviews with 17 participants recruited from abovementioned department and hospital.

Participants’ inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) having a confirmed histological diagnosis of Maxillofacial Head and Neck Tumor, (2) being scheduled to undergo or having completed a full course of OMT as part of their radiotherapy protocol, (3) being aged 18 years or older, (4) being able to communicate normally, and (5) providing informed consent by signing the informed consent form.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) severe cognitive impairment or psychiatric disorders, (2) prevalence of severe complications, (3) concurrent participation in another interventional clinical trial that could potentially significantly alter their treatment experience.

Interview Guide

Based on the research objectives, a preliminary interview outline was drafted by reviewing relevant domestic and international literature, consulting experts, and holding discussions with the research team members. Pre-interviews were conducted with two patients who had oral cancer. Based on the results and further consultations with experts in oral medicine, nursing, radiotherapy, oncology, and rehabilitation, the research team compiled, integrated, and revised the feedback from the interviewees and experts to finalize the formal semi-structured interview outline, as depicted in Figure 1. Thematic saturation was achieved by the 17th patient interview. After conducting two additional interviews with no new themes emerging, the interviews were concluded. Each interview lasted 20–30 minutes.

Semi-structured Interview Guide for Patient Experiences with Oral Microbiota Transplantation.

Data Collection

The interviewer was a master’s student responsible for managing the work related to patients undergoing OMT in the department. Prior to the interview, a strong trusting relationship was established with the interviewees. However, we acknowledge that the interviewer’s dual role (clinical manager and researcher) could introduce social desirability bias. To mitigate this bias, prior to each interview, the interviewer explicitly stated that: (a) the study was independent of clinical care, (b) refusal to answer or expressing negative opinions would not affect the participant’s medical treatment or their relationship with the department. Importantly, the established trusting relationship also helped reduce superficial response bias; because patients felt safe, they were more willing to disclose sensitive emotions such as pain and anxiety—deep-level data that a stranger interviewer might not have captured. The interviewer had received qualitative research training and possessed prior interview experience and skills. An empty consultation room or ward was chosen as the interview location, based on the interviewees’ preference. After consent was obtained, the interview was audio-recorded, and the interviewees’ facial expressions, gestures, and emotional changes were observed and documented using a four-domain non-verbal behavior framework (facial expressions, gestures, paralinguistic cues, and emotional shifts), with field notes written within 15 minutes post-interview.

Data Analysis

From July to December, 2025, interviews were conducted with 17 OMT patients before the transplantation, during the 10th and 20th sessions, during the final session, and one month after completion of the OMT. Within 24 hours after each interview, the recordings were transcribed verbatim into written transcripts. Two researchers independently coded the transcripts following the Colaizzi 7-step method: (1) recording and thoroughly reading all interview transcripts, (2) extracting relevant statements, (3) coding significant statements and recurring viewpoints, (4) summarizing common features and concepts from the coded viewpoints, (5) comprehensively describing the relationships between research themes and observed phenomena, (6) aggregating similar viewpoints to refine the themes, and (7) returning the interview results to participants for verification. Discrepancies in coding were resolved through discussion within the research team; if no consensus was reached, a third senior researcher was consulted. The interview data was managed using NVivo 15.0 software.

Following this, a process map was constructed based on the fundamental steps outlined in relevant literature. These involved: (1) focusing on the challenges of full-cycle management for patients undergoing OMT, identifying the target population and key issues; (2) establishing the process framework by integrating literature reviews, clinical practice, and group discussions; (3) conducting in-depth interviews to comprehensively gather patients’ experiences and perceptions regarding full-cycle disease management; and (4) analyzing interview results to populate the map and refine additional details.

Rigor

Before the interview, the interviewer thoroughly investigated and recorded the clinical data of the participants, including Tumor Site, Pathological Type, Radiation Dose (Gy), to ask precise questions tailored to individual treatment trajectories and reduce recall bias. During the interview, if a participant provided vague answers or if the content did not align with objective records, the interviewer promptly repeated, probed, and clarified the information. Within 24 hours after the interview, the transcribed results were returned to the participant for verification and confirmation until accuracy was ensured.

Ethical Considerations

The study was conducted in strict accordance with the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of The Ninth People’s Hospital Affiliated to Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine [Approval No.: SH9H-2024-T437-3]. All participants read the study’s purpose and were assured of their right to withdraw at any time without affecting their care. Written informed consent was obtained prior to interviews, and this consent included permission for the publication of anonymized responses and direct quotes. All data were anonymized and stored securely on a password-protected standalone hard disk accessible only to the research team.

Results

Demographic Characteristics of Participants

A total of 65 interviews were conducted with 17 patients, generated 52 initial codes, which were integrated into 15 sub-topics. They comprised 12 males (70.6%) and 5 females (29.4%), with a mean age of 42.8 years (standard deviation [SD] = 12.5, range: 20–59). The majority were married (64.7%) and employed (58.8%). Educational backgrounds varied, with the largest proportion having completed junior college (35.3%). The most common tumor site was the tongue (41.2%), and the predominant pathological type was squamous cell carcinoma (76.5%). Treatment Journey and Core Experiences of OMT Patients

The experiences of patients with head and neck cancer undergoing radiotherapy during OMT can be classified into four stages: pre-, early-stage, mid-to-late-stage, and post-OMT. Each stage is accompanied by specific diagnostic and therapeutic procedures, core challenges, and emotional fluctuations. The patient journey map is depicted in Figure 2.

Patient Experience Journey Map: Stages, Themes, Emotions, and Thoughts about OMT during Radiotherapy.

Pre OMT: Constructing the Rationality of Decision Making Between Therapeutic Uncertainty and Hope

The patient’s decision is not the result of an objective comparison of data but rather a choice made under the dual pressures of insufficient information and time constraints in deciding whether to undergo OMT for the prevention and treatment of oral mucositis.

The Limitations of the Decision-Making Environment

Radiotherapy patients encounter numerous coordination barriers during the admission phase, such as cumbersome medical procedures like surgical arrangements and scheduling examinations. Because of physical discomfort or psychological stress, they often struggle to coordinate effectively, leaving insufficient time for making decisions regarding OMT. This results in initial confusion upon first exposure.

Right after being admitted to the hospital, I was busy scheduling tests and waiting in line for radiation therapy. Although I had heard about the benefits of microbiota transplantation, when the medical staff came to collect saliva samples, I still felt a bit caught off guard. (P6)

Information Pieced Together by Doctors and Fellow Patients

Patients passively or actively encounter information about OMT from various sources, initiating their personal understanding and evaluation process, which constitutes a significant aspect of the decision-making process.

Hearing that some doctors recommended it, I decided to give it a try with an open mind. (P1)

When I went for radiotherapy positioning, I met a fellow patient who had undergone surgery. She recommended trying this microbiota transplantation, as she didn’t experience any mucositis throughout her treatment. I’m also considering giving it a try. (P10)

Deepening the Risk-Benefit Assessment

Patients weigh the pros and cons of OMT therapy with limited information. They actively seek information related to efficacy and safety to reduce the uncertainty of decision-making by obtaining key data through questions such as: “Do you have any data on the cure rate of microbiota transplantation?” (P11) or “What are the components of the transplanted bacterial solution? I’d like to check if there are any side effects” (P3). On the one hand, they acknowledge the potential benefits of the therapy, believing that “more treatment options mean more hope” (P4). On the other hand, they express deep concerns about the safety of emerging therapies, repeatedly agonizing over questions like “Could there be any potential side effects?” (P8). This highlights the core dilemma during the decision-making period—the tension between weighing risks and benefits.

Prevention as the Core Driver of Decision Making

Patients’ self-adjustment was primarily motivated by the desire to prevent radiotherapy side effects. They leveraged their prior medical knowledge to establish initial trust in the new therapy:

I work in the healthcare industry and have heard about fecal microbiota transplantation, which has shown significant efficacy for certain diseases. This is the first time I’ve heard of oral microbiota transplantation, and I’d like to give it a try. (P7)

Despite “not knowing what oral microbiota transplantation is or what the oral microbiome entails” (P5), patients adopted the mindset of “trying the transplantation as a preventive measure.” (P8) “To minimize uncertainty as much as possible” (P12), they emphasized the importance of taking action.

Early-Stage OMT: Balancing Hope for Treatment Against Physiological Rejection

During early-stage OMT, patients’ experiences primarily revolve around a challenging trade-off between seeking therapeutic hope and coping with multidimensional rejection reactions. This stage encompasses the following intertwined themes.

Sensory Protest and Physiological Rejection of Bacterial Suspension

When a bacterial suspension prepared from “another person’s saliva” is introduced into the patient’s oral cavity as a therapeutic medium, its physical properties first trigger direct sensory discomfort and instinctive resistance, leading to physiological unease and reflexive aversion.

It always feels like someone else’s saliva is directly entering my mouth, and the discomfort is quite intense. (P2)

Even with impaired taste, other senses continue to sound alarms:

Although radiotherapy causes taste disorders, the sense of smell remains, and I can still detect the foul odor. (P7)

This constitutes the first barrier to treatment acceptance, requiring patients to engage in sustained internal psychological persuasion.

Discrepancy Between Reality and Perception

Patients often imagine the medical concept of “transplantation” as more grandiose and modern; thus, the actual operational medium delivers a cognitive shock.

When I heard ‘transplant,’ I thought it would be a major surgery, but when it actually happened, I realized it was someone else’s saliva—it was hard to overcome the psychological discomfort. (P13)

Another patient remarked, “Just this tiny bag?” (P9). This adjustment process, from “high-tech surgery” to “another person’s saliva,” marks the psychological starting point for patients to reinterpret and accept the essence of the treatment.

Safety Concerns and the Urgent Need for Coping Strategies

Once treatment begins, patients’ anxiety quickly shifts from “whether to proceed” to “how to proceed,” reflecting deep concerns about operational safety and daily life management. This concern highlights their urgent desire for efficacy alongside the resulting anxiety about responsibility. Their questions directly address risk control and procedural details:

Has this saliva been tested? Are there any potential risks? (P14);

How long should I hold the suspension in my mouth? Does rinsing afterward affect the treatment? (P15).

To ensure treatment effectiveness and integration into daily life, they crave clear guidance:

Could there be a detailed instructional video? I’d like to review the specific precautions again (P9).

They also express concerns about their habits:

Do I need to watch my diet? I’m afraid eating irritating foods might affect bacterial colonization (P17).

Stigma Perception and Social Self-Isolation

Patients generally anticipate that this therapy may be difficult for others to understand, potentially leading to prejudice, thereby fostering a sense of “stigma.” Consequently, they actively conceal treatment details from their social circles fearing judgment from others, resulting in psychological isolation.

When I went home on weekends during the break from radiotherapy, I didn’t dare let friends know about this treatment; I only mentioned it briefly when fellow patients asked. (P14)

To mitigate this stigma and discomfort, patients expressed hope for more conventional treatment formats:

This is still in liquid form. I hope the dosage form can be improved in the future, like sprays or lozenges (P10).

Mid-to-Late OMT: Efficacy Attribution, Growth of Self-Efficacy, and Transformation of Patient Roles

Mid-to-late stage OMT is a critical phase where patients transition from passive recipients of treatment to active managers of their condition. Core tasks involve interpreting oral physiological signals, evaluating transplantation efficacy, adjusting self-health behaviors, and reconstructing identity, thereby fostering a sense of disease control and motivation for treatment adherence. During this stage, patients’ experiences exhibit multidimensional characteristics, summarized by the following four core themes.

Cognitive Struggles and Emotional Fluctuations in Efficacy Attribution

Patients engage in continuous cognitive efforts to verify treatment efficacy, eagerly seeking explanations for changes in their oral mucosa or even their overall physical state, often accompanied by confusion and emotional turbulence. When symptoms persist, they feel frustrated and question the treatment’s effectiveness:

Why does my mucositis still exist after this microbiota transplantation? Is it useless? (P6).

Conversely, when symptoms improve, they cautiously attribute the progress to the transplantation while remaining skeptical:

My dry mouth and taste disturbances aren’t severe. Could this be a potential effect of the microbiota transplantation? (P15).

I still don’t understand how transferring someone else’s ‘good bacteria’ into my mouth can help me recover (P1).

Although this ongoing dialogue between bodily perception and scientific explanation is a psychological necessity for patients to comprehend the treatment and sustain adherence, it also constitutes a major source of cognitive burden and emotional instability.

Proactive Exploration of Self-Management Practices and Treatment Adherence

With the growth of self-efficacy, patients actively shift from passive recipients to proactive self-managers, manifesting in various behaviors. These include inquiring about microbiota test results: “Will my saliva be tested? Can I know the results?” (P4); seeking personalized dietary guidance: “Eating is difficult now—are there any suitable food recommendations?” (P17); voluntarily delaying oral intake to enhance microbiota colonization: “I’ll endure not eating or drinking temporarily to maximize the colonization effect” (P13); actively researching oral care products to mitigate colonization failure risks: “Are there any recommended oral care products? I’m afraid improper care might lead to colonization failure” (P11); purchasing probiotic supplements as adjuvant therapy: “After attending the health education session, I’ve already bought some probiotic yogurt” (P3); and reinforcing treatment confidence and compliance by comparing their condition with that of fellow patients:

Compared to other radiotherapy patients at the same stage, my mucositis is much milder—I’ll definitely complete the full treatment cycle (P15).

The pivotal Role of External Support Systems

During this phase of subjective speculation and emotional fluctuations, the professional patience of healthcare providers and the emotional support of family members are irreplaceable. “The medical staff are very patient and attentive, which gives me great confidence” (P16); “My family has been encouraging me; their support has boosted my confidence a lot” (P2). Such support directly translates into patient confidence and adherence, helping them overcome discomfort during treatment. “Now I can convince myself to tolerate the discomfort of the bacterial solution; it’s for my recovery” (P9).

Treatment Integration Dilemmas and Identity Continuity Anxiety

When faced with suboptimal efficacy, patients’ anxiety escalates from concerns about treatment effectiveness to deeper existential doubts about the possibility of a cure. This anxiety reflects the underrecognized risks of emerging adjuvant therapies, where patients may equate treatment failure with the ineffectiveness of modern medicine for their condition:

There’s no obvious improvement—I wonder how things would be if I hadn’t undergone microbiota transplantation (P8).

This engenders a sense of crisis regarding the continuation of one’s identity, coupled with confusion about the synergistic use of multiple treatments—

Why is microbiota transplantation not very effective for me? Can my disease still be cured? (P5).

Post OMT: Anxiety Over the Disruption of Continuity in Support

When the OMT treatment officially concludes, patients transition from a structured, protected medical environment back to their daily lives. Their core challenge during this transitional period is in their ability to swiftly establish a sustainable personal support system to cope with long-term uncertainties.

Dependence on and craving for accessible professional support

Patients become acutely aware that they are returning to a routine medical environment lacking specialized OMT expertise, which paradoxically intensifies their reliance on the professional support they once had. Their concerns directly highlight the gap in the continuity of care:

There are no doctors specializing in microbiota transplantation where I live. Can I still contact you if I experience discomfort? (P12).

Patients express a desire to establish periodic, two-way communication mechanisms, not only hoping for proactive follow-ups from healthcare providers but also emphasizing their own agency as “information providers”:

I hope a doctor can check in on me regularly, and I’d also like to proactively share updates with you (P3).

Seeking peer communities to build an empirical knowledge base

In the absence of adequate professional support, patients actively seek out peer communities, with the primary goal of constructing a targeted repository of experiential knowledge:

Could we create a patient group? I’d like to learn how others manage their recovery after microbiota transplantation (P15).

By engaging with others who have undergone similar transplant experiences, they gain practical recovery insights better suited to everyday life, compensating for the lack of detailed lifestyle guidance in professional medical advice and bolstering their confidence in long-term self-management.

Normalizing the fear of recurrence and accepting long-term uncertainty

During the extended recovery period post-OMT, patients must learn to manage the persistent fear of symptom recurrence:

What should I do if oral mucositis returns after the microbiota transplantation? (P10),

while simultaneously accepting the inherent uncertainty of long-term disease management. This fear manifests as concerns about the recurrence of oral mucositis and confusion over critical post-treatment practices such as diet and care:

Are there any special dietary requirements? (P11).

At its core, this reflects a desire for clear, long-term recovery guidelines to mitigate the risks of disease progression.

Discussion

This longitudinal qualitative research study systematically depicts, for the first time, the comprehensive experiences and psychological journeys of patients with head and neck cancer undergoing concurrent OMT during radiotherapy. The findings reveal that patients undergo a dynamic, nonlinear psychosocial adaptation process comprising four distinct stages. This four-stage journey map can be understood through Meleis’s Transition Theory, which describes health-illness transitions as processes involving uncertainty, identity reconfiguration, and support needs.25 From the decision-making phase marked by information negotiation and hope construction, to sensory aversion and stigma in the early treatment phase, followed by active management and efficacy attribution during mid-to-late treatment, and ultimately transitioning to a profound longing for continuity of care post-treatment. Their journey map transcends traditional static evaluations of treatment “toxicity” or “compliance,” uncovering the multidimensional and complex experiences patients encounter while navigating emerging therapies.

Empowering Decision Making Under Time and Information Constraints

This study revealed that patients making treatment decisions under the dual pressures of time constraints and information asymmetry primarily rely on fragmented information provided by healthcare professionals and fellow patients, prioritizing the preventive value of treatment. This aligns with the common dilemma faced by patients with cancer when considering novel adjuvant therapies—wavering between hope for treatment26 and the “uncertainty of evidence”27 accord with Mishel’s Uncertainty in Illness Theory. Therefore, nursing staff should intervene early in radiotherapy planning, offering authentic, professional clinical data, and conveying hope while managing uncertainty through personalized communication.28 Allocating dedicated and sufficient consultation sessions at key decision-making points, such as before saliva collection,29 or combining direct evidence from OMT with analogous evidence from FMT30 can help illustrate the safety of such therapies and the shared psychosocial challenges they entail. Additionally, exploring the establishment of a trained peer supporter system,31 involving patients who have completed treatment sharing their experiences, may be beneficial. This nursing-led, proactive, continuous communication strategy can empower patients, alleviate decision-making pressure, and establish realistic expectations.32

Addressing Unique Treatment-Induced Psychosocial Challenges

Patients undergoing OMT experience a dual psychological conflict: while they eagerly anticipate therapeutic efficacy, they simultaneously exhibiting physiological and psychological rejection toward the “foreign” material.33 Additionally, they may develop a sense of stigma associated with microbiome-modulating interventions.34 These factors constitute distinct psychosocial challenges that differ from those encountered in conventional radiotherapy or chemotherapy. This dynamic trajectory aligns closely with Bandura’s Self-Efficacy Theory,35 which posits that self-efficacy is not innate but develops through mastery experiences, vicarious learning, and verbal persuasion. Patient acceptance of OMT is not an instantaneous process but evolves dynamically—progressing from limited decision making under external pressure, to gradual adaptation amid internal conflict, to self-efficacy-driven active management, and ultimately to continuity anxiety triggered by transitions in support systems. Nursing practices must respond to these challenges with targeted interventions. For instance, explaining the safety testing protocols during microbial preparation can enhance patients perceived medical legitimacy of the procedure.36,37 Health education initiatives within clinical units should conceptualize the patient “experience” as an observable, stage-specific continuum,38 providing clear milestones for implementing precision nursing interventions.

The Influence of OMT’s Biological Uncertainty on Patient Experiences

Beyond the psychosocial challenges, many patient emotions and behaviors are rooted in the inherent biological and technical uncertainties of OMT—an experimental therapy not yet matured into a standardized intervention. First, sampling and microbiota variability leads to heterogeneous responses,39 explaining some patients ask, Why OMT not very effective for me? Second, host–microbe interactions—influenced by immune status, oral environment, and radiation dose—create outcome uncertainty,40,41 reflected in patients’ requests for cure rate data and concerns about potential side effects. Third, barriers to clinical translation (eg., lack of standardized protocols) limit treatment continuity,13 directly contributing to patients’ anxiety over the disruption of support. Acknowledging these uncertainties does not diminish OMT value but contextualizes patient experiences within the real-world challenges of an emerging therapy. Nursing communication should transparently address these variabilities to foster realistic expectations while sustaining hope.

Cultivation of Self-Efficacy and the Evolution of Nursing Roles

Patients’ self-management behaviors do not emerge automatically at the onset of treatment but develop dynamically with the support of healthcare professionals and family, alongside their interpretation of symptoms.42 Nursing interventions should first ensure that patients achieve psychological acceptance of treatment and social adaptation, laying the foundation for their subsequent self-empowerment.43 During this phase, the role of nurses shifts from task executors to coaches, collaborators, and enablers.44 Specific measures include: co-developing individualized oral care and dietary plans,45 conducting health education on OMT in more accessible formats such as videos and diagrams to help patients understand the necessity of microbial regulation,46 and encouraging patients to maintain symptom diaries and collaboratively interpret bodily signals and treatment responses with healthcare providers.47 Such collaborative care not only enhances patients’ sense of control and adherence but also generates continuous data that provide valuable insights to evaluate the personalized efficacy of OMT.

Addressing the “Support Gap” Post-Treatment

The conclusion of OMT does not signify the end of a patient’s needs; rather, it marks the beginning of a new vulnerable phase characterized by anxiety over the abrupt loss of professional support.48 Patients’ eagerness for continued guidance, peer communities, and long-term rehabilitation planning highlights a common shortcoming in current oncology care models during their transition from the treatment to the “survivorship phase.”49

Therefore, designing a structured continuity-of-care framework for OMT,50 led and implemented by the nursing team, is essential. Key components include: developing clear post-discharge self-management guidelines,51 utilizing mobile health technologies to establish remote monitoring and consultation channels for timely symptom intervention,52 and formally establishing and maintaining OMT patient support groups coordinated by nursing staff to share experiences and provide emotional support.53 These measures are critical to ensure the long-term benefits of OMT, improve patients’ quality of life, and achieve comprehensive, full-cycle care.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, because the participants were all from a single hospital, the generalizability of the findings is limited. Second, the data were primarily collected through semi-structured interviews focusing on patients. Future research should incorporate interviews with healthcare providers or clinical observation data. Third, because this was a qualitative study, further large-scale mixed-methods research or randomized controlled trials are needed to quantify the specific effects of OMT on clinical outcomes such as patients’ quality of life and the severity of mucositis. Fourth, the follow-up period was limited to one month post-treatment, which is insufficient for assessing longer-term adaptation, such as sustained emotional changes. Future studies should extend the follow-up period to capture the full trajectory of patient adaptation.

Conclusions

This study employed patient journey mapping to provide an in-depth exploration of the comprehensive experience of patients with head and neck cancer undergoing OMT—a complex therapeutic intervention. However, the findings are derived from a single-center qualitative study and do not claim statistical generalizability; OMT itself remains an experimental therapy with inherent biological and technical uncertainties. Successfully integrating this cutting-edge microecological therapy into clinical practice necessitates the establishment of a refined patient-centered care support system. This requires nursing practices to deliver empowering support during decision-making stages, address and intervene in unique psychosocial adaptation challenges during treatment, emphasize patients’ self-efficacy, and implement continued care post-treatment. The study offers preliminary empirical evidence and clear actionable directions, facilitating a critical paradigm shift in oncology nursing from mere “side effect management” to a “holistic treatment journey experience.” It thereby lays both theoretical and practical foundations for implementing truly patient-centered precision care in broader innovative therapy domains.
