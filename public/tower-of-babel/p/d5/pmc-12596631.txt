Effect of coagulants on physiochemical properties, texture, color and microstructure characterization of cow milk cheese
Authors: Azra Akbar, Muhammad Ikram Nabeel, Amna Jabbar Siddiqui, Syed Ghulam Musharraf
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cheese is a globally significant dairy product, with production increasing due to high consumer demand. As a nutrient-rich food, it provides essential nutrients for human health. However, low cheese yield remains a major economic challenge for cheesemakers. This study examines the effects of different rennet /coagulants calf, pig, microbial, and acetic acid on the physicochemical properties of cow milk cheese. Key parameters such as yield, color, texture, sensory attributes, and microstructure were analyzed. Cheese made with pig rennet showed significantly higher hardness (p < 0.05) and a greater fat-to-protein ratio than calf rennet cheese, while calf rennet produced a higher yield. Scanning electron microscopy revealed that calf rennet cheeses had a smooth, continuous casein network, whereas pig rennet cheese exhibited irregular aggregates. Sensory evaluations highlighted distinct differences between calf and pig rennet cheese. These findings provide valuable insights into enzyme selection for cheese production, influencing quality and consumer preference.

Introduction

Cheese is one of the most widely consumed dairy products globally, with approximately 35–40 % of all processed milk (equivalent to about 19 to 20 million tons) used for its production. More than 3000 different types of cheese are prepared worldwide, varying according to regional traditions and requirements (Santiago-López et al., 2018). Cheese is known for its rich nutritional value and diverse sensory attributes, undergoing complex biochemical processes that significantly affect its physicochemical properties, texture, color, and microstructure. Coagulation, which is a critical process in cheese production, is primarily facilitated by rennet, which transforms liquid milk into solid curds by enzymatically cleaving κ-casein, a specific milk protein. Rennet is a complex mixture of enzymes extracted from the stomach lining (abomasum) of young mammals. It can be sourced from various animals such as calves, pigs, lambs, or goats, and microbial and genetically engineered alternatives are also available (Fox et al., 2015; Fox et al., 2017).

Calf rennet is traditionally widely used, but it can only meet a portion (20–30 %) of the cheese industry's coagulant demand due to its high cost (Alihanoğlu et al., 2018). Plant-derived enzymes have gained popularity as alternatives in cheese production (Hashim et al., 2011). With growing ethical concerns, supply constraints, and dietary preferences, alternative sources of rennet, such as pig rennet, have been explored. Although pig rennet functions similarly to calf rennet, it is less commonly used due to cultural and religious reasons, but it can still produce cheese with comparable textural and sensory properties. Microbial rennet offers a vegetarian-friendly and more sustainable option, although it may lead to differences in cheese properties due to variations in enzymatic profiles. From raw milk to finished cheese, several steps, including pasteurization, addition of coagulant, salting, and ripening, are involved in the cheese-making process (Liu et al., 2021). Rennet, animal, vegetable, or microbial, is the most commonly used coagulant. However, vegetable and microbial rennet may reduce cheese yield, enhance bitterness, and not develop desirable flavors during extended ripening, limiting their use in industrial cheese production (Fresno et al., 2023). Despite their drawbacks, alternatives to calf rennet are sought due to increasing global cheese consumption (Tadjine et al., 2020).

Rennet also plays a crucial role in influencing cheese texture by cleaving peptides and forming a three-dimensional protein matrix known as curd (García-Gómez et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2021). Milk composition, manufacturing techniques, and ripening conditions significantly affect cheese characteristics. The concentration of rennet directly impacts the coagulation rate and final cheese quality (Li et al., 2022). Moreover, the type of coagulants (enzymes) used directly influences the characteristics of cheese, such as its flavor, texture and sensory properties. Various factors, including rennet type, coagulation properties, milk pH, and temperature, influence cheese yield, texture, and flavor (Fresno et al., 2023). Cheese-making studies explore alternatives to calf rennet, focusing on various parameters such as volatile molecule profile, fatty acids, texture, and sensory properties (Camin et al., 2019). Additionally, the research investigates the impact of rennet sources and brine concentration on cheese properties (Türkmen & Güler, 2022). Tofalo et al. (2015) found that Pecorino di Farindola cheese made with pig rennet had faster casein cutting compared to calf rennet due to its higher proteolytic activity (Di Giacomo et al., 2013). Various studies have worked to distinguish animal rennet from recombinant chymosin. Research in cheese production covers different types of cheese (Güler et al., 2021; Türkmen & Güler, 2022), geographical origin (Semjon et al., 2018) and ripening time. Food quality is a key indicator in the food industry, involving multiple characteristics from production to consumer (Ghasemi-Varnamkhasti et al., 2019). Blue cheese and Camembert are particularly popular globally, especially in France. Due to cheese's importance in daily diets, many studies have explored the microbial, biochemical, sensory, odor, and structural properties of camembert cheese (de Oliveira et al., 2017).

The objective of this study was to analyze and compare the effects of different coagulants including calf, pig, microbial rennet, and acetic acid on the chemical composition, sensory quality, texture, color, microstructure, and coagulation behavior of cheese, an area that has not been extensively explored previously. We investigated various coagulants to reveal their interactions and understand their impacts on the cheese-making process to evaluate their technological suitability for cheese production. To achieve this, we conducted comparative experiments to examine the differences among cheeses produced with these coagulants and to correlate these differences with the compositional variations. Furthermore, understanding the sensory perception of cheeses produced with different enzymes among consumers could provide valuable insights for product development and marketing strategies.

Material and methods

Materials

Raw cow's milk was collected as a bulk sample from a local dairy farm in the Karachi region of Pakistan. The milk was thoroughly homogenized and then divided into four equal aliquots (n = 4), each assigned to a different coagulant (calf rennet, pig rennet, microbial rennet, and acetic acid). Each batch of cheese was prepared separately to maintain consistency in processing conditions. The rennet used in this study included microbial rennet (1:20,000 IMCU/mL) from R. miehei, calf rennet (1:16,000 IMCU/mL), and porcine rennet (1:10,000 IMCU/mL), all procured from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Analytical-grade sodium chloride (NaCl) for brine preparation, calcium chloride (CaCl₂), and acetic acid were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (Burlington, MA, USA).

Cheese making procedure

Fresh raw cow milk (1 L) was used to produce cheese samples by using different rennet sources, including calf rennet, pork rennet, microbial rennet, and organic acid (acetic acid). The milk was heated to approximately 38 °C, and CaCl2 (15 % w/v) was added to the milk, followed by continuous stirring for 5 min. Subsequently, 0.13 g of enzyme (rennet) was added to the milk and thoroughly mixed. After coagulation, the curd was sliced into cubes, separated from the whey, and transferred into perforated cylindrical molds without any pressure applied. After draining the whey, the cheeses were de-molded and immersed in cool water until they were firm enough to retain their shape. Subsequently, the cheese was soaked in brine (10 % NaCl) for 2 h. The cheeses were then divided into pieces, vacuum-sealed in sterile polyethylene bags, and stored at 4 °C for further analysis. Photographs of the cheeses were captured as depicted in Fig. S1.

Analytical procedures

Physicochemical analysis and cheese yields

The biochemical analysis of the prepared cheese followed the Official Methods of Analysis (Helrich, 1990) for food. Various parameters including yield, total solids, moisture content, carbohydrates, protein, and fat content were determined. The protein content was assessed using the Kjeldahl method and calculated with a conversion factor of 6.38. Crude fat was estimated through solvent extraction employing a Soxhlet extraction unit (Helrich, 1990). Moisture content was determined by heating the samples to a constant weight at 105 °C for 6 h. Ash content was determined by heating the sample to 600 °C overnight in a muffle furnace following the Association of Official Analytical Chemists guidelines (Helrich, 1990). Total carbohydrate content (TCC) was calculated by subtracting the percentages of crude protein, ash, and fat from 100 %. Caloric content (kcal per 100 g of cheese) was estimated using the Atwater conversion factors (Paul & Southgate, 1978). pH was measured using a pH meter with 10 g of grated cheese in 10 mL of deionized water (632 pH meter). Each sample was analyzed in triplicate.

Cheese microstructure analysis

The microstructure of the cheese samples was analyzed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, model Thermo Fisher Apreo 2C Lo Vac) following a modification procedure outlined by (Soltanı et al., 2016). Before analysis, samples were lyophilized, fixed on graphite tape, and coated with a thin layer of gold (Au) using Denton Vacuum Desk IV equipment and examined under high vacuum conditions to generate high-resolution SEM images. The samples were imaged at an acceleration voltage of 10 kV and micrographs of cheese samples were recorded at different magnifications.

Texture profile analysis

Textural parameters were analyzed using a modified protocol based on the method described by (Güler et al., 2021). TPA was carried out using a Universal testing machine (D-89079 Ulm, Zwick/Roell, GmbH& Co, Germany), equipped with a load cell of 100 N. Cheese block samples were cut into (3 × 3 × 3) cm3 pieces, bored from the cheese using a cork borer and a slicer. The test conditions were: 20 °C room temperature; two consecutive cycles of 50 % compression; cross-head at a constant speed of 50 mm/min and a cylindrical probe with a diameter of 2 cm was employed and the textural characteristics measured included hardness, cohesiveness, adhesiveness, chewiness, gumminess, and springiness. Parameters measured for cheese samples were a) Hardness (maximum force at the end of the first compression cycle), b) Gumminess (product of cohesiveness and hardness), c) Chewiness (product of cohesiveness, hardness, and springiness), d) Cohesiveness (strength of cheese), e) Springiness (product of hardness and Cohesiveness). The cheese samples were equilibrated to 25 °C prior to textural analysis, and the measurements were repeated three times.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was conducted using a TGA/DSC PERKIN ELMER instrument in a nitrogen atmosphere, maintaining a gas flow rate of 50 cm3/min. Around 15 mg of fresh cheese was placed in 100 μL aluminum crucibles equipped with pierceable lids. The samples were subjected to heating from 0 °C to 400 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min, and each sample analyzed in duplicate. A blank aluminum crucible with a pierceable cover was also heated under the same conditions. To account for the buoyancy impact on the empty crucible during heating, subtract the blank curve from the sample TGA curve. Graphs were generated to illustrate the relationship between sample weight and temperature.

FT-IR analysis

The FT-IR spectra were measured using an infrared spectrometer (Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS50 Research Grade). The freeze-dried cheese samples were crushed and then pressed with KBr. Thirty scans were co-added at a normal resolution of 4 cm−1 within a 4000 to 400 cm−1 region with OMNIC software (OMNIC). The atmospheric background was deducted automatically to minimize the disruption of the atmosphere during measurement. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate and the final results are presented as the mean spectrum. Furthermore, the secondary structure of cheese protein was analyzed according to the FT-IR spectrum. To remove the interference of moisture and distinguish between the helix and random coil structures, the amide III spectra were selected to analyze the secondary protein structure. Data of the amide III spectra were pretreated according to Wang et al. by Peakfit (4.12) software. We used the following criteria to identify the secondary structure corresponding to peaks in amide III: 1290 to 1330.

Colorimetric analysis

The cheese samples' color parameters, including [CIELAB; lightness (L*), redness (a*), and yellowness (b*)] were measured using the colorimeter manufactured by Shenzhen Linshang Technology Co., Ltd. The device was equipped with (illuminant D65) and set at an observation angle of 10°. Before measurement, calibration was performed using black and white ceramic calibration plates. Hue angle and chroma value were determined as follows; hue angle = arctan (b*/a*) and chroma value = (a2 + b2)1/2. Three readings were obtained from various regions of the inner surface of each cheese sample.

Sensory analysis

A sensory evaluation of all cheese samples was carried out by a panel comprising nine members, including both males and females, who were knowledgeable about the product and received appropriate training to ensure accurate assessments. The assessments were conducted under standard lighting conditions in individual booths. Each cheese sample was cut into cubes. The samples were left at room temperature (20 ± 2 °C) for thirty minutes before being presented to the panelists. To avoid bias, the samples were presented to the panelists in a randomized sequence, each labeled with a unique five-digit code. Panelists used a descriptive scorecard with a 5-point intensity scale, where 1 indicated the lowest intensity and 5 indicated the highest, to evaluate each cheese sample based on appearance, aroma, flavor, texture, and stretchability. In this study, aroma refers to volatile-derived olfactory sensations perceived orthonasally (by smelling) and retronasally (during consumption), whereas flavor refers to the overall integrated perception of aroma, basic taste qualities (sweet, salty, sour, bitter, umami), and mouthfeel. Panel training (two days) included exposure to reference aroma and taste standards and calibration of the descriptors. For aroma assessment, panelists first performed orthonasal sniffing of coded cheese samples presented in covered cups, and then proceeded to taste the samples to assess flavor and other sensory attributes using the same 5-point intensity scale. Between samples, panelists cleansed their palates with water and unsalted crackers. All evaluations were performed under controlled lighting and temperature conditions and in a randomized order to minimize bias. Overall acceptability was also assessed, as these parameters are considered crucial factors in traditional bread acceptance.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was conducted using the SPSS software. The studies were carried out in triplicate to analyze the physicochemical properties of the cheeses, while sensory quality was evaluated by nine panelists. Data on physicochemical properties, sensory evaluations, and texture were studied using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). The mean values of the triplicates, along with their standard deviation (± SD).

The coagulating properties of milk significantly impact cheese yield. When comparing cheese yield based on the kind of coagulant utilized, cheese made with calf rennet demonstrates a higher yield compared to cheese made with pig rennet. Texture profile analysis

The effect of different rennet sources on cheese texture was evaluated through texture profile analysis, with mean values and standard deviations of texture profile analysis parameters presented in Table. 2. Cheese texture is primarily influenced by its composition, structure, and intermolecular forces. The cheese underwent analysis for hardness, springiness, cohesiveness, gumminess, and chewiness. Springiness refers to how quickly a product returns to its original shape after deformation, while cohesiveness measures its ability to withstand further deformation compared to its response to initial deformation. Hardness indicates the force needed to achieve a specific deformation. The cheese produced with pig rennet was significantly harder than those made with calf rennet (p < 0.05), which can be attributed to the lower moisture retention observed in physicochemical analysis. This higher hardness directly impacted the sensory evaluation, where panelists reported a firmer and less creamy mouth feel for pig rennet cheese compared to calf rennet cheese. Similarly, cheeses made with microbial rennet exhibited higher gumminess and chewiness, which was reflected in the lower stretchability scores in sensory analysis (Fig. 2). These findings suggest that microstructural differences, particularly in the casein network, influence the texture perception, a mechanism supported by microstructural and molecular-interaction (Wei et al., 2024). Microbial rennet and acetic acid exhibited intermediate hardness. Cheeses coagulated with animal rennet exhibited the lowest gumminess, whereas cheeses made with microbial rennet samples showed the highest gumminess. Chewiness showed an opposite trend to hardness, with the highest values found in cheeses coagulated with microbial and organic acid and the lowest in those using animal coagulants. Cheese made with calf and pig rennet showed the lower springiness values, followed by cheeses coagulated with organic acid and microbial rennet, whereas microbial rennet cheese showed higher values. Sensory analysis of cheeses

The sensory characteristics of cheese are influenced by its physicochemical properties, which are essential for identifying and describing its distinctive attributes. Quantitative descriptive analysis (QDA) was employed to evaluate the sensory attributes of cheeses made from different sources of rennet: calf, pig, microbial, and acetic acid. The sensory evaluation, depicted in Fig. 2, revealed that the appearance, taste, flavor, texture, stretchability, and overall acceptability of all cheese products were significantly affected by the coagulants. A sensory evaluation was conducted by a trained panel of nine members (n = 9), consisting of five males and four females. Panelists were selected based on prior experience in dairy product evaluation. A structured two-day training session was conducted to standardize sensory perception. Training included exposure to reference cheese samples, calibration of sensory descriptors, and practice evaluations using a 5-point intensity scale (1 = very low, 5 = very high). Attributes assessed included appearance, aroma (volatile-derived olfactory sensation from orthonasal and retronasal perception), flavor (overall perception integrating aroma, basic tastes, and mouthfeel), texture, and stretchability. Assessments were performed under controlled ISO 8589-compliant conditions to minimize external bias. Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (n = 9), with significant differences indicated at p < 0.05 (**p < 0.001). Milk coagulation rennet (enzymes) is characterized by high specific caseinolytic activity and low general proteolytic activity, as proteolysis strongly impacts cheese sensory properties. A significant effect of calf rennet on the aroma and pungent aroma of pig rennet cheese was observed, the impact of acetic acid on these aroma attributes was less pronounced. Panelists noted that cheeses produced with pig rennet were generally harder than those made with calf rennet.

Mouth-feel and flavor were significantly affected across all types of cheeses. Cheese made with pig rennet was perceived as more sour than calf rennet, suggesting that calf rennet cheese is more suitable for soft and spreadable cheese preparation. A previous study found that cheese produced with pig rennet exhibited less bitterness compared to cheese obtained from calf rennet (Suzzi et al., 2015). The bitterness in cheeses is linked to hydrophobic peptides produced by the proteolytic action of chymosin and starter cultures. Therefore, the reduced bitterness in cheeses prepared with pig rennet may be attributed to the higher aminopeptidase activity of non-starter microorganisms, which helps reduce the intensity of bitterness (Tofalo et al., 2015). The pronounced fresh flavor of cheeses made with calf rennet could be attributed to slower volatile evolution over time. Similarly, Hernández et al. explored the effects of lipolysis and proteolysis on sensory analysis for Idiazabal cheese produced from ewe's raw milk. A previous study on goat's milk cheese found that those coagulated with vegetal rennet were less granular and had a creamier texture compared to those made with animal rennet. However, other researchers observed no statistically significant differences in the texture parameters of goat cheese when using vegetal coagulant, microbial coagulant, and calf rennet. Future studies will focus on comparing the effect of ripening on cheeses prepared with pig rennet versus calf rennet.

FT-IR spectroscopy analysis

In cow milk cheese, the casein matrix structure is maintained through a series of interactions, including major hydrophobic interactions, electrostatic interactions, and hydrogen bonds (Foegeding & Drake, 2007). The FT-IR spectra of cheese can reveal changes in the intermolecular interactions of cow milk cheese, specifically by examining the characteristic absorption peaks of protein and fat functional groups. The FT-IR spectra of cow milk cheese made with different coagulant sources show four significant absorption peaks at 3440, 2930, 2840, and 1730 cm−1. Notably, the peak at 3440 cm−1 is more pronounced in acetic acid and microbial cheese compared to others. According to (Yang et al., 2022), this peak shifts rightward from 3500 cm−1, indicating an increase in intermolecular hydrogen bonds. Consequently, hydrogen bonds in the complex formation might weaken, allowing stronger electrostatic interactions to dominate in cheeses. The absorption peaks at 2930 and 2840 cm−1 are associated with the ν(C—H) stretching vibrations of the methyl (−CH3) and methylene (−CH2) groups in proteins and lipids (Ong et al., 2020). Rennet structure and its role in cheese differentiation

Enzymes are proteins produced by living organisms that catalyze chemical reactions with remarkable efficiency in an environmentally friendly manner. Compared with chemical catalysts, enzymes offer significant advantages such as high specificity and catalytic activity.

Rennet (enzyme) is a complex mixture of proteolytic and lipolytic enzymes, including chymosin, pepsin, and lipase. Its primary role in cheesemaking is to cleave κ-casein, destabilize casein micelles, and initiate curd formation (; Fox & McSweeney, 2017). The structural properties of the enzymes present in different rennet sources largely determine their composition, substrate specificity, and consequent impact on cheese quality. Although both belong to the aspartic proteases family (EC 3.4.23), characterized by the presence of two highly conserved aspartic acid residues in the bilobal active site, their amino acid composition and active site configuration differ, influencing their catalytic activity and cheesemaking performance (Yegin et al., 2011).

Calf rennet is dominated by chymosin, an aspartic protease of approximately 323 amino acids. It contains two essential aspartic acid residues at its catalytic site and demonstrates high substrate specificity. It selectively hydrolyzes the Phe105-Met106 bond in κ-casein, releasing glycomacropeptide and producing a fine, continuous casein network that results in firm curds with consistent texture (Fox et al., 2017). Porcine rennet, by contrast, contains higher proportions of pepsin (∼326 amino acids), which shares 60 % structural similarity with calf rennet but differs in its substrate specificity and exhibits lower cleavage specificity (Tang & Wong, 1987). This broader proteolytic activity extends to κ-, αs-, and β-caseins, leading to faster proteolysis and producing curds. This activity can also lead to the development of bitter flavors due to the release of small peptides (Jacob et al., 2011). Microbial rennet derived from Mucor miehei is also an aspartic protease, but it differs in substrate-binding regions and cleavage-site preferences compared to calf chymosin, which alters peptide profiles and contributes to variations in texture and sensory perception (Güler et al., 2021). In contrast, acid coagulation relies on reducing milk pH to ∼4.6, the isoelectric point of casein, leading to micelle precipitation without enzymatic κ-casein cleavage. The curd thus formed is weaker and exhibits higher syneresis than enzymatically induced gels. These structural and mechanistic distinctions among calf, porcine, microbial, and acid coagulants provide a clear biochemical explanation for the observed physicochemical, textural, and sensory differences in cheese samples.

Conclusion

This study investigated the impact of various coagulants, including enzymatic (calf, pig, and microbial coagulants) and acetic acid, on the physicochemical properties, texture, color, and microstructure characterization, sensory quality, and coagulation behavior of unripened cow milk cheeses vary significantly depending on the source of enzymes used in their production. The findings reveal significant differences in cheese properties depending on the type of enzyme used for coagulation. Different enzymes led to variations in protein and fat content, moisture levels, texture parameters such as hardness, gumminess, adhesiveness, and springiness, as well as color attributes. Microstructural analysis showed distinct arrangements of casein micelles and fat globules in cheeses produced with different enzymes. These results emphasize the importance of selecting appropriate enzymes in cheese production to achieve the desired quality attributes. Comparing studies on the effects of different coagulants in the scientific literature can be challenging due to variations in coagulation measurement units. Regardless, cheese labels should provide comprehensive information, including details about the type and source of the coagulant, enabling consumers to make informed purchasing decisions. However, further research is needed to examine the time stability and shelf life of these cheeses to ensure their acceptance in the market.
