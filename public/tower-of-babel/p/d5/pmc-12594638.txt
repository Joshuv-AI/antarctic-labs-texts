Epidemiology of acute myeloid leukemia in children and adolescents (1990–2021): a global burden of disease study
Authors: Fan Jia, Yuan Li, Tingting Hao, Guoqian Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute myeloid leukemia (AML) remains a significant global health concern in children, though its epidemiological landscape has markedly changed over recent decades. Understanding these evolving trends can guide targeted healthcare strategies and resource allocation. Utilizing data from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) database spanning 1990–2021, we analyzed global, regional, and national trends in AML incidence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) among children and adolescents. We applied annual percentage change (APC), estimated annual percentage change (EAPC), and correlations with the socio-demographic index (SDI) to quantify trends and influencing factors. From 1990 to 2021, global childhood AML incidence decreased by 37%, with mortality and DALYs declining by 40% and 42%, respectively. The most pronounced reductions occurred in children under 5 years of age. Substantial disparities emerged across SDI regions, with the high-middle SDI group exhibiting the sharpest declines in incidence (EAPC = − 2.96%), mortality (–3.44%), and DALYs (–3.51%). In contrast, the low SDI region saw mortality rise by 8% and DALYs increase by 5%. South Asia carried the highest absolute disease burden, while Oceania recorded the highest population-adjusted rates. Nationally, China reported the greatest incidence and India the highest mortality, despite both countries achieving significant reductions. Despite aggregate gains, childhood AML burden remains concentrated in lower-SDI settings, where exposure-related attribution is rising and outcome improvements lag. Introduction

Childhood cancer remains the leading cause of death among children and adolescents in high-income countries (Kyu et al. 2018; Dang-Tan et al. 2007), with acute leukemia (AL) accounting for more than one-third of all childhood cancers (Ssenyonga et al. 2022). AL is primarily categorized into acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) and acute myeloid leukemia (AML), with ALL constituting approximately 80% and AML 15%−20% (Ward et al. 2014; Seth et al. 2015). These two subtypes differ significantly in their pathophysiology, treatment strategies, and prognosis, with AML being particularly challenging and one of the deadliest malignancies in children (Inaba et al. 2021).

AML is a clonal malignant disorder originating in the bone marrow (Papaemmanuil et al. 2016), characterized by abnormal proliferation and differentiation arrest of progenitor cells, leading to impaired hematopoiesis. In severe cases, this results in life-threatening cytopenias and transfusion dependence (DiNardo et al. 2023). The clinical presentation and genetic background of AML are highly heterogeneous, further complicating treatment. Although intensive chemotherapy achieves a complete remission rate of approximately 80% after induction therapy, about 20% of patients relapse (Teixeira et al. 2023). The 5-year survival rate for AML has improved from historically low levels to 33%−78% (Bonaventure et al. 2017; Creutzig et al. 2012), but relapse and the persistence of leukemia stem cells (LSCs) remain significant challenges in treatment (Voso et al. 2019). Even with consolidation therapy, over 50% of patients remain at risk for relapse (Ramos et al. 2015; Shao et al. 2023; Damiani et al. 2022). For children aged 5 years and older, AML has become the second most prevalent cancer after ALL (Ni et al. 2022). Although previous studies have examined the national or regional burden of AML (Zhou et al. 2024), no comprehensive analysis has yet evaluated pediatric AML trends and disparities across countries, regions, and Socio-demographic Index (SDI) strata over three decades using standardized methods. Addressing this gap could inform targeted interventions and strengthen health systems. The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) database offers a unique resource for such an assessment, providing harmonized data across 204 countries and territories from 1990 to 2021. Leveraging these data, we systematically analyzed incidence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) due to AML among individuals aged ≤ 20 years. Our findings reveal significant disparities in the AML burden across different regions, countries, and economic levels, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where higher incidence and mortality rates prevail. These disparities underscore the profound impact of unequal distribution of healthcare resources and differences in public health policies on the global AML burden. We also emphasize the importance of targeted preventive measures for specific populations and regions, particularly in low-income countries, where improving early diagnosis, enhancing treatment options, and increasing public health investment may be key to reducing the AML burden.

Methods

Overview and data collection

Widely regarded as one of the most comprehensive and systematic global epidemiological initiatives, the GBD data source is spearheaded by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation at the University of Washington. Its primary goal is to quantify the health losses attributable to a broad array of diseases, injuries, and risk factors. Through the GBD framework, researchers are enabled to perform comparative evaluations of incidence and mortality at national, regional, and global scales (Ferrari et al. 2024). For this study, data on AML were extracted from GBD 2021, using International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes (ICD-10: C92.0–C92.9; ICD-9: 205.0–205.9) to identify AML cases. In these analyses, three principal metrics, mortality, incidence, and DALYs, are employed to capture the overall disease burden. The corresponding data can be accessed at: https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results/. Race- and ethnicity-specific analyses could not be conducted because these parameters are not reported in the GBD database. This cross-sectional investigation, which examines disease trends over time and across regions, does not include personally identifiable information. Consequently, the Ethics Committee of Kunming Yan’an Hospital granted a waiver of informed consent. The study was conducted in full compliance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines (Elm et al. 2008).

Age group categorization

Sociodemographic index

The SDI is a composite metric that reflects the level of socio-economic development in a given country or region, encompassing factors such as economic structure and scale, educational attainment, living standards, and social welfare (Kocarnik et al. 2022). The SDI ranges from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating greater socio-economic advancement. According to the GBD database, countries and regions are categorized into five SDI tiers—low, low-middle, middle, high-middle, and high—thereby enabling investigations into how socio-economic indicators and geographic disparities shape the burden of childhood AML.

Statistical analysis

Drawing on data from the GBD database, we first calculated incidence, mortality, and disability rates per 100,000 population, along with their 95% uncertainty intervals (UI) (Collaborators 2015). The Joinpoint regression model was employed to determine the annual percent change (APC) and its 95% confidence interval (CI) to assess trends within each independent time segment (Liang et al. 2025). To assess temporal trends in childhood AML incidence, mortality, and DALYs from 1990 to 2021, we employed a log-transformed linear regression model to estimate the average annual percentage change (EAPC) and its CI (Ding et al. 2022). A trend was classified as significantly increasing if both the EAPC and its lower 95% CI exceeded zero or decreasing if the EAPC and upper 95% CI were below zero. All statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.3.3, with significance set at p < 0.05.

Results

Acute myeloid leukemia in children: global trends

Incidence

Our analysis reveals significant dynamic changes in the global epidemiological patterns of childhood AML, demonstrating a consistent downward trajectory in incidence rates. Notably, the APC reached its lowest between 2019 and 2021 at − 4.49% (95% CI, − 5.16% to − 3.81%) (Fig. 1A). In 2021, an estimated 11,516 (95% UI, 8,442.72–15,048.57) new childhood AML cases were reported globally, representing a 37.29% reduction (95% UI, − 62.00% to − 8.55%) compared to 1990. Correspondingly, the global incidence rate per 100,000 population declined from 0.81 (95% UI, 0.50–1.39) in 1990 to 0.44 (95% UI, 0.32–0.57) in 2021, yielding an EAPC of − 1.99 (95% CI, − 2.05 to − 1.93) (Table 1).

Mortality

Consistent with incidence trends, childhood AML-associated mortality exhibited a pronounced decline over the past three decades. The APC between 2019 and 2021 marked the lowest rate observed, at − 4.60% (95% CI, − 5.28% to − 3.92%) (Fig. 1B). From 1990 to 2021, AML-related deaths in children decreased by approximately 40.00% (95% UI, − 63.95% to − 13.78%), with an estimated 10,443 deaths (95% UI, 7,575.97–13,722.20) in 2021. The mortality rate per 100,000 population decreased substantially, from 0.78 (95% UI, 0.47–1.34) in 1990 to 0.40 (95% UI, 0.29–0.52) in 2021, corresponding to an EAPC of − 2.16 (95% CI, − 2.22 to − 2.10), highlighting sustained improvements in survival outcomes (Table 2). AML-related mortality rates have similarly declined across all age groups, with the greatest reduction in children younger than 5 years (61.36%, 95%UI 37.62%–78.12%) and the least reduction among adolescents aged 15–19 years (32.60%, 95%UI 12.93%–51.57%) (Fig. 2B). Gender disparities revealed that girls under 5 years old experienced lower AML-related mortality rates compared to boys, while among adolescents aged 15–19 years, boys showed lower mortality rates than girls (Fig. 2B).

DALYs

Aligned with incidence and mortality trends, DALYs attributable to childhood AML have markedly declined over the past three decades. The lowest APC was observed between 2019 and 2021, at − 4.85% (95% CI, − 5.50% to − 4.20%) (Fig. 1C). In 2021, global DALYs associated with childhood AML were estimated at 843,193 (95% UI, 612,150.83–1,109,402.14), representing a 41.75% reduction (95% UI, − 64.99% to − 14.81%) since 1990. Similarly, the DALY rate per 100,000 population decreased from 64.10 (95% UI, 38.40–112.11) in 1990 to 31.99 (95% UI, 23.22–42.09) in 2021. The corresponding EAPC was − 2.22 (95% CI, − 2.28 to − 2.15), underscoring the global progress achieved in reducing the overall burden of childhood AML (Table S1). Moreover, DALYs associated with AML have decreased across all age categories, again most significantly among children under 5 years (61.32%, 95%UI 37.6%−78.09%) and least among those aged 15–19 years (32.58%, 95%UI 12.90%−51.55%) (Fig. 2C). Consistent gender patterns were observed, with girls younger than 5 years demonstrating lower AML-related DALY rates than boys, whereas among adolescents aged 15–19 years, boys exhibited lower DALY rates compared to girls (Fig. 2C).

Acute myeloid leukemia in children: sdi regional trends

Incidence

Among the five SDI regions, the Middle SDI region recorded the highest absolute number of childhood AML cases both in 1990 (7,367; 95% UI 4,291.54–12,665.52) and in 2021 (3,706.94; 95% UI 2,760.06–5,050.35), despite a 49.68% (95% UI − 72.20% to − 22.31%) decrease over this period. The largest decline in incidence rates was observed in the High-middle SDI region, with an EAPC of − 2.96 (95% CI − 3.10 to − 2.81) (Table 1 and Fig. S1A).

Mortality

Only the Low SDI region exhibited an increase (48.76%) in childhood AML-related deaths between 1990 and 2021, whereas the Middle SDI region consistently had the highest number of deaths (3,374.79; 95% UI 2,512.80–4,609.46 in 2021). Of the remaining four SDI regions, the High-middle SDI region recorded the steepest decline (− 69.86%) in deaths from 1990 to 2021, whereas the High SDI region had the fewest AML-related deaths in 2021 (650; 95% UI 576.49–698.73). In terms of mortality rate per 100,000, the Middle SDI region was highest in 2021 (0.45; 95% UI 0.34–0.62), while the High SDI region was lowest (0.28; 95% UI 0.25–0.30). The largest annual percentage reduction in mortality rate occurred in the High-middle SDI region (EAPC − 3.44; 95% CI − 3.61 to − 3.27) (Table 1 and Fig S1B).

DALYs

In 2021, the greatest number of childhood AML-related DALYs was found in the Middle SDI region (269,448; 95% UI 199,811.23–368,545.30), representing a 54.24% (95% UI − 75.04% to − 28.90%) decrease from 1990. The High-middle SDI region experienced the most pronounced decline in DALY rates (EAPC − 3.51; 95% CI − 3.67 to − 3.35) over the study period (Table S1 and Fig. S1C).

The observed increase in childhood AML-related mortality and DALYs in low SDI regions likely reflects systemic disparities in healthcare infrastructure, access to early and accurate diagnostics, and availability of effective treatment modalities. In many low SDI countries, delayed diagnosis due to limited awareness and weak referral networks can result in more advanced disease at presentation. Moreover, resource constraints often lead to gaps in the timely administration of curative therapies, shortages of supportive care (such as antibiotics, blood products, or intensive care), and insufficient access to novel treatments or clinical trials. These factors may collectively contribute to persistently higher mortality and DALY burdens in low SDI settings despite overall global progress. Strengthening pediatric cancer registries and improving healthcare delivery systems are essential to address these inequalities.

Acute myeloid leukemia in children: geographic regional trends

Incidence

Of the 21 GBD regions, South Asia had the highest number of childhood AML cases in 2021 (2,688; 95% UI 1,825.56–3,719.64), whereas Australasia had the fewest (40; 95% UI 33.66–48.58). Oceania recorded the highest incidence rate in 2021, and Western Sub-Saharan Africa the lowest. Against the global mean SDI of 0.67 in 2021, 11 regions (e.g., the Caribbean, Australasia) showed incidence rates above the global average (0.44 per 100,000), whereas 10 regions (e.g., Eastern Europe, South Asia) fell below(Table 1 and Fig. S2A).

Mortality

South Asia also had the largest number of AML-related childhood deaths in 2021 (2,587; 95% UI 1,762.52–3,573.37), while Australasia had the fewest (19.78; 95% UI 17.42–22.59). Oceania reported the highest mortality rate (1.11 per 100,000; 95% UI 0.64–1.61), in contrast to Western Sub-Saharan Africa, with the lowest (0.17 per 100,000; 95% UI 0.07–0.28). Between 1990 and 2021, Oceania exhibited the smallest decline in mortality rate (EAPC − 0.16; 95% CI − 0.26 to − 0.07), whereas East Asia showed the largest (EAPC − 4.30; 95% CI − 4.56 to − 4.04). In 2021, Oceania and Western Sub-Saharan Africa had the highest and lowest mortality rates, respectively (Table 2 and Fig S2B).

DALYs

South Asia bore the highest childhood AML-related DALYs in 2021 (209,621; 95% UI 142,699.73–289,533.65), while Australasia recorded the lowest (1,577; 95% UI 1,390.49–1,805.72). Oceania had the highest DALY rate (89.74 per 100,000; 95% UI 50.58–130.16), and Western Sub-Saharan Africa the lowest (14.94 per 100,000; 95% UI 6.27–24.54). From 1990 to 2021, the DALY rate in Oceania declined the least (EAPC − 0.15; 95% CI − 0.25 to − 0.05), whereas East Asia experienced the steepest drop (EAPC − 4.40; 95% CI − 4.64 to − 4.15). Of the 21 GBD regions, 10 regions showed DALY rates above the global average of 31.99 per 100,000 in 2021, while 11 regions were below this threshold(Table S1 and Fig. S2C).

In such contexts, children may present with more advanced disease and experience higher mortality or longer periods of disability, thereby elevating the DALY burden even as incidence rates fall. Strengthening early detection programs and improving access to high-quality care are critical to addressing these persistent health disparities.

From 1990 to 2021, the proportion of deaths from AML attributable to environmental and occupational risk factors exhibited distinct temporal patterns across different SDI strata (Global, High, High-middle, Middle, Low-middle, and Low SDI). Discussion

AML is a rapidly progressing hematological malignancy characterized by frequent relapse and therapy resistance, necessitating a multifaceted diagnostic approach that includes morphological, immunophenotypic, cytogenetic, and molecular evaluations (Herrity et al. 2023; Haferlach et al. 2020; Grob et al. 2023; Heuser et al. 2023). Standard treatment, comprising chemotherapy and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, is often complicated by adverse events such as infection, anemia, and myelosuppression, which not only compromise the quality of life but also escalate treatment complexity and healthcare costs (Forsberg et al. 2024). Disease etiology involves genetic, environmental, and lifestyle factors, with exposure to particular chemicals, drugs, and ionizing radiation recognized as potential contributors (Zhang et al. 2018). Moreover, long-term smoking, high body mass index, unhealthy diets, and sedentary behavior have each been implicated in increased AML risk (Li et al. 2017; Bi et al. 2022; Krug et al. 2020). This growing global burden of AML varies markedly across regions and socioeconomic levels, fueling widespread discussion regarding its prevention and control strategies.

Taken together, the sustained global declines in childhood AML incidence, mortality, and DALYs mask widening inequities aligned with sociodemographic development. Our SDI-stratified EAPCs align with international registry comparisons showing faster survival gains in higher-resource settings, while the slower, and in some cases adverse, trends in low-SDI regions are consistent with gaps in timely diagnosis, supportive care, and access to definitive therapy. Our analysis contributes three advances: (i) a child-specific, three-decade global view using harmonized EAPCs, (ii) systematic SDI and geographic disaggregation to localize disparities, and (iii) GBD-based risk-attribution trends that reveal divergent environmental/occupational exposures across SDI strata. These findings together indicate that improvements in AML outcomes are attainable but unevenly realized, underscoring the need for context-specific system strengthening.

Global health outcomes have improved substantially over the past decades, yet our analysis reveals persistent disparities between countries and regions. Overall life expectancy has risen markedly (from about 67 years in 2000 to 73 years in 2019 globally), but progress has been uneven (Liao et al. 2024). Many low-income countries (LICs) continue to lag, due in part to weaker health systems, fewer resources, and socio-political instability. Inadequate healthcare investment and poor quality of care are major contributors to these gaps. A large proportion of premature deaths in LICs are considered avoidable with timely, effective healthcare – an estimated 5 million deaths per year are attributed to suboptimal quality of care, with an additional 3.6 million due to lack of access (Seward et al. 2021). Consistent with these findings, about 4.5 billion people worldwide were not fully covered by essential health services in 2021, underscoring the vast unmet need in healthcare provision (Bigirinama et al. 2024). Such deficiencies help explain why countries with low health expenditures and frail systems see worse health indicators. Conversely, nations that have heavily invested in universal health coverage and quality improvement tend to exhibit better outcomes, as strong primary care and hospital services reduce amenable mortality (Macinko et al. 2019).

Another critical driver of disparity is the presence of conflict and instability. Countries afflicted by war or civil strife often experience a reversal of health gains, as violence and disorder devastate infrastructure and disrupt services. Armed conflicts directly destroy healthcare facilities, decimate the health workforce, and restrict access to care, leading to sharp increases in mortality (Ajenifuja et al. 2018; Akik et al. 2020). For example, severe national conflicts in sub-Saharan Africa have been shown to cut life expectancy by more than two years on average, accompanied by significant rises in infant mortality (Akik et al. 2020). Indeed, during the recent Tigray conflict in Ethiopia, health service delivery “decreased to a minimum,” leaving large portions of the population without basic care (David et al. 2025). Empirical studies broadly confirm that higher conflict intensity is associated with elevated maternal, child, and all-cause mortality rates in affected areas (Garry et al. 2020; Jawad et al. 2020). These findings highlight how war, political instability, and humanitarian crises can stall or even reverse decades of health progress. Overall, the world remains off-track to achieve equitable health for all by 2030, as recent years have seen stagnation in health service coverage gains (Lugten et al. 2022). Without addressing the above structural issues, from health system underinvestment to conflict, global disparities are likely to persist.

In light of these findings, there is a pressing need for specific and targeted approaches to AML prevention and treatment in resource-limited settings. Efforts could include strengthening healthcare infrastructure (e.g., building specialized pediatric oncology centers, providing advanced training to healthcare providers, and improving laboratory diagnostics), expanding access to essential medications and novel therapeutic agents, and enhancing patient follow-up to detect recurrence or complications early. Additionally, fostering international collaborations, such as multi-center clinical trials or global health partnerships, could facilitate knowledge exchange and accelerate the implementation of successful treatment protocols in lower-SDI regions.

Overall, our findings underscore both the progress made and the persisting challenges in reducing the global burden of pediatric AML. Through concerted efforts to strengthen healthcare systems, ensure equitable treatment access, and develop innovative therapies, it may be possible to sustain the downward trend in AML incidence and mortality and improve outcomes for children worldwide.

Prospect

Given the SDI-linked disparities observed, the promise of precision therapeutics and immunotherapy for pediatric AML will only translate into population-level gains if equitable pathways for molecular diagnostics, clinical trial participation, and affordable access are developed across resource settings. Looking forward, it will be essential to invest in precision medicine and immunotherapy research to further improve survival and quality of life for pediatric AML patients worldwide. Enhanced genomic profiling and targeted therapies, alongside robust supportive care, hold promise for reducing both short-term mortality and long-term sequelae. By coupling these scientific advancements with equitable resource allocation, we can more effectively bridge the gap between high- and low-SDI countries, ultimately mitigating the long-term global burden of pediatric AML.

Limitations

Our findings should be interpreted in light of data and modeling constraints. The availability and quality of childhood cancer data vary widely across countries; in many low-income settings, sparse civil registration and cause-of-death information necessitate model-based estimation, which may imperfectly recover true patterns. Changes in diagnostic practice over time and under-ascertainment can also influence trends. These issues are partly mitigated by the GBD’s standardized estimation and bias-adjustment procedures, but residual uncertainty remains, especially for small absolute counts and for countries with limited primary data. When interpreting our results, it is crucial to acknowledge limitations in the underlying data and the modeled nature of GBD estimates. Health data quality and availability vary greatly across countries, and in many LICs, reliable data are sparse. In numerous low-income countries, there is an absence of comprehensive civil registration and vital statistics systems, which forces a reliance on indirect methods (like verbal autopsy surveys) and statistical models to estimate mortality and cause-of-death patterns (Coates et al. 2021). The GBD study employs complex computational models (e.g., Bayesian statistical methods and predictive covariates) to generate health estimates in these data-poor environments (Lazarus et al. 2022). In summary, some of the between-country differences we discuss may partly reflect data limitations rather than true differences. Caution is warranted, especially for LIC estimates that rely heavily on modelling. Despite these caveats, the GBD methodology adheres to standardized protocols and uses various statistical techniques to adjust for biases and improve data coverage, lending a degree of credibility to the broad trends. We have high confidence in the major patterns identified (e.g., the large gap between high-income and low-income regions), but smaller changes or rankings should be interpreted with mindfulness of the underlying data quality constraints.

Conclusion

From 1990 to 2021, global incidence, mortality, and DALYs related to childhood AML have declined significantly, yet substantial disparities persist across regions, SDI tiers, and age groups. Middle SDI regions continue to carry the highest absolute burden, while high-middle SDI regions have achieved the greatest reductions, reflecting the benefits of targeted interventions and strengthened health systems. In contrast, rising mortality and DALY rates in low SDI regions, and persistently high burdens in South Asia and Oceania, signal areas of critical concern requiring immediate and focused global attention. Children under 5 years in low-SDI countries remain especially vulnerable, often facing delayed diagnosis and limited access to effective treatment.

To further reduce the global burden of childhood AML, urgent policy action is needed. This includes prioritizing resource allocation and health system strengthening in low-SDI and high-burden regions, expanding early diagnosis and screening programs, ensuring equitable access to modern therapies, and addressing environmental and occupational risk exposures. Enhanced investment in cancer registries, capacity-building for healthcare workers, and international collaboration will be essential for narrowing disparities and accelerating progress toward health equity. Only through such comprehensive, targeted strategies can we continue the momentum and strive to eliminate preventable childhood AML deaths worldwide.
