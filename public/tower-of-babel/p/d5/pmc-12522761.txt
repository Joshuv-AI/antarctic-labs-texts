Rare double primary carcinoma in ileocecal region: cecal tubular adenocarcinoma combined with appendiceal goblet cell adenocarcinoma

Background

Appendiceal goblet cell adenocarcinoma (GCA) is uncommon, accounting for less than 5% of primary appendiceal tumors [1]. GCA is a rare appendiceal malignancy with features of both neuroendocrine tumors and adenocarcinomas [2], but its behavior is more akin to adenocarcinoma [3]. GCA is composed of goblet cells, along with varying numbers of endocrine cells and Paneth-like cells. Due to its rarity, GCA often presents with non-specific symptoms such as abdominal pain or, in advanced stages, abdominal masses, bowel obstruction, or ascites. It is typically incidentally detected during appendectomy for acute appendicitis or during other surgical procedures such as colectomy. The rarity of this case is that appendiceal GCA is combined with tubular adenocarcinoma of cecum. According to our search scope, there are two similar cases reported in the literature [4, 5]. This report aims to provide new insights into the diagnosis and management of this complex condition.

In clinical practice, timely and accurate diagnosis is crucial for patients, as it directly impacts prognosis and treatment strategies. This report details a rare case of primary cecal tubular adenocarcinoma combined with appendiceal goblet cell adenocarcinoma, analyzing its clinical features, imaging, cytology, histological morphology, immunohistochemistry, and differential diagnosis to enhance understanding of this rare disease.

Case demonstration

An 86-year-old male presented with a 2-month history of abdominal pain and diarrhea. The pain was intermittent, and the diarrhea was characterized by loose stools without tenesmus. The pain was relieved after defecation. There were no symptoms of chills, fever, nausea, vomiting, or urinary complaints. Computed tomography(CT) at an outside hospital revealed hepatic cystic lesions and thickening of the ascending colon wall. The patient was admitted for further evaluation and treatment. Physical examination revealed no significant tenderness, rebound tenderness, or rigidity, and no masses were palpable.

Abdominal and pelvic CT scans showed irregular thickening of the ascending colon wall with markedly inhomogeneous enhancement, enlarged surrounding lymph nodes, and blurred fat planes. The imaging suggested ascending colon cancer. Since the patient’s symptoms were primarily gastrointestinal, a preoperative chest CT scan was not performed, and therefore the pulmonary status was not assessed prior to surgery. Tumor markers revealed elevated carbohydrate antigen 19 − 9 (58 U/mL), carcinoembryonic antigen (254.6 µg/L), and ferritin (57.0 µg/L).

Painless colonoscopy revealed: (1) suspected carcinoma in the transverse colon? (2) multiple colorectal polyps (Yamada type I-III) and a laterally spreading tumor (LST) in the transverse colon. Biopsy pathology indicated adenocarcinoma, and confirmed the presence of multiple polyps (Yamada type I-III) and an LST in the transverse colon. Due to intestinal stricture and obstruction, the advancement of the colonoscope was limited, preventing a complete total colonoscopy. This led to an inaccuracy in determining the definitive location of the lesion. Consequently, the biopsy results could only provide the histological nature of the tumor, while its precise anatomical location relied on intraoperative exploration and examination of the resected specimen. Integrating the patient’s imaging findings and clinical presentation, the overall preoperative assessment still favored a right-sided colon cancer; however, the appendiceal lesion was not identified.

Given the presence of intestinal obstruction and clear surgical indications, the patient underwent laparoscopic right hemicolectomy with mesenteric lymph node dissection and ileocolic anastomosis. Intraoperative exploration confirmed a tumor located in the cecum with involvement of the appendix, penetrating the serosa and invading the adjacent peritoneum, accompanied by multiple enlarged lymph nodes. The specimen was subsequently submitted for pathological examination.

In conclusion, the preoperative CT results were consistent with the intraoperative exploration results. The intestinal endoscopy was limited by technical limitations and caused some deviation in positioning. The final diagnosis was based on the intraoperative findings and pathological results.

Gross examination: In the ileocecal region, an ulcerative mass measuring 58 × 43 × 34 mm was identified. The cut surface of the mass was grayish-white, solid, and of intermediate consistency, invading the entire thickness of the bowel wall. Four polypoid masses, 5–10 mm in diameter, were noted around the tumor, with grayish-white, solid cut surfaces and intermediate consistency, and without distinct stalks. The appendix in the ileocecal region was 23 mm in length and 5–7 mm in diameter, with a smooth serosal surface and no obvious nodules. A total of 24 mesenteric lymph nodes were palpated, ranging in diameter from 3 to 7 mm.

Histologically, the cecal ulcerative mass revealed tubular adenocarcinoma(Figs. 1 and 2) with atypical glandular structures infiltrating into the muscularis propria of the bowel wall. The glands were oval or round in shape, with large, hyperchromatic nuclei that exhibited a vesicular appearance. The tumor cells showed significant cellular atypia and prominent mitotic activity. Some tumor cells exhibited mucinous differentiation.

Within the cecal wall near the base of the appendix and within the muscularis propria of the appendix, tumor cells(Figs. 3, 4 and 5) were arranged in glandular or nested patterns. These areas contained numerous typical goblet cells with abundant, lightly stained, vacuolated cytoplasm resembling intestinal goblet cells. The nuclei were predominantly basally located, round or oval in shape, hyperchromatic, and mitoses were rare. The tumor exhibited invasive growth, disrupting the normal appendiceal wall structure and infiltrating the entire appendiceal wall. In some areas, the tumor extended to the serosal surface, with glandular structures intermixed within the stroma, invading surrounding tissues. This is manifested as a distinct lesion characteristic different from that of cecal adenocarcinoma.

The two lesions exhibited significant differences in both histomorphology and immunophenotype, supporting the diagnosis of synchronous cecal adenocarcinoma and appendiceal goblet cell adenocarcinoma, rather than goblet cell differentiation of a single adenocarcinoma at the appendiceal base.

According to the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging system, the overall pathological stage of the cecal tubular adenocarcinoma was T4aN0M0, stage IIC, while the appendiceal goblet cell adenocarcinoma was staged as T4N0M0, stage IIB.

Based on the clinical history, pathological morphology, and immunohistochemistry, the patient was diagnosed with primary cecal tubular adenocarcinoma combined with appendiceal goblet cell adenocarcinoma.

According to the pathological diagnosis, adjuvant chemotherapy was recommended. However, considering the patient’s advanced age and the potential risks and complications of chemotherapy, the patient and family declined further treatment after detailed discussion. The patient was closely monitored with regular follow-up. At 7 months postoperatively, chest CT revealed a newly developed small nodule in the right upper lobe of the lung, with a well-defined margin, no spiculation, and no pleural effusion or mediastinal lymphadenopathy. In combination with the clinical history and tumor marker levels (CA19-9: 32 U/ml), this finding was considered consistent with pulmonary metastasis from right-sided colon cancer. Given the patient’s advanced age and the family’s refusal of invasive procedures, no biopsy of the pulmonary lesion was performed.

The patient was subsequently treated with a capecitabine plus bevacizumab regimen. Due to significant hand–foot syndrome and poor tolerance, the regimen was changed to raltitrexed plus bevacizumab. During follow-up, serial chest and abdominal CT scans demonstrated stability of the right upper lobe pulmonary nodule without evidence of enlargement or new lesions; the right anterior chest wall pleural nodule also remained stable, and no local recurrence was detected at the abdominal surgical site. After an additional 8 months of follow-up, the patient remained in good general condition.

Discussion

Appendiceal goblet cell adenocarcinoma (GCA) is a rare malignant tumor originating from the appendiceal mucosa [6]. It accounts for approximately 1% of gastrointestinal tumors and 5% of appendiceal tumors [7]. GCA is characterized by diffuse infiltration of the appendiceal wall, with or without serosal involvement, and may extend along the appendix to involve the cecum. GCA, composed of goblet cells, may also contain varying numbers of endocrine cells and Paneth-like cells. GCA is classified as a neuroendocrine tumor between carcinoid tumors and adenocarcinomas but exhibits behavior more similar to adenocarcinoma. In the latest WHO classification of digestive system tumors, appendiceal goblet cell carcinoid has been renamed appendiceal goblet cell adenocarcinoma and is categorized as a special type of adenocarcinoma [8]. The coexistence of GCA and cecal tubular adenocarcinoma is extremely rare, with only one previously reported case.

In the existing literature, cases of appendiceal GCA occurring synchronously with colorectal adenocarcinoma are extremely rare. As early as the report by Vincenti et al., the first case of synchronous appendiceal GCA and cecal adenocarcinoma was described, with discussion on whether the two represented distinct or related oncological entities [4]. More recently, Mao et al. reported another similar case in which the patient presented with severe anemia; no appendiceal lesion was identified preoperatively, and the diagnosis was ultimately confirmed by right hemicolectomy and pathological examination [5]. Compared with these cases, the present patient also manifested synchronous dual primary tumors, but the initial clinical presentation was intestinal obstruction, and discrepancies in preoperative localization were observed. Taken together, these reports and our case highlight that appendiceal GCA can occur synchronously with colorectal adenocarcinoma, that preoperative diagnosis remains challenging, and that definitive confirmation still relies on surgical and pathological evaluation.

The clinical presentation of GCA varies with tumor grade. Low-grade GCA often presents with symptoms of acute appendicitis, while high-grade GCA may present with symptoms related to metastasis, such as abdominal pain, bloating, and bowel obstruction [9, 10]. GCA grows rapidly, has a relatively favorable prognosis, but a high recurrence rate and potential for metastasis. Peritoneal dissemination is the most common metastatic pathway, and nearly half of female patients may develop ovarian metastasis. Peritoneal dissemination is also the most common cause of disease-specific death.

GCA predominantly affects adults, with a male-to-female ratio of approximately 1:2 and an average age of 50 years. Clinical symptoms include abdominal pain, bloating, and bowel obstruction. Due to the non-specific nature of these symptoms, GCA is often misdiagnosed as acute or chronic appendicitis, leading to delayed treatment.

Imaging studies typically show uniform or non-uniform thickening of the appendix, with ill-defined soft tissue masses in the appendiceal region, sometimes involving the terminal ileum. In cases of peritoneal metastasis, imaging may reveal low-density lesions in affected organs, as well as enlarged retroperitoneal or mesenteric lymph nodes. Imaging studies can suggest the presence of the disease, reduce the rate of missed diagnoses, and facilitate timely diagnosis and treatment.

Grossly, GCA may appear normal or slightly swollen, with a grayish-white, firm to hard texture, or as non-dilated mucinous nodules. There is often no distinct mass, and the borders with surrounding tissue are indistinct. Due to its non-specific gross appearance, GCA is easily overlooked or misdiagnosed as acute or chronic appendicitis based solely on gross examination.

Histologically, tumor cells are arranged in glandular or nested patterns, with abundant pale cytoplasm resembling intestinal goblet cells. The nuclei are basally located, round or oval, hyperchromatic, and mitoses are rare. The tumor exhibits invasive growth, destroying the normal appendiceal structure and infiltrating the entire appendiceal wall. In some areas, it may extend to the serosal surface, with glandular structures invading the surrounding tissue.

Immunophenotypically, tumor cells are positive for CK, CEA, CK20, CDX-2, and Ki-67 (约30%+). CK is a relatively reliable marker indicating an epithelial origin. Combined with CDX2, Syn, CgA, and Ki-67, the diagnosis can be further confirmed.

Molecular genetic studies indicate that GCA lacks mutations in genes associated with colorectal adenocarcinoma (TP53, KERAS, APC) but shares some genetic alterations with ileal carcinoids [11], such as loss of homologous chromosome 11q, 16q, and 8q. Recent studies have shown that GCA exhibits mutations in WNT signaling pathway genes, including USP9X, NOTCH1, CTNNA1, CTNNB1, and TRRAP. Mutations in chromatin remodeling genes, such as ARIDA, ARID2, KDM6A, and KMT2D, have also been identified. Research indicates that TP53, ARID1A, SMAD4, and KRAS are the most common mutations in GCA, appendiceal adenocarcinoma, and appendiceal neuroendocrine tumors [12].

Given the rarity of GCA and its potential for misdiagnosis, it is crucial to differentiate GCA from other tumors when it exhibits diverse histological features and varying proportions of adenocarcinoma and mucinous components.

Acute and Chronic Appendicitis: Early GCA may present with “appendicitis-like” symptoms. However, acute and chronic appendicitis are non-neoplastic inflammatory diseases. Histologically, they lack glandular or nested tumor structures and do not exhibit invasive growth. Immunohistochemistry, lesion characteristics, and histological features can be used to differentiate between GCA and appendicitis.

Carcinoid Tumors: GCA is characterized by prominent goblet cells and abundant mucin secretion, often with glandular structures and increased Ki-67. Syn, CgA, and CD56 may not be strongly positive. In contrast, carcinoid tumors exhibit nested or cord-like cell arrangements, with rich interstitial vasculature. The tumor cells are relatively uniform in size and shape, with scant cytoplasm, round nuclei, and inconspicuous nucleoli. They do not exhibit significant mucin secretion. Immunohistochemistry shows strong positivity for Syn, CgA, and CD56.

Signet Ring Cell Carcinoma: GCA may diffusely infiltrate the appendiceal wall as single cells or cell cords, resembling signet ring cell carcinoma. The key to differentiation lies in whether the appendiceal mucosa is destroyed and whether neuroendocrine markers are expressed. Signet ring cell carcinoma cells exhibit marked pleomorphism, often with a primary mucosal lesion, and are negative for neuroendocrine markers.

Mucinous Adenocarcinoma: Some GCAs produce large amounts of mucin forming mucin pools. However, mucin pools in GCA contain small clusters of goblet cells, and there is no pre-existing mucosal lesion. Neuroendocrine markers are positive. In contrast, mucinous adenocarcinoma also forms mucin pools, but the cells lack goblet cell features. Mucinous adenocarcinoma exhibits marked cellular atypia and mitoses, often with pre-existing in situ cancer, and does not express neuroendocrine markers.

Tubular Adenocarcinoma: GCA is composed of goblet cell-like tumor cells with abundant mucin and often expresses neuroendocrine markers. Tubular adenocarcinoma, on the other hand, is composed of colorectal-type tall columnar cells forming typical tubular structures. The cells have scant cytoplasm, marked atypia, and variable nuclear polarity. Tubular adenocarcinoma lacks neuroendocrine components.

Hereditary colorectal cancer syndromes: Although the patient was of advanced age and the immunohistochemical staining of the cecal tubular adenocarcinoma demonstrated intact expression of mismatch repair proteins (MLH1, PMS2, MSH2, and MSH6), indicating proficient MMR (pMMR) and substantially reducing the likelihood of Lynch syndrome while supporting a diagnosis of sporadic carcinoma, the synchronous occurrence of appendiceal goblet cell adenocarcinoma theoretically still necessitates consideration of this hereditary disorder. For such cases with dual malignancies, MMR protein testing serves as an essential initial screening tool. In the present case, taking into account the patient’s advanced age, overall condition, and tolerance to further procedures, a shared decision was made with the patient and family not to pursue additional germline genetic testing.

In terms of treatment, surgery is the mainstay for GCA, typically involving right hemicolectomy even with negative surgical margins [13]. The term “local resection” generally refers to simple appendectomy or limited segmental resection, which is mostly applicable to early-stage, low-grade lesions confined to the appendix without serosal or adjacent organ invasion. If the lesion involves the appendiceal base, extends to the cecum, or is larger in size, right hemicolectomy is required to achieve adequate resection margins and regional lymph node dissection. The ENETS guidelines recommend fluoropyrimidine-based chemotherapy for unresectable local or distant metastatic disease. Adjuvant chemotherapy is often administered post-surgery [14]. In cases of metastasis or recurrence, the NCCN guidelines suggest treating GCA according to colorectal cancer chemotherapy regimens (FOLFOX/CAPEOX) [15], with some patients also receiving targeted therapies (bevacizumab/cetuximab).In the present case, because the ileocecal tumor infiltrated the full thickness of the bowel wall and involved the appendiceal base, the patient underwent laparoscopic right hemicolectomy with regional lymph node dissection and ileocolic anastomosis. This procedure allowed simultaneous resection of both the cecal and appendiceal lesions, ensured adequate surgical margins, and provided comprehensive regional lymphadenectomy, in accordance with the surgical principles for advanced-stage colorectal adenocarcinoma and appendiceal GCA.

Surgical recommendations for appendiceal GCA according to stage: Early GCA confined to the mucosa or submucosa of the appendix: appendectomy alone is sufficient. Lesions involving the appendiceal base or measuring > 2 cm in diameter: right hemicolectomy with regional lymph node dissection is recommended. Lesions with invasion into the cecum or adjacent colon: right hemicolectomy or more extensive resection is required. Unresectable disease or cases with distant metastasis: systemic chemotherapy with or without targeted therapy is indicated.

In recent years, large sample studies on appendicitis GCA have shown [16] that the 5-year survival rate of 1225 patients in the British Cancer Registry was about 68.6%, and tumor staging and grading were the most important prognostic factors. Prognosis is influenced by tumor stage and grade. Higher-grade GCA exhibits greater invasiveness, higher recurrence and metastasis rates, and worse prognosis [17].

This case report has certain limitations. First, the pulmonary nodule detected postoperatively was not pathologically confirmed by biopsy; the diagnosis of metastasis was mainly based on radiologic follow-up and clinical assessment. Although the therapeutic response supported this judgment, histological evidence was lacking. Second, baseline chest CT was not performed, making it impossible to completely exclude the presence of small pulmonary metastases at the time of initial diagnosis. Therefore, for patients with appendiceal malignancies carrying potential metastatic risk, we recommend that baseline systemic imaging-including chest CT-should be considered routine, and that pathological confirmation should be pursued whenever feasible to achieve more accurate staging and to better guide therapeutic decision-making.

Conclusion

The overall survival of GCA depends significantly on tumor stage and grade. GCA has a better prognosis than adenocarcinoma but worse than carcinoid tumors. Appendiceal goblet cell adenocarcinoma is rare, with non-specific clinical symptoms that are easily overlooked. The coexistence of GCA and cecal tubular adenocarcinoma poses diagnostic challenges, increasing the risk of missed or misdiagnosis. Histological morphology and immunohistochemistry are crucial for diagnosis. This report details a case of cecal tubular adenocarcinoma combined with appendiceal goblet cell adenocarcinoma and analyzes its clinical and pathological features. We also review relevant literature to enhance understanding of this rare tumor and prevent misdiagnosis.
