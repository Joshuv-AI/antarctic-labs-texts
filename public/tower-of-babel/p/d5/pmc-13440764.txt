Pseudoaneurysm of the superficial temporal artery after head-to-head collision between football players: A case report
Authors: Gabriela A. Brożek, Magdalena M. Gołuchowska, Kornelia M. Kliś, Borys M. Kwinta, Tadeusz J. Popiela, Roger M. Krzyżewski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Superficial temporal artery (STA) pseudoaneurysm is an uncommon sequela of arterial injury due to blunt head trauma. We present a case of post-traumatic pseudoaneurysm of the left STA. Epidemiology, pathophysiology, clinical aspects and treatment modalities are also discussed.Case Description: An 18-year-old male with a history of a head-to-head collision with another football player presented to the emergency department with a pulsatile mass in the left temporal region. Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) confirmed a pseudoaneurysm of the left STA. Compression therapy was chosen as the first-line treatment. Follow-up evaluation revealed no significant improvement. The patient was eligible for surgical excision of the aneurysm sac with ligation of afferent and efferent vessels, which was conducted successfully, with no need for further interventions.Conclusion: This case highlights the importance of including the STA pseudoaneurysm in the differential diagnosis for blunt head injuries in the temporal area. The presence of palpable pulsatility enables primary differentiation. Computed tomography angiography and DSA are accurate diagnostic tools. Certain cases are unresponsive to conservative compression therapy and require surgical interventions. The superficial anatomical location may render embolization less favorable.

INTRODUCTION

The superficial temporal artery (STA) arises from the external carotid artery as one of its terminal branches. Its course begins at the level of the neck of the mandible, then the artery traverses the parotid gland. After crossing the zygomatic process of the temporal bone, it separates into its final branches: frontal and parietal. The frontal branch runs anteriorly, while the parietal, posteriorly.[20] The area of blood supply of the STA is rich and embraces the lateral region of the face (both the skin and muscles), as well as the parotid gland, temporomandibular joint and temporoparietal fascia. The anterior scalp also receives an abundant blood supply from STA.[22] What is clinically important is that the long course of STA over bony structures and its superficial location predispose it to injury. As a result, hemorrhage, aneurysms, or even occlusion of the vessel may occur.[23] Among STA aneurysms, pseudoaneurysms, true and dissecting aneurysms can be distinguished.[9]

Pseudoaneurysm of the STA is a rare entity, constituting only 1% of aneurysms related to trauma. The first to report it was Bartholin in 1644. The preponderance of young men and the elderly prone to falls can be observed. It is primarily attributed to a blunt head injury to the temporal area.[15]

In general, pseudoaneurysms contribute to 95% of aneurysms of the STA following trauma, while true aneurysms are solely 5%.[15] Pseudoaneurysms, unlike true aneurysms, do not contain all histological layers of the vascular wall. The communication between the vessel of origin and the pseudoaneurysm is preserved.[8]

In this paper, we present a case of a pseudoaneurysm of the STA in an 18-year-old male patient.

CASE REPORT

An 18-year-old male presented to the emergency department due to a subcutaneous hematoma in the left temporal area, present since the day before. On history taking, the patient was neurologically intact and reported a head injury in the form of a head-to-head collision with another player during a football match, 11 days before admission. The history was negative for any chronic diseases and long-term medications. Physical examination revealed a small nodule with palpable pulsatility over the left temple [Figure 1]. No other abnormalities were found. A computed tomography (CT) scan revealed no pathological findings, including no evidence of intracranial hemorrhage, nor post-traumatic changes in the bony structures. On CT angiography (CTA), an aneurysm of the left STA was identified, measuring 13 × 10 × 9 mm, with a 2.5 × 2.6 mm neck [Figure 2]. The patient was consulted by a neurosurgeon on call and was deemed not to require urgent interventions due to the extracranial location of the aneurysm. He was referred to the neurosurgery outpatient clinic, where on the following day, after consulting an interventional neuroradiologist, he was qualified for elective treatment, digital subtraction angiography (DSA) with possible subsequent embolization of the aneurysm.

A pulsatile nodule over the patient’s left temple (white circle).

(A) Axial and (B) coronal computed tomography angiography scan of the patient’s head. An aneurysm of the left superficial temporal artery, 13 × 10 × 9 mm in diameter, with the neck measuring 2.5 × 2.6 mm is visible.

Four days later, the patient was admitted to the department of neurosurgery. On admission, he complained of visual floaters and 2 out of 10 pain intensity on the Numeric Rating Scale. Otherwise, consistent with the previous findings. DSA was performed under local anesthesia, through transradial access. The presence of a left STA pseudoaneurysm was confirmed, with delayed flow into the aneurysm, incomplete sac opacification and contrast agent stasis [Figure 3]. Due to the superficial location of the aneurysm and its relatively small size, embolization was aborted. The intraprocedural course was uneventful. The patient was discharged home and instructed to apply compression therapy on the left temporal area until the follow-up appointment in the outpatient clinic in 3 weeks.

Digital subtraction angiography image showing a pseudoaneurysm of the left superficial temporal artery with incomplete opacification of the aneurysmal sac.

Follow-up evaluation revealed no marked decrease in the aneurysm’s size. Thus, the patient was eligible for a surgical excision of the aneurysm. Under general anesthesia, through a linear incision and dissection of adjacent tissue, the pseudoaneurysm was exposed and fully delineated, along with its afferent and efferent vessels [Figure 4A]. The proximal and distal vessels were ligated [Figure 4B]. The specimen was prepared for histopathological examination [Figure 4C]. The gross examination revealed markedly thickened walls [Figure 4D]. A monopolar stimulating probe was utilized intraoperatively to assist in identifying and preserving the frontal branch of the facial nerve. Follow-up CT and magnetic resonance imaging were unremarkable. The patient reported a complete alleviation of his symptoms and was able to return to his daily activities.

Intraoperative view depicting the surgical management of the pseudoaneurysm. (A) Following a linear incision and tissue dissection, the lesion was exposed. (B) Ligation of afferent and efferent vessels was performed before its excision. (C) The macroscopic appearance of post-resection pseudoaneurysm. (D) A cross-section of the pseudoaneurysm of the superficial temporal artery demonstrating the morphology and thickness of its constituent walls.

DISCUSSION

Pseudoaneurysms result from partial transection of the arterial wall, causing the formation of a blood-filled cavity within surrounding tissues, which eventually becomes enclosed by a fibrous pseudocapsule.[6] Cases of STA pseudoaneurysm formation post-blunt head trauma, including falls and sports-related injuries, motor vehicle accidents, dog bites, parotid gland surgery, botulinum toxin injection, Gardner traction, use of pin-type head-holders and intracranial pressure monitoring device placement have been published.[4,11,12]

STA is one of the two terminal branches of the external carotid artery, along with the internal maxillary artery. Its superficial course across bony structures with no adequate protection from overlying tissues makes it exceptionally vulnerable to blunt trauma and thus susceptible to pseudoaneurysm formation. The two predominantly exposed sections of STA are: the segment traversing the zygomatic arch and the distal portion, above the superior temporal line.[6]

Clinically, STA pseudoaneurysm typically presents as a progressively growing, pulsatile mass in the temporal, frontal or preauricular region.[4,12] It usually occurs 2–6 weeks post-injury and can expand even up to 6 cm in diameter.[11] The patients may report discomfort in the affected area, ranging from mild tenderness to sharp pain, soft-tissue swelling, pressure-like sensation, headache or even chronic migraines and tinnitus.[4,17,21,24] On auscultation, a systolic bruit over the mass of the pseudoaneurysm may be audible.[16] Applying pressure on the proximal STA usually causes cessation of the pulsations.[27] When punctured, a STA pseudoaneurysm does not decrease in size and active arterial bleeding is observed at the puncture site; otherwise, a simple hematoma should be suspected.[13] The differential diagnostic workup should also consider a lipoma, cyst, abscess, inflamed lymph node and arteriovenous fistula.

For further evaluation, CTA, magnetic resonance angiography, DSA or Doppler ultrasonography may be used. On Doppler ultrasound, the Taegeuk pattern, or the so-called “Yin-Yang” sign, is pathognomonic of pseudoaneurysms and represents direct communication between the STA and turbulent flow.[12] In our case, CTA was chosen as the first-line diagnostic tool, considering the suspected traumatic mechanism of the pseudoaneurysm formation and the need to exclude concomitant intracranial and osseous injuries. Subsequent DSA was performed to further confirm the diagnosis and assess the feasibility of endovascular treatment. Although practical, Doppler ultrasound was not included in the diagnostic workup.

As for the treatment modalities, several approaches have been proposed. Conservative management with compression therapy may lead to occlusion and eventual resolution of the pseudoaneurysm by inducing intraluminal thrombus formation. Gradual pressure is applied with the practitioner’s thumb just proximal to the neck of the pseudoaneurysm for approximately 30–40 min, until the pulsating flow diminishes.[13] It has not been recommended due to the risk of bony erosions and cosmetic disfigurement on long-term follow-up,[9] but may be considered for minimally invasive management of small lesions.[13] Surgical intervention, the most frequently used treatment modality, comprises ligation of the afferent and efferent ends of the pseudoaneurysm and sac excision with thrombus extraction, if needed.[5] STA reconstruction with STA-STA anastomosis might be included.[19] There was one case of postprocedural permanent facial nerve palsy after resection of a giant STA pseudoaneurysm. To minimize the risk of such a complication, the surgery may be performed with continuous facial nerve monitoring. A few cases of endovascular treatment have also been described. STA embolization with liquid and fibered platinum coils of microcoils can be an effective treatment modality.[10] For frail patients with multiple comorbidities, thrombin injection might be a good alternative. Under local anesthesia and ultrasound guidance, the thrombin solution component is injected directly into the lumen of the pseudoaneurysm, leading to its immediate thrombosis.[28]

The available evidence supports surgical excision as the most consistently effective treatment modality for STA pseudoaneurysms. In the systematic review by Thomassen et al., surgery was successful in all 67 reported cases, whereas embolization carried a success rate of 69% (9 out of 13), including all five cases of thrombin injection reported at the time of that synthesis being successful.[26] Surgery offers the advantages of definitive lesion elimination and histopathological confirmation, at the cost of general anesthesia in most cases, potential facial nerve injury, wound infection and cosmetic scarring. Endovascular embolization is attractive when the lesion is deep or located in the periparotid area of proximal STA, after penetrating or iatrogenic injuries, which favor angiograms for the evaluation of the lesions, or in patients unfit for surgery. However, communication between the frontal branch of the STA and the supraorbital or supratrochlear branches of the ophthalmic artery introduces a risk of inadvertent embolization to the ocular circulation, making the frontal branch lesions less suitable to undergo endovascular treatment. A possibility of ischemic stroke should also be taken into consideration in the setting of nontarget embolization.[7,10,14,25,27]

The evolving trajectory of the management strategy in our case highlights decision points that arise in clinical practice. The initial decision to aim for compression therapy with a defined 3-week reassessment as the first-line treatment was based on the relatively small size of the lesion, the neuroradiologist electing not to proceed with embolisation, and the absence of features mandating urgent intervention. Follow-up demonstrated no meaningful reduction in lesion size or the symptoms. Given the risk of rare but reported rupture with extradural hematoma formation and absence of regression after a trial of compression, continued observation was judged inappropriate.[19] Surgical excision was therefore offered as the most reliable definitive treatment.

A number of case reports on STA pseudoaneurysm of sports-related traumatic origin have been reported, including pseudoaneurysm development following injuries acquired from a lacrosse or cricket ball strike, a heading duel during a soccer match, a fist strike during a basketball game, and fall from a bicycle. Often, adolescent or young adult males, these patients may also be encountered beyond the emergency department setting, such as sports medicine clinics. Apart from commonly observed swelling and pain in the area of injury, no specific pattern of clinical course is apparent in this group. The time to presentation and mass development after trauma is variable, although gradual enlargement is typically observed. The use of helmets may not prevent the development of STA pseudoaneurysm among athletes.[1-3,18,25,29]

CONCLUSION

STA pseudoaneurysm is a rare vascular lesion, typically of traumatic origin. Our case highlights the importance of including it in the differential diagnosis for blunt head injuries. The presence of palpable pulsatility enables primary differentiation between a STA pseudoaneurysm and a simple subcutaneous scalp hematoma. The management of STA pseudoaneurysms still poses a significant clinical challenge, with certain cases being unresponsive to conservative compression therapy and requiring surgical interventions. The superficial anatomical location may render embolization less favorable.
