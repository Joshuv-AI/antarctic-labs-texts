Translation and psychometric evaluation of the Vietnamese version of the Clinical Reasoning Scale among nursing students
Authors: Thi Kim Chi Do, Bui Minh Thu, Nga Thi Thuy Nguyen, Nuzul Sri Hertanti, Yeu-Hui Chuang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClinical reasoning is a core competency in nursing education. Despite its importance, Vietnam lacks a validated instrument to measure nursing students’ clinical reasoning, limiting opportunities for evaluating this essential competency. Therefore, this study aimed to translate the Clinical Reasoning Scale (CRS) into Vietnamese and examine the psychometric properties of the Vietnamese version (CRS-V).MethodsData were collected from senior nursing students at ten 3-year colleges and 4-year universities across northern, central, and southern Vietnam. In total, 600 senior nursing students participated in this study. The CRS-V was cross-culturally adapted and administered via an online survey. Its validity was examined through content validity, concurrent validity, and construct validity using both exploratory factor analysis and confirmatory factor analysis. Reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, McDonald’s omega, and a test–retest analysis.ResultsThe CRS-V had a good content validity index with scores ranging 0.85–1 for the items and an excellent overall content validity index of 0.96. The exploratory factor analysis identified a single factor comprising 16 items. The confirmatory factor analysis provided an acceptable fit model with one factor. The scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.96, McDonald’s ω = 0.97). The test-retest stability of the CRS-V was good with an intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.83 (95% confidence interval = 0.74, 0.89). No floor or ceiling effects were observed.ConclusionsThis study provides initial evidence supporting the validity and reliability of the CRS-V among Vietnamese nursing students. Although the CRS-V may be useful for assessing clinical reasoning in nursing education, further validation studies are needed before broader educational or evaluative applications can be strongly recommended.Trial registration numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12912-026-04964-7.

Background

Clinical reasoning is a core competency in nursing [1, 2]. Although many definitions exist, it is generally described as a thinking process that uses various strategies to recognize patients’ problems [3]. This process includes several steps, such as collecting and prioritizing information, followed by integrating and analyzing that information to reach a clinical explanation or conclusion [4]. Nurses without adequate clinical reasoning may make incorrect decisions, which could jeopardize patient safety [5, 6]. Therefore, clinical reasoning ability is essential and should be cultivated from the early stages of nursing education [7, 8]. Promoting this competency in undergraduate nursing students can enable them to translate theoretical knowledge into effective patient care, ultimately improving overall care quality [9].

In the nursing literature, the terms ‘clinical reasoning’, ‘critical thinking’, and ‘clinical judgment’ are sometimes used interchangeably; however, they represent distinct concepts [4, 10]. Critical thinking provides the cognitive foundation that supports clinical reasoning, whereas clinical judgment is the outcome derived from the reasoning process [11]. Critical thinking is a knowledge-based cognitive process that is not dependent on a specific situation or discipline [12] and can therefore be assessed using standardized examinations not specific to nursing [11, 13]. In contrast, clinical reasoning requires a scientific knowledge base and the ability to apply that knowledge to specific clinical situations through cognitive and metacognitive processes [14]. Therefore, there is a need for discipline-specific instruments to measure clinical reasoning in nursing practice in order to better understand this competency [11, 14]. Regarding clinical judgment, Tanner [15] emphasizes not only the cognitive and metacognitive processes involved in thinking and reasoning, but also the psychomotor actions and affective responses of the caregiver. This implies that clinical judgment takes into account not only knowledge and its application to a specific patient, but also the caregiver’s affective domain and the clinical environment [11, 15]. Although these concepts are closely related, no single instrument can fully assess the development of all three competencies [11]. Accordingly, each of these competencies should be evaluated using specialized instruments tailored to its own unique characteristics.

Several instruments have been developed or adapted to assess clinical reasoning in nursing, including the Clinical Reasoning Competency Scale (CRCS) [6], the Clinical Reasoning Assessment Rubric (CRAR) [16], the Clinical Reasoning Evaluation Simulation Tool (CREST) [17], the Nurses’ Clinical Reasoning Scale (NCRS) [1], and the Clinical Reasoning Scale (CRS) [18]. However, several of these instruments were not originally designed specifically for nursing students. For example, the CRCS [6] was developed to measure clinical reasoning competency in practicing nurses, while the CRAR [16] was initially created for pre-registration osteopathy students. Although the NCRS [2] was developed for use by both nursing students and registered nurses, the previous systematic review has recommended the development of measurement instruments tailored separately to nurses and nursing students [16].

Recently, the 16-item CRS was developed as a measure of clinical reasoning in nursing students, grounded in the Framework of Competencies of Clinical Reasoning for Nursing Students [17]. Its development was guided by expert consensus through qualitative synthesis and a Delphi review. The CRS comprises four domains: identification of clinical cues, confirmation of problems, determination and execution of actions, and reflective evaluation. It has demonstrated acceptable reliability and validity, supported by internal consistency, a confirmatory factor analysis, and content validity assessment. Validation across three nursing program types with large, diverse samples provided supporting evidence for its use within nursing education settings [17]. More recently, the translated CRS was evaluated in Turkey, and strong internal consistency and high test-retest stability were reported [18]. These findings suggest that the CRS is a suitable instrument for assessing nursing students’ clinical reasoning.

To the best of our knowledge, Vietnam lacks psychometrically validated instruments for assessing clinical reasoning ability in nurses or nursing students, which is an essential competency for making sound clinical decisions, minimizing risks, and ensuring patient safety. Given its theoretical foundation and rigorous methodological development, the CRS was selected for adaptation because of its close alignment with Vietnamese nursing curricula and its emphasis on the nursing process. Therefore, this study aimed to translate the Clinical Reasoning Scale (CRS) into Vietnamese and examine the psychometric properties of the Vietnamese version (CRS-V).

Materials and methods

This study employed a cross-sectional design and is a part of a larger project on nursing students’ clinical reasoning in Vietnam.

Settings and participants

Data were collected from ten nursing colleges and universities located in northern, central, and southern Vietnam using convenience sampling. In Vietnam, nursing colleges typically offer 3-year Bachelor of Nursing programs, while universities provide 4-year programs. Nursing students from both three-year college and four-year university programs are trained under a standardized national competency framework issued by the Ministry of Health and are therefore expected to demonstrate similar baseline levels of clinical reasoning competence [19]. The national competency framework is implemented consistently across regions of Vietnam. Graduates from both colleges and universities are qualified as registered nurses and share the same scope of nursing practice [20].

Eligible participants were full-time senior (final-year) nursing students enrolled in these institutions. Students who did not have access to an internet-connected device required for completing the online survey were excluded. Final-year nursing students were selected because clinical reasoning develops progressively across the nursing curriculum [21]. Moreover, in Vietnam, by this stage, students have completed most core theoretical courses and undertake hospital-based clinical practicum placements [19], enabling meaningful assessment of this competency.

The sample size was determined based on established recommendations for factor analysis. The previous literature recommends that at least 300 participants are needed to obtain stable and replicable factor structures [22, 23]. Stone [24] also noted that when sample sizes exceed 400, the Chi-square test of exact fit becomes overly sensitive, often leading to model rejection. Based on this guidance, we selected 300 participants for an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and another 300 for a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA).

From the larger project sample of 1039 eligible students, 600 participants were selected using stratified random sampling [25]. Stratification was based on two criteria: geographic region (northern, central, and southern) and program type (3-year vs. 4-year). This approach was applied to enhance sample representativeness across regions and educational programs. From each region, 200 students were selected, with equal representation from program types (100 students from 3-year colleges and 100 from 4-year universities). In the southern region, however, only 73 eligible students were enrolled in 3-year colleges; therefore, an additional 27 students were randomly selected from 4-year universities to reach the regional target of 200 participants.

SPSS was used to implement stratified random selection. This process generated six datasets based on the combination of region and program type (college and university students from the northern, central, and southern regions). Each set was then randomly assigned to two equal groups, which were combined into subsample 1 (for the EFA) and subsample 2 (for the CFA). This procedure ensured proportional representation across regions and program types while maintaining independence between the two subsamples for validation purposes.

Instruments

Demographic questionnaire

A demographic questionnaire collected information on participants’ age, sex, marital status, region of residence, part-time employment in a clinical setting, type of program, type of institution (public or private), and prior attendance in a clinical reasoning course.

The Clinical Reasoning Scale (CRS)

The CRS, developed by Huang [17], was used to measure clinical reasoning and is appropriate for nursing students across various program types. The scale includes 16 items, divided into four sub-dimensions, with each dimension consisting of four items, including awareness of clinical cues, confirmation of clinical problems, determination and implementation of actions, and evaluation and reflection [17]. Items are scored on a five-point Likert-type scale, with total scores ranging 16–80. Higher scores indicate better clinical reasoning readiness. The CRS demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.89) and good construct validity in a Taiwanese nursing student population [17]. This instrument was translated and culturally adapted in the present study.

The Critical Thinking Disposition Scale (CTDS)

The eleven-item Critical Thinking Disposition Scale (CTDS), developed by Sosu [26], was used to examine the concurrent validity of the CRS-V. It was rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with total scores ranging from 11 to 55. Higher scores reflect a stronger critical thinking disposition. The Vietnamese version of the scale (CTDS-V) was translated and validated by Nguyen [27] and demonstrated good reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.88).

The translation process of the CRS-V

The English version of the CRS was obtained with permission from the original author for translation and use in Vietnam [17]. The translation followed established guidelines for cross-cultural instrument adaptation [28]. Two independent bilingual translators produced forward translations into Vietnamese: one was a nursing faculty member with strong linguistic competence, and the other held a master’s degree in English and had 10 years of experience teaching English to nurses in a public college. The two forward translations were reconciled into a single version through discussions between the research team and the translators. This version was then back-translated into English by two independent bilingual translators, one being a nurse with a PhD currently practicing in Australia and the other a nursing PhD faculty member at a public university in Vietnam, both of whom were blinded to the original instrument. The research team reviewed the back-translated versions with the back translators, resolving discrepancies and ensuring semantic, conceptual, and experiential equivalence with the original CRS.

Validity and reliability

Face and content validity

The preliminary CRS-V was pilot-tested with 41 senior nursing students to assess clarity and comprehensibility. These students were not included in the main study sample. Following this, content validity was evaluated by seven experts, including five nursing faculty members and two PhD-prepared nurses with more than 15 years of experience in nursing management and education. Experts rated the relevance of each item using a four-point scale (1 = “not relevant,” 4 = “highly relevant”). Item-level content validity indices (I- CVIs) were calculated as the proportion of experts assigning ratings of 3 or 4 to each item. Scale-level content validity was assessed using the average scale content validity index (S-CVI/Ave) [29]. Thresholds of I-CVI ≥ 0.78 and S-CVI/Ave ≥ 0.90 were considered indicative of acceptable content validity [29].

Concurrent validity

Concurrent validity of the CRS-V was examined by assessing its correlation with the Vietnamese version of the Critical Thinking Disposition Scale (CTDS-V). Pearson’s correlation was used for approximately normally distributed continuous data, whereas Spearman’s rank correlation was applied when the data were ordinal or not normally distributed. Correlation coefficients ranging from 0.5 to 0.6 were interpreted as indicating a moderate to strong association between the measures [30].

Construct validity

For the construct validity, subsample 1 was used for the EFA, and subsample 2 for the CFA. The equivalence of the two subsamples was evaluated by the communality ratio (S), which preserves the variance structure of the original sample. An S value of close to 1 suggests successful subsample division [31]. The EFA was performed using principal axis factoring with varimax rotation. The suitability of subsample 1 was confirmed by a Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) value above the recommended cutoff of 0.80 and a significant Bartlett’s test of sphericity (p < 0.001), indicating adequate factorability [32]. Factors with an eigenvalue of > 1 were extracted, and models were considered acceptable when at least 60% of the variance was explained [33]. Factor loadings were considered significant if > 0.45 [34].

For the CFA, multivariate normality was evaluated using Mardia’s normalized estimate of kurtosis, with values exceeding 5 suggesting violation [35]. In cases of non-normality, a bootstrap procedure with 1000 resamples was employed using a maximum likelihood estimation, and bias-corrected confidence intervals were reported [36]. Model fit was assessed using multiple indices: χ2/df < 3.0 [37], root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA < 0.08), standardized root mean square residual (SRMR < 0.08), comparative fit index (CFI > 0.90), Tucker-Lewis index (TLI > 0.90) [38], and normalized fit index (NFI) ≥ 0.90 [37]. Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) was reported for relative model comparison purposes only, with lower values indicating better fit.

Convergent and discriminant validity

Convergent validity was evaluated using the composite reliability (CR > 0.7) and average variance extracted (AVE > 0.5), calculated based on standardized factor loadings from the CFA [39]. Discriminant validity was evaluated using the heterotrait–monotrait ratio (HTMT) based on the item-level correlation matrix for the multidimensional model. HTMT values greater than 0.85 were interpreted as indicating insufficient discriminant validity between constructs [39].

Measurement invariance

Measurement invariance was evaluated across region and gender groups using multi-group CFA to examine the stability of the measurement model. Configural, metric, scalar and strict invariance were tested sequentially. Given that χ2 test is sensitive to sample size, significant changes in model fit were defined based on differences in CFI (ΔCFI ≤ 0.01), TLI (ΔTLI ≤ 0.01), and RMSEA (ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.015), which were used to confirm invariance at each level [40]. When strict invariance was not supported, partial strict invariance was examined by freeing selected residual variances across groups.

Reliability

Reliability was examined through internal consistency, test-retest methods, and measurement error. The internal consistency was estimated using Cronbach’s α and McDonald’s ω, with values of ≥ 0.70 indicating acceptable reliability [41, 42]. Previous research has emphasized that reliability estimates should be derived from sufficiently large samples to minimize statistical error [43]. Charter [44] recommended a minimum sample size of 400 to obtain a precise estimate of the population Cronbach’s alpha. Accordingly, the internal consistency of the scale was evaluated using the total sample (N = 600). In addition, corrected item–total correlations were examined to assess the degree to which each item was associated with the overall scale. A corrected item–total correlation below 0.30 suggests that an item may not adequately reflect the underlying construct and may therefore warrant removal [45].

The test-retest reliability was conducted using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), with values of > 0.9 indicating excellent reliability, 0.75–0.90 good reliability, 0.50–0.75 moderate reliability, and < 0.50 poor reliability [46].

The standard error of measurement (SEM) and the smallest detectable change (SDC) were calculated to examine measurement error. The SEM reflects the amount of random measurement error in a scale and indicates its reliability [47]. The SEM was calculated using the formula SEM = SDpooled × √(1-ICC). The SDC was calculated using the formula SDC = (1.96 × √2 × SEM) /√n, which indicates the smallest detectable changes at the group level that can be interpreted as a real change beyond measurement error [47].

Ceiling and floor effects

Floor and ceiling effects were also examined in the current study. The percentage of individuals who scored the highest or lowest values on the scale was determined. A percentage exceeding 15% indicates the presence of a floor or ceiling effect [48].

Data collection procedures

After obtaining approval from the participating colleges and universities, one of the researchers distributed study information to eligible students via email or Zalo (a widely used social media platform in Vietnam) and invited students to attend an online meeting. This meeting was conducted via Google Meet, during which the researcher explained the study’s objectives, procedures, and addressed any questions from participants. Only those who met the inclusion criteria and provided informed consent were eligible to participate. Participants then received a Google Forms link to complete the questionnaire. To ensure completeness of data, the “required” response option was enabled for all items, thereby preventing missing data. Data collection was conducted from May to September 2025.

As to the test-retest reliability, participants who completed the baseline questionnaires were re-contacted and invited to complete the CRS-V a second time after a 2-week interval. To ensure accurate record linkage, respondents accessed a second Google Form using the same email address as at the baseline; submissions made with a different email were excluded. Ultimately, 70 nursing students (out of the 600) completed the retest survey.

Data analysis

Data was analyzed using SPSS vers. 25.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and AMOS vers. 24.0 (IBM). Descriptive statistics, including the mean, standard deviation (SD), frequency, and percentage, were used to summarize students’ demographic characteristics. The assumption of normality was assessed with histograms and absolute values of skewness and kurtosis [49]. In addition, McDonald’s ω can be calculated with the omega function in the psych package for R [50]. All statistical analyses were performed with a significance level set at p < 0.05.

Multivariate outliers were assessed using Mahalanobis distance, with cases exceeding the critical chi-square value at p < 0.001 considered potential outliers [32]. Sensitivity analysis was conducted to evaluate the impact of excluding these cases on CFA results.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted following the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The Institutional Review Board of Hanoi University of Public Health approved this study (Hanoi, Vietnam; 025–070/DD-YTCC). Ethical approval covered all phases of data collection, including both the initial survey and the test–retest assessment. All participants were informed about the purpose and methods of the study, the anonymity and confidentiality of their responses, the voluntary nature of participation, and their right to withdraw at any time without penalty. Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Face validity

The draft CRS-V was evaluated for face validity by 41 nursing students who did not participate in the main survey. A dichotomous scale was used, and participants were also invited to suggest revisions. Minor wording adjustments were recommended to improve clarity and comprehensibility. All items received ratings of at least 90% for both clarity and comprehensibility, so no items were removed.

Content validity

The content validity was assessed by seven experts. Each item was rated for relevance on a four-point scale (1 = not relevant, 4 = highly relevant). Item-level CVIs (I-CVIs) were computed as the proportion of experts assigning ratings of 3 or 4. In this study, I-CVIs ranged 0.85–1. The scale-level CVI (S-CVI/Ave), calculated as the average of the I-CVIs across all items, was 0.96.

Concurrent validity

Since CRS-V scores were not normally distributed, concurrent validity was assessed using Spearman’s rank-order correlation with the CTDS-V. The correlation between the CRS-V and CTDS-V was statistically significant, indicating a moderate positive correlation (ρ = 0.56, p < 0.001).

Factor analysis

The calculated S-index was 0.995, which is very close to 1, indicating that the two subsamples were equivalent.

Exploratory factor analysis (EFA)

The KMO value was 0.963 and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (χ2 = 4632.58, p < 0.001), confirming that data for subsample 1 were appropriate for a factor analysis. Using principal axis factoring with varimax rotation, the EFA suggested a one-factor solution with an eigenvalue of > 1.0, which accounted for 66.08% of the total variance. All other components had eigenvalues of < 1.0 and thus were not retained based on Kaiser’s criterion. As shown in Table 2, all 16 items loaded strongly on the single factor, with factor loadings ranging 0.682–0.868.

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA)

Mardia’s test indicated that data from subsample 2 were not multivariate normally distributed (p < 0.001). Therefore, a bootstrapping procedure with 1000 samples was applied to evaluate the model fit.

Based on the EFA conducted in this study, a unidimensional structure was suggested. However, the original version of the CRS, grounded in the underlying theory, supported a four-factor structure. Accordingly, we tested two competing CFA models. Model 1 specified four first-order factors consistent with the original scale: factor 1 (items 1–4), factor 2 (items 5–8), factor 3 (items 9–12), and factor 4 (items 13–16). Model 2 specified a single-factor model in line with the EFA results.

Model 1 showed acceptable fit indices. However, the latent factors (F1–F4) were very highly correlated (0.90–0.98). The HTMT values ranged from 0.93 to 0.96, exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.85, indicating poor discriminant validity among the four factors in this sample (Supplementary Table S1). Model 2 (one-factor model) showed that all 16 items (CRS1–CRS16) had moderate to strong standardized loadings (0.63–0.87) (Fig. 1). The model fit indices were χ2/df = 2.85, RMSEA = 0.079 (90% confidence interval (CI) [0.068–0.089]), SRMR = 0.03, CFI = 0.96, TLI = 0.95, and NFI = 0.93, all supporting adequate model fit (Table 3). Item error variances ranged from 0.128 to 0.332, with no negative or excessively large values observed. Thus, model 2 was retained as the final model for the CRS-V. Multivariate outliers were identified using Mahalanobis distance. Sensitivity analysis excluding these cases resulted in slightly improved fit indices (Supplementary Table S2) and consistent standardized factor loadings (Supplementary Fig S1). These findings suggest that outliers did not substantially affect the factor structure or overall conclusions.

Convergent and discriminant validity

The analysis indicated that the one-factor model demonstrated adequate convergent validity, with a CR of 0.96 (> 0.70) and an AVE of 0.63 (> 0.50), confirming that the observed variables converged well on the underlying construct. Because HTMT is intended to assess discriminant validity between multiple constructs, it is not applicable to the unidimensional model.

Measurement invariance of the CRS-V across regions and genders

Measurement invariance was initially evaluated for both the one-factor and four-factor models. However, when the four-factor model was examined across regions, a non-admissible solution was obtained because the latent covariance matrix was not positive definite. Therefore, measurement invariance results are reported for the final one-factor model.

Measurement invariance of the CRS-V was examined across regions and gender using multi-group CFA (Table 4). Across regions, the configural model showed acceptable fit (χ²/df = 2.30, CFI = 0.908, RMSEA = 0.066), supporting the same factor structure across regional groups. Metric invariance was supported, with minimal changes in fit (ΔCFI = 0.002, ΔRMSEA = 0.002, ΔTLI = 0.008). Scalar invariance was also established (ΔCFI = 0.003, ΔRMSEA = 0.004, ΔTLI = 0.010), indicating comparable item intercepts across regions. Strict invariance was not supported (ΔCFI = 0.026, ΔRMSEA = 0.006, ΔTLI = 0.016); however, partial strict invariance was achieved after freeing selected residual constraints, with acceptable changes in model fit (ΔCFI = 0.010, ΔRMSEA = 0.001, ΔTLI = 0.003) (Table S4; Supplementary Table S3 and Figure S2).

Test-retest reliability

The analysis included 70 senior nursing students. The ICC was 0.83 (95% CI [0.74, 0.89], p < 0.001), indicating good stability of the instrument over time.

Measurement error

The SEM was 5.7, and the group-level SDC was 1.9, which indicated the smallest detectable change at the group level.

Floor or ceiling effects

There was 1.5% (9/600) of total participants that achieved the lowest possible score (16), and 23 participants (3.8%) that achieved the highest possible score on the CRS-V (80). Because both percentages were below the recommended 15% cutoff, no floor or ceiling effects were observed.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to translate and culturally adapt the CRS for use in Vietnam and to evaluate its validity and reliability among nursing students. The content validity findings indicate that the CRS-V items are conceptually appropriate and relevant within the Vietnamese nursing education context. The consistency of content validity results across the original CRS [17] and its Turkish adaptation [18] further suggests that the core construct underlying clinical reasoning is stable across cultural and educational settings. Although the expert panel size in this study was consistent with commonly accepted recommendations for content validation, future research may benefit from involving larger and more diverse panels to capture broader educational and clinical perspectives and to further strengthen evidence for content validity [29].

In this study, concurrent validity was examined by relating the CRS-V to the CTDS-V, since no validated tool specifically measuring clinical reasoning is currently available in Vietnam. This choice was grounded in both theoretical and empirical considerations. Theoretically, although the concepts of clinical reasoning and critical thinking are sometimes used interchangeably, critical thinking is regarded as a foundational cognitive process that underpins clinical reasoning in nursing education [11]. Empirically, the CTDS-V showed strong reliability and validity among Vietnamese senior nursing students, making it a suitable comparator for examining related cognitive constructs [27]. The observed moderate positive association between the CRS-V and CTDS-V supports the criterion-related validity of the CRS-V. However, concurrent validity has not been examined in previous validation studies of the CRS [17, 18].

Construct validity of the CRS-V was examined using both exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses. When an instrument is applied in a new population or context, an EFA is recommended to examine its factor structure [51]. Although EFA was not reported in previous validation studies of the CRS [17, 18], its inclusion in the current study provides additional empirical insight into how the construct manifests in a different educational setting. Similar patterns have been reported in studies of other clinical reasoning instruments across different cultural contexts, such as Korea [52], Japan [53].

For the CFA, both a one-factor model and a four-factor model were tested, and both models demonstrated acceptable overall fit. Nevertheless, the high inter-factor correlations and lack of discriminant validity observed in the four-factor model suggest substantial overlap among the domains in this sample. One possible explanation for this finding is the inherently dynamic and cyclical nature of the clinical reasoning process. Although clinical reasoning can be conceptualized as comprising distinct phases, these phases are not clearly bounded in practice [54]. Instead, they often overlap, merge, and are revisited iteratively as nurses move back and forth between them before reaching decisions and actions [54]. However, the observed overlap may also reflect the educational approach increasingly adopted in Vietnam, where clinical reasoning is taught and practiced in a more integrated manner [55]. This may be related to the competency-based educational orientation introduced by the Ministry of Education and Training of Vietnam in 2021, which emphasizes the integration of knowledge, skills, and professional attitudes in education [56]. Moreover, these findings may partly reflect methodological challenges in distinguishing closely related dimensions using self-report measures.

In addition, two correlated residuals were retained after model evaluation and were considered theoretically plausible. Items 12 (“I can provide theory- and evidence-based nursing interventions”) and 13 (“I can evaluate effectiveness of problem solving from a variety of aspects”) represent sequential stages within the nursing process [57] and clinical reasoning cycle [52]. Item 12 focuses on implementing nursing interventions, whereas Item 13 focuses on evaluating their effectiveness. These activities are closely linked within the nursing process, as evaluation typically follows the implementation of nursing interventions [57]. This sequential relationship may explain the residual association observed between the two items. Similarly, both items 15 (“I can reevaluate a patient’s needs if the problem is not resolved”) and 16 (“I can reflect on the steps of problem solving for improvement, whether the problem is resolved or not”) reflect a process of reviewing and refining clinical actions after evaluating patient outcomes. Reevaluating patient needs and reflecting on problem-solving performance are conceptually related activities that often occur together when nurses monitor outcomes and refine future actions. Although these residual associations were considered theoretically justifiable, they may also indicate a limited degree of local dependence. Therefore, the correlated residuals should be interpreted with caution and confirmed in future studies using independent samples. Accordingly, the present findings should not be interpreted as rejecting the original four-domain conceptualization, but rather as suggesting substantial overlap among the domains within this sample, which may help explain the emergence of the one-factor structure. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted with caution and regarded as preliminary and context-specific pending replication in other samples and settings.

The CRS-V demonstrated sufficient reliability, supported by an acceptable internal consistency and good test-retest reliability. The Cronbach’s alpha result is similar to that reported in the Turkish validation study [18] and slightly higher than that reported in the original CRS development study [5]. Both Cronbach’s alpha and McDonald’s ω exceeded commonly accepted thresholds, indicating that the items function cohesively to assess clinical reasoning. However, very high internal consistency coefficients (Cronbach’s alpha > 0.90) may also reflect a high degree of item homogeneity, raising the possibility of item redundancy [58]. Accordingly, this finding suggests that future studies could apply item response theory (IRT) methods to examine item-level performance, identify redundant items, and further refine the scale while preserving its conceptual coverage [59]. The CRS-V also demonstrated good test–retest reliability, indicating stability of scores over time. In comparison, previous studies assessed stability using Pearson correlations and paired t-tests [18], while others did not examine this property [17]. However, the precision of ICC estimates is influenced by retest sample size, with larger samples providing more stable reliability estimates [60]. Therefore, the test-retest findings in the present study should be interpreted with caution given the limited retest sample size (n = 70).

The SEM of the CRS-V was 5.7, meaning that an observed score may vary by about ± 5.7 points from a respondent’s true score due to measurement error. The SDC at the group level was 1.9, representing the minimum change in mean CRS-V scores that can be interpreted as real rather than measurement error. Reporting the SEM and SDC is important because they provide transparent information on measurement errors and offer useful reference points for future research [47].

This study examined the floor and ceiling effects of the CRS. The results suggested that the CRS-V was able to capture variability in clinical reasoning scores within this sample [48]. However, floor and ceiling analyses do not directly measure how well the scale can detect changes or distinguish between groups [48]. Therefore, the absence of floor and ceiling effects does not necessarily indicate the scale’s discriminative ability or sensitivity to change. These analyses are descriptive and should be interpreted with caution.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, because the CRS-V was administered as a self-assessment survey, responses may have been influenced by social desirability bias and common method variance. To mitigate this, anonymity was assured during data collection. Second, the use of convenience sampling at the institutional level and the inherent nature of online surveys may have introduced selection bias, as students without access to internet-connected devices could not be included. In addition, the sample consisted only of senior nursing students, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to students in earlier stages of nursing education. Future studies should employ alternative data collection methods and include students from different academic years to enhance representativeness and external validity. Third, the present study primarily provides evidence supporting the structural validity and internal consistency of the CRS-V within a cross-sectional context. Predictive validity, known-group validity, and responsiveness to change were not evaluated. Therefore, the ability of the CRS-V to predict clinical performance, discriminate between different levels of clinical reasoning competence, or detect changes over time, remains unclear. Future studies using longitudinal, experimental, and criterion-related designs are needed to further establish these psychometric properties and evaluate the broader applicability of the CRS-V. In addition, formal differential item functioning (DIF) analysis was not conducted in this study, limiting the evaluation of item-level equivalence across subgroups. Although measurement invariance across regions and gender was supported, this provides only preliminary evidence of equivalence and does not replace DIF analyses. Future studies should conduct formal DIF testing across a broader range of demographic and educational subgroups to strengthen evidence of the scale’s fairness. Accordingly, the present study should be considered an initial psychometric evaluation rather than a comprehensive validation of the CRS-V.

Conclusions

The findings provided initial evidence supporting the face, content, concurrent, and construct validity, and the acceptable reliability of the CRS-V among Vietnamese nursing students. The findings primarily support the structural validity and internal consistency of the scale within a cross-sectional context. Although the CRS-V may be useful for assessing clinical reasoning in nursing education and future research, further validation studies examining predictive validity, responsiveness, and broader psychometric properties are needed before broader educational or evaluative applications can be strongly recommended.
