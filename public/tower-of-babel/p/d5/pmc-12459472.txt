Heterogeneity of career success and its relationship with turnover intention among nurses: A cross-sectional study using latent profile analysis
Authors: Zifeng Li, Luhuan Yang, Zuyang Xi, Huiqin Liu, Yuanzhi Fu, Xiaojing Zhou, Xiaojuan Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study examines the diverse profiles of career success among nurses, identify demographic factors associated with each profile, and analyze the relationship between these profiles and turnover intention. A cross-sectional study was conducted involving 2322 nurses. Data were collected in March 2024 using an electronic survey that included scales for measuring career success and turnover intention. Latent profile analysis was used to classify nurses based on career satisfaction, intra-organizational competitiveness, and extra-organizational competitiveness. Logistic regression identified factors influencing career success profiles, and hierarchical regression assessed the relationship between these profiles and turnover intention. Nurses were categorized into 4 profiles “low career success” (20.51%), “moderate career success” (50.78%), “high satisfaction–low competitiveness” (12.49%), and “high career success” group (16.19%). Nurses satisfied with their income and those who had participated in career training were more likely to be in the high career success profile (P < .05). Conversely, nurses with more than 15 working years and holding a junior title were less likely to be in the high career success profile (P<.05). After adjusting for demographic variables, career success profiles significantly predicted turnover intention (P < .05). Career success among nurses is heterogeneous and can be classified into 4 distinct profiles, each with implications for turnover intention.

1. Introduction

Career success, defined as “the achievement of desirable work-related outcomes at any point in a person’s work experiences over time,” encompasses both personal fulfillment and professional achievements, directly influencing professional behavior and the quality of clinical care.[1–3] In China, the career success of nurses currently remains at a moderate level, indicating substantial room for improvement.[4,5] Empirical studies have identified multiple determinants of nurses’ career success, including personal resources, positive work attitudes, and job-related factors.[6] Higher career success not only enhances self-worth and work motivation but also contributes to improved care outcomes and greater workforce stability.[7,8]

Turnover intention (an individual’s psychological inclination to leave their current organization) is widely recognized as a precursor to actual turnover behavior.[8] In China, the nursing workforce faces considerable supply–demand pressures. By 2023, there were 5.637 million registered nurses, accounting for 45.14% of the total health workforce.[9] Despite governmental efforts to enhance the quality and quantity of nursing services, persistent structural issues (such as regional imbalances, disparities in competency, and limited job satisfaction) remain.[10–12] These challenges are further exacerbated by rapid socioeconomic development, leading to increased job stress, inadequate compensation, and limited career development opportunities for nurses. A national survey revealed that 22.02% of nurses in tertiary public hospitals recently considered leaving their jobs,[13–15] which poses significant threats to the continuity and quality of healthcare services.

The Conservation of Resources Theory (COR) provides a robust theoretical framework for understanding the relationship between career success and turnover intentions. According to COR, resources are central to individuals’ assessment of and response to stress. When resources are threatened or lost, individuals may experience stress reactions.[16] Nurses with lower career success often face resource scarcity, which may lead to stress responses, increased job dissatisfaction, and ultimately higher turnover intentions, as they lack sufficient motivation to protect and value their current work resources.[17]

Despite growing interest in this topic, most studies still treat career success as a homogeneous, continuous construct measured by total scale scores. Such variable-centered approaches overlook the heterogeneity in how nurses perceive career success. Person-centered analytical methods, such as Latent Profile Analysis (LPA), offer a more nuanced approach by identifying unobserved subgroups based on response patterns to multidimensional indicators.[18,19] However, limited research has applied LPA to career success among nurses, and even fewer studies have linked these profiles to turnover intention.

1.1. Literature review

Most existing studies on nurse career success employ variable-centered approaches, classifying participants based on overall scale scores into high- or low-level groups.[4,5,7] This oversimplifies the construct and overlooks its multidimensional nature, which includes career satisfaction, intra-organizational competitiveness, and external organizational competitiveness. To address this limitation, Zhang et al[3] employed LPA among Chinese nurses and identified 3 heterogeneous subgroups: “low career success,” “moderate career success,” and “high career success.” While these groups showed a gradient in total scores, the key contribution of their study was the identification of latent heterogeneity, highlighting that career success is not uniformly experienced.

Understanding these profiles requires identifying factors associated with career success. Evidence suggests that career success among nurses is driven by multi-level factors. At the individual level, demographic characteristics, personality traits, psychological capital, rumination, and self-efficacy are key factors. Organizational-level factors include perceptions of pay equity, organizational climate, and the working environment. At the occupational level, frequent night shifts, workload, effort–reward imbalance, and job-related risk also play important roles.[6] Notably, older nurses often face dual challenges of professional stagnation and burnout, leading to diminished perceptions of success. However, systematic career training programs have shown potential in reversing this trend.[20]

In terms of outcomes, career success has been consistently linked to reduced turnover intention. Zhong et al[21] found a significant inverse relationship between career success and turnover intention among senior nurses in tertiary hospitals in Inner Mongolia in China. Chen et al[22] reported that career success partially mediates the relationship between feedback-seeking behavior and turnover intention. Fu et al[23] further demonstrated that career success and work engagement jointly mediated the impact of work–family support on turnover intention among hemodialysis nurses. Despite these insights, all studies employed linear regression models, which assume population homogeneity and may overlook profile-specific behavior. For instance, nurses in a “high satisfaction–low competitiveness” profile might still exhibit unexpectedly high turnover intention, an area that remains underexplored.

Although Zhang et al[3] pioneered the use of LPA to identify distinct profiles of career success, their study did not examine how these profiles relate to turnover intention. As a result, the field lacks a clear understanding of which career success subgroups are most at risk of leaving. 2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design

This was a cross-sectional study design conducted in March 2024. This study adhered to the strengthening of the reporting of observational studies in epidemiology (STROBE) statement.[24]

2.2. Study participants

The study employed a convenience sampling method to select nurses from a tertiary hospital in Yichang, China. The inclusion criteria were: passing the Nursing Licensure Examination; at least 1 year of work experience; and informed consent and voluntary participation. Exclusion criteria included: assistant nurses; and nurses on leave due to vacation, training, or further studies.

2.3. Sample size

According to the rule of thumb sample size estimation method, the sample size was calculated as 10 to 20 times the number of variables.[25] This study included 28 items, comprising a demographic questionnaire (11 items), career success scale (11 items), and turnover intention scale (6 items). Consequently, the required minimal sample size was calculated as N = (11 + 11 + 6) × 20 = 560. Considering the 20% rate of invalid questionnaires, the sample size N = 560/(1–20%) = 700. Previous studies have shown that the minimum sample size for LPA should be above 500.[26,27] Therefore, the final sample size for this study was not <700. A total of 2550 questionnaires were distributed, with 2322 valid responses, yielding an effective response rate of 91.06%.

2.4. Instruments

2.4.1. Demographic characteristics

The questionnaire was divided into 3 sections: demographic characteristics, career success scale, and turnover intention scale. The demographic questionnaire collected data on gender, age, educational level, working years, department, professional title, position, income satisfaction, employment mode, night shifts, and participation in career training.

2.4.2. Career Success Scale (CSS)

The CSS, initially developed by Eby et al,[28] was translated and adapted by Yan et al for use in China.[29] The scale comprises 3 dimensions: career satisfaction (items 1–5), intra-organizational competitiveness (items 6–8), and extra-organizational competitiveness (items 9–11). Respondents rated each item on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Total scores ranged from 11 to 55, with higher scores indicating greater career success. The Chinese version of the CSS has been validated among healthcare professionals,[30] and in this study, the scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency with a Cronbach α coefficient of 0.902.

2.4.3. Turnover Intention Scale

The Turnover Intention Scale, developed by Michael et al and later revised by Lee et al,[31,32] assesses 3 dimensions: the likelihood of an employee quitting their current job (items 1 and 6); the employee’s motivation to find another job (items 2 and 3); and the likelihood of the employee securing an external job (items 4 and 5). Responses are recorded on a reverse-scored 4-point Likert scale, ranging from “never” to “often,” with higher scores indicating stronger turnover intention. In this study, the scale showed good internal consistency with a Cronbach α coefficient of 0.793.

2.5. Data collection

The current study was conducted through a questionnaire survey in March 2024. Before the formal survey, a pre-survey was conducted with 20 nurses to assess the clarity and adequacy of the questionnaire items. The data from this pre-survey were excluded from the final analysis. For the formal survey, data were collected via an online questionnaire. The research team briefed the nursing department head at the participating hospital on the study’s objectives, target participants, and questionnaire procedures. The department head then disseminated the questionnaire link through the hospital’s nursing management WeChat group. Nursing managers from various departments further shared the link with their staff.

An electronic version of the informed consent was provided on the first page of the questionnaire. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, who were fully informed about the study’s objectives, significance, and data confidentiality. Participation was voluntary, with the option to withdraw at any time. This study does not involve minors. IP address tracking was implemented to prevent multiple submissions, and respondents were required to fully complete the questionnaire before submission to ensure data integrity. The survey was conducted anonymously to protect participants’ privacy, and any questionnaires with uniform responses (e.g., all items rated identically) were excluded from analysis. This study received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of Yichang Central People’s Hospital (approval number 2024-118-01). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration and the ethical guidelines established by the Regional Research Committee.

2.6. Statistical analysis methods

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 27 (IBM Corporation, Armonk ) and Mplus 8.3 (Muthén & Muthén Corporation, Los Angeles) software. A 2-sided test with a P-value < .05 was considered statistically significant. Categorical data were described as frequencies and percentages, while continuous data were presented as mean ± standard deviation. LPA was performed in Mplus 8.3 to categorize career success types among nurses, exploring models ranging from 1 to 6 profiles. The optimal number of profiles was determined using several criteria: Akaike Information Criterion, Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), Adjusted BIC, Vuong–Lo–Mendell–Rubin Likelihood Ratio Test, Lo–Mendell–Rubin Adjusted Likelihood Ratio Test (LMRT), Bootstrapped Likelihood Ratio Test (BLRT), and entropy.[33,34] Lower values of Akaike Information Criterion, BIC, and Adjusted BIC indicate better model fit. For the LMR and BLR tests, a P-value < .05 suggests that a model with n categories is superior to a model with n-1 categories. An entropy value >0.8 indicates accurate classification.[35–38]

Once the optimal latent profile for career success was identified, a chi-square test was performed to initially assess the influence of demographic variables on the identified profiles. Subsequently, demographic variables that showed statistical significance in the chi-square test were included as independent variables, while the latent career success profile served as the dependent variable. Multiple logistic regression analyses were then conducted to determine the key factors influencing the latent profile for career success. Effect sizes were reported as odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals. Finally, the impact of potential career success profiles on turnover intention was analyzed. Stepwise linear regression was performed to evaluate the effect of demographic variables (independent variables) on turnover intention (dependent variable). Hierarchical regression analysis was then conducted to examine the association between career success profiles and nurses’ turnover intention, adjusting for socio-demographic characteristics as covariates.

2.7. Common method deviation test

This cross-sectional survey utilized self-reported data, which may introduce potential common method variance (CMV).[39] To address this, we employed an anonymous questionnaire and randomized item order. Harman single-factor test was conducted to assess the presence of CMV.[40] All items from the career success and turnover intentions scales were included in the factor analysis. Harman single-factor test results showed that the first common factor explained 36.13% of the variance in the unrotated solution and 26.42% in the rotated solution, both below the 40% threshold. These findings suggest that CMV was not a significant concern in this study.

4. Discussion

This study examined heterogeneity of career success and their influencing factors among Chinese nurses, and investigated the relationship between these categories and turnover intentions. We identified 4 latent categories of career success, influenced by working years, professional title, income satisfaction, and career training. Furthermore, after controlling for general information, nurses with high career success had significantly lower turnover intentions than those with low career success.

Previous studies have typically categorized nurses’ career success into low, moderate, and high groups.[3] However, this study employed LPA to identify 4 latent categories: “low career success,” “moderate career success,” “high satisfaction–low competitiveness,” and “high career success.” These findings differ from previous studies, potentially influenced by respondent backgrounds, sample size, subjective biases in LPA categorization, and other factors. Additionally, this study identified a subgroup of nurses labeled as “high satisfaction and low competitiveness,” characterized by high career satisfaction but moderate to low competitiveness within and outside the organization. This may be attributed to all respondents being from a municipal public tertiary care hospital. In this setting, nurses may enjoy favorable compensation (salary, benefits, work environment) but face limited career advancement opportunities due to organizational constraints. Furthermore, as the sole tertiary public hospital in the region, competitive pressures from external sources are minimal, reducing job insecurity and potentially fostering complacency rather than proactive competition among nurses.

In this survey, the majority of nurses (71.32%) reported moderate or low levels of career success, consistent with prior studies.[3] Moreover, over one-fifth of the nurses belonged to the “low career success” group. To address this issue, nursing managers should implement comprehensive measures to enhance nurses’ career success. These measures might involve offering career development support, enhancing the work environment, providing incentives, and bolstering training and development programs.[41]

Nurses with over 15 working years demonstrated a higher likelihood of being classified into the “low career success,” “moderate career success,” or “high satisfaction–low competitiveness” profiles compared to the “high career success” group. This finding aligns with prior research,[42] which associates shorter career tenure with elevated career success levels. Specifically, early-career nurses (typically with limited work experience) exhibit significant career success potential due to their strong exploratory drive, intellectual curiosity, and proactive career orientation.[42] These individuals often display heightened adaptability and motivation, enabling rapid skill acquisition and early professional achievements. However, prolonged tenure paradoxically correlates with career stagnation. Nurses at advanced career stages frequently encounter systemic barriers, including constrained skill advancement and limited opportunities for specialty development, which collectively undermine perceived career success.[43]

Compared to nurses in higher positions, those in junior roles were more inclined to fall into the “low career success” or “moderate career success” categories. Yang et al and Lin et al has found that nurses with higher professional titles exhibit higher levels of career success, aligning with the findings of this study.[44,45] Firstly, the advancement of professional titles often comes with the deepening of specialized skills and the accumulation of work experience, enabling nurses with higher titles to demonstrate greater professional competence and the ability to handle complex situations at work, thus making it easier for them to gain professional recognition and achievements.[40] Secondly, nurses with higher titles typically possess more opportunities and resources for career development, such as participating in advanced training and undertaking significant projects, which contribute to further enhancing their professional competitiveness and sense of success. Furthermore, the promotion of professional titles may also be accompanied by improvements in salary and benefits, as well as an elevation in social status. These factors, acting in concert, augment the professional satisfaction and successful experiences of nurses with higher titles.[45]

Nurses who were dissatisfied or only moderately satisfied with their income were less likely to be in the “high career success” category. The finding is consistent with the results of Wang et al.[5] Income level serves as a critical indicator of objective career success, reflecting an organization’s recognition of an individual’s professional value and achievements.[5] This metric significantly influences nurses’ job satisfaction.[46,47] Specifically, higher income satisfaction among nurses often correlates with greater perceived organizational contribution and more pronounced professional accomplishments. This recognition and achievement not only enhance nurses’ job satisfaction but also strengthen their sense of career success.

Nurses who participated in career training were more likely to be in the “high career success” group, aligning with Park findings.[48] This outcome may be partially attributed to these nurses having more clearly defined career plans. Nurses who undergo training are often more intentional and better prepared in their career planning, possessing a deeper understanding of their career goals, professional values, and the necessary skills and knowledge. This clarity enables them to more effectively develop and implement career development strategies, thereby increasing their likelihood of achieving career success.

This study found that career success profiles significantly influenced turnover intentions. Nurses in the “low career success” group had significantly higher turnover intentions than those in the “high career success” group, even after controlling for demographic factors. The findings of Wang et al and Hao et al demonstrate a negative correlation between career success and turnover intention,[8–17] which aligns with the results of this study. Individuals with higher levels of career success accumulate diversified resource reserves throughout their professional trajectories. The accumulation of these resources not only fulfills basic needs but also serves as a core driver for sustained career growth, enhanced job satisfaction, and strengthened organizational commitment.[16] For these individuals, their current roles are not merely a means to meet livelihood needs but also a critical pathway to achieve self-actualization and gain social recognition, significantly reducing their turnover intentions.

Conversely, individuals with lower career success often experience resource scarcity. Deficiencies in material resources, unfavorable work conditions, and limited personal resources may lead to feelings of frustration, anxiety, and marginalization. According to the COR theory, individuals adopt defensive strategies to mitigate further resource loss when faced with actual or potential resource depletion.[49] For these nurses, turnover may be perceived as a strategic choice to escape unfavorable environments and seek better career opportunities.

Based on the research findings, the following hierarchical intervention strategies are recommended for nursing administration departments: Optimize the compensation and training system, establish a performance reward mechanism linked to the level of career success, and prioritize providing a green passage for promotion to senior titles and specialized nurse certification training for nurses with more than 15 working years; Implement career cycle management, establish an early warning system for turnover risk using the results of LPA, and customize personalized retention plans in combination with nurses’ career success categories. Enhance nurses’ sense of career success to reduce turnover intention.

This study has several limitations: first, sample selection bias: the survey respondents were from a single medical unit, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader nursing population. Second, survey inaccuracy and bias: some nurses may have provided inauthentic responses due to subjective biases, potentially affecting the reliability and validity of the data. Third, correlation versus causation: the cross-sectional design of this study examines correlations but does not establish causation between career success and turnover intentions. Fourth, lack of control for confounding factors: the identified profiles of career success explained only 2.1% of the total variance in turnover intention. Factors such as job stress and organizational culture were not accounted for, which could confound the results.

5. Conclusion

The characteristics of career success among Chinese nurses are heterogeneous, influenced by factors such as working years, department, professional title, income satisfaction, and career training. Nurses in the low career success group exhibit the highest turnover intentions. Nursing managers can personalize interventions based on these influencing factors to improve career success, reduce turnover intentions, and minimize the loss of nursing human resources, thereby maintaining workforce stability.

Future research should advance this topic through 4 key directions: first, validate profile generalizability by conducting multi-center sampling across diverse healthcare settings, including primary care clinics and teaching hospitals, to reduce sampling bias associated with single-site data. Second, employ mixed-method designs that integrate longitudinal surveys with qualitative interviews to minimize self-reporting bias and better capture contextual factors affecting career success. Third, establish causal pathways by employing cohort designs to track actual turnover behavior, rather than intentions, and by conducting randomized controlled trials to assess targeted interventions, such as career development programs for nurses with low career success. Fourth, control for key confounders by incorporating variables such as job stress and organizational culture, aiming to improve the model’s explanatory power (currently R² = 2.1%) and enhance its robustness.
