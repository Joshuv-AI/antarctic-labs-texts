Dear Editor,

We read with interest the prospective cohort study by Kotal et al. on acute paraquat poisoning, an area of significant public health relevance in India.1 The authors deserve credit for systematically documenting cases from the tertiary care center in eastern India and for highlighting the alarming mortality associated with paraquat poisoning. Their work contributes important regional data and reinforces the urgent need for preventive strategies.

At the same time, we would like to raise certain methodological considerations. First, the study does not specify standardized definitions for key outcomes such as acute kidney injury, acute liver injury, and acute respiratory distress syndrome. Without reference to accepted criteria (e.g., KDIGO, biochemical thresholds, Berlin definition), there is a risk of outcome misclassification, which limits reproducibility and comparability with other cohorts.

Second, there are important concerns regarding exposure measurement and missing data. The ingested volume of paraquat was self-reported and dichotomized at >10 mL without justification, producing an unstable odds ratio with a very wide confidence interval. In addition, urine dithionite testing was performed in only 39 of 70 patients, with missing results mainly in those who were anuric or died early. This pattern of missingness is nonrandom and undermines the prognostic claims made for this test.

Finally, the generalizability of findings is restricted, as nearly all patients were referrals with a median delay of 48 hours to tertiary care. Such referral bias means the cohort likely overrepresents severe disease, limiting applicability to the broader spectrum of paraquat ingestion.

While the authors conclude with a call for banning paraquat—a sentiment we strongly agree with—the current analysis does not establish robust prognostic markers or risk stratification models. To strengthen future studies, we recommend prespecifying diagnostic definitions, employing continuous exposure modeling with appropriate regression (risk ratios rather than odds ratios for common outcomes), and addressing missing data with sensitivity analyses. Incorporating severity scores such as APACHE II or SOFA would further enhance generalizability.2

In conclusion, while Kotal et al. contribute importantly to understanding paraquat poisoning in Eastern India, strengthening details around standard definitions, exposure measurement, and referral bias mitigation would enhance the article's interpretability and utility for clinicians and policymakers.
