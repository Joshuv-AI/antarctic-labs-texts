Frequency, kinetics, and clinical significance of HHV-6 DNAemia after PTCy-based hematopoietic cell transplant∗
Authors: Elisabetta Xue, Sanchita Das, Hyoyoung Choo-Wosoba, Dimana Dimitrova, Mustafa A. Hyder, Francis A. Flomerfelt, Brian Dawson, William Telford, Kalpana Upadhyaya, Kamil A. Rechache, Christopher G. Kanakry, Jennifer A. Kanakry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•Many HCT recipients have high-level HHV-6 DNAemia spikes at 3 to 5 weeks after HCT, usually without serious associated complications.•HHV-6 spikes typically decline rapidly without therapy, but in some, HHV-6 remains detectable in CD4+ T cells, presumably as latent virus.

Introduction

Human herpesvirus 6 (HHV-6) is a ubiquitous infection of childhood that establishes latency through chromosomal integration in the subtelomeric region of multiple cell types, including leukocytes, bone marrow progenitors, and brain tissue.1,2 Detection of HHV-6 in the blood has been widely reported after allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplant (HCT). As with other latent herpesviruses, detection of HHV-6 DNAemia in whole blood and plasma3 does not necessarily equate to reactivation. Although HHV-6 has the potential to contribute to morbidity and mortality,4 HHV-6–associated disease is rare.5 Commonly accepted HHV-6–associated diseases are encephalitis, myelosuppression, and possibly pneumonitis.6,7 HHV-6 has also been associated with increased transplant-related mortality.4 Other clinical syndromes have been reported,7,8 as well as negative HHV-6 effects on T-cell reconstitution.9,10 However, the clinical significance of these associations and their direct vs indirect nature require further investigation.

As with the lymphotropic latent herpesvirus Epstein-Barr virus (EBV),11 HHV-6 DNAemia may reflect shedding of latent viral DNA from cells of high turnover; indeed, HHV-6 has been demonstrated in the blood, saliva, gastrointestinal tract, glial cells, and tonsillar epithelia of healthy individuals without acute infection.12 Thus, the possible interpretations of HHV-6 detection in the blood after HCT may span from being clinically insignificant, indirectly significant as a prognostic marker, or serving as a marker of clinically significant herpesvirus infection. Differing from preemptive therapy for cytomegalovirus (CMV), preemptive antiviral therapy for HHV-6 has a less favorable risk-benefit ratio and does not decrease the incidence of HHV-6 end-organ diseases.13,14 This could be due to a short time between first detection in the blood and development of symptoms,15 but also could suggest that most lytic, and therefore antiviral-sensitive, infection is transient, rendering organ disease rare.

HHV-6 DNAemia can be derived from either the host or the donor graft.16 Detection of HHV-6 in the blood has been reported as an early post-HCT event,13 occurring a median time of 3 weeks after HCT,15,17 usually followed by rapid decay of DNAemia.15 Incidence ranges from 30% to 70% across the studies,5,18 likely also due to heterogeneity in monitoring and detection, with most centers testing for HHV-6 DNAemia only if clinically suspected. Registry data show that non-CMV herpesviruses detection after HCT is associated with increased transplant-related mortality, and HHV-6, not EBV, was the dominant contributor.5 Registry data also show that posttransplantation cyclophosphamide (PTCy)–based graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) prophylaxis is associated with higher rates of non-CMV herpesviruses detection after HCT,5 particularly in those patients receiving HLA-haploidentical grafts.

Across 235 PTCy-treated patients treated on 6 interventional allogeneic HCT clinical trials, we have prospectively monitored HHV-6 in whole blood in real-time on a weekly basis from start of conditioning through day +100 and at serial later time points. Herein, we analyzed the frequency, kinetics, and clinical associations of HHV-6 DNAemia.

Methods

Patient and transplant characteristics

From 2015 to 2024, 235 HCTs were performed under trials NCT03922724, NCT03983850, NCT04959175, NCT05436418, NCT03663933, and NCT02579967 within the National Cancer Institute. All trials used PTCy-based GVHD prophylaxis, regardless of HLA match or donor/recipient relatedness. Based on the individual study protocol designs (supplemental Table 1), the conditioning regimens included myeloablative (MAC; busulfan-fludarabine), reduced-intensity (fludarabine-melphalan with low-dose total body irradiation, fludarabine-cyclophosphamide low-dose total body irradiation, or pentostatin-cyclophosphamide-busulfan19), and/or non-MAC (pentostatin-cyclophosphamide) intensities, with or without horse antithymocyte globulin. Per protocol, graft sources included either bone marrow or peripheral blood stem cells, and adjunct immunosuppression consisted of sirolimus or tacrolimus, with or without mycophenolate mofetil.

Patients with <28 days of follow-up (n = 4) or with previous HCT within 1 year (n = 9) were excluded. Donor-recipient pairs with either recipient (n = 1) or donor (n = 1) or both (n = 3) having >5.5 × log10 copies per mL of HHV-6 before HCT were assumed to have endogenous HHV-6 (eHHV-620; formerly referred to as inherited chromosomally integrated HHV-66) and were excluded. Data were censored at the time of post-HCT unplanned chemotherapy or cellular therapy. Patients are routinely followed up in our institution for at least 100 days after HCT and then returned to local care but return to the National Cancer Institute at 6 months, 9 months (3 studies), 1 year, twice yearly for the second year, and yearly thereafter for 5 years.

HHV-6 quantification

HHV-6 levels in whole blood were prospectively monitored weekly in the National Institutes of Health Clinical Center clinical laboratory from the start of conditioning until day +100 after HCT and at each subsequent follow-up as indicated earlier, using a laboratory-developed real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR). The assay targeted a 155–base pair region of the UL38 region of the HHV-6 genome. The multiplexed TaqMan PCR used 2 probes, each specific for HHV-6A or HHV-6B. An internal control was spiked into every specimen to control PCR inhibition and to assess extraction efficiency; commercially available standards for HHV-6A and HHV-6B were used to generate standard curves, which were then used for viral quantitation.

When tested in bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL), qualitative HHV-6 was reported qualitatively without viral DNA quantification; HHV-6 was measured and quantified on cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) when clinically indicated. HHV-6 qPCR quantification was also performed in plasma and sorted cell subsets on selected fresh or cryopreserved specimens based on high-level whole-blood HHV-6 levels at that time to identify the blood compartment(s) in which the HHV-6 resided. Peripheral blood leukocyte cell sorting was performed by fluorescent-activated cell sorting to isolate CD3+CD4+, CD3+CD8+, CD3−CD56+ (natural killer [NK]), CD14+ (monocytes), and CD15+CD14− (neutrophils) cells (supplemental Table 2; supplemental Figure 1). In 4 patients, CD19+ cells were sorted instead of neutrophils. The analytical measurement range of the qPCR was 2.40 × log10 copies per mL (∼250 copies per mL) to 6.4 × log10 copies per mL (∼2.5 × 106 copies per mL).

Data collection

The database was locked on 20 December 2024. For patients with HHV-6 detection, signs/symptoms occurring around HHV-6 first detection and spike were collected, including fever, rash, gastrointestinal symptoms, neurologic symptoms, pneumonitis, and transaminitis. Because all patients were participating in a clinical trial, prospective clinical attributions of these early posttransplant signs/symptoms were performed by the treating providers and the research team, based on laboratory/radiologic findings and/or the resolution upon treatment. In this study, we defined as unexplained all symptoms/signs that were not supported by a diagnosis of GVHD, documented infection, drug/chemotherapy toxicity, sinusoidal obstruction syndrome, and/or engraftment/cytokine release syndrome. Fever of unknown origin was defined as fever without a documented source of infection or clear immunologic etiology.

For patients without HHV-6 detection, signs/symptoms occurring between days +14 to +35 after HCT, the timeframe when HHV-6 spikes typically occurred, were collected.

Statistical analysis

For cumulative incidence of HHV-6 DNAemia, competing-risk events included death or subsequent cell therapy or chemotherapy. Spearman correlation was used to compare HHV-6 DNAemia in whole blood and plasma samples. The associations of variables of interest and the HHV-6 DNAemia level were examined through univariate analyses by conducting a Fisher exact test, an exact Cochran-Armitage test, and an exact Wilcoxon rank-sum test for categorical, ordinary, and continuous characteristics of the variables, respectively. Multiple variable logistic regression analyses were performed as per the supplemental Methods. Nonrelapse mortality and overall survival were assessed using both log-rank test and a time-specific test at year 1 (Fisher exact test); P values were adjusted using the Holm method to control the family-wise error rate.

The study was approved by the National Institutes of Health institutional review board, and all participants provided written informed consent. Participants were enrolled in one of the subsequent clinical trials registered at www.clinicaltrials.gov (identifiers: NCT04959175, NCT05436418, NCT02579967, NCT03983850, NCT03922724, and NCT03663933).

Results

HHV-6 DNAemia incidence, kinetics, and risk factors

Of 235 total patients, 217 (92%) met study criteria and were included in this analysis (Table 1); 149 patients had at least 1 post-HCT HHV-6 detection in whole blood, for a day-100 cumulative incidence of HHV-6 DNAemia of 69% (95% confidence interval [CI], 62-74); all but 1 were HHV-6 variant B. HHV-6 DNAemia became detectable at a median 21 days after HCT (range, before HCT to 98 days; supplemental Figure 2); 86 patients (40%) had peak DNAemia that was considered low (<4 × log10 copies per mL; Figure 1A), whereas 63 (29%) reached ≥4 × log10 copies per mL (high-level HHV-6 DNAemia; Figure 1B) at a median 27 days (range, 20-91) from HCT and 8 days (range, −5 to +74) from the day of neutrophil engraftment. Of 62 patients with high-level HHV-6B DNAemia, all but 1 experienced DNAemia spike early after HCT, before day +48; 1 patient, after having very low HHV-6 DNAemia, had a late spike reaching 5.34 × log10 copies per mL at day +98, during multiple post-HCT complications including late-onset sinusoidal obstruction syndrome, recurrent CMV reactivation, and multiorgan failure. Given the likely different biology for this late detection, analysis of risk factors and associated symptoms were conducted excluding this patient. Of the remaining 61 patients, reduction to <3 × log10 copies per mL occurred within 4 weeks in 45 patients (73%), including 25 patients in which it occurred within 1 week (40%; Figure 1C-D). In the remaining 17 (27%), detection of >3 × log10 copies per mL persisted for >1 month (Figure 1E), with a slower trend of decreasing values over time, many with subsequent fluctuation of HHV-6B DNAemia again of >3 × log10 copies per mL in the following weeks. Among the 55 patients with high-level HHV-6B DNAemia with follow-up through day +100, over half had no (16%) or unquantifiable low-positive HHV-6 (42%) on all subsequent follow-up checks, whereas 23 (42%) had quantifiable HHV-6 on subsequent checks; of these, 5 (9%) had levels ≥3 × log10 copies per mL. At last evaluation, 48 of 163 (29%) surviving patients still had detectable HHV-6 in whole blood, up to 3.83 × log10 copies per mL.

Early post-HCT high-level HHV-6 DNAemia was more frequent among patients with the following univariate factors: HCT for hematologic malignancy, MAC, marrow graft, HLA-haploidentical donor, reduced-dose PTCy, or sirolimus as PTCy-adjunct GVHD prophylaxis (supplemental Table 3). Multivariate analysis was limited by grouping of several variables based on the protocol-specified HCT platforms but showed that high-level HHV-6 DNAemia was associated with MAC (supplemental Table 4).

HHV-6 distribution within blood compartments

HHV-6 DNAemia in plasma and sorted cellular subsets was quantified in selected available samples to study HHV-6 distribution in the peripheral blood. There was a strong correlation between whole-blood initial spike level and same-day plasma levels (n = 48 paired samples; Spearman ρ = 0.85; P < .0001; Figure 2A). We analyzed plasma longitudinally before and after whole-blood spikes in 17 patients: HHV-6 appeared and peaked in plasma and whole blood simultaneously; however, it disappeared from plasma within 2 weeks but tended to persist for much longer in whole blood (Figure 2B-C). Thus, there were no cases in which HHV-6 was still positive in plasma after becoming negative in whole blood. Clinical findings associated temporally with HHV-6 spikes

During HHV-6 spike, 79% of patients developed at least 1 unexplained clinical finding. Compared with events in weeks 3 to 5 after HCT among patients without high-level HHV-6 DNAemia, patients with high-level HHV-6 DNAemia had higher incidences of fever of unknown origin (36% vs 21%; P = .036), transaminitis (52% vs 27%; P = .001), rash (27% vs 12%; P = .009), and gastrointestinal symptoms (23% vs 7%; P = .0036). The only patient with HHV-6A DNAemia had a spike on day +21 and concomitantly developed fever, transaminitis, and gastrointestinal symptoms of unclear etiology; all symptoms resolve spontaneously without treatment.

Neurologic disease and neurologic symptoms

Seven patients in our study population had unexplained neurologic symptoms, including confusion, anterograde amnesia, and neuralgia: 5 and 3 had concomitant HHV-6 DNAemia of >4 × log10 copies per mL and >5 × log10 copies per mL, respectively; in 2 of these cases, CSF analysis was performed and both excluded HHV-6 involvement. All episodes resolved spontaneously after a median 7 days (interquartile range, 5-10).

Although HHV-6 DNAemia of >5 × log10 copies per mL has been reported to have high specificity for HHV-6 encephalitis,21,22 15 of 19 (79%) patients with >5 × log10 copies per mL had no neurologic symptoms.

We documented 1 case of definitive HHV-6–related post-HCT acute limbic encephalitis confirmed by elevated HHV-6 in CSF and supported by magnetic resonance imaging findings: neurologic symptoms developed 27 days after HCT and were accompanied by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone, fever, rash, and transaminitis. Complete resolution without long-term sequelae was achieved with supportive care and 8 weeks of combinatorial treatment with ganciclovir and foscarnet. Of note, on day +28, during symptom onset, HHV-6 spiked to 5.68 × log10 copies per mL and 5.34 × log10 copies per mL in whole blood and plasma, respectively, and slowly decreased over time, remaining at >3 × log10 copies per mL in whole blood but was negative in plasma, up to the last follow-up 3 years after HCT, despite the clinical absence of symptoms by 110 days after HCT. During the initial event, HHV-6 could be found in CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, NK cells, monocytes, and neutrophils, whereas at the 3-year evaluation, it was found almost exclusively in CD4+ T cells. Interestingly, this same patient developed numerous herpesvirus-related post-HCT complications, including CMV infection requiring preemptive therapy (4 months after HCT), shingles after acyclovir discontinuation and live varicella-zoster virus vaccination (2 years after HCT), and herpes simplex virus-2 labialis (3 years after HCT) despite being permanently off systemic immunosuppression since day +80.

HHV-6 in the BAL fluid

In the first 100 days after HCT, HHV-6 was found in BAL fluid in 10 of 19 tested patients, 6 of whom had concomitant HHV-6 in the blood. HHV-6 was a secondary finding, with all 10 patients being concomitantly diagnosed with bacterial (n = 6) and/or viral (n = 6) pulmonary infection. In 2 cases, HHV-6 was demonstrated to persist in BAL for >1 month; however, its pathogenic role was unclear, and DNAemia quantification in BAL fluid was not available.

Other HCT outcomes associated with HHV-6 spikes

We observed a trend toward lower lymphocyte counts during early high-level HHV-6 spikes (Figure 4A). Although on day +14 there was no statistically significant difference in T-cell, B-cell, and NK cell counts (Figure 4B), on day +28 CD4+ T-cell counts were significantly lower in patients with concurrent high-level HHV-6 DNAemia than those without (27 vs 56 cells per μL; P = .0013; Figure 4C). Notably, at day +100, there were no statistically significant differences seen in CD3+, CD4+, and CD8+ T-cell reconstitution between the 2 groups. Interferon gamma enzyme-linked immunospot assay was performed serially on day +28, +100, and +365 in 10 patients each with and without high-level HHV-6 spikes and did not show consistent trends between these 2 groups (supplemental Figure 3). Impact of antiviral treatment on HHV-6 DNAemia

Other than for the patient diagnosed with HHV-6 encephalitis, no HHV-6–directed treatment was given. Twelve patients with ongoing HHV-6–positive DNAemia were started on antivirals for concurrent CMV infection; in all but 1 case, HHV-6 continued to be detectable in whole blood after antiviral start. No data on plasma HHV-6 DNAemia were available in these patients to correlate with antiviral treatment.

Discussion

Uniform prospective monitoring demonstrates that the incidence of HHV-6 DNAemia after PTCy-based HCT is very high. Spikes in HHV-6 DNAemia, particularly elevations in plasma, are often transient and resolve without intervention, and high-level HHV-6 DNAemia did not forebode serious HHV-6–associated disease, suggesting an unlikely role of preemptive antiviral therapy to accelerate HHV-6 clearance or prevent serious HHV-6–associated diseases.17,23 The term “reactivation” has been avoided in this study because HHV-6 DNAemia should not immediately be interpreted to be, and acted upon as, lytic, replicating virus that is susceptible to, or warrants, antiviral therapy.

The high incidence of HHV-6 DNAemia in our population is in line with previous observations of PTCy-treated patients5 and may represent a higher incidence than in calcineurin inhibitor–based platforms.8 In our PTCy-treated cohort, high-level HHV-6 DNAemia was associated with hematologic malignancy, MAC, marrow graft, reduced-dose PTCy, sirolimus use, and HLA-partially mismatched donor. However, given the trial-required treatment platform, there was substantial collinearity between variables. For example, protocol NCT03983850 used a platform of MAC, HLA-haploidentical bone marrow with reduced-dose PTCy and sirolimus for hematologic malignancies; thus, disentangling the relative contribution of each variable on HHV-6 DNAemia is impossible. If there is indeed a higher rate of HHV-6 reactivation with reduced-dose PTCy, this may relate to stronger immune activation, because other infections, including CMV, appear reduced.24

HHV-6 encephalitis was a very rare event in our cohort, occurring in 1 patient with multiple longitudinal issues related to herpesvirus control. Incidence of HHV-6 encephalitis has been reported at 1% to 12% in HCT recipients, reaching higher rates in cord blood recipients.21 Although previous studies suggested that HHV-6 DNAemia of ≥5 × log10 copies per mL is specific for the development of HHV-6–associated encephalitis,21,22 only 1 of 19 patients in our cohort with documented HHV-6 DNAemia of ≥5 × log10 copies per mL developed encephalitis. Thus, very-high-level HHV-6 DNAemia should prompt close clinical monitoring for neurologic symptoms, but alone it is insufficient for HHV-6 CNS disease and thus should not prompt radiographic evaluation or CSF sampling in the absence of clinical symptoms. Given the rarity, HHV-6 encephalitis might actually herald an idiosyncratic, broader vulnerability in such patients to other herpesvirus-related complications and inform the need for closer monitoring.

Although severe manifestations such as encephalitis were rare, most patients with high-level HHV-6 DNAemia developed at least 1 transient, otherwise unexplained sign/symptom during spikes, including fever, rash, transaminitis, and/or gastrointestinal symptoms. Most importantly, the incidences of these signs/symptoms were higher than in those without HHV-6 spikes. Although the causative relationship between HHV-6 spikes and these symptoms cannot be confirmed, this association still suggests that HHV-6 should be considered among possible etiologies when other more frequent causes have been ruled out. Alternatively, these occurrences may represent transient low-level alloimmunity, and HHV-6 DNAemia may be an epiphenomenon. The detection of HHV-6 in BAL of patients with early post-HCT pneumonitis has been associated with increased risk of fatal respiratory failure.7 In our cohort, HHV-6 was found in BAL in almost 50% of the tested cases, although, given also the lack of HHV-6 quantification, its pathogenic role was unclear. Notably, not all had concomitant HHV-6 in the blood, confirming a previous study suggesting replication and viral shedding in the lungs can occur independently from the peripheral blood.7

HHV-6 DNAemia peaked simultaneously in plasma and whole blood but tended to clear more quickly from plasma, in line with previous reports.25 We hypothesize that plasma detection might represent a brief period of lytic reactivation and virion production in the periengraftment period that is quickly controlled endogenously. Indeed, we observed lower day +28 CD4+ T-cell reconstitution in patients who had high-level HHV-6 DNAemia early after HCT, supporting the lympholytic nature of this virus; this detrimental effect on CD4+ T-cell numbers was transient and was not sustained at later time points. The spike and decline of HHV-6 DNAemia in PTCy-treated patients, coupled with the low incidence of treatment-requiring HHV-6 disease, argues that PTCy recipients have early and sufficient control of HHV-6 to prevent uncontrolled infection. The lack of differences in HHV-6–specific T-cell numbers between high-level vs low-level DNAemia suggests the magnitude of this immune control may not be directly correlated with the level of HHV-6 DNAemia. The lack of HHV-6 DNAemia clearance in whole blood in response to therapy for concurrent CMV infection further supports the hypothesis that many detections, particularly later post-HCT, represent latent virus harbored in CD4+ T cells, which would not respond to lytic virus–directed therapy. Approximately 29% of survivors in our study had long-term detectable HHV-6 in whole blood, in line with previous reports on rate of detectable HHV-6 in healthy individuals.12,26 Of note, in some long-term HCT survivors, HHV-6 became detectable again after a period of weeks to months of negativity: it is unclear whether this was a result of continued low-level lytic infection of immune cells, dynamic reconstitution, and trafficking of latently infected immune cells, or technical limitation in detecting low-level HHV-6 DNAemia.15

A supplementary explanation to effective immune control of HHV-6 could be that HHV-6 kills infected cells: entrance of HHV-6B into host cells relies on CD134/OX-40,27 which is expressed on activated cells and also may modulate Foxp3+ regulatory T-cell function. It is unknown whether this likely lytic viral activity might be selectively killing activated T cells, or whether it may be modulating the balance between conventional vs regulatory T cells. In this paradigm, HHV-6 spiking may be a biomarker of stronger alloreactivity and persistence of HHV-6–bearing CD4+ T cells would suggest persistence of previously activated CD4+ T cells. Indeed, in our cohort, high-level HHV-6 DNAemia appeared to be associated with acute and chronic GVHD, as previously reported,28 which fits with this hypothesis. We have previously shown that alloreactive T cells do persist after PTCy,29 yet only some patients develop GVHD likely due to the presence of regulatory immune populations.29, 30, 31, 32 Whether HHV-6 is “pruning” some activated alloreactive or pathogen-specific T-cell populations is unknown. Studies in non-HCT settings pointed at association of HHV-6, including eHHV-6B, which is known to be able to reactivate as a transmissible virus, with specific autoimmune conditions33,34; further supporting that HHV-6 detection may be an indirect marker of activated T cells. Whether HHV-6 levels truly serve as a biomarker of increased predisposition to acute and/or chronic GVHD requires further study.

Whole-blood specimens are not suited to distinguish latent vs lytic infection and thus a positive DNAemia in whole blood should not be immediately interpreted as active replication. If whole blood and plasma specimens are evaluated concurrently, this may afford insights into the lytic vs latent virus composition, because HHV-6 DNAemia detection in plasma appeared transient, which is more suggestive of a lytic kinetic. However, even latent virus can be extruded from cells into plasma, so detection in plasma in individuals with eHHV-6 is expected chronically. Conversely, lack of detection in plasma in the setting of whole-blood detection should reassure against the presence of substantial lytic virus. This flips the perspective on the potential significance of HHV-6 in the blood of PTCy-treated patients and aligns it more with our understanding of EBV-associated disease risk in PTCy-treated patients, which is very low despite frequent EBV detection in the blood.

More work is needed to demonstrate whether administering antivirals during the HHV-6 spike, in the absence of proven organ disease, would be of any short- or long-term benefit. Given the paucity of severe clinical manifestations herein, the clinical utility of serial HHV-6 monitoring in PTCy-treated HCT recipients appears limited; nevertheless, measuring HHV-6 levels in the presence of otherwise unexplained clinical symptoms could provide an explanation for such events.

The strengths of our study include uniform, prospective, weekly measurements of HHV-6 DNAemia along with prospectively captured clinical episodes. This study has some limitations: because virus levels spiked and declined often within a few days, weekly measurements likely missed some peaks, thus incorrectly categorizing some patients into the low HHV-6 DNAemia group. This limitation, as well as the merits of measuring both in whole blood and plasma, should be taken into consideration in the design of future research studies. Immunohistochemistry staining and qPCR for HHV-6 DNA were not performed on tissue, limiting direct associations. Tissue assessment would be necessary to understand the relationship between rash, transaminitis, and other unexplained clinical findings and HHV-6. Finally, the study design involved 6 clinical trials, each with distinctive treatment platforms that included strong collinearities among transplant-specific variables. Thus, multivariate analyses were limited in their ability to identify dominant risk factors for HHV-6 DNAemia. Similarly, it is unclear whether HHV-6 truly independently associates with GVHD risk, as reported by others,28 or whether it is tracking with other transplant-specific features that associate with GVHD.

In conclusion, we comprehensively characterized kinetics of HHV-6 DNAemia in a large cohort of PTCy-based HCT recipients and observed a very low incidence of encephalitis despite frequent high-level HHV-6 DNAemia. We found that HHV-6 is associated with otherwise unexplained clinical signs/symptoms. Finally, the association between HHV-6 and increased risk of GVHD is of potential immunologic, biologic, and clinical significance that invites further study.
