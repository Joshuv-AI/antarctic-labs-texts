Diagnostic evaluation of pleural effusion in patients with non-small lung cancer affects time to diagnosis and treatment initiation
Authors: Julie Lin, Samira Shojaee, Basim Ali, Roland Bassett, David Ost, Horiana Grosu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalignant pleural effusion (MPE) is a common complication in patients with non-small cell lung cancer and often signifies advanced disease. Timely diagnosis of MPE is critical for initiating appropriate therapy; however, cytologic analysis from thoracentesis has limited sensitivity, which often results in diagnostic delays due to the need for repeat procedures. We sought to identify factors associated with these delays.MethodsWe conducted a multicenter retrospective cohort study of adult patients (≥18 years) with presumed or confirmed non-small cell lung cancer and no radiographic evidence of distant metastasis who underwent their first diagnostic intervention for a pleural effusion (either thoracentesis or pleuroscopy). The primary outcome was time from the initial diagnostic pleural intervention to initiation of cancer treatment. Linear regression was used to evaluate associations between clinical variables and diagnostic/treatment delays.ResultsA total of 136 patients were included. Factors associated with delays from first pleural intervention to treatment initiation included final pathologic stage, the cytologic/histopathologic diagnosis at the first intervention, and number of interventions. Patients who did not receive a diagnosis at the first intervention experienced an average delay of 14.9 days compared with those diagnosed with malignancy at the first intervention. Each additional intervention was associated with a 19.4-day increase in time to treatment initiation. Similarly, time to definitive diagnosis increased by 24.5 days per additional intervention. Patients with a prior cancer history experienced an average delay of 13.9 days. Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) scores 1 or 3 were associated with shorter diagnostic delays compared with scores of 0 or 2.ConclusionsDiagnostic delays in patients with non-small cell lung cancer and pleural effusion are significantly influenced by the results of the initial intervention and the need for repeat procedures. Earlier use of definitive diagnostic modalities such as pleuroscopy may be associated with reduced time to diagnosis and treatment initiation, particularly in patients with a high clinical suspicion of MPE.Keywords: Pleural effusion, non-small cell lung cancer, thoracentesis, pleuroscopy

Introduction

Pleural effusions are a common clinical condition encountered worldwide, representing a significant work load for pulmonologists (1,2). In the United States alone, there are approximately 1.5 million new pleural effusions diagnosed each year (3). These pleural effusions can be caused by a variety of underlying conditions, including congestive heart failure, pneumonia, malignancy, and pulmonary embolism. In the context of cancer, the presence of pleural effusion may indicate metastatic spread of tumor. Malignant pleural effusions (MPEs) occur in 7% to 15% of lung cancer cases and complicate the course of many other types of cancer (4).

Definitive diagnosis of MPE typically involves thoracentesis with cytologic analysis of pleural fluid or histopathologic analysis of pleural tissue obtained via pleuroscopy, video-assisted thoracoscopy, or image-guided biopsy. Regardless of the underlying cause, the initial diagnostic and therapeutic approach for evaluation of pleural effusion typically begins with thoracentesis. Thoracentesis is widely performed in clinical practice and serves as the cornerstone in evaluation of patients with pleural effusion (5).

Although thoracentesis plays a crucial role in the diagnostic evaluation of pleural effusion, cytology is unfortunately only moderately sensitive in detecting malignancy (6,7). The overall sensitivity of pleural fluid cytology for malignancy was found to be 58% (8). In addition, diagnostic sensitivity varies depending on the type of malignancy. For instance, adenocarcinoma has a higher cytologic detection rate compared with squamous cell carcinoma or lymphoma (7,9). Because of the low sensitivity of cytology for malignancy, patients often undergo repeat thoracentesis before a more definitive diagnostic procedure such as pleuroscopy or video-assisted thoracoscopy is considered.

Pleuroscopy and video-assisted thoracoscopy for pleural biopsy are considered the gold standard for diagnostic evaluation of pleural disease because they offer the advantage of direct visual examination of the visceral and parietal pleura and allow identification of the appropriate biopsy site (10). Pleuroscopy is associated with a high diagnostic sensitivity for malignancy and has a low complication rate (11,12).

Guidelines generally recommend up to three cytologically negative thoracenteses before considering pleural biopsy to establish a definitive diagnosis. However, in cases where malignancy is highly suspected, clinicians may proceed with pleural biopsy after one or two thoracenteses. This stepwise approach can introduce delays in diagnosis and treatment initiation for some patients.

This multicenter retrospective study was designed to evaluate the time to diagnosis of MPE in patients with non-small cell lung cancer, aiming to identify factors that are associated with delays in diagnosis and treatment initiation. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2025-1-2527/rc).

Methods

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was performed with Institutional Review Board approval (IRB number PA 2020-1116 and HM20017646) from MD Anderson Cancer Center and Virginia Commonwealth University, respectively. Data were obtained from a retrospective multicenter cohort of consecutive patients who underwent their first pleural intervention for a pleural effusion between January 1, 2010 and December 31, 2021. We cross-referenced our dataset with the MD Anderson Tumor Registry for validation. The need for individual consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study design.

Patient population

We included adult patients aged 18 years and older with presumed or confirmed non-small cell lung cancer without evidence of metastatic disease (i.e., brain, bone, adrenal), who were treatment-naïve and in whom a diagnosis of MPE would upstage the lung cancer to stage IV (M1a disease due to MPE, based on tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) 9th edition lung cancer staging) and impact treatment decisions. All patients were undergoing their first pleural intervention (thoracentesis or pleuroscopy) for pleural effusion workup.

We excluded patients with strong clinical evidence of metastatic disease defined as imaging showing multiple metastases in a typical clinical pattern, such as positron emission tomography scan or bone scan showing multiple bone metastases, computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging of the brain showing metastatic disease, or CT of the chest/abdomen or pelvis with findings sufficiently definitive for metastatic disease.

The primary outcome was time from the first pleural intervention (thoracentesis or pleuroscopy) to initiation of treatment in patients with presumed or confirmed non-small cell lung cancer without other findings of metastasis.

Statistical analysis

The primary endpoint of interest was the time (in days) from the first pleural intervention (either pleuroscopy or thoracentesis) to definitive diagnosis and the initiation of treatment. Linear regression was used to assess the association between covariates of interest and time intervals (13). All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.1. All statistical tests used a significance level of 5%. No adjustments for multiple testing were made.

Results

A total of 136 patients were included in the analysis. Patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Most patients (n=123, 90%) had thoracentesis as their first pleural intervention, and 86 patients (63%) were diagnosed with malignancy after the first intervention (Table 2). Fifty-two patients (38%) underwent repeat pleural interventions; in 36 of these patients (69%), the reason for repeat pleural intervention was lack of a diagnosis, and in the remaining 16 patients (31%), the repeat pleural intervention was done for molecular markers (Table 3).

In univariate linear regression analysis, factors significantly associated with the time from the first pleural intervention to treatment included final pathologic stage, the cytologic/pathologic diagnosis at the first intervention, and the number of interventions (Table 4). Patients whose first pleural intervention did not show malignant cells experienced, on average, a delay of 14.9 additional days before treatment initiation compared with those who received a diagnosis at the first pleural intervention. When analyzing the number of interventions as a continuous variable, each additional intervention was associated with an average delay of 19.4 days. When categorized, a second intervention added 14.8 days, a third intervention added 48.4 days, and a fourth intervention added 62.0 days (all compared with a single intervention).

In a separate univariate linear regression model evaluating the time from first pleural intervention to definitive diagnosis (Table 5), the following variables were significantly associated with time to diagnosis: Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status, history of cancer, final pathologic stage, cytologic/pathologic diagnosis at first intervention, and number of interventions. An ECOG performance status score of 1 or 3 was associated with a shorter time to diagnosis compared with scores of 0 or 2. Patients with a prior history of cancer were associated with, on average, an additional 13.9-day delay in definitive diagnosis. Patients without malignant cells detected at the first intervention were associated with an average delay of 26.7 days in achieving a definitive diagnosis compared with those who received a definitive diagnosis of malignancy. When considering the number of interventions as a continuous variable, each additional intervention was associated with a 24.5-day delay to definitive diagnosis, on average. When categorized, a second intervention was associated with a 20.9-day delay, 57.6-day delay for a third intervention, and 82.9-day delay for a fourth intervention (all compared with a single intervention).

Discussion

Our study shows that the need for multiple diagnostic pleural interventions is independently associated with significant delays in both establishing a definitive diagnosis and initiating treatment in patients with non-small cell lung cancer and pleural effusion. Specifically, each additional pleural intervention was associated with an added 20 to 60+ days of treatment delay, depending on the number of interventions. Furthermore, patients whose initial pleural intervention did not yield a malignant diagnosis were associated with significantly longer diagnostic timelines, highlighting the consequences of relying on low sensitivity cytologic tests in high-risk populations.

Our findings are consistent with previously published studies. Alzghoul et al. found that when patients with suspected MPE underwent concurrent thoracentesis and biopsy, this intervention was associated with a substantial reduction in time to diagnosis, time to molecular results, and time to oncology consultation (14). Their findings support a more integrated and streamlined diagnostic approach, especially in patients with a high pretest probability of malignancy (14).

Further supporting a more definitive first-line approach, Martinez-Zayas et al. conducted a meta-analysis estimating the diagnostic sensitivity of thoracentesis at ~64% (ranging from ~45% in mesothelioma to ~82% in breast cancer), whereas thoracoscopy achieved a sensitivity of ~93% (15). Complication rates were low and similar between the two modalities (~4%), making thoracoscopy a diagnostically superior and relatively safe alternative (15).

Ongoing cost analysis studies to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of thoracoscopy vs a second thoracentesis after a first thoracentesis that was cytologically negative for MPE showed that thoracoscopy was a cost-effective strategy, with an incremental cost per complication avoided of $6,217.34, well below the commonly used willingness-to-pay threshold (16). Their sensitivity analysis revealed that thoracoscopy remained cost-effective unless its complication rate exceeded 4%, or its cost rose above $5,928. In contrast, repeat thoracentesis was favored only if its cost dropped below $593 or the prevalence of MPE exceeded 76% in the population, conditions rarely met in real-world clinical settings (16).

Our study does have limitations pertinent to retrospective design, and because of small sample size, selection of participants may not be representative of general population. Complications rates are also underreported due to small sample size. Furthermore, due to small sample size, we are unable to make conclusions regarding certain variables associated with delays such as ECOG. We also realize another factor that may not be representative of the general population is the setting in which the study was conducted which is at an academic center; community-based hospitals or private practice settings may require external referrals for additional workup due to lack of equipment availability or specialists. Another limitation is for patient undergoing pleuroscopy as initial procedure there may be a selection bias based on institutional expertise and pretest probability of malignancy. As far as diagnostic yield limitations, we also recognize that cytology yield varies across centers, influenced by availability of subspecialized thoracic and pleural pathologists, laboratory experience, use of molecular testing, immunohistochemistry cell blocks and specimen handling protocols. We also recognize that CT guided or ultrasound guided pleural biopsies are other modalities to obtain diagnostic pleural biopsies. We did not analyze these two modalities because only two patients in our cohort underwent image guided pleural biopsies.

Taken together, these findings suggest that early use of thoracoscopy or pleural biopsy can be considered in patients being evaluated for a pleural effusion who have non-small cell lung cancer and a high clinical suspicion for MPE, particularly when initial cytology results are negative. Delays introduced by repeated low-sensitivity procedures not only postpone critical treatment decisions but may also adversely affect patient outcomes, quality of life, and health care resource utilization.

Conclusions

In patients with non-small cell lung cancer and high clinical suspicion for MPE, earlier use of definitive diagnostic modalities such as pleuroscopy to diagnose the MPE and stage the lung cancer may be associated with reduced time to diagnosis and treatment initiation.
