High platelet-to-red blood cell ratio and outcomes in trauma patients requiring massive transfusions
Authors: Chi Peng, Yibin Guo, Fan Yang, Qi Chen, Lei Li, Faran Bokhari, Zhichao Jin, Shuogui Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUncontrolled bleeding contributes to 40% of trauma deaths. While higher platelet-to-red blood cell (PLT/RBC) transfusion ratios may improve outcomes, the optimal ratio remains unclear. This study aimed to determine the threshold of PLT/RBC ratio and its impact on in-hospital mortality in trauma patients requiring massive transfusions.MethodsThis retrospective, multicenter study used 2014–2018 National Trauma Database (NTDB) data. Adult patients admitted to Level I/II trauma centers with massive transfusions within 24 h of emergency department (ED) admission were included. Patients were divided into high-PLT group (PLT/RBC ratio > 0.7) and low-PLT group (ratio ≤ 0.7). Primary outcomes: 24-hour and 30-day mortality; secondary outcomes: transfusion-related adverse events.ResultsAmong 9,330 patients (median age 37 (26–54) years; 78.9% male), 46.1% had a high PLT/RBC ratio. Restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a nonlinear relationship: mortality dropped significantly at > 0.7 and stabilized above 1.5. After inverse probability treatment weighting, the high-PLT group showed lower 24-hour mortality (OR, 0.45; 95% CI, 0.42–0.48) and 30-day mortality (OR, 0.66; 95% CI, 0.62–0.70). However, the high ratio group experienced higher rates of adverse events, including pulmonary embolism, acute kidney injury, and sepsis. Subgroup analyses confirmed consistent survival benefits despite increased adverse events. Sensitivity analysis further supported the robustness of these findings.ConclusionsIn this multicenter study, a high PLT/RBC ratio (> 0.7) was associated with improved survival in trauma patients requiring massive transfusions, reducing 24-hour and 30-day mortality. However, it also increased the risk of adverse events, with a ceiling effect observed at ratios above 1.5. These findings underscore the need for high-quality clinical trials to validate the benefits of high PLT/RBC ratios and optimize transfusion strategies for trauma patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13017-025-00645-z.Keywords: Platelet, Transfusion, Trauma patients, Massive bleeding, Survival outcomes

Introduction

Trauma, the leading cause of death among adults in the United States, places a significant burden on the healthcare system [1]. Uncontrolled bleeding poses a major challenge in trauma care, accounting for approximately 40% of trauma-related deaths [2, 3, 4, 5]. Effective trauma management relies on adherence to damage control resuscitation protocols, including the use of tranexamic acid (TXA), fluid management, and high ratios of blood product transfusions [6, 7, 8, 9]. Additionally, preventing traumatic coagulopathy is critical, as it is a key factor associated with high mortality rates in bleeding patients [10, 11].

The role of platelets (PLTs) in trauma-induced coagulopathy (TIC) has gained attention, particularly in relation to mild thrombocytopenia and PLT dysfunction [12, 13]. PLTs play a crucial role in hemostasis, including local activation, aggregation, and thrombin generation, which confer early benefits. Additionally, PLT transfusion may have long-term effects by aiding endothelial recovery and exerting anti-inflammatory actions [14, 15]. A randomized controlled trial of 680 trauma patients showed that high PLT transfusion rates improved hemostatic outcomes and survival [9]. Holcomb et al. [16] found that an increased PLT-to-red blood cell (RBC) ratio was independently associated with reduced mortality at six hours post-injury. Similarly, Inaba et al. [17] investigated the impact of PLT transfusions in trauma patients requiring massive blood transfusions. They found that survival rates increased progressively with higher PLT-to-RBC ratios. While previous research has highlighted the unique role of high PLT/RBC ratio transfusions in trauma populations, the optimal transfusion ratio for this group remains unclear. A more crucial question is, whether there is a ceiling effect on the survival benefit brought by a high PLT/RBC ratio.

We hypothesized that in trauma patients requiring massive transfusions, high PLT/RBC ratios would be inversely associated with in-hospital mortality, and this association would exhibit a nonlinear relationship, where survival benefits plateau beyond a specific PLT/RBC ratio.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a “registry-based secondary analysis” based on the National Trauma Database (NTDB) in the United States. The study covered the period from January 1, 2014, to December 31, 2018. The NTDB, collected and managed by the American College of Surgeons, encompasses data from over 780 trauma centers nationwide. It provides detailed information on blood product utilization at two time points after patient arrival in the emergency department (ED): 4 h and 24 h. The Institutional Review Board of the Naval Medical University approved the study protocol, and informed consent was waived due to the de-identified nature of the data. This study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [18].

Study participants

Patients over 18 years of age who were admitted to Level I or II trauma centers and received massive transfusions (defined as more than 10 units of RBCs within the first 24 h of ED admission) were included in the study [19]. Patients with missing transfusion information, burn injuries, incomplete outcomes, orthose who died before reaching the ED/weretransferred to another facility while in the ED, were excluded. To address potential survival bias, we excluded patients whose PLT/RBC ratios were inconsistent between the 4-hour and 24-hour time points. Consistency in the ratio was defined as an absolute difference in PLT and RBC of less than 1 at both time points. This approach aimed to eliminate patients who received “compensatory transfusions” after the initial 4 h [20].

Exposure and primary outcome measure

We categorized patients into two groups based on the ratio of PLT to RBC levels. The primary outcomes of the study were 24-hour mortality and 30-day mortality, while secondary outcomes included the occurrence of transfusion-related adverse events (pulmonary embolism (PE), deep vein thrombosis (DVT), acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), acute kidney injury (AKI), and sepsis). For the time distribution of outcome, most deaths (especially those within 24 h) occurred before the peak period of complications, and complications were predominantly concentrated in patients who survived beyond 72 h. To ensure consistency and comparability in transfusion volumes, we converted all transfusion data for the analyzed blood products to milliliters using standardized conversion factors. Specifically, 1 unit of blood product was defined as follows: RBC, 300 mL; FFP, 250 mL; and PLT, 50 mL [21, 22, 23]. According to previous literature [9, 22, 23], platelet units were defined as single-donor components derived from whole blood (50 mL/unit), with the actual therapeutic dose administered clinically consisting of a 300 mL pooled unit formed by combining 6 such units, consistent with the standardized calculation methods in studies like PROPPR [9]. The PLT/RBC ratio was defined as the ratio of units of blood products (one single-donor PLT concentrate to one RBC unit).

Statistical analysis

Restricted cubic splines were used to explore the potential nonlinear relationship between the PLT/RBC ratio and 24-hour in-hospital mortality. The optimal knots for the model were selected based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). Confounding factors included age, race, ethnicity, sex, medical history (Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD), Cerebrovascular Accident (CVA), diabetes, hypertension, Myocardial Infarction (MI)), initial systolic blood pressure upon arrival at the ED, heart rate, respiratory rate, Glasgow Coma Score (GCS) , Injury Severity Score (ISS), type of injury, receipt of hemorrhage control procedures within 24 h, total plasma volume transfused within 24 h, hospital trauma level, hospital bed count, hospital type, and teaching status.

To balance baseline characteristics among populations with different platelet transfusion ratios, we constructed an inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) propensity score. The propensity score model estimated the probability of each patient receiving a high PLT/RBC ratio based on relevant characteristics, including age, race, ethnicity, sex, medical history (COPD, CVA, diabetes, hypertension, MI), initial systolic blood pressure upon arrival at the ED, heart rate, respiratory rate, GCS score, ISS score, type of injury, receipt of hemorrhage control procedures within 24 h, total plasma volume transfused within 24 h, hospital trauma level, hospital bed count, hospital type, and teaching status. The IPTW weights, calculated as the inverse of the probability of receiving the actual treatment, were used to balance baseline characteristics across groups. A weighted logistic regression model was then employed to estimate the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) for each outcome and adverse event in the high platelet ratio group.

Subgroup analysis

To further explore the heterogeneity of coagulopathy mechanisms in trauma populations, we conducted subgroup analyses stratified by different trauma groups: blunt trauma patients, penetrating trauma patients, and patients in shock (SBP < 90 mmHg). Additionally, patients were categorized based on the Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS): body region 1 (head) with an AIS score greater than 3, body region 3 (chest) with an AIS score greater than 3, body region 4 (abdomen) with an AIS score greater than 3, and body region 5 (extremities) with an AIS score greater than 3. Restricted cubic splines were employed to analyze the potential nonlinear relationship between various PLT/RBC ratios and 24-hour in-hospital mortality. To control for confounding factors, IPTW was applied, enabling us to examine the primary outcomes across different research contexts.

Sensitivity analysis

To enhance the robustness of our research findings, multiple imputation was used to handle missing data. Specifically, a multivariate imputation method based on sequential regression was employed. The variables to be imputed were gender, race, systolic blood pressure, pulse rate, heart rate, GCS, ISS, and hospital teaching status. The percentage of missing data is provided in eTable 1 of the Supplement. Additionally, to address potential bias from unmeasured confounding factors, we calculated the E-value to further strengthen the robustness of our results [24, 25]. The E-value represents the minimum strength of association required between an unmeasured confounder and both the outcome and the exposure factor to fully explain the observed treatment-outcome relationship.

Results

Restricted cubic splines for outcomes

The inclusion and exclusion criteria for the study are presented in Fig. 1. A total of 9,330 patients were included in the analysis, with a median age of 37 years (interquartile range (IQR): 26–54 years). Of these, 1,969 (21.1%) were female and 7,357 (78.9%) were male (Table 1). Restricted cubic spline curves were used to depict the 24-hour in-hospital mortality rates for the overall cohort and subgroups (Fig. 2). A significant trend was observed in the relationship between the PLT/RBC ratio and in-hospital mortality. When the PLT/RBC ratio approached 0, the OR was approximately 5, but it decreased rapidly as the ratio increased. It was found that when the ratio was below 0.7, the curve intersected with the X-axis (the reference line where the outcome risk is 1, indicating a turning point at which this ratio had a significant impact on the outcome. As the ratio increased further, the OR gradually declined and stabilized at ratios above 1.5, indicating an “upper limit effect”. Subgroup analysis also demonstrated that the PLT/RBC ratio was approximately 0.7 among different subgroups all along (see eTable 2 in the Supplement).

Patient characteristics

The study population was stratified into two groups based on the PLT/RBC ratio: a high PLT/RBC ratio group (ratio > 0.7) and a low PLT/RBC ratio group (ratio ≤ 0.7). Of the 9,330 patients, 4,305 (46.1%) were classified into the high PLT/RBC ratio group. Unweighted patient characteristics are provided in eTable 3 of the Supplement, while treatment details and adverse events are summarized in Table 2. In the low PLT/RBC ratio group, the median (IQR) number of transfusions within 24 h was 18.0 (13.0–28.9) units for RBC and 8.0 (5.0–12.0) units for PLT. In contrast, the high PLT/RBC ratio group received a median of 16.3 (12.5–24.5) units of RBC and 18.0 (12.0–29.4) units of PLT within 24 h. The overall 24-hour in-hospital mortality rate was 30.6% (2,859 patients).

Logistic regression model

The propensity score distributions overlapped between the high PLT/RBC group and the low PLT/RBC group (see eFigure 1 in the Supplement). In the primary analysis, after adjusting for confounding factors using IPTW, the absolute 24-hour in-hospital mortality rates were 22.2% (high PLT group) and 37.9% (low PLT group), with an OR of 0.45 (95% CI: 0.42–0.48) (Fig. 3; Table 2). Similarly, the absolute 30-day in-hospital mortality rates were 41.3% (high PLT/RBC ratio group) and 49.9% (low ratio group), corresponding to an OR of 0.66 (95% CI: 0.62–0.70) (Table 2).

Results of the Sub-analysis

The results of secondary outcomes are shown in Table 3. The incidence of adverse events was significantly higher in the high-ratio group, with the following outcomes: overall adverse event rate (OR: 1.50, 95%CI: 1.40–1.61); PE (OR: 1.22, 95% CI: 1.02–1.45); DVT (OR: 1.37, 95% CI: 1.23–1.53); ARDS (OR: 1.24, 95% CI: 1.08–1.42); AKI (OR: 1.71, 95% CI: 1.55–1.89); sepsis (OR: 1.40, 95% CI: 1.21–1.63). In subgroup analyses, after adjusting for factors such as different mechanisms of injury and various injury sites, the high PLT-ratio group continued to demonstrate a favorable survival prognosis, although the occurrence of adverse events remained elevated (see Fig. 3, eTables 4, 5, 6, 7 and 8 in the Supplement).

Sensitivity analysis

In the multivariable logistic regression analysis for 24-hour in-hospital mortality, the E-value for the OR was 3.87. This suggests that the observed association could potentially be explained by an unmeasured confounder associated with both the treatment and the outcome, with each association having an OR of at least 3.87, conditional on the measured confounders. Similarly, the E-value for 30-day in-hospital mortality was 2.40, further supporting the robustness of the findings. The E-values for adverse outcomes also showed consistent results, as detailed in the eTable 9 of the Supplement.

Discussion

In this multicenter cohort study, we identified a nonlinear relationship between the PLT/RBC ratio and 24-hour in-hospital mortality. The main finding of this study was that a transfusion ratio of PLTs to RBCs greater than 0.7 was associated with improved prognosis. Specifically, lower PLT/RBC ratios demonstrated a clear dose-response relationship with adverse outcomes, whereas higher PLT/RBC ratios exhibited a ceiling effect in terms of favorable outcomes. Our study identified critical thresholds at which the OR changed significantly and determined an optimal transfusion ratio. Subgroup analyses further supported the association between this PLT/RBC ratio and 24-hour mortality, although some variability was observed across subgroups. These findings aligned with previous research, including a large clinical trial that demonstrated that in patients with severe trauma, early transfusion of plasma, platelets, and red blood cells in a 1:1:1 ratio resulted in more effective hemostasis within 24 h and reduced mortality from hemorrhage compared to a 1:1:2 ratio [9]. Other studies have also demonstrated that a higher PLT/RBC ratio was associated with reduced early mortality rates [21, 26, 27]. For instance, Rijnhout et al. [28] found that a higher PLT/RBC ratio significantly reduced mortality rates at 1–6 h, 24 h, and 28–30 days compared to a lower ratio. In some studies, the PLT/RBC ratio with optimal clinical benefits for massive transfusion in trauma patients was approximately 1:1, while other studies suggested that a high PLT/RBC ratio (≥ 0.7) may be associated with increased mortality within the first 1–6 h compared to a lower ratio (< 0.7) [28, 29]. Notably, the optimal threshold for platelet transfusion remains controversial, because it is very difficult to find a balance between saving blood products and reducing the mortality rate [30, 31, 32]. Regarding survival benefit resulting from the high PLT/RBC ratio, previous studies have found that platelets are key effectors in hemostasis and thrombus formation, playing a central role in the recovery process following trauma [33]. The platelet-derived mediators (such as the antifibrinolytic plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 [PAI-1]) or to extracellular vesicles released during storage may also contribute to the survival benefit [34]. Moreover, it is worth mentioning that while a high PLT/RBC ratio can provide survival benefits in certain cases, yet it may also lead to adverse effects in patients. Indeed, our research found that high platelet transfusion proportions were associated with negative outcomes, including PE, DVT, ARDS, AKI, and sepsis. These findings were consistent with previous studies. For example, a study on massive transfusion practices in Japan reported that the incidence of transfusion-related adverse events increased linearly with higher PLT ratios [35]. Similarly, Peralta et al. [36] found that patients in the group receiving a high PLT: RBC ratio had significantly higher rates of ventilator-associated pneumonia (38.9% vs. 10.8%; P = 0.02) and wound infections (50% vs. 10.8%; P = 0.002). Another retrospective study involving 1,777 trauma patients suggested that high doses of platelets may predispose patients to adverse events mediated by platelet aggregation, potentially promoting micro thrombosis, thromboembolic events, and multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS) [37]. As for the pathophysiological mechanisms, previous studies have found that TIC always changes from a hypocoagulable state to a hypercoagulable state, and the increase in platelets may be associated with post-injury venous thromboembolism [10]. In addition, the presence of toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) on platelets can induce coagulation abnormalities, including decreased coagulation intensity shown by thromboelastographic, and simultaneously release pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as IL-6), exacerbating the systemic inflammatory response and organ damage [38]. Moreover, studies have shown that there are quantitative and qualitative defects in all platelet functions in TIC models in both humans and animals, and it indicated that platelets were related to the pathogenesis multiple organ failure [10, 14, 39]. Thus, the high ratio of PLT transfusions may be disadvantageous. It is particularly important to find the threshold of the infusion ratio between low mortality and high complications. The nonlinear relationship between platelet transfusion ratios and mortality across different populations, injury mechanisms, and injury sites supports this hypothesis. Interestingly, we found that a PLT/RBC ratio > 1.5 do not further improve survival, suggesting a ceiling effect. This supports targeting ratios in the 0.7–1.5 range to maximize survival while minimizing risks of adverse events (e.g. pulmonary embolism at very high ratios. Additionally, we observed that in some populations, exceeding 1.5 can lead to increased mortality, which supported our earlier hypothesis that a higher PLT/RBC ratio was not necessarily better. Therefore, we believed that setting the ratio between 1 and 1.5 is reasonable and can provide guidance for clinical decision-making.

Strengths and limitations

For trauma patients requiring massive transfusions, a PLT/RBC ratio between 1:1 and 1:1.5 is recommended, although this recommendation requires validation through randomized controlled trials (RCTs).

Our study also has some limitations. First, it is a retrospective observational study. Although we have adjusted for confounding factors in our model, we did not include transfusion timing, the use of prehospital blood products or medications in our research due to the database’s lack of this information. For instance, owing to the fact that the database only includes data on platelet and packed red blood cell transfusion volumes at two discrete time points (4 h and 24 h post-resuscitation), we are unable to determine the specific time point during resuscitation at which the 0.7–1.5 ratio should be achieved. Second, we are unable to determine the source of platelet products recorded in the trauma database, that is, whether they were obtained through apheresis or pooled from whole blood. Third, due to the retrospective design of this study, causal inference is limited, and results should be only interpreted as associational. Prospective RCTs are needed to validate threshold of PLT/RBC ratios. Finally, this study is based on data from NTDB, and the observed associations may not generalize to healthcare systems with different transfusion protocols or resource allocations. The influence of U.S.-specific factors (e.g., prehospital care systems, blood product availability) on the results should be considered.

Conclusion

This multicenter study revealed that in trauma patients requiring massive transfusions, a high PLT/RBC ratio (greater than 0.7) was associated with improved survival outcomes, manifested in reduced 24-hour and 30-day in-hospital mortality rates. However, it was also accompanied by an increased incidence of adverse events. Moreover, a ceiling effect appeared to exist when the ratio exceeded 1.5. These findings highlighted the necessity for more high-quality clinical trials to verify the benefits of a high PLT/RBC ratio in the trauma patient population.
