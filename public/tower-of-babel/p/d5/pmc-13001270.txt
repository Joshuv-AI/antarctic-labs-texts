Electroretinographic patterns of retinitis pigmentosa in a Palestinian cohort: a clinical and functional characterization
Authors: Ibrahim Taha, Liana Al-Labadi, Khalil Huraibat, Orwa Nasser, Yazan Gammoh, Yousef Awlad Mohammad, Yousef Shanti, Lubna Abu Samrah, Mohammad Abu Safya, Rema Obar, Aya Farah, Tareq Fuqaha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Retinitis pigmentosa (RP) is a progressive rod–cone dystrophy and the most common inherited retinal disease worldwide. Electroretinography (ERG) provides an objective measure of retinal function and is essential for staging severity and guiding therapy. However, ERG data from Middle Eastern populations remain limited. In this cross-sectional study, 71 Palestinian patients with clinically confirmed RP underwent full-field ERG (ffERG) using ISCEV 2022 standards. Responses were classified into mild, moderate, and severe categories. Clinical variables including age, disease duration, best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), and optical coherence tomography (OCT) findings were analyzed. Global ERG severity grading revealed severe impairment in 40.8% of patients; rod-specific DA 0.01 b-wave amplitude-based classification identified severe rod dysfunction in 32.4%. A strong negative cross-sectional correlation was observed between disease duration and DA 0.01 b-wave amplitude (r = − 0.95, p < 0.0001), consistent with cumulative rod photoreceptor loss over time; visual inspection of the scatter plot confirmed a monotonic relationship without evidence of outlier leverage. BCVA and OCT-derived central macular thickness showed no significant correlation with ERG parameters, underscoring the functional–structural dissociation in RP. Genetic testing (35.2% of cases) identified mutations in RPGR, RHO, USH2A, CRB1, and others; genotype–ERG observations are exploratory given small subgroup sizes. These findings establish baseline ERG profiles for Palestinian RP patients, support ERG-based severity grading, and emphasize the importance of electrophysiology for diagnosis, prognostic counseling, and therapeutic eligibility in underrepresented populations.

Introduction

Retinitis pigmentosa (RP) refers to a genetically diverse group of retinal dystrophies marked by progressive degeneration of photoreceptors, beginning predominantly with rod cells and later involving cones. Clinically, at early stages of the disease, this results in night blindness and peripheral visual field constriction, and eventually, at later stages of the disease, a central vision loss. RP is the most common inherited retinal dystrophy worldwide, with an estimated prevalence of approximately 1 in 4,000 individuals [1, 2]. The disease is highly heterogeneous, both phenotypically and genetically, with mutations identified in more than 80 genes to date [2]. Rod photoreceptors are typically the first to degenerate, followed by secondary cone cell death, often due to non-cell-autonomous mechanisms [3]. This degenerative sequence is consistent with the characteristic clinical findings of early scotopic dysfunction followed by photopic decline, as confirmed by electrophysiological testing and imaging modalities [4].

Electroretinography (ERG), particularly full-field ERG, has become a cornerstone in the diagnostic workup and functional assessment of RP. As an objective and quantifiable measure of global retinal function, ERG plays a critical role in both the early detection and longitudinal monitoring of retinal degeneration. In early disease stages, ERG abnormalities can precede visible fundus changes, making it invaluable for identifying subclinical or atypical RP presentations [5]. Moreover, ERG allows functional staging of disease severity by quantifying the amplitude and implicit times of a-wave and b-wave responses, correlating with the integrity of photoreceptor and inner retinal activity [6].

The full-field ERG, following the standardized ISCEV protocol, remains the gold standard for assessing generalized retinal function. This includes scotopic (rod-dominant), photopic (cone-dominant), 30 Hz flicker, and mixed response recordings [7]. These measures are highly sensitive to both disease presence and progression. For example, reductions in rod-mediated b-wave amplitudes are typically among the earliest indicators of RP [8], while cone responses often remain measurable into later stages, especially in autosomal dominant forms [7].

Importantly, ERG also provides prognostic insight. A more severe attenuation of photopic and scotopic responses often correlates with worse visual acuity and more rapid functional decline. Beyond disease confirmation, ERG is increasingly used in therapeutic contexts—such as eligibility screening and efficacy assessment in clinical trials for gene therapy and retinal implants [9, 10].

Multifocal ERG (mfERG) has further enhanced our ability to map localized retinal function, especially in cases of sector RP or patients with preserved central vision. While mfERG is more sensitive for central cone dysfunction, full-field ERG remains indispensable for assessing global rod-cone pathology [11].

Despite extensive global research on ERG applications in RP, there is a notable gap in population-specific data, particularly among Arab and Palestinian cohorts. Given the regional genetic diversity and distinct healthcare access patterns, establishing normative ERG profiles and disease-specific functional markers in this population is essential for accurate diagnosis, personalized care, and future participation in clinical trials [12, 13]. Palestinian patients are of particular relevance given the high consanguinity rates in this region, which increase the prevalence of autosomal recessive RP forms and may result in a distinct distribution of causative mutations compared to outbred Western populations. Notably, we do not propose that ERG waveform morphology fundamentally differs across ethnic groups; rather, the population-specific value lies in documenting mutation prevalence, disease burden, and access to electrophysiological services in this underrepresented community.

This study aims to address this gap by characterizing full-field ERG patterns in a Palestinian cohort diagnosed with RP, evaluating the relationship between electrophysiological metrics and clinical features such as age, visual acuity, and disease duration.

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional descriptive study was conducted at the Optometry Clinic of the Arab American University of Palestine (AAUP), located in Ramallah, West Bank, from October 2022 to June 2025.

Participants

Individuals were recruited based on a confirmed clinical diagnosis of RP. The inclusion criteria were patients presenting with night blindness and peripheral visual field loss, with supportive fundus findings such as bone-spicule pigmentation, vessel attenuation, and optic disc pallor [2]. Genetic testing results were documented when available but were not required for inclusion.

Eligible participants were aged 11 years and older and able to comply with ERG testing. Participants were excluded if they had other ocular pathologies affecting retinal function (for example, diabetic retinopathy or macular degeneration, optic atrophy, foveal hypoplasia, LCA, Best, etc.), or any other anterior segment or media pathologies such as trachoma, corneal scarring, dense cataracts, or poor-quality or incomplete ERG recordings.

Electroretinographic assessment

Full-field electroretinography (ffERG) was performed using the Metrovision MonPack® system (Metrovision, Pérenchies, France), in accordance with the ISCEV Standard for clinical ERG (2022 update) [7, 14]. All participants underwent 20 min of dark adaptation prior to scotopic testing and 10 min of light adaptation before photopic testing. Pupillary dilation was achieved using 1% tropicamide. Contact lens electrodes (Burian-Allen type) were used for recording, with reference electrodes placed at the ipsilateral outer canthus and a ground electrode at the forehead. Signal amplification and bandpass filtering were performed in accordance with the ISCEV 2022 specifications. Stimulus flash intensities for DA 0.01, DA 3.0, LA 3.0, and 30 Hz Flicker protocols were set per ISCEV 2022 standard values as implemented by the MonPack® software. Waveforms were analyzed for a-wave and b-wave amplitudes (µV) and implicit times (ms), and verified by two trained examiners for accuracy. ERG recordings were obtained from both eyes of each participant; however, each patient was treated as a single analytical unit for all statistical analyses. For ERG amplitude reporting (Table 2 and correlation analyses), the right eye (OD) was used as the primary reference, consistent with standard practice in RP research. Best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was analyzed separately for OD and OS due to the documented asymmetry of visual loss in RP.

Reference ranges for ERG interpretation

Reference values were derived from the ISCEV 2022 Standard and verified against manufacturer-provided normative data [7]. Responses were considered subnormal if amplitudes were below the 2.5th percentile of the age-adjusted normative range or if implicit times exceeded the 97.5th percentile (Table 1). It should be noted that the ISCEV normative guidance represents population-level reference thresholds rather than laboratory-specific norms established for this device and cohort. Laboratory-specific normative data using the Metrovision MonPack® system were not independently established in this study, which represents a limitation; instead, ISCEV 2022 thresholds were combined with manufacturer-validated reference ranges from the MonPack® platform [14] to provide a consistent framework for abnormality classification. This approach is standard practice in clinical ERG settings but may carry a small risk of misclassification for borderline responses, and all borderline cases were therefore reviewed by two trained examiners.

ERG severity classification and clinical data collection

ERG results were stratified into three severity categories based on waveform amplitude and morphology. Responses were classified as mild when amplitudes fell within the normal range or were only mildly reduced, with preservation of typical waveform characteristics. Moderate responses were defined by a substantial reduction in amplitude accompanied by degraded but still discernible waveforms. Severe responses referred to extinguished or non-recordable ERG signals across most or all test conditions. To ensure objective and reproducible grading, two complementary classification approaches were applied. First, an overall ERG severity grade was assigned based on global waveform morphology assessed across all ISCEV standard protocols (scotopic and photopic combined): mild when waveforms were near-normal or only mildly attenuated; moderate when responses were substantially reduced but still discernible across most conditions; and severe when responses were extinguished or non-recordable across most or all protocols. Second, for the purpose of quantitative subgroup comparisons, patients were stratified using a single objective parameter—the DA 0.01 b-wave amplitude—with the following explicit thresholds: mild ≥ 100 µV; moderate 40–99 µV; and severe < 40 µV or non-recordable. These DA 0.01 thresholds are consistent with those used in prior ERG-based RP severity staging studies [15, 16] and were applied uniformly across all subgroup analyses, logistic regression modeling, and ROC analysis. Because the global classification integrates information from all ERG protocols while the DA 0.01-specific classification focuses on rod-mediated responses alone, the two approaches yield slightly different group distributions, which is expected and reflects their complementary analytical perspectives. This classification is consistent with prior studies showing that ERG amplitude strongly correlates with disease severity in RP [15, 16].

For each participant, clinical and demographic data were collected, including age, gender, and best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), which was assessed using a Snellen chart and converted to logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (LogMAR) units for analysis. The duration of disease was documented based on patient-reported age of symptom onset. Where available, optical coherence tomography (OCT) imaging was used to evaluate central macular thickness and ellipsoid zone integrity. Genetic testing results, if available, were also recorded to facilitate potential genotype–phenotype correlations. OCT images were reviewed by two trained examiners and ellipsoid zone (EZ) integrity was graded qualitatively using a three-tier classification: (1) intact — EZ line fully visible across the fovea; (2) disrupted — EZ line partially absent or irregular; (3) absent — EZ line not detectable. This qualitative grading was used to supplement central macular thickness as a structural proxy for cone photoreceptor layer integrity. We acknowledge that quantitative EZ length measurement (in mm) or automated layer segmentation would provide more objective structural data, and we recommend such metrics for future studies.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 25, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Continuous variables were summarized using mean ± standard deviation or median and interquartile range, as appropriate. Categorical data were presented as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons (e.g., ERG severity vs. clinical variables) were made using one-way ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. Pearson correlation coefficients were used to assess relationships between ERG metrics and age, BCVA, or disease duration; Spearman rank correlation is recommended as an additional robustness check given the non-normal distribution of some ERG variables, and is planned for future work. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For the logistic regression model, the relatively small sample size (n = 71) relative to the number of predictors carries a risk of overfitting; confidence intervals should therefore be interpreted with caution. No external validation dataset was available; the predictive model should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Arab American University, under approval number R-2025/A/45/N. All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee, and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to inclusion in the study.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

A total of 71 patients diagnosed with RP were included in the study. The cohort consisted of 39 males (54.9%) and 32 females (45.1%), with a mean age of 36.9 ± 20.2 years (range: 11–61 years). The age range was verified to be accurate; the minimum age of 11 years corresponds to the mean minus 1.28 SD, and the maximum age of 61 years to the mean plus 1.19 SD, reflecting the wide age spectrum of RP onset in this cohort. The mean disease duration was 20.9 ± 13.2 years, reflecting a wide variability in onset and progression across individuals.

Best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), converted to LogMAR for statistical consistency, showed a mean of 0.85 ± 0.38 LogMAR (approximately 20/142 Snellen) in the right eye (OD) and 1.01 ± 0.49 LogMAR (approximately 20/205 Snellen) in the left eye (OS). Bilateral visual impairment was common, though BCVA distribution varied, with several patients preserving central acuity despite severe peripheral dysfunction.

Family history of RP was positive in 39 patients (54.9%), while 32 (45.1%) denied known familial cases. OCT imaging was available in 68 patients; the mean central macular thickness was 224.3 ± 43.8 μm, with notable thinning in advanced cases. Structural preservation of the ellipsoid zone was qualitatively noted in a subset of patients with better preserved cone function. Ellipsoid zone (EZ) integrity grading, available in 68 patients, revealed intact EZ in 18 patients (26.5%), disrupted EZ in 31 patients (45.6%), and absent EZ in 19 patients (27.9%). These qualitative findings are consistent with progressive photoreceptor layer loss in RP and align with the distribution of ERG severity categories. However, given that EZ grading was performed qualitatively without quantitative EZ length measurement, structural conclusions should be regarded as exploratory and interpreted alongside functional ERG data rather than as standalone measures.

ERG testing was successfully performed on all patients using the Metrovision MonPack® system. Full-field ERG responses were classified based on ISCEV standards and compared to age-adjusted normative values.

Patients with severe ERG patterns tended to be older (mean age: 50.1 years). Although mean disease duration and central macular thickness showed numeric trends consistent with greater severity, group differences did not reach statistical significance (disease duration: p = 0.564; OCT thickness: p = 0.882), reflecting the high inter-individual variability in this cohort.

Subgroup analysis by ERG severity

To further explore the relationship between electrophysiological dysfunction and clinical characteristics, patients were stratified into three groups based on the DA 0.01 b-wave amplitude: mild (≥ 100 µV; n = 21, 29.6%), moderate (40–99 µV; n = 27, 38.0%), and severe (< 40 µV; n = 23, 32.4%).

Group-wise comparisons are summarized in Table 3. The mean age was comparable across groups, ranging from 44.1 to 46.5 years (p = 0.958). Mean disease duration was also similar, with values between 16.4 and 20.9 years (p = 0.564).

Best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) in the right eye (OD) did not differ significantly between groups (p = 0.519). However, BCVA in the left eye (OS) showed a statistically significant difference across ERG severity levels (p = 0.0038). Patients in the severe ERG group exhibited worse visual acuity in the left eye (mean LogMAR 1.33 ± 0.39; approximately 20/428 Snellen) compared to those in the mild and moderate groups. This left-eye finding should be interpreted with caution: the right eye BCVA did not show a significant difference (p = 0.519), and the asymmetry between eyes may reflect interocular variability inherent to RP rather than a true group-level effect. Additionally, multiple comparisons across clinical variables were performed without adjustment for multiplicity, increasing the risk of a type I error for this isolated finding.

Central macular thickness on OCT was slightly lower in the severe group but did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.882). Similarly, photopic ERG parameters, including LA 3.0 b-wave amplitude and 30 Hz flicker responses, were not significantly different among the three severity groups (p = 0.370 and p = 0.700, respectively).

These results suggest a possible association between left-eye visual acuity and global ERG severity; however, this finding must be interpreted cautiously given the lack of a significant right-eye parallel, the absence of multiplicity correction, and the cross-sectional study design. Structural and photopic function measures did not significantly differ across ERG severity groups, consistent with the well-known dissociation between peripheral rod dysfunction and preserved central cone function in RP. Disease duration strongly predicts rod dysfunction: correlation analysis

Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to assess the association between rod-mediated retinal function, represented by DA 0.01 b-wave amplitude, and key clinical variables. The results are summarized in Table 4.

A strong and statistically significant negative correlation was found between disease duration and DA 0.01 b-wave amplitude (r = − 0.95, p < 0.0001), indicating that longer duration of RP is closely associated with more severe rod dysfunction. See Fig. 1. While this correlation is exceptionally strong, it should be interpreted in context: the cohort spans a wide range of disease duration and the cross-sectional design means that each patient contributes only one data point. This study design inherently samples different individuals at different cumulative stages of disease, which can produce steep cross-sectional slopes even if individual progression rates are more modest. Visual inspection of the scatter plot confirmed a monotonic relationship without evidence of outlier leverage. Spearman rank correlation is planned as an additional robustness check in future work.

A moderate inverse correlation was also observed with age (r = − 0.49, p < 0.001), suggesting that rod responses decline with advancing age. The moderate correlation between age and disease duration raises the possibility of collinearity; partial correlation analysis controlling for age is recommended to formally disentangle these effects in future work. No statistically significant correlations were detected between DA 0.01 b-wave and BCVA (OD or OS), OCT thickness, or other photopic parameters.

These findings suggest that disease duration shows the strongest cross-sectional association with rod system deterioration in this cohort, while other clinical markers such as BCVA or OCT thickness may not consistently reflect early or intermediate electrophysiological changes.

BCVA and OCT thickness show no significant correlation with ERG parameters

Photopic ERG responses, including LA 3.0 a-wave, LA 3.0 b-wave, and 30 Hz flicker amplitudes, demonstrated low or no correlation with age, BCVA (OD or OS), or OCT thickness, indicating that cone system preservation was highly variable across patients, and often independent of clinical or structural markers. (See Table 4).

Visual acuity measures, particularly BCVA in the left eye (OS), showed weak to no correlation with most ERG parameters. For instance, BCVA (OS) correlated minimally with DA 0.01 b-wave (r = − 0.02) and LA 3.0 b-wave (r = 0.01), suggesting that visual acuity alone may not reliably reflect the extent of retinal dysfunction in RP.

Similarly, central macular thickness on OCT was not significantly correlated with any of the ERG parameters, including both scotopic and photopic measures. This finding supports the concept that functional decline, particularly in rod-mediated pathways, may precede measurable structural degeneration in the central retina.

Disease duration as independent predictor of severe ERG loss: logistic regression

To identify clinical predictors of severe ERG loss, a binary logistic regression model was constructed using disease duration, BCVA in the left eye (OS), and central macular thickness (OCT) as independent variables. Severe ERG loss was defined as a DA 0.01 b-wave amplitude less than 40 µV. BCVA in the left eye (OS) was included as a predictor variable based on the statistically significant group difference observed in the subgroup analysis; however, its inclusion should be considered exploratory given the asymmetric nature of this finding and the potential for type I error from multiple comparisons.

The model showed that disease duration was the strongest predictor, with an odds ratio (OR) of 1.38 (95% CI: 0.99–1.92, p = 0.055), indicating a trend toward increased likelihood of severe ERG impairment with longer disease duration. The 95% confidence interval overlaps 1.0, indicating that statistical significance was not achieved at the conventional p < 0.05 threshold. Given the small sample size and number of predictors, this model should be interpreted with caution and regarded as hypothesis-generating.

BCVA (OS) and OCT thickness were not independently associated with severe ERG loss. BCVA (OS) had a wide confidence interval (OR = 11.47, 95% CI: 0.01–10,361.22, p = 0.482), reflecting instability likely due to inter-individual variability. OCT thickness showed a modest negative association (OR = 0.95, p = 0.181), but was not statistically significant.

These findings suggest that disease duration may serve as a clinically useful indicator of cumulative rod photoreceptor loss, while visual acuity and central macular structure may not reliably predict functional ERG loss when considered independently. This logistic regression model should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than as a validated clinical decision tool, given the small sample size, lack of external validation, and wide confidence intervals. The combination of disease duration, BCVA, and OCT data nonetheless offers a clinically accessible basis for risk stratification pending prospective validation. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis

To evaluate the discriminative performance of clinical predictors for severe ERG loss, a ROC analysis was conducted using a logistic regression model incorporating disease duration, BCVA in the left eye (OS), and central macular thickness. The model’s ability to correctly classify patients with and without severe DA 0.01 b-wave attenuation (< 40 µV) was assessed.

The resulting area under the curve (AUC) was 0.84, indicating good diagnostic accuracy. This suggests that the combination of these clinical variables offers substantial predictive value for identifying patients at risk of advanced rod dysfunction. The ROC curve is presented in Fig. 2. However, it must be noted that this AUC was derived from the same dataset used to build the model (internal validation only), and no independent test set or cross-validation was performed. The reported AUC of 0.84 is therefore likely optimistic and subject to overfitting bias. External validation in an independent cohort will be necessary before this predictive model can be recommended for clinical application.

Genetic diagnosis and associated RP genes

A total of 25 patients (35.2%) in our cohort underwent genetic testing and received a confirmed molecular diagnosis of retinitis pigmentosa. Several known RP-associated genes were identified, reflecting both syndromic and nonsyndromic forms of the disease. These included RPGR, RHO, USH2A, EYS, FAM161A, CRB1, and TULP1. (See Fig. 3).

Mutations in RPGR, associated with X-linked inheritance, were identified in six male patients. These individuals typically presented with early-onset and severe rod-cone dystrophy, with extinguished scotopic and photopic ERG responses at a relatively young age. In contrast, patients with RHO mutations (n = 4) demonstrated a milder disease course, often retaining central vision and measurable cone function into mid-adulthood.

Five patients carried USH2A mutations, which were associated with combined retinal degeneration and sensorineural hearing loss. These individuals commonly exhibited extinguished rod responses and moderate cone involvement. Mutations in EYS (n = 3) and FAM161A (n = 2) were associated with autosomal recessive RP, typically presenting with early onset and severe functional impairment.

Two patients were found to have CRB1 mutations, characterized by early-onset RP with structural retinal thickening on OCT and markedly reduced ERG amplitudes. One patient carried a mutation in TULP1, which has been associated with severe early-onset RP, particularly in families with a history of consanguinity.

Although the sample size was limited, these genetically diagnosed cases demonstrated expected phenotypic variability across different gene groups and support the clinical utility of molecular testing in RP. All gene-specific subgroup sizes were small (n = 2–6 per gene), and none of the genotype–ERG comparisons were subjected to formal statistical testing. These observations should therefore be regarded strictly as descriptive and hypothesis-generating. Definitive conclusions about genotype-specific ERG phenotypes in this population require adequately powered studies with complete genetic characterization.

Discussion

This study offers one of the first in-depth electroretinographic characterizations of RP within a Palestinian cohort, providing critical insights into the functional landscape of this inherited retinal dystrophy in a genetically underrepresented population. Using ffERG according to ISCEV standards, we were able to categorize RP severity and establish correlations with clinical parameters such as age, disease duration, visual acuity, and macular structure. Our findings reinforce known disease mechanisms while also contributing novel regional data with implications for diagnosis, staging, and therapeutic planning.

The most notable finding in our study was the strong inverse cross-sectional relationship between disease duration and rod-specific ERG responses, particularly the DA 0.01 b-wave amplitude (r = − 0.95, p < 0.0001). This association is consistent with the temporal sequence of photoreceptor degeneration in RP, where rod dysfunction precedes cone involvement, as described in the widely accepted rod-cone degeneration model [17, 18]. Although the magnitude of this correlation is unusually high for a biological relationship, it is explicable by the wide disease duration range in our cohort and the cross-sectional sampling design, in which individuals at different cumulative stages of disease were captured at a single time point, producing a steep aggregate slope. This should not be interpreted as implying that all individual patients progress at a uniform rate proportional to this slope.

This finding aligns with other studies demonstrating that duration of visual symptoms correlates with electrophysiological decline in RP [15, 16]. Importantly, our analysis revealed that disease duration showed the strongest cross-sectional association with ERG outcomes compared to either age or OCT macular thickness, suggesting its potential utility as a cross-sectional marker of cumulative functional loss. These data also underscore the value of ERG in tracking disease dynamics, especially in settings where longitudinal imaging may not be routinely available [7]. We note, however, that disease duration is a patient-reported variable based on symptom onset, which may be subject to recall bias and may not precisely reflect the biological onset of photoreceptor degeneration. Caution is warranted in extrapolating these cross-sectional correlations to individual longitudinal predictions.

It is important to contextualize this strong association within the known biological heterogeneity of RP. The disease encompasses more than 80 causative genes and exhibits substantial inter-individual variability in progression rate, driven by genotype, modifier alleles, environmental factors, and inheritance pattern [2, 18]. In genetically heterogeneous cohorts such as ours, a single dominant driver of ERG decline—such as disease duration—may capture the cumulative effect of this variability when assessed cross-sectionally across a wide spectrum of disease stages [16, 17]. Patients with longer symptom duration have, by definition, experienced a greater cumulative burden of photoreceptor degeneration regardless of their specific genotype, which likely contributes to the strength of this correlation. Our cohort encompasses multiple inheritance patterns (X-linked, autosomal dominant, and autosomal recessive) and a range of phenotypic severity, from mild rod dysfunction to near-complete retinal failure, further supporting the interpretation that the correlation reflects a broad population-level trend rather than a uniform rate of decline. It must be emphasized, however, that this is a cross-sectional study, and the observed association between disease duration and ERG amplitude represents a snapshot of cumulative functional loss captured at a single time point, not a direct measure of individual progression rates. Inferences about the temporal dynamics of ERG decline should be interpreted as biologically plausible extrapolations from cross-sectional data, consistent with the rod-cone degeneration model, rather than as longitudinally validated trajectories. Prospective longitudinal studies with serial ERG assessments will be essential to confirm individual progression rates and to determine whether disease duration retains its predictive utility within specific genotypic subgroups.

An interesting finding was the limited correlation between best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) and global ERG responses. While patients with severe ERG loss showed significantly reduced BCVA in the left eye, overall correlations between BCVA and DA 0.01 or photopic ERG amplitudes were weak and statistically insignificant. This observation reflects the well-documented functional disconnect in RP, where foveal cones can remain structurally and functionally preserved despite widespread peripheral rod loss ([19], p. 4).

Similar findings have been reported in X-linked and autosomal dominant RP, where patients retain central vision into advanced stages due to a foveal sparing effect [8, 20]. This emphasizes the importance of including objective, full-field functional testing in the clinical assessment of RP rather than relying solely on central acuity metrics, which may not reflect disease burden in its entirety [21].

We employed a practical classification of ERG responses into mild, moderate, and severe categories based on waveform amplitude and morphology. This system mirrors the classification proposed by Oner and Kahraman [22], which has gained support as a reproducible approach to ERG-based RP severity grading.

The clinical value of this stratification lies in its applicability to prognostic planning and personalized therapeutic decision-making. Patients exhibiting preserved cone-mediated responses—particularly in the photopic and 30 Hz Flicker ERG protocols—may still qualify for emerging interventions such as gene therapy, optogenetic stimulation, or retinal prostheses, all of which require at least partial preservation of inner retinal circuitry and viable downstream neuronal pathways [23, 24]. As such, functional ERG grading serves not only as a diagnostic and monitoring tool but also as a guide for clinical trial eligibility and timing of advanced therapeutic interventions. Our ROC analysis yielded an AUC of 0.84 for internally discriminating severe from non-severe rod dysfunction using disease duration, BCVA (OS), and OCT thickness; however, as this represents internal validation only, it should not yet be interpreted as a validated screening tool pending external confirmation in an independent cohort.

Interestingly, central macular thickness (CMT), as measured by OCT, did not significantly correlate with full-field ERG parameters in our cohort. This finding is consistent with earlier reports showing that structural integrity of the macula does not necessarily reflect global retinal function in RP [8, 15]. The fundamental difference lies in the imaging scope: OCT provides a focal, macula-centric assessment, while full-field ERG evaluates panretinal function, including peripheral retinal integrity. As a result, significant functional loss—especially rod-mediated—may progress undetected by OCT alone, particularly in early-stage RP when the macula appears morphologically intact. Importantly, OCT assessment in our study was limited to central macular thickness and qualitative ellipsoid zone grading, without quantitative EZ length measurement or automated photoreceptor layer segmentation. This limits the precision of structure–function comparisons, and our conclusions regarding structural integrity should be considered preliminary.

Our findings are consistent with this dissociation and further align with those of Huang et al., [8], who explored structure–function correlations across RP subtypes using OCT and full-field ERG in typical and pericentral RP. Their study demonstrated that the diagnostic utility of OCT may depend on the underlying disease pattern, with strong correlations in typical RP but not pericentral RP. In our unstratified Palestinian cohort, the absence of a significant structure–function relationship may reflect phenotypic heterogeneity, including both typical and atypical RP variants. These observations highlight the need for future studies to incorporate RP subtype stratification, potentially enhancing the interpretive power of structure–function correlations [25].

Together, these findings reinforce the complementary but non-equivalent roles of OCT and ERG in the clinical evaluation of RP. While OCT remains a critical tool for assessing central retinal morphology and monitoring macular complications, ERG continues to offer a more comprehensive assessment of functional decline across the full retinal field.

Genetic analysis identified pathogenic variants in RPGR, RHO, USH2A, EYS, FAM161A, CRB1, and TULP1 in 35.2% of participants who underwent testing. Among these, RPGR-associated cases, all of whom were male, exhibited early-onset disease and extinguished ERG responses, consistent with the severe phenotype typically observed in X-linked retinitis pigmentosa (XLRP) [26]. Conversely, patients with RHO mutations retained cone function longer and had milder ERG attenuation, reflecting the phenotype of autosomal dominant RP [27].

Notably, mutations in USH2A and CRB1—both associated with syndromic or structurally complex RP forms—also appeared in our cohort. While USH2A is commonly linked with Usher syndrome type II and exhibits progressive ERG decline, CRB1 mutations are often associated with preserved central retinal thickness despite severe peripheral dysfunction, potentially complicating structure–function interpretation [28].

These preliminary observations suggest a potential relationship between specific genetic subtypes and ERG profiles; however, given that genetic testing was available in only 35.2% of our cohort, these genotype–ERG associations must be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than as definitive conclusions. Broad statements about genotype-driven ERG behavior are not warranted at this sample size, and the observed trends should be understood as directional signals consistent with published literature, pending confirmation in larger, adequately powered studies. Nonetheless, the genetic heterogeneity captured in our cohort—spanning X-linked, autosomal dominant, and autosomal recessive inheritance—highlights that ERG-based functional profiling yields clinically meaningful data across genetically diverse patients. This preliminary functional mapping of the Palestinian RP population may help prioritize which genetic subgroups warrant targeted molecular investigation and may serve as a foundation for future genotype-driven studies. With more comprehensive genetic characterization in future prospective cohorts, it will be possible to determine whether distinct ERG progression trajectories are reliably associated with specific causative mutations in this population, and to develop population-specific genetic panels for this underrepresented community. In the era of precision medicine and gene therapy, this integration of preliminary ERG and genetic data represents a meaningful first step toward equitable diagnostic and therapeutic access. Amato et al., [29].

Our findings contribute urgently needed data from a Middle Eastern, Arab population, where RP is understudied despite high rates of consanguinity and autosomal recessive inheritance [30]. The phenotypic and genetic diversity captured in our study highlights the potential influence of regional founder mutations and sociocultural marriage patterns. We do not claim that ERG waveform characteristics are inherently different in Palestinian patients compared to other populations; rather, this study establishes the first systematic functional profile in this cohort, which is essential for identifying population-specific disease burden and mutation spectra, and for ensuring future clinical trial eligibility criteria are inclusive of this group.

In the context of precision medicine and ongoing clinical trials, establishing baseline functional profiles for understudied populations is not only diagnostic but also essential for ensuring equitable access to future gene and cell-based interventions [31]. Moreover, public health systems in such regions may benefit from investing in scalable, objective diagnostic tools like ERG to facilitate early detection and referral [32].

Study strengths and limitations

Among the strengths of this study are its well-defined cohort, standardized ISCEV-based ERG testing, and the integration of clinical, functional, and genetic data. It represents one of the first efforts to document ERG patterns in a Palestinian RP population and provides a framework for future multicenter studies in the region.

However, several limitations of this study must be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design restricts our ability to draw conclusions about the natural history or longitudinal progression of retinitis pigmentosa in this cohort. Serial ERG measurements over time would be necessary to evaluate individual disease trajectories and the temporal dynamics of functional decline. Second, genetic testing coverage was incomplete, with only a subset (35.2%) of participants undergoing molecular analysis. This limited the depth of genotype–phenotype correlations and likely reflects broader issues of cost, availability, and accessibility of genetic services in low- to middle-income settings. Third, while full-field ERG provided valuable panretinal functional data, additional modalities such as multifocal ERG (mfERG) or wide-field imaging could have yielded more granular information about central cone function and peripheral structural integrity, respectively [33]. These tools may be particularly useful in early or atypical RP subtypes where localized retinal function is preserved. Future studies should aim for more comprehensive testing protocols, increased genetic characterization, and prospective designs to further strengthen the translational relevance of ERG findings. Fourth, normative reference values were drawn from ISCEV 2022 guidelines and manufacturer data rather than from a locally established laboratory normative dataset for the Metrovision MonPack® system, which may introduce borderline misclassification. Future studies in this setting should establish device-specific, age-stratified normative ranges using a local healthy control cohort. Fifth, the logistic regression model and ROC analysis were performed on the same dataset without external validation, and the reported AUC of 0.84 should be regarded as an internal estimate subject to optimism bias. Sixth, structural OCT findings were limited to qualitative EZ grading and central macular thickness without quantitative photoreceptor layer metrics, restricting the strength of structure–function inferences.

Conclusion

This study supports the use of full-field ERG as an objective tool for functional staging and prognostic assessment in RP, demonstrating a strong cross-sectional association between disease duration and rod-mediated ERG amplitude, and limited association with central visual acuity or macular structure. By presenting novel data from a Palestinian cohort, it contributes to the growing evidence base for RP characterization in underrepresented Arab populations and underscores the need for inclusive diagnostic standards. ERG-based severity classification may serve as a valuable clinical tool for therapeutic decision-making and for expanding access to future interventions in genetically diverse populations.
