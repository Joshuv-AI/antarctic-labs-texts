Agrobacterium tumefaciens-derived cytokinin induces the transdifferentiation of pavement cells to trichomes in tobacco leaves
Authors: Fengjiao Wang, Jialin Liu, Yunyun Shi, Shuang Wu, Xiang Zhou, Lingling Ye, Zhenbiao Yang
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

The development of multicellular organisms relies on a precise balance between cellular stability and plasticity. In Waddington’s classical epigenetic landscape, cell differentiation is generally viewed as a unidirectional descent into a stable functional state, thereby ensuring tissue integrity (Shen et al., 2004). Transdifferentiation, defined as the conversion of one differentiated cell type into another without passing through a pluripotent state capable of giving rise to all embryonic cell types, has been documented in animal systems, and this process may occur either directly or via a transient dedifferentiation step (Jopling et al., 2011; Nguyen et al., 2016). In plants, the acquisition of pluripotency by terminally differentiated cells via dedifferentiation is common, but the conversion of one cell type into another without passing through a pluripotent state, i.e., transdifferentiation, is rare. Only a few in vitro systems, such as the Zinnia mesophyll-to-tracheary-element model (Fukuda and Komamine, 1980; Nguyen et al., 2016), have been reported to exhibit transdifferentiation. Consequently, it remains largely unresolved whether fully differentiated plant cells can be directly reprogrammed in planta, especially under non-axenic conditions.

In the leaf epidermis, guard cells, pavement cells, and trichomes arise from the division of protodermal cells during early leaf development (Zuch et al., 2022). Mature leaves contain a fixed number of trichomes, which does not increase once epidermal differentiation has ceased. Pavement cells are therefore widely regarded as terminally differentiated and incapable of initiating new trichomes. Here, we provide evidence that a cytokinin derived from Agrobacterium tumefaciens can induce the transdifferentiation of fully differentiated pavement cells into glandular trichomes in intact Nicotiana benthamiana leaves.

This phenomenon was first observed during routine Agrobacterium-mediated transient expression assays. Following infiltration of fully expanded N. benthamiana leaves with A. tumefaciens strain GV3101, we consistently detected a pronounced increase in trichome density at the infiltration sites compared with adjacent untreated regions. This phenotype was reproducibly observed at 14 days post-infiltration (dpi), regardless of whether GV3101 carried an empty vector or a 35S::RUBY reporter construct (Figure 1A; Supplemental Figure 1A), indicating that GV3101-induced trichome formation occurs independently of transgene expression. To determine whether the effect was strain-specific, six commonly used Agrobacterium laboratory strains (GV3101, C58C1, K599, EHA105, LBA4404, and AGL1) were infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves. Strikingly, only GV3101, C58C1, and K599 triggered pronounced de novo trichome formation on mature leaves, whereas the other strains failed to produce a detectable increase relative to mock-treated controls (Figure 1B). Because both trichome-inducing and non-inducing groups included C58-derived strains (Figure 1C), the phenotype could not be explained by strain lineage. Comparative analysis of Ti plasmid composition revealed that the trichome-inducing strains shared multiple virulence-associated modules and carried the cytokinin biosynthetic gene tzs (encoding trans-zeatin synthase), which was absent from the non-inducing strains (Figure 1C). Consistent with this hypothesis, a GV3101 derivative lacking the Ti plasmid (ΔTi) failed to induce trichome formation and phenocopied the mock treatment, whereas GV3101 carrying the Ti plasmid significantly increased trichome number (Figures 1D and 1E). Measurement of endogenous cytokinins further showed that trans-zeatin levels were significantly higher in GV3101-infiltrated leaves than in GV3101(ΔTi)- and mock-treated leaves (Figure 1F). In addition, the strain EHA105(tzs), generated by introducing tzs into the non-inducing strain EHA105, induced a significant increase in trichome formation comparable to that of GV3101 (Figures 1D and 1E). Likewise, exogenous application of the synthetic cytokinin 6-benzylaminopurine (6-BA) recapitulated the trichome-induction phenotype at 7 dpi (Figures 1D and 1E). Similar cytokinin-associated trichome induction has been reported at later time points (14 and 25 dpi) in tobacco (Chen et al., 2026; Saebel et al., 2025) and during early leaf development in Arabidopsis, tomato, and poplar (Gan et al., 2007; Maes et al., 2008; Maes et al., 2011; Maes and Goossens, 2010; Zhou et al., 2013), suggesting that cytokinin-mediated regulation of trichome development may be broadly conserved across species. Further examination of GV3101-induced trichomes revealed several atypical features rarely observed during normal development, including trichome bifurcation, clustering, and the emergence of multiple trichomes from a single epidermal cell (Figure 1G). These results indicate that Ti plasmid-derived tzs-dependent cytokinin production underlies GV3101-induced trichome formation in mature pavement cells.

We then examined epidermal morphology using scanning electron microscopy. During normal leaf development, trichomes arise from small, undifferentiated protodermal cells, whereas fully expanded leaves are primarily composed of interdigitated, puzzle-shaped pavement cells and mature trichomes (Figure 1H; Supplemental Figure 2). Consistent with their terminally differentiated state, mock-treated mature leaves showed no increase in trichome number over time (Supplemental Figures 3A and 3B). In contrast, GV3101 infiltration induced dome-shaped protrusions on the surface of differentiated pavement cells as early as 5 dpi, followed by the progressive accumulation of multicellular trichomes at later time points, 7 and 9 dpi (Figure 1I; Supplemental Figures 3A and 3B). At 9 dpi, although the total number of pavement cells was comparable to that in mock-treated controls, approximately 45% of pavement cells were associated with trichomes, compared with approximately 5% in mock-treated leaves (Supplemental Figures 3C and 3D). By 14 dpi, nearly all pavement cells had either formed trichomes or exhibited protrusions indicative of trichome initiation (Supplemental Figure 1B). Together, these data suggest that GV3101-induced trichome formation is a continuous and dynamic process rather than a synchronous event occurring across all cells and that new trichomes arise directly from mature pavement cells without requiring substantial pavement cell division. Among mature leaves at different positions on the same plant, trichome induction was more pronounced in leaves that were older and more fully expanded (Supplemental Figure 5), suggesting that fully expanded pavement cells retain a previously unrecognized competence for fate conversion.

Transcriptomic profiling of N. benthamiana leaves infiltrated with GV3101 revealed extensive and sustained transcriptional changes. A substantial proportion of genes was upregulated at 5 dpi, and this induction was maintained at 7 and 9 dpi (Figure 1J; Supplemental Figures 3C, 3D, and 4; Supplemental Table 1). Among these genes were several core regulators of trichome development (Li et al., 2025), including TRANSPARENT TESTA GLABRA1, which encodes a WD40-repeat protein that serves as a core component of the MYB–bHLH–WD40 complex and is essential for trichome differentiation and anthocyanin biosynthesis, and GLABRA2, which encodes a homeodomain-leucine zipper transcription factor that acts downstream of this complex and directly promotes trichome cell fate specification. Other genes implicated in trichome development, such as MIXTA1 and the glandular trichome-specific gene WRKY2, were also strongly upregulated. These genes were predominantly found in expression modules 8 and 10 and showed sustained induction from 5 to 9 dpi (Figure 1J; Supplemental Figure 4B; Supplemental Table 3). In contrast, genes associated with pavement cell morphogenesis, cell cycle regulation, and cell differentiation, including IQD21, CYCD1;1, and TCP transcription factors such as TCP4, TCP7, and TCP21, were associated with clusters 1, 3, 5, and 12 and were downregulated following GV3101 infiltration (Figure 1J; Supplemental Figure 4B; Supplemental Table 3). Genes co-expressed with trichome regulators, including those in clusters 8 and 10, were associated with zeatin biosynthesis, plant hormone signal transduction, and secondary metabolite biosynthesis pathways (Supplemental Figure 4B). Parallel transcriptomic analyses following exogenous 6-BA treatment showed induction of key trichome regulatory genes at 5 and 7 dpi, largely recapitulating the transcriptional activation observed following GV3101 infiltration (Figure 1J; Supplemental Figure 3D; Supplemental Table 2). However, the effect of cytokinin treatment on pavement cell identity genes was weaker than that of GV3101 treatment (Figure 1J; Supplemental Table 2). These results indicate that cytokinin treatment primarily promotes the acquisition of trichome identity rather than triggering a global loss of pavement cell identity or dedifferentiation.

In summary, infiltration of N. benthamiana leaves with A. tumefaciens induces de novo trichome formation from fully differentiated pavement cells on mature leaves. This process is characterized by the emergence of dome-shaped protrusions from differentiated pavement cells followed by the development of multicellular trichomes. It can be induced by A. tumefaciens strains harboring Ti plasmids that encode the cytokinin biosynthetic gene tzs, such as GV3101. GV3101(ΔTi) does not trigger trichome formation, and introduction of tzs into a non-inducing strain is sufficient to induce trichome formation, as is the exogenous application of the cytokinin 6-BA. Transcriptomic profiling showed coordinated upregulation of genes encoding trichome fate regulators, together with activation of cytokinin biosynthesis, plant hormone signal transduction, and secondary metabolite biosynthesis pathways. Although A. tumefaciens-induced trichome formation has also been reported in other studies (Chen et al., 2026; Saebel et al., 2025), our findings suggest that A. tumefaciens-derived cytokinins can trigger the transdifferentiation of mature pavement cells into trichomes, highlighting a previously unrecognized potential for cell fate plasticity and transdifferentiation in differentiated epidermal cells. However, the mechanism underlying cytokinin-induced transdifferentiation and the cellular trajectory of this process remain to be determined. Approaches such as live-cell imaging, lineage tracing, high-resolution methods such as single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics, and analysis of downstream cytokinin targets will be required to address these questions. Moreover, leaf trichomes protect plants from biotic and abiotic damage, such as insect feeding, by producing secondary metabolites, many of which have pharmaceutical or other values for humans (Tissier et al., 2012; Liu et al., 2023), including artemisinin and cannabinoids. The ability to transiently and locally induce glandular trichomes on fully expanded leaves also provides a flexible platform for inducible cellular transdifferentiation and the production of valuable metabolites in mature tissues. This strategy enables spatially and temporally controlled metabolic engineering without permanent developmental perturbation.
