The association of estimated citrate load with frozen plasma versus four-factor prothrombin complex concentrate for coagulopathy after cardiac surgery: a post hoc exploratory analysis of the FARES-II randomised controlled trial
Authors: Kenichi A Tanaka, Amir L Butt, Kenneth E Stewart, Gita Pezeshki, Sylvia Werner, Justyna Bartoszko, Kamrouz Ghadimi, Cristina Solomon, Kofi Vandyck, Jerrold H Levy, Jeannie L Callum, Sigurd Knaub, Oliver Hegener, Keyvan Karkouti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFrozen plasma (FP) is the standard haemostatic therapy after cardiopulmonary bypass, but recent trials indicate that FP is inferior to four-factor prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC). Citrate content may contribute to this difference. We compared estimated citrate exposure between patients receiving FP and those receiving PCC and explored its association with postoperative bleeding.MethodsThis post hoc analysis of the FARES-II randomised trial (30 November 2022 to 28 May 2024; 12 North American centres; 420 adults; 207 FP, 213 PCC) calculated estimated citrate load from all transfused products using published citrate-content data. The primary outcome was per-patient cumulative estimated citrate load at 12 and 24 h post-investigational medicinal product. Secondary outcomes were the hourly trajectory of group-total citrate delivery and its association with 12-h chest tube drainage by quartile.ResultsIn the FP group, total estimated citrate exposure was substantially higher, and the median per-patient estimated citrate load was approximately six-fold greater at 12–24 h. Administration of citrate-containing products continued through 8 h in patients receiving FP but was largely confined to the first 2 h in patients receiving PCC. Across all quartiles of estimated 12-h citrate load, patients treated with FP had higher estimated citrate exposure and chest tube drainage; patients treated with PCC in the highest quartile exhibited drainage volumes similar to those of patients treated with FP in the third quartile.ConclusionsThis exploratory, hypothesis-generating analysis demonstrated markedly higher estimated citrate exposure with FP than PCC. Although causality cannot be established without direct citrate measurement, FP was associated with higher estimated citrate load and more variable chest tube drainage. Factor concentrates may reduce this burden, warranting further mechanistic investigation.ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05523297; https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05523297?term=NCT05523297&rank=1Keywords: cardiac surgery, citrate load, frozen plasma, prothrombin complex concentrate, transfusion

Complex cardiac surgery supported by cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) is frequently associated with coagulopathic bleeding that is primarily treated with platelets and frozen plasma (FP). Reported FP transfusion rates during cardiac surgery range from 15% to 30%, but rates can exceed 50% in reoperative procedures.1, 2, 3 However, conventional use of FP has been called into question by a recent randomised clinical trial, FARES-II, which demonstrated that four-factor prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC; Balfaxar or Octaplex; Octapharma Pharmaceutical Production GmbH, Vienna, Austria)4 at a dose of 1500–2000 IU was significantly more effective than 3–4 units of FP in achieving haemostasis (77.9% vs 60.4%; P<0.001).5 Post-intervention international normalised ratio (INR) improved after either intervention, but the change was numerically greater for PCC vs FP (−0.84 vs −0.70; P=0.008). While the difference could be attributed to highly concentrated vitamin K-dependent factor levels in PCC (by approximately 25-fold over FP), there may be other reasons for faster and more efficient recovery of haemostasis in the PCC group. The relatively small difference in the INR change between the two groups does not fully explain a 1.8-fold higher rate of haemostatic failure, as signified by the need for continued red blood cell and haemostatic component use within the first 24 h after surgery.

A potentially important difference between FP and PCC that has not been rigorously examined is citrate content. FP is well known for its high citrate content, nearly 1 g per single unit.6,7 In contrast, commercial PCCs contain substantially lower amounts of sodium citrate (40–90 mg per 500 IU).4 The large amounts of citrate in FP prevent premature clotting by maintaining ionised calcium (Ca2+) below 0.25 mmol L−1 during storage. At this Ca2+ concentrations, the gamma-carboxyglutamic acid (Gla) domains of vitamin K-dependent procoagulant factors (factors VII, IX, and X) are structurally disordered and functionally impaired.8 These Gla domains contain seven-to-eight Ca2+-binding sites that are essential for the conformational transitions required for phospholipid membrane binding and haemostatic activation; accordingly, factors delivered in FP must undergo Gla-domain reconformation before becoming haemostatically active. Upon transfusion, excess citrate from FP chelates circulating Ca2+, inducing a transient fall in systemic Ca2+. Sulemanji and colleagues9 modelled this in vitro and showed that mixing only 7% of FP with heparinised whole blood instantaneously reduces Ca2+ by 50%. When citrate chelation continues during repeated transfusions of citrate-containing blood components, restoration of functionally competent Gla-domain conformation is expected to be delayed. Indeed, in vitro thrombin generation is significantly delayed in citrate-anticoagulated plasma and is only incompletely restored by recalcification with calcium chloride.10

Based on the FARES-II trial dataset,5 we conducted this exploratory post hoc analysis to characterise the estimated citrate exposure delivered with each treatment strategy and to test whether it differed in clinically relevant magnitude. The primary aim was to estimate per-patient cumulative citrate exposure over the first 12–24 h after initial investigational medicinal product (IMP) administration in the FP and PCC arms. The secondary aims were to characterise the hourly trajectory of citrate delivery in each arm and to explore whether the estimated 12-h citrate load was associated with 12-h chest tube drainage volume.

Methods

Study population

This post hoc analysis was conducted using data from the FARES-II trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT05523297),5 which was performed between 2022 and 2024 in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and all applicable regulatory requirements. This post hoc analysis was within the scope of the approvals from institutional review boards or research ethics boards obtained at each site before trial initiation. The analysis included all 420 consenting patients undergoing cardiac surgery (aged ≥18 yr) in the FARES-II analysis set who had developed bleeding related to coagulation factor deficiency after termination of CPB during surgery (Supplementary Fig. 1). Of these, 207 patients received FP and 213 received PCC as the initial IMP. The study was performed at 12 hospitals in Canada and the United States from 30 November 2022 to 28 May 2024.5 Details of the trial protocol, including participant inclusion and exclusion criteria, and the statistical analysis plan have been published.5,11

Estimates of citrate load as a primary exposure

The primary outcome of this post hoc analysis was per-patient cumulative estimated citrate load at 12- and 24-h post-intervention. Secondary outcomes were the group-total hourly trajectory of estimated citrate delivery and the association between 12-h estimated citrate load and 12-h chest tube drainage volume across estimated citrate quartiles. To estimate total citrate exposure over 12–24 h following initial IMP administration, we collected time-stamped data on the use of allogeneic blood products and factor concentrates from the FARES-II study.5 Citrate content estimations were based on methods reported by Alghanem and colleagues,7 with modifications (Supplementary Table 1). For red blood cells, we used 0.23 g of citrate per unit, without adjusting for cellular citrate metabolism. For FP, because of variability in citrate content among plasma batches, we selected the midpoint value from commercial plasma products: 0.77 g per unit.12 This is lower than the reported estimates of 0.84–0.99 g per unit,6,7 but likely provides a more conservative estimate of citrate load via FP. We used citrate estimates of 0.78 g for both pooled (4 units) and apheresis units, assuming similar volume (∼320 ml).7,13 For five-unit cryoprecipitate pools, a citrate estimate of 0.29 g was used.7 For PCC, both IMP and non-IMP, we used the midpoint citrate concentration in commercial 4-factor prothrombin complex concentrate (Balfaxar or Octaplex; Octapharma), ranging from 16.8 to 23.4 mmol L-1, corresponding to 0.077 g of citrate per 500 IU dose.4 Fibrinogen concentrate was assumed to contain 0.049 g of citrate per 1 g dose.14 Recombinant activated factor VII (NovoSeven; Novo Nordisk A/S, Bagsværd, Denmark) does not contain citrate.15

Time course of citrate load

The start of initial IMP administration was designated as time zero. Running totals of estimated citrate load were calculated over 12- and 24-h periods. For each patient, all transfused products, including the initial IMP, were sorted by the recorded start time, and the associated estimated citrate content was cumulatively summed over time. To evaluate the time course, the 24-h period post-IMP was divided into 60-min intervals. Each product’s start time was used to assign it to the appropriate interval. Estimated citrate from the initial IMP administration was included in the first 60-min interval, and estimated citrate contributions from subsequent products were then aggregated within the respective hourly intervals. Per-patient total estimated citrate exposure at 12 and 24 h was calculated by summing the estimated citrate content from all products administered within those timeframes in both the FP and PCC groups.

Distributions of estimated citrate load and postoperative bleeding

To explore the relationship between estimated citrate load and postoperative bleeding, we first pooled all patients to characterise the overall association between 12-h estimated citrate load (in grams) and chest tube drainage (in ml). Subsequently, patients in each group were stratified into quartiles of 12-h estimated citrate load. This interval was chosen because any differences in the haemostatic efficacy of the IMP are less likely to be reflected in chest tube drainage beyond the 12th h because of the recovery of endogenous coagulation factors.16,17

Statistics

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise cumulative estimated citrate load over 12–24 h and its time course in each group. Results were graphically analysed to assess the overall distributions of 12-h estimated citrate load and chest tube drainage.

After patients in each group were stratified into quartiles based on their 12-h estimated cumulative citrate load (first quartile [Q1]: lowest; fourth quartile [Q4]: highest), the mean and standard deviation of estimated citrate load and chest tube drainage were calculated within each quartile, and compared using t-tests. All data management and analyses were conducted using SAS v9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Prism v10.1.2 (GraphPad, Boston, MA, USA) software was also used to produce the figures.

Results

All patients in the FP group (n=207) and the PCC group (n=213) were included in the analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1). As detailed in the original publication,5 demographic and surgical characteristics were similar between the groups (median age, 66 [inter-quartile range, 57–73] yr; 309 male patients [74%]; and 296 patients [70%] had undergone complex operation). Fig. 1a summarises the group-total cumulative estimated citrate exposure from the IMP over the 12-h period following IMP administration. In the FP group, the initial estimated citrate contribution from IMP-FP was followed by continued, substantial estimated citrate exposure from non-IMP products, particularly over the first 12 h. In contrast, the PCC group had a smaller initial estimated citrate contribution from IMP-PCC and lower cumulative estimated citrate contributions from non-IMP products. The per-patient median (inter-quartile range) cumulative estimated citrate load from all products at 12 h post-IMP was 3.9 (3.1–5.9) g in the FP group and 0.60 (0.31–1.5) g in the PCC group; these estimations remained largely unchanged at 24 h: 3.9 (3.1–6.0) g for FP and 0.66 (0.31–1.7) g for PCC.

In contrast, the FP group demonstrated sustained loading, continuing until the 8th h, at which point the estimated rates of the two groups converged.

Table 1 shows the per-patient 12 h estimated citrate load stratified by quartiles, with the corresponding 12 h chest tube drainage volumes. Across all quartiles, the PCC group consistently demonstrated lower mean estimated citrate exposure compared with the corresponding quartiles in the FP group (P<0.001). Notably, the PCC group had the largest proportion of patients in the lowest quartile, and the highest quartile (Q4) of the PCC group had a mean estimated citrate load comparable with that of the second quartile (Q2) of the FP group. Discussion

This exploratory analysis calculated the estimated citrate load for two different sources of coagulation factors used in haemostatic therapy in patients with bleeding cardiac surgery and identified markedly different estimated citrate loading patterns over the 12- to 24-h period following intervention with either FP or PCC. Median estimated citrate loads per patient treated with the FP were 6.5 times higher at 12 h and 5.9 times higher at 24 h compared with those in patients treated with PCC. Notably, the mean 12-h estimated citrate load in Q4 of the FP group was 9.1 g, which was nearly three-fold higher than in Q4 of the PCC group. FP has the highest citrate content among blood products commonly administered after CPB. Although citrate is often regarded as a clinically benign additive that is rapidly cleared by the liver, its terminal half-life is approximately 36 min in patients who are non-cirrhotic and critically ill, and nearly doubled in those with cirrhosis.18 Given the prevalence of congestive heart failure (17.4%) and prolonged CPB duration (mean, 174 min) in the FARES-II cohort, normal citrate metabolism cannot be assumed. Although direct measurements were unavailable, elevated circulating citrate from sustained FP transfusion likely exacerbated cycles of Ca2+ depletion and impaired recovery of thrombin generation. Based on a terminal half-life of 36 min, as characterised by Kramer and colleagues,18 citrate from each successive unit of FP would be partially but not fully cleared before the next unit’s citrate load was added, particularly during the high-rate transfusion periods observed in the FP group over the first 8 h post-intervention. The time-integrated difference in estimated citrate exposure between the two groups, therefore, remains substantial even after pharmacokinetic correction for normal elimination kinetics, and would likely be further amplified by the hepatic and haemodynamic impairments common in patients undergoing cardiac surgery.

Mann and colleagues10 demonstrated in vitro that thrombin generation is significantly delayed and attenuated in citrate-anticoagulated plasma compared with plasma treated with corn trypsin inhibitor, which selectively blocks the contact pathway without altering Ca2+. Pre-incubation of citrated plasma with calcium chloride partially restored thrombin generation, but residual calcium-citrate complexes continued to impair coagulation by delaying the onset of thrombin formation. The structural basis of this calcium dependence resides in the Gla domain: high-affinity inner Ca2+ binding sites maintain the structural integrity of the hydrophobic protrusion (omega-loop) essential for insertion into the activated platelet phospholipid surface, while lower-affinity outer Ca2+ binding sites directly anchor the Gla domain to the negatively charged membrane.8 In citrated FP, depletion of Ca2+ below 0.25 mmol L−1 prevents occupancy of all these sites, leaving the factors in disordered, haemostatically inactive conformations. This creates a compounding haemostatic disadvantage after FP transfusion: excess delivered citrate simultaneously chelates Ca2+ from the recipient’s circulation, and the Gla domains of the transfused factors must then compete for the same depleted Ca2+ pool to achieve reconformation. The dual burden of systemic hypocalcaemia and delayed factor activation prolongs the time to functional haemostasis beyond what would be expected from post-treatment INR values alone.

The superior haemostatic efficacy of PCC over FP cannot be fully explained by the relatively small differences in INR values (typically 0.1–0.15 lower with PCC).5,17,19 Importantly, INR reagents contain thromboplastin (tissue factor) at supraphysiological concentrations, in the nanomolar range, whereas in vivo coagulation is triggered by tissue factor concentrations in the femtomolar to picomolar range.20,21 Standard INR assays are performed in citrate-anticoagulated plasma and recalcified with calcium chloride to achieve a final Ca2+ concentration of 2.5–5 mmol L−1. The supraphysiological tissue factor concentration and the recalcification process may together obscure the in vivo inhibitory effects of citrate or calcium–citrate complexes.10

Discrepancies between INR values and clinical haemostasis were also reported by the Innsbruck group in a prospective randomised study comparing factor concentrate therapy (fibrinogen concentrate and PCC or both; n=50) with FP (n=44) for the treatment of trauma-induced coagulopathy.22 FP-based resuscitation (median, 14 units) maintained INR values but was associated with higher rates of massive transfusion and persistently poor bleeding scores compared with the factor concentrate group (median fibrinogen dose, 8 g; median PCC dose, 2000 IU). We estimate that the citrate load from 14 units of FP would exceed 10 g, compared with only 0.7 g from the combined fibrinogen/PCC regimen. These findings closely mirror our present data and support the hypothesis that excess citrate from FP contributes to prolonged impairment of procoagulant function.

Conventionally, the superiority of PCC over FP is attributed to its delivery of vitamin K-dependent factors in a highly concentrated form, permitting administration in a substantially smaller volume. This enables more rapid repletion of vitamin K-dependent factors and typically shorter treatment durations than FP.17 In addition, the lower citrate content of PCC after reconstitution with sterile water would enable vitamin K-dependent factors to regain their optimal Gla-domain configurations more rapidly. The observed difference in haemostatic outcomes between the FP and PCC groups is therefore likely multifactorial, with estimated citrate load representing one modifiable contributor among several. This hypothesis requires prospective investigation incorporating non-citrate blood sampling alongside direct measurement of blood citrate concentrations, divalent cations and coagulation testing at defined time points after PCC or FP administration, to confirm citrate load difference and to identify the coagulopathic citrate threshold.

There are notable limitations to this study. First and most importantly, citrate content was estimated from published product specifications rather than directly measured in patient blood samples. Actual plasma citrate concentrations and their haemostatic consequences could not be determined from this dataset. Calcium chloride administration and Ca2+ concentrations were not routinely recorded and could not be included in the analysis. It should also be noted that citrate content per unit of blood product varies between batches, donors, storage conditions, and processing methods, including whether plasma is derived from whole blood or apheresis donation, introducing further uncertainty into the per-patient estimates. Furthermore, inter-individual variability in hepatic and splanchnic citrate metabolism cannot be inferred from these data; the estimates therefore represent product-delivered citrate rather than measured systemic exposure, and do not account for patient-to-patient differences in clearance. In addition, the estimated citrate load is a surrogate for transfusion volume: patients who bled more received more blood products, thereby receiving more citrate. This bidirectional relationship makes it impossible to determine from these data alone whether citrate causally contributed to bleeding or merely reflected it. The biological plausibility for a citrate-mediated contribution to haemostatic impairment rests on the mechanistic evidence summarised in the Discussion, namely in vitro thrombin generation studies, ionised calcium kinetics, and Gla-domain structural data,8, 9, 10 rather than on the within-arm quartile gradient. A prospective study with direct citrate measurement and concurrent coagulation testing is needed to determine whether citrate burden is a causal contributor or a marker of treatment assignment.

In conclusion, this exploratory, hypothesis-generating post hoc analysis of the FARES-II study identifies markedly different estimated citrate loading patterns between the FP and PCC groups. Approximately six-fold higher estimated citrate exposure in the FP group correlated with increased postoperative chest tube drainage. Although a causal relationship between citrate burden and impaired haemostasis cannot be established because of the lack of direct citrate measurements, these findings raise an important hypothesis. Citrate delivered with FP simultaneously chelates Ca2+ from the recipient’s circulation, while the Gla domains of transfused factors require these Ca2+ ions to achieve reconformation. The dual burden of systemic hypocalcaemia and delayed factor activation may be obscured and, consequently, underappreciated in the citrate-recalcification system used for coagulation assessment. Factor concentrate-based therapies, including PCC, could reduce citrate burdens, and future studies are needed to explore the potential clinical relevance of this approach. If established, strategies to minimise citrate burden post-CPB could help to further improve clinical outcomes for patients.
