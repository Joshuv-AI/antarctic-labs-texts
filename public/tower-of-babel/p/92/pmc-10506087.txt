Genitourinary Mpox: A case report & primer for urologists
Authors: Grace Helene Wegrzyn, Melissa Kilianek, Soumya Yallapragada, Patricia Vidal, Ryan W Dobbs
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractSince May 2022, there has been a worldwide outbreak of Mpox infection, a zoonotic viral disease caused by the monkeypox virus, outside of central and west Africa where it is endemic. In this report, we describe a case of a 23-year-old African American male patient with HIV infection and normal CD4 counts who presented with painful, evolving skin lesions at the base of his penis which were found to be a manifestation of Mpox. Early recognition of the symptomatology caused by Mpox is important for urologists as cases of this virus continue to rise in the United States.Keywords: Mpox, Monkeypox virus, Genital lesions, Emerging communicable diseases, Urogenital diseases

Introduction

Monkeypox, or Mpox an enveloped, double-stranded DNA virus in the Orthopoxvirus genus and Poxvridae family is a viral zoonotic infection with animal hosts such as rodents and non-human primates initially found in central and west Africa. Transmission to humans via close contact with infected species or from materials contaminated with the virus was first identified in 1970 in the Democratic Republic of the Congo. In 2003, the first outbreak outside of Africa occurred in the United States and was linked to contact with infected pet prairie dogs.1,2

In May 2022, multiple cases of Mpox were identified in non-endemic countries resulting in the first widespread outbreak of community acquired disease affecting the men who have sex with men (MSM) community.1,2

Since August 2023, there have been 89,385 confirmed global cases of Mpox with 34.4% of the cases presenting in the United States.2 The typical incubation period ranges from 6 to 13 days followed by an infective period characterized by fever, intense headache, lymphadenopathy, myalgias and fatigue. Usually, 1–3 days after the appearance of fever, an eruptive skin rash occurs on the face, extremities, and mucous membranes. The genitourinary area is the most common location of the rash which most often presents with vesiculopustular lesions.1,3 Herein, we present a case report of Mpox from the John H. Stroger Hospital of Cook County. (JHSHCC).

Case presentation

A 23-year-old African American male with HIV, poorly compliant with antiviral therapy and recent treatment for chlamydia and syphilis within the past year, presented to JHSHCC with GU lesions. Urology was initially consulted for uncontrolled left scrotal pain penile lesions. On exam, patient was noted to have multiple ulcerative “punched out” lesions with malodorous purulent drainage and significant tenderness to palpation located at the base of the penis (Fig. 1). Examination also revealed tender bilateral inguinal lymphadenopathy and scattered pustules with central pitting at various stages of healing on bilateral upper extremities, chest, back and the left lower face. Bilateral cervical lymphadenopathy was also appreciated.

Ulcerated genital lesions.

The patient reported to JHSHCC after confirmation by the Illinois Department of Public Health for a diagnosis of monkeypox from a prior outside hospital visit.

Initial symptoms began two weeks prior to presentation at JHSHCC with painful bumps around the base of his penis. He presented to an outside hospital for a pruritic whole-body rash and fever that resolved the next day. Upon discharge, the patient was prescribed doxycycline but did not pick up the medication.

One week later, the patient presented to an additional outside hospital for worsening symptoms of fever, painful, foul-smelling cream-colored bumps at the base of his penis and scrotum, and new lesions on his torso and upper extremities. The patient reported a recent unprotected sexual encounter with a male partner within the last month and the patient was admitted overnight for pain control. He received 1g oral azithromycin due to concern for possible haemophilus ducreyi infection.

At JHSHCC, the patient was started on vancomycin and piperacillin/tazobactam for empiric skin and soft tissue infection coverage and concern for possible superinfection of monkeypox lesions. He was continued on triple antiviral therapy while admitted and awaiting medical home placement for continued treatment. Infectious disease and HIV services were also consulted. Infectious disease recommended initiating tecovirimat, a novel inhibitor of the orthopoxvirus VP37 envelope wrapping protein, 600mg twice daily for 14 days.

Two days after admission, the patient was transitioned to oral antibiotic therapy and instructed to continue sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim 800mg/160mg twice daily and amoxicillin/clavulanate potassium 500 mg/125 mg three times daily for 10 days at time of discharge. He also continued daily maintenance therapy of Bictegravir/emtricitabine/tenofovir 50mg/200mg/25mg. Outpatient follow-up 3 weeks later demonstrated resolution of penile lesions and inguinal lymphadenopathy.

Discussion

Given the recent outbreak of Mpox in 2022, urologists must be familiar with the various genitourinary presentations of Mpox. This virus can present with a variety of lesions and may be associated with a systemic viral prodrome, as exhibited in our patient, or may present as asymptomatic genital lesions.1,2 The immunocompromised and MSM population are at increased risk of contracting genitourinary Mpox and should be encouraged to notify recent sexual partners of possible transmissions.2

Though the diagnosis of Mpox does not necessarily indicate surgical management, a urology consultation may be essential for evaluation of the genital lesions. These lesions are susceptible to bacterial superinfections as they can progress to open and ulcerated wounds that are exposed to potential bacterial or viral contaminants. Colonizing skin flora are opportunistic pathogens that can contribute to soft tissue infections when exposed to disrupted epithelial integrity compromised by the monkeypox infection.3 Cellulitis and even sepsis have emerged in the setting of other Orthopoxvirus-associated diseases such as cowpox and should therefore not be dismissed as a possible progression of monkeypox infection.3,4 If adequate oral antibiotic coverage of antistreptococcal and antistaphylococcal organisms fails to resolve a clinically worsening lesion, intravenous antibiotics may be required, or coverage may need to be broadened to account for possible involvement of gram negatives or anaerobes. Extensive wounds may even require moistened occlusive dressings to encourage resolution.5

Due to variations of lesion development and resolution ((macular → papular → vesicular → pustular → scab), that usually resolve within 2–3 weeks), GU Mpox may easily be confounded with more commonly encountered sexually transmitted infections such as herpes simplex, molluscum contagiosum, chancre, or granuloma inguinale.

Conclusion

Though genitourinary Mpox is primarily managed medically, the urologist may be consulted for evaluation of genital lesions. Urologists should maintain high suspicion for superinfection of lesions and ensure the diagnosis of Mpox is not overlooked. Patients with suspected monkeypox infection should be followed closely for resolution of the lesions and are provided with adequate antibacterial coverage for suspected opportunistic pathogens.

Monkeypox should be on the differential diagnosis in addition to commonly encountered sexually transmitted infections when evaluating perianal or genital lesions due to the varying symptomatology that manifests in a Mpox infection, especially in the high-risk populations of MSM and HIV or otherwise immunocompromised patients.
