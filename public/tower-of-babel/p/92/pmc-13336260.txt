The peripheral immune landscape of human breast cancer
Authors: Esmeralda García-Torralba, Aitziber Buqué, Lorenzo Galluzzi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe clinical management of patients with breast cancer is largely dictated by tumor stage, disease subtype and (at least in some settings) specific molecular features thereof (e.g., BRCA1 mutations, PD-L1 positivity). However, not all patients with breast cancer respond to therapeutic approaches that a priori are precisely targeted to their disease. At least in part, this may reflect the fact that breast neoplasms are highly heterogeneous from a broad immunological perspective, even when they exhibit similar disease subtype and molecular features. Thus, integrating extra immunological parameters into current decision-making algorithms may considerably improve clinical management in this large patient population. In this context, circulating indicators of global immune fitness and ongoing anticancer immunity stand out as particularly promising tools for minimally invasive prognostic and/or predictive assessments. Here, we summarize and critically discuss the circulating immune landscape of human breast cancer, whenever possible comparing across disease subtypes, stages, and treatment outcomes.

Introduction

Breast cancer remains a major global health burden, with an estimated 2.3 million new diagnoses and more than 650,000 deaths reported worldwide in 2022. 1 , 2 Thus, despite substantial progress in screening, classification and treatment, 3-6 the current clinical management of breast cancer remains suboptimal, calling for the urgent development of more effective strategies to control this deadly disease.

As it stands, disease stage (i.e., tumor size, lymph node involvement and distant dissemination), subtype and patient-related factors are the major determinants of clinical decision making in patients with breast cancer. 7 , 8 More specifically, early-stage disease is generally managed by a locoregional approach involving surgery with appropriate axillary staging and radiotherapy that—when indicated—is integrated with systemic interventions. The latter may be administered either before surgery (neoadjuvant therapy) or after it (adjuvant therapy). Conversely, unresectable (locally advanced) and metastatic breast cancers are primarily managed with systemic therapy, locoregional approaches being reserved for selected clinical indications. 7 , 8

Disease subtype has a major influence on such a systemic therapeutic component. Thus, breast cancers expressing hormone receptors (HRs) such as estrogen receptor 1 (ESR1, best known as ER) and/or progesterone receptor (PGR, best known as PR), but not erb-b2 receptor tyrosine kinase 2 (ERBB2, best known as HER2), are most often treated with endocrine therapy (ET) alone or combined with CDK4/6 inhibitors (when locally advanced or metastatic). 9 Conversely, patients with HER2+ tumors generally receive HER2-specific agents, including modern HER2-targeted antibody-drug conjugates, 10 whereas women with triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) are most often treated with aggressive, taxane-based chemotherapy or (in selected cases, see below) immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). 11

Additional features that influence clinical decision making in patients with breast cancer include: (1) a transcriptional signature of recurrence (commonly known as 21-gene recurrence score), which is generally employed to allocate patients with HR+HER2- breast cancer to adjuvant chemotherapy; 12 (2) mutations in BRCA1 DNA repair associated (BRCA1) or BRCA2, which define eligibility to PARP inhibitors; 13 and (3) the intratumoral levels of CD274 (best known as PD-L1), which are commonly used to allocate patients with TNBC to ICIs (despite suffering from numerous limitations including variability in assessment methodologies and imperfect reproducibility). 11 , 14

All these clinicopathological and molecular criteria, however, remain insufficient to precisely identify subsets of patients with breast cancer that would invariably benefit (or not) from a specific therapeutic course. Indeed, women with apparently comparable tumors often experience markedly different clinical trajectories and responses to therapy. 15-17 Such a variability highlights the inherent limitations of a tumor-centric framework and suggests that additional layers of biological complexity are insufficiently captured by current classification systems.

The tumor microenvironment (TME), especially in its immunological component, has recently emerged as a critical determinant of disease progression and treatment sensitivity in multiple oncological settings, including breast cancer. 18-23 However, with the exception of PD-L1 levels (see above) and the abundance of stromal tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), which has been consistently associated with improved disease outcome in patients with HER2+ breast cancer and TNBC, 24-28 immunological biomarkers have not yet been incorporated into routine breast cancer management, despite an abundant preclinical and clinical literature supporting their value (at least in specific patient populations). 21-23

In this setting, circulating immune cells and soluble immunological mediators (i.e., cytokines and chemokines) stand out as particularly practical proxies not only of global immune fitness, but also of ongoing anticancer immune responses, as (unlike intratumoral parameters) they can be assessed with minimal invasiveness and repeatedly over time, enabling longitudinal monitoring of disease evolution and treatment response. 29 , 30 Here, we examine the circulating immune landscape of breast cancer, encompassing both cellular and soluble components, and discuss its variability across disease subtypes, stages, and treatment contexts. We surmise that the incorporation of (intratumoral and) systemic immunological indicators into existing paradigms may refine patient stratification and ultimately contribute to clinical decision making, with the goal of improving outcomes for this diverse patient population.

Circulating immune cells

Patients with breast cancer can experience considerable shifts in the overall abundance of circulating immune cell subsets that may be caused (directly or indirectly, via alterations in the hematopoietic output of the bone marrow) by natural disease progression as well as by the response of malignant cells to therapy. Recent large-scale single-cell analyzes across various inflammatory conditions including breast cancer support the view that circulating immune cells may be harnessed as “living biomarkers” of disease states, although the precise utility of such high-dimensional approaches for clinical breast cancer management remains to be defined. 31 Thus, while relatively underinvestigated and hence less understood than immunological features of malignant lesions themselves, some of the alterations in the circulating immune compartment of patients with breast cancer may offer a convenient and minimally invasive means for prognostic and predictive evaluations that may soon add to the diagnostic armamentarium currently available to clinicians (Table 1).

Compared to 47 healthy controls and 401 patients with benign breast disease, 134 women with breast cancer exhibited reductions in the circulating levels of T lymphocytes, notably CD8 + cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs), 55 coupled with an increase in the relative abundance of blood-borne natural killer (NK) cells 56 and CD4+ T cells, as well as with an increased expression of cytotoxic immune effector molecules like granzyme B (GZMB) and perforin 1 (PRF1) in most lymphocytic subsets. 36 An increase of peripheral markers of immune activation coupled with an accumulation of dysfunctional CD8+ and CD4+ T cells has also been documented in a cohort of 65 Ethiopian patients with primary breast cancer compared to healthy individuals of the same ethnicity. 57 Of note, numerical alterations of the peripheral immune compartment appear to be more pronounced in patients with HER2- vs HER2+ disease, correlating with a skewing in circulating CD8+ CTLs towards antigen-inexperienced and central memory phenotypes (but limited intratumoral CD8+ CTL abundance as well as decreased numbers of tumor-specific CD8+ CTLs in the circulation). 58 Such a relative increase in the abundance of circulating CD4+ T cells may reflect not only a decrease in the absolute number of circulating CD8+ T cells, but also an increase in blood-borne regulatory T (T REG ) cells 59 , 60 over T H 1 CD4 + T cells, 61 as documented in multiple cohort of patients with breast cancer across disease subsets. 57 , 62-64 Besides these numerical alterations, the circulating lymphoid compartment of patients with breast cancer, notably in its T and NK cellular components, appears to exhibit limited sensitivity to activating stimuli ex vivo, starting as early as at localized disease stages. 65 Moreover, as assessed in 6 healthy volunteers, 12 patients with primary breast cancer and 30 women with metastatic disease, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) analyzed as a whole acquire transcriptional signatures of dysfunction alongside tumor progression, including increased expression levels of transcripts encoding PD-L1 and forkhead box P3 (FOXP3). 66

Flow cytometry and functional assessments on circulating NK cells from patients with breast cancer vs healthy women also revealed a progressive downregulation of NK cell activating receptors coupled with compromised cytotoxicity alongside disease progression, although such alterations were less pronounced in the periphery that within the tumor microenvironment (TME). 67 In line with this notion, a proteomic analysis of PBMCs from 104 patients with metastatic HER2- breast cancer vs 20 healthy donors revealed an increase in circulating CD8+ CTLs and TREG cells coupled with profound signs of dysfunction, including an elevated expression of co-inhibitory T cell receptors (at least partially correlated to treatment status) as well as an overall shift of the circulating immune compartment toward a less cytotoxic profile associated with interleukin 17 (IL17) secretion. 68 Similarly, γδ T cells (a fraction of which produce IL17) 69 were found to be increased amongst 65 patients with primary breast cancer of Ethiopian descent, 57 and Vδ1+ γδ T cells from patients with metastatic TNBC have been shown to produce more IL17 than their counterparts from healthy women. 70 Some of the aforementioned findings have been recapitulated in a cohort of 92 patients with metastatic TNBC, 44 women with non-metastatic TNBC and 65 healthy individuals, also revealing a significant drop in differentiated (but not naïve) B lymphocytes 71-73 alongside disease progression, although in this scenario limited differences in co-inhibitory T cell expression beyond an increase in PD-1 levels on TREG cells in patients with disseminated disease could be detected. 70 Importantly, circulating TREG cells haven suggested to closely represent their intratumoral counterparts, 54 pointing to this specific immune cell subtype as a convenient proxy to assess (at least some) intratumoral immunological parameters.

Patients with metastatic TNBC have also been shown to exhibit reductions in the circulating abundance of dendritic cells (DCs) 74-76 compared to patients with localized disease and healthy women, potentially linked to previous exposure to treatment. 70 Besides numerical alterations, blood-borne DCs from patients with breast cancer reportedly exhibit limit sensitivity to cytokine-driven activation ex vivo. 65 Moreover, the circulating monocytes from about 40% breast cancer patients appear to be refractory to inflammatory stimuli ex vivo, rather preserving a secretory profile dominated by immunosuppressive, pro-metastatic and angiogenic cytokines. 77 Conversely, the peripheral immune compartment of patients with metastatic TNBC is enriched for classical monocytes and neutrophils with superior migratory of oxidative capacity, perhaps encompassing myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs). 63 , 70 , 78 A similar elevation in blood-borne monocytes has been documented in 104 patients with metastatic HER2- breast cancer. 68 Moreover, immature myeloid cells were found to be increased in the circulation of 34 patients with early stage BCs compared to 68 healthy adults, as well as in a cohort of 75 patients with invasive breast cancer compared to localized disease, especially so in the TNBC subgroup. 79 Interestingly, most of the alterations imposed by developing BCs to the peripheral immune compartment of mice can be effectively reversed by surgical tumor removal. 63 However, these preclinical breast cancer models are often incapable of forming spontaneous metastasis that may be accelerated by surgery-driven systemic immunosuppression (see below), implying these observations may not be directly translatable to patients.

As in many other oncological indications, patients with breast cancer receiving chemotherapy undergo considerable rearrangements in the peripheral immune compartments, at least in a fraction of cases exhibiting some degree of lymphopenia short after treatment initiation. 80 Importantly, not all circulating leukocytes exhibit similar sensitivity to chemotherapeutic regimens commonly employed in patients with breast cancer. For instance, B cells, TH1-polarized CD4+ T cells and NK cells were the most susceptible to decline (at least initially) in a cohort of 56 patients with TNBC undergoing neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) with nanoparticle albumin-bound paclitaxel, with limited alterations in circulating CD8+ CTLs. 81 That said, several months upon NACT completion, most circulating immune cells tend to rebound to normal levels, although with considerable alterations in specific subsets, including a persisting decrease of memory B cells over their naïve counterparts, as well as naïve and effector CD4+ T cells over their memory counterparts. 82 Moreover, it has been suggested NACT increases the circulating abundance of CD3+, CD4+, CD8+ T cells and NK cells across breast cancer subtypes (especially in NACT-sensitive patients, see below). 36 Conversely, trastuzumab reportedly elevates the circulating levels of CD3+, CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, but favor B and NK cell depletion, in patients with HER2+ breast cancer not experiencing pCRs (but not their trastuzumab-sensitive counterparts). 37 In patients with metastatic HER2- breast cancer, ICI-containing regimens based on metronomic cyclophosphamide, capecitabine and vinorelbine induced a specific reprogramming of circulating immune cells characterized by reductions in CD4+ central memory T cells coupled with increases in classical monocytes and NK cells and limited alterations of B cell and DC abundance. 38 Moreover, in a small series of 11 patients with early-stage TNBC, an individualized mRNA vaccine delivered after standard-of-care (SOC) regimens elicited high-magnitude circulating T-cell responses that remained functional for years and progressively acquired late cytotoxic effector and stem-like memory features, further illustrating how longitudinal blood monitoring can capture durable treatment-induced immunity. 83

Consistent with the notion that local tissue injury can rapidly reshape systemic immunity, patients receiving intra-operative radiotherapy during breast-conserving surgery exhibited an early local recruitment of plasmacytoid DCs, eosinophils and monocytes that was paralleled by transient increases in circulating eosinophils and monocytes and by systemic signs of emergency hematopoiesis. 44 Interestingly, surgery itself has also been shown to cause alterations of the peripheral immune compartment, notably an inflammation-driven expansion of MDCSs, 84 that (in preclinical breast cancer models) supports the outgrowth of dormant cancer cells at distant anatomical sites, at least in part by promoting the generation of pro-metastatic niches, 85 , 86 potentially via aggravated NK dysfunction. 84 , 87 , 88 These latter findings support the use of anti-inflammatory agents in a peri-operative settings with the specific aim of limiting metastatic disease progression.

Importantly, the circulating neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) has been consistently associated with dismal disease outcome in patients with a variety of tumors including breast cancer. 80 , 89 However, while pre-treatment NLR appears to independently predicts poor prognosis—particularly in patients receiving adjuvant chemotherapy—findings in the neoadjuvant setting remain inconsistent, at least potentially due to differences in disease subtype, stage, treatment regimen, and cut-off values across individual studies. 52 , 90 In the metastatic setting, an elevated NLR has also been associated with poor disease outcome, although its prognostic impact may be influenced by clinical variables such as tumor burden and treatment response. 50 , 51 Interestingly, some reports indicate that a decline in the NLR following therapy correlates with improved prognosis, supporting a role for NLR assessments as dynamic rather than static biomarkers. 53 , 91 The platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) and monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR) have also been investigated for their prognostic relevance in patients with breast cancer. However, their clinical value remains uncertain due to variations in study methodologies and the hitherto unresolved need for prospective validation studies. 92

Assessing circulating leukocytes in a more granular manner (including approaches incorporating machine learning) 93 may offer superior prognostic or predictive information compared to rather coarse parameters such as the NLR. Consistent with this, a high frequency of circulating CD8+ CTLs with a diverse TCR repertoire showed a trend for association with improved responses to NACT in patients with HER2⁺ breast cancer. 41 Likewise, in a cohort of 47 patients HR+ breast cancer, limited numbers of circulating naïve CD8+ CTLs and effector memory CD4+ T cells correlated with residual disease after NACT. 49 A similar detrimental impact on treatment responses has been attributed to poor responsiveness of the peripheral CD4+ T cell compartment to IL6 stimulation in 40 patients with various breast cancer subtypes receiving SOC disease management, 46 as well as to low circulating CD4+ T cells at baseline in 63 patients with TNBC receiving the PD-L1 blocker durvalumab. 39 In the latter setting, treatment sensitivity correlated with the durvalumab-driven expansion of circulating γδ T cells as well as to the longitudinal loss of blood-borne CD8+ and CD4+ T cells. 39 Moreover, women with breast cancer experiencing pronounced increases in blood-borne CD3+ and CD8+ T cells coupled with sizeable drops in circulating B cells experienced improved responses following NACT compared to individuals experiencing limited alterations of the peripheral immune compartment in a cohort of 134 patients with diverse breast cancer subtypes. 36 In a series of 111 patients with TNBC receiving a PD-1 blocker in the neoadjuvant setting, longitudinal increases in circulating TREG cells and eosinophils (as well a reduction in circulating CD1c+ DCs) were associated with pathological complete response (pCR), on-treatment eosinophil expansion also correlating with progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival. 34 Finally, the responsiveness of peripheral TREG cells collected at baseline to cytokine stimulation ex vivo has been successfully harnessed as a proxy for intratumoral TREG cell polarization and predict disease relapse in two cohort of 40 and 38 patients with diverse breast cancer subtypes. 54 Apparently at odds with these findings, transcriptional signatures of superior cytotoxicity in PBMCs collected at surgery have been associated with extensive residual disease in a series of 83 patients with breast cancer, including 44 TNBC cases, subjected to NACT. 94 At least hypothetically, these latter findings may reflect the persistence of an overall inefficient anticancer immune response in patients failing to eradicate disease upon NACT.

The amount of CD16+ NK cells in the circulation and their capacity to actively mediate antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) has been associated with improved pCR rates in a small cohort of 18 patients with HER2+ receiving neoadjuvant trastuzumab. 35 That said, the overall abundance of CD57+ NK cells has also been proposed to negatively correlate with pCR rates in a larger cohort of 66 patients with HER2+ breast cancer undergoing the same therapeutic approach, a setting in which increased numbers of blood-borne NK cells were not linked to altered ADCC, but to detrimental changes in CD16-induced IL2-dependent proliferation ex vivo. 40 Moreover, neoadjuvant trastuzumab has been reported to limit ADCC in PBMCs out of a series of pre- (n = 41) and post- (n = 25) blood samples from women with HER2+ breast cancer, with the lack of pembrolizumab-recoverable ex vivo ADCC activity being associated with pCR. 37 Conversely, an increased abundance of circulating IL7R+ (activated) γδ T cells has been shown to correlate with shortened PFS in a cohort of 23 patients with HR+HER2- breast cancer receiving CDK4/6 inhibitors plus hormonotherapy. 33 Similar findings were obtained by assessing the abundance of CD3+CD4+CD8- T cells (most of which are γδ T cells) in baseline blood samples from the aforementioned cohort plus an extra series of 29 patients with HR+HER2- breast cancer treated with a comparable paradigm. 33

In a cohort of 39 patients with TNBC, elevated levels of myeloid cells in the circulation have been associated with reduced pCR rates. 49 However, these results have not been confirmed in 4 independent patient cohorts, rather suggesting a positive correlation between circulating monocyte abundance sensitivity to NACT. 48 At least potentially, such an apparent discrepancy may be accounted for by the activation of specific transcriptional programs in the monocytic compartment of responders vs non-responders. 48 Additional work is required to formally address this possibility.

Cytokines

Patients with breast cancer can exhibit marked fluctuations in the systemic levels of circulating cytokines. Although such soluble immunological cues have received less attention than the intratumoral immune landscape, accumulating evidence suggests that dynamic changes in cytokine profiles may provide accessible, minimally invasive biomarkers with prognostic and predictive value, potentially complementing and expanding the diagnostic tools currently available in the clinical practice for this cancer type (Table 1).

While it has been suggested that patients with HER2- breast cancer (but less so women with HER2+ breast cancer) would exhibit decreased circulating levels of various cytokines encompassing IL2, IL6 and IL8, 58 serum IL8 levels are increased in a majority (>65%) of patients with advanced breast cancer, overall being significantly higher than in healthy volunteers. 47 This is also true for the circulating levels of other pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin 1 beta (IL1B, best known as IL1β) and tumor necrosis factor (TNF), 95 as well as for the blood-borne amounts of the pro-angiogenic and immunomodulatory cytokine vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA), when comparing patients with metastatic vs early-stage breast cancer and/or healthy donors or women with benign breast disorders. 96 In the latter setting, circulating VEGFA levels failed to correlated with any clinicopathological factor, but were increased in patients receiving tamoxifen-based hormonotherapy. 96 Plasma transforming growth factor beta 1 (TGFB1, best known as TGF-β) concentrations have also been shown to be increased in a cohort of 44 patients with stage IIIB/IV breast cancer (across disease subtypes) as compared to 36 healthy donors. 97 Interestingly, patients with breast cancer (especially TNBC) also exhibit increased circulating levels of tumor-derived extracellular vesicles (EVs) expressing transforming growth factor beta receptor 2 (TGFBR2), 98 but whether these two latter observations are mechanistically connected to each other remains unclear. Of note, in patients with breast cancer undergoing breast-conserving surgery with intra-operative radiotherapy, the postoperative wound fluid not only was rapidly enriched in macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF) and IL3, but also exhibited transcriptional signs of signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) signaling, consistent with an early locally generated cytokine program capable of coordinating innate immune recruitment and systemic myeloid mobilization. 44

The circulating levels of various cytokines have also been attributed prognostic or predictive value in multiple cohorts of patients with breast cancer. Thus, an early increase in serum C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 9 (CXCL9) levels identified patients with NK cell-rich HER2+ breast cancers exhibiting pronounced sensitivity to neoadjuvant trastuzumab. 43 Conversely, elevated plasma concentrations of TGF-β and CXCL1, positively correlating with the abundance of circulating tumor cells, have been linked with poor prognosis in 61 patients with metastatic breast cancer (across disease subtypes). 42 Moreover, increased C-C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2) levels at baseline as well as longitudinal elevations of IL6 have been associated with shortened PFS in cohorts of 23 and 166 patients with HR+HER2- breast cancer receiving CDK4/6 inhibitors plus hormonotherapy, respectively. 33 , 45 Serum IL8 was also identified as an independent prognostic factor for post-relapse survival amongst 69 patients with either operable or advanced breast cancer. 47 Finally, at least in preclinical breast cancer models, surgery has been proposed to promote metastatic disease progression by favoring an increase in MDSC-targeting cytokines including CCL2, IL6 and colony stimulating factor 3 (CSF3, best known as G-CSF). 85 , 86

Collectively, these observations suggest that patients with breast cancer experience considerable alteration of their peripheral immune compartment, including (but not limited to) surges in pro-inflammatory cytokines that expand circulating MDSCs in support of disease progression. Of note, these changes appear to be more pronounced in patients with TNBC compared to other breast cancer subtypes, potentially reflecting its relatively more inflammatory nature. Conventional therapies including NACT also influence blood-borne immune cells and cytokines in women with breast cancer, and many of these changes have been associated with prognostic or predictive factors in specific patient cohorts. While these findings support the notion that systemic immune proficiency is required for therapeutically relevant tumor-targeting immune responses, 99 , 100 which may also explain why adjuvant PD-L1 blockage does not improve outcomes in patients with TNBC 101 (while neoadjuvant PD-L1 or PD-1 blockage does), 11 , 102-104 whether any of these parameters can be harnessed in support of routine clinical decision making, however, remains unclear.

Concluding remarks

Taken together, these observations suggest that the circulating immune compartment—encompassing both cellular and soluble mediators—captures a biologically and clinically relevant dimension of breast cancer that may not be adequately recapitulated by cancer-intrinsic or intratumoral parameters alone. Indeed, fluctuations in circulating leukocyte subsets and cytokine networks not only appear to accompany natural disease progression and response to therapy, but have also been repeatedly associated with clinical outcome across patient cohorts. Thus, systemic immune features may provide orthogonal information to established clinicopathological and molecular variables, potentially contributing to a more integrated and functionally informative framework for patient stratification. In this context, promising biomarkers include the circulating levels of CCL2 and IL6, at least in patients with HR+ HER2- breast cancer receiving CDK4/6 inhibitors plus hormonotherapy, 33 , 45 but additional studies are required to formally establish their actual prognostic and/or predictive value.

Indeed, several barriers hinder the clinical utility of circulating immune biomarkers. First, available evidence largely derives from relatively small and heterogeneous cohorts, often lacking harmonization in patient selection, sampling procedures, and analytical pipelines. Second, circulating immune parameters are inherently dynamic and subject to modulation by tumor-unrelated factors, including comorbidities and concomitant therapies, which complicates their interpretation. Third, most associations remain correlative, with limited mechanistic insight into the causal contribution of specific systemic immune alterations to disease progression or therapeutic responsiveness. Finally, the integration of circulating biomarkers with tumor-centric features, including genomic indicators and intratumoral immune parameters, remains insufficiently explored.

Overcoming these limitations will require coordinated efforts along two main axes. First, prospective, well-annotated clinical studies incorporating longitudinal sampling are needed to validate candidate biomarkers and establish robust thresholds with clinical utility, ideally in the context of ongoing therapeutic trials. Second, integrative, multi-parametric approaches combining systemic and tumor-localized features—potentially supported by high-dimensional profiling and computational modeling—are expected to yield superior predictive power.

Altogether, while circulating immune parameters are unlikely to replace established determinants of clinical decision making in the near future, their incorporation into multimodal frameworks may enable more precise, dynamic, and adaptive management of patients with breast cancer.
