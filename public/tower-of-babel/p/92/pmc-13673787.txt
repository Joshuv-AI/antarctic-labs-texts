A qualitative study on barriers and motivators to attending teaching sessions among Singapore Junior Doctors
Authors: Nur Emillia Roslan, Khaing Zin That, Chaoyan Dong, Astrid Melani Suantio, Qiao Qi Teo, Priscilla Pei Sze Chiam, Wai Lun Moy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPostgraduate medical training plays a critical role in preparing junior doctors for independent practice, yet participation in teaching sessions remains challenging in many clinical training environments. Existing literature highlights persistent tensions between clinical service demands and learning, compounded by diverse learner needs, limited resources, and entrenched workplace cultures. Similarly, in Singapore, junior doctors are expected to attend structured teaching during working hours, but attendance remains suboptimal. This study aims to explore these junior doctors’ perception of the barriers and motivators influencing their attendance at these teaching sessions, and how they negotiate learning within service-intensive environments.MethodsWe conducted a qualitative exploratory study in March 2025 at a large tertiary hospital in Singapore. Fourteen junior doctors participated in semi-structured focus group discussions. Discussions explored their perceptions of the value of teaching sessions, motivations for attendance, barriers encountered, and suggestions for improvement. Transcripts were analysed inductively using thematic analysis, supported by independent coding and iterative discussion among the research team.ResultsParticipants valued the teaching sessions as they help to address knowledge gaps and enhance clinical confidence. Motivation to attend was influenced by both extrinsic factors (e.g., lunch, attendance requirements) and intrinsic factors (e.g., interest in relevant topics). However, attendance was hindered by clinical workload and interruptions during sessions. Participants recommended protected training time, flexible delivery formats and improved content selection. Supportive departmental learning culture and timely administrative reminders further facilitated participation.ConclusionsJunior doctors perceive teaching sessions as valuable and are motivated to engage in learning, but face workload-related barriers that limit attendance. Curated topic selection to enhance clinical relevance, providing multiple formats to deliver learning resources and ensuring protected training time may help improve participation. The participants’ perspectives offer practical strategies that educators can use to enhance attendance and strengthen postgraduate teaching. Background

Postgraduate medical training is a crucial phase in the development of competent doctors, bridging the gap between medical school and real-world independent practice. Research in health professions education (HPE) recognises workplace learning as central to postgraduate training. Junior doctors learn through participation in authentic clinical practice, supervision, and reflection on patient care experiences. However, workplace learning is often opportunistic. Structured teaching sessions thus complement components of postgraduate curricula by providing intentionally scheduled opportunities for learning [1].

Current evidence on structured teaching sessions has been mixed [2, 3]. One of the factors that may contribute to its overall effectiveness is the attendance rates amongst its target audience. Ensuring junior doctors’ participation in core teaching in the workplace remains a global challenge. Existing literature highlights multiple contributory factors, including the tension between clinical service and learning opportunities, the variation in learners’ needs, and constraints due to limited teaching resources [4–6] Among these, clinical load and workplace learning culture have been cited as the most influential yet hardest factors to change [7] In contrast, the structure and content of the teaching sessions, appear to have less impact on their learning [7] This underscores a misalignment between where efforts are directed and where the greatest impact could be achieved.

These challenges are similarly evident in Singapore where junior doctors form the backbone of the public healthcare system. This group includes Postgraduate Year (PGY) 1 trainees, Medical Officers (MO, second year onwards), and Residents (enrolled in accredited specialist training programmes). Although they follow different training pathways, these junior doctors work together in the hospital environment. Most of their work is in the inpatient wards, with some exposure to the outpatient clinics. In addition to their clinical duties, they are required to attend at least four hours of structured teaching per week to consolidate learning from clinical experience and to reinforce core competencies required for safe practice. These teaching sessions are conducted in the hospital during working hours, usually between 12 − 1 pm or 1–2 pm.

The learning outcomes in these sessions are for junior doctors to improve their knowledge and competencies in acute clinical management, escalation of care and professional judgement, which may not be consistently acquired through opportunistic workplace exposure alone. The contents of these sessions help them contextualise and integrate their day-to-day clinical experiences.

Limited research has examined how junior doctors perceive the usefulness of these sessions, and how they negotiate participation in teaching despite heavy clinical service demands [8, 9] By applying the adult learning theory, their perspectives on workplace learning can be further explored [10].

The aim of this study is to explore junior doctors’ perceptions of barriers and motivators influencing their attendance at teaching sessions. It seeks to deepen our understanding of how they prioritise and negotiate learning within a highly-service-intensive healthcare system. These insights may help educators refine postgraduate curricula to better address junior doctors’ current learning needs.

Methods

Setting

This study was conducted in March 2025 in the Department of General Medicine in a 1,000-bed tertiary public hospital serving the North-East region of Singapore. This region has a resident population of approximately 477,620. The Department of General Medicine is the largest clinical department in the hospital, occupying 50–70% of inpatient beds. The department comprises approximately 190–200 junior doctors, who work in teams under the supervision of the consultants.

Structured teaching in the department follows a regular schedule designed to cater to the educational needs of junior doctors at different levels of training. This includes a weekly session targeted specifically for PGY 1 trainees, a twice-weekly Medicine Core Series open to all junior staff, and a monthly Peer Review Learning or Morbidity and Mortality (M&M) presentation attended by both junior and senior doctors. At the time of the study, attendance at teaching sessions was variable, with some reporting participation in almost all weekly sessions, while others attended as few as two to three sessions per month.

Study design and participants

We conducted a qualitative exploratory study using semi-structured focus group discussions (FGD) to explore junior doctors’ perceptions of attending teaching sessions. The research team comprised six General Medicine consultant physicians (N.E.R., T.K.Z., W.L.M., Q.Q.T., A.M.S., P.C.P.S.) and one educationalist (C.D.) experienced in conducting qualitative research studies. Before data collection, the research team met and reviewed the discussion guide, and C.D. briefed the team on focus group facilitation.

The interview guide was developed by the research team through reviewing the published research on similar topics and refined through internal discussion [8, 11–16]. Although the guide was not formally piloted, minor edits were made after the initial focus group to improve clarity and flow of questioning while keeping consistency with the study aims.The interview guide is available in Supplemental Appendix A. The guide served as a flexible framework to ensure key thematic areas were consistently explored across all focus groups, rather than as a fixed questionnaire to be administered sequentially. Interviewers used open-ended questions and exercised judgement to pursue emerging threads of dialogue that were relevant to study aims. This approach is consistent with the principles of semi-structured qualitative interviewing, which prioritises participant-led exploration.

Purposive sampling was employed to ensure representation across postgraduate training levels (PGY 1, PGY 2–4, PGY 5–7, PGY 8 and above) to reflect different stages of clinical experience and learning needs. Participants were recruited via email invitations sent to all junior doctors in the department and all volunteers who responded were included. Volunteers were then allocated into focus groups deliberately stratified by training level. All data were anonymised and kept confidential.

Three focus groups were formed with a total of 14 participants. Table 1 summarises the participants’ demographics. Participants represented a range of postgraduate training levels (PGY 1 – PGY 8), including 8 PGY 1 trainees, 2 PGY 2–4 trainees, 3 PGY 5–7 trainees, and 1 PGY 8. The majority of participants were male (n = 12; 86%), with 2 female participants (14%).

Focus group discussions were conducted in private meeting rooms in the hospital, and each lasted approximately 60 min. Discussions were audio-recorded with participants’ consent, transcribed using Microsoft Teams and manually verified for accuracy by the lead interviewers.

Following completion of the three focus group discussions, the research team reviewed all transcripts and noted recurring patterns across the discussions. These recurring patterns addressed the study aims within our single-site setting. In view of this, we stopped further data collection.

Personnel and reflexivity

The research team comprised six General Medicine consultant physicians and one educationalist.

Five consultant physicians (N.E.R., T.K.Z., Q.Q.T., A.M.S., P.C.P.S.) are core faculty members of the PGY 1 training programme who are directly involved in designing and delivering structured teaching sessions for PGY 1 trainees. P.C.P.S. also serves as Programme Director, while N.E.R. and A.M.S. are Associate Programme Directors. This dual role as both researchers and stakeholders in the teaching curriculum represents a significant positionality that we acknowledge directly. Notably, one team member (W.L.M.) serves as the Associate Designated Institutional Official for postgraduate medical education, holding broader oversight responsibility for the postgraduate training environment beyond the departmental level. As W.L.M. was not involved in conducting the focus group discussions, his positionality did not influence data collection.

C.D. is an educationalist from the Education Office, with expertise in qualitative research methodology but without clinical training or involvement in teaching delivery. Her perspective as a non-clinician without personal investment in the teaching curriculum provided an important counterbalance to the clinical faculty’s insider positionality. C.D. played a central role in facilitating the focus group discussions and guiding the analytical process.

Recognising that our roles as senior faculty members could influence participants’ responses, we took deliberate steps to minimise potential power differentials. Interviewers (Q.Q.T., T.K.Z., C.D.) were not direct supervisors of any participant. At the start of each focus group, participants were informed that their responses would remain confidential and would not affect their professional evaluations.

The positionality risks were mitigated through ongoing reflexive team discussions throughout data analysis, and with the inclusion of C.D. as a non-clinician analytical counterbalance.

Data analysis

We conducted a reflexive thematic analysis guided by the six-phase framework described by Braun and Clarke [17]. This approach was selected for its flexibility and suitability in identifying patterns across diverse viewpoints, which we anticipated given the heterogeneous experience and backgrounds of our junior doctor cohort. Our initial coding was guided by the semi-structured focus group discussion guide. The study team reviewed the initial codes and discussed the themes, which went a step beyond the surface level of descriptive analysis, and explored the emerging themes to address the underlying issues such as assumptions, tensions between clinical service and training, and patterns of junior doctors’ attendance in teaching session.

Each transcript was independently reviewed by two members of the research team. In the first phase, reviewers familiarised themselves with the data through repeated reading. In the second phase, initial codes were generated independently by two members of the research team. In the third phase, an AI language model (ChatGPT 4.1, OpenAI) was used as an assistive tool to help organise researcher-generated codes into provisional thematic clusters. This was used only after initial independent coding had been completed by the research team. All AI-generated suggestions were treated as provisional. In the fourth phase, the research team reviewed the provisional themes against the full dataset to assess their coherence and fit. In the fifth phase, themes were defined and named, with subthemes identified where appropriate. Discrepancies identified by the cross-checker at any stage were resolved through consensus discussion among the team. In the sixth phase, the final write-up was produced, with final themes, interpretations, and conclusions developed exclusively through research team discussion. Throughout the process, the research team had regular discussions to analyse how our roles as clinician-educators might influence interpretation.

The roles of the members of the research team in data collection, transcript verification, and data analysis are summarised in a table in Supplemental Appendix B.

Ethics

This study was reviewed by SingHealth Centralized Institutional Review Board (IRB Ref: 2025 − 0153) and qualified for an exemption according to its policies.

Results

Four themes were developed from the analysis. These themes show that junior doctors’ engagement with structured teaching was shaped not only by the content of individual sessions or motivation, but by the interaction between professional identity, service demands, perceived clinical relevance, and access to flexible learning opportunities. Some themes correspond to the interview guide; the analysis sought to interpret how these areas interacted within the clinical setting.

Theme 1: teaching as clinical scaffolding for becoming a doctor

Participants valued teaching sessions that were practical and applicable to their daily clinical work (see Table 2). These sessions were viewed as more than knowledge transmission and helped them to translate prior knowledge into clinical decision-making. This was shown during discussions of common on-call scenarios, when escalation decisions were needed. Specialist-led updates and Morbidity and Mortality (M&M) Rounds were also viewed as valuable learning opportunities. Curated summaries of key messages and facilitated reflection on near-miss events further enhanced the participants’ learning experience. Conversely, sessions perceived as repetitive or pitched below their level were considered less beneficial, which at times contributed to selective non-attendance.

Theme 2: motivation is shaped by relevance, competence and workplace support

Motivation for attending teaching sessions was shaped by the interaction between extrinsic incentives, intrinsic interest, perceived relevance, and workplace support (see Table 3). Extrinsic motivators included incentives such as the provision of lunch and expectations tied to key performance indicators (KPIs). Intrinsic motivators stemmed from personal interest in the topics, perceived usefulness for clinical work, and a genuine commitment to learning. Participants also highlighted a supportive departmental culture and appreciated timely reminders for upcoming sessions.

Theme 3: service demands constrain the junior doctors’ role as learners

Clinical service demands hamper junior doctors’ attendances for structured teaching sessions outside of the wards, particularly during periods of high patient volume or limited staffing (see Table 4). Lunchtime teaching sessions often coincided with ward work, patient discharges and updating patients’ next of kin. Frequent text messages and calls from ward staff also disrupted their ability to focus during teaching. Participants did not view non-attendance as a reflection of a lack of interest in learning. Rather, they described patient care responsibilities as the priority. This created a tension between their role as frontline clinicians versus their role as postgraduate learners.

Discussion

Using an interpretivist approach informed by reflexive thematic analysis, this study identified key tensions shaping junior doctors’ participation in structured teaching. As early-career clinicians, junior doctors must balance the demands of patient care with the need to engage in postgraduate training. Alongside on-the-job supervision, structured teaching sessions remain an important component of their education. Attendance at these teaching sessions was shaped by professional identity as a doctor-in-training, workplace culture, learner motivation and clinical service demands.

Consistent with prior literature [4, 7], clinical workload emerged as a primary barrier to attendance in our study. Participants often prioritised patient care over attending formal teaching, responding to ward tasks, calls from nurses and allied health staff instead of postponing non-urgent tasks until after the teaching sessions. These pressures contribute to cognitive overload and reduce receptiveness to learning. Even when participants were physically present in a classroom, their attention was often divided by pending ward responsibilities. Importantly, participants did not frame this tension as a simple conflict between “work” and “learning”. Rather, ward participation itself was seen as central to becoming a doctor. From a workplace learning perspective, the ward represented the primary site of professional identity formation. Some junior doctors found it difficult to step away from this primary community of practice to attend structured teaching, especially during busy periods.

Protected training time was proposed as a potential solution in multiple studies [4, 6, 7] and was echoed by our participants. ‘Protected training time’ refers to a designated period during which junior doctors are relieved of routine clinical duties so that they can participate in teaching. Our department had previously trialled this approach in selected wards by asking nurses and allied health staff to limit non-urgent contact with junior doctors during teaching sessions. This initiative produced positive outcomes. It also signalled that the department valued learning within the clinical environment, giving junior doctors a sense of psychological permission to prioritise their role as learners temporarily. However, scaling this across the institution and sustaining it long term remains challenging and would require broader organizational support, consistent enforcement, and adequate staffing to ensure clinical care is not compromised.

Motivation was another important influence on participation. They influence not only whether junior doctors attend teaching but also the depth of engagement. Participants described both extrinsic motivators, such as lunch provision, face-to-face interactions with their peers and KPI-linked attendance, and intrinsic motivators, such as personal interest in certain topics and the desire to be competent. Although institutional practices may strengthen extrinsic motivation, sustained engagement is more likely to depend on intrinsic motivation and a supportive learning culture. These findings align with self-determination theory, which proposes that learners’ engagement is supported by autonomy, competence, and relevance [18]. Sessions that were clinically relevant, appropriately pitched, and aligned with their future goals attracted more participants. By contrast, repetitive or overly basic sessions were seen as less useful, especially to more senior trainees.

Our findings suggest a strong preference for self-directed learning amongst junior doctors. Participants valued concise pre-reading and access to resources that could be revisited in their own time. Some described reviewing materials after work, indicating an active commitment to learning beyond scheduled teaching hours. They also suggested having a dedicated channel for follow-up questions after they had time to reflect on or apply the knowledge. These preferences suggest that accessible, clinically relevant, asynchronous resources may support independent learning when formal attendance in teaching sessions is disrupted. Importantly, participants were not disengaged from learning when unable to attend formal teaching sessions. Rather, they adapted their learning strategies to the demands of a high-intensity clinical environment.

Our findings suggest that improving engagement requires more than just increasing incentives or adjusting schedules. Participation was shaped by interaction between clinical workload, professional identity, workplace culture, learner motivation, and access to learning resources. Addressing these factors may help postgraduate programmes improve their learning experience.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the participants were drawn from a single clinical department in one teaching hospital. As local departmental practices and cultural norms likely shaped these findings, their applicability to other departments or institutions may be limited. Second, as a qualitative exploratory study, the findings describe participants’ perceptions and experiences rather than quantifying the prevalence and magnitude of the issues identified. Third, the reasons for lower female participation were not explored. It is possible that the perspectives of female junior doctors are under-represented in these focus group discussions. Fourth, there is potential selection bias, as participants who were available or willing to be interviewed may differ from those who could not participate in the study due to high workloads or persistent non-attendances. Social desirability bias may also have influenced responses, with participants possibly overstating motivation or perceived value and underreporting barriers. Lastly, thematic saturation was deemed to have been reached based on the researcher’s coding of the transcripts. The research team’s use of two coders and a cross-checker for each transcript serves to improve the validity of the thematic saturation.

Future research using larger, multi-site, and mixed-methods designs could further validate these findings, quantify their prevalence and impact, and evaluate the outcomes of the proposed interventions.

Conclusions

Junior doctors perceive clear value in structured teaching sessions and remain keen to participate despite competing service demands, with motivation arising from both external drivers and intrinsic personal goals. The participants’ insights and suggestions offer valuable, actionable guidance for educators seeking to enhance attendance, engagement, and overall effectiveness of postgraduate training.
