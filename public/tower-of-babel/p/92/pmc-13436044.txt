Narcissistic Traits and Narcissistic Personality Disorder in Dermatology With a Focus on Body‐Dysmorphic Disorder
Authors: Eszter Szlávicz, Péter Osváth, Jutta Major, Tamás Bancsók, Zsuzsanna Lengyel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNarcissistic personality disorder has experienced intense attention in recent years, however, only scant reports illluminate this topic in consideration of skin and aesthetic care. Contrary to popular belief, the central element of narcissistic personality disorder is fragile self‐esteem, fear of shame and abandonment. Patients compensate by relying on external reinforcements and drawing attention to themselves, in which they demand the admiration of others. We often encounter cases in which narcissistic pathology is present, particularly, in the realm of aesthetic care. Narcissistic personality disorder can also be exacerbated by the influence of dysmorphophobia and other psychiatric disorders. Doctor‐patient relationships can prove to be particularly challenging, in which professionals possess vast responsibility in how they manage the performance of interventions. Moreover, the use of the term, narcissistic, requires caution: the topic of narcissism has become extremely popular in recent years, yet unprofessional approaches (e.g., in social media) hinders the recognition of serious personality disorders. In our article, a dermatologically focused examination of narcissistic personality disorder is presented, including general misconceptions and contradictions.

Introduction

Nowadays, the topic of narcissistic personality disorder (NPD) typically attracts attention, in which the adjective “narcissistic” is frequently used, to the point of overuse. However, the use of the term, in some cases, is incorrect, preventing the recognition of cases in which there is the distinct possibility of a personality disorder. Since narcissistic pathologies are closely related to the search for positive feedback and the expectation of admiration from others, achieving an attractive appearance can be particularly paramount for those affected [1]. Therefore, one expects to see more individuals suffering from NPD in dermatological and aesthetic clinics. However, relatively few publications confront this dilemma [2, 3, 4, 5].

In this article, we wish to present how narcissistic pathologies are linked to skin and/or aesthetic complaints. Our broader goal is to draw attention to the true nature of NPD and the most important misconceptions associated with it. We emphasize the premise that this type of personality disorder profoundly affects the individual's ability to maintain and nurture interpersonal relationships and characterizes a vulnerable population from the perspective of mental health. Recently, the rise of social media has also been linked to the more frequent appearance of narcissistic traits, since active posting is also a means of gaining admiration and feedback. The use of filters and image editing reflects the need for perfectionism among patients afflicted with NPD; it may set unattainably high standards and also harm reality testing [6, 7]. Additionally, the topic of narcissism itself has developed into a trend often depicted on social media platforms, in which there are countless posts referencing narcissism, resulting in a vast number of individuals eliciting arbitrary diagnoses [8]. Based on what has been formerly described, we want to emphasize that this is a sensitive topic, knowledge of which may also be crucial to health professionals employed in dermatological and aesthetic practices.

Narcissistic Traits and Narcissistic Personality Disorder

Havelock Ellis is the first reference associated with the myth of Narcissus in a psychological context, the Greek youth who falls in love with his own reflection. The term narcissism originates with Paul Adolf Nacke. However, these descriptions characterize narcissism in a completely different sense than when compared with contemporary history, often times sexually perverse. At one time, narcissism was used to designate a condition in which the object of a person's sexual desires was his own body [9, 10, 11]. In Three Essays on the Theory of Sexuality, Sigmund Freud, the founder of psychoanalysis, introduced the concept of “narcissistic libido”. This suggests psychic energy is directed towards the protection and maintenance of the ego. However, imbued with the understanding and study of personality disorders, the topic of narcissism gained a new meaning: strikingly, psychotherapy professionals witnessed an increase in the number of patients who described the feeling of emptiness and the problem of self‐determination as the primary object of their suffering [1, 12].

Personality disorders are characterized by specific, persistent deviations in behavior contrary to cultural norms. According to descriptive similarities, personality disorders are classified into three groups: cluster A (odd or eccentric disorders), cluster B (emotional or erratic disorders), and cluster C (anxious or fearful disorders) [13]. Cluster B contains borderline, histrionic, narcissistic, and antisocial personality disorders. Common features of these conditions include disturbances of social interactions in one's emotional life and a tendency to certain forms of proclivity resulting in self‐harm [4, 13]. Prior to NPD, borderline pathology received significant attention and is likely the most intensively studied among the personality disorders. The most important characteristics of borderline personality disorder are impulsivity and the resulting self‐harm, instability, chronic feelings of emptiness, and ambivalence (idealization and devaluation). Fear of abandonment and risk‐seeking behavior can also be observed, and in the case of borderline patients, we must also consider the risk of suicide. Less commonly, borderline personality disorder may have dermatological implications, including:

1. Self‐inflicted skin lesions.

2. More frequent body modification procedures.

3. Higher number of STD cases‐note risk‐seeking sexual behavior.

4. Skin symptoms associated with addictions (substance abuse can be a form of self‐harm in borderline pathologies) [2, 14, 15, 16].

In a study highlighting dermatological patients engaged in self‐mutilation, 37% were diagnosed with borderline personality disorder [2, 17]. The self‐harm associated with the disease is closely related to the disturbance of mentalization, which includes difficulties in understanding the emotional states and beliefs of others. Modern psychotherapy approaches aim to overcome the mentalization deficit in borderline patients (mentalization‐based therapy [MBT], developed by Peter Fonagy and Anthony Bateman) [15, 18, 19].

Narcissistic pathologies exhibit many similar features to borderline personality disorder, yet there are significant differences. The situation is complicated to the point that they can even appear as comorbidities [2]. Common characteristics include the appearance of a feeling of emptiness, fear of abandonment, idealization, and devaluation in interpersonal relationships. However, there are important differences in their attitudes towards the other individual: in contrast to the dramatism of borderline patients, narcissistic individuals rarely express emotions. In narcissistic pathologies, the patient expects admiration and care from the partner, yet at the same time objectifies the other individual and is unable to demonstrate empathy [13, 16, 20].

On the other hand, NPD forms a spectrum. The continuum extends from minor involvement of the personality structure to malignant narcissism [21, 22]. Distinctively, narcissistic traits are present during development, and healthy narcissism guarantees an individual's adequate self‐esteem, well‐functioning interpersonal relationships, and adequate problem‐solving skills [10, 23]. In the case of pathological narcissism, the problem manifests itself during early childhood, in which a child's emotions are not adequately reflected [1, 24]. Psychoanalytically‐oriented professionals Kernberg and Kohut have studied in depth the origins and manifestations of NPD. There were some differences in their perspectives; however, they both concur that if the parental attitude is not aligned with the child's current needs and emotional state, the child adopts behaviors which attract parental attention. All of these mechanisms contribute to the increased need for attention and special treatment among individuals afflicted with NPD [25, 26].

A common misconception regarding NPD is that it often characterizes egocentric individuals anointed with excessive self‐esteem [8, 27]. The essence of NPD is a fragile self‐esteem, innermost fear of shame, and difficulties in self‐determination, which lead to a craving for external feedback. On the other hand, inhibited, socially withdrawn individuals can also suffer from the disease, who secretly cherish fantasies of their own greatness [1, 28]. Based on the above, making a diagnosis can be challenging. Many experts are currently debating the issue of categorical and dimensional diagnosis of NPD. Regarding the categorical diagnosis, the DSM identifies nine criteria and describes the fulfillment of five of these as a cut‐off. However, some authors believe this is too narrow, and the dimensional approach is used to overcome this, which is suitable not only for defining grandiose but also for other NPD phenotypes. It also takes into account self‐agency, which includes an individual's motivations, decision‐making skills, and sense of control. The dimensional model considers four types of individual functioning (intimacy, empathy, identity, and self‐direction), which are organized into two dimensions (self and interpersonal relationships). Additionally, two personality traits are characteristically predominant: grandiosity and attention‐seeking. Both the Diagnostic and Statistic Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM‐5, Section III) and the International Classification of Diseases, Eleventh Revision (ICD‐11) recommend a hybrid diagnosis (a combination of categorical and dimensional approaches), since this is the ideal predictor regarding the presence of NDP [1].

Clinical research also concludes there are multiple types of narcissism. While the grandiose type is characterized by arrogance and a tendency for exploitation, the vulnerable type is inhibited yet has grandiose fantasies about himself and is critical of others. Moreover, Russ et al. identified three subtypes of NPD through the use of Q‐factor analysis, labeled grandiose/malignant, fragile, and high‐functioning/exhibitionistic [29, 30]. Although the high‐functioning exhibitionist type exaggerates their own importance, they can be successful and energetic professionals. In contrast, the grandiose/malignant type exhibits a lack of guilt, combat power struggles, and exhibits manipulative behaviors. However, some authors illustrate that not all cases can be classified into the categories described above, and trait‐based models of the personality disorder may clarify the various controversies [27, 31].

There have also been studies which have examined how the emergence and development of social media has adversely affected the prevalence of NPD [6, 12]. In a 2017 publication, Andreassen et al. reported higher levels of narcissism may underlie addictive use of social media [32]. Based on the work of Casale and Banchi, a connection exists between the grandiose type and problematic use of certain social media platforms; however, they did not identify as a consistent effect regarding the development of narcissistic pathologies [6]. Kristindottir et al. identified how sharing of self‐presented contents and the need for positive feedback contribute to the formation of communal narcissism (seeking admiration by portraying themselves as altruistic, caring, and morally superior) [33].

How Is Narcissism Encountered in the Realm of Dermatological and Aesthetic Care?

Since skin changes are visible to others, it is not surprising that skin diseases have a profound impact upon our self‐esteem. A common element of psychological problems related to dermatological diseases is the fear of shame and embarrassment, which can lead to socially avoidant behavior [34, 35, 36, 37]. Children with skin diseases often become victims of bullying [38, 39, 40]. It is not surprising to discover that skin patients are constantly preoccupied with their lesions, which are accompanied by stigmatization. Several studies have examined the impact of skin diseases on the quality of life and mental health. Gieler et al. reported the consequences of acne as having a lifelong impact, including a higher incidence of social isolation, depression, and suicide [41]. Problems with sexual relationships have been described in cases involving vitiligo, along with feelings of embarrassment and depression [42, 43]. The role of psychological factors has been particularly well studied in both psoriasis and atopic dermatitis [44, 45, 46]. Higher suicide rates have also been described in cases of psoriasis [47, 48].

Since individuals suffering from skin diseases often focus on themselves, in which the fear of shame is strong, the incidence of narcissistic injuries (criticism, rejection, or failure that threatens a person's self‐esteem and self‐image) is consequentially high in skin disorders [2, 34]. Individuals with inherent narcissistic traits may suffer particularly severe psychological damage. However, the dermatological aspects of NPD are most evident in relation to body‐dysmorphic disorder (BDD) [2, 16].

BDD belongs to the obsessive‐compulsive and related disorders as defined in DSM‐5. A common feature of this group of disorders is the appearance of obsessions (repetitive, intrusive thoughts) and compulsive actions. Performing the latter reduces tension and anxiety, and the feeling of relief can also have a kind of addictive aspect, which fuels and maintains pathological behaviors [49, 50, 51].

The description of dysmorphophobia dates back to the 19th century, documented in a case report written by Sigmund Freud. The patient, Sergei Pankejeff, had grown obsessed with a nose deformity [52]. The most important features of the disease are the overwhelming preoccupation with appearance, a sense of imperfection, or ugliness. The compulsions include constant checking of perceived physical deformities and imperfections, in which patients seek positive feedback from their environment. Concerns regarding beauty and attractiveness consume a large portion of patients' time, and if they resort to aesthetic procedures, financial burdens also arise. Individuals often go from one specialty to another, a phenomenon known as doctor shopping. Meanwhile, their social relationships are damaged due to the behavioral changes [49, 53, 54]. Additionally, the loss of quality in social relationships can contribute to major depressive disorder, in which the most serious complication is suicide. It should be emphasized the rate of suicidal ideations in BDD is particularly high, occurring in more than half of patients afflicted with BDD [53, 55].

Childhood and adolescence are considered to be a particularly sensitive period for the development of body image. BDD is therefore often linked to trauma experienced in early life, which can affect the entire personality structure [56]. Regarding comorbidites, affective disorders (major depression and bipolar disorder), anxiety, and eating disorders all merit discussion. Substance abuse can also accompany BDD [49, 53, 54]. It is not uncommon for BDD to have an underlying personality disorder. Phillips and McElroy describe the co‐occurrence of BDD and avoidant personality disorder as the most common disorder [57]. In addition to BDD, histrionic disorder and NPD are the most commonly documented disorders [2, 16, 58]. The risk of suicide is particularly high when BDD is associated with borderline personality disorder. Moreover, Semiz et al. reported, the prevalence of BDD was 54.3% in patients diagnosed with borderline personality disorder [59]. In another study, Szily et al. described how borderline patients exhibit disturbed body schema and perceptual body image [60].

Among personality disorders, narcissistic pathologies are considered to be the most important pathologies closely related to BDD. From a psychological perspective, the two disorders share a significant common denominator: high standards and a tendency towards perfectionism (Figure 1) [51, 57]. Dorian Gray syndrome, which can be considered a variant of BDD and is named after the main character in Oscar Wilde's novel, aptly exemplifies the connection between BDD and narcissism [61, 62].

Similarities and differences between narcissistic personality disorder and body dysmorphic disorder.

In consideration of cosmetic interventions, co‐occurrence of BDD is well known. However, it also warrants attention to cluster B personality disorders including NPD, which interestingly, is one of the most frequent disorders [2, 5, 16, 57, 63]. A study related to cosmetic botulinum toxin type A claimed narcissistic and histrionic personality traits are higher among patients undergoing Botox treatment [64]. While the prevalence of NPD in the general population is estimated at or about 6% with male predominance: men (7.7%); women (4.8%), the rate among those who undergo cosmetic treatments is estimated to be 25%. BDD is also common among these patients [2, 65]. Additionally, in consideration of aesthetic care, it also warrants examining the intertwining subjects referencing body image, narcissism, and the effects of social media. It was mentioned that the expansion of internet platforms has affected narcissism. Similar statements can be made regarding BDD: the use of filters and posts characterizing aesthetic interventions may induce psychological disorders in a self‐stimulating manner. The use of online platforms has become particularly widespread during the pandemic. New terms and expressions emerged regarding the use of webcams and filters, all of which exemplify this trend. Posts referencing aesthetic procedures can be motivated by both exhibitionism and the need for societal validation. Certain studies report that some individuals have undergone cosmetic procedures specifically to look better in selfies [6, 7, 51, 62]. However, the amount of reinforcement can never be enough for a patient with narcissistic personality disorder, nor for BDD patients, and their behavior can also lead to the involvement of other vulnerable individuals.

Doctor‐Patient Relationship and Therapy for Narcissistic Personality Disorder

In dermatological and aesthetic practice, it is of particular importance to recognize the signs of NPD [3, 16]. The grandiose type is relatively easier to identify: individuals have a sense of entitlement, they claim to be looking out for their best interests, and therefore, are typically demanding. Suspicious behavior manifests itself if and when the healthcare professional's feelings are consistently devalued and disrespected. Narcissistic patients may thus display manipulative and even emotionally abusive behavior towards their treating physician. It is especially challenging if the underlying personality disorder is later discovered in connection with the narcissist's initial charismatic and charming qualities. As noted earlier, individuals who appear withdrawn and anxious may also suffer from NPD, which too often can prove difficult to recognize [16, 66].

The problems outlined can be avoided based on the following elements of awareness: first, it is important to acquire a meticulously detailed anamnesis from the patient. Typically, physicians determine how often the patient has undergone aesthetic procedures in the past, including the presence of other body modifications [67, 68]. Physicians inquire why the patient elected to seek out a specialist while observing his/her attitude towards the previous doctor. If he/she speaks disparagingly regarding the previous specialist, it may be indicative that the individual is suffering from a narcissistic disorder. It should also raise suspicion if and when the patient characterizes his/her physician in an overtly flattering manner, excessively praising physicians and healthcare professionals. It is also important to clarify why the patient wants to seek help and to determine the nature of his/her disorder [16, 66].

Physicians and healthcare professionals endeavor to maintain objectivity and composure when taking medical history, since patients afflicted with personality disorders can often behave in a manipulative manner and stir negative feelings in those in their midst. The most important criterion for physicians and healthcare officials to remember is not to identify with the patient's devaluation and to always espouse professional principles and humanity (Table 1). However, it is paramount that physicians and healthcare professionals consistently represent and maintain their own boundaries in the doctor/patient relationship. Additionally, it may be important to have a witness present during the examination and to prepare an appropriate written consent form, as a strategic defense against the patient's potential legal action. The patient may be engaging in exploitative behavior, which may make a physician feel threatened. It is important to understand and acknowledge that in these situations, the patient is also feeling threatened, which can be traced back to unconscious fears related to the disintegration of the self. Despite potential difficulties, it can help to assure the patient through mutual respect. Depending on the case, physicians and healthcare professionals endeavor to direct their attention to the suffering lingering and brooding behind the complaints [16, 65, 66]. This can also facilitate involvement in psychotherapy. Physicians and healthcare professionals must bear in mind (similarly to BDD) that when undertaking unnecessary aesthetic interventions, the psychological problem may worsen [51, 55, 57]. In the case of BDD, questionnaires suitable for screening are now available (e.g., the dysmorphic concern questionnaire) [69]. As a vital element of the medical examination, physicians also have the opportunity to clarify co‐morbid psychiatric disorders [67, 69].

Today, NPD is still considered challenging to treat, and there is no uniform protocol regarding recommended forms of treatment. Narcissistic traits were reported to be associated with variations in prefrontal and insular brain structure. However, there is no pharmacological approach which is supported by evidence [70]. Certain psychotherapy methods usher in promise [70, 71, 72, 73]. Transference‐focused therapy is postulated to be the most effective form of treatment associated with NPD [1, 71]. Similar to borderline personality disorder, there is increased hope for MBT and also schema therapy. Mentalization, i.e., understanding the opinions, motivations, and feelings of others, can also be impaired in the case of narcissism [72, 73, 74]. However, the path to recovery requires a long therapeutic process and the intensive presence of the therapist.
