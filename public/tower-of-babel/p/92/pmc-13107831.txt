Effectiveness of stratified anatomical ablation (“BCXL”) for persistent atrial fibrillation: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Jie Yang, Yi Zhang, Xia Wang, Shizhu Bian, Yuanbin Song, Bo Zhang, Changsong Liu, Wenzhu Gu, Jianwei Zhao, Ping Qu, Mengjia Sun, Hu Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Background

Driven by advancements in catheter technology, catheter ablation has established itself as the mainstay of treatment for atrial fibrillation (AF) [1]. The 2024 European Society of Cardiology guidelines elevated the recommendation for ablating drug-refractory persistent AF to Class I (Level of Evidence A) [2]. Pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) serves as the standard procedural strategy [3]. However, a recent meta-analysis revealed that PVI alone maintains sinus rhythm (SR) in only 57% of patients with persistent AF at a mean follow-up of 2.7 years [4]. Consequently, adjunctive ablation strategies targeting AF triggers and substrates have been extensively investigated [5, 6]. Notably, adding linear or complex fractionated atrial electrograms (CFAE) ablation to PVI did not improve outcomes but increased the incidence of iatrogenic atrial tachycardia [7], raising concerns regarding the indiscriminate application of extensive ablation.

Critically, the pathophysiology and severity of persistent AF are heterogeneous. Tools such as the CAAP-AF score stratify patients into low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups for recurrence. Evidence suggests that PVI alone suffices for low-risk patients, whereas intermediate- and high-risk patients with advanced atrial substrates exhibit high recurrence rates with this approach [8]. Therefore, improving outcomes in intermediate- and high-risk cohorts is pivotal to enhancing overall procedural success.

To date, however, no tailored catheter ablation strategy has been established for these high-risk cohorts. Although the surgical Maze procedure achieves high success rates through extensive anatomical isolation, providing a conceptual template [9], translating these principles into linear catheter ablation strategies remains challenging [10].

Building on this rationale, we implemented a “stratified anatomical ablation” strategy for persistent AF in 2017. We stratified patients based on the CAAP-AF score combined with left atrial (LA) low-voltage zone (LVZ) assessment. Low-risk patients underwent PVI alone, whereas intermediate- to high-risk patients received anatomical linear ablation. This stratification strategy aims to ensure appropriate ablation, mitigating the recurrence risks of inadequate ablation while preventing the collateral damage associated with excessive ablation. The multilevel anatomical linear ablation employed in high-risk patients targets atrial anatomical segregation to maximize therapeutic benefit. This study describes this novel strategy and evaluates its effectiveness compared with that of traditional PVI.

Methods

Patients

We retrospectively assessed patients with persistent AF undergoing catheter ablation at Xinqiao Hospital, Army Medical University, between July 2017 and July 2021. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee and Institutional Review Board of Xinqiao Hospital. Informed consent forms signed by patients were included in every reviewed chart. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age 18–80 years; (2) diagnosis of persistent or long-term persistent AF; and (3) undergoing de novo AF catheter ablation. Persistent AF was defined as a sustained arrhythmic event lasting > 7 days but < 1 year, and long-term persistent AF as lasting > 1 year. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) prior surgical or catheter-based AF ablation; (2) LA diameter ≥ 60 mm (measured via parasternal long-axis view on two-dimensional echocardiography); (3) concomitant malignant tumors; and (4) pregnancy.

Clinical data, including demographics, perioperative measures, and follow-up outcomes, were retrospectively extracted from medical records. Patients were stratified into two cohorts based on the ablation strategy received: the stratified anatomical ablation (BCXL) group and the traditional PVI group.

Stratification utilized the CAAP-AF score [11], which evaluates six clinical factors (Supplementary Table 1). A CAAP-AF score > 5 indicated an intermediate-to-high risk of recurrence. Additionally, the presence of left atrial (LA) low-voltage zone (LVZ)—defined as voltage ≤ 0.5 mV during high-density mapping in sinus rhythm (SR)—indicated intermediate-to-high risk. Reflecting the institutional standard of care during the early study period, this cohort received a uniform strategy of traditional, small-circle PVI (IASA ratio ≤ 55%) regardless of individual recurrence risk. This cohort was treated under a prospectively applied, personalized protocol utilizing a two-step approach: (a) risk stratification and (b) stratified ablation. Low-risk patients received extensive encircling PVI (EEPVI; IASA ratio > 55%), whereas intermediate-to-high-risk patients received EEPVI plus personalized anatomical linear ablation. The primary outcome was the incidence of atrial tachyarrhythmia (AF, atrial flutter, or atrial tachycardia) lasting ≥ 30 s occurring after a three-month blanking period following the index procedure, independent of antiarrhythmic drug use.

Preoperative preparation and intra-procedural setting

Patients underwent anticoagulation with warfarin or direct oral anticoagulants for ≥ 3 weeks prior to catheter ablation. For patients receiving warfarin, the target international normalized ratio was maintained at 2.0–3.0; direct oral anticoagulants were discontinued on the day of the procedure. Antiarrhythmic drugs, excluding amiodarone, were discontinued for ≥ 5 half-lives before the procedure. Patients fasted for ≥ 8 h preoperatively. Procedures were performed under conscious sedation and analgesia using a continuous intravenous infusion of dexmedetomidine and remifentanil. A 6-Fr decapolar diagnostic catheter was advanced into the coronary sinus via the femoral vein. Following double transseptal puncture, a multielectrode mapping catheter (PentaRay, Biosense Webster) was introduced for electroanatomic mapping. An irrigated contact-force ablation catheter (THERMOCOOL SMARTTOUCH® or SMARTTOUCH® SF, Biosense Webster) was subsequently positioned. Procedures utilized a three-dimensional electroanatomic mapping system (CARTO 3, Biosense Webster). Radiofrequency ablation settings included a power limit of 40–45 W and a temperature limit of 40 °C. Energy delivery for all ablation lines was guided by the ablation index. The ablation index serves as an objective, quantifiable endpoint integrating contact force, power, and duration. Target ablation index values were as follows: 500–550 for the anterior wall of the pulmonary veins and LA roof; 350–400 for the posterior wall; 550–600 for the mitral isthmus; 380–400 for the inferior line; and 400–450 for the septal line and superior vena cava. Ablation aimed to achieve contiguous, transmural lesions. Interlesion distance was maintained at ≤ 3 mm to ensure continuity. To quantify the extent of ablation, the isolated antral surface area (IASA) was calculated using the 3D mapping system. Specifically, the isolated antral surface areas (encircled by the PV ablation lines) were manually delineated on the LA geometry during SR. The system calculated the sum of these areas to derive the total IASA. Subsequently, the LA posterior wall area—bounded superiorly by the roof line, inferiorly by the posterior mitral annulus, and laterally by the PV isolation lines—was quantified.

PVI ablation

Patients in the PVI group underwent traditional PVI, defined as an IASA ratio (IASA divided by the combined area of the IASA and LA posterior wall) of ≤ 55%. Activation mapping and ablation were performed if AF organized into atrial flutter or atrial tachycardia. Cavotricuspid isthmus (CTI) ablation was performed if (1) the right atrial (RA) long-axis dimension was ≥ 45 mm; (2) preoperative electrocardiogram suggested CTI-dependent atrial flutter,; or (3) intraprocedural mapping confirmed CTI dependence. If AF persisted, synchronized direct-current cardioversion was utilized. Upon restoration of SR, bidirectional block of the PVs and CTI (if ablated) was confirmed.

BCXL ablation

“Big Circle” (BC) ablation refers to a modified extensive encircling PVI (EEPVI) technique. It is characterized by an IASA ratio > 55% (Fig. 1A). The objective is continuous bilateral electrical isolation. All patients in the BCXL group underwent EEPVI. The component “XL” denotes adjunctive anatomical linear ablation performed post-EEPVI. We used the symbol “X” to represent the variable location and number of ablation lines. For patients with a CAAP-AF score of 6–8, roof and mitral isthmus lines were created following EEPVI (Fig. 1B) [12]. Similarly, for patients with a CAAP-AF score > 8, anterior and posterior wall lines were added to the roof and mitral isthmus ablations (Fig. 1C). Subsequently, LA substrate mapping was conducted in SR (post-cardioversion). If an LVZ was detected in the anterior or posterior wall, the corresponding ablation line was added. In rare cases where the CAAP-AF score was ≤ 5, substrate mapping was performed. If an LVZ was detected, the strategy was adapted: anterior wall LVZs prompted anterior and roof lines; posterior wall LVZs prompted roof, mitral isthmus, and posterior lines; and LVZs in the lateral mitral isthmus or roof prompted roof and mitral isthmus lines. In both cohorts, the occurrence of atrial tachycardia or atrial flutter prompted targeted ablation (Fig. 1D); however, these focal ablations were not classified as “X” lines. The procedural endpoint for roof and mitral isthmus lines was confirmed bidirectional block; block validation was not routinely performed for anterior and posterior wall lines.

Postoperative medication and follow-up

Following the procedure, oral anticoagulants were continued for three months. Long-term anticoagulation decisions were based on the CHA2DS2-VASc score reassessed at three months. Lifelong therapy was recommended for males with a score ≥ 2 and females with a score ≥ 3; otherwise, discontinuation was considered. Follow-up visits or telephone consultations occurred at 3, 6, 12, and 24 months post-procedure. Assessments included symptom review and routine or 24 h ambulatory electrocardiography (Holter monitoring). Recurrences were managed with antiarrhythmic drugs or redo ablation, based on patient preference.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (range), as appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. Arrhythmia-free survival was estimated using Kaplan-Meier analysis. Group comparisons utilized Student’s t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables, and Pearson’s chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Logistic regression assessed predictors of single- and multiple-procedure success. Variable selection utilized backward elimination. Multivariable models included variables based on clinical relevance, univariate significance (p < 0.2), multicollinearity checks, and principal component analysis. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To minimize confounding, optimal propensity score matching balanced baseline characteristics between the BCXL and PVI groups. Variables with a standardized mean difference (SMD) > 0.1 after matching underwent further multivariable adjustment. The propensity score matching model utilized an optimal matching algorithm incorporating the following covariates: CAAP-AF score, sex, age, atrial dimensions, body mass index (BMI), ejection fraction, CHA2DS2-VASc score, and comorbidities. Analyses were performed using SPSS version 20 (SPSS Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Statistical support was provided by a contract research organization (Appletree CI Group, Switzerland).

Results

Baseline characteristics

Between July 2017 and July 2021, 300 patients with persistent AF underwent ablation in our hospital. Ultimately, 244 patients met the inclusion criteria (Fig. 2): 159 in the BCXL group and 85 in the traditional PVI group. Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The cohort was predominantly men, with a mean age of 59.14 ± 11.25 years. The groups did not differ significantly regarding sex, age, or BMI. AF duration was categorized into four intervals: < 1, 1–3, 3–5, and ≥ 5 years; the distribution was similar between groups. Regarding cardiac dimensions, mean RA and right ventricular (RV) diameters were significantly larger in the BCXL group than in the PVI group (RA: 41.08 ± 5.00 mm vs. 39.13 ± 6.42 mm, p = 0.009; RV: 33.02 ± 7.03 mm vs. 30.74 ± 6.46 mm, p = 0.014). Other cardiac dimensions showed no significant differences in other diameters. The CHA2DS2-VASc score and the prevalence of left ventricular dysfunction were comparable between groups. Comorbidity rates, including coronary heart disease, hypertension, stroke/Transient Ischemic Attack (TIA) /thromboembolism, prior cardiovascular events, and hyperthyroidism, were similar. However, the prevalence of structural heart disease was significantly higher in the BCXL group (18.2% vs. 7.1%, p = 0.018). Conversely, preoperative amiodarone use was significantly more frequent in the PVI group (22.4% vs. 7.5%, p = 0.001) (Table 1).

Stratified outcome and ablation strategy

The BCXL group comprised 56 low-risk patients (35.2%) and 103 intermediate- to high-risk patients (64.8%). Ordered logistic regression identified CAAP-AF score, LA size, AF duration, and LA LVZ as independent predictors of AF risk (Table 2). Among intermediate- to high-risk patients, ablation lines included roof (98.1%), mitral isthmus (94.2%), CTI (56.3%), anterior wall (32.0%), and posterior wall (17.5%). Regarding the number of adjunctive lines in this cohort, 29.1% received two lines, 45.0% received three, 19.4% received four, and 4.9% received five.

Therapeutic outcomes

The overall success rate was significantly higher in the stratified ablation group than the traditional ablation group (80.4% [123/153] vs. 63.8% [51/80], p = 0.006; Fig. 3). After adjusting for confounders (CAAP-AF score, LA LVZ, age, sex, and BMI), stratified anatomical ablation remained an independent predictor of success (OR: 3.513; 95% CI: 1.725–7.154; p = 0.001; Table 4 and Supplementary Table 2).

To address potential confounding from temporal trends in operator experience, an additional multivariable logistic regression included ‘Procedure Year’ as a covariate (Supplementary Table 3). Procedure year was not a significant predictor (OR: 1.118; 95% CI: 0.853–1.465; p = 0.419), whereas the BCXL strategy remained independently associated with improved outcomes (OR: 3.317; 95% CI: 1.608–6.840; p = 0.001). Furthermore, sensitivity analysis of 12- to 24-month follow-up data confirmed the robustness of these results, showing that the BCXL strategy maintained significantly higher arrhythmia-free survival than PVI (Log-rank p = 0.007; see Supplementary Fig. 1). However, analysis restricted to the 0–12 month period showed no significant difference in survival (Log-rank p = 0.728; see Supplementary Fig. 2).

Subgroup analysis showed a significant difference in success rates between BC and PVI groups among low-risk patients (87.0% vs. 69.7%, p = 0.048). Similarly, intermediate- to high-risk patients in the BCXL group achieved a significantly higher success rate than those in the PVI group (76.8% vs. 59.6%, p = 0.032). These findings indicate superior therapeutic efficacy in both low-risk and intermediate- to high-risk cohorts.

Propensity score matching and sensitivity analysis

To mitigate baseline imbalances, we performed propensity score matching. Optimal matching created 80 matched pairs; standardized mean differences (SMD) for most covariates fell below 0.1, indicating balanced baseline characteristics (Table 5). In this balanced cohort, the ablation success rate remained significantly higher in the BCXL group (81.3% vs. 63.8%, p = 0.021). To address residual confounding, multivariable logistic regression adjusted for variables with post-match SMD > 0.1. After adjusting for amiodarone use and LA diameter, the BCXL strategy remained an independent predictor of success (OR: 2.43; 95% CI: 1.16–5.25; p = 0.020; Table 6).

Discussion

This study is the first to implement a risk-based stratification strategy for persistent AF, tailoring ablation protocols to specific risk profiles. Our results demonstrate that this personalized strategy significantly improves clinical outcomes, achieving an overall success rate of 80.4%, which exceeds rates reported in the literature [13, 14]. We observed that the disparity in ablation success rates between intermediate-to-high-risk and low-risk subgroups is a key driver of the overall difference in outcomes between the BCXL and PVI groups. Although extensive ablation beyond PVI appears associated with improved outcomes, the specific utility of risk stratification in guiding these strategies warrants further validation. Moreover, stratified anatomical ablation emerged as an independent predictor of success. Sensitivity analyses further confirmed the robustness of our primary findings. The survival advantage of the BCXL strategy persisted after propensity score matching and multivariable adjustment, suggesting the benefit derives from the technique rather than patient selection bias.

Inconsistent outcomes in studies investigating additional ablation for persistent AF may stem from the lack of patient stratification. In low-risk patients, excessive ablation may exacerbate myocardial injury, provoke iatrogenic atrial tachycardia, and compromise efficacy. Therefore, identifying high-risk patients prone to recurrence is crucial when designing ablation strategies. We utilized the CAAP-AF score as the primary stratification tool. This selection relied on previous findings confirming the utility of the CAAP-AF score as a simple preoperative predictor of arrhythmia recurrence [11]. The CAAP-AF score classifies patients into low-, intermediate-, and high-risk categories, corresponding to different stages (early, intermediate, and late) of AF progression. The natural history of AF involves this progression, characterized by continuous deterioration of the atrial substrate [15]. Higher scores correlate with advanced substrate remodeling, indicating severe atrial fibrosis and lower treatment success rates. Thus, the CAAP-AF score serves as a surrogate for atrial substrate status. However, this scoring system has limitations. Intraoperative mapping may reveal LVZs even in patients with low scores, potentially attributable to atrial myopathy or other factors. Therefore, we refined stratification accuracy by combining the preoperative CAAP-AF score with intraoperative substrate assessment (LVZ mapping).

AF mechanisms comprise initiation and maintenance components. In early-stage AF, initiation is primarily driven by pulmonary vein activity [16], whereas maintenance mechanisms remain weak; consequently, PVI yields favorable outcomes. However, in advanced stages, maintenance mechanisms predominate, manifesting as progressive electrical and structural atrial remodeling. Consequently, recurrence rates after PVI alone are high. Delayed-enhancement magnetic resonance imaging studies classifying LA fibrosis have identified Grade IV fibrosis as a predictor of the highest recurrence rates [17]. Therefore, intraprocedural substrate mapping is critical for detecting LVZs, which serve as surrogates for fibrosis. Fibrosis uncouples cardiomyocytes, reducing conduction velocity and promoting anisotropy and slow conduction. The STABLE-SR study demonstrated higher post-ablation recurrence rates in patients with atrial LVZs than those with normal substrates [18]. Similarly, the DECAAF trial found that AF ablation outcomes depend on the degree of atrial fibrosis rather than the specific ablation strategy [19].

Numerous ablation strategies targeting atrial substrate remodeling have been investigated [20]. Natale et al. identified optimal power settings (high-power short-duration; 50 W/10–15 s) for the inferior posterior wall, suggesting that targeting this region enhances procedural success [21]. In addition to PVI, we performed adjunctive ablation in intermediate- to high-risk patients with advanced atrial remodeling. We utilized anatomical linear ablation to segmenting the atrial area, disrupting AF maintenance mechanisms in a manner mimicking the surgical Maze procedure. Furthermore, these lines may intersect other potential mechanisms along the pathway, including drivers, fibrosis, and ganglia [22]. Mitral isthmus and LA roof lines are pivotal [23, 24], effectively isolating the posterior wall from the anterior LA and reducing the critical mass of fibrillation-susceptible tissue. Additionally, evidence implicates the ligament of Marshall and the mitral isthmus ridge in macro-reentry circuits [25], supporting the role of the mitral isthmus line in preventing reentry.

For LVZs detected during mapping, our linear ablation strategy aimed to partition the atrial tissue into discrete segments rather than focally isolate abnormal potentials. Specifically, anterior wall LVZs prompted anterior wall lines, whereas posterior wall LVZs prompted modified posterior wall lines. Extensive research on posterior wall isolation [26] highlights that the posterior wall shares embryological origins with the pulmonary veins [27]. Fibrosis in the posterior wall induces significant conduction abnormalities [28] and remodeling [29, 30], facilitating AF maintenance. We employed a modified posterior wall line to enhance precision and minimize the risk of esophageal injury.

Notably, a time-restricted analysis of the first-year follow-up revealed no significant difference in success rates between strategies. This highlights that the principal advantage of the stratified BCXL strategy lies not in preventing early recurrence, but in securing long-term rhythm stability. The emergence of benefit after 12 months aligns with the progressive nature of atrial remodeling. This suggests that the extensive, anatomy-based lesion set of the BCXL strategy protects against the attrition of success observed with PVI alone, likely by ensuring comprehensive and durable modification of the arrhythmogenic substrate.

This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective, non-randomized study conducted at a single high-volume center, selection bias related to the use of historical data is possible. Furthermore, although propensity score matching and multivariable regression adjusted for measured confounders, these methods cannot account for unmeasured or unknown variables. Therefore, the observed association between the stratified ablation strategy and improved outcomes should be interpreted with caution; despite their robustness, these findings do not establish causality. We are currently conducting a prospective randomized clinical trial (ChiCTR2200067081) to validate the efficacy of this strategy. Second, relying on intermittent 24-hour Holter monitoring may have overestimated success rates in both groups. Future studies employing continuous or implantable monitoring are warranted to verify these findings.

Conclusions

Combining preoperative CAAP-AF scoring with intraoperative LVZ identification refines risk stratification in patients with persistent AF. Based on this stratification, PVI and adjunctive ablations can be applied in a tailored manner to minimize unnecessary collateral injury. Anatomical linear ablation creates barriers within atrial tissue, reducing the critical mass for fibrillation and targeting initiation and maintenance mechanisms. This study underscores the progressive nature of persistent AF. Furthermore, early implementation of rhythm control may interrupt the pathogenic progression of AF and optimize patient outcomes.
