Empirical study on the relationship between economic growth and implied carbon emissions in Western China’s undertaking of international industrial transfer
Authors: Yanhong Wang, Lin Wang, Ying Wang, Yibing Wu, Menglian Li, Xiong Wang, Weiran Zhou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the macro context of global climate change, given the profound challenges that the intensifying greenhouse effect poses to global ecological balance and sustainable economic and social development, the role of foreign direct investment (FDI) is increasingly acknowledged, particularly regarding its dual impact on the environment: while serving as a catalyst for economic growth, it might also exacerbate carbon emissions in host countries. This paper focuses on the panel data of 11 provinces and cities in the western region (excluding Xizang) from 2006 to 2021, calculates the implied carbon of foreign-invested enterprises in the western region, and explores the relationship between the implied carbon of foreign-invested enterprises in the western region and the economic development of the western region. The results indicate that from 2006 to 2021, the implied net carbon value of import and export trade of foreign-invested enterprises in the western region was greater than zero and roughly exhibited an upward trend year by year. FDI has an environmental deficit in terms of carbon emissions in the western region; FDI in the western region has a significant positive effect on the implied carbon emissions in import and export trade; The GDP of the western region has a significant positive effect on the implied carbon emissions of import and export trade. Therefore, this article puts forward corresponding suggestions from five aspects: policy incentives and regulatory constraints, technology research and innovation drive, clean energy development and resource recycling, international cooperation and regional linkage, and public awareness enhancement and cultural advocacy.Keywords: International industrial transfer, Foreign direct investment, Implied carbon, Economic growthSubject terms: Climate sciences, Ecology, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Industrial transfer refers to an economic process through which industries transfer from one region to another due to changes in product demand or resource supply. Economic development in the western region is relatively lagging; thus, rapid economic growth can be achieved through the transfer of both domestic and foreign industries. Industrial transfer can facilitate the upgrading of the industrial structure in the western region. On the one hand, by undertaking overseas industrial transfer, the western region can implant some industries that the western region itself does not have or is scarce, and extend the industrial chain; On the other hand, the transfer of advanced industries in the western region promotes coordinated regional development and maintains the security and stability of the industrial chain, which is of great significance for building a modern industrial system1–3; Furthermore, industrial transfer can enhance the agglomeration effect in the western region. The development of industrial agglomeration in the western region not only significantly reduces costs but also facilitates the clustering of related supporting enterprises on a larger scale. Through industrial transfer, we can enhance industrial innovation and transform the labor force’s employment structure in the region. Innovation and talent are key drivers, and digital transformation is an important factor1–4.The country and various provinces and cities in the western region attach great importance to the issue of undertaking industrial transfer. Such as the Guiding Opinions of the State Council on Undertaking Industrial Transfer in the Central and Western Regions (Guofa [2010] No. 28). In 2018, the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology revised and released the “Guidelines for Industrial Development and Transfer (2018 Edition)”, which made undertaking industrial transfer a key component of implementing the Western Development Strategy. In 2023, at the opening ceremony of the third “the Belt and Road” International Cooperation Summit Forum, China announced that it would completely eliminate restrictions on foreign investment access in manufacturing. The degree of openness to foreign investment industry access has deepened. However, there is a certain contradiction between undertaking industrial transfer and reducing pollution and CO2 emissions5,6. On the one hand, the greater the contribution of industrial undertaking to economic growth, the greater the total amount of carbon emissions transferred, and the more difficult the task of emission reduction; On the other hand, in international industrial transfer, developed countries are more inclined to transfer their high energy consuming, high pollution, and high emission industries; The process of international industrial transfer is often also a process of pollution transfer and carbon emission transfer, with a large number of “hidden carbon emissions” and “transferred carbon emissions” phenomena7. Studying the mechanisms for energy conservation, emission reduction, and low-carbon development in China’s western region, particularly in the context of international industries, is essential for implementing the ‘14th Five-Year Plan’ for comprehensive energy conservation and emission reduction efforts. It also reflects China’s commitment to achieving peak CO2 emissions by 2030 and carbon neutrality by 2060. It is also necessary for the western region to vigorously promote the establishment of a modern industrial system and to accelerate the development of high-quality productivity.

Literature review

Entering the 21st century, foreign direct investment (FDI) has become the predominan form of international industrial transfer, bringing economic benefits and becoming a driving force for economic growth during international industrial transfer. However, FDI also has a certain degree of impact on the local environment8–10.The views of domestic and foreign scholars on the correlation between international industrial transfer based on FDI and environmental pollution can be roughly divided into the following aspects: (1) There are positive effects of undertaking international industrial transfer on the environment. FDI provides developing countries with advanced technological support and management experience, enabling them to achieve clean and green production, thereby improving environmental quality and regional sustainable development capabilities11,12. For instance, research by Song Deyong and Ji Shidong indicates that FDI inflows can alleviate the pressure on China’s CO2 emissions, and they attribute this outcome to technological spillovers from FDI13,14.HAYAT K.(2022) and other researchers found that FDI has reduced the carbon emissions of countries along the “the Belt and Road”15.Zaman et al. (2021) discovered that FDI can promote the sustainable economic development of the Belt and Road countries, and the degree of regional integration has played a positive role in regulating the impact of FDI on the sustainable economic development of host countries16. (2) International industrial transfer can be detrimental to the host country’s environment, manifesting as negative effects. Most studies suggest that although FDI can stimulate economic growth, it can also lead to more industrial pollution and environmental degradation. Polluting industries transferred from underdeveloped areas become “pollution havens” for multinational corporations, while economically underdeveloped areas tend to reduce the strength of environmental regulations and attract FDI at the cost of sacrificing the environment17–21.For example, Ji Zhiying’s (2015) research found that foreign direct investment and China’s environmental pollution conform to the environmental Kuznets curve. Environmental regulations have no promoting effect on pollution emissions control, and local governments lack enthusiasm and foresight in controlling environmental pollution22. Zhang Yun et al. (2023) found that FDI has a significant carbon emission transfer effect, and its impact is more significant in low - and middle-income countries; Foreign direct investment can transfer carbon emissions to host countries by transferring polluting industries and changing energy structures23. (3) The environmental impact of FDI on host countries is complex, with both positive and negative effects. For instance, Grossman and Krueger (1991) proposed that foreign direct investment increases the ecological pressure on host countries while promoting economic development. On the other hand, with the improvement of the economy and the demand for environmental quality, it will increase environmental protection investment and improve environmental quality24; Zhang Yanbo and Guo Yajun (2009) believe that the increase in the stock of FDI in China has led to the expansion of economic scale and the aggravation of pollution emissions caused by the heavy pollution of economic structure, and the technology transfer it induces has brought positive environmental effects25. Li Yueming et al. 2023 found that the inflow of FDI within a certain scale in China as a whole exhibits a “pollution halo” effect, which reduces carbon emissions. When it reaches the turning point, it will show the opposite effect of increasing emissions26. Jiang Xinying et al. 2019 found that FDI only plays a role in carbon reduction in Beijing, Tianjin, and Shanghai, while hindering carbon reduction in other provinces27. (4) The impact of FDI on carbon emissions in host countries. There are currently two main views on the relationship between FDI and carbon emissions. One perspective is that FDI is concentrated in high energy consuming industries, and many multinational corporations are transferring high polluting and high emission industries to developing countries with relatively loose environmental regulations, which increases the environmental pressure on host countries, especially in economically underdeveloped countries where FDI is an important factor leading to an increase in carbon emissions28,29, Especially in countries with concentrated FDI, they tend to pursue economic development and ignore the accumulation of carbon emissions brought about by FDI30,31; Another perspective is that FDI inflows can reduce the carbon emission intensity in host countries or may have no significant impact.For instance, Blackman et al. 1999 analyzed U.S. investments in Chinese thermal enterprises and determined that FDI likely enhances energy efficiency, thereby contributing to carbon emission reductions32; Perkins et al. 2009 analyzed the relationship between FDI and carbon emission efficiency in 98 underdeveloped countries and found that FDI is beneficial for improving the carbon emission efficiency of host countries33; Tian Zuhai et al. (2023) found that FDI indirectly promotes the enhancement of carbon productivity by increasing the intensity of independent research34, Blanco et al. 2013 applied the Granger causality test to assess the relationship between FDI and CO2 emissions in Latin America from 1980 to 2007, finding no compelling evidence that FDI leads to increased CO2 emissions35.

Building on this, the article utilizes panel data from 11 provinces and cities in the western region spanning 2006 to 2021. It systematically examines the relationship between FDI, economic growth, and the carbon emissions of foreign-invested enterprises. The aim is to offer theoretical support and practical guidance for attracting foreign investment, transforming growth patterns, and fostering environmental coordination and unity in the region’s economic development.

Currently, there are various methods to measure carbon emissions from the production of a country’s exported products, with the majority employing input-output models36. As shown in Tables 1 and 3, from 2006 to 2021, the implied carbon emissions from the export trade of foreign-invested enterprises in the western region significantly exceeded the implied carbon emissions from import trade. The net value of the implied carbon emissions from import and export trade (referred to as JC) was greater than zero, and the total emissions showed an overall upward trend. (1) Steady growth period (2006–2010): The economy of the western region continued to grow rapidly, attracting a large amount of foreign investment, particularly in the manufacturing and heavy industry sectors. Foreign invested enterprises may have expanded their production scale to meet the demands of domestic and foreign markets, leading to increased energy consumption and carbon emissions. (2) Significant leap period (2011): JC surged to 13.0868 million tons, with a growth rate of 336.590% compared to 2010. Following the global financial crisis, the Chinese government and relevant departments in the western region implemented large-scale economic stimulus plans, leading to the expansion of foreign-invested enterprises and increased production. (3) Volatile growth period (2012–2014): JC continues to grow, but the growth rate slowed down. During this period, foreign-invested enterprises further expanded their production technology and scale, and the western region continued to provide preferential policies to attract foreign investment. (4) Short decline period (2015–2016): JC decline. The Chinese government has strengthened the enforcement of environmental regulations, and foreign-invested enterprises may face higher environmental standards and emission reduction requirements. The transformation of the economy in the western region from heavy industry to a service-oriented and consumption oriented economy may have affected the carbon emissions of foreign-invested enterprises. (5) Continuous growth period (2017–2021): JC fluctuated at a high level and remained relatively high overall, reaching a new high in 2021. The economic development in the western region has entered a new normal, with a slowdown in growth rate, leading to adjustments in production and business strategies by foreign-invested enterprises, resulting in a slowdown in output growth. The continuous implementation of environmental policies and the establishment of carbon emission trading markets may promote the application of emission reduction technologies and the improvement of energy efficiency. After the COVID-19 epidemic, China’s economy recovered rapidly, and the production activities of foreign-invested enterprises increased rapidly. Due to disturbances in the global supply chain, as a stable production base, foreign-invested enterprises in the western region may face more export orders and a surge in exports.

The western region is a net exporter of trade-implied carbon emissions. In addition to reducing a certain amount of CO2 emissions through import trade, the carbon footprint of these exported goods in the western region remains in the local area when participating in import and export trade activities. Behind the trade surplus, there is a clear ecological environment deficit in the western region, and the impact of international trade on the implied carbon emissions in the western region is negative, exerting enormous pressure to the local ecological environment.

Hausman test

The Hausman test can be used in panel models to choose between using a random effects model or a fixed effects model. The fixed effects model assumes that the individual influence in the model is fixed, while the random effects model assumes that the individual influence is random. The null hypothesis of the Hausman test is that individual effects are not correlated with explanatory variables. Zhu Changning et al. (2024)39 conducted a Hausman test on the model and found a P-value of 0.0000, which is less than 0.05. Therefore, a fixed effects model was adopted. If the p-value is less than 0.05, the null hypothesis is rejected and a fixed effects model should be chosen; If the p-value of the Hausman test is greater than 0.05, the null hypothesis is accepted. Suggestions for future recommendations

Policy incentives and regulatory constraints

The western region should formulate a series of practical and feasible low-carbon policies, including tax incentives, fiscal subsidies, financial support and other incentive measures for industrial low-carbon development, as well as restrictive policies such as environmental standards and regulatory requirements. Establish an assessment index system that encompasses carbon emission intensity, energy utilization efficiency, green technology innovation, and other relevant metrics, regularly evaluate the industry’s progress in low-carbon transformation, and ensure the achievement of low-carbon objectives.

Technology research and innovation drive

The relevant departments in the western region should increase financial support for low-carbon technologies, encourage the research and innovation of low-carbon technologies, and actively introduce cutting-edge international low-carbon technologies and equipment to enhance the low-carbon level of local industries. Construct a platform for low-carbon technology innovation and transformation that integrates industry, academia, and research with application. This platform should facilitate cooperation and exchange among universities, research institutions, and enterprises, and encourage the innovation and application of low-carbon technologies.

Clean energy development and resource recycling

Efforts should be directed towards developing clean energy sources, such as solar and wind energy, to reduce dependence on fossil fuels and consequently lower carbon emissions. Establish a comprehensive resource recycling system to enhance the utilization of industrial waste and by-products, thereby reducing resource consumption and environmental pollution.

International cooperation and regional linkage

Establish partnerships with international low-carbon organizations and research institutions to jointly promote research into and the dissemination of low-carbon technologies, and to share experiences and achievements in the field of low-carbon development. Furthermore, strengthen cooperation among provinces in the western region regarding low-carbon development, jointly formulate low-carbon development strategies, and foster regional coordinated development.

Public awareness enhancement and cultural advocacy

Utilize various media and platforms, including educational institutions and community activities, to widely disseminate the concept of low-carbon living and enhance public environmental awareness.Advocate for a green lifestyle, such as choosing public transportation, conserving water and electricity, and encourage the public to integrate low-carbon practices into their daily lives.
