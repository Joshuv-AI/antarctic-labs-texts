CCDC78 is required for prostate cancer cell growth and serves as a prognostic biomarker for high-risk disease
Authors: Liangzhen Zhu, Wenken Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs the second most common malignancy among men worldwide, prostate cancer is commonly diagnosed through PSA testing and puncture biopsy. However, these methods have limitations in risk assessment. Clinically, low-risk patients typically require only active monitoring rather than immediate intervention, whereas high-risk patients need timely treatment. Therefore, more precise biomarkers are needed to identify prostate cancer at different risk levels.MethodsDifferentially expressed genes (DEGs) between high-risk and low-risk prostate cancer samples were identified using RNA-seq. Introduction

Prostate cancer ranks as the second most frequent malignancy globally and was the fifth leading cause of cancer-related mortality among men in 2022 [1]. There were 1.5 million newly diagnosed cases and 397,000 deaths worldwide due to this disease [1]. Most prostate tumors tend to grow slowly and patients with low-grade tumors have relatively low risk and limited aggressiveness. However, a small percentage (25–30%) represents a high-risk subtype (Gleason score 8–10) that is inclined to metastatic progression [2]. Despite recent advances, prostate cancer remains a significant medical problem affecting men, with overtreatment of the inherently benign disease and undertreatment of a high-risk subtype for prostate cancer. Therefore, it is very important to distinguish between low-risk and high-risk cases of prostate cancer.

At present, the clinical diagnosis and prognosis of prostate cancer largely rely on digital rectal examination, serum prostate-specific antigen (PSA) levels, and the Gleason score from biopsy pathology [3]. Initially approved by the FDA in 1986, PSA testing is widely used for early prostate cancer screening; however, it remains controversial due to its high false positive rate and the associated risks of biopsies and overtreatment [4]. Generally, there is a near-linear association between PSA levels at initial diagnosis and outcomes for patients, i.e. the higher the PSA level, the worse the prognosis [5, 6]. However, some patients with high-grade disease (Gleason score 8–10) but low PSA expression (≤ 2.5 ng/L) may have a particularly poor prognosis [5]. Moreover, PSA as a non-specific marker is also commonly expressed in the adrenal glands, small intestine, kidneys, and salivary tissues [7]. Therefore, more accurate biomarkers are still needed to identify high-risk prostate cancer beyond PSA testing and the pathological Gleason score from biopsy.

In this study, using RNA-seq, we identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between high-risk (Gleason score 9, GS9) and low-risk (Gleason score 6, GS6) prostate cancer. Based on TCGA, Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) was performed to cluster closely related genes into the same module, thereby discovering the association between gene networks and phenotypes of high-risk (GS9) and low-risk (GS6) prostate cancer. Moreover, we identified DEGs between normal and prostate cancer tissues in TCGA-PRAD. Subsequently, we conducted a series of bioinformatics analyses on the intersecting genes from the above three gene sets, including lasso regression analysis, multivariate Cox regression analysis, as well as survival prognosis analysis. Finally, we identified that CCDC78 could serve as a prognostic biomarker for high-risk prostate cancer.

Materials and methods

Clinical tissue samples

All clinical tissue samples from patients with prostate cancer were collected from Nanxishan Hospital of Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region. The study was approved by the ethics committee of the same hospital on February 20, 2024 (ethics approval No. NXSYY-2024-171-01). Written informed consent was obtained from every participant. Following radical prostatectomy, a small portion of each tissue sample was stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to determine the pathological stage, while the remaining tissue was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for 10 min and subsequently stored at − 80 °C.

Total RNA extraction

Total RNA was extracted from cells and clinical tissue samples of prostate cancer using the TRIzol extraction method [8]. RNA quality was assessed by spectrophotometry and agarose gel electrophoresis, after which the samples were used for subsequent experiments.

Real-time PCR analysis

First, cDNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription cDNA synthesis kit (Invitrogen). Subsequently, quantitative real-time PCR was performed on a ViiA 7 Real-time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) using SYBR Green master mix. GAPDH was used as an internal control for normalization. Relative mRNA expression levels were determined using the comparative threshold cycle (Ct) method.

The primer sequences utilized were as follows:

CCDC78.

Forward: 5ʹ-CAAGGAGCTGGTCGACATT-3ʹ,

Reverse: 5ʹ-AGCCGAAGGATCTCACTCT-3ʹ;

GAPDH.

Forward: 5ʹ-ACACCCACTCCTCCACCTTTG-3ʹ,

Reverse: 5ʹ-TCCACCACCCTGTTGCTGTAG-3ʹ.

RNA-seq data

RNA extracted from three GS9 and three GS6 prostate cancer tissues was enriched using Oligo(dT) beads. The mRNA molecules were then fragmented into fragments of 200–300 bp, followed by reverse transcription to generate cDNA. The cDNA was subjected to end repair, A-tailing, and adapter ligation. After these modifications, the cDNA library was constructed by PCR amplification. Finally, mRNA transcriptome sequencing was performed on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform.

Differential expression analysis

In this study, the DESeq2 R package was used for differential expression analysis [9]. The cutoff values for screening DEGs were set at |log2FoldChange| ≥ 1.5 and P < 0.05. The ggplot2 R package was used to present volcano plots.

TCGA database

We downloaded the gene expression data of 51 normal and 483 prostate adenocarcinoma (PRAD) samples from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA, https://www.cancer.gov/tcga) using TCGAbiolinks R package [10]. Differential expression analysis was performed with the DESeq2 R package, and genes with |log₂FoldChange| ≥ 1.5 and P < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed. In addition, we also downloaded the clinical data of 483 PRAD patients for further analysis.

WGCNA analysis

WGCNA serves as a robust bioinformatics tool for analyzing gene expression profiles. Briefly, it creates a weighted gene co-expression network to identify modules of genes that are closely related in their expression patterns, and these modules can be associated with specific interesting phenotypes, such as Gleason score [11]. In this study, the top 50% of expression variants were selected for WGCNA analysis, and the key module exhibited a high correlation with Gleason score (GS6 versus GS9). A total of 175 PRAD samples (45 GS6 and 130 GS9) were included in the WGCNA analysis. To obtain a scale-free topology, a soft-thresholding power of β = 6 was applied. Subsequently, the weighted adjacency matrix was converted into a topological overlap measure (TOM) matrix to evaluate the connectivity within the network [12]. The clustering dendrogram based on the TOM matrix was constructed using average linkage hierarchical clustering [13]. For module detection, the minimum module size was established at 50, and the similarity threshold for merging modules was set at 0.25.

Venn diagram analysis

To further identify potential prognostic genes for prostate cancer, we performed Venn diagram analysis on gene sets obtained from three different analyses, including key module genes associated with Gleason score from WGCNA, DEGs between normal and tumor tissues in TCGA-PRAD, and DEGs between three GS9 and three GS6 prostate cancer samples.

LASSO and cox regression analysis

To identify key genes in intersection gene sets obtained by different methods, the glmnet R package was used to conduct a LASSO regression with P < 0.05 as the threshold based on TCGA-PRAD [14, 15]. The TCGA-PRAD cohort also was analyzed sequentially through multivariate Cox regression with P < 0.05 as the threshold [16, 17]. The survivalROC R package was used to evaluate the accuracy of the model.

The expression of CCDC78 in public database

To further explore the expression level of CCDC78 in prostate cancer, we analyzed publicly available tumor gene expression datasets, including MSK_Cancer Cell_2010 [18], CPGEA [19], MSKCC [20] and MCTP_Nature_2012 [21].

Enrichment analysis of CCDC78 coexpressed genes

Genes coexpressed with CCDC78 and their corresponding Pearson correlation coefficients were obtained from cBioPortal using the “Co-expression” feature [22, 23]. CCDC78-coexpressed genes with a Pearson correlation coefficient > 0.4 and P < 0.05 were selected for KEGG and GO enrichment analysis.

Immune infiltration analysis

The TIMER2 tool was used to explore the relationship between CCDC78 expression and immune infiltration in prostate cancer [24]. The ‘Purity Adjustment’ was set to conduct immune infiltration analysis based on EPIC algorithms.

Human prostate cancer cell culture

LNCaP (SNL-356) and VCaP (SNL-468) cell lines were purchased from SUNNCELL for this study. LNCaP cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium, and VCaP cells were cultured in DMEM medium, both supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂. Transient transfection of siRNA was performed using GP-transfect-mate (GenePharma).

The siRNA sequences were used:

siCCDC78_1.

Sense: 5ʹ- GCUUGGACCUGGAACCAUU-3ʹ,

Antisense: 5ʹ- AAUGGUUCCAGGUCCAAGC-3ʹ;

siCCDC78_2.

Sense: 5ʹ- AGAGCUACUGGUUGCCUAC-3ʹ,

Antisense: 5ʹ- GUAGGCAACCAGUAGCUCU-3ʹ;

Colony formation assay

LNCaP cells transfected with siRNA were seeded onto six-well plates at a density of 2,000 cells per well and cultured for 2 weeks. The cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution for 20 min. ImageJ software was used to quantify the staining results.

Spheroid formation assays

LNCaP cells transfected with siRNA were seeded onto 96-well ultra-low attachment plates at a density of 1,500 cells per well and cultured for 5 days. Images of the formed spheres were captured, and sphere areas were measured using ImageJ software.

Western blotting

Cells were lysed in lysis buffer (50 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 1% (v/v) Triton, 150 mM NaCl, 10 mM CaCl₂, and 1× protease inhibitors). Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher). For immunodetection, membranes were blocked in blocking buffer and then incubated with antibodies against AR (CST, #5153) and GAPDH (ABclonal, AC033).

Cell growth and apoptosis assays

Cell proliferation was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit 8 (CCK-8, Beyotime). Cellular growth, expressed as percentage confluence, and cell death were monitored using the Incucyte S1 live-cell imaging and analysis system, with SYTOX™ Green dye employed to identify dead cells. Apoptosis was further evaluated via flow cytometry (BD FACSCelesta) using the Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (Beyotime, C1062S). Data analysis was performed using FlowJo software (version 10.9.0).

Cell migration assay

Cell scratch wound‑healing assay was used to evaluate cell migration. LNCaP and PC3 cells (5 × 10⁵ per well) were seeded in 6‑well plates and cultured to 90% confluence. The monolayer was scratched with a P‑200 pipette tip to create a wound across the well diameter. After two PBS washes to remove dislodged cells, fresh FBS‑free medium was added. Images of the scratch region were taken at indicated times using an inverted microscope and then quantified with ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.5.0. Quantitative variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Intergroup differences were evaluated using Student’s t-test (for two groups) or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) (for three or more groups), after confirming normality and homogeneity of variance. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

RNA-seq analysis

Firstly, we collected clinical tissue samples from prostate cancer patients with low and high Gleason scores for RNA-seq analysis, as shown in Fig. 1A. Based on the thresholds of |log₂FoldChange| > 1.5 and P < 0.05, we performed differential expression analysis and identified 656 upregulated genes and 426 downregulated genes in patients with high-risk prostate cancer, including CCDC78 (Fig. 1B). Subsequently, we conducted KEGG and GO enrichment analyses on these DEGs. KEGG analysis suggested that DEGs were mainly enriched in pathways related to tumor progression, particularly the calcium signaling pathway (Fig. 1C), which is strongly associated with the occurrence and development of hormone-sensitive, castration-resistant, and aggressive variant forms of prostate cancer [25]. Moreover, GO-BP analysis also revealed that DEGs were involved in tumor progression-related biological processes, including cytoplasmic translation, production of molecular mediators of immune response, B cell-mediated immunity, and immunoglobulin-mediated immune response (Fig. 1D). Finally, the results of GO enrichment analysis for cellular component and molecular function are shown in Fig. 1E and F.

Identification of highly correlated gene module

To explore genes highly correlated with high-risk prostate cancer, we used WGCNA to identify the gene module that exhibited a high correlation with Gleason score (GS6 vs. GS9). First, we determined the soft-thresholding power to be 6 (Fig. 2A) and identified eleven modules based on the gene dendrogram (Fig. 2B). Among these, the green module showed the strongest positive correlation with the Gleason score phenotype (r = 0.49, P = 7 × 10⁻¹², Fig. 2C). The pink module exhibited a comparable correlation. However, KEGG enrichment analysis revealed a stark contrast in their biological relevance: genes in the green module were significantly enriched in pathways related to the cell cycle and DNA repair, whereas those in the pink module were not significantly enriched (Figures S1A and S1B). Consequently, subsequent analyses focused exclusively on the green module. In addition, the green module exhibited a high correlation between gene significance for Gleason score and module membership, further indicating that these genes are strongly associated with high-risk prostate cancer (Fig. 2D).

Venn diagram analysis

Differential expression analysis between normal and prostate cancer tissues was performed based on the TCGA-PRAD cohort, using thresholds of |log₂FoldChange| ≥ 1.5 and P < 0.05. We identified 600 upregulated genes and 780 downregulated genes in prostate cancer patients, including CCDC78 (Fig. 2E). Venn diagram analysis was conducted on gene sets obtained from the three analyses described above: the key module genes, DEGs between normal and prostate cancer tissues, and DEGs between three GS9 and three GS6 prostate cancer samples. Finally, 14 overlapping genes were identified for subsequent analysis: MYBL2, FEV, ISX, HJURP, CENPM, PRR7, CCDC78, ZMYND10, UBE2C, CDC20, NEK2, KIF4A, RRM2, and HAGHL (Fig. 2F).

Construction of prognostic risk model

Next, a prognostic risk model for prostate cancer was constructed based on TCGA-PRAD. First, we applied the 14 identified genes to perform LASSO regression analysis and, based on AIC and BIC, selected four genes with an optimal lambda value of 0.04295 (Fig. 3A and B). Subsequently, multivariate Cox regression analysis was performed on these four genes, and CCDC78 (P = 0.013) was identified as an independent prognostic signature for Progression-Free Survival (PFS) (Fig. 3C). Survival ROC curves generated from the prognostic signature yielded AUC values all above 0.7, indicating that CCDC78 effectively predicts prostate cancer prognosis at 1-year (AUC = 0.750), 3-year (AUC = 0.775), and 5-year (AUC = 0.750) time points (Fig. 3D). The 1-, 3-, and 5-year nomogram calibration curves are shown in Figures S2A–C. To enhance the applicability of this model in clinical practice, a nomogram incorporating clinical parameters (age, Gleason score, and CCDC78 expression) and a risk score was established (Fig. 3E). Finally, Kaplan-Meier curves showed that patients in the high CCDC78 expression group had shorter PFS than those in the low expression group (Fig. 3F). Furthermore, the prognostic value of CCDC78 for PFS was validated using an independent external dataset [26] (Figure S2D).

High expression of CCDC78 in high-risk PCa

First, CCDC78 expression was significantly upregulated in prostate cancer based on the TCGA-PRAD and CPGEA databases (Fig. 4A and D), and its expression level progressively increased with advancing Gleason score (Fig. 4B). Second, relative CCDC78 expression was also elevated in prostate cancer with a Gleason score of 9 (Fig. 4C). Finally, CCDC78 expression was significantly higher in metastatic tumors than in primary tumors (Fig. 4E and F).

Enrichment analysis of CCDC78 coexpressed genes

To investigate the potential mechanisms of CCDC78 in tumorigenesis, the STRING database was used to identify 10 CCDC78-binding proteins supported by experimental evidence, and a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed (Fig. 5A). CCDC78 coexpression genes in PRAD were downloaded from cBioPortal and subjected to GO and KEGG enrichment analyses. KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that CCDC78 coexpression genes were enriched in pathways related to tumor progression, including DNA replication, oxidative phosphorylation, and thermogenesis (Fig. 5B). Interestingly, GO-BP enrichment analysis showed that these genes were mainly enriched in immune-related pathways, such as immunoglobulin production, production of molecular mediators of immune response, B cell-mediated immunity, and immunoglobulin-mediated immune response (Fig. 5C). Moreover, GO-CC enrichment analysis demonstrated that CCDC78 coexpression genes were enriched in the immunoglobulin complex (Fig. 5D), which plays a pivotal role in both protective and pathological immune responses [27]. Finally, we found that CCDC78 expression was negatively correlated with tumor infiltration level in TCGA-PRAD (Fig. 5E).

CCDC78 promotes the growth of prostate cancer cells

To verify whether CCDC78 promotes prostate cancer cell growth, we performed transient knockdown using two different siRNAs targeting CCDC78, which effectively reduced the cellular mRNA levels of CCDC78 (Figure S3A). Using an Incucyte live-cell imaging and analysis platform, we found that CCDC78 knockdown markedly suppressed the growth confluence of LNCaP cells (Fig. 6A). Similarly, CCK-8 assays revealed that CCDC78 knockdown significantly suppressed the proliferation of LNCaP and VCaP cells (Figs. 6C and 7B). Apoptosis was assessed using the Incucyte Cytotox dye, which labels apoptotic cells, and CCDC78 knockdown markedly promoted apoptosis in LNCaP cells (Fig. 6B and Figure S3B). Flow cytometry analysis further confirmed that knockdown of CCDC78 induces apoptosis in LNCaP and VCaP cells (Figs. 6D and 7A). The role of CCDC78 in prostate cancer tumorigenesis was further evaluated using colony formation and spheroid formation assays, both of which showed that CCDC78 knockdown exerted tumor-suppressive effects (Figures S3C/S3D and Fig. 7C). To assess cell migration, transient knockdown of CCDC78 was performed in LNCaP and PC3 cells using siRNA, followed by wound healing assays. The results showed that depletion of CCDC78 did not significantly alter the wound closure rate in either cell line (Figure S4A and Figure S4B), suggesting that CCDC78 does not play a major role in regulating migration in these prostate cancer cells. Importantly, knockdown of CCDC78 reduced expression of the prostate cancer driver factor AR (Fig. 6E). Compared with wild-type LNCaP cells, knockdown of CCDC78 partially restored proliferative activity in AR-overexpressing LNCaP cells (Fig. 6F, G). Taken together, these findings indicate that CCDC78 supports prostate cancer cell growth by sustaining AR activity.

Discussion

Prostate cancer is the second most common malignancy among men worldwide. While PSA testing is widely used for early screening, its utility in risk assessment is limited. A large-scale study of 1,031 patients showed that the overall accuracy of biopsy in matching postoperative pathological Gleason scores was approximately 58.3% [28]. Moreover, 41% of patients had a lower Gleason score on biopsy than on radical surgery specimens. Clinically, patients with a low Gleason score typically require active monitoring rather than immediate intervention [29]. Given that the accuracy of biopsy-derived Gleason scores is only around 50%, this underestimation can prevent patients from receiving timely early intervention. Indeed, a higher Gleason score is significantly correlated with poor prognosis [30]. Therefore, more precise biomarkers are needed to identify high-risk prostate cancer.

WGCNA enables the systematic discovery of prognostic molecules in tumors by leveraging the co-expression architecture of the transcriptome [31–33]. Instead of individually ranking genes, the algorithm groups highly co-expressed genes into modules, whose eigengenes can be correlated with clinical outcomes such as overall survival, disease-free survival, or treatment response [34]. However, no studies have yet used WGCNA to identify prognostic markers for high-risk prostate cancer. In this study, we applied WGCNA to identify the key module associated with high-risk versus low-risk phenotypes using TCGA-PRAD data. Differential gene analysis is a widely used first-pass strategy for screening potential cancer biomarkers [35, 36]. Here, we collected prostate cancer tissue samples and used H&E staining to distinguish different Gleason scores. Subsequently, we performed RNA-seq to identify DEGs between low and high Gleason score groups. We also identified DEGs between prostate cancer and normal tissues based on TCGA-PRAD. From the three analyses, we obtained 14 intersecting genes for further investigation. Finally, we combined LASSO and Cox regression analyses to identify CCDC78 as a prognostic marker for distinguishing high-risk from low-risk prostate cancer.

Currently, CCDC78 has been identified as the causative gene of autosomal dominant centronuclear myopathy-4 (CNM4) [37]. In recent years, several studies have also found that CCDC78 may serve as a useful prognostic marker in cancer. For instance, it has been shown to be a novel prognostic marker in colon cancer and diffuse large B-cell lymphoma [38–40]. Another study investigated CCDC78 as a potential prognostic biomarker in colorectal cancer, incorporating both clinical correlation and functional validation [41]. However, the function of CCDC78 in prostate cancer cells remains unexplored. In this study, we experimentally demonstrated that CCDC78 is essential for the growth of prostate cancer cells. Therefore, CCDC78 warrants further investigation as a candidate molecule in prostate cancer.

Immunotherapy has emerged as a new approach in cancer treatment, by activating the adaptive immune system in cancer patients to target both primary and metastatic tumors and curb tumor progression [42]. Over the past few years, immunotherapies such as immune checkpoint inhibitors, chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy, sipuleucel-T, and others have gained clinical approval and shown efficacy against a range of tumor types [43]. However, prostate cancer is known as a cold tumor, due to sparse lymphocytic infiltration, a highly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, and high expression of immune checkpoint molecules [44]. In this study, we found that genes coexpressed with CCDC78 are enriched in tumor immunity-related signaling pathways. Moreover, bioinformatic analysis of the TCGA-PRAD cohort revealed that CCDC78 expression is inversely correlated with the level of tumor immune infiltration. This observation raises the possibility that prostate cancers with high CCDC78 expression may exhibit an immune-desert phenotype, which is often linked to poor prognosis and reduced response to immunotherapy. However, these correlative findings are hypothesis-generating and require further experimental validation to establish a functional link between CCDC78 and immune modulation.

Beyond its potential immunomodulatory role, we investigated the direct effect of CCDC78 on prostate cancer cell growth. Functionally, CCDC78 knockdown reduced AR protein levels and partially rescued proliferation in AR‑overexpressing LNCaP cells, indicating that CCDC78 supports prostate cancer growth by sustaining AR activity. In contrast, CCDC78 knockdown did not significantly affect cell migration in wound‑healing assays, suggesting that CCDC78 primarily regulates proliferation rather than motility in these prostate cancer cells. Whether CCDC78 also intersects with the cell cycle and DNA repair pathways enriched in the green WGCNA module remains to be determined. Nevertheless, the present findings establish CCDC78 as a previously unrecognized positive regulator of AR signaling in prostate cancer cells.

This study has some important limitations. First, although mRNA levels do not always represent protein levels, CCDC78 protein was undetectable in multiple large-scale prostate cancer proteomic cohorts [45–47], making it challenging to study CCDC78 at the protein level. Second, our functional experiments were performed in vitro using multiple prostate cancer cell lines; whether CCDC78 plays a similar role in vivo remains to be determined. Third, the observed correlation between CCDC78 expression and immune infiltration is based on publicly available transcriptomic data and requires experimental validation. Finally, the prognostic value of CCDC78 should be further evaluated in independent, prospective cohorts before clinical translation.

In summary, we identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between high-risk and low-risk prostate cancer samples through RNA-seq analysis. Through comprehensive bioinformatics analysis of multiple tumor databases, we identified CCDC78 as a prognostic marker for high-risk prostate cancer. We further found that genes coexpressed with CCDC78 are involved in immune regulation, and that CCDC78 expression is inversely correlated with the level of tumor immune infiltration in prostate cancer. Most importantly, we demonstrated that CCDC78 plays a critical role in the tumorigenesis and growth of prostate cancer cells. In conclusion, these findings strongly suggest that CCDC78 could serve as a prognostic biomarker for high-risk prostate cancer.
