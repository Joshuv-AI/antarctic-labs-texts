Massive right ventricular outflow tract pseudoaneurysm after Konno: Safe sternal re-entry for triple-valve replacement and right ventricle–pulmonary artery reconstruction
Authors: Sofia Hu, Alexander K Reed, Alex R Dalal, Madelaine Bustos, Y Joseph Woo
Published: 2026

Addressing pseudoaneurysms of the RVOT requires PVR and reconstruction extending to the PA.

Central Message

Exclusion of right ventricular outflow tract pseudoaneurysms is technically feasible with multidisciplinary preoperative planning and extensive multigraft reconstruction.

Pseudoaneurysm formation is a rare complication of surgery on the right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT) resulting from dehiscence of patch or conduit material from the right ventricle.1 RVOT interventions—ranging from the Konno-Rastan, Ross, and Nikaidoh procedures to tetralogy of Fallot repairs and right ventricular (RV) to pulmonary conduits—have increased in prevalence. With improving survival after these procedures, complications such as pseudoaneurysms of the RVOT may be seen more frequently, particularly among adults with congenital heart disease. The anterior position of the RVOT places these pseudoaneurysms at increased risk for injury during sternal re-entry. Previous reports of RVOT pseudoaneurysm have been limited to pediatric cases2, 3, 4 or transcatheter approaches.3,5

Here, we present the case of an adult patient who developed a RVOT pseudoaneurysm after the Konno-Rastan procedure and underwent a complex reconstruction. This report highlights key technical strategies to ensure safe re-entry and exclusion of an RVOT pseudoaneurysm. Institutional review board approval was not required by university policy. Written consent for publication of images and videos was obtained from the patient.

Case Presentation

The patient is a 33-year-old woman who was diagnosed at 10 years old with dysplastic aortic valve and subaortic stenosis. At 24 years old, she underwent resection of the subaortic stenosis, RVOT augmentation with the Konno procedure using a woven double velour vascular graft as the aortoventriculoplasty patch, and aortic valve replacement with a 23-mm Mosaic bioprosthetic valve (Medtronic). This Konno patch was further augmented with a bovine pericardium patch covering both the ascending aorta and RVOT. The patient subsequently developed pulmonary stenosis and mitral valve endocarditis resulting in severe mitral stenosis and mitral regurgitation. The patient underwent an attempted mitral valve replacement at an outside institution 6 months before her presentation, but the procedure was aborted after re-entry because of a large pseudoaneurysm of the RVOT obstructing safe placement of the aortic crossclamp. Postoperatively, she developed hemorrhagic shock and returned to the operating room for repair of an injury to the left internal mammary artery.

Upon presentation to our institution, the patient reported persistent dyspnea. Preoperative transthoracic echocardiography revealed trace aortic regurgitation, severe mitral stenosis (mean gradient 17 mm Hg) and mitral regurgitation, moderate tricuspid regurgitation, and severe pulmonic stenosis (peak and mean gradients 64 and 36 mm Hg, respectively). Electrocardiogram demonstrated a right bundle branch block. Computed tomography angiography of the chest demonstrated a RVOT pseudoaneurysm immediately posterior to the sternum and anterior to the pulmonary artery (PA) and aorta (Video 1). The pseudoaneurysm measured 4.2 cm wide, 1.4 cm deep, and 4 cm long and arose from an ostium in the anterior wall of the RV measuring 0.8 cm. Several sternal wires were involved or immediately adjacent to the anterior wall of the pseudoaneurysm. Given her age, she was recommended for open surgical repair, as well as replacements of the pulmonic, mitral, and aortic valves.

To facilitate safe fourth-time, sternal re-entry and reduce perfusion of the pseudoaneurysm, interventional cardiology was consulted for embolization of the pseudoaneurysm. This was performed via femoral venous access. Wire access of the pseudoaneurysm was obtained through its ostium from the RVOT and confirmed by fluoroscopy, and a total of 16 embolization coils and 2 vascular plugs were placed (Video 2). Contrast angiography of the RVOT and main PA confirmed no residual perfusion of the pseudoaneurysm and no protrusion of the devices into the RVOT. An interval computed tomography angiography demonstrated near complete obliteration of the pseudoaneurysm cavity (Video 3).

One month after coil embolization, the patient proceeded to the operating room for repair (Video 4). First, a femoral venous line and right axillary artery chimney graft were placed to facilitate emergent cannulation for cardiopulmonary bypass if the pseudoaneurysm was entered on re-entry. After safe redo sternotomy and lysis of mediastinal adhesions, bicaval cannulation was performed, and cardiopulmonary bypass was initiated using the right axillary artery. No bleeding was encountered when the pseudoaneurysm was intentionally opened, and all coils and devices were removed to reveal the right ventricle and PA. The PA was incised through the stenosis and into the bifurcation (Figure 1, A). The RVOT was incised proximally, and the moderator band was divided to further open the RVOT.

Attention then turned to the aorta. Given that there was only a short segment of ascending aorta, a hemiarch replacement under circulatory arrest was planned to generate more clearance for aortic root surgery. Cooling was initiated, and an aortic crossclamp was placed across the mid-ascending aorta. The proximal ascending aorta was incised, and the heart was arrested with direct antegrade cardioplegia. The previous aortic bioprosthetic valve was explanted (Figure 1, B). Upon reaching 28 °C, the crossclamp was removed, the aorta was transected at the proximal ascending aorta, and retrograde cerebral perfusion via the superior vena cava was performed. Hemiarch replacement was performed using a branch graft, and antegrade circulation was reinitiated using the side branch. The coronary buttons were mobilized.

Attention turned to the mitral valve which was approached transeptally and replaced in standard fashion with a 25-mm St. Jude mechanical valve (Abbott). The interatrial septotomy was closed, and focus turned to the aortic valve. A composite graft with a 21-mm St. Jude mechanical valve was constructed on the back table. Annular sutures along the lower portion of the Konno patch were placed using 2-0 braided polyester sutures to create a planar annulus and seat the composite graft (Figure 1, C). The coronary buttons were reimplanted in orthotopic position. The proximal aortic anastomosis was completed and the crossclamp removed.

The right side was now readdressed. A 26-mm woven double velour vascular graft was anastomosed to the PA bifurcation. A 36-mm woven double velour vascular graft was anastomosed to the RVOT and onto the original Konno patch and right ventricular free wall. The posterior suture line was buttressed with bovine pericardium. Within the RVOT graft, a 29-mm bioprosthetic valve was anastomosed with running 3-0 polypropylene suture (Figure 1, D). Both the RV and PA grafts were shortened, beveled, and anastomosed together with running 3-0 polypropylene suture to reconstruct the RVOT. The patient was weaned off cardiopulmonary bypass without issue.

Postbypass transesophageal echocardiogram demonstrated preserved biventricular function with mitral and aortic valve mean gradients of 4 and 11 mm Hg, respectively. The patient was taken to the intensive care unit postoperatively, where she was extubated on the day of surgery. After an uneventful recovery, she was discharged to home on postoperative day 13. Transthoracic echocardiogram imaging at 1-month follow-up redemonstrated normal biventricular function and showed peak and mean pulmonary valve gradients of 6 and 3 mm Hg, respectively.

Discussion

RVOT pseudoaneurysms present a unique challenge during sternal entry and cardiopulmonary bypass initiation. These issues can be circumvented with appropriate preoperative planning and intraoperative strategy. First, angiographic and transcatheter approaches can be used to characterize and decrease blood flow to areas of concern. Embolization of the pseudoaneurysm depressurizes and reduces perfusion to the space, significantly reducing the risk of bleeding if the pseudoaneurysm is inadvertently entered during sternal re-entry. Second, planned peripheral cannulation at femoral or axillary sites enables controlled initiation of cardiopulmonary bypass. Preoperative computed tomography angiography should be reviewed for proximity of the RVOT pseudoaneurysm to the sternum as a measure of bleeding risk and to determine whether there is sufficient access to the aorta for immediate cannulation during re-entry. We recommend obtaining peripheral micropuncture access before all sternal re-entries and planning for peripheral cannulation when there is concern for the potential inability to quickly establish central cannulation. Third, near total explantation of graft material is required to resect the RVOT pseudoaneurysm. Division of the moderator band can be performed without significant consequence to right ventricular function and to optimize opening of the RVOT. Lastly, although pulmonary homografts can be used to fashion an RV-PA conduit, the proximal extension of the RVOT reconstruction can result in large size discrepancies between the RVOT and PA. We therefore found it beneficial to use 2 separate straight grafts rather than a single conduit, which enables tailoring of the angle and trajectory of the RVOT and is less prone to kinking. We believe this is the first report of a RVOT pseudoaneurysm after a Konno-Rastan procedure managed with this approach and conclude that this open surgical approach can be undertaken safely to reconstruct these types of lesions.
