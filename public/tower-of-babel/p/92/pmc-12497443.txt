Patterns of immune recovery after axicabtagene ciloleucel for aggressive B‐cell lymphoma
Authors: André Airosa Pardal, Ana Benzaquén, Pablo Granados, Paula Amat, Rafael Hernani, Aitana Balaguer‐Roselló, Ariadna Pérez, Marta Villalba, Pedro Asensi, Blanca Ferrer, Lourdes Cordón, Irene Pastor‐Galán, Juan Montoro, María José Remigia, Juan Carlos Hernández‐Boluda, Amparo Sempere, Jaime Sanz, María José Terol, Carlos Solano, Pedro Chorão, José Luis Piñana
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHematotoxicity after anti‐CD19 chimeric antigen receptor T‐cell therapy has drawn attention for potential long‐term effects, and yet, recovery of lymphocyte subsets and immunoglobulin levels beyond B‐cell aplasia remains poorly understood. We retrospectively analyzed 76 consecutive axicabtagene ciloleucel (axi‐cel) recipients with relapsed/refractory aggressive B‐cell lymphoma between 2020 and 2024, focusing on basic immune recovery patterns and their impact on outcomes. Median CD8+ T and NK cells increased to >300/mm3 and >100/mm3, respectively, within 2 months after infusion. CD4+ T‐cell recovery was slower, with 42% cumulative incidence of >200/mm3 cell count at 12 months. B‐cells were detectable in 18% at 12 months. Immunoglobulin levels initially declined and then plateaued, with 51% incidence of immunoglobulin G (IgG) levels > 400 mg/dL at 12 months. Modified EASIX > 2.00 was associated with delayed kinetics of CD3+ T‐cells, CD4+ T‐cells, and CD8+ T‐cells, and cumulative dexamethasone dose > 120 mg with delayed recovery of CD4+ T‐cells, NK cells, and IgG. At 1 month post‐infusion, low CD4+ T‐cell count and high CAR‐HEMATOTOX predicted worse 12‐month progression‐free survival (hazard ratio [HR] 2.81 and 3.34) and overall survival (HR 3.21 and 4.41), respectively. After axi‐cel, CD4+ T‐cells and IgG recovered slowly during the first year, while CD8+ T and NK cells increased rapidly. A higher modified EASIX score may predict slower cellular recovery, while corticosteroids may delay both cellular and humoral recovery. Lower CD4+ T‐cell count was associated with worse outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Chimeric antigen receptor T‐cells (CAR‐T) have shown curative potential in aggressive B‐cell lymphomas, but some concerning toxicities have nonetheless been observed. 1 , 2 Hematotoxicity, especially late‐onset anemia, thrombocytopenia, neutropenia, and cellular and humoral defects, is of particular interest during long‐term follow‐up. 3 , 4 , 5 Anemia and thrombocytopenia have been extensively studied and reviewed in meta‐analyses, 6 and the CAR‐HEMATOTOX score, which includes bone marrow function (hemoglobin, neutrophil, and platelet counts) and inflammatory markers (ferritin and C‐reactive protein), has been proven to be useful for predicting the incidence and duration of CAR‐T‐related hematotoxicity, as well as infectious complications and overall mortality. 5 Rejeski et al. found that biphasic neutrophil recovery was associated with improved survival and that prolonged neutropenia predisposed to severe infections, possibly increasing non‐relapse mortality (NRM). 7 Regarding cellular immune recovery, recent data have shown that low CD4+ T‐cell and B‐cell lymphocyte counts persist in 25%–60% of recipients at 12 months after CAR‐T therapy, 4 , 8 although with subtle differences between anti‐CD19 products. 9 Persistent CD4+ T‐cell count < 200 cells/mm3 in older patients may increase the risk of late opportunistic infections, even beyond the first year after CAR‐T therapy. 10 Persistent hypogammaglobulinemia, usually defined as immunoglobulin G (IgG) < 400 mg/dL, has also been reported in almost half the patients at 3 months 11 and 12 months. 4 With the exception of B‐cell aplasia, which has been linked to CAR‐T activity and disease relapse, 10 immune recovery profiles by lymphocyte subtype and gammaglobulin levels remain poorly characterized after anti‐CD19 CAR‐T therapy in aggressive B‐cell lymphoma recipients.

This study aims to analyze basic cellular and humoral immune recovery patterns and their impact on CAR‐T outcomes in a homogeneous cohort of axicabtagene ciloleucel (axi‐cel) recipients for relapsed/refractory aggressive B‐cell lymphomas.

METHODS

Patients and procedures

This retrospective analysis included all consecutive adult patients ≥ 18 years old with a diagnosis of aggressive B‐cell lymphoma undergoing CAR‐T‐cell infusion with axi‐cel at Hospital Universitari i Politècnic La Fe (HUPLF) and Hospital Clinic Universitari de València (HCUV) between January 2020 and July 2024. Inclusion criteria included patients with at least two available lymphocyte subpopulation and Ig determinations at different time points (including pre‐infusion). Exclusion criteria included early death (≤28 days after CAR‐T infusion). All patients provided informed consent for data collection. The study was approved by the institutional review board of both centers and was in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (reference 2025/588/Axi‐RecIm for HUPLF and 2024/252 for HCUV).

Patients were enrolled to receive axi‐cel as second‐line therapy or beyond for refractory or relapsed disease. Bridging therapy while waiting for production was at the discretion of the referring physician and cell therapy unit team. Patients received lymphodepletion with fludarabine 30 mg/m2 and cyclophosphamide 500 mg/m2, at Days −5, −4, and −3 prior to CAR‐T infusion (Day 0). All patients received anti‐infectious prophylaxis with levofloxacin 500 mg and fluconazole 100 mg or posaconazole 300 mg per day (from admission or neutropenia till neutrophil recovery above 500/µL or 2–4 weeks after corticosteroid withdrawal in the case of posaconazole), trimethoprim‐sulfamethoxazole 800/160 mg thrice weekly, and acyclovir 800 mg twice daily until 6–12 months after CAR‐T and according to CD4 T‐cell levels. Patients who developed cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune effector cell‐associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) were treated according to international practice recommendations. 12 Briefly, in HUPLF, tocilizumab 8 mg/kg/day up to 3 days was used as first line for Grade 1 CRS in the first 24 h after infusion, persistent Grade 1 fever (>72 h), or CRS Grade ≥2, and in case of deterioration or no response, dexamethasone was prescribed as second line. In HCUV, tocilizumab 8 mg/kg/day up to 3 days along with three doses of 10 mg dexamethasone every 12 h was initiated for CRS ≥ 2. For ICANS in HUPLF, dexamethasone 10 mg every 6 h was prescribed for Grade 2, dexamethasone 20 mg every 6 h or methylprednisone 1000 mg/day for 1–3 days for Grade 3, and in case of deterioration, anakinra at a maximum dose of 10 mg/kg every 8 h was used as second line. In HCUV, dexamethasone 10 mg every 6 h was started for ICANS Grade ≥2. In case of progression in the first 24 h or no response in 48–72 h, anakinra 8 mg/kg/day was prescribed until resolution. Prophylactic Ig replacement was not routinely performed, except in cases with severe infections (requiring additional hospitalization for intravenous antimicrobials) and IgG levels below 400 mg/mL.

Patients were closely monitored in the outpatient clinic after hospital discharge. They were instructed to stay at a 1‐h distance from the hospital within the first month, and regular visits were scheduled during the first 3 months after CAR‐T‐cell infusion. Baseline disease status was assessed through PET‐CT before lymphodepletion, at 3, 6, and 12 months after CAR‐T for all patients, and when clinically indicated. After patient discharge from the ambulatory setting to the referral hospitals, follow‐up was performed in person and/or by telephone, and electronic medical records were consulted for a minimum of 18 months after infusion.

Immune reconstitution monitoring

Immunophenotyping was performed using flow cytometry (FC) on fresh whole‐blood samples collected in ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid to monitor the absolute lymphocyte count and lymphocyte subsets. The following lymphoid populations were quantified: T‐cells (CD3+), subdivided into CD3+ CD4+ T‐cells and CD3+ CD8+ T‐cells; B‐cells (CD19+); and NK cells (CD3− CD16+ CD56+). BD Multitest 6‐color TBNK reagents (containing a combination of CD3, CD4, CD8, CD16+ CD56, CD19, and CD45 monoclonal antibodies conjugated to fluorochromes) were used for this purpose. Samples were analyzed on a FACSCanto‐II cytometer using FACSCanto software (Becton Dickinson, San Jose, CA, USA) at both centers. Automated cell gating was reviewed, and percentages and absolute counts were compared with the manufacturer's reference values. Lymphocyte counts and Ig levels were assessed at Days 30, 60, 90, 180, and 365 after CAR‐T in both centers.

Definitions

Patients who relapsed after prior response to immunochemotherapy (at least partial remission [PR]) were classified as chemosensitive, while patients not achieving this response were categorized as refractory. B‐cell aplasia was considered as indetectable CD19+ cell count through FC analysis, as described above. Overall survival (OS) was defined as survival from the time of infusion, with death from any cause as an event. Progression‐free survival (PFS) was defined as survival without lymphoma relapse/progression from the time of CAR‐T‐cell infusion. Adverse events were graded following the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) v5.0. CRS and ICANS were graded based on retrospective review, following the American Society for Transplantation and Cellular Therapy consensus criteria. 13 CAR‐HEMATOTOX 5 and modified EASIX (mEASIX: serum creatinine, lactate dehydrogenase, and platelet count) 14 were calculated prior to lymphodepletion. CD4+ T‐cell recovery was defined as a count >200/mm³ and IgG recovery as levels >400 mg/dL.

Endpoints and statistical analyses

The primary endpoint was to characterize the kinetic pattern of lymphocyte subsets and Ig recovery from the time of infusion up to 12 months, using time‐point analyses. The secondary outcomes were to estimate the incidence of immune recovery and identify factors that influenced immune recovery over time while exploring the impact of immune markers at 1 month after CAR‐T‐cell infusion in PFS and OS at 12 months.

Categorical variables were presented as percentages and compared using Pearson's chi‐square test. Continuous variables were expressed as mean and median values, along with their respective ranges, and compared using the Mann–Whitney U or Kruskal–Wallis test.

Immune recovery kinetics were represented using absolute values along time, using the exact date of sample extractions. Locally estimated scatterplot smoothing curves were constructed to visually illustrate data trends. The 1‐year cumulative incidence of CD4+ T‐cell, B‐cell, and IgG recovery was estimated using a competing risks framework, where NRM and disease relapse were treated as competing events. For 1‐year NRM cumulative incidence estimates, disease relapse was treated as the competing event. To analyze factors that impacted on kinetics for each lymphocyte subset or Ig subtype, we used a logarithmic transformation to construct linear regression models and interpret coefficients. Linear mixed‐effect models were created to estimate slopes and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), with random intercepts for each patient and fixed effects for patient, disease, and CAR‐T‐related variables. Covariates with an adverse effect on immune recovery kinetics were represented by negative slope values.

Unadjusted time‐to‐event analyses were performed using Kaplan–Meier estimates and log‐rank tests. To estimate the effect of lymphocyte subsets or Ig subtypes levels on OS and PFS at 12 months, we developed landmark Cox regression models, controlling for significant baseline and CAR‐T‐related covariates. Landmarks were established from the date of lymphocyte and Ig assessment at 1 month (±15 days) after CAR‐T‐cell infusion. To determine optimal thresholds for lymphocyte and Ig values, the maxstat R package was used, 15 along with a 10‐fold cross‐validation to prevent overfitting. A bootstrap procedure with 1000 iterations calculated the concordance index (c‐index), and the cutoff with the highest c‐index stratified patients into low‐ and high‐count groups in Cox models.

For mixed‐effects linear models, variable selection began with a full model approach, followed by stepwise backward selection based on the Akaike Information Criteria. In Cox models, the LASSO penalty was used, with 10‐fold cross‐validation determining the optimal lambda value while maintaining a 10:1 sample–variable ratio. In both analyses, bootstrapping procedures for model fitting were performed 1000 times on resampled data sets, retaining variables selected in at least 50% of iterations. Key covariates included patient characteristics, diagnosis, treatment responses, and immune recovery metrics. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05, with analyses conducted using R version 4.1.3. For detailed statistical methods, see the Supplementary Material.

Overall, 32 patients deceased at a median of 163 days after CAR‐T infusion (range, 36–1680 days). Twenty‐three out of 76 (30%) deceased from disease relapse/progression: 7 before Day +100, 10 from Day +100 to 12 months, and 6 beyond 12 months. Of the remaining nine patients (12%) who deceased from NRM causes, seven succumbed to infections at a median of 52 days after CAR‐T infusion (range 37–830). Four patients deceased from bloodstream infection (one from Candida krusei, one from Escherichia coli, one from both Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Stenotrophomonas maltophila, and the last one from Salmonella), whereas two deceased patients from COVID‐19. Finally, one patient deceased from microbiologically undocumented septic shock. Additionally, one patient deceased from late recurrent ICANS at 6 months (confirmed in autopsy by the presence of CAR‐T‐cells in cerebral tissue), and one patient deceased from an unknown cause at 55 months. Twelve‐month OS and PFS were 69% (95% CI 59–80) and 54% (95% CI 44–67), respectively. Twelve‐month NRM was 8% (95% CI 3–16).

Factors associated with 1‐year PFS and OS

Using landmark analysis at 1 month (±15 days) after CAR‐T‐cell infusion, 74 patients had available data points for lymphocyte subsets and 65 patients for Ig. After group stratification using the threshold maximizing differences in PFS, the factors negatively impacting 12‐month PFS in univariate analysis were low CD4+ T‐cell count (≤43/mm3, 38% of patients), low CD8+ T‐cell count (≤258/mm3, 58% of patients), low NK cell count (≤21/mm3, 27% of patients), and low IgG (≤498 mg/dL, 25% of patients). In multivariate analysis, we included the CD4+ T‐cell count, response to treatment, CAR‐HEMATOTOX, tocilizumab, and dexamethasone cumulative doses. Significant variables for 12‐month PFS were low CD4+ T‐cell count (hazard ratio [HR] 2.81, 95% CI 1.17–6.73, P = 0.020), disease refractoriness before axi‐cel (HR 2.96, 95% CI 1.27–6.93, P = 0.012), and high CAR‐HEMATOTOX (HR 3.34, 95% CI 1.32–8.42, P = 0.011). Using the 65 patients with lymphocyte subset and available Ig values, in multivariate analysis IgG levels, response to treatment, mEASIX, and dexamethasone cumulative dose were included. No immune recovery variable showed statistical significance. Figure 2A presents the PFS curves according to the CD4+ T‐cell count groups at 1 month post‐infusion. DISCUSSION

This study describes the immune recovery patterns of lymphocytes and immunoglobulins after axi‐cel and their potential impact on outcomes. While CD8+ T‐cells and NK cells showed a steady increase within the first months post‐infusion, CD4+ T‐cells' and IgG recovery occurred more gradually, but the CD4+ T‐cell count remained below 200 cells/mm3 and IgG levels below 400 mg/dL in a substantial proportion of patients at 12 months after CAR‐T. Our analyses revealed that a high pre‐infusion mEASIX was associated with slower lymphocyte subset recovery, whereas higher cumulative doses of corticosteroids predicted slower CD4+ T and NK cell and humoral recovery. Notably, lower CD4+ T‐cell count at 1 month post‐infusion and high CAR‐HEMATOTOX were both associated with worse PFS and OS. Our results depict the immune reconstitution kinetics and highlight the clinical implications of faster immune recovery in terms of outcomes in axi‐cel recipients with aggressive B‐cell lymphoma.

Our findings on CD4+ T‐cell recovery following CAR‐T therapy are in line with previous reports, 16 , 17 as only 42% of patients were able to recover CD4+ above 200 cells/mm3 at 12 months. In contrast, we confirmed that NK cells and CD8+ T‐cells have faster recovery patterns, as previously reported. 4 This phenomenon may be partly attributed to the intrinsic higher proliferative capability of CD8+ T‐cells and/or higher proliferation in response to infections. 4 , 18 Intriguingly, this immune recovery pattern is very similar in the allogeneic stem cell transplant setting. 19 We also found that the median immunoglobulin levels show an early decline after infusion, reaching a nadir at 6 months and recovering IgG values above 400 mg/dL in 51% of patients at 12 months, similar to the percentage observed (47%) in a prior study. 4 Our observation is also consistent with a prior study that reported an IgG nadir at 6 months and 53% low IgG levels at 12 months after CAR‐T. 16 Overall, our results support an association between axi‐cel and hypogammaglobulinemia, 20 which may last over 12 months. B‐cell aplasia persisted in more than 90% at 6 months and in over 70% at 12 months in our cohort. The rate of B‐cell aplasia in our series is higher than previously reported rates, which range from 25% to 60%. 10 , 16 , 21 , 22 Given the high B‐cell aplasia rate in our series, we were not able to detect any associated conditions. However, the higher steroid and anakinra use in our study, despite similar levels of CRS and neurological complications, may partly explain this finding. Likewise, differences between B‐cell aplasia definitions may also account for these discrepancies. 4

Interestingly, we found two conditions associated with delayed immune recovery: higher mEASIX predicted slower CD3+, CD4+, and CD8+ T‐cell recovery, while higher cumulative corticosteroid doses delayed CD4+ T‐cell, NK cell, and humoral recovery. This is in contrast with a prior study in which delayed CD4+ T‐cell recovery was correlated with older age but not with previous lines of therapy, bridging regimen, CRS severity, or use of corticosteroids. 10 The mEASIX has recently attracted interest for predicting complications and lower OS after CAR‐T. 14 , 23 In our study, a high mEASIX score was associated with delayed cellular recovery (including CD4+ T‐cells) at 1 month post CAR‐T. Low CD4+ T‐cell count, in turn, predicted lower OS. This relationship may explain why mEASIX can predict OS, thereby supporting the consistency of our findings. Nonetheless, this observation should be confirmed in future studies. The negative effect of corticosteroids on immune recovery is not unexpected, given that they suppress NK cell activity, reduce the function of phagocytic and antigen‐presenting cells, and decrease CD4+ and CD8+ T lymphocyte levels. 24 In contrast to a previous large retrospective study, 9 the CAR‐HEMATOTOX score was not useful in predicting slow immune recovery in our series. This difference may be partly attributable to the weight in our cohort of post‐infusion variables not included in this score, such as the use of corticosteroids and lower rates of tocilizumab.

Our study found that slower CD4+ T‐cell recovery negatively impacted both 1‐year PFS and OS, consistent with a previous publication. 9 Although infectious complications were not specifically addressed in this work, it is conceivable that impaired immune recovery confers greater infectious risk, which consequently contributes to higher NRM. 25 Besides lower infectious risk, immune recovery is not typically regarded as impacting durable disease remission to the same extent as other factors such as initial response, baseline tumor volume, extramedullary disease, or lymphodepletion regimen. 26 However, our study found that a lower CD4+ T‐cell count negatively impacted PFS and OS, as did the CAR‐HEMATOTOX score. One could hypothesize that a dysregulated immune system early after CAR‐T infusion hinders its effectiveness, coinciding with observations in a tumor microenvironment (TME) study, where an immunosuppressive TME inhibited CAR‐T‐cell function. 27 However, our findings contrast with those of a recent study, 28 in which NK cell recovery, but not CD4⁺ T‐cell recovery, was associated with improved PFS and OS in patients treated with axi‐cel, tisagenlecleucel, or lisocabtagene maraleucel. Several differences between our study and that of Luan et al. may partly explain this discrepancy. The most relevant include the lack of fixed time points for assessing immune cell recovery in that study, which may have increased analytical variability. Furthermore, this study did not account for cumulative steroid exposure, a factor known to impact lymphocyte recovery. Additionally, Luan et al. reported an inverse correlation between inflammatory markers, including lower levels of CRP, ferritin, TNF‐α, and IL‐10, and NK cell recovery. This suggests that NK cell recovery may serve as a surrogate marker of more effective disease control at the time of CAR‐T infusion. Further research is needed to clarify the specific contributions of individual lymphocyte subsets to CAR‐T‐cell therapy outcomes.

Finally, our findings support the utility of immune recovery monitoring after cellular therapy. The EBMT published a multicenter study on immune monitoring, in which 61% of centers acknowledged the impact of monitoring on clinical decisions, though only 24% used this information to guide prophylactic or preemptive CRS/ICANS treatment and 37% to guide anti‐infectious prophylactic strategies. 29 There is a clear need for immune monitoring guidelines, including management actions to be taken, so that CAR‐T centers can standardize practices and foster international collaboration. Prospective studies on immune reconstitution are also warranted.

Our study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the lymphocyte subpopulation analysis was restricted to CD4+, CD8+, NK, and B‐cell lymphocyte subsets, which limited a detailed characterization, such as cell activation and exhaustion, and outcome correlation. Axi‐cel CAR‐T‐cell expansion and/or persistence were not analyzed, thus precluding correlation with immune recovery. The retrospective nature and a somewhat small sample size could be seen as further drawbacks. The limited number of observations may have reduced the statistical power of our tests, and variables that may have subtle but clinically relevant impact on outcomes may have not been detected. Additionally, the potential for overfitting in the value cutoffs and predictive models for immune recovery and outcomes should be considered. Therefore, it is essential that these findings be validated in larger cohorts to enhance their reliability and generalizability. Finally, as most patients received axi‐cel in third or later lines of treatment, our findings may not be generalizable to second‐line therapy. However, our study also has notable strengths. By focusing on a single CAR‐T product and specific B‐cell histological subtypes, we ensured a uniform evaluation, enhancing the consistency of results. Moreover, we comprehensively evaluated patient and disease characteristics, along with CAR‐T management variables, enabling a robust analysis of immune recovery factors. Finally, the use of an accessible lymphocyte subset and Ig panels in daily clinical practice supports the real‐world applicability of our findings.

In conclusion, CD4+ T‐cells and IgG show slow recovery through the first year after axi‐cel, while CD8+ T‐cell and NK cell counts increase steeply during the first months. Higher mEASIX may predict slower cellular recovery, while corticosteroids may slow both cellular and humoral recovery. Lower CD4+ T‐cells are associated with worse outcomes. Harmonization and prospective studies are needed to establish the role of immune monitoring after CAR‐T in aggressive B‐cell lymphoma.
