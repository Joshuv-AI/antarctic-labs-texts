Pleural aspergillosis: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Jun Lan, Yaqi Li, Xia Zhou, Guofen Chen, Jiaorong Yang, Jia Huang, Yi Li, Shigong Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic pulmonary aspergillosis is an uncommon form of Aspergillus infection. It typically occurs in people with a weakened immune system and underlying lung disease. Our case demonstrates that pulmonary aspergillosis can occur in immunocompetent middle-aged men with a rare pleural effusion as the main imaging manifestation. A 45-year-old Chinese man with chronic exposure to wet crops presented to our emergency department with a two-year history of cough which worsened over the past 10 days. An initial chest CT showed right pleural effusion with compressive pulmonary atelectasis. Bronchoscopy was unremarkable, but next-generation sequencing of bronchoalveolar-lavage fluid revealed Aspergillus fumigatus. Chronic pulmonary aspergillosis was diagnosed based on typical personal contact history, clinical manifestations and microbiological results. The patient responded well to voriconazole and was discharged 20 days later with oral voriconazole medicine. The case illustrates that bloody pleural effusion could be a rare condition of chronic pulmonary aspergillosis.Keywords: Pleural effusion, Pleural aspergillosis, Chronic pulmonary aspergillosis

Introduction

Chronic pulmonary aspergillosis (CPA) is a fungal infection caused by inhalation of spores of Aspergillus spp. (most commonly Aspergillus fumigatus), which primarily affects immunocompetent or mildly immunocompromised patients due to an underlying lung disease (e.g., COPD, tuberculosis, non-tuberculous mycobacterial infection, or lung cancer) [1]. The 5-year mortality rate is 40-50% [2]. Global epidemiological data show that approximately 3 million people suffer from CPA, with an estimated 1.2 million suffering from CPA as a sequela of pulmonary tuberculosis (TB), 411,000 suffering from CPA as a complication of allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA) and 72,000 suffering from CPA as a complication of sarcoidosis, demonstrating the magnitude of the problem from a global health perspective [3].

Clinical symptoms of this disease include cough with or without sputum, hemoptysis, and dyspnea while those with pleural effusion are less frequently reported. In this paper, we report a case of chronic pulmonary aspergillosis with bloody pleural effusion.

Case presentation

A 45-year-old man, a farmer with long exposure to wet corn,was admitted to our hospital because of recurrent cough for two years which worsened for the past ten days. The patient reported that the cough was more pronounced during winter and springtime, and he was previously diagnosed with bronchitis. During the course of this disease, the patient's cough was aggravated at the beginning, accompanied by chest tightness and shortness of breath after activity, without fever or chills. CT of the lung at the local county hospital revealed a right pleural effusion with compression and atelectasis of the right lower lobe (Fig. 1A and B). A thoracentesis was performed, and bloody pleural effusion was drained. However, following bereavement notification of a family member, the patient exhibited symptoms of acute respiratory distress. Consequently, he underwent intubation and ventilation, and was subsequently transferred to our department. At our hospital, in terms of first physical examination: the temperature was 38.4 °C, heart rate 113 beats/min, respiratory rate 15 beats/min, blood pressure 184/107 mmHg(no vasoactive agents), SPO2 99% (invasive ventilator assisted ventilation, VC mode, FiO2 50%, VT 420ml, PEEP 8cmH2O), and low breath sounds in the right lung, fine moist rales were ausculated in the left lower lung. The right chest tube was fixed in place, and bloody drainage fluid was seen. His laboratory findings on admission revealed decrease in hemoglobin(51g/l, reference range: 139-184g/l), moderate C-reactive protein level (44.01 mg/l, reference range: 0-5 mg/l) and procalcitonin (0.58ng/ml, reference range<0.046ng/ml) and a normal level of leukocyte count and percentage of central granulocytes. The serum T-cell spot test was positive. The total number of cells in the pleural fluid was 702000 × 106/L, the number of nucleated cells was 114 × 106/L, the number of mononuclear cells was 100 × 106/L, and the number of multilobar cells was 14 × 106/L, but liquid-based cytology of the pleural fluid did not show any tumor cells, while pleural fluid cultures also showed no microbial growth. Serum (1-3)-beta-D-glucan test, galactomannan test, mycobacterium tuberculosis DNA in sputum, acid-fast staining of sputum, and sputum culture were also negative. Pulmonary CT suggested mixed density shadow in the right thoracic cavity with compressive lung atelectasis (Fig. 1C and D). Bronchoscopy was also performed, and the lower airway mucosa was smooth, with a small amount of sputum, no congestion, and no neoplasm; at the same time bronchoalveolar-lavage fluid for culture and next-generation sequencing (NGS) was prescribed.

Intravenous meropenem 1g twice daily was empirically administered, but the patient continued to have fever every day. Two days later, the NGS results of alveolar lavage fluid suggested Aspergillus fumigatus (sequence number 346), though the culture of alveolar lavage fluid was negative. Considering the clinical symptoms, chest imaging, antibiotic resistance, and NGS results, the patient could be clinically diagnosed with chronic pulmonary aspergillosis. We administered intravenous voriconazole 0.2g twice daily antifungal treatment, following which, the patient's body temperature gradually returned to normal after 2 days. The bloody drainage fluid in the chest cavity gradually decreased, and the endotracheal tube was removed after 6 days. The chest tube was removed after 8 days, and a review of lung CT after 14 days showed that the pleural effusion on the right side was significantly reduced, and the oozing was decreased compared to the previous one (Fig. 1E and F). The patient improved and was discharged with oral voriconazole 0.2g twice daily for 1 month.

Discussion

Aspergillosis is a mycotic disease caused by Aspergillus, a filamentous fungus [12]. The most common pathogenic species responsible for pulmonary disease is Aspergillus fumigatus. Aspergillus fumigatus is often found in moist soil and moldy grains. Aspergillus fumigatus can produce a large number of spores and spread over a large area through airborne transmission. The spores enter the human body via the respiratory tract and cause disease through direct infection, allergic reaction, aspergillosis and other mechanisms. It is eliminated by mucociliary clearance and innate immune mechanisms in immunocompetent hosts without underlying lung disease. However, if the host has underlying pulmonary diseases or immunosuppression, it can easily progress to pulmonary aspergillosis [13].

According to the interaction between the pathogen and the underlying lung disease and host immunity, aspergillosis can be divided into five categories: invasive pulmonary aspergillosis (IPA), tracheobronchial aspergillosis (TBA), chronic pulmonary aspergillosis (CPA), aspergillosis bronchitis, acute community-acquired aspergillosis pneumonia, and allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA) [14]. Among them, chronic pulmonary aspergillosis is more difficult to diagnose because it has no specific symptoms or imaging findings. Many clinical symptoms can be found in patients with CPA, such as productive cough, and hemoptysis, while systemic symptoms can include weight loss and fatigue, which are non-specific. Moreover, some patients can be asymptomatic. The asymptomatic period can last 2-10 years, or even longer. In some cases, CPA can be combined with other lung diseases, such as tuberculosis and COPD, which may lead to missed diagnoses. On the other hand, radiographic changes associated with CPA are also the most important evidence for the diagnosis of CPA. The most common imaging findings in CPA include cavitation (63.9%), fungal ball (36.7%), pleural thickening (32.0%), and bronchiectasis (31.3%) [15], rather than pleural effusion. We reviewed 8 articles on aspergillosis with pleural effusion as the main clinical manifestation and summarized the nature of pleural effusion (details in Table 1). We found that the nature of pleural effusion of aspergillosis was mainly exudate, and the common cells found in the effusion were lymphocytes and eosinophils. There was no obvious specificity in the biochemistry of pleural effusion. However, to our knowledge, there were no studies in the literature exploring bloody pleural effusions in CPA.

A review of the literature reveals a paucity of research addressing the mechanism of hemorrhagic pleural effusion in aspergillosis. However, the histopathology of lung nodules in patients with invasive pulmonary aspergillosis has been found to consist of coagulative necrosis of the lung tissue and acute hemorrhage. In some nodules, only acute hemorrhage without mycelium was observed [16]. Therefore, we speculate that Aspergillus can directly invade blood vessels and cause bleeding, but its invasion mechanism needs to be further explored.

In terms of laboratory tests, depending on the individual and the stage of the disease, laboratory abnormalities commonly seen in patients with CPA may vary. C-reactive protein and erythrocyte sedimentation rate may be elevated, indicating an ongoing inflammatory response in patients with CPA [1]. Still, they are not specific for the diagnosis. At the same time, the specificity and sensitivity of serum G test, GM test, and Aspergillus fumigatus IgG antibody detection also need to be verified [17], which increases the difficulty of diagnosis. Bronchoscopy, as an essential test in respiratory diseases, not only provides insight into the airway mucosa but also allows for the retention of alveolar lavage fluid to refine cultures and next-generation sequencing (NGS) tests. Among them, alveolar lavage NGS is an emerging technology that simultaneously sequences billions of nucleic acid fragments to rapidly and comprehensively detect the presence of a wide range of micro-organisms in samples, including bacteria, viruses, fungi, atypical pathogens and parasites.

This method has the advantages of being broad spectrum, low time consumption and high accuracy, which makes it possible to detect the presence of pathogens in patients with respiratory infections within 3 days. In a retrospective study of the use of metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) to diagnose pulmonary aspergillosis in non-neutropenic patients, mNGS managed to reach a sensitivity of 91.7% for diagnosing pulmonary aspergillosis, which was significantly higher than that of conventional etiological methods (33.3%) and serum (1,3)-β-D-glucan (33.3%). In addition, mNGS showed superior performance in discovering co-pathogens (84.6%) of pulmonary aspergillosis [18]. Furthermore, many case reports and studies suggest that NGS of alveolar lavage fluid can be used as an alternative diagnostic method or as a complementary method for the diagnosis of pulmonary aspergillosis and to assist the clinician in making appropriate decisions on management [17,19,20].

Notwithstanding the plethora of diagnostic tools at a medical professional's disposal, diagnosing chronic pulmonary aspergillosis remains a formidable challenge. Because chronic pulmonary aspergillosis can occur in people with normal immunity, and the early symptoms are not typical, imaging may not have obvious findings. Moreover considering the current widespread overuse of antibiotics, the positive rate of laboratory culture is declining.

This is the first time that we found a case of chronic pulmonary aspergillosis with bloody pleural effusion. We believe that pleural effusion caused by chronic pulmonary aspergillus may be due to the pleural inflammatory response caused by Aspergillus infiltration, or the invasion of the lung or pleural vessels by Aspergillus, but bloody pleural effusion is rare, and further consideration of the mechanism of this disease is needed. By reviewing the literature, we would have liked to explore whether there were specific indicators of the nature of pleural effusion in patients with Aspergillus, but unfortunately, our review was not fruitful in this regard.

Therefore, the clinical manifestations, signs and imaging of chronic pulmonary aspergillosis are diverse and non-specific, which can be easily confused with other respiratory diseases such as tuberculosis, pneumonia, lung malignant tumor, bronchiectasis and pleural disease. Thus attention should be paid to differentiating these diagnoses. A high degree of vigilance is needed when assessing patients with unexplained pleural effusion, and pulmonary aspergillus infection should be considered in these patients.

Conclusion

A patient with a personal contact history of depository and with unexplained pleural effusion, should be investigated for Aspergillus infection.
