Initial fluid resuscitation in septic shock: Reassessing the 30 mL/kg paradigm
Authors: Jean-Louis Teboul, Jiao Liu, Olfa Hamzaoui
Published: 2025

Initial fluid resuscitation remains a cornerstone in the management of septic shock, where hypovolemia is consistently present and, if uncorrected, leads to organ hypoperfusion and dysfunction via reduced cardiac output.

This hypovolemia is both absolute and relative in nature. Absolute hypovolemia arises from frequent reduced oral intake in the hours preceding hospital admission and is often compounded by external fluid losses (e.g., vomiting, diarrhea) or internal redistribution (e.g., third-spacing into the gastrointestinal tract). In addition, increased capillary permeability—partly due to the degradation of the endothelial glycocalyx[1]—may contribute to fluid extravasation into the interstitial space. Relative hypovolemia results from sepsis-induced vasodilation and an increase in venous capacitance, particularly within the splanchnic circulation.

Historically, clinical practice favored early administration of large volumes of intravenous fluids to correct presumed absolute and relative hypovolemia, even in the absence of reliable tools to assess volume status. During the 1980s and 1990s, mechanical ventilation often utilized high tidal volumes (10–15 mL/kg),[2] potentially compromising systemic venous return.[3] Simultaneously, mean arterial pressure (MAP) targets were frequently set above 80 mmHg,[4] and vasopressors were typically initiated only after completion of fluid administration.[5] Moreover, strategies aiming to maximize cardiac output—popularized by the works of Shoemaker et al.[6] in high-risk surgical patients—further encouraged liberal fluid resuscitation. Together, these factors have likely contributed to the widespread adoption of aggressive initial fluid loading in septic shock.

Contemporary evidence has driven major shifts in practice—including lower tidal volume ventilation, lower MAP targets (65–70 mmHg), early vasopressor initiation, and the abandonment of routine cardiac output maximization.

Although an aggressive initial fluid resuscitation mindset still persists among some clinicians, this uniform liberal approach is now considered outdated, particularly in the absence of objective volume assessment. Recent studies have demonstrated that a substantial proportion of patients with septic shock become fluid-unresponsive shortly after initiation of resuscitation,[7] suggesting that their initial fluid requirements were rather limited. In patients with underlying cardiovascular comorbidities, administration of 30 mL/kg of crystalloids within 3 h may pose additional risks. Furthermore, the deleterious effects of fluid accumulation in septic patients are now well established,[8] and excessive fluid administration may exacerbate endothelial injury and glycocalyx disruption.[1]

Although indiscriminate fluid administration is clearly inappropriate, overly restrictive strategies may also be harmful, especially in patients with profound hypovolemia due to substantial fluid losses or abdominal sepsis.

An individualized, balanced approach to initial resuscitation is therefore essential, though international guidelines have issued divergent recommendations.

The Surviving Sepsis Campaign (SSC) recommends administering at least 30 mL/kg of intravenous crystalloids within the first 3 h (weak recommendation, low-quality evidence).[9] This guidance is primarily based on a retrospective observational study that found increased mortality in patients who received less than this volume.[10] However, closer scrutiny of this study reveals that most patients did not fulfill strict criteria for septic shock. Notably, 79% never required vasopressors, and 85% were never mechanically ventilated. Another rationale for the 30 mL/kg threshold is that it mirrors the average volume of fluids administered before randomization in large randomized controlled trials (RCTs) such as Protocolized Care for Early Septic Shock (ProCESS).[11] However, this justification is tenuous, as RCTs are conducted under highly controlled conditions that do not reflect real-world heterogeneity. Moreover, the standard deviation in fluid volumes administered was substantial (±20 mL/kg), indicating marked variability among patients even in this selected population.[11] Recommending a minimum of 30 mL/kg without individualized evaluation also implicitly assumes that all patients have an absolute volume deficit exceeding two-thirds of their plasma volume (approximately 45 mL/kg)—a presumption that clearly does not hold for every case.

In contrast, the European Society of Intensive Care Medicine (ESICM) has adopted a more cautious approach.[12] In response to the PICO (Patient, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome) question, “Should 30 mL/kg of fluid be used versus other approaches for initial resuscitation in critically ill patients with sepsis or septic shock?,” the ESICM guideline suggests administering up to 30 mL/kg of intravenous crystalloids during the initial phase of resuscitation (typically within the first 3 h), depending on the clinical context (conditional recommendation, very low certainty of evidence). Moreover, the accompanying commentary underscores: (1) the importance of tailoring fluid administration to the individual patient’s characteristics and clinical context and (2) the need to assess fluid responsiveness, when feasible, before further fluid administration, recognizing that some patients may require more than 30 mL/kg, while others may require significantly less.[12]

Thus, while the SSC experts suggest a minimum of 30 mL/kg,[9] ESICM suggests a maximum of 30 mL/kg during the initial resuscitation period, leaving room for individualized adjustments based on dynamic clinical evaluation and patient-specific factors.[12]

These opposing recommendations highlight the central role of individualized initial fluid management, particularly in the context of septic shock, where the first few hours of care are critical to patient outcomes. Estimating fluid requirements based solely on body weight is insufficient, especially given the heterogeneity of sepsis and the wide variability in the degree of hypovolemia across patients.

Ideally, bedside-applicable subphenotypes—such as preload responsiveness and venous congestion states—can help guide optimal fluid administration strategies, even without advanced hemodynamic monitoring. When feasible, repeated use of dynamic indicators of preload responsiveness allows clinicians to identify the point at which a patient becomes fluid unresponsive, thereby helping to prevent excessive fluid accumulation.[8] In this context, the passive leg raising test can be useful, especially when evaluating changes in stroke volume surrogates such as changes in pulse pressure,[13] or more accurately, changes in velocity-time integral measured by transthoracic echocardiography.[14] Regardless of the approach, clinicians should always consider the broader clinical picture, including the source of infection, comorbidities, estimated fluid losses, and readily available hemodynamic markers – such as pulse pressure.[15] Most importantly, fluid management should be guided by frequent hemodynamic reassessment, rather than waiting the full 3 h.
