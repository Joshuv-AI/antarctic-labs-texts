Russula nuda, a new species of Russula subsect. Xerampelinae (Russulaceae) from Japan
Authors: Masaya Shimizu, Akio Tonouchi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA new species, Russula nuda, from Japan is described and illustrated. The species is characterized by a glabrous, white pileus with a pale brown center, a white stipe, a context that turns brown when bruised or dried, a pale olive reaction to FeSO4, a fishy odor, and globose to ellipsoid basidiospores with numerous isolated warts. It grows in deciduous broadleaf forests dominated by Japanese beech (Fagus crenata). Maximum likelihood phylogenetic analyses were performed using an ITS dataset and an LSU-rpb2-tef-1a dataset. Based on morphological and molecular evidence, we place this new taxon within Russula subsection Xerampelinae.Keywords: Basidiomycota, molecular phylogeny, morphology, new taxon, taxonomy

The genus Russula Pers. (1796) is a large group of ectomycorrhizal fungi in the family Russulaceae, with a worldwide distribution (Buyck et al., 2018; Looney et al., 2018). This genus exhibits significant diversity in macro- and micro-morphological characteristics as well as chemical properties (Buyck et al., 2018; Romagnesi, 1967; Sarnari, 1998; Singer, 1986). Currently, Russula is classified into nine subgenera (Buyck et al., 2018, 2020, 2024).

Russula subgenus Russula, as emended by Buyck & V. Hofst., is primarily characterized by a context that either remains unchanged or undergoes yellowing, browning, reddening, graying, or blacking when bruised; sometimes with distinct odors ranging from agreeable to unpleasant; mild to strongly acrid tastes; white to yellow spore prints; basidiospores with an amyloid suprahilar spot; and one- to multi-celled gloeocystidia (Buyck et al., 2018). This subgenus is further divided into a core and a crown clade (Adamčík et al., 2019).

Russula subsect. Xerampelinae Singer, which belongs to the crown clade (Adamčík et al., 2019), is mainly characterized by a fishy odor, a context that turns yellow or brown when bruised or dried, and a blue-green to olive-green or gray-green reaction to FeSO4 (Singer, 1986). Several species within this subsection have been described based on morphological and molecular phylogenetic analyses. In China, these include Russula paragraveolens S.H. Wang, G.J. Li, R.L. Zhao & B. Cao, R. paraxerampelina C.Y. Niu, T.Z. Liu & G.J. Li, R. prunicolor C.Y. Niu, T.Z. Liu & G.J. Li, and R. pseudograveolens S.H. Wang, G.J. Li, R.L. Zhao & B. Cao (Niu et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2023). In Mexico, R. flavobrunnescens A. Kong & Buyck, R. madrensis A. Kong & Buyck, and R. sancti-pauli A. Kong & Buyck have been reported (Adamčík et al., 2019). From the United States, R. olympiana Noffsinger & Buyck and R. neopascua Noffsinger & C.L. Cripps have been described, while R. lapponica H. Kaufm. & Weholt is known from Sweden (Noffsinger et al., 2024). However, in Japan, the diversity of this subsection remains largely unexplored, with only R. xerampelina (Schaeff.) Fr. reported to date (Imazeki & Hongo, 1989; Katumoto, 2010). During a survey of the Japanese species of R. subsect. Xerampelinae, we collected white basidiomata with a fishy odor and a mild taste in Aomori Prefecture, northern Japan. Based on morphological and molecular phylogenetic analyses, we propose these newly collected specimens as a new taxon.

Basidiomata were collected in 2023 and 2024. Notes and photographs were taken to document their macromorphological characteristics. Specimens were dried using a food dehydrator (Marutaka, Tokyo, Japan) and deposited in the Herbarium Hirosaki University, Fungi (HHUF), Hirosaki City, Aomori Prefecture, Japan. Color designations followed the Color Ruler by Munsell System (expanded version; Japan Color Enterprise, Tokyo, Japan).

Fresh or dried basidiomata were tested for chemical reactions using 10% (w/v) FeSO4 solution, 10% KOH solution, and sulfovanillin (SV) solution. Microscopic observations of the fungal structures were conducted using an Olympus BX-50 light microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) under a 100× oil immersion objective lens. Basidiospores were examined in Melzer's reagent to assess amyloid reactions, while other microscopic features were observed after staining small sections with 3% KOH solution. The pileipellis was tested with cresyl blue solution to detect ortho- or metachromatic reactions, following the method of Buyck (1989). The presence or absence of acid-resistant incrustations on the cap cuticle was determined according to Romagnesi (1967). A section of the cap cuticle was immersed in carbol fuchsin for 10 min, treated with 10% HCl solution for 1 min, and then mounted in sterile water for observation. Cystidial contents were tested using sulfovanillin solution following the method of Caboň et al. (2017). Pleurocystidium density was estimated according to Buyck (1991), while spore ornamentation density was assessed using the method of Adamčík and Marhold (2000). Measurements of the basidiospores and the lamellae are reported as (MIN−)AV−SD−AV+SD(−MAX) for length and width, where MIN is the minimum, MAX is the maximum, AV is the average, and SD is the standard deviation. The Q value represents the length-to-width ratio of the basidiospores. Measurements were taken from 100 basidiospores across two specimens, while all other microscopic characteristics were measured 30 times. Hyphal terminations and pileocystidia were examined near both the pileus edge and center. For scanning electron microscopy (SEM), small fragments of dried lamellae were mounted on double-sided adhesive tape on an aluminum stub. The samples were coated with platinum-palladium using a JFC-1600 auto fine coater (JEOL, Akishima, Japan) and examined under a JSM-7000F microscope (JEOL) at an accelerating voltage of 5.0 kV to observe the surface ornamentation of the basidiospores.

Genomic DNA was extracted from dried basidiomata using MightyPrepTM reagent for DNA (TaKaRa Bio, Kusatsu, Japan) following the manufacturer's instructions. Four loci were targeted for PCR amplification and sequencing: (i) the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region of the nuclear ribosomal DNA, (ii) the nuclear ribosomal large subunit (LSU) gene, (iii) the second-largest subunit of RNA polymerase II (rpb2) gene, and (iv) the translation elongation factor 1-alpha (tef-1a) gene. The ITS region was amplified using the primer pair ITS1F/ITS4 (Gardes & Bruns, 1993; White et al., 1990), while the LSU region was amplified using the primer pair LR0R/LR5 (Cubeta et al., 1991; Vilgalys & Hester, 1990). The rpb2 region was amplified using the primer pair bRPB2-6F/bRPB2-7R (Matheny, 2005), and the tef-1a region was amplified in two overlapping segments with the primer pairs EF1-983F/EF1-1567R and EF1-1577F/EF1-2218R (Rehner & Buckley, 2005). PCR reactions were carried out using MightyAmpTM DNA Polymerase Ver.3 (TaKaRa Bio) under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 98 °C for 2 min, followed by 35 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s (denaturation), 55 °C for 15 s (annealing), and 68 °C for 1 min (elongation), with a final extension at 68 °C for 5 min. The resulting PCR products were purified using a Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (Nippon Genetics, Tokyo, Japan). DNA sequencing was performed using a Big Dye Terminator 3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) on an ABI 3700 automated DNA Sequencer (Applied Biosystems).

A total of 16 newly obtained DNA sequences (four each for ITS, LSU, rpb2, and tef-1a) were deposited in DDBJ/EMBL/GenBank (Supplementary Tables S1, S2). For phylogenetic analyses of the ITS and combined LSU-rpb2-tef-1a datasets, ingroup taxa were selected from both the newly generated sequences obtained in this study and previously published datasets (Adamčik et al., 2019; Niu et al., 2024; Noffsinger et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2023). Based on the results of Wang et al. (2023), R. emetica [voucher: lw81 (TUB), UPS (UE05.10.2003-11)] was chosen as outgroup species for the ITS dataset, while R. raoultii (strain: 561/BB 08.674) and R. viscida (strain: 425/BB 07.298) were used as outgroup species for the LSU-rpb2-tef-1a dataset. All sequences were aligned and trimmed using MEGA version 11 (Tamura et al., 2021). Unreliable aligned regions were identified and removed using the -automated1 option in TrimAl v. 1.2 (Capella-Gutiérrez et al., 2009). Maximum Likelihood (ML) phylogenetic trees were reconstructed using IQ-TREE v. 1.6.12. (Nguyen et al., 2015). The best-fit nucleotide substitution models were selected using ModelFinder (Kalyaanamoorthy et al., 2017), based on the corrected Akaike Information Criterion (AICc) (Sugiura, 1978). The TIM2e+R3 model was applied to ITS, TN+F+R4 to LSU, TIM2+F+R3 to rpb2, and K2P+G4 to tef-1a. Branch support values were estimated using 1000 ultrafast bootstrap (UFBoot) replicates (Minh et al., 2013), along with the SH-aLRT method (Guindon et al., 2010), and Approximate Bayes (aBayes) support (Anisimova et al., 2011).

The ITS dataset comprised 53 taxa and 615 aligned nucleotide positions, while the LSU-rpb2-tef-1a dataset included 50 taxa and 1969 aligned nucleotide positions. The alignment and tree files have been deposited in Figshare (doi: 10.6084/m9.figshare.28327424).

In the ITS phylogenetic tree (Fig. 1), R. subsect. Xerampelinae formed a monophyletic group with strong support (SH-aLRT = 94, aBayes = 1, UFBoot = 99). Within this group, our four specimens clustered together in a highly supported clade (SH-aLRT = 100, aBayes = 1, UFBoot = 100), clearly distinct from other known species in this subsection. Similarly, in the LSU-rpb2-tef-1a phylogenetic tree (Fig. 2), R. subsect. Xerampelinae also formed a monophyletic group with strong support (SH-aLRT = 100, aBayes = 1, UFBoot = 100). Once again, our specimens formed a well-supported clade (SH-aLRT = 100, aBayes = 1, UFBoot = 100), distinct from previously described taxa.

Diagnosis: Russula nuda is distinguished from other species in Russula subsect. Xerampelinae by its glabrous, white pileus with a pale brown center, white stipe, and globose to ellipsoid basidiospores, with numerous isolated warts, growing in deciduous broadleaf forests dominated by Fagus crenata.

Type: JAPAN, Aomori Pref., Nakatsugaru-gun, Nishimeya-mura, Shirakami Natural Science Park, elev. 296 m, 15 Jun 2024, M. Shimizu (holotype, HHUF 30690).

Gene sequences ex-holotype: LC856608 (ITS), LC856612 (LSU), LC856616 (rpb2), LC856620 (tef-1a).

Etymology: From the Latin “nuda”, meaning a glabrous pileal surface.

Japanese common name: “Buna-no-yukishiro-hatsu” (referring to its white basidiomata found in deciduous broadleaf forests dominated by Fagus crenata).

Description: Basidiomata small- to medium-sized. Pileus 35-80 mm diam, hemispherical when young and plano-convex with a depressed center when mature; peeling to 1/2-2/3 of the radius, cuticle glabrous, slightly viscid when wet, white (N9.5) to white (10YR9/1) near the pileus margin, very pale yellow (2.5Y9/2), pale brown (2.5Y8/4), or yellow (2.5Y8/8) near the pileus center. Lamellae 3.3-7 mm broad, adnate, edges entire, subdistant to close, white (N9.5) when young, becoming very pale yellow (2.5Y9/2) to pale brown (2.5Y8/4) when mature, forked, lamellulae present, taste mild. Stipe 35-94 × 10-25 mm, central or excentric, cylindrical, subclavate or clavate, longitudinally rugulose, annulus absent, surface dry, white (N9.5), bullous to stuffed. Context white (N9.5), firm to fragile, turning reddish-yellow (7.5YR6/8) to strong brown (7.5YR5/8) when bruised or dried, pale olive (5Y6/4) with FeSO4, pale yellow (5Y8/3) with KOH, and dusky-red (7.5R3/4) with SV, taste mild, odor fishy when dried. Spore print not obtained.

Basidiospores (6.1-)7.3-8.3(-9.2) × (5.5-)6-6.7(-7.3) µm, 7.8 × 6.4 μm on average, globose to ellipsoid, Q = (1.03-)1.13-1.32(-1.49), 1.22 on average, ornamentation consists of relatively large, moderately distant to very dense [ (5-)7-11(-14) warts in a 3 µm circle] amyloid warts, (0.6-)0.8-1.2(-1.3) µm high, predominantly isolated, but occasionally fused [ (0-)2-4 fusions in the circle], line connections absent or rarely present, suprahilar spot moderately large, amyloid. Basidia (31-)37-45(-49) × (9-)11-14(-16) µm, 40.7 × 12.6 μm on average, subclavate to clavate, thin-walled, 4-spored, sterigmata up to 10 µm long. Basidiola (23-)28-38(-41) × (8-)9-11(-12) µm, 33.3 × 10.2 μm on average, cylindrical or subclavate, thin-walled. Pleurocystidia dispersed ca. 500-600/mm2, (49-)59-76(-90) × (10-)12-16(-19) µm, 67.3 × 14.2 μm on average, cylindrical, clavate, fusiform, or lanceolate, apically acute or obtuse, occasionally with a 4-14 µm long appendage, thin-walled; completely or partly filled with heteromorphous, fibrillose, or crystalline content, turning weak red (5R5/4) in SV. Cheilocystidia smaller, (44-)50-68(-79) × (9-)10-13(-15) µm, 59.1 × 11.5 μm on average, cylindrical, clavate, fusiform, or lanceolate, apically acute or obtuse, occasionally with a 2-4 µm long appendage, thin-walled; completely or partly filled with heteromorphous, fibrillose to crystalline content, turning weak red (5R5/4) in SV. Marginal cells well differentiated, (15-)19-27(-30) × (3-)4-6(-7) µm, 23.0 × 5.2 µm on average, cylindrical or lageniform, apically mainly obtuse, constricted or attenuated, thin-walled. Pileipellis orthochromatic in cresyl blue, not sharply delimited from the underlying context, 110-170 µm thick, two-layered, suprapellis 40-85 µm thick, composed of suberect hyphal terminations forming a trichoderm, subpellis 40-100 µm thick, composed of horizontal, irregularly oriented, dense, pilear hyphae. Acid-resistant incrustations absent. Hyphal terminations near the pileus margin slightly flexous, thin-walled; terminal cells (16-)21-34(-42) × (3-)3.5-5(-5.5) µm, 27.8 × 4.0 µm on average, mainly subulate or lageniform, sometimes subcylindrical, often apically attenuated; subterminal cells usually equally wide (3-5 µm). Hyphal terminations near the pileus center slightly flexous, thin-walled; terminal cells (10-)15-27(-40) × (3-)3.5-6(-7.5) µm, 21.5 × 4.9 µm on average, mainly subcylindrical or lageniform, sometimes subulate, often apically attenuated; subterminal cells usually equally wide (3-8 µm). Pileocystidia near the pileus margin thin-walled, 1-4-celled; terminal cells (26-)39-69(-78) × (4.5-)5.5-8(-10) µm, 54.3 × 6.8 µm on average, cylindrical, clavate, or fusiform, mainly apically obtuse; completely or partly filled with heteromorphous, fibrillose to crystalline content, weak red (5R5/4) to gray (5Y5/1) in SV. Pileocystidia near the pileus center thin-walled, 1-3-celled, smaller; terminal cells (25-)28-57(-72) × (3.5-)5.5-8.5(-10) µm, 42.8 × 6.9 µm on average, cylindrical, clavate, or fusiform, mainly apically obtuse; completely or partly filled with heteromorphous, fibrillose to crystalline content, weak red (5R5/4) to gray (5Y5/1) in SV. Clamp connection absent in all tissues.

Habitat and distribution: Solitary or scattered on soil in deciduous forests dominated by Fagus crenata, or near Betula maximowicziana, F. crenata, and Quercus crispula. It occurs in summer (June to July). Currently known only from Aomori Prefecture in northern Japan.

Additional specimens examined: JAPAN, Aomori Pref., Nakatsugaru-gun, Nishimeya-mura, Shirakami Natural Science Park, elev. 296 m, 15 Jun 2024, M. Shimizu, HHUF 30688 and HHUF 30689; Aomori City, Namioka, Bonzyu-san, elev. 181 m, 16 Jun 2024, M. Shimizu, HHUF 30691.

A combination of characteristics, including a fishy odor, a context that turns brown when bruised or dried, and a pale olive reaction to FeSO4, supports the placement of R. nuda in R. subsect. Xerampelinae (Singer, 1986). This is further supported by ITS and LSU-rpb2-tef-1a phylogenetic trees (Figs. 1, 2), which confirm its distinction from other known species within this subsection.

Macromorphologically, R. nuda resembles R. felleicolor Bon & Jamoni and R. metachroa Hongo, both of which exhibit white to pale brown pilei. However, R. felleicolor differs from R. nuda by its deep green FeSO4 reaction and its preference for subalpine Salix forests (Kibby, 2021). Russula metachroa is distinguished from R. nuda by its pruinose pileus surface, strongly acrid taste, larger basidiospores (9-12 × 6.5-8.5 µm or 8-11 × 7-9 µm), and occurrence in mixed forests dominated by Pinus densiflora and Quercus serrata (Imazeki & Hongo, 1989). Russula xerampelina shares the same fishy odor as R. nuda, but differs in its red pileus with a blackish center, partially or completely red stipe, and basidiospores with warts connected by short, scattered lines. It predominantly grows in coniferous forests (Kibby, 2021; Kränzlin, 2005). Additionally, R. faginea Romagn., which grows in Fagus-dominated forests like R. nuda, can be distinguished from R. nuda by its wine-red to copper-red pileus, with yellow or brown tones intermixed, and its dark green FeSO4 reaction (Kibby, 2021; Kränzlin, 2005). These distinct features further support the classification of R. nuda as a novel species within Russula subsect. Xerampelinae, emphasizing its morphological and ecological uniqueness.
