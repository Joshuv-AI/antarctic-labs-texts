Intestinal Angioedema Induced by Promethazine in a Postoperative Patient With Gastric Cancer: A Case Report
Authors: Peng Xu, Sanbao Shi, Qichao Liu, Rui Ma, Cheng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntestinal angioedema is a rare but severe manifestation of hypersensitivity that mimics acute abdomen. This first reported case of promethazine‐induced intestinal angioedema underscores the importance of recognizing drug‐induced etiologies to prevent unnecessary surgery and enable conservative management.Keywords: case report, gastric cancer, intestinal angioedema, postoperative, promethazine

Introduction

The gastrointestinal tract is highly vulnerable to pathologic insults, manifesting interstitial fluid accumulation, dysmotility, and compromised epithelial barrier function [1]. Intestinal edema commonly occurs in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), gastroschisis, and cirrhosis [2].

Allergic angioedema can extend to the bowel, resulting in intestinal edema [3]. Such presentations are readily mistaken for other etiologies. Here, we describe a rare case of promethazine‐induced intestinal angioedema in a postoperative gastric‐cancer patient.

Case Presentation

A 69‐year‐old woman presented with hematochezia and lower‐abdominal pain for 1 day. Six months earlier, she had undergone a laparoscopic radical gastrectomy with overlap gastrojejunostomy and side‐to‐side jejunojejunostomy for poorly differentiated gastric adenocarcinoma. She subsequently completed four cycles of XELOX chemotherapy. Cycle 5 was deferred 3 days earlier because of thrombocytopenia. She was readmitted within 24 h of discharge with recurrent bleeding and abdominal pain. The patient had no history of allergic reactions to food, medications, or antihistamines, including promethazine.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Management

Admission laboratory data: white blood cell (WBC) count 13 × 109 L−1, neutrophils 83.3%, absolute neutrophil count 10.83 × 109 L−1. Albmin level (ALB) 40.6 g/L, C‐reactive protein C (CRP) 51.32 mg/L, PT 11.7 s, PTA 91.7%, FIB 4.19 g/L, D‐Dimer 12.91 mg/L, INR 1.02, APTT 28.7 s, TT 15.7 s, FDR 42.57 μg/mL. Contrast‐enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen and pelvis revealed diffuse colonic‐wall edema, free pelvic fluid, and hazy fat stranding with fluid‐density collections (Figure 1A). No endoscopic evaluation was undertaken. Physical examination revealed stable hemodynamics, a soft, non‐distended abdomen devoid of peritoneal signs, active bowel sounds, and spontaneous stool passage. Serum lactate, a marker for intestinal ischemia, was not measured. However, stable hemodynamics and absence of peritoneal signs made acute bowel necrosis unlikely. C1‐esterase inhibitor (C1‐INH) deficiency—associated with hereditary or acquired angioedema—was considered unlikely given the patient's advanced age at onset, absence of family history, and temporal association with promethazine administration. C1‐INH testing was therefore not performed. Review of the medication record revealed that 10 mg of intravenous promethazine hydrochloride had been administered to treat nausea and vomiting before symptom onset. Given recent surgery and chemotherapy, acute mesenteric ischemia, intestinal perforation, and severe infectious colitis (e.g., Clostridioides difficile) were initially considered. However, absent peritoneal signs and stable hemodynamics argued against perforation or advanced ischemia. CT findings diffuse symmetric edema without pneumoperitoneum, bowel wall pneumatosis, or large vessel occlusion—supported a non‐ischemic, non‐surgical etiology. The temporal correlation between symptom onset and promethazine administration suggested a drug‐induced hypersensitivity reaction. Based on the temporal correlation among drug administration, CT findings of diffuse colonic‐wall thickening and peri colonic fat stranding, and peripheral neutrophilia, we diagnosed promethazine‐associated intestinal angioedema.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

Conservative management with nil per os (NPO) and total parenteral nutrition (TPN) was initiated to minimize intestinal workload and reduce mechanical irritation to the edematous bowel. A 5‐day course allowed submucosal edema and inflammation to resolve, coinciding with symptom resolution (abdominal pain, melena) and confirmed by follow‐up CT. The repeat CT revealed resolution of colonic‐wall oedema and pelvic fluid (Figure 1B). Laboratory parameters returned to normal (WBC 5.6 × 109 L−1; neutrophils 53.9%, and absolute neutrophil count 3.02 × 109 L−1. ALB 36.0 g/L, CRP 36.43 mg/L, PT:12.0 s, PTA 86.8%, FIB 4.58 g/L, D‐Dimer 10.45 mg/L, INR 1.05, APTT 27.9 s, TT 15.8 s, FDR 32.94 μg/mL). Oral intake was cautiously resumed, and the patient was discharged without relapse.

Discussion

Intestinal angioedema develops when fluid accumulates within the submucosal layer of the bowel wall, producing mural thickening [4]. Gastro‐intestinal involvement is often insidious, mimicking acute abdomen characterized by vague pain, nausea, or vomiting that may prompt unnecessary laparotomy [5]. Rarely, massive transudation into the intestinal lumen, bowel wall, and peritoneal cavity can rapidly deplete intravascular volume, precipitating hypovolemic shock [6]. Drug‐induced intestinal angioedema has been reported previously. Angiotensin‐converting‐enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs) are the most frequently implicated agents; they precipitate angioedema by inhibiting bradykinin and substance‐P degradation, thereby increasing vascular permeability [7, 8, 9]. While ACE inhibitor‐induced intestinal angioedema is driven by bradykinin accumulation, NSAID‐induced cases involve leukotriene synthesis [10, 11]. Promethazine‐induced angioedema likely represents histamine‐mediated hypersensitivity [12]. Despite distinct mechanisms, these drug reactions share clinical features: presentation mimicking acute abdomen, risking unnecessary laparotomy; CT findings of circumferential bowel wall thickening and submucosal edema enabling non‐invasive diagnosis; and prompt drug discontinuation as the cornerstone of management, typically yielding rapid resolution without surgery.

Promethazine is an H1‐receptor antagonist with antihistaminic, antimuscarinic, antiemetic, and sedative effects. It is widely used to control nausea and vomiting and to treat allergic conditions [13, 14]. Rare case reports describe H1‐antihistamine‐associated hypersensitivity reactions, most commonly allergic contact dermatitis [15]. However, intestinal angioedema attributable to promethazine has not previously been described. The present case is, to our knowledge, the first report of promethazine‐induced intestinal angioedema.

Although promethazine is widely prescribed for allergic disorders, it can rarely precipitate anaphylaxis and intestinal angioedema. Physicians should therefore consider this adverse reaction in the differential diagnosis when abdominal pain and hematochezia follow administration of promethazine and should exclude mesenteric thrombosis, perforation, and colonic malignancy to minimize diagnostic error.
