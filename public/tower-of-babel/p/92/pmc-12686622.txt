Mutagenesis of the PALB2 WD40 domain identifies variants defective in interaction with BRCA2 and DNA repair
Authors: Thiago T Gomes, Anna Beatriz R Elias, Huaizhi Huang, Chunling Hu, Thales C Nepomuceno, Giuliana de Gregoriis, Daniel S Chrispim, Larissa B Skaf, Fernanda ARL Szpunar, Guilherme Suarez-Kurtz, Fergus J Couch, Alvaro NA Monteiro, Marcelo A Carvalho
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPALB2 (Partner and Localizer of BRCA2) is a tumor suppressor gene in which germline pathogenic variants predispose to breast and pancreatic cancer. The PALB2 protein is critical for maintaining genome integrity and plays a significant role in homologous recombination DNA repair by recruiting BRCA2 to sites of damage via the PALB2 WD40 domain. However, the impact of most PALB2 WD40 missense variants on PALB2 activity, the BRCA2 interaction, and predisposition to cancer remains poorly explored. Here, we assessed the functional impact of 78 PALB2 missense variants reported as variants of uncertain clinical significance or with conflicting interpretation in ClinVar. We identified variants that disrupt binding to BRCA2, promote PALB2 subcellular mislocalization, and protein instability. We also identified nine variants with a severe impact on BRCA2 binding and homologous recombination repair. We propose that these variants, which consistently show loss of function in different functional assays, represent pathogenic cancer predisposing variants.Keywords: cancer, cancer biology, breast cancer, DNA repair, PALB2, VUS, functional analysis

PALB2 (Partner and Localizer of BRCA2, also known as FANC-N [Fanconi anemia complementation group N]) is a tumor suppressor gene associated with inherited genetic disorders. Germline biallelic inactivation of PALB2 is related to a rare subtype of Fanconi anemia syndrome, whereas its monoallelic inactivation substantially increases the lifetime risk of breast and ovarian cancer (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7). In addition, germline pathogenic variants in PALB2 are associated with hereditary pancreatic cancer and are suspected to increase chordoma susceptibility (8, 9). Family based studies focusing on nonsense and frameshift variants indicate that carriers of PALB2 loss-of-function variants may face a cumulative lifetime risk of developing breast cancer that varies from 30% to 60%, depending on family history, similar to individuals carrying BRCA2 pathogenic variants (10, 11, 12, 13).

Approximately half of all documented PALB2 variants are missense mutations that result in single amino acid substitutions, yet information on their impact on protein functionality is often lacking (14). Notably, 98% of PALB2 missense variants in the ClinVar database are currently classified as variants of uncertain clinical significance (VUS) or have conflicting classifications (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/). To date, the p.Arg1117Lys variant is the only PALB2 missense alteration that is recognized as likely pathogenic or pathogenic by an expert panel (ClinVar, three gold stars) in ClinVar. However, the mechanism by which this variant leads to loss of function is due to aberrant splicing (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/). The absence of established clinically relevant pathogenic missense variants poses a significant challenge in the clinical classification of PALB2 VUS.

PALB2 encodes a protein that plays a pivotal role in the maintenance of genomic integrity by promoting DNA repair through homologous recombination (HR) (15). It functions as a molecular bridge between the two major breast cancer susceptibility gene products, BRCA1 and BRCA2 (16, 17, 18). The BRCA1–PALB2–BRCA2 protein complex mediates RAD51 recruitment to DNA-damaged sites, promoting accurate HR repair and tumor suppression (17, 18). PALB2 N-terminal (NT) coiled-coil motif heterodimerizes with BRCA1, whereas its C-terminal (CT) WD40 domain binds the NT region of BRCA2 (15, 17, 18). Moreover, PALB2 directly interacts with RAD51 to efficiently promote its recombinase activity (19, 20).

Biochemical-, cellular-, and family-based studies underscore the critical role of these interactions to cancer suppression (15, 21, 22, 23, 24). Previously, we and others applied systematic analyses to functionally characterize 136 PALB2 missense VUS. All missense variants with consistent functional impact were located in the coiled-coil motif and in the WD40 domain of the PALB2 protein (21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26).

The WD40 domain of PALB2 is folded in a β-propeller structure composed of seven blades, with the specific region for BRCA2 binding residing within the interface of the fourth and fifth blades (27). Mutations occurring outside the recognized BRCA2-binding pocket, such as p.Trp912Gly, p.Gly937Arg, and p.Ile944Asn, can lead to defects in HR repair, potentially because of significant alterations in the WD40 domain structure (21, 22, 23, 28). Indeed, the cancer-associated PALB2 p.Trp1038ter variant results in structural damage to the WD40 domain. This structural impairment exposes a hidden conserved nuclear export signal leading to cytoplasmic accumulation (26). However, the structural determinants of PALB2 function are still poorly known.

Here, we assessed the functional impact of 78 naturally occurring missense variants located in the WD40 domain reported in ClinVar (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/). Our data reveal several PALB2 variants with severely compromised function that are likely to constitute variants associated with elevated breast and ovarian cancer risk.

Results

Variant selection

We evaluated missense variants located in the WD40 domain reported in ClinVar (N = 434 as of March 2021) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/) using the in silico predictor Align GVGD, which uses Grantham difference and Grantham variation to predict functional effects of missense variants (29, 30, 31). Align GVGD defines a sequence of grades (C65, C55, C45, C35, C25, C15, and C0) from greatest impact (C65) to least (C0) (29). We identified 72 C25–C65 VUS that had not been functionally evaluated (Fig. 1A and Table S1) (21, 22, 23). We also included two missense variants from this region (amino acids 859–1186) that have been classified as likely benign/benign (LB/B) in ClinVar (p.Gly998Glu and p.Val932Met) and other three missense variants previously analyzed with an intermediate phenotype (p.Gly1043Ala, p.Leu1119Pro, and p.Trp1140Arg) (21). Because there are no missense variants classified as LP/P (likely pathogenic/pathogenic) in this region, we used the synthetic p.Ala1025Arg and the naturally occurring p.Thr030Ile, which was previously shown to have compromised activity in HR (21), as loss-of-function controls.

BRCA2 interaction analysis

We conducted a quantitative analysis of the PALB2 WD40 domain–binding activity to the BRCA2 N terminus (amino acids 1–60) using a previously described mammalian two-hybrid (M2H) approach (Fig. 1B) (9, 21, 22). The missense variants displayed a range of BRCA2 binding activity from comparable to WT to almost complete loss of interaction (<3% of the WT binding activity) (Figs. 2A and S1A). In addition to the two LB/B controls, 16 variants exhibited binding activity that was not statistically different from the WT PALB2 fragment (Figs. 2A and S1A). Fourteen variants (p.Glu943Lys, p.Gly971Arg, p.Leu972Pro, p.Arg976Thr, p.Leu977Pro, p.Val991Gly, p.Phe1016Ser, p.Gly1028Val, p.Gly1028Asp, p.Thr1030Asn, p.Ile1035Asn, p.Gly1068Trp, p.Asn1096Lys, and p.Pro1097Arg) showed less than 5% of BRCA2-binding activity, similar to loss-of-function controls (p.Thr1030Ile and p.Ala1025Arg) (Fig. 2A). Forty-four variants displayed intermediate (between 5% and 70% of WT activity) binding activity, suggesting that a subset may have sufficient hypomorphic effects to influence cancer risk (Fig. 2A). Nonconservative amino acid substitutions in propeller blades 3 to 5 and in residues implicated in the interaction with BRCA2 (Fig. 2A, red arrows) showed a more pronounced effect on binding. While only 2/12 (16.6%) variants tested in loops (Fig. 2A, squares), 13/63 (20.1%) variants tested in β-sheets led to loss of interaction with BRCA2 (<5% of WT activity). Propeller blades 3 to 5 also contained most of the variants defective in BRCA2 binding in the set tested (Fig. 2B). Immunoblotting analysis indicated that protein instability of the VP16-fused PALB2 WD40 domain was not a common mechanism underlying the functional impact of these variants, except for p.Lys1163Ile and p.Tyr1185Asp (Fig. 2C).

PALB2 variant subcellular localization

Many loss-of-function PALB2 WD40 variants were located outside the PALB2–BRCA2 interaction region (Val1019–Leu1069) (27). Since these variants displayed levels of expression comparable to the WT protein (Fig. 2B), we hypothesized that impairment of binding could be due to protein mislocalization. To test this hypothesis, we transiently expressed human embryonic kidney 293FT (HEK293FT) cells with GFP-tagged full-length (FL) PALB2 missense variants and assessed cytoplasmic and nuclear localization of GFP-tagged PALB2 variants using fluorescence microscopy (Fig. 3A). Examining the results of the BRCA2-binding assay, we selected 29 PALB2 variants, which included four variants with WT-like behavior, 14 loss-of-function (<5% of WT activity), and 11 hypomorphic variants representing the range of variation (Fig. S1). We also included the nonsense variant p.Trp1038Ter, previously demonstrated to have abnormal cytoplasmic localization (26).

All four variants with activity similar to the WT in the BRCA2-binding assay (p.Val932Met, p.Leu939Val, p.Gly998Glu, and p.Asp1122Val) displayed less than 25% of cells with cytoplasmic localization. Two variants, p.Gly1068Ala and p.Leu1172Val, that scored as hypomorphic in binding to BRCA2 displayed slightly higher rate of cells with cytoplasmic localization but statistically indistinguishable from WT PALB2. Cells expressing 11/13 loss-of-function variants exhibited >50% of cells with cytoplasmic expression, similar to the loss-of-function control p.Trp1038Ter (Figs. 3B and S2A). Most variants that were hypomorphic in BRCA2 binding were also hypomorphic in subcellular localization (Figs. 3B and S2B).

Western blot analysis of subcellular fractionated nuclear and cytoplasmic lysate was consistent with fluorescence microscopy data, in that it showed that the majority of PALB2 variants that were present in the cytoplasm also displayed reduced nuclear protein levels when compared with PALB2 WT (Fig. 3C). Analysis of whole cell lysates revealed decreased overall steady-state protein levels for the PALB2 WD40 variants with cytoplasmic localization, suggesting impaired protein stability (Fig. S2C).

PALB2 variants and HR proficiency

Next, we sought to determine the activity of the PALB2 missense variants in HR repair. We employed a well-established GFP-reporter system to monitor the HR-dependent restoration of GFP function following the induction of a double-stranded break by the I-SceI enzyme (22). B400 mouse mammary tumor cells with a stably integrated GFP-reporter system, and deficient for endogenous Palb2, were cotransfected with human PALB2–coding constructs (WT or variants) along with an I-SceI-expressing plasmid (Fig. 4A). Loss-of-function p.Thr1030Ile, p.Ala1025Arg, and pathogenic p.Tyr551Ter variants were used as negative controls for HR repair.

The majority of variants defective in the BRCA2 binding exhibited a substantial deficiency in HR repair (Fig. 4B). Nine variants (p.Gly971Arg, p.Leu972Pro, p.Leu977Pro, p.Val991Gly, p.Phe1016Ser, p.Gly1028Asp, p.Gly1028Val, p.Ile1035Asn, and p.Pro1097Arg) showed a similar decrease in HR repair efficiency as the truncated variant p.Tyr551Ter, which lacks the entire WD40 domain, or the p.Ala1025Arg non-natural null variant (Fig. 4B). Analysis of steady-state protein levels shows that several variants displaying reduced HR repair activity have reduced protein stability (Fig. 4C). However, lower steady-state levels were not the only explanation for reduced HR repair activity as demonstrated by low HR repair activity of p.Ala1025Arg, p.Leu977Pro, and p.Val991Gly despite their expression levels being close or higher than WT levels (Fig. 4, B and C). Conversely, some variants (e.g., p.Val1036Ala, p. Met1049Thr, and p.Gly1068Ala) with reduced levels (<50% of the WT protein) displayed HR repair activity comparable to the WT protein (Fig. 4, B and C).

Several variants with compromised interaction with BRCA2 retained partial HR repair activity (Fig. 4B). Notably, p.Gly1068Trp, which displayed a severe loss of interaction with the BRCA2 NT region (amino acids 1–60), exhibited HR repair activity comparable to the WT and to the positive controls, p.Leu939Val and p.Asp1122Val (Fig. 4B). While the results from the BRCA2 interaction assays were linearly correlated with results from mislocalization assessment (R2 = 0.73), they were logarithmically correlated with results from HR repair (R2 = 0.67) (Fig. 4D).

To determine the extent to which additional sequences in BRCA2 and PALB2 not included in the initial M2H (BRCA2, amino acids 1–60 and PALB2, amino acids 859–1186) contributed to the interaction, we tested the p.Gly1068Trp variant using a modified M2H assay with an extended BRCA2 construct (amino acids 1–360) and by coimmunoprecipitation using FL PALB2. We confirmed the reduced interaction with BRCA2 in the M2H assay and in coimmunoprecipitation experiment (Fig. 5, A and B).

Interestingly, two different substitutions at the same residue, p.Trp1140Arg and p.Trp1140Ser, led to similar BRCA2 binding and subcellular localization profiles, yet diverged sharply in HR activity. However, variant p.Trp1140Arg presented with severely compromised interaction to endogenous RAD51, whereas the p.Trp1140Ser had residual RAD51 binding, and p.Gly1068Trp variant retained full binding comparable to the WT PALB2 (Fig. 5C).

Taken together, these data suggest that minimal binding is required for HR activity, and once that minimal is reached, HR repair is efficient, at least in the context of the heterologous HR repair assay, and that deficiency in BRCA2 or RAD51 interaction may underlie the impaired HR repair activity.

Data integration for pathogenicity assessment

Next, we combined functional data available for missense variants in the WD40 domain of PALB2 tested in our study (Table S2). Nine VUSs, p.Gly971Arg, p.Leu972Pro, p.Leu977Pro, p.Val991Gly, p.Phe1016Ser, p.Gly1028Asp, p.Gly1028Val, p.Ile1035Asn, and p.Pro1097Arg, exhibited loss of function in all assays in which they were tested. They exhibited <5% of BRCA2 binding activity, comparable to the null artificial variant p. Ala1025Arg shown to be loss of function in several independent assays (Table S2). Furthermore, more than 50% of cells expressing those variants mislocalize PALB2 to the cytoplasm, similarly to the p.Trp1038Ter, a truncating variant shown to be loss of function in other assays (Table S2). Finally, these nine variants were also defective in HR repair, with an activity comparable to the p.Tyr551Ter variant that truncates all the WD40 domains and has been shown independently to be a loss-of-function variant (22, 23). These are rare alleles, with a population allele frequency <1 × 10−6 or have never been seen in large nonclinical cohorts (e.g., gnomAD). These nine variants are likely to constitute likely pathogenic/pathogenic variants.

Discussion

Germline pathogenic variants in PALB2 lead to a highly elevated risk of breast and moderate risk of ovarian cancer (7, 10, 11, 32). Thus, accurate assessment of variant pathogenicity is critical for genetic counseling and cancer surveillance of carriers. However, 98% of PALB2 missense variants are VUS or have conflicting interpretations.

Given the typically low allele frequency of individual variants, functional analysis serves as the primary alternative for assessing pathogenicity associated with PALB2 VUS. Several groups have developed assays to assess the activity of PALB2 variants in different cellular processes (21, 22, 23). This concerted effort led to the analysis of over 130 missense variants and revealed that most variants that impacted PALB2 function were located in the coiled-coil motif and in the WD40 domain (14). In the present study, we focused on the WD40 domain, which is essential for PALB2 association with BRCA2 and proper HR functionality (15). We examined the impact of 78 naturally occurring ClinVar-reported missense variants classified as VUS or with conflicting interpretations. We assessed their ability to interact with BRCA2, and for a subset, we examined the subcellular localization profile and efficiency to promote HR repair.

Limitations of our study are that the BRCA2 binding and subcellular localization assays are performed with ectopically expressed fragments, and the HR assay is performed in mouse cells. Although we cannot rule out that these heterologous systems will not affect results, these assays, which assess relevant biochemical processes of PALB2 related to cancer risk, have been crossvalidated and found to effectively identify loss-of-function variants (3, 4, 5, 15, 21, 22, 23, 26). The current analysis revealed several partial loss-of-function, hypomorphic variants, but it is unclear whether all these represent reduced penetrance pathogenic variants (RPPVs), which are associated with a clinically relevant elevation of risk, albeit lower than the risk conferred by truncating variants. We expect that a fraction of PALB2 WD40 missense hypomorphic variants will be RPPVs, but this classification will depend on the calibration of each assay with known RPPVs. Given that PALB2-deficient cells with HR impairment phenocopy the loss of BRCA1 and are sensitive to poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitors, it will be important to determine whether cells expressing hypomorphic variants will respond to poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibition.

Variants in propeller blades 3 to 5 were more likely to impact function. While this was expected for propeller blades 4 and 5 because of direct interaction with BRCA2 (27), this was not the case for blade 3. The identification of five loss-of-function variants that disrupt BRCA2 binding in blade 3 (p.G971Arg, p.Leu972Pro, p.Arg976T, p.Leu977Pro, and p.Val991Gly) outside the BRCA2 binding pocket indicates that these variants lead to a major disruption in the WD40 domain structure and function. The high rate of mislocalized PALB2 in cells expressing these variants suggests that these alterations expose the hidden nuclear export sequence, ultimately leading to PALB2 accumulation in the cellular cytoplasm (26).

Interestingly, the data show that some variants with minimal binding to BRCA2 in the M2H assay were proficient in HR repair. This observation suggests that the M2H assay is able to detect small perturbations that affect binding to BRCA2 fragments. However, some of these perturbations might not be biologically relevant in the context of the FL proteins with additional stabilizing interactions and co-operativity, although structural studies have shown that the small NT region of BRCA2 is sufficient for the association between PALB2 and BRCA2 (27). Experiments with p.Trp1140Ser and p.Trp1140Arg variants, a residue located close to the Leu1143 residue important for RAD51 interaction (25), illustrate that loss of interaction to BRCA2 or to RAD51 may compromise HR activity. Functional assays based on specific biochemical interactions illuminate the potential mechanisms by which a variant may lead to loss of function but may fail to identify pathogenic variants that disrupt function through a different mechanism.

PALB2 WD40 domain variants that were mislocalized to the cytoplasm also showed lower overall PALB2 protein levels, consistent with observations from Boonen et al. (23) that mislocalized variants are prone to cytoplasmic degradation. We also noticed that protein levels of individual variants, although consistent within replicates of the same assay, vary considerably across different assays. This variation can, at least in part, be attributed to differences in construct design and ectopic expression approaches in each assay. In BRCA2 interaction studies, PALB2 variants are expressed in a CT fragment context (amino acids 859–1186) fused to the herpes virus VP16 activation domain driven by a constitutive SV40 early promoter. In localization studies, FL PALB2 variants fused to GFP were expressed using a cytomegalovirus immediate early promoter. Protein levels in assays that rely on ectopic expression are less sensitive to identify pathogenic variants in other HR genes such as BRCA1 (32, 33, 34). Therefore, caution is warranted when inferring pathogenicity using the result of a single functional assay based on ectopic expression.

Finally, this study identified nine rare missense variants in the PALB2 WD40 domain with loss-of-function phenotypes across all three assays. Although LB/B variants were included as controls, no likely pathogenic/pathogenic control currently exists in the WD40 domain. Loss-of-function controls identified in independent assays were used as negative controls, and these indicated that the nine variants have severely compromised function and are likely to constitute pathogenic variants. Further studies will be necessary to confirm the proposed clinical classification.

Experimental procedures

Plasmids and mutagenesis

For the M2H assay, we used two previously generated constructs, pCMX-GAL4 DNA-binding domain (DBD)-BRCA2 NT (amino acids 1–60) and pVP16 AD-PALB2 CT WT (amino acids 859–1186) (19). A new pCDNA3 GAL4 DBD-BRCA2 (amino acids 1–360) construct was also used. It was obtained by cloning the human BRCA2 complementary DNA (cDNA) (amino acids 1–360) into pGBT9 using XmaI/SalI restriction sites followed by subcloning of GAL4 DBD-BRCA2 amino acid 1–360 fragment into pCDNA3 using HindIII/SalI restriction sites. PALB2 variants were generated by site-directed mutagenesis using the pVP16 AD-PALB2 CT WT construct as template, PrimeStar DNA polymerase (Takara Bio, Inc), and primers (Table S3), as previously described (19).

For the subcellular localization assays, pGFP C2-PALB2 FL WT construct was obtained by cloning the human PALB2 cDNA (National Center for Biotechnology Information Reference Sequence: NM_024675.4) into the pEGFP C2 vector using EcoRI/SalI restriction sites. Variants were generated by site-directed mutagenesis as described above using pGFP C2-PALB2 FL WT as template. A pmCherry N1-H2B construct was obtained by subcloning the human H2B cDNA into the pmCherry N1 vector using KpnI/BamHI restriction sites and pBABE NEO-H2B-GFP as donor (kindly provided by Dr L.K. Teixeira, Instituto Nacional de Câncer, Brazil).

For the homology-directed repair assay, we used the previously generated pOZC-HA-FLAG-PALB2 FL WT construct, and variants were generated by site-directed mutagenesis using pfu turbo and pOZC-HA-FLAG-PALB2 WT as template (20). All constructs generated in this study were confirmed by Sanger sequencing.

Cell lines and culture

HEK293FT cells were purchased from Invitrogen and maintained in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. The mouse mammary tumor cell line B400 (Palb2−/−; Trp53−/−) was cultivated in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium/F-12 media supplemented with 5 μg/ml insulin, 5 ng/ml epidermal growth factor, 5 ng/ml cholera toxin, 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin–streptomycin and was maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere (20). Cells were periodically assessed for mycoplasma contamination and authenticated by short tandem repeat analysis.

M2H assay

M2H assays were conducted using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega) as previously described (19). In brief, HEK293FT cells were seeded in a 96-well plate and cotransfected 24 h later with the pCMX-GAL4 DBD BRCA2 NT (amino acids 1–60) (or pCDNA3-GAL4 DBD BRCA2 (amino acids 1–360) and pVP16-PALB2 CT (WT or variant) constructs together with pG5Luc reporter and pGR-TK internal control using polyethylenimine (Polysciences, Inc). The cell culture medium was replaced with fresh medium 3 h post-transfection. The reporter assay was conducted 24 h post-transfection, with the BRCA2–PALB2 WT interaction set as 100%.

Whole cell protein extraction and immunoblotting analysis

Western blot analysis was used to assess the levels of VP16 AD-PALB2 CT fusion proteins. HEK293FT cells were seeded in 6-well plates, transfected 24 h later with indicated constructs, and lysed 24 h after transfection with mild-radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (50 mM Tris–Cl [pH 7.4]; 150 mM NaCl; 1% v/v NP-40, and 1 mM EDTA [pH 8.0]) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (IC; #P8340 and #P0044, respectively; Merck). Insoluble cellular debris were removed by centrifugation (16,000g for 10 min at 4 °C). Whole cell extracts were evaluated by immunoblotting using primary antibodies anti-VP16 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology; catalog no: sc-7545) and anti-GAPDH (Santa Cruz Biotechnology; catalog no.: sc-32233), followed by secondary horseradish peroxidase–conjugated mouse IgG kappa–binding protein (Santa Cruz Biotechnology; catalog no.: sc-516102).

Immunofluorescence microscopy

HEK293FT cells were seeded and transfected as described previously. Cells were harvested 24 h post-transfection and plated over glass cover slides previously coated with gelatin (2% w/v in PBS) and allowed to attach for 24 h. Cells were subsequently washed once with PBS and fixed with 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature and washed with PBS once again. Slides were mounted using ProLong Gold Antifade with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (Thermo Fisher Scientific; catalog no.: P36935) and analyzed using fluorescence microscopy (Zeiss Observer Z.1). PALB2 subcellular localization was determined using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health).

Cellular fractionation and immunoblotting analysis

HEK293FT cells were seeded and transfected as described above. Cells were harvested and resuspended 48 h post-transfection in ice-cold buffer A (20 mM Tris–Cl [pH 7.4]; 10% v/v glycerol; 10 mM KCl; 0.2% w/v NP-40; 1 mM EDTA [pH 8.0], and 1 mM DTT) supplemented with IC, incubated for 5 min on ice, and centrifugated (1000g for 10 min at 4 °C) to separate nuclear (pellet) and cytoplasmic (supernatant) fractions. The latter was recovered, centrifugated (16,000g for 5 min at 4 °C), and recovered again as the final cytoplasmic fraction. The nuclear fraction was washed thrice with ice-cold buffer A, supplemented with IC, and then resuspended in ice-cold buffer B (20 mM Tris–Cl [pH 7.4]; 20% v/v glycerol; 10 mM KCl; 400 mM NaCl; 1 mM EDTA [pH 8.0], and 1 mM DTT) supplemented with IC, followed by sonication, and incubated for 30 min on ice. After centrifugation (16,000g for 10 min at 4 °C), the supernatant was recovered as the final nuclear fraction. Immunoblotting was carried out using primary antibodies anti-GFP (Sigma–Aldrich; catalog no.: G1544), anti-GAPDH (Santa Cruz Biotechnology; catalog no.: sc-32233), and anti-Histone H3 (Sigma–Aldrich; catalog no.: 07-690), followed by secondary horseradish peroxidase–conjugated mouse IgG kappa–binding protein (Santa Cruz Biotechnology; catalog no.: sc-516102) or secondary anti-rabbit (Santa Cruz Biotechnology; catalog no.: sc-2357).

Homology-directed repair assay

Homology-directed repair assay was conducted as previously described (20). Briefly, B400/DR-GFP mouse mammary tumor cells (Palb2- /-; Tp53- /-) were cotransfected with hPALB2-expressing constructs along with I-SceI vector using Xtremegene 9 reagent (Roche), and GFP-positive cells were quantified by fluorescence-activated cell sorting. Results were normalized as fold change using PALB2 p.Tyr551Ter pathogenic variant set as 1 and PALB2 WT set as 5.

Coimmunoprecipitation

For the GAL4-BRCA2 NT assay, HEK293FT cells were seeded in 6-well plates, cotransfected 24 h later with indicated constructs, and lysed 48 h after transfection with mild-RIPA buffer. Protein A/G PLUS-Agarose beads (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) were incubated with 4 μg of anti-GAL4 antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology; sc-510) for 1 h in mild-RIPA buffer. Beads were washed three times with mild-RIPA buffer and then incubated with 2 μg of bovine serum albumin for 1 h in mild-RIPA buffer. Beads were then washed three times and incubated overnight with 2 mg of HEK293 whole lysates in mild-RIPA buffer supplemented with IC. Beads were washed three times with mild-RIPA and eluted with Laemmli buffer. Immunoblotting was carried out using anti-GFP (Sigma–Aldrich; catalog no.: G1544) and anti-GAL4 DBD (Santa Cruz Biotechnology; catalog no.: sc-510).

For the endogenous RAD51 assay, HEK293T cells were transfected with indicated constructs, exposed to ionizing radiation (5 Gy) 48 h post-transfection, and whole cell lysates were obtained with mild-RIPA buffer supplemented with IC after 5 h following ionizing radiation treatment. GFP-Trap magnetic agarose (Chromotek) was incubated overnight with 6 mg of cell lysate and then washed three times with mild-RIPA and eluted in Laemmli buffer. Immunoblotting was carried out using anti-GFP (Millipore; catalog no.: MAB3580) and anti-RAD51 (Abcam; catalog no.: EPR 4030).

Densitometry assessment

Densitometry readings were obtained using ImageJ software. To calculate the nuclear/cytoplasmic ratio, we first normalized PALB2 nuclear levels using histone H3 and PALB2 cytoplasmic levels using GAPDH. The normalized values were used to obtain the ratio, setting the WT ratio in each blot to 1.
