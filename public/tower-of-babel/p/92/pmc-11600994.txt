PM2.5 exposure and household income are associated with lung function abnormalities in children with sickle cell disease
Authors: Jelte Kelchtermans, Julian Allen, Anita Bhandari
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractRationaleCardiopulmonary dysfunction is a major contributor to mortality among persons with sickle cell disease (pwSCD). Despite this, little is known regarding environmental drivers of lung function decline.ObjectiveWe hypothesized that environmental and socioeconomic variables have a significant effect on lung function in pwSCD that can be detected by spirometry.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed all spirometry results from pwSCD followed in the Pediatric Pulmonology clinic at the Children's Hospital of Philadelphia since 1 January 2016.ResultsThe study included 349 spirometry tests from 128 patients, primarily “Black or African American” (88%) and male (61%). More frequent exposure to PM2.5 above 25 μg/m3 was associated with higher odds of obstruction. Specifically, when compared to incidence of exposure to PM2.5 above 25 μg/m3 <25th percentile, both pwSCD exposed to 25th–75th percentile and pwSCD >75th percentile had higher odds of obstruction on spirometry (25th–75th: odds ratio [OR]: 9.6, p = .017; >75th: OR: 31.85, p = .002) despite correction for potential confounders. Similarly, median household income below the mean was associated with higher odds of restriction (OR: 4.37; p = .009).ConclusionsWe report higher odds of obstruction in pwSCD frequently exposed to PM2.5 concentrations above 25 μg/m3 and higher odds of restriction in pwSCD with lower household income. Our findings link spirometry patterns to modifiable risk factors indicating that there may opportunities for early intervention in pwSCD that have been referred to a pulmonology clinic. Further research is needed to assess if these findings can be generalized to the wider population of pwSCD.

INTRODUCTION

Sickle cell disease (SCD) continues to cause over 80,000 childhood deaths globally every year. 1 Improvements in care have impacted SCD outcomes in the United States, with life expectancy increasing from 28 years in 1979 to 43 years in 2017. 2 However, as life expectancy for persons with SCD (pwSCD) improved, challenges posed by chronic complications associated with the condition have come into focus. 2 Specifically, chronic renal and cardiopulmonary dysfunction are now the primary contributors to mortality among pwSCD in the United States. 3 , 4 Developing strategies to address these chronic complications may further extend life expectancy in the United States and benefit pwSCD worldwide.

A promising approach is to optimize care for childhood asthma and wheezing in pwSCD, as both are risk factors for cardiopulmonary dysfunction and increased morbidity in SCD. 5 Encouragingly, recent work highlighted that pediatric pulmonology subspecialty involvement in SCD care is associated with reductions in hospitalizations and systemic steroid prescriptions, demonstrating that improvements are within reach. 6 , 7

Unfortunately, spirometry, one of the most common tools used to monitor long‐term care in patients with asthma or wheezing, can be challenging to interpret due to the frequent presence of abnormal patterns in pwSCD. Obstructive patterns are common in children, while restrictive patterns are more typical in adults, but it remains unclear if these abnormalities are caused by SCD directly or through interactions with other variables. 8 , 9 Understanding how SCD causes these abnormalities is a prerequisite to addressing them. We hypothesized that socioeconomic and environmental variables have a significant effect on lung function as measured by spirometry in pwSCD. To test this hypothesis, we completed a cross‐sectional study looking at spirometry tests completed by pwSCD followed in the Pediatric Pulmonology Clinic at the Children's Hospital of Philadelphia (CHOP).

METHODS

Population

Patients followed at the Pediatric Pulmonology Clinic at CHOP were included in this study. CHOP is a quaternary referral center located in the North‐Eastern United States. Inclusion criteria included a SCD related ICD code associated with their electronic medical record (EMR), namely International Classification of Diseases‐9 (ICD9) codes 282.41, 282.42, 282.60, 282.61, 282.62,282.63, 282.64, 282.65, 282.68, or 282.69 or ICD10 codes D57.1, D57.20, or D57.40. Based on the specific ICD code, a patient had they were classified as having hemoglobin SS (HgSS), hemoglobin S‐C disease (HgSC), or hemoglobin sickle cell β thalassemia (HgSβthal). One patient had both a hemoglobin SC and a hemoglobin HgSβthal diagnostic code associated with their EMR and was excluded from analysis. Furthermore, to be included, an in‐person visit in the Pediatric Pulmonology Clinic after 1 January 2000, and at least one spirometry after 1 January 2016 with height recorded in the EMR were required. The 1 January 2016 cut‐off represents the start of absolute spirometry results as opposed to calculated percent predicted being recorded in the CHOP EMR allowing for calculation of percent predicted using Global Lung Function Initiative (GLI)–global race neutral reference equations. Birth year and month were obtained from the EMR. All ages reported are estimated based on the assumption that patients were born on the first of the month. This study was deemed to constitute exempt human subjects research by the CHOP institutional review board.

Patient variables

Based on EMR classification, patients were categorized as “Black or African American,” “White,” and “Other or Missing (NA).” Similarly, patient ethnicity was classified as “Hispanic or Latino” or “Not Hispanic or Latino” based on EMR recorded ethnicity, and sex classification was based on recorded sex. Patients were classified as having mild‐, moderate‐, or severe‐asthma based on the most severe ICD10 code found in the EMR. Patients with ICD9 code E66.9 or ICD10 code Z68.54 were classified as obese. Household income was approximated using 2021 zip‐code level census bureau data with the zipcodeR package. 10 Since prior studies have demonstrated worse quality of life in pwSCD making below 200% of the federal poverty level and the median estimated household income in our cohort was approximately 180% the 2021 federal poverty level, this median income was considered to be a reasonable cut off below which we would expect that patients may be adversely affected by lower income. 11 , 12 As such, household income was analyzed as a binary variable (“below median” or “above median”). History of acute chest syndrome (ACS) and bone marrow transplant (BMT) was recorded as a binary variable based on the presence or absence of relevant ICD codes in the patients' EMR. Since time of BMT could not be ascertained, patients with a history of BMT were excluded from analysis.

Spirometry data

Spirometry results obtained after 1 January 2016, in patients older than five and younger than 21 years old, and with absolute value for forced expiratory volume 1 s (FEV1), forced vital capacity (FVC), and height available were included. We used the GLI calculator to calculate z‐scores and predicted FEV1/FVC ratio using race neutral reference equations. 13 A restrictive spirometry pattern (restriction) was defined as an FVC z score < −1.645 and FEV1/FVC > 0.8 and an obstructive spirometry pattern (obstruction) was defined as an FEV1/FVC < 0.8 and FVC z score > −1.645. 14 , 15 , 16 Mixed patterns were not used to assign either restrictive or obstructive status.

Air quality data

PM2.5 exposure data for Pennsylvania and New Jersey between 1 January 2000 and 21 October 2023 was obtained using the pregenerated PM2.5 concentration files available on the EPA's daily data website. 17 The 1 January 2000 cut‐off was chosen as data availability significantly improved starting 2000. Subject home ZIP codes were matched to counties using the reverse zipcode function available in the zipcodeR package. 10 The American Thoracic Society (ATS) recommends that short‐term PM2.5 exposure standards should be set at 25 μg/m3 and long‐term standards at 8 μg/m3. 18 For patients born after 31 December 1999, the average PM2.5 exposure per day and the number of days with an average PM2.5 above 25 μg/m3 over the total number of days with data available between birth and the last included spirometry were calculated. Patients with an incidence of PM2.5 above 25 μg/m3 below the 25th percentile were considered to be exposed to low levels of air pollution, those between the 25th and 75th percentile to average levels of air pollution, and those above the 75th percentile to high levels of air pollution.

Analysis

FEV1/FVC ratio was compared with predicted FEV1/FVC ratio using a paired t test. Logistic regression models were created in R to assess which factors were associated with patients having at least one occurrence of obstruction or restriction on spirometry. 19 , 20 , 21 Variables included in these models were sex, ethnicity, race, SCD diagnosis (HgSS, HgSC, or HgSβthal), history of ACS, income, pollution exposure, obesity, and asthma status. Since only one patient was classified as White, race was entered as a binary variable with levels “Black or African American” and “Not Black or African American.” The vif function from the car package was used to test for multicollinearity in these models, and the variance inflation factor for all variables was below 1.5 in both models. 22

RESULTS

Population

A total of 128 patients met inclusion criteria (Table 1). Most patients were Black or African American (88%), not Hispanic or Latino (93%), and male (61%). The most common SCD diagnosis was HgSS disease (71%), followed by HgSC (20%) and HgSβthal (9%). Just under half of the patients had a history of ACS (48%) and most had a diagnosis of asthma (85%) with moderate or severe asthma the most common (46% of all patients). The average age at time of first recorded spirometry was 12.9 ± 4.3 years and the average age at time of spirometry per patient was 13.5 ± 4.2 years. The average number of spirometry results per patient was 2.7 ± 2.9. The mean PM2.5 exposure was 10.3 ± 1.1 μg/m3 with 2.8 ± 1.4% of days with a PM2.5 above 25 μg/m3, the 25th percentile for days with a PM2.5 above above 25 μg/m3 was 1.7% of days and the 75th percentile was 3.9% of days. PM2.5 exposure was not available for 29 patients (23%). The median household income was $46,462.

Pulmonary function test (PFT) data

Data from a total of 349 spirometry tests was available for analysis. Mean FEV1 z score was −1.56 ± 1.10, mean FVC z score was −0.98 ± 1.21, and mean FEV1/FVC was 80.92 ± 10.66. The observed FEV1/FVC was significantly lower than predicted FEV1/FVC (mean difference −7.48; p < 2.2 × 10−16; 95% confidence interval [CI]: −8.56 to −6.41). Out of these 349 tests, 76 tests from 39 patients (30% of patients) met criteria for restriction and 102 tests from 41 patients (32% of patients) met criteria for obstruction. We created logistic regression models to investigate which factors were associated with patients having at least one occurrence of obstruction or one occurrence of restriction on spirometry (Table 2). Compared to incidence of high PM2.5 below the 25th percentile, both average PM2.5 exposure (25th–75th percentile), and high PM2.5 exposure (above 75th percentile) were associated with higher odds of obstruction (average exposure: odds ratio [OR]: 9.6, p = .017; 95% CI: 1.83–83.56; high exposure: p = .002; OR: 31.85; 95% CI: 4.59–372.25; Figure 1). An approximated household income below the mean was associated with higher odds of restriction (OR: 4.37; p = .009; 95% CI: 1.50–14.10; Figure 2).

DISCUSSION

We hypothesized that socioeconomic and environmental variables have a significant effect on lung function as measured by spirometry in pwSCD. Our results clearly describe higher rates of obstruction in pwSCD frequently exposed to extreme PM2.5 concentrations and higher rates of restriction in pwSCD with lower approximated household income. These findings suggest an association between modifiable environmental and socioeconomic factors and lung health in pwSCD.

It is unsurprising that air quality affects pulmonary health in pwSCD given the well described link between air quality and childhood asthma morbidity, respiratory hospitalizations in children, and short‐term complications of SCD. 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 However, our results indicate that air quality is associated with changes in lung function significant enough to be captured by standard spirometry, supporting its use as both a screening tool and measure to assess the effectiveness of interventions aimed at ameliorating the impact of air pollution exposure.

Furthermore, there was a significant population‐wide gap between predicted and observed FEV1/FVC ratios. While our study was neither powered nor designed to examine the root causes of this further, future studies could examine if these findings are limited to a population of pwSCD followed by pediatric pulmonology, frequently for comorbid asthma, or universal in pwSCD. In our analysis, a history of moderate or severe asthma seemed to play a role. A better understanding of how PM2.5 and other environmental and socioeconomic variables influence pulmonary health in pwScd, especially those with asthma, may reveal avenues to prevent or influence this gap between predicted and observed FEV1/FVC. In this context, it is worth noting that out of the 99 patients with air pollution data available, only three were exposed to an average PM2.5 concentration < 8 μg/m3—the chronic exposure standard supported by the ATS. 18 As such, our study did not include a true “best case” scenario for pwSCD. These findings are consistent with previous data demonstrating that in the United States, air pollution exposure is closely linked to race and socioeconomic status. 27 , 28

Beyond the question of modifiable risk factors, our study is the first to use race‐neutral as opposed to race specific spirometry reference equations in pwSCD. While traditionally the use of equations specific to race have been employed, recently race agnostic predicted equations have been shown to be more predictive of disease related outcomes. 29 , 30 Since both PM2.5 exposure and socioeconomic status are closely linked with race in the United States, these race neutral equations may have enabled us to detect findings obscured in studies using race specific equations.

Limitations of our study are; First given the cross‐sectional nature of our study we are unable to draw causative conclusions. While the associations described are intriguing, follow up studies are clearly required to identify and address the mechanisms that underpin these associations. In addition data set did not allow for detailed evaluation of the effect of clinical confounders with both asthma and ACS only available as categorical variables derived from ICD codes, potentially explaining the limited impact of these variables in the presented data. Furthermore, relying on ICD codes for these diagnoses does not capture potential variability in how ACS is diagnosed. Recognizing these constraints, future research could delve deeper into the nuanced relationships between these variables and pulmonary outcomes in pwSCD. Although air pollution exposure was assessed using county level data which is an inexact way to assess patient level exposure, it is worth noting that despite the inexact nature of this data, prior studies using monitoring data from central measuring stations have shown clear associations between PM2.5 exposure and healthcare utilization in both asthma and SCD. 31 , 32 , 33 In addition, we only had access to patients' month and year of birth but not the exact day of birth. Consequently, we assumed that all patients were born on the first of the month. Given that patients were at least 5 years old at the time of PFT, it appears unlikely that this assumption significantly impacted the described relationships. Finally, our findings link spirometry patterns to modifiable risk factors in pwSCD referred to a pulmonology clinic. Further research is needed to assess if these findings can be generalized to the wider population of pwSCD.

We report higher odds of obstruction in patients with SCD frequently exposed to PM2.5 concentrations above 25 μg/m3 and higher odds of restriction in patients with lower household income. Further research is needed to assess the drivers of these relationships, particularly to determine if specific exposure patterns or clinical interventions can modify these outcomes. Although our study was underpowered to address these questions directly, we hope that future interinstitutional collaborations will help to address these important issues.
