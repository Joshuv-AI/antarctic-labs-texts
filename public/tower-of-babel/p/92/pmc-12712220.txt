Pathogenicity analysis and control strategies to combat avian diseases: mechanisms of increased virulence of Marek’s disease virus and the development of vaccines against poultry red mites
Authors: Shiro MURATA
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe occurrence of avian diseases has been greatly reduced by improved sanitation on poultry farms and the development of prevention programs through vaccination and other measures. However, the poultry industry still faces several concerns. Marek’s disease (MD) has caused significant economic losses to the poultry industry in the past; however, the introduction of vaccines has successfully contributed to a reduction in MD occurrences. Nevertheless, sporadic MD occurrences are still observed, and increased virulence has been reported owing to the changes in viral characteristics. In addition to infectious diseases, infestation with poultry red mites (PRMs; Dermanyssus gallinae), which are small hematophagous ectoparasites of poultry, has caused severe declines in productivity of the poultry industry worldwide. Furthermore, in addition to animal hygiene, problems caused by PRMs are of concern in terms of public health and animal welfare. The emergence of acaricide-resistant PRMs has made their control difficult. Therefore, the establishment of novel control strategies is eagerly awaited. Among the issues of avian diseases facing the poultry industry, this review highlights research on the mechanisms of increased virulence of MD virus and the development of a novel approach for PRM control.Keywords: anti-poultry red mite (PRM) vaccine, Marek’s disease virus, Meq, poultry red mite, virulence

INTRODUCTION

Improved sanitation and vaccine development have greatly contributed to the reduction in infectious diseases in poultry. Marek’s disease (MD), which manifests as malignant lymphomas and neurological signs in chickens infected with the MD virus (MDV) [13], caused significant economic losses worldwide in the 1960s [70], but the introduction of live vaccines on poultry farms has led to a drastic reduction in MD occurrences [97]. However, changes in virus properties have increased the virulence of MDV field strains, and sporadic occurrences of MD have been reported in vaccinated chickens [97]. Approximately half of countries in the world have reported MD cases [10]; this widespread distribution raises concerns about potential economic impacts. However, accurate estimates regarding the infection rates, economic losses, and disease distribution across countries remain insufficient; therefore, reliable and comprehensive statistical data are required globally to implement effective control of MD [10]. In Japan, MD is reported annually in more than a dozen farms. In addition, there have been sporadic occurrences of other infectious diseases such as infectious bronchitis and infectious laryngotracheitis; therefore, continued monitoring of poultry farms is warranted to prevent disease occurrence. Moreover, increased outbreaks of the highly pathogenic avian influenza beyond the species in recent years have caused serious concerns worldwide [9], resulting in significant negative impacts on farmers and consumers. Thus, the poultry industry still faces the threat of infectious diseases, and the establishment of appropriate control measures is crucial.

In addition to infectious diseases, another concern affecting poultry farmers is the economic losses caused by the infestation with poultry red mites (PRMs), Dermanyssus gallinae (De Geer 1778), a hematophagous ectoparasite of poultry, which causes serious issues worldwide [78, 89]. The widespread distribution of PRMs has been reported in Europe and Asia, including Japan [15, 78, 79; Control Red Mite, https://www.coremi.eu/about-coremi/mites.html]. The egg-laying industry in European countries suffers annual losses of €360 million due to reduced productivity caused by PRMs and the costs of countermeasures [33]. Blood sucking by PRMs cause anemia, weight loss, abnormal behavior such as feather pecking and agitation, poor health status, and stressful conditions that cause a decline in egg production and quality, leading to a huge negative impact on the poultry industry [78]. PRMs exhibit characteristics that they actively feed on blood at night and usually hide in the crevices of poultry houses after sucking blood. Owing to the small size of PRMs, their detection is problematic, particularly when their population density within poultry houses is low. In contrast, if PRMs are readily visible during the day, the poultry house is assumed to be severely contaminated with PRMs. In poultry farms, PRMs are currently controlled by the use of acaricides; however, their improper and prolonged use has led to the selection of acaricide-resistant PRMs, making it challenging to control PRMs [78]. Therefore, effective novel strategies are required to control PRMs [78]. Additionally, PRMs are potentially involved in the transmission of several avian pathogens among chickens in poultry houses [78]. Furthermore, PRM infestations could cause dermatitis and rash in humans [1, 17, 36, 62], and some zoonotic agents have been detected in PRMs [16, 65, 71, 77]. Thus, in addition to concerns regarding sanitation on poultry farms and the welfare of farmed chickens, the expansion of PRM contamination has the potential to cause public health issues. Therefore, a One Health approach has been proposed to combat these issues raised by PRMs [76].

To counter the risk of disease outbreaks on poultry farms, the establishment of effective control strategies, in addition to appropriate hygiene environments and periodic monitoring, is warranted. Additionally, to establish control strategies, we need to understand disease occurrence on poultry farms and the characteristics of each prevalent pathogen. This review focuses on avian diseases such as MDV and PRMs, both of which are widespread on poultry farms worldwide, including Japan, and are persistent issues that require continuous and appropriate management in the poultry industry. To mitigate economic losses caused by both agents, in addition to thorough hygiene measures, it is required to establish effective control methods and monitoring procedures specific to each agent, as well as to elucidate the characteristics of each agent and the pathogenesis in chickens. For the purpose of providing insights regarding the resolution of issues related to both agents, in this review, the mechanisms of increased virulence and characteristics of Japanese MDV strains are described, and research on the development of vaccines as a novel control strategy against PRMs are discussed.

Evolution of MDV virulence

MDV (family Orthoherpesviridae; subfamily Alphaherpesvirinae; genus Mardivirus; species Mardivirus gallidalpha2) infects chickens through the respiratory tract, causing cytolytic infection of B and T cells and establishing latent infection of CD4+ T cells, some of which transform and develop lymphomas [10]. In the past, MD caused significant economic losses in the poultry industry; however, the application of turkey herpesvirus and attenuated MDV strains as vaccines dramatically reduced the economic losses caused by MD [70, 97]. This represents a major success in controlling infectious diseases using vaccines. However, to date, enhanced virulence of MDV field strains has been observed, and MD outbreaks in vaccinated chickens have been reported [97]. Currently, the virulence of MDV strains is classified into four pathotypes: mild (mMDV): virulent (vMDV), very virulent (vvMDV), and very virulent plus (vv+MDV). MDV virulence is determined by comparing the ability to cause clinical signs in vaccinated or unvaccinated MD-susceptible chickens with those of the reference strains [98]. Due to the limited availability of MD-susceptible chickens in Japan, the virulence of Japanese strains has not been determined. However, the Japanese strain Kgs-c1 exhibited a moderate disease progression compared to the vv+MDV strain, implying lower virulence than vv+MDV (unpublished observation). The protective efficacy of vaccines against MD relies on inhibiting disease progression such as tumorigenesis and neuropathy, while their efficacy in suppressing viral infection and transmission is low [10]. The emergence of highly virulent MDV strains is thought to be due to the selection of more virulent strains by immunological pressure associated with vaccination [90]. Therefore, to combat the risk of future outbreaks and to develop novel vaccines, the mechanisms underlying MDV-induced tumorigenesis, vaccine-induced disease prevention, and enhanced virulence need to be elucidated.

Effects of differences in Meq sequences on MDV virulence

The pathogenicity of MDV has been reported to be affected by differences in the host’s genetic background, such as the major histocompatibility complex haplotypes [10, 31]. Additionally, as viral factors that affect the pathogenicity, the effects of some viral genes, particularly the meq gene, on the pathogenicity have been demonstrated [18, 19]. The meq gene has been identified as an oncogene essential for MDV-induced tumorigenesis [35]; indeed, a recombinant MDV lacking the meq gene failed to develop tumors in infected chickens [49]. The meq gene product, Meq, has a bZIP structure consisting of a basic region and a leucine zipper on the N-terminal side and regulates the transcription of various viral and host genes involved in tumorigenesis as a transcriptional factor [47]. In addition, Meq has been reported to interact with other host proteins, such as p53 [22], a tumor suppressor, C-terminal binding protein [11], which is involved in transcriptional regulation, and stimulator of interferon genes (STING) [42], which induces innate immune responses; therefore, the multiple functions of Meq are thought to be involved in pathogenesis (summarized in Fig. 1). At the meq locus, a high mutation rate was observed among MDV genes when comparing sequences among various MDV strains [63]. Characteristic polymorphisms are present in the basic region and C-terminal transactivation domain of Meq, depending on MDV virulence (Table 1) [75]. In addition to these polymorphisms, some MDV strains encode Meq with insertions or deletions in its transactivation domain (Fig. 2). The long isoform of Meq, L-Meq, which includes an insertion in the transactivation domain, was first identified in the vaccine strain, CVI988 [40], suggesting its role in low virulence. However, L-Meq has been detected in highly virulent strains in Australia [66]. In contrast, a short isoform of Meq, S-Meq, in which a deletion was observed in the transactivation domain, has been reported [54, 55, 60, 91]. Genetic recombination technology using bacterial artificial chromosomes has enabled assessment of the effects of Meq sequences on MDV virulence in animal experiments using recombinant MDVs (rMDVs) [87]. Kumar et al. showed that the cellular composition in tumors was altered by differences in Meq sequences [38], and Conradie et al. revealed that Meq sequences affect MDV virulence [18, 19]. Notably, the substitution of Meq of a highly virulent rMDV with L-Meq of CVI988 did not decrease MDV virulence [18], suggesting that the insertion in Meq is not responsible for the low MDV virulence. Thus, the influence of Meq sequences on virulence and pathogenesis is becoming increasingly clear. However, the effect of each polymorphism or insertion/deletion in Meq on MDV virulence has not been fully elucidated.

Structure and functions of Meq, an oncoprotein of Marek’s disease virus. Meq encodes a 339-amino-acid-protein consisting of the N-terminal proline-glutamine-rich (Pro/Gln), the basic region (Basic), and the leucine zipper (ZIP), as well as the C-terminal transactivation domain. Meq dimerize with Meq itself or other bZIP proteins, such as activator protein 1 (AP-1) family protein, via ZIP and acts as a transcriptional factor, thereby modulating the expression of various cellular and viral genes. In addition, Meq can interact with other host proteins, including C-terminal binding protein (CtBP) [11], p53 [22], stimulator of interferon genes (STING) [42], interferon regulatory factor 7 (IRF7) [42], Janus kinase 1 (JAK1) [90], and tyrosine kinase 2 (TYK2) [104]. CtBP binding motifs (20PLDLS24 and 49PDGLS53) and p53 binding domain are present in the N-terminal sides. Meq interacts with STING and JAK1 via the C-terminal region. Meq interacts with IRF7 and TYK2 can interact with Meq via both of N-terminal and C-terminal regions. In some MDV strains, the putative retinoblastoma (Rb) binding motif (117LAHCE121) is identified in the ZIP [75]. In addition, Meq can interact with histone deacetylases [43] and heat-shock protein 70 [103], linking to multiple cellular functions, including neoplastic transformation.

We analyzed the effect of insertion/deletion in Meq on the virulence of MDV in animal experiments using rMDVs because L-Meq was first identified in our laboratory [40] and S-Meq was found in recent field strains in Japan [60]. We replaced the Meq sequence of the rMDV generated from RB-1B, a highly virulent MDV strain, with L-Meq or S-Meq, whose sequences were matched with RB-1B-Meq, except for the insertion or deletion. rMDV encoding L-Meq exhibited significantly higher mortality and tumor incidence than rMDV encoding wild-type Meq, whereas the virulence of rMDV was dramatically decreased by the deletion in the transactivation domain of Meq [69]. Thus, insertion into the transactivation domain of Meq contributes to enhanced virulence, suggesting that polymorphisms in CVI988-L-Meq are associated with low virulence. Intriguingly, deletion in the transactivation domain observed in some recent field strains in Japan has decreased virulence. In previous MD cases in Japan, S-Meq was detected in samples from unvaccinated gamecocks with MD [60]; therefore, MDV encoding S-Meq may have evolved differently from those in commercial poultry farms. In addition to the insertion and deletion in Meq, we are currently assessing the effects of polymorphisms in the bZIP and transactivation domains of Meq on MDV virulence because we previously showed that these polymorphisms affect the transcriptional regulatory ability of Meq [56, 58]. Identifying the polymorphisms that affect MDV virulence could serve as a useful indicator for estimating the virulence of field strains and understanding the history of MDV evolution. Furthermore, advances in the next-generation sequencing technology have facilitated whole-genome analysis of MDV as discussed below, revealing the polymorphisms in other viral genes such as ICP4 and UL36 among MDV strains [102]; however, the obvious association between these polymorphisms and MDV pathogenicity remains unclear. To elucidate the factors that determine MDV virulence, further investigation is required regarding the effects of polymorphisms in other viral genes, as well as their combinations with polymorphisms in the meq gene, on MDV pathogenicity.

Genetic characteristics of MDV strains in Japan

Since the effect of Meq sequences on MDV virulence has been suggested [75], the Meq sequences of MDV distributed in various countries have been reported to date [25, 48, 85]. Our surveillance of MDV isolates in Japan showed that the Meq sequences of MDV strains from various regions of Japan shared unique polymorphisms in the transactivation domain (Table 1) [56]. Additionally, to characterize the viral genome sequence, we isolated a Japanese MDV strain (Kgs-c1) that was not contaminated with vaccine strains [50] from chickens with MD and conducted whole-genome sequencing of the Kgs-c1 strain [57]. MDV genomes are known to be separated into two major clusters, North America and Eurasia, which includes European and Chinese strains [90]. Kgs-c1 was classified into the Eurasian cluster, suggesting that Kgs-c1 has evolved to be similar to Asian and European strains [57]. In the United States, HVT was initially used as a first-generation vaccine [60]. When MD outbreaks were confirmed in HVT-vaccinated flocks, a bivalent vaccine with HVT and SB-1, a strain of Mardivirus gallidalpha3 (previously classified as MDV serotype 2), was introduced as a second-generation vaccine. Finally, CVI988, an attenuated MDV strain, was introduced as a third-generation vaccine against emerging virulent strains in the field [70]. However, CVI988 was introduced earlier in Japan and Europe than in the US; therefore, the differences in the history of vaccine introduction appear to be reflected in the differences in genomic characteristics [57, 90]. In addition, the characterization of the two new Japanese strains showed that both were classified into the Eurasian cluster and were genetically close to Kgs-c1 [102]. Notably, phylogenetic analysis of the meq genes also formed clusters consisting of isolates from North America and other regions, including European and Chinese isolates, and meq genes of the Japanese strains were closely related to one another and classified into the cluster of other regions. These data suggest that meq sequences are partially reflected in the genomic characteristics of MDV strains [57, 102]. Thus, Japanese strains have similar genetic characteristics; however, polymorphisms in some viral genes have also been found between Kgs-c1 and the newly isolated Japanese strains [102], suggesting a change in the characteristics of MDV strains in Japan. Therefore, periodic monitoring is required to identify mutations in viral genes crucial for virulence, including meq, along with changes in genomic characteristics.

Establishment of a novel strategy for the control of PRMs

Chemical pesticides such as carbamates, organophosphates, and pyrethroids have been widely used for the control of PRMs. Due to the emergence of acaricide-resistant PRMs, PRM infestation causes serious issues in the poultry industry in many parts of the world, and the susceptibility of PRMs to acaricides varies significantly among farms [72]. Additionally, neurotoxic agents such as spinosad and fluralaner are available for PRMs; however, spinosad has been shown to be insufficient as a stand-alone treatment for controlling them [20]. On the other hand, fluralaner, an isoxazoline compound used to control pests in companion animals, was introduced to poultry farms, and its administration in drinking water is effective in controlling PRMs [81, 86]. Additionally, fluralaner offers the advantage of a short withdrawal period. In Europe, the chemicals currently licensed for the control of PRMs are limited [67]; in particular, fluralaner is the only chemical licensed in Germany at the time of writing. Therefore, a more sophisticated approach is required to prevent the development of acaricide resistance, and the concept of Integrated Pest Management (IPM) has been proposed [20]. IPM aims to minimize impacts on animals, humans, and the environment and to slow down the emergence of acaricide-resistant PRMs by reducing the use of chemical acaricides. This is achieved by continuous monitoring, preventing the invasion and proliferation of PRMs through non-chemical control methods, and using chemical acaricides as a last resort. In the framework of IPM, efforts to establish non-chemical control methods are a crucial component, and various studies have been conducted to establish new strategies, including the development of repellents using plant-derived ingredients [14, 80, 83], biological control using pathogens and organisms that serve as predators against PRMs [41, 88], application of semiochemicals, such as kairomones and pheromones [37, 51], and vaccine approaches (summarized in Table 2). Of these, we focused on establishing a vaccine-based control strategy, which has been studied as a control method for ticks that transmit pathogens [21, 92]. The vaccine-based strategy for PRMs is based on the principle that the antibodies produced by immunization with recombinant proteins of molecules that have physiologically crucial functions in PRMs are taken up during PRM blood sucking, thereby inhibiting protein functions and damaging the tissues in PRMs. The advantage of vaccine approaches is that a long-term effect can be expected if high antibody titers are induced for a certain period, which could contribute to a reduction in acaricide use. Thus, the vaccine-based control has the potential to be integrated into the IPM framework in terms of preventing the proliferation of PRMs without the use of chemical acaricides [20]. Here, the identified vaccine antigens and future prospects for anti-PRM vaccines are discussed.

Identification of vaccine antigens and assessment of their efficacies

The vaccine antigens identified to date are listed in Table 2. Although the efficacy of several antigens has been demonstrated, the results are affected by the condition of PRMs, the environment, and the methods; therefore, it would be inappropriate to simply compare the efficacy reported in each study. Because the anti-PRM vaccine is thought to exert its effect when PRMs take up antibodies into their bodies during blood sucking, we considered that vaccine antigens are molecules that have important physiological functions, are expressed during blood sucking, and are expressed in the midgut, a tissue that is readily exposed to antibodies. Several studies, including whole genome and RNA sequencing, have been conducted to obtain genetic information about PRMs [4, 12, 29, 73]. To identify the molecules highly expressed in blood-fed PRMs, we compared the transcriptomes of blood-fed and starved PRMs and performed a domain search to predict the functions of each molecule [29]. The expression levels of the identified molecules at each developmental stage of blood-fed and-starved PRMs were further analyzed using quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) [2]. Additionally, expression in the midgut has been confirmed through laser capture microdissection and reverse transcription-PCR [59], or in situ hybridization [74]. Thus, we identified molecules that satisfied our criteria as vaccine antigens. Subsequently, the efficacies of vaccine antigens, including mortality and fecundity, were assessed in PRMs artificially fed with plasma from chickens immunized with the recombinant proteins of each antigen [2]. Various molecules have been identified as potential vaccine antigens, such as: enzymes and their inhibitors, which are predicted to be involved in the growth or blood digestion of PRMs, such as proteases [3, 59] and a protease inhibitor [27]; ferritin 2 (FER2) [95, 101], which is involved in iron transport from the midguts to other tissues; and molecules predicted to be expressed on the cell membrane, such as copper transporter [28] and adipocyte plasma membrane-associated protein (APMAP) [30]. Notably, the combination of immune plasma against different vaccine antigens, cystatin and APMAP, exhibited an increased mortality in PRMs [27], suggesting the utility of “cocktail vaccines” combining multiple antigens. For the practical application of anti-PRM vaccines, the development of cocktail vaccines is a promising strategy to enhance their efficacy. However, no increase in the efficacy was observed in the combination of immune plasma against cystatin and cysteine protease, respectively (unpublished observation). Therefore, the combination of molecules involved in different physiological processes may be preferable as antigens for cocktail vaccines. Further investigation is required to identify appropriate combinations as antigens for cocktail vaccines.

Potential application of anti-PRM vaccines for the control of other avian mites

Similar to PRMs, infestation with other avian mites, such as northern fowl mites (NFMs; Ornithonyssus sylviarum) and tropical fowl mites (TFMs; Ornithonyssus bursa), can cause economic losses in poultry farms. NFMs are distributed worldwide [34, 61, 79, 84], whereas TFMs are found only in tropical and subtropical areas [79, 84]. Both mites suck blood from chickens, and their morphology resembles that of PRMs [23]. Therefore, anti-PRM vaccine antigens highly conserved among avian mite species may have broad-spectrum efficacy; these would be more ideal antigens for the development of “universal vaccines” against hematophagous avian mites. We examined the feasibility of four antigens, glutathione S-transferase (GST) [96], FER2 [95], cysteine protease [94], and histamine release factor [93], as universal vaccine antigens against hematophagous avian mites. Immune plasma samples against recombinant proteins of each antigen from PRMs cross-reacted with those from TFMs and NFMs. Moreover, we observed the increased mortality of PRMs fed with immune plasmas against each antigen from other mite species in vitro, implying its potential for application as a universal vaccine against avian mites. However, these effects were assessed on PRMs because of the limited availability of NFMs and TFMs in Japan. Therefore, further assessment using NFMs and TFMs in hens is required to evaluate the application of anti-PRM vaccines to other avian mites.

Future perspectives

The development of vaccines and improvements in sanitary conditions have greatly contributed to the reduction in avian diseases. However, the poultry industry in several countries faces various problems. In this review, the mechanisms underlying the increased virulence of MDV and the development of anti-PRM vaccines as a novel strategy to control PRMs were discussed. Both are common problems in poultry farms worldwide, potentially harming the health of chickens and reducing their productivity. Elucidation of the effects of Meq sequences on MDV virulence could be useful for monitoring the risk of future outbreaks caused by MDV circulating on poultry farms, and the Meq sequence that contributes to low virulence may be informative for the development of novel vaccines against MD. Currently, the application of recombinant viruses derived from pathogenic MDV strains lacking the meq gene has been investigated as a novel vaccine; however, careful evaluation and modification are required to address side effects such as thymus atrophy [26, 39, 44]. Furthermore, previous reports suggest that the effector function of γδ T cells against MDV-infected or transformed cells, in addition to the response elicited by CD8 T cells, is crucial for the protective efficacy of vaccines against MD [52, 53, 68]. Therefore, for the development of next-generation vaccines, it would be desirable to clarify how differences in the pathogenicity and Meq sequences among MDV strains affect the responses of both T cell subsets and to create innovative strategies for their efficient activation. The development of anti-PRM vaccines is a promising approach as a novel method to control PRMs; however, in field trials, the efficacy has declined with a reduction in antibody titers [7]. Thus, maintaining antibody titers is a major issue for practical applications. The application of exposed antigens that are injected into the host during blood feeding by PRMs may be an approach for maintaining antibody titers [32]. Additionally, in most studies, the efficacy of vaccine antigens has been verified by immunization using recombinant proteins; therefore, vectors [64] or other immunization procedures are required to vaccinate chickens more easily for practical applications. Thus, further studies that include combinations of highly effective antigens suitable for cocktail vaccines, appropriate adjuvants, and easy-to-use vaccines are warranted. An oral vaccine based on nanoparticles, as explored for the prevention of salmonellosis, may provide a potential solution for maintaining antibody titers and developing easy-to-use vaccines [24, 82]. By developing basic research into applied research, with the aim of contributing to production frontlines, it is hoped that the outcomes will lead to improvements and solutions to various global issues facing the poultry industry.
