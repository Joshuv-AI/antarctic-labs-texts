Role of β-adrenergic signaling and the NLRP3 inflammasome in chronic intermittent hypoxia-induced murine lung cancer progression
Authors: Jianxia Sun, Xinyun Jia, Zhiqiang Zhang, Yang Yang, Chuntao Zhai, Baosheng Zhao, Yuzhen Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundObstructive sleep apnea (OSA), characterized by chronic intermittent hypoxia (CIH), is a prevalent condition that has been associated with various forms of cancer. Although some clinical studies suggest a potential link between OSA and lung cancer, this association remains uncertain, and the underlying mechanisms are not fully understood. This study investigated the role of the catecholamine-β-adrenergic receptor (βAR) and the NLRP3 inflammasome in mediating the effects of CIH on lung cancer progression in mice.MethodsMale C57BL/6 N mice were subjected to CIH for four weeks, with Lewis lung carcinoma cells seeded subcutaneously. Propranolol (a βAR blocker) or nepicastat (an inhibitor of catecholamine production) was administered during this period. Tumor volume and tail artery blood pressure were monitored. Immunohistochemical staining and immunofluorescence staining were employed to assess protein expression of Ki-67, CD31, VEGFR2, PD-1, PD-L1, and ASC specks in tumor tissues. ELISA was used to detect catecholamine and various cytokines, while western blot assessed the expression of cyclin D1, caspase-1, and IL-1β. In vitro tube formation assay investigated angiogenesis. NLRP3 knockout mice were used to determine the mechanism of NLRP3 in CIH.ResultsCIH led to an increase in catecholamine. Catecholamine-βAR inhibitor drugs prevented the increase in blood pressure caused by CIH. Notably, the drugs inhibited CIH-induced murine lung tumor growth, and the expression of Ki-67, cyclin D1, CD31, VEGFR2, PD-1 and PD-L1 in tumor decreased. In vitro, propranolol inhibits tube formation induced by CIH mouse serum. Moreover, CIH led to an increase in TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, IFN-γ and sPD-L1 levels and a decrease in IL-10 in peripheral blood, accompanied by activation of NLRP3 inflammasomes in tumor, but these effects were also stopped by drugs. In NLRP3-knockout mice, CIH-induced upregulation of PD-1/PD-L1 in tumor was inhibited.ConclusionsOur study underscores the significant contribution of β-adrenergic signaling and the NLRP3 inflammasome to CIH-induced lung cancer progression. These pathways represent potential therapeutic targets for mitigating the impact of OSA on lung cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12931-024-02969-x.

Background

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a pervasive disorder characterized by repetitive upper airway occlusions during sleep, leading to chronic intermittent hypoxia (CIH), heightened respiratory efforts, and sleep fragmentation [1]. Its prevalence has gradually increased in recent years, affecting an estimated nearly 1 billion individuals worldwide [2]. Over the past few decades, clinical data, coupled with in vivo and in vitro experiments, point to key mechanisms underlying OSA-related diseases, including sympathetic nerve activation, oxidative stress, and inflammation [3]. OSA, as an independent risk factor, is intricately linked to many diseases, such as cardiovascular disease, cognitive impairment and metabolic disorders [1]. This multifaceted impact underscores the far-reaching consequences of OSA on overall health. Notably, emerging research suggests a noteworthy association between OSA and the prevalence and incidence of cancer [4, 5].

Lung cancer holds the grim distinction of being the leading cause of cancer-related fatalities [6]. The potential connection between OSA and lung cancer has sparked interest. Some studies indicate a noteworthy link, revealing that individuals with OSA face a roughly 30% higher risk of developing lung cancer compared to those without OSA [7]. Additionally, OSA severity in a sleep cohort is associated with an increased risk of death in stage III and IV lung cancer patients [8]. However, the relationship between OSA and lung cancer remains contentious, marked by conflicting results in various studies [9, 10]. This debate has spurred an expanding body of research delving into the intricate connections. CIH, a key feature of OSA, has surfaced as a critical factor linking OSA to lung cancer. Hye et al. found that CIH promote murine lung cancer progression, and the effect may be related to Wnt/β-catenin signaling and Nrf2 signaling [11]. In addition, Noelia et al. confirmed the crucial role of COX-2 signaling in CIH-exacerbated lung cancer progression [12]. It can be seen that the impact of CIH on lung cancer is multifaceted. Yet, further exploration is needed to uncover the deeper mechanisms at play in this relationship.

The sympathetic-adrenomedullary axis is an important neuroendocrine system that plays an essential role in chronic stress. Increased efferent sympathetic nerve activity is one of the pathological mechanisms in patients with OSA. Interestingly, OSA causes chronic elevations in plasma catecholamines concentrations in adults and children [13, 14], and these findings have been demonstrated in animals exposed to CIH [15]. Catecholamine, including dopamine (DA), norepinephrine (NE), and epinephrine (EPI), undergo a conversion process. DA is transformed into NE by dopamine β-hydroxylase (DBH), and NE generates EPI through phenylethanolamine-N-methyltransferase transmethylation. DA primarily affects the central nervous system, while NE and EPI, known for their role in the “fight-or-flight” response, act through α-adrenergic receptors (α-AR) and β-adrenergic receptors (β-AR) [16].

Sympathetic nervous system activation, causing a significant release of catecholamine, is a recognized factor in stress-related cancer [17]. It mentioned that stress-induced NE activates β-AR signaling and promotes pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma cell proliferation through sympathetic innervation or local accumulation. In addition, in many solid tumors, such as ovarian and pancreatic cancers, EPI increases the expression of VEGF, promoting tumor angiogenesis by activating β2-AR-HIF-1α [16]. Furthermore, chronic stress-induced elevation of catecholamines can inhibit the immune response and contribute to tumor immune evasion [16]. In summary, these findings highlight the essential role of catecholamine-βAR in promoting cancer. However, it is unclear whether the chronic increase in catecholamine contributes to the progression of lung cancer in mice caused by CIH, and the mechanism is uncertain.

In patients with OSA, CIH has been found to enhance the baseline inflammatory response, playing a key role in some disease development, such as cardiovascular diseases [18]. The NLRP3 (nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-like receptor protein 3) inflammasome is an intracellular sensor that activates inflammation. It consists of NLRP3, ASC (apoptosis-associated speck-like protein with a caspase recruitment domain), and pro-caspase-1. When activated, this complex triggers the activation of caspase-1, leading to the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1β and IL-18, which drive inflammation. Studies have shown that NLRP3 inflammasomes may be key targets for controlling inflammation in chronic diseases [19]. Evidence also suggests that NLRP3 activation is an early response to hypoxia and is regulated by hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha (HIF-1α) [20]. Given that CIH promotes systemic inflammation and NLRP3’s role in this process, we hypothesize that NLRP3 could be a crucial mediator of CIH-induced lung cancer progression.

The purpose of this study is to explore the mechanism by which catecholamine-βAR signaling and NLRP3 inflammasomes contributing the effects of CIH on lung cancer progression, with the aim of identifying potential therapeutic targets within these signaling pathways.

Methods

Cells culture

Mouse Lewis lung carcinoma cells (LLC) and SVEC4-10 cells were purchased from the National Infrastructure of Cell Line Resource (Shanghai, Chinese). All cells were incubated in Dulbecco’s Modification of Eagle’s Medium(DMEM)containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, 2115802RP, Gibco, Life Technologies Australia Pty Ltd, Australia), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37℃ in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2.

Cell counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

Cell viability was measured with a CCK-8 assay (MC0301, Jackson Bio). SVEC4-10 cells were seeded at a density of 3 × 103 cells/well in 96-well plates. Waiting adherent, cells were incubated with 0, 0.5, 1, 2, 5, and 10 µM propranolol for 48 h. Based to kit instructions, 10 µl CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated at 37℃ for 4 h. Then, the absorbance of each well at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Epoch2, Biotek, Formant, USA).

Animals and chronic intermittent hypoxia exposure

Seventy-two 6-week-old male C57BL/6 N mice (weighing 19 ~ 21 g) were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology (China). 6-week-old male C57BL/6 N NLRP3 knockout mice were donated by Professor Xianwei Wang (Xinxiang Medical University, Henan, China). All mice were housed in standard cages with a 12:12-hour light-dark cycle and had free access to water and food. The β-AR blocker propranolol (Prop) and dopamine β-hydroxylase inhibitor nepicastat (Nepi) used in the experiment were purchased from Yabang Epson Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd (Jiangsu, China) and Adooq, China, respectively. One g of carboxymethylcellulose sodium (CMC-Na, Sangon Biotech) powder was dissolved in 100 ml of ultrapure water in a water bath at 60°C, which was used as vehicle.

CIH exposure was accomplished with an O2 controller (Oxycycler A84XOV, BioSpherix, USA). The pattern consisted of cycles of 30 s of FiO2 0.21 (21%) followed by 20 s of FiO2 0.06 (6%) for 8 h every day. This pattern mimics the oscillations of arterial oxygen saturation observed in patients with moderately OSA. The mice were randomly assigned to the six groups (n = 12 in each group): control plus vehicle (Con + Vehi), control plus propranolol (Con + Prop), control plus nepicastat (Con + Nepi), CIH plus vehicle (CIH + Vehi), CIH plus propranolol (CIH + Prop) and CIH plus nepicastat (CIH + Nepi). The control group was reared in a room air environment. The body weight of the mice was measured every two days. Prior to daily exposure, the mice were administrated 6 mg/kg body weight of propranolol or 10 mg/kg body weight of nepicastat or vehicle through oral gavage.

On the 7th day of exposures to room air or CIH, a total of 3 × 105 LLC cells in 100 µl phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) were injected subcutaneously into the right flank of the mouse. When the tumor became palpable, its length (L) and width (W) were recorded using an electric caliper on a weekly basis. Tumor volume (V, mm3) was calculated as 0.52 × L × W2. The experiment lasted 4 weeks.

The model for C57BL/6 N wild type and NLRP3 knockout mice was the same as above (n = 5 in each group).

Blood pressure measurement

The systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP) and mean arterial pressure (MAP) were measured non-invasively with a tail cuff method (BP-2010 A; Softron Biotechnology Ltd., Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Before measurement, the mouse was first allowed to acclimate to its surroundings for approximately 10 min. The blood pressure data were the average of 3 consecutive measurements, which differ within 10 mmHg from each other. Blood pressure was measured twice during the experiment: once the day before the start and once the day before the end of the experiment.

Serum and tissue sample preparation from LLC-implanted mice

At the end of the experiment, all mice were anesthetized using inhaled isoflurane (RWD LIFE SCIENCE), and blood was collected via cardiac puncture. The tumor tissue was carefully peeled off, weighed, and photographed. The tumor tissues were either frozen in -80℃ freezer for further analysis or fixed in 10% formalin for histological examination. The blood was centrifuged at 4℃ and 12,000 rpm for 15 min to obtain the serum, which was then frozen in -80℃ freezer for ELISA and cell experiment.

Enzyme linked immunosorbent assay, ELISA

Serum NE, EPI, TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, IFN-γ, IL-10 and sPD-L1 were measured using a murine ELISA kit respectively (mlbio, shanghai, ml063805 (NE), ml002049 (EPI), YJ002095 (TNF-α), ml063159 (IL-6), ml301814 (IL-1β), YJ037873 (IL-10), YJ002277 (IFN-γ), ml058347 (sPD-L1)) according to manufacturer’s instructions. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Epoch2, Biotek, Formant, USA).

Tube formation assay

Matrigel (BD-356237, Coring, New York, USA) was added to 96-well plates at 50 µl/well with a 1:2 (DMEM) dilution and incubated at 37℃ for 45 min. SVEC4-10 cells were inoculated into 96-well plates at 3 × 104/well. The cells were subsequently treated with either mouse serum or in combination with 2 µM propranolol. and incubated at 37°C for 4 h. The formation of capillary-like structures was observed and photographed under a 100x bright-field microscope (Ts2, Nikon, Japan), and the capillary tubes were quantified by measuring the junction points of the tubule structure using Fiji ImageJ. The experiment was repeated three times for each treatment.

Immuno-staining

Fixed tumor tissues were embedded in paraffin (KD-BM, KEDEE, Zhejiang, China) and sectioned into 4-µm slices (HistoCore BIOCUT, Leica Biosystems). The sections were then heated at 65℃ for 1 h, followed by two 20-minute washes in xylene to remove the paraffin. Subsequently, the sections were rehydrated in 100% ethanol (3 min), 95% ethanol (3 min), 80% ethanol (3 min), 60% ethanol (3 min). Antigen retrieval was performed in 0.01 M citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95℃ for 10 min. To block endogenous peroxidase activity, the sections were treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide at room temperature for 10 min. Following this, the sections were permeabilized in ultrapure water containing 0.2% Triton for 10 min. Afterward, the sections were blocked with 10% goat serum in PBS for 1 h. The sections were then incubated with the primary antibodies: anti-mouse Ki67 (1:500, 12202 S, Cell Signaling Technology), CD31 (1:200, 77699 S, CST), VEGFR2 (1:400, 9698 S, CST), PD-L1 (1:500, 66248-1-Ig, Proteintech), PD-1 (1:1000, 18106-1-AP, Proteintech) at 4℃ overnight. Following three washes with PBS for 15 min each, the sections were incubated with GTVision™ III Detection System Mouse & Rabbit Kit (HRP/DAB) (GK500705, Gene Tech). Finally, the sections were stained with hematoxylin for 5 s to visualize nuclei. All images were observed via the microscope (DM4B, Leica, Germany) and photographed with camera (LAS X, Leica, Germany).

Immunofluorescence staining was employed to detect ASC (apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a CARD) spots in tumor tissue. The sections were exposed to rabbit-anti-mouse ASC primary antibody (1:800, 67824 S, CST) at 4℃ overnight, then were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488 Goat Anti-rabbit IgG (1:300, 4412 S, CST) in the dark at room temperature for 1 h. DAPI was added dropwise for enclosing the sections. The images were analyzed with Fiji ImageJ.

Western blotting

Tumor tissues were cut into small pieces and suspended in RIPA lysis buffer (AR0105, Boster Bio) containing 1% protease inhibitor (AR1182, Boster Bio), 1% PMSF (AR1192, Boster Bio) and 5% phosphatase stop (4906845001, Roche). After being ground on ice for 10 min, the lysate was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4℃. The protein content of the supernatant was determined using the BCA assay (BCA02, DING GUO). A total of 50 µg protein for each sample was separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and then transferred to a PVDF membrane (IPVH00010, Millipore, MA, USA). After blocking with 5% non-fat dry milk (232100, Boster Bio) for 1 h, the membrane was incubated with primary antibodies against cyclin D1 (1:1000, #55506, CST), caspase-1/cleaved caspase-1 (1:1000, WL03450, Wanleibio), pro-IL-1β/IL-1β (1:500, sc-52012, Santa Cruz), and β-actin (1:1000, 20536-1-AP, Proteintech) at 4℃ overnight. After washing, the membrane was incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit or goat anti-mouse secondary antibodies (IH-0011, DING GUO; bs-0296G-HRP, Bioss) at room temperature for 1 h. The immunoreactive protein signals were visualize using Super Signal West Pico Chemiluminescent Substrate (AR1174, Boster Bio) and captured with the Amersham Imager 600 system (GE, America). The results were quantified with Fiji ImageJ. The experiment was repeated three times.

Statistical analyses

The data are expressed as the mean (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\stackrel{-}{X}$$\end{document}) ± standard deviation (SD) and were analyzed using SPSS 26.0. Both normality test and variance homogeneity test are carried out. Two-way ANOVA and multiple comparison tests were used for multiple groups of data. Results

The suppression of catecholamine-βAR effectively inhibits CIH-induced murine lung cancer progression

During the experiment, there was no significant difference in the body weight of mice in each group (Fig. 1A). With the extension of the experimental time, tumors formed and gradually grew. We found that after CIH treatment, the volume of xenograft tumors was significantly increased compared to the Con + Vehi group (Fig. 1B and C, P < 0.001). It confirmed the positive effect of CIH in promoting the growth of lung xenograft tumor. Notably, either propranolol or nepicastat significantly inhibited the CIH-induced tumor growth (P < 0.001). Moreover, the tumor weight-to-body weight ratio increased by CIH decreased significantly after taking medication (Fig. 1D, P < 0.001). In addition, we did observe that the levels of NE and EPI were upregulated in the CIH + Vehi group compared to the Con + Vehi group (Fig. 1E and F, P < 0.01). Administration of nepicastat decreased the levels of NE (P < 0.05) and EPI induced by CIH. All of these pointed to catecholamine-βAR mediating the role of lung xenograft tumor growth by CIH.

Notably, CIH-induced sympathetic activation, known to stimulate adrenal medulla catecholamine production, often leads to clinically increased blood pressure [21]. Pre-experiment, blood pressure showed no significant differences between groups (Fig. S1A). After 4 weeks of CIH, mice exhibited significantly higher blood pressure than Con + Vehi (Fig. S1B, P < 0.01). Both propranolol and nepicastat effectively lowered CIH-induced elevation of blood pressure (P < 0.05), albeit to varying degrees. These findings suggest that CIH activates the sympathetic-adrenal medullary axis, possibly explaining CIH’s promotion of lung xenograft tumor growth through catecholamine-βAR.

The suppression of catecholamine-βAR effectively inhibits CIH-induced tumor proliferation

To assess the impact of CIH on tumor proliferation and explore the potential involvement of catecholamine-βAR inhibition, we conducted IHC staining for Ki-67, a nuclear antigen indicative of cell proliferation and reflective of the tumor multiplication rate. Our analysis revealed a significant increase in Ki-67 positive expression in the CIH + Vehi group compared to the Con + Vehi group (Fig. 1G and H, P < 0.001). Notably, the proliferative effect induced by CIH was markedly mitigated following the administration of propranolol and nepicastat (P < 0.001), underscoring the pivotal role of catecholamine-βAR in mediating CIH-induced tumor proliferation. This observed trend was further substantiated by the modulation of cyclin D1 protein levels, as illustrated in Fig. 1I and J. Specifically, CIH prompted an upregulation of cyclin D1, a phenomenon subsequently alleviated by the administration of the aforementioned inhibitory agents. Collectively, our findings demonstrate that the inhibition of catecholamine-βAR effectively suppresses CIH-induced tumor proliferation.

Catecholamine-βAR mediates CIH-induced tumor angiogenesis, both in vivo and in vitro

Angiogenesis, a crucial aspect of tumor growth, facilitates the provision of essential nutrients for tumor expansion. In our in vivo investigation, we conducted CD31 immunohistochemistry assays (Fig. 2A) to assess the presence of endothelial cells and tumor angiogenesis. CIH significantly increased microvascular formation compared with the control group (Fig. 2B, P < 0.001). Importantly, treatment with propranolol and nepicastat effectively suppressed CIH-induced microvascular formation (P < 0.001). Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) serves as a potent inducer of angiogenesis, with VEGF receptor 2 (VEGFR2) playing a pivotal role in mediating VEGF-induced angiogenesis [22]. We further performed VEGFR2 immunohistochemistry assays (Fig. 2C). The results depicted in Fig. 2D indicated a significant increase in the expression levels of VEGFR2 following CIH exposure (P < 0.001) compared to the Con + Vehi group. However, administration of propranolol and nepicastat significantly curtailed the elevation induced by CIH (P < 0.01). These results underscore the indispensable role of catecholamine-βAR in CIH-induced tumor angiogenesis in vivo.

To further elucidate the contribution of catecholamine-βAR in promoting angiogenesis, we conducted tube formation experiments in vitro. SVEC4-10 cells were treated with serum from mice in each group (Fig. 2E). As anticipated, cells treated with serum from the CIH + Vehi group exhibited accelerated tube wall formation (Fig. 2F, P < 0.001), indicating a heightened ability of mouse serum post-CIH to promote tube formation. Importantly, treatment with 2 µM propranolol (Fig. S2) hindered the accelerated tube formation induced by the serum from CIH-exposed mouse (Fig. 2H, P < 0.001), suggesting that catecholamine-βAR actively promote angiogenesis in vitro. In summary, both in vivo and in vitro experiments illustrated the promoting effects of catecholamine-βAR on CIH-induced tumor angiogenesis.

The suppression of catecholamine-βAR inhibits CIH-induced PD-1/PD-L1 immune evasion

Programed cell death 1 (PD-1), mostly expressed on the surface of activated T cells, is a negative regulator of immune responses. The binding of PD-1 and PD-1 ligand 1 (PD-L1) promotes the inactivation of T cells, leading to immune evasion [23]. In order to explore the effect of CIH on PD-1/PD-L1 immune evasion, PD-1/PD-L1 IHC staining was used to detect tumor immune evasion levels (Fig. 3A). Compared with the Con + Vehi group, the positive staining degree of PD-1 in the CIH group was significantly deeper (Fig. 3B, P < 0.001), the same to PD-L1(Fig. 3C, P < 0.001). We hypothesized that catecholamine-βAR may influence their expression in tumors. As expected, propranolol or nepicastat treatment resulted in a significant decrease in PD-1/PD-L1 expression in tumors than the CIH + Vehi group (P < 0.01). This result confirmed our previous hypothesis that catecholamine-βAR does contribute to CIH-induced PD-1/PD-L1 increases.

As one of the important immune checkpoint molecules that can be targeted by cancer immunotherapies, PD-L1 also has a soluble form (sPD-L1). Several studies have shown that high levels of sPD-L1 are strongly associated with tumor progression. Higher sPD-L1 levels have a worse prognosis [24]. Therefore, we also measured the level of sPD-L1 in the mice serum by ELISA. The data from the CIH + Vehi group revealed elevated sPD-L1 concentrations as compared with the Con + Vehi group (Fig. 3D, P < 0.001), whereas giving propranolol or nepicastat during CIH resulted in a lower outcome than CIH treatment alone (P < 0.001). It suggested that CIH also upregulated serum sPD-L1 levels in mice, which was associated with catecholamine-βAR. Interestingly, we analyzed the correlation between sPD-L1 levels and tumor weight-to-body weight ratio and found a positive correlation (Fig. 3E, P < 0.05). As previous studies have suggested, sPD-L1 may be closely related to the malignant progression of tumor.

Catecholamine-βAR mediates the activation of NLRP3 inflammasomes induced by CIH

To assess NLRP3 inflammasome activation in tumor tissues, we conducted ASC specks immunofluorescence assays (Fig. 4A). ASC fluorescent spots in the tumor tissue increased after CIH compared to the Con + Vehi group (Fig. 4B, P < 0.001), a response suppressed after either propranolol or nepicastat application (P < 0.01). And with that, the levels of cleaved caspase1 and cleaved IL-1β in tumor tissues following CIH were upregulated (Fig. 4C, D and E), but thereby decreased after drug treatment. Similarly, IL-1β was also significantly elevated in CIH mice serum, which was inhibited by propranolol and nepicastat (Fig. 4F, P < 0.01). At the same time, there was a significant positive correlation between the level of IL-1β and the tumor weight-to-body weight ratio (Fig. 4G, P < 0.05).

The suppression of catecholamine-βAR inhibits CIH-induced inflammatory response, correlating with tumor progression

To investigate the potential association between catecholamine-βAR and CIH-induced inflammation, various inflammation-related factors were examined. As depicted in Fig. 5A, B and C, the levels of pro-inflammatory factors (TNF-α, IL-6, and IFN-γ) were significantly higher in the CIH + Vehi group compared with the Con + Vehi group (P < 0.001). Remarkably, mice treated with either propranolol or nepicastat exhibited a significant decrease in these factors compared with the CIH + Vehi group (P < 0.05), indicating a mitigating effect of the drugs. Moreover, following exposure to CIH, there was a significant decrease in the anti-inflammatory factor IL-10 levels (Fig. 5D, P < 0.001), with partial recovery observed after drug treatment (P < 0.01). These findings suggest that CIH promotes the inflammatory response with the involvement of catecholamine-βAR. Furthermore, a correlation between these inflammatory factors and the tumor weight-to-body weight ratio was observed (Fig. 5a, b, c and d, P < 0.05), implying a subtle relationship between chronic inflammation and CIH-induced tumor progression.

The activation of NLRP3 inflammasome is associated with CIH-induced upregulation of PD-1/PD-L1 in the tumor tissues

Ding Y et al. revealed a link between NLRP3 and tumor progression, underscoring its close association with tumor-related inflammation and a significant positive correlation with PD-1 and PD-L1 [25]. Thus, we analyzed the relationship between IL-1β and sPD-L1 and found a significant positive correlation between the two (Fig. 6A, P < 0.01). To explore whether CIH-induced upregulation of PD-1 and PD-L1 is linked to NLRP3 inflammasome activation, we conducted experiments with NLRP3 knockout (NLRP3−/−) and wild-type (WT) mice. Compared to CIH-WT mice, CIH-NLRP3−/− mice exhibited inhibited tumor growth (Fig. S3A, S3B, P < 0.05), reduced ASC speck fluorescence (Fig. 6B and C, P < 0.05) indicating NLRP3 inflammasome activation, and increased levels of cleaved caspase-1 and IL-1β proteins (Fig. 6D, E and F, P < 0.05), suggesting altered inflammatory response. Immunostaining showed decreased PD-1 and PD-L1 levels in tumor tissues from NLRP3−/− mice (Fig. 6G, H and I, P < 0.01), indicating NLRP3’s potential modulation of PD-1 and PD-L1 expression. These findings underscore NLRP3’s critical role in tumor progression and the upregulation of PD-1 and PD-L1.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that CIH significantly accelerates lung cancer progression in murine models. This tumor-promoting effect was effectively reduced by β-adrenergic blockade, inhibition of catecholamine production, and in NLRP3 knockout. These findings underscore the critical role of β-adrenergic signaling and the NLRP3 inflammasome in mediating the impact of CIH on lung cancer progression. Catecholamine elevation in blood due to sympathetic nerve activation is a known characteristic of patients with OSA[13]. Our study observed that levels of NE and EPI, which were increased by CIH, decreased upon administration of nepicastat. This indicates the effectiveness of nepicastat in mitigating sympathetic activity. Additionally, both propranolol and nepicastat prevented CIH-induced blood pressure increases, indicating an overactivation of the sympathetic nerve in CIH mice. This could explain the suppression of CIH-induced blood pressure increases after the drugs administration in our study. Notably, propranolol and nepicastat lowered blood pressure during CIH but not under normal conditions. This discrepancy may be attributed to β-adrenergic stimulation, which likely contributes to certain pathological features induced by CIH, but not all[26].

OSA-associated CIH has been linked to accelerated lung cancer growth, although the underlying mechanisms are complex [11, 12]. In our study, we observed that propranolol and nepicastat effectively inhibited CIH-accelerated growth of murine subcutaneous xenograft lung tumors, and this inhibition was associated with a decrease in the expression of Ki-67 and cyclin D1, markers of cell proliferation. Previous research suggests that catecholamine, such as EPI and isoproterenol (ISO), activate signaling pathways that promote cell proliferation, particularly through βARs [27, 28]. Additionally, β2-AR expression significantly correlates with Ki-67 expression in lung adenocarcinoma [29]. Therefore, we propose that catecholamine acting through βARs may contribute to CIH-induced tumor progression by promoting tumor proliferation.

Tumor angiogenesis facilitates tumor progression by providing necessary nutrients for growth. CIH exposure is known to promote lung cancer angiogenesis [11]. Our findings revealed a notable reduction in tumor microvascular density and VEGFR2 expression subsequent to the administration of propranolol and nepicastat. This observation holds significance considering VEGF’s role as a robust instigator of tumor angiogenesis. Upon binding with VEGFR2, VEGF initiates the phosphorylation cascade of downstream PI3K, thereby governing pivotal processes such as endothelial cell survival, migration, and the formation of blood vessels [22, 30]. Previous research has shown that catecholamine-mediated β-AR activation promotes tumor angiogenesis in various cancer models, including pancreatic, colon, and prostate cancers [31–33]. Our findings suggest that the inhibitory effects on angiogenesis by propranolol and nepicastat contribute significantly to preventing the progression of CIH-induced lung cancer. Additionally, the in vitro tube formation assay revealed that propranolol significantly reduced the tube formation of SVEC4-10 cells induced by serum from CIH-exposed mice, indicating activation of βAR as a potential mechanism underlying CIH-promoted tumor angiogenesis. Therefore, the involvement of catecholamine acting through βARs in upregulating VEGFR2 to promote angiogenesis may be a critical mechanism mediating CIH-accelerated lung cancer progression.

Our findings demonstrate that propranolol and nepicastat effectively inhibited the CIH-induced upregulation of PD-1/PD-L1, both are implicated in CIH-induced lung cancer progression [34]. It was found that β-adrenergic signaling induced the activation of p38 MAPK, leading to phosphorylation of elF2α and subsequent upregulation of PD-L1 [35]. Moreover, inhibiting β-adrenergic signaling was found to decrease the number of PD-1 receptors on CD8+ T cells [36]. Overall, catecholamine-βAR involvement in CIH-induced murine lung cancer progression is mediated through PD-1/PD-L1 upregulation.

Previous research has demonstrated that activation of βARs activates NLRP3 inflammasomes, leading to oxidative stress and cardiac dysfunction [37]. In our study, we observed an increase in ASC fluorescence specks in tumor tissues exposed to CIH, which decreased following treatment with propranolol and nepicastat. Administration of these drugs significantly decreased protein expression levels of caspase-1 and IL-1β. Moreover, IL-1β levels in peripheral blood were positively associated with tumor weight-to-body weight ratio, indicating a link between catecholamine acting through βARs induce NLRP3 inflammasome activation. Our findings also revealed a significant positive correlation between sPD-L1 and IL-1β levels, suggesting a connection between NLRP3 inflammasome activation and the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway. NLRP3 has been shown to regulate PD-1/PD-L1 expression across various cancer types [25]. Treatment with MCC950, an NLRP3-specific inhibitor, reduced PD-L1 expression in Hep3B hepatoma cells, indicating that intrinsic NLRP3 activation in cancer cells upregulates PD-L1 expression. Additionally, exogenous IL-1β induced PD-L1 expression in lung cancer cell lines through NF-κB and STAT3 signaling [38], while IL-1β from M1 macrophages activated NF-κB, inducing PD-L1 expression in hepatocellular carcinoma [39]. Furthermore, it has reported that IL-1β production mediates the expansion and activation of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), leading to the accumulation of PD-1+ CD8+ T cells, thereby promoting lung tumor progression [40]. In our study, CIH-induced upregulation of PD-1/PD-L1 levels in tumors was attenuated in NLRP3−/− mice, suggesting that CIH upregulates PD-1/PD-L1 by activating the NLRP3 inflammasome. Overall, NLRP3 inflammasome activation contributes to CIH-induced PD-1/PD-L1 upregulation in tumors, possibly through IL-1β-mediated pathways. This highlights a role for tumor cells or tumor-associated macrophage cells (TAMs) in regulating PD-L1/PD-1 expression via NLRP3 activation. Additionally, some studies have shown that PD-1/PD-L1 signaling can, in turn, regulate the activation of NLRP3 inflammasome by inhibiting STAT3, leading to immune escape [41]. Further research is needed to clarify the interaction between NLRP3 inflammasome and PD-1/PD-L1 pathway after CIH exposure. The interplay between these pathways promotes inflammation and immune suppression, supporting tumor growth. Targeting both pathways could counteract CIH-induced tumor growth and immune evasion, and combining inhibitors may disrupt this feedback loop, making it a promising area for future research and treatment.

It has been reported that CIH-induced lung cancer growth is associated with an inflammatory response [42]. Our previous results similarly demonstrated the activation of NLRP3 inflammasomes by CIH exposure. Interestingly, we found that after giving propranolol and nepicastat, CIH-induced changes in the levels of several peripheral blood cytokines, was inhibited, including changes in TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, IFN-γ, IL-10 and sPD-L1. Cumulative research underscores the neuro-immune system’s role as a complex signaling network, where the sympathetic nervous system’s influence on immune function emerges as pivotal in the advancement and worsening of chronic immune-mediated diseases [43]. As a significant neurotransmitter, NE directly regulates immune tissues such as the spleen, bone marrow, and thymus. Additionally, the signaling through βAR also prompts immune cells to produce various inflammatory factors [44]. The activation of the sympathetic nervous system induced by CIH is likely to trigger the regulation of catecholamine-βAR signaling in the systemic immune system, leading to alterations in peripheral blood cytokine levels. In this study, we found that the levels of these humoral factors were somewhat correlated with tumor progression. Research has verified that within chronic inflammation, TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β foster lung cancer advancement by enhancing cancer cell proliferation, angiogenesis, and the expression of invasive proteins through diverse signaling pathways [40, 45, 46]. Previous studies suggest that catecholamine acting on βAR directly impact IFN-γ secretion in T cells. The timing of adrenergic engagement during cell activation—whether before, during, or after—leads to decreased, unchanged, or increased IFN-γ secretion, respectively [47]. This implies that the observed elevation of IFN-γ in our study might be regulated sequentially by CIH and catecholamine. Another study revealed that IL-10’s impact on tumor cell viability in vitro is indirect. Instead, it fosters the infiltration of CD8+ T cells into tumors, leading to subsequent IFN-γ secretion and tumor suppression [48]. The decrease in IL-10 induced by CIH may diminish this effect. Moreover, sPD-L1, a PD-L1 homolog, serves as a biomarker in cancer patients with OSA, showing a positive correlation with tumor progression [49]. In summary, catecholamine acting on βAR are implicated in the extensive inflammatory response network triggered by CIH and collectively contribute to regulating tumor progression.

Our results suggest that βAR blockers could be a promising treatment for OSA patients with lung cancer, but several limitations must be addressed. Although our findings from murine models are significant, translating these results to humans is challenging due to anatomical and physiological differences. Additionally, our results indicate that propranolol reduces PD-1/PD-L1 levels, which could be problematic for OSA patients with lung cancer who are also receiving immune checkpoint inhibitors. Therefore, the clinical application of βAR blockers must consider individualized patient differences and acceptability, highlighting the need for a more personalized treatment approach. Moreover, propranolol’s non-selectivity makes it difficult to discern the specific roles of β1-AR and β2-AR in CIH-induced lung cancer progression, as different βAR subtypes have distinct effects on tumors. For instance, β1-AR overexpression depletes CD8+ T cells and promotes tumor growth [50], whereas high β2-AR levels in macrophages are linked to faster breast cancer progression [51], and β2-AR is notably higher in lung cancer than β1-AR [52]. Future research will use selective β1-AR and β2-AR antagonists, along with β2-AR knockout mice, aims to clarify their roles after CIH exposure and guide more targeted therapies.

Conclusion

Our study underscores the significant roles of β-adrenergic signaling and the NLRP3 inflammasome in CIH-induced lung cancer progression. These pathways represent potential therapeutic targets for mitigating the impact of OSA on lung cancer.
