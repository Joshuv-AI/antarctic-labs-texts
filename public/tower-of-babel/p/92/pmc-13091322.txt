Dynamics in the polyphenolic profile of sprouted mung bean (Vigna radiata L.): Impact of short-term germination and dehulling
Authors: Christina Wintersohle, Lena Löwe, Ismail-Hakki Acir, Lara Etzbach, Andreas Schieber, Ute Schweiggert-Weisz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Phenolic compounds in mung bean (Vigna radiata L. Wilczek) are important bioactive constituents whose levels and composition change during germination. However, their distribution between seed fractions (cotyledon and hull) and their compositional changes in dehulled cotyledons during early germination remain insufficiently understood. This study investigated how dehulling affects phenolic compound distribution and examined how short-term germination (4–48 h) influences the phenolic content and composition of dehulled mung bean sprouts using UHPLC-DAD-MSn.Among the 12 detected phenolic compounds, 11 were tentatively identified. Total phenolic content was highest in the hull (10,055.3 μg/g dw) and lowest in the ungerminated cotyledon (304.3 μg/g dw). Vitexin and isovitexin were the predominant flavonoids in both fractions, with the hull contributing 66.0% and 75.5% of total vitexin and isovitexin, respectively. During germination, several apigenin glycosides, particularly isovitexin-O-rhamnoside, and acylated apigenin derivatives, accumulated. These findings highlight time-dependent changes in flavonoid composition during early mung bean germination.

Introduction

Mung bean (Vigna radiata L. Wilczek) seeds are an excellent source of protein and have received considerable attention in recent research for their use as protein- and nutrient-rich flours and protein ingredients (Seetapan et al., 2023; Shrestha et al., 2023; Wintersohle et al., 2023).

For efficient fortification of food products with mung bean protein ingredients, dehulling is an essential step because the hull contains colored pigments such as chlorophyll and antinutritional compounds that can interfere with intestinal protein absorption and negatively affect the appearance and color of the final products (Huang et al., 2022; Mubarak, 2005; Wintersohle et al., 2024). At the same time, mung bean hulls are particularly rich in phenolic compounds, such as flavonoids, which significantly contribute to the health-promoting attributes of the seeds (Luo et al., 2016). Consequently, dehulling leads to the loss of bioactive compounds known for their antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties (He et al., 2016). Interestingly, dehulled mung bean sprouts showed elevated levels of phenolics expressed as total phenolic content (TPC) after 48 h of germination (Wintersohle et al., 2024), suggesting that substantial changes in the phenolic content occur in the cotyledon fraction during short-term germination. Because mung bean hulls adhere firmly to the cotyledon, short-term germination is a practical approach to simplify dehulling. Furthermore, dehulling in combination with germination may offer a strategy to restore the total phenolic content.

Germination of mung bean seeds is an efficient and cost-effective method for improving their nutritional profile. During this process, substantial transformations occur in macronutrients, micronutrients, and bioactive compounds (Ali & Elozeiri, 2017). Protein degradation during germination enhances their digestibility and modifies technofunctional properties (El-Adawy et al., 2003). At the same time, antinutritional compounds are reduced (Mubarak, 2005). Compared with ungerminated seeds, mung bean sprouts exhibit significantly higher levels of antioxidant compounds, including phenolic acids and flavonoids (Galieni et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2022). Furthermore, a three-day germination period has been shown to enhance the antiproliferative effects and cytoprotective effects of mung bean seeds (Kapravelou et al., 2020).

Since germination facilitates hull removal while also improving both the profile and concentration of polyphenols in mung beans, combining dehulling with germination may offer a balanced approach. This strategy may preserve the health benefits associated with phenolic compounds while fulfilling the practical demands for production of visually appealing protein- and nutrient-rich ingredients. Therefore, understanding the phenolic composition of the dehulled cotyledon fraction during short-term germination is of particular importance.

Previous studies have investigated the TPC and polyphenol profile of whole mung bean seeds and sprouts, often including the hull, and typically focusing either on the comparison between initial and final stages of germination or on multiple time points across several days (Kapravelou et al., 2020; Tajoddin et al., 2014). In contrast to previous studies focusing on whole seeds and longer germination periods, the present study specifically addresses short-term germination (4–48 h) in combination with dehulling and focuses on the cotyledon fraction. This approach enables a time-resolved characterization of early changes in the flavonoid profile that are directly relevant for the production of light-colored protein ingredients. Therefore, the aim of the present study was to analyze how different short-term germination periods affect the content and profile of phenolic compounds in the dehulled mung bean sprouts, with a particular emphasis on the early dynamics of apigenin-derived flavonoids.

Material and methods

Mung beans

Mung beans (Vigna radiata (L.) Wilczek) from Chinese organic farming were purchased from Keimgrün GmbH (Leun, Germany).

Chemicals and standards

Ultrapure water was obtained from a Direct-Q® Water Purification System. LC-MS grade water (filtered at 0.2 μm), methanol (≥ 99.9%), acetonitrile (≥ 99.9%) and formic acid (99%) from VWR International GmbH (Darmstadt, Germany) were used. For quantification of polyphenols, gallic acid monohydrate (purity ≥98%) from Fluka (Honeywell International Inc., Morristown, New Jersey, USA), kaempferol (purity ≥97%) from Phytoplan (Diehm & Neuberger GmbH, Heidelberg, Germany), ferulic acid (purity ≥99%) from Sigma-Aldrich (Merck KGaA, Taufkirchen, Germany), and vitexin (purity ≥95.0%) purchased from Supelco (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) were used as standards.

Mung bean germination

Mung bean sprouts were prepared according to Wintersohle et al. (2024). Whole mung bean seeds were used for germination. The seeds were washed and soaked in tap water 1:5 (w/v) at 20 °C ± 1 °C for 16 h in the dark. Subsequently, the beans were dehulled and dried, and the obtained seeds were referred to as “cotyledon”: The resulting hull fraction was preserved for the analysis of the polyphenolic profile of the mung bean hull and for the calculation of contribution rates. The other soaked beans were used for germination, spread between two wet cotton towels and placed in a combi steamer (iCombi Pro Steamer, Rational AG, Landsberg am Lech, Germany). The germination was carried out in the dark at 30 °C and 100% humidity. The beans were germinated for a duration of 4, 8, 12, 24, 32, and 48 h. After germination, aliquots of 100 g were taken, washed, and seed coats were manually removed. The germination process was interrupted by rapid cooling with liquid nitrogen. The samples were stored overnight in a freezer at −28 °C and subsequently lyophilized (Model Alpha 2–4 LSC, Christ GmbH, Osterode, Germany). All samples were ground into a fine flour (< 150 μm) for analysis and stored at −28 °C until polyphenol extraction. For comparison, unprocessed mung bean seeds, including the hull, referred to as “whole bean”, were also ground into flour (< 150 μm) and analyzed. Germination was performed in triplicate and the cotyledon fraction was used as the baseline to assess changes induced by dehulling and germination in the dehulled samples.

Extraction of polyphenols

For the qualitative and quantitative determination of polyphenols, the lyophilized mung bean flours were used and extracts from each sample were prepared. An aliquot of 0.5 g sample was dispersed in 3 mL extraction solvent consisting of methanol/water (70:30) containing 1% formic acid. The solution was mixed well and centrifuged (9000 g for 5 min). The supernatant was collected in a 10 mL flask. The pellet was redispersed in 3 mL of the extraction solvent and the extraction procedure was repeated twice. Prior to UHPLC analysis, the extract was filtered through 0.2 μm Chromafil RC-15 filters (Macherey-Nagel, Düren, Germany) and used for UHPLC-DAD and UHPLC-ESI-MSn analysis.

UHPLC-DAD quantification of polyphenols

UHPLC-DAD analysis was performed on a Prominence UFLC system (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) equipped with two Nexera X2 LC-30 CE high-pressure gradient pumps, a Prominence DGU-20A5R degasser, a Nexera SIL-30 AC Prominence autosampler (15 °C, injection volume 10 μL), a CTO-20 AC Prominence column oven at 40 °C, and a SPD-M20A Prominence diode array detector. An Acquity HSS-T3 RP18 column (150 × 2.1 mm; 1.8 μm particle size) combined with a precolumn (Acquity UPLC HSS T3 VanGuard, 100 Å, 2.1 × 5 mm, 1.8 μm) both from Waters GmbH (Eschborn, Germany) was used. Analytes were eluted with a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min using the following gradient: 0.5 min, 1% B; 1.5 min, 5% B; 5 min, 10% B; 9 min, 18% B; 11 min, 19% B; 13 min, 19% B; 16 min, 20% B; 18 min, 20% B; 21 min, 35% B; 22 min, 100% B; 24 min, 100% B; 25 min, 1% B with A being water/formic acid (99/1; v/v) and B being acetonitrile/formic acid (99/1; v/v). Data acquisition and processing were performed by the LabSolutions software version 5.85 (Shimadzu Corp., Kyoto, Japan). External standard calibration curves of ferulic acid, gallic acid, kaempferol, and vitexin were used for quantification at 280 nm. Hydroxycinnamic acid derivatives were expressed as ferulic acid equivalents (FAE), while benzoic acid derivatives were expressed as gallic acid equivalents (GAE). The total phenolic acid content was determined as the sum of all detected phenolic acids and quantified as their respective equivalents, expressed as μg/g dry weight (dw). Apigenin derivatives were expressed as vitexin equivalents per gram of dw (μg VE/g dw). The total apigenin derivatives were calculated as the sum of all newly formed apigenin-derived compounds. Kaempferol and flavonoid derivatives were quantified and expressed as kaempferol equivalents (μg KE/g dw). The total phenolic content was calculated as the sum of all individual compounds quantified as their respective equivalents and expressed as μg/g dw.

UHPLC-mass spectrometry (MS) analysis of polyphenols

UHPLC-MS analysis of the fractions was performed on an Acquity UPLC I-Class system (Waters GmbH, Eschborn, Germany) consisting of a binary pump, an autosampler cooled at 10 °C, a column oven set at 40 °C, and a DAD scanning from 190 to 800 nm. An Acquity HSS-T3 RP18 column (150 × 2.1 mm; 1.8 μm particle size) combined with a precolumn (Acquity UPLC HSS T3 VanGuard, 100 Å, 2.1 × 5 mm, 1.8 μm), both from Waters GmbH (Eschborn, Germany), was used for separation. The UHPLC was coupled to an LTQ-XL ion trap mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific Inc., Darmstadt, Germany) equipped with an electrospray interface (ESI) operating in negative ion mode for polyphenols (MS2, 35% normalized collision energy; MS3, 45% normalized collision energy). The capillary was set at 350 °C with a voltage of −21 V for ESI-. The source voltage was maintained at 4 kV at a current of 100 μA. The tube lens was adjusted to −60 V. For identification, mass spectra were recorded in the range of m/z 50 to 900. The injection volume was 5 μL. To identify the unknown components of the samples, Thermo Xcalibur™ Qual Brwoser version 2.2 (Thermo Scientific Inc., Darmstadt, Germany) was used.

Identification of polyphenols

Phenolic compounds were identified according to the UV/Vis spectrum (positions of absorption maxima λmax) and mass spectrum and compared with literature data (Farag et al., 2021; Kapravelou et al., 2020; Silva et al., 2014; Tang et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2020). For comparison of mass spectrometric data, the Human Metabolome Database (https://hmdb.ca/) and MassBank of North America (MoNA) (https://mona.fiehnlab.ucdavis.edu) were used (last accessed: February 27, 2026).

Calculation of the contribution rate

The contribution rate was calculated according to Luo et al. (2016). In this approach, the content of the target components is related to their respective weight distributions. The contribution rate was calculated using the following equation:(1)CR%=CAA×WA(CAA×WA+CAB×WB)with CAA= concentration of component A in fraction A [μg/g dw], WA= proportion of the respective fraction A [%], CAB= concentration of component A in fraction B [μg/g dw], WB= proportion of the respective fraction B [%].

Statistics

All results are shown as means of three replicates and the data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analysis and Principal Component Analysis (PCA) of the results were performed using XLSTAT software (version 2022.3.1, Addinsoft Technologies, Paris, France). For pairwise comparisons, an ANOVA with Bonferroni post-hoc test (selected significance level p ≤ 0.05) was used.

Mung bean seeds were germinated under controlled conditions. The progression of sprouting was visually monitored, and morphological changes in the dehulled sprouts are illustrated in Fig. 1. Following soaking, water absorption initiated the rupture of the outer seed coat, thereby facilitating the hull removal. Peak 1 (m/z 329) was tentatively identified as phenylacetic acid-O-hexoside, due to comparable fragmentation pattern observed in the MS database MoNA. The fragment ions at m/z 209 and 239 indicate the cleavage of the glycosidic bond in the MS2 spectra (−120 Da and −90 Da), and the release of the free aglycone (m/z 167). Peak 2 was characterized as 2-O-p-coumaroyltartronic acid according to its characteristic absorption maxima for hydroxycinnamic acids at 238 and 316 nm and by matching fragment ions at m/z 177, 147, 119, and 93 with comparable entries in the HMDB database. Peak 3 exhibited a precursor ion of m/z 593 and was annotated as vicenin 2 (apigenin-6,8-di-C-glycoside). Its MS2 spectrum exhibited a characteristic fragmentation of the C-glycosyl moieties with fragment ions at m/z 473 (−120 Da) and 503 (−90 Da), followed by subsequent fragmentation to m/z 311 and 283 in the MS3 spectrum, consistent with reported data (Silva et al., 2014). Peak 4 exhibited a parent ion at m/z 639 and was assigned as 2″-O-(3,4-dimethoxycinnamoyl)-vitexin. In the MS2 spectrum, a dominant fragment ion at m/z 431 was observed, corresponding to a neutral loss of 208 Da. According to reported fragmentation patterns of acylated C-glycosyl flavones, a neutral loss of 208 Da is characteristic of a 3,4-dimethoxycinnamoyl moiety. The absence of a simultaneous loss of acyl and hexose residue indicates that the acyl group is located at the 2″ position (Wang et al., 2019). Subsequent MS3 fragmentation of the ion at m/z 431 yielded fragment ions at m/z 311 and 283, consistent with those reported for vitexin. The compound corresponding to peak 5 was tentatively identified as apigenin-6,8-di-C-(6”-O-p-cumaroyl)-glycoside. The fragmentation pattern of the precursor ion at m/z 739 showed comparable product ions at m/z 473, 503, 311 (MS2) and m/z 282, 283 (MS3) to vicenin 2 (peak 3). The most prominent loss of −266 Da is characteristic for a p-coumaroyl hexose conjugates substituent at the 6″-O position of the C-glucosylated glucose moiety, as evidenced by the simultaneous loss of the acyl group and the hexose residue (Wang et al., 2019). Peak 6 with absorption maxima at 285 and 330 nm appeared at m/z 517. The observed fragment ions in MS2 at m/z 471 (−46 Da) and 449 (−68 Da) correspond to common neutral losses observed for flavonoids (Jiang & Gates, 2024). A further loss of −162 and −18 Da in MS3 suggested a O-glycoside moiety and a loss of H2O; supporting the tentative identification as a flavonoid derivative (Farag et al., 2021). Peak 7, detected only in the hull and whole mung bean samples (Fig. S1, S3), showed a parent ion at m/z 449 and was characterized as dihydrokaempferol-C-hexoside. The MS2 spectrum showed dominant cross-ring cleavages of C-glycosyl flavonoids fragment at m/z 329, corresponding to a neutral loss of −120 Da, together with a fragment at m/z 359 (−90 Da). A minor fragment at m/z 287 was also observed, consistent with the formation of the deprotonated aglycone of dihydrokaempferol (Farag et al., 2021). Peaks 8 and 9 were annotated as vitexin and isovitexin, respectively. Based on the parent ions m/z 431 and the characteristic fragment ions m/z 311 and 283 for vitexin, and m/z 413, 341, 311, and 283 for isovitexin, together with agreement with literature data and confirmation by comparison with the vitexin standard (Kapravelou et al., 2020;Tang et al., 2014; Yang et al., 2020). The presence of fragment ions at m/z 413 and 341, which are characteristic of 6-C-glycosylated flavonoids such as isovitexin, were used in this study to differentiate between the isomeric compounds vitexin and isovitexin. (Castro et al., 2025). Peak 10 exhibited a parent ion at m/z 577 corresponding to isovitexin-O-rhamnoside. The loss of −266 Da leading to the base peak at m/z 311 (MS2) is interpreted as a combined cleavage of an O-type deoxyhexose (−146 Da) and the characteristic cross-ring cleavage of the C-type hexose (−120 Da) within the isovitexin core. Since the presence of rhamnose has been reported in mung bean sprouts (Tang et al., 2014; Yang et al., 2020), the deoxyhexose was assigned as rhamnoside. The presence of the fragment ions m/z 341 and 413 (MS2) further supports the structure of isovitexin. Peak 11 (m/z 517) was tentatively identified as malonylated isovitexin. The fragment ion m/z 473 in the MS2 spectra indicates a loss of CO2 (−44 Da), further fragmentation of m/z 473 to m/z 431 and 311 in MS3 represents the loss of the remaining malonyl group (−42 Da) and a C-type glycoside binding (−120 Da), respectively (Piasecka et al., 2015). The fragment ions m/z 311, 413, and 341 and absorption maxima are comparable to isovitexin. Peak 12 showed an absorption maximum at 291 nm, a precursor ion of m/z 355, and generated fragment ions at m/z 161 and m/z 193 (MS2). Based on this data, Peak 12 was tentatively identified as coniferyl ferulate (Kapravelou et al., 2020).

Distribution of the phenolic content in the hull and cotyledon fraction

TPC of the whole mung bean seeds reached 1068.7 μg/g dw, whereas the mung bean hull exhibited the highest TPC concentration, with 10,055.3 μg/g dw. The predominant flavonoids identified in both hull and cotyledon fractions were vitexin and isovitexin. The hull contained 3254.3 μg VE/g dw of vitexin and 6568.3 μg VE/g dw of isovitexin (Table S1). In the whole mung bean seeds, vitexin and isovitexin concentrations were 394.9 μg VE/g dw and 573.1 μg VE/g dw, respectively. Previous research by Yao et al. (2011) reported vitexin concentrations ranging from 120.0 to 3000.0 μg/g dw and isovitexin from 30.0 to 1160.0 μg/g dw across 110 different mung bean seed samples. Dehulling reduced the content of vitexin by approximately 66.1% and of isovitexin by about 70.2%. Luo et al. (2016) similarly confirmed that these flavonoids are predominantly localized in the hull. Further, dihydrokaempferol-C-hexoside was detected in the mung bean hulls (232.7 μg KE/g dw) and in whole seeds (100.7 μg KE/g dw), with its presence in the whole seeds primarily attributed to residual hull material, as this flavonoid was not observed in the cotyledon fraction. This observation underscores the tissue-specific distribution of polyphenols in mung beans and highlights the importance of careful sample preparation when interpreting polyphenol profiles. Overall, these findings reveal a distinct distribution of secondary metabolites across mung bean components and emphasize the relatively low phenolic and flavonoid contents in dehulled, non-germinated seeds. Although the hull represents only 7.4% of the mung bean's weight, it contributed disproportionately to both TPC and the major flavonoids. Fig. 3 illustrates the contribution rates of TPC (Fig. 3B) and of the main flavonoids, vitexin (Fig. 3C) and isovitexin (Fig. 3D), in the mung bean hull and cotyledon fractions. The hull accounted for 71.7% of the TPC, while the cotyledon contributed the remaining 28.3%. This distribution is predominantly attributable to the high concentrations of vitexin (Fig. 3C) and isovitexin (Fig. 3D) in the hull. In the whole bean, cotyledon, and hull fractions, a consistently higher proportion of isovitexin compared to vitexin was observed. These results underscore that dehulling significantly reduces the TPC of mung bean seeds, primarily due to the loss of these two key flavonoids. From a technological perspective, this highlights the challenge of balancing the removal of undesirable seed coat components with the retention of bioactive compounds during the production of protein ingredients.

Effects of germination on the phenolic content in dehulled sprouts

Total phenolic content: The changes in TPC during germination are shown in Fig. 4. During the early stages, TPC remained relatively stable, ranging from 304.3 to 487.8 μg/g dw in the cotyledon and 12 h germinated sample, respectively. Notably, visible sprout growth was not observed during this period (Fig. 1). A marked increase was observed after 24 h of germination, with the highest TPC recorded at 48 h, reaching 1418.7 μg/g dw, which is more than four times higher than in the dehulled cotyledon fraction. These results indicate a progressive increase in TPC during germination, ultimately reaching levels comparable to those of untreated whole mung beans (including the hull). Thus, germination significantly enhanced the TPC in dehulled mung beans, with peak levels observed after 48 h. This increase reflects the cumulative effect of time-dependent phenolic biosynthesis during germination, including both de novo synthesis via the phenylpropanoid pathway and subsequent modification steps such as glycosylation. From a technological perspective, this effect is particularly relevant in the context of short-term germination, as it indicates that even early processing stages can contribute to the recovery of polyphenols in the cotyledon fraction.

Phenolic acids: Phenolic acids serve as precursors in numerous biosynthetic pathways, which are pivotal to the production of polyphenolic compounds (Kumar & Goel, 2019). During germination, their synthesis is initiated by the activation of phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), which catalyzes the conversion of L-phenylalanine to trans-cinnamic acid, marking the start of the phenylpropanoid pathway. Subsequent hydroxylation of trans-cinnamic acid produces various hydroxycinnamic acids, which do not accumulate in free forms in plant tissue but undergo further transformation, for example, into hydroxybenzoic acids. These transformations contribute to the dynamic profile of phenolic acids in germinating seeds (Zagoskina et al., 2023).

Previous studies have reported the presence of ferulic, gallic, and p-coumaric acids in mung beans germinated for 1–7 days (Gan et al., 2016; Kapravelou et al., 2020; Tang et al., 2014). In the present study, 2-O-p-coumaroyltartronic acid was identified as the predominant phenolic acid in germinated mung beans, with the highest concentration being recorded after 48 h (122.7 μg FAE/g). This compound was not detectable in whole mung bean seeds and during early germination stages but began to accumulate after 24 h (20.3 μg FAE/g). This hydroxycinnamic tartronic acid ester had previously been detected in mung beans by Strack et al. (1985), who documented an accumulation of p-coumaroyl-tartronate throughout mung bean germination, particularly in the roots of germinated seeds. Furthermore, phenylacetic acid-O-hexoside emerged after 24 and 48 h of germination, with concentrations ranging from 19.4 to 44.8 μg GAE/g. Phenylacetic acid is a plant auxin with antimicrobial activity, which has previously been found in peas (Cook, 2019). Auxins are crucial phytohormones essential for cell elongation, wound healing, and various metabolic pathways (Ahmed et al., 2020). Coniferyl ferulate is synthesized by esterification of coniferyl alcohol and ferulic acid derived from lignan biosynthesis (Grabber et al., 2008). This compound became detectable after 8 h of germination and accumulated up to 163.8 μg FAE/g dw after 48 h. Lignans are associated with several biological activities, including antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and insecticidal properties, thus contributing to the defense of the mung bean sprout (Wang et al., 2022). The detection of coniferyl ferulate after 8 h of germination suggests that the lignan biosynthetic pathway is actively engaged at an early stage in germination, signaling the plant's preparation for growth and defense.

Flavonoids: Flavonoids are well known plant secondary metabolites with antioxidant properties. Vitexin and isovitexin are among the most abundant flavonoids found in mung bean seeds (Tang et al., 2014; Yang et al., 2020). Vitexin is a C-glycosylated flavone (apigenin-8-C-glucoside), while isovitexin is its 6-C-glycoside isomer. Both compounds have been widely reported for their anti-inflammatory and antioxidative properties (He et al., 2016).

In this study, vitexin and isovitexin were identified as the principal flavonoids within the cotyledon fraction, accounting for 44.0% and 56.0% of the TPC, respectively. Both compounds reached their highest concentrations at 8 h of germination (143.4 and 198.4 μg VE/g dw, respectively) and declined to their lowest levels by 48 h (83.1 and 79.7 μg VE/g dw, respectively). Comparable findings were reported by Tang et al. (2014), who also observed a decline in both compounds in sprouted whole mung beans.

While vitexin and isovitexin levels decreased slightly during germination, the total phenolic content increased significantly (Fig. 4). This observation can be attributed to a shift in flavonoid composition. The accumulation of newly formed apigenin-derived compounds was already evident after 12 h of germination and occurred prior to the decline of vitexin and isovitexin. This suggests that these compounds are not solely formed from the transformation of pre-existing flavones, but also arise from de novo apigenin synthesis, indicating an early activation of the phenylpropanoid pathway.

In the present study, the group of apigenin derivatives included vicenin 2 (peak 3), 2″-O-(3,4-dimethoxycinnamoyl)-vitexin (peak 4), apigenin-6,8-di-C-(6”-O-p-cumaroyl)-glycoside (peak 5), isovitexin-O-rhamnoside (peak 10), and malonylated isovitexin (peak 11). After 24 h, the concentration of these derivatives exceeded the combined levels of vitexin and isovitexin and continued to increase, reaching a maximum of 875.3 μg VE/g dw after 48 h of germination.

Among the detected compounds, isovitexin-O-rhamnoside was the earliest to increase during germination (4 h) and exhibited the most pronounced accumulation, rising approximately sixfold within the first 24 h of germination (Table S1). After 48 h, its concentration reaching 477.1 μg VE/g dw (Table S1). These findings suggest that isovitexin-O-rhamnoside plays a key role in the early metabolic response of the cotyledon, potentially contributing to stress adaptation during seedling development. Furthermore, the biosynthesis of flavonoid O-glycosides is catalyzed by O-glycosyltransferases, but only a limited number of these enzymes are capable of glycosylating flavonoid C-glycosides such as isovitexin (Zhang et al., 2022). The observed accumulation of isovitexin-O-rhamnoside therefore points towards the activity of a yet unidentified O-glycosyltransferase involved in the rhamnosylation of the isovitexin during early germination.

In addition, several new apigenin derivatives, including vicenin 2, 2″-O-(3,4-dimethoxycinnamoyl)-vitexin, and apigenin-6,8-di-C-(6″-O-p-coumaroyl)-glycoside, were detectable after 24 h of germination, indicating a progressive diversification of the flavonoid profile. Their formation suggests the involvement of acyltransferases or additional glycosyltransferases, highlighting that flavonoid metabolism extends beyond simple C-glycoside synthesis.

Vicenin 2 (peak 3) is synthesized by C-glycosyltransferases, which catalyze the formation of C—C bond in C-glycosylated flavonoids. These enzymes act on flavonoid precursors such as 2-hydroxynaringenin rather than on vitexin or isovitexin themselves. Accordingly, the increase in vicenin 2 is likely to reflect de novo biosynthesis rather than the modification of existing compounds (Ito et al., 2017).

Both O-glycosides and C-glycosides represent important classes of plant secondary metabolites that contribute to antioxidant defense, stress tolerance, and the regulation of developmental processes, particularly during early seedling establishment (Galland et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2024). Glycosylation influences flavonoid polarity, stability, and overall physicochemical characteristics. For instance, vitexin-O-glycosides have been reported to exhibit greater aqueous solubility than the corresponding aglycone (Hofer, 2016; Wu et al., 2021).

Overall, the results show that early germination is associated with the transition from vitexin- and isovitexin-dominated profiles towards a broader spectrum of apigenin-derived compounds. After 24 h, the formation of C-glycosides and acetylated flavonoids becomes more pronounced, indicating a stage-dependent modulation of flavonoid metabolism in mung bean cotyledons. This time-resolved shift from a limited number of dominant flavonoids towards a more diverse set of apigenin-derived compounds provides a more differentiated view of flavonoid remodeling during early germination than approaches focusing primarily on total phenolic content or major compounds.

The dehulling process had a marked effect on the phenolic content, leading to a significant reduction in vitexin and isovitexin concentrations in the cotyledon sample. The uniformity of the polyphenolic profile positioned the whole mung bean sample and the dehulled cotyledon fraction in proximity within the central region of the left quadrant. The sprouted mung bean samples from the early stages of germination (4–12 h) cluster closely with the cotyledon and whole mung bean samples, reflecting a high degree of similarity in their polyphenolic composition. The polyphenolic profiles after the first 8 h of germination were mainly defined by the presence of vitexin and isovitexin. However, the concentrations of these compounds were notably lower compared to those observed at later germination stages. After 24 h of germination, changes in the polyphenolic profile emerged. The levels of apigenin derivatives increased, especially of isovitexin-O-rhamnoside and malonylated isovitexin, with significant accumulation observed after 48 h. Additionally, compounds such as 2-O-p-coumaroyltartronic acid, 2″-O-(3,4-dimethoxycinnamoyl)-vitexin, and apigenin-6,8-di-C-(6”-O-p-cumaroyl)-glycoside were exclusively detected in sprouted mung beans above germination periods of 24 h.

The polyphenolic profile of sprouted and dehulled mung beans exhibited both an expansion in diversity and an increase in concentration throughout short-term germination, as reflected in the distribution of samples on the right side of the PCA plot. Germination for 48 h resulted in the accumulation of nine newly synthesized phenolic compounds in dehulled mung bean sprouts. After this short-term germination period, mung bean sprouts were characterized by elevated levels of apigenin derivatives alongside a slight reduction in vitexin and isovitexin concentrations. These results further support the observed stage-dependent modulation of flavonoid composition and highlight the progressive diversification of the polyphenolic profile during early germination in the cotyledon fraction.

Conclusions

This study provides a comprehensive characterization of the polyphenolic profiles of mung bean hulls, cotyledons, and dehulled sprouts, revealing the dynamic changes in flavonoid composition during early germination. Across germination, 12 phenolic compounds were detected, including 11 tentatively identified metabolites, nine of which showed pronounced accumulation, highlighting the temporal specificity of flavonoid biosynthesis. Dehulling of mung beans resulted in a notable loss of flavonoids, particularly vitexin and isovitexin, leading to a marked reduction in total phenolic content. Germination mitigated these losses by promoting the synthesis of glycosylated and acetylated apigenin derivatives and phenolic acids, thereby increasing the overall polyphenolic concentration. After 48 h, a pronounced accumulation of apigenin derivatives was observed alongside a modest decline in vitexin and isovitexin.

The results further demonstrate that the total phenolic content remains largely stable during the first 12 h of germination, a time window that facilitates dehulling for the production of light-colored protein ingredients. At the same time, the phenolic composition already redistributes within this early phase due to the accumulation of isovitexin-O-rhamnoside and malonylated isovitexin, indicating an early, stage-dependent modulation of flavonoid metabolism.

Importantly, this study provides a time-resolved characterization of apigenin-derived flavonoids in the dehulled cotyledon fraction during early germination. In contrast to studies focusing on whole seeds and longer germination periods, the present work highlights distinct temporal patterns of compound formation, including the early accumulation of specific derivatives and the subsequent diversification of the flavonoid profile.

From a technological perspective, the combination of dehulling and short-term germination represents a promising approach to balance the removal of undesirable seed coat components with the retention or regeneration of bioactive compounds in the cotyledon. For applications requiring high polyphenol content, extended germination can at least partially compensate for the losses caused by dehulling.

Future studies should focus on identifying the enzymes responsible for these modifications and elucidating the biological functions of the newly synthesized metabolites. Overall, these findings provide new insights into flavonoid metabolism in mung bean cotyledons during early germination and underline the relevance of controlled germination as a strategy to align technological processing requirements with nutritional quality.
