Peripheral cytokine levels in patients with idiopathic parkinson’s disease undergoing subthalamic nucleus deep brain stimulation
Authors: Ersoy Kocabıcak, Sedat Sen, Hafize Uzun, Sait Ozturk, Mustafa Onur Yildiz, Erdem Tuzun, Onder Taskin, Dursun Aygun, Yasin Temel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a movement disorder caused by dopaminergic neuron degeneration. In addition to L-DOPA and agonists, deep-brain stimulation (DBS) is also used in appropriate patients in the middle and advanced stages. The most common target for DBS is the subthalamic nucleus (STN). STN stimulation results in significant improvement in the clinical manifestations of the disease. In addition to its ameliorating effect on well-known clinical findings, stimulation has secondary effects that are not sufficiently known. This study aims to investigate the effects of STN-DBS on peripheral pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokine levels in patients with idiopathic PD, and to explore whether changes in these biomarkers correlate with clinical improvements following DBS. The study included 32 patients with idiopathic PD who underwent STN-DBS at Neurosurgery Clinics. The mean Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS) motor score improved from 45.71 (preoperative, Med OFF) to 17.90 (postoperative, STIM ON / Med OFF) and to 13.10 (6 months after surgery), corresponding to 60.8% and 71.3% improvements, respectively. LEDD decreased significantly over time (p < 0.001), and BDI scores improved significantly postoperatively. MMSE scores increased progressively at each follow-up. Medical treatment ameliorated the effect on UPDRS score by 49.1% in the preoperative period. However, no statistically significant changes were observed in serum cytokine levels (IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α) across the study period. No correlations were found between cytokine levels and clinical variables. STN-DBS is an effective and safe treatment for improving motor symptoms in patients with idiopathic PD, and it may also contribute to a reduced need for medication and gradual cognitive improvement. However, peripheral cytokine levels (IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, and TNF-α) remained unchanged, suggesting that the clinical benefits of STN-DBS are unlikely to be mediated directly through peripheral immune modulation. Nevertheless, more complex immunological mechanisms cannot be excluded. Further studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up are warranted to clarify these potential effects.

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a neurodegenerative disorder caused by the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta and striatum, affecting approximately 1–2% of individuals over the age of 60. L-DOPA and dopamine agonists have been used for the medical treatment of the disease for many years. However, drug-related complications develop in most patients after 5 years of medical treatment1. Deep brain stimulation (DBS) can be applied in appropriate patients with L-DOPA response2. In 1993, Pollak and colleagues performed subthalamic nucleus-DBS (STN-DBS) for the first time3. Since then, this method has been developed and continues to be used in many patients. STN-DBS has gained an important place in the treatment of PD today thanks to imaging methods, neurophysiologic recording, and software technology4.

The insertional effects of DBS represent a crucial yet underappreciated phenomenon with significant implications for optimizing therapeutic protocols and enhancing patient outcomes5. Chronic subthalamic nucleus stimulation in PD has been shown not to disrupt hypothalamic-pituitary (HP) function or the diurnal rhythm of peripheral cortisol secretion, indicating preserved neuroendocrine regulation despite DBS intervention6. In studies conducted in epileptic rats with status epilepticus, findings to better understand the mechanisms of action of DBS, it was found that DBS applied to the anterior thalamic nuclei may show anti-inflammatory and anti-apoptotic properties7.

Interleukin-beta (IL-1β) is a pro-inflammatory cytokine that promotes fever, acute phase protein synthesis. IL-6 is a proinflammatory cytokine that provides B lymphocyte proliferation-differentiation and immunoglobulin (Ig) production. It is most important in fever and the acute protein phase production. Tumor necrosis factor-alpha and beta (TNF-α and TNF-β) are important mediators of the acute infectious response. It is the most important cytokine in Gram (−) infection8. IL-10 is one of the main anti-inflammatory cytokines as a factor that inhibits cytokine synthesis9.

The role of inflammation and immune response in neurodegenerative diseases, such as PD, has been increasingly recognized. Cytokines are signaling molecules that mediate and regulate immunity, inflammation, and hematopoiesis10,11. Given that STN-DBS is an effective therapeutic intervention for PD, understanding how it influences immune responses could provide valuable insights into the broader effects of the treatment on brain function and patient health. Preclinical studies suggest that STN-DBS not only modulates motor circuits but also influences neuroinflammation, astrocyte activity, and peripheral immune responses. These findings support the relevance of exploring systemic immune markers, such as peripheral cytokines, in patients undergoing DBS10–14.

We chose the 1-month and 6-month postoperative time points to capture both the early effects, including micro-lesion and immediate post-surgical changes, and the more stable intermediate-term effects of STN-DBS, providing a comprehensive assessment of clinical and immunological outcomes. There is no study in the literature that was done in such a manner. Thus, our clinicians decided on this timing solely depending on their clinical experience. The study aims to determine whether changes in cytokine levels, particularly pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α) and the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10, are correlated with the clinical outcomes observed after STN-DBS. Additionally, the study aims to contextualize these findings within preclinical and experimental evidence on motor symptoms and levodopa-induced motor complications, exploring whether modulation of the inflammatory pathway through STN-DBS may contribute to symptom management and provide a potential therapeutic mechanism for improving patients’ overall condition.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

This prospective study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Institutional and/or National research Ethics Committee and with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Approval for this study was granted by Ondokuz Mayıs University, Faculty of Medicine Ethics Committee for Clinical Studies (Approval date: 29.04.2016; Number: B.30.ODM.0.20.08/260). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients included in the study.

Data collection

Patients with idiopathic PD who were eligible for STN-DBS and who met the inclusion criteria were consecutively enrolled in the study over a defined period between January 2016 and December 2018 at the Neuromodulation Unit of Ondokuz Mayıs University Faculty of Medicine and the Department of Brain and Neurosurgery at Firat University Faculty of Medicine. Thirty-six patients were included in the study. Specifically, four patients were excluded from the study due to hemolysis observed in their blood samples, which rendered the cytokine measurements unreliable. A total of 32 patients completed the study. The medications used by patients for PD were evaluated using the total L-DOPA equivalent daily dose (LEDD). Neuropsychological tests such as the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) and the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) were routinely evaluated before and after surgery. MMSE was selected for its practicality, wide use, and suitability for serial assessment.

Inclusion criteria

I) Age > 18 years old; ii) Patients with idiopathic PD according to the MDS-PD diagnostic criteria; iii) Implementation of STN-DBS therapy; iv) MMSE test score > 26; v) DBS treatment for more than three months; vi) Agreeing to shut down the deep brain stimulator.

Exclusion criteria

I) A formal diagnosis of dementia; ii) Refusal to accept DBS device deactivation or to provide informed consent; iii) Presence of persistent severe psychiatric disorders; Iv) Severe cognitive impairment determined through clinical evaluation, neuropsychological assessment, and imaging findings (e.g., diffuse atrophy or extensive ischemic lesions). Patients with mild to moderate cognitive deficits were eligible, reflecting real-world DBS practice; v) Presence of systemic diseases contraindicating surgery; vi) Hemolytic, lipemic, or icteric serum samples, which could compromise the accuracy and reliability of biochemical analyses.

Patients with severe cognitive impairment were excluded based on clinical evaluation, neuropsychological assessment, and imaging findings (e.g., diffuse atrophy or extensive ischemic changes). Patients with mild to moderate cognitive deficits were eligible for inclusion, consistent with real-world clinical DBS practice.

Diagnosis and discrimination

Ancillary tests are used to exclude other diseases included in the differential diagnosis15. The MDS-PD criteria are used to diagnose PD. An expert neurologist diagnosed each patient as having PD or non-PD, regardless of the International Parkinson and Movement Disorder Society criteria (gold standard, clinical diagnosis). Then, a second neurologist evaluated the presence/absence of each item from the International Parkinson and Movement Disorder Society criteria. According to these criteria, bradykinesia must be present in combination with either resting tremor or rigidity. In addition, supportive criteria strengthen the diagnosis, including a clear and dramatic beneficial response to dopaminergic therapy, the presence of levodopa-induced dyskinesia, rest tremor of a limb, and ancillary evidence such as olfactory loss or cardiac sympathetic denervation when available16.

Clinical evaluation

Age, gender, disease duration, and disease subtype were recorded. Patients were evaluated preoperatively, at 1 month postoperatively, and again at a mean of 5.1 ± 0.6 months after surgery (range: 4–6 months). Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS) part III motor scores, mini-mental test results, Beck depression scores, and medical treatment doses were recorded. Postoperative evaluations were performed under three standardized clinical conditions: (i) Stimulation Off – Medication Off (Stim OFF - Med OFF), where both DBS and antiparkinsonian medications were discontinued; (ii) Stimulation On – Medication Off (Stim ON - Med OFF), where DBS was active but medications were withheld; and (iii) Stimulation On – Medication On (Stim ON - Med ON), where both DBS and medications were active. When evaluating UPDRS III scores, Med OFF and Med ON assessments were performed preoperatively, and three consecutive assessments were conducted postoperatively based on stimulation and medication status (Stim OFF – Med OFF, Stim ON – Med OFF, Stim ON – Med ON). Med OFF examinations were performed at least 12 h after discontinuation of antidopaminergic treatment, and Med ON examinations were performed with 1.5 times the morning L-DOPA dose.

All patients underwent a clinical evaluation in the best ON- ON-medication state by a neurologist expert in movement disorders. Motor impairment and disease stage were assessed by means of MDS-UPDRS parts I–IV. and Hoehn & Yahr staging scale, respectively17,18. Section 1-mentation, behavior, and mood; Section 2-activities of daily living (scored for “on” and “off”); Section 3-motor exam; and Section 4-complications. Each subscale has a 0–4 rating, where 0 = normal, 1 = slight, 2 = mild, 3 = moderate, and 4 = severe.

Collection of samples

Fasting venous blood samples obtained from the participants in an EDTA anticoagulant tube were centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 5 min, and all biochemical parameters were analyzed in serum samples on the same day. Plasma cytokine levels were assessed preoperatively and at 1 month and 6 months after surgery. Plasma samples for IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-10 measurement were stored at − 80 °C until the study.

Measurement of plasma cytokine levels

The plasma IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-10 levels were measured by the immunoassay according to the manufacturer (Human Magnetic Luminex Assay, Human Premixed Multi-Analyte Kit, R&D Systems, Inc., Minneapolis, MN, USA). In this process, a Human magnetic premixed microparticle cocktail of antibodies (Kit Lot Number L141975) was used to magnetically label the samples.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software version 21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) to evaluate the data. Results are given as mean ± standard deviation. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to determine whether the data fit the normal distribution. The statistical significance for dependent groups with normally distributed data was evaluated using the t-test. For comparisons across more than two points (preoperative and postoperative periods), repeated measures ANOVA was used for normally distributed data, and the Friedman test was applied for non-normally distributed data. Where a significant overall effect was observed, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using the Bonferroni correction for ANOVA and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test with Bonferroni adjustment for Friedman test results. For parameters that did not fit the normal distribution, Spearman’s correlation analysis was used to evaluate the direction and strength of the linear relationship between two continuous variables. p < 0.05 has been considered statistically significant.

Discussion

The main responsible factor in the pathogenesis of PD is dopaminergic neuron degeneration. There is widespread neuroinflammation. Increased synthesis of proinflammatory cytokines in PD is an indicator of this19–22. Due to this mechanism, anti-inflammatory therapies have been tried many times for the treatment of PD23. When the effects of bilateral STN-DBS on motor symptoms and neuropsychiatric symptoms in PD were evaluated in our study, it was observed that STN-DBS improved motor symptoms and levodopa-induced complications, as reported in previous studies24. No significant changes were observed in proinflammatory (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) or anti-inflammatory (IL-10) cytokine levels before and after STN-DBS, nor were these levels correlated with clinical outcomes. This may be since peripheral blood cytokine levels have very low titers (3.2 × 103 pg/mL) and are affected by many factors such as sleep, vital signs, fasting, etc. The existence of many factors affecting peripheral blood cytokine levels should not be ignored. Peripheral blood cytokine levels can be affected by multiple factors, including sleep quality, circadian rhythms, fasting status, acute infections, medications, stress, and physiological parameters such as vital signs. In our study, some of these variables could not be fully identified or controlled due to practical limitations, such as the outpatient clinical setting, ethical constraints on invasive monitoring, and the inherent variability of patient behaviors. Additionally, our findings are supported by several preclinical and experimental studies (motor symptoms and levodopa-induced motor complications)7,25–28. The present study provides preliminary data on the peripheral inflammatory response following STN-DBS and highlights the need for long-term investigations to understand its immunological effects during PD.

DBS is an effective treatment method in PD29. It is known that bilateral STN-DBS applied in the early period provides better results on motor complications compared to medical treatment alone30. Patients were receiving either 5 or 12 daily doses of L-DOPA in combination with multiple dopamine agonists preoperatively, with the corresponding LEDD values presented in Table 3. There are many factors that cause this effect. The decrease in the patients’ L-DOPA requirement and the decrease in complications related to medical treatment are among these factors. The decrease in motor fluctuations, the end-of-dose deterioration, and the absence of peak-dose dyskinesias are other factors. The patients in our study had a mean disease duration exceeding 10 years and were receiving high-dose LEDD therapy, indicating their suitability for STN-DBS. When the patient group included in our study is evaluated, while medical treatment in the pre-surgical period provides a 49% improvement on UPDRS III scores, this rate increases to 64% with stimulation alone (Stim ON-Med OFF) in the post-surgical period and to 75% with the addition of medical treatment. This observation may be attributed to residual neuroplastic effects induced by STN-DBS, optimized post-operative medication adjustments, and patient selection factors favoring more consistent responders. This benefit of STN-DBS on motor scores in the first year has also been shown in previous studies31. The 22% improvement between preoperative Med OFF and early postoperative Stim OFF/Med OFF scores is attributed to the micro-subthalamotomy effect created by microelectrode recording (MER) trajectories in the postoperative period, as reported in previous studies32.

The MMSE is commonly used to evaluate cognitive status, especially in postoperative contexts33. In the present study, the significant reduction in BDI scores indicates an improvement in depressive symptoms after DBS. When interpreting BDI scores, the potential for exaggeration inherent in self-reported measures should be considered. Similarly, the increase in MMSE scores suggests enhanced cognitive function, likely influenced by both the effects of DBS and secondary factors such as improved mood and motor function. The observed postoperative improvement in MMSE scores may reflect not only the cognitive effects of subthalamic DBS but also confounding factors such as test–retest learning effects, improved motor function enabling better test performance, and overall improvement in mood, sleep quality, or general health status. It is important to interpret this improvement cautiously, considering these possible influences. MMSE was selected as the cognitive assessment tool in this study due to its wide clinical use, feasibility for repeated assessments, and ability to provide a global overview of cognitive status. Nonetheless, its limitations, such as ceiling effects and limited sensitivity to subtle deficits, are acknowledged, and future studies would benefit from incorporating more comprehensive neuropsychological evaluations. In addition to its effects on PD, studies in rat models have shown that STN-DBS inhibits serotonin neurons in the midbrain dorsal and median raphe nuclei5,34. Another preclinical study found that STN-DBS reduced serotonin levels in the medial prefrontal cortex35. These data were found to be associated with mood changes after DBS36.

In addition to these and similar factors, when the anatomical connections of the subthalamic nucleus with the hypothalamic pituitary axis are taken into consideration, possible effects on these systems are also possible. Steinhardt et al.37 investigated the effects of STN-DBS on sleep–wake regulation and circadian rhythms in patients with PD. The study demonstrated that STN-DBS can influence sleep architecture and circadian rhythmicity, suggesting potential benefits beyond motor symptom control. A potential effect on corticosteroid synthesis may be one of the main reasons for this situation in rats37,38. In addition to the modulation applied to the medial thalamus region, similar effects may also occur in STN-DBS, and peripheral corticosteroid levels may be affected6. In our study, we aimed to explore the potential effects of STN-DBS on the hypothalamic pituitary axis indirectly, by assessing changes in peripheral pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokine levels, although corticosteroid levels were not directly measured. We did not find any difference in cytokine levels before and after surgery in patients who underwent STN-DBS. We also did not find any relationship between anti-inflammatory cytokine levels and their clinical reflections. There may be several reasons for this situation. Even if the HPA and corticosteroid synthesis are affected, it may not affect peripheral blood cytokine levels. Amorim et al.7 investigated the anti-inflammatory and anti-apoptotic effects of DBS when administered during status epilepticus (SE). In their study, they showed that DBS has anti-inflammatory and anti-apoptotic effects when given to animals undergoing SE. However, they could not conclude a direct relationship between the reduced excitotoxicity, anti-inflammatory, and anti-apoptotic effects of DBS. Similarly, they could not explain why DBS only affected certain inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6, but not TNFα). Our results differ from the findings reported by Chang et al.28, who reported a postoperative reduction in TNF-α and other inflammatory markers in the central nervous system (CNS) of PD patients undergoing STN-DBS. In their study, TNF-α levels differed significantly between the three groups (preoperative DBS PD patients, postoperative DBS PD patients, and controls); however, no significant correlations were observed between TNF-α levels and clinical measures such as UPDRS III or Hoehn–Yahr stage in preoperative or matched patients. The results suggest that PD patients exhibit a persistent inflammatory profile, potentially involving activated immune cells in the CNS, and that DBS may exert therapeutic effects by modulating this central inflammatory environment. In contrast, our analysis focused on peripheral blood cytokine levels, which may not fully reflect central immune processes due to the selective permeability of the blood-brain barrier. These differences underscore the importance of evaluating both central and peripheral compartments when investigating the immunomodulatory effects of DBS in PD28. The fact that peripheral blood cytokine levels have very low titers and are affected by many factors, such as sleep, vital signs, hunger, etc., may also have caused this situation. The existence of many factors affecting peripheral blood cytokine levels should not be ignored.

In addition to dopaminergic neurodegeneration and neuroinflammation, recent studies have highlighted the critical role of innate immunity in the pathophysiology of neurodegenerative disorders, including PD. Chronic activation of microglia, the brain’s resident immune cells, leads to sustained production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and oxidative stress, creating a self-perpetuating cycle of neuronal damage38,39. This suggests that PD is not solely a neurodegenerative disease but also a disorder with significant immune system involvement. Moreover, the orexin system—traditionally associated with sleep-wake regulation and arousal—has been increasingly recognized for its role in neurodegeneration. Dysregulation of orexin signaling may contribute to several PD-related non-motor symptoms, such as sleep disturbances, mood changes, and autonomic dysfunction. Recent evidence indicates that orexinergic pathways may interact with neuroinflammatory and neuroendocrine mechanisms, thereby influencing disease progression39,40. Including these perspectives helps to position DBS, particularly STN-DBS, within a broader neurobiological context, where its therapeutic effects may involve modulation not only of motor circuits but also of immune and neuroendocrine pathways.

The exact mechanisms through which DBS reduces inflammation and thus exerts a neuroprotective effect are not yet fully understood. However, the existing literature indicates that there appears to be a shared foundational effect, which involves the neuroprotective and potentially anti-inflammatory benefits of DBS in rats7,40,41. It should be considered that some of the findings are based on animal models and may not be directly generalizable to humans. Therefore, additional clinical research is required to better understand the mechanisms behind DBS that contribute to these neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory outcomes.

Limitations of the study

This study is subject to several limitations. One limitation of the study is its relatively small sample size and the absence of longitudinal measurements. Peripheral blood cytokine levels are known to fluctuate in PD22,42, and the selective permeability of the blood-brain barrier may contribute to differences between central and peripheral cytokine levels. Examining cytokine values in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) could clarify this discrepancy; however, repeated CSF sampling was not performed due to ethical concerns and the practical challenges associated with serial lumbar punctures in PD patients. Additionally, although experimental studies in rats12 have shown that STN-DBS can modulate peripheral immune responses, our findings in human patients did not reveal robust changes in peripheral cytokine levels, likely reflecting species differences and the high variability of these markers in clinical settings.

Conclusion

PD exhibits marked interindividual variability, influenced by factors such as disease progression, which can affect inflammatory markers. STN-DBS is an effective and safe treatment method for advanced PD, particularly in patients with motor complications unresponsive to medical therapy. Although experimental studies (in rodents) suggest neuroprotective and anti-apoptotic effects of DBS, its exact mechanisms remain unclear. STN-DBS did not significantly alter peripheral cytokine levels at 1 and 6 months postoperatively. Given the annual increase in UPDRS motor sub-scores and the long-term use of DBS, longer follow-up is needed to assess delayed immunological effects. These findings suggest that the benefits of STN-DBS are unlikely to be mediated by peripheral immune mechanisms and may instead involve central pathways not detectable in peripheral blood. We recommend that future studies include longer-term follow-up, ideally extending beyond 12 months, with larger sample sizes and serial cerebrospinal fluid analysis, to fully assess delayed immunological effects and clarify whether STN-DBS modulates central or peripheral neuroimmune pathways in PD. An increase in the UPDRS motor sub-score per year by a certain number of points and the long-term use of DBS, longer follow-up is needed to assess delayed immunological effects.
