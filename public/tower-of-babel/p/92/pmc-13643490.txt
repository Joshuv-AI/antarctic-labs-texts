Development and validation of an electronic health record-based frailty index in the UK Biobank
Authors: Grace I Bowman, Nigel Seymour, Chandra Reynolds, Luke M Evans
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFrailty, an age-related loss of the ability to withstand stressors, is commonly measured using health deficit indices, often using survey or questionnaire data. We aimed to develop an electronic frailty index (eFI) using electronic health record (EHR) data linkages in the UK Biobank and assess its association with mortality.MethodsWe calculated an eFI using 43 deficits, each corresponding to phecodes mapped to the United Kingdom (UK) and international classification coding systems. We compared this eFI to a validated 49-item survey-based FI for the UK Biobank (UKB) and assessed associations of the eFI with risk of all-cause mortality (follow-up ≤ 10.2 years) and mortality following a stressor (heart attack or stroke) using Cox proportional hazard models.ResultsMean eFI in this cohort (N = 208,982) was 0.058 (SD = 0.06) and was higher in females than males. A 10% higher baseline frailty was associated with higher risk of all-cause mortality (HR (95% CI)=2.00 (1.93-2.07)), although the magnitude of this association decreased when adjusting for socioeconomic-related covariates (HR (95% CI)=1.44 (1.38-1.51)). Associations were stronger in men than women. Electronic frailty index predicted mortality following both heart attack and stroke (HR (95% CI) = 1.59 (1.25-2.04) and HR = 1.33 (1.13-1.57), respectively).ConclusionsThis EHR-based eFI has robust associations with mortality, suggesting that it can be used as a valid measure of frailty in the UKB and can potentially be applied to other datasets with EHR data.

Introduction

Frailty, an age-related state of reduced physiological resilience and increased vulnerability to adverse events, captures physical and cognitive dimensions of aging.1 Severe frailty is observed in up to 24% of older adults.2 As populations age, the number of severely frail individuals will rise,3 increasing the toll on families, society, and health care systems.4,5

There are multiple approaches for measuring frailty, each with their own benefits and drawbacks. Two primary operationalizations including the frailty phenotype6 and the cumulative deficit model.7 The frailty phenotype assesses frailty based on five criteria: reduced grip strength, slow gait, self-reported fatigue, reduced physical activity, and unintentional weight loss.6 While the frailty phenotype provides a useful classification criterion, many large datasets, such as electronic health records, do not contain all or some of the items. Alternatively, the frailty index (FI) is based on a deficit accumulation model, where frailty is conceptualized as the aggregate of health deficits across many domains.1 FIs are validated instruments quantifying health differences among individuals reflecting the accumulation of deficits across multiple domains that predict future morbidity and mortality.1,7 While not directly classifying individuals as frail or not frail, higher FI scores are associated with risk of other age-related diseases and outcomes, such as falls and incident dementia.1

Frailty indices have largely been measured using survey data and/or in-person assessment, limiting their incorporation into clinical practice and implementation in large-scale biobanks. Electronic health records (EHRs) have been recently used for the development of electronic frailty indices (eFIs), with different implementations incorporating diagnosis codes,8,9 claims,10,11 and laboratory measures.9 Electronic health records are becoming increasingly available to researchers using biobanks through data linkages, and are often present even when the availability of survey data is limited, however, different diagnostic codes used in different health systems and countries can require re-development. Furthermore, the comparability of eFIs to survey-based methods has received limited attention.

Here, we addressed these two challenges directly by designing a phecode-based eFI implementation and comparing the eFI to a survey-based FI in the same dataset. First, we developed this eFI using standardized EHR-based phecodes12,13—manually curated groups of codes that reflect disease categories in realistic ways—in the UK Biobank (UKB). By using phecodes rather than relying on CTV3 or ICD10 codes, we aim to produce a measure that enables straightforward, harmonized analyses across samples and data sources. Second, we applied this eFI to the UKB, for which an survey-based frailty index (sFI) has already been developed and validated—making the UKB, which contains both survey data and EHR linkages for over 200,000 individuals, an ideal dataset for the development and validation of an eFI—allowing for a direct comparison of both overall EHR- and survey-based FI as well as the individual items that compose them in the same biobank-scale dataset.

Here, we present this phecode-based implementation of eFI in the UKB, assess the phenotypic correlations between sFI and eFI for UKB participants, and validate the eFI through association with mortality—both all-cause mortality and mortality following a stressor (heart attack or stroke).

Methods

Study population

The UKB14 is a long-term cohort study with over 500,000 participants aged 40-69 recruited between 2006 and 2010. Participants were given an initial assessment, which occurred at 22 assessment sites in England, Scotland, and Wales and collected questionnaire and interview data at baseline covering demographics, lifestyle, perceived health and wellness, environmental exposures, as well as personal and family medical history. Additionally, UKB participants can opt into data linkages, including electronic medical records and death registries. As of June 2025, nearly 230,000 individuals have linked primary care data, containing codes covering diagnoses, prescriptions, and referrals.

FI construction

Survey-based frailty index

For each individual, we constructed a sFI using a validated 49-variable deficit model,16 using data from the first survey wave. More information on the sFI variable construction can be found in Table S1 and Supplementary Material, SOM. Our construction of sFI largely follows Williams et al.’s16 work, although we did not impute missing data, while Williams et al.’s imputed up to nine items for 19.6% (N = 96,197) of the cohort. Instead, we calculate sFI based on the number of items present (the number of endorsed items divided by the number of non-missing items). To ensure temporal alignment between the sFI and eFI (see next), only the baseline assessment was used to generate participants’ sFIs.

Electronic health record-based frailty index

We constructed an eFI using a 43-item deficit model (Table S2), including items conceptually overlapping with deficits present in Williams et al.’s sFI when they were available; 39 of the items conceptually overlapped. This overlap was purposeful, to allow for as many direct comparisons between items as possible. However, in the eFI, these items were based on diagnostic codes (eg, whether an individual had one of the EHR codes associated with cancer) and in the sFI they were based on self-reported health history (eg, whether an individual reported a history of cancer). The eFI was developed using phecode definitions13,15 (Phecode Map 1.2) using the primary care data available through data linkage, which is coded using Read2 and CTV3 codes. We provide a lookup (Table S3) that maps the 43 deficits, corresponding to phecodes (v1.2), to ICD9, ICD10, Read 2, and CTV3 codes. Our implementation measured eFI at the available record closest to the date of an individual’s baseline assessment (Data Field 53). Individuals who did not have an EHR record within 1 year before or after their UKB assessment were removed from the analysis to ensure temporal alignment between the eFI and sFI.

Given the longitudinal nature of EHR data, and the importance of capturing age-related deficits through the eFI, for some items, individuals are required to be at or above age 50 at the time of diagnosis for that deficit to be endorsed. Our eFI is cumulative, such that if an individual had endorsed a deficit at any point in the record, that deficit was considered endorsed regardless of time that elapsed between the endorsement and the last available record (ie, a deficit could not be “un-endorsed”). All individuals’ eFIs were calculated as the number of deficits endorsed divided by the total number of deficits that could be recorded in the EHR data.

Power analyses

We estimated the power to detect associations16 using the empirical variance of eFI and sFI, and the observed number of deaths.

Validation of eFI and comparisons with sFI

All analyses were performed in R (version 4.5.1) using the survival (version 3.8.3), psych (version 2.5.3), and data.table (version 1.17.8) packages (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). We plotted FI distributions and sFI-eFI associations on the FI scale of 0-1. We assessed item-level correlations between matched sFI and eFI deficits using Pearson’s r and quantified agreement between measures using Cohen’s kappa.

We first assessed the association of FI with mortality risk, for both sFI and eFI. We hypothesized, based on prior literature1,8–11,15 that higher sFI or eFI would be associated with higher risk of mortality. We used Cox proportional hazard models.

Attained age was used to account for staggered baseline assessments and variable age at assessment for sFI and varying lengths of follow-up time, as in Williams et al.15 Attained age was calculated using month and year of birth (UKB data fields 52 and 34, respectively [all participants were assigned the 1st day of the month as their day of birth, as date of birth data is considered sensitive and not released by the UKB; age was only given as month and year]), date of baseline attendance (data field 53) or date of last available EHR record (for sFI and eFI, respectively), and exit at date of death (data field 40,000) or censorship May 31, 2016 (the last date at which GP data was available for all participants as of November 2025 [UKB data linkage censoring dates]). The dataset was restricted to individuals with an EHR entry 1 year before or after their date of baseline attendance to avoid temporal discrepancies between age at eFI and sFI. On average, the difference between the date of baseline attendance and EHR entry was 50 days.

FI, on a scale of 0-1, was first multiplied by 10, as in Williams et al. so that the resultant hazard ratios can be interpreted such that 0.1 higher FI corresponds to a 10% increase in frailty. We used Kaplan-Meier survival curves—unadjusted for covariates—for eFI and sFI to illustrate estimated survival probability for individuals throughout the follow-up period. We plotted curves at 0.1 increments across the FI distribution (<0.1, 0.1 to <0.2, 0.2 to <0.3, 0.3 to <0.4, and ≥0.4), with five groups for sFI—as in Williams et al.—and four groups for eFI, collapsing the highest two groups.

To test the robustness of the estimated hazard ratios to additional factors, such as socioeconomic status (SES) variables, we estimated effects of FI using five different Cox hazard models for association with all-cause mortality, varying in the covariates used. We tested: (1) FI as a single independent variable, without covariate adjustment, (2) adjusting for sex (data field 31), (3) adjusting for sex and self-reported ethnicity (data field 21,000), (4) adjusting for sex, self-reported ethnicity, age, and age2, and (5) with sex, self-reported ethnicity, age, age2, Townsend Deprivation Index (TDI; data field 22,189), and assessment center (data field 54) included as covariates. Sex, self-reported ethnicity, and assessment center were treated as categorical variables. Age was centered at 50 years, and age2 was calculated from the centered age variable. Townsend Deprivation Index was mean-centered. We estimated these models using the whole sample and stratified by sex (in which case sex was excluded as a covariate).

As more individuals had UKB assessment data than linked EHR records, we also ran the sFI hazard models on the whole UKB sample and subsets of individuals that did and did not opt in to the data linkage, to test whether there is a difference in how sFI predicts mortality between those with and without EHR data. For this supplemental comparison (Figures S1-S3, Table S4), the dataset included all individuals with linked EHR data; given the goal of assessing difference in sFI mortality prediction within the linked and unlinked groups and no need to align eFI with sFI, this analysis did not exclude individuals without an EHR entry within 1 year before or after baseline assessment.

Frailty is conceptualized as the ability to withstand stressors. To test whether our eFI captures this conceptualization, we modeled the association of prior eFI with mortality risk following a stressor–either heart attack or stroke. We subsetted to only include individuals who had experienced the stressor being investigated (either heart attack or stroke [based on phecodes mapped to the UKB EHR data]) and excluded individuals who had additionally experienced the other stressor prior to the stressor of interest. The sample was further restricted to individuals who had experienced a heart attack or stroke between January 1, 2005 and May 31, 2016, additionally requiring that individuals had a preceding EHR entry within 2 years of the stressor, and that they experienced the stressor at or after age 50. This last calculated eFI prior to the heart attack or stroke was the eFI used to predict mortality risk following the stressor. We used the last available record before the heart attack or stroke event to calculate the eFI to avoid risk of reverse causation. Attained age was used to account for variation in the date and age at which the stressor occurred and varying lengths of follow-up time and was calculated using date of birth, date of the stressor, and exit at date of death or censorship May 31, 2016.

All models of association of eFI with mortality risk following stressor used Model E, including all covariates, with Kaplan-Meier survival curves plotted as above (without covariate adjustment).

There were some differences between individuals who opted into the GP data linkage as compared to the full UKB sample, including differences in assessment center characteristics and lower mortality of those with GP data linkages compared to those without (Figures S1-S3, Table S3). Distributions of eFI and sFI were both right-skewed and followed the same general shape, but with the eFI containing a much higher proportion of individuals at low frailty scores than the sFI (Figure S4). Mean eFI and sFI were higher for females than for males: 0.059 (0.06) and 0.056 (0.05) for eFI, and 0.148 (0.08) and 0.140 (0.08) for sFI, for females and males, respectively. When comparing these FI constructions in the same individuals, eFI and sFI were moderately positively correlated (Figure 1). Item-level correlations between deficits present in both the eFI and sFI varied considerably across the 39 comparable items, ranging from r = 0.03 to r = 0.94 (Table S5). Similarly, Cohen’s Kappa coefficients ranged from κ = 0.01 to κ = 0.94, reflecting low to almost perfect agreement for conceptually overlapping deficits as captured by eFI and sFI (Figure S5). Diabetes (r = 0.94, κ = 0.94), heart attack (r = 0.81, κ = 0.80), and asthma (r = 0.75, κ = 0.74) had particularly high concordances, while insomnia (r = 0.08, κ = 0.009), fatigue (r = 0.08, κ = 0.02), depression (r = 0.05, κ = 0.007), and dental problems (r = 0.03, κ = 0.008) had particularly low concordances (Table S5, Figure S5).

Power analyses

The large sample led to high power to detect eFI risk on mortality (Figure S6). There was >80% power to detect an eFI HR ≥ 1.05 for all-cause mortality. There was >80% power to detect an eFI HR ≥ 1.23 for mortality following a stroke and >80% power to detect an eFI HR ≥ 1.32 for mortality following a heart attack.

Associations with mortality following a stressor

We additionally investigated individual health outcomes following a stressor—specifically a heart attack or stroke—and whether our eFI (measured prior to the stressor) was predictive of subsequent mortality, as another validation of our eFI. Electronic frailty index predicted mortality following both heart attack and stroke (HR (95% CI)=1.59 (1.25-2.04) for heart attack, N = 2 689; HR = 1.33 (1.13-1.57) for stroke, N = 4,519; Tables S10 and S11). As time following a stressor increases, individuals with higher eFI scores have a lower survival probability (Figure 4, Table S12).

Discussion

We created a measure of frailty using EHR data for >200,000 participants in the UKB cohort, validating it through comparison to the survey-based FI for the UKB (Williams et al.15), association with all-cause mortality, and association with all-cause mortality following a stressor. Our eFI measure displayed expected characteristics when considering observed distribution, sex differences, and association with mortality, illustrating the utility of this phecode-based eFI for future studies of frailty using UKB data, and potential transferability to other datasets containing EHR data through the phecode-based FI map. Further, by directly comparing this eFI to an sFI developed in the same dataset, this work highlights both the convergent validity of an EHR-derived frailty measurement and meaningful differences that arise from different measurement sources. Comparing the prevalences of conceptually related items from the survey and EHR data points to some of these differences—a considerable strength of this study. Variability in item-level agreement is likely reflective of differences between self-reported and clinically diagnosed conditions, with conditions like diabetes and heart attack showing high concordance,17,18 while subjective, symptoms-based conditions such as insomnia and fatigue show low concordance,19,20 perhaps because they have not been discussed with or diagnosed by a primary care physician. Further, the wording of certain sFI questions could also influence this concordance; for example, endorsement of insomnia is based on the question: “Do you have trouble falling asleep at night or do you wake up in the middle of the night?” as compared to endorsement of diabetes: “Has a doctor ever told you that you have any of the following conditions?,” with selections including heart attack, asthma, and diabetes. These differences (Figure S5) could explain the modest individual-level correlation between sFI and eFI (Figure 1).

Despite these differences between EHR and survey-derived FIs, our eFI replicates commonly observed group differences in FI, such as the differences observed between sexes.21 Our analysis of associations of eFI with survival were consistent with estimated hazard ratios for EHR-based FIs in other datasets and with associations observed in the UKB using survey-based FI measurements.8,9,22,23

Given known disparities in health status—including frailty status—among older adults based on sex, race, and socioeconomic status,24,25 we explored hazard models that included adjustment for individual-level socioeconomic status (TDI) and geography (assessment center, likely also corresponding to SES and other variables) alongside ethnicity, sex, and age. When adjusting for TDI and assessment center, the magnitude of the eFI’s association with mortality decreased, suggesting the unadjusted mortality association may be partly accounted for by these socioeconomic and geographic factors, again consistent with prior studies in other datasets.

Our study is not without limitations. Use of an EHR to develop a FI presents unique challenges, especially when considering the potential role of socioeconomic factors. These concerns are even more salient in the case of a largely privatized and fragmented health care system, such as in the United States. Given that our eFI does not robustly adjust for missingness, it may not be transferable to health care systems where EHR data are spread across different systems and more difficult to unify. Furthermore, by not adjusting for missingness, the eFI may have a systematic bias if applied to systems where individuals with fewer financial, medical, or social resources have less access to care and are therefore less likely to get a diagnosis for the same condition as someone with greater capital. Future work should consider methods for handling this missingness, especially if applying this work to large datasets based in the United States with EHR data, such as the All of US Research Program.26 Additionally, given that this FI construction is based on EHR data, its items rely on strictly diagnostic criteria, without any items focusing on perceived health and wellbeing. However, our phecode-based FI map (Table S2) will help enable studies of the transferability of eFI across biobanks, including those with alternative coding systems. Our comparisons between sFI and eFI relied on a 1-year window of time surrounding the initial assessment; while we chose a narrow window to minimize the potential to accumulate deficits, the eFI and sFI measures may reflect changes that occurred in that intervening time.

Ultimately, we designed an EHR-based measure of frailty and validated it through comparison to a survey-based FI in the same dataset, association with all-cause mortality, and association with mortality following a stressor. By generating an FI for EHR data in the UKB that is potentially portable to other datasets by using phecodes, studies of FI can possibly be extended to those without extensive survey data, leverage the longitudinal records embedded in EHR data for exploring the developmental processes of aging, and increase sample sizes for genetic studies of frailty.
