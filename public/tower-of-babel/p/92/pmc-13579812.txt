Bridging the service gap: health facility challenges in HPV vaccine uptake in rural Uganda
Authors: Mbonigaba Rukarama Evarist, Ronald Arineitwe Kibonire
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCervical cancer remains a major cause of morbidity and mortality among women in Uganda. Although the Human Papillomavirus (HPV) vaccine is highly effective in preventing cervical cancer, completion of the recommended two-dose schedule remains low, particularly in rural settings. Rukiga District was selected for this study due to persistently low HPV second-dose (HPV2) completion rates compared with national targets. This study assessed health facility-level barriers influencing HPV vaccine completion among adolescent girls aged 9–14 years in rural Uganda.MethodsA mixed-methods cross-sectional study was conducted between June and September 2022 in selected Health Centre II (HC II), Health Centre III (HC III), and Health Centre IV (HC IV) facilities in Rukiga District. A household survey involving 292 caregivers of eligible adolescent girls was conducted using systematic random sampling. The primary outcome was completion of the two-dose HPV vaccination schedule (HPV2). Quantitative data were analysed using logistic regression to identify factors associated with vaccine completion. In addition, 21 key informant interviews involving 11 healthcare workers and 10 Village Health Team (VHT) members were conducted and analysed thematically to explore contextual barriers affecting HPV vaccine uptake and completion.ResultsThe HPV vaccine completion rate was 23.49%, indicating low coverage. In multivariable analysis, vaccine stock-outs and cold-chain challenges (adjusted odds ratio [AOR] = 1.75, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.04–2.93; p = 0.004) and understaffing of healthcare workers (AOR = 1.97, 95% CI: 1.05–3.68; p = 0.006) were the only statistically significant predictors of HPV vaccine completion. Although limited healthcare worker knowledge (AOR = 0.94, 95% CI: 0.70–1.24) and absence of government programmes targeting out-of-school girls (AOR = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.73–1.29) were not statistically significant in the adjusted model, qualitative findings highlighted them as important contextual barriers. Additional challenges identified included weak outreach systems, transportation constraints, misconceptions about HPV vaccination, and limited community awareness.ConclusionHPV vaccine completion in rural Uganda remains low and is strongly influenced by health system constraints, particularly vaccine supply-chain disruptions and human resource shortages. Strengthening vaccine logistics, improving staffing levels, enhancing healthcare worker capacity, and expanding outreach strategies targeting underserved populations are essential for improving vaccine completion and achieving national immunisation targets.Keywords: Human Papillomavirus vaccination, Cervical cancer, Health system barriers, Rural Uganda, Vaccine completion

Introduction

Cervical cancer is a leading cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality among women globally, with approximately 90% of deaths occurring in low- and middle-income countries [1, 2]. It is the fourth most common cancer among women worldwide and remains the most prevalent gynaecological malignancy in many low-resource settings. Persistent infection with high-risk Human Papillomavirus (HPV) types is the primary cause of cervical cancer, with HPV-16 and HPV-18 accounting for nearly 70% of all cases worldwide [3–6].

In Uganda, cervical cancer remains a major public health challenge and is the leading cause of cancer-related mortality among women. The disease accounts for a substantial proportion of all female cancer diagnoses annually [7, 8]. Recent estimates indicate age-standardised incidence and mortality rates of approximately 56.7 and 40.1 per 100,000 women, respectively, figures that are substantially higher than global averages [9–11]. These statistics highlight the urgent need for effective preventive interventions to reduce the burden of HPV-related diseases.

The World Health Organization (WHO) identifies HPV vaccination as a key strategy for the elimination of cervical cancer as a public health problem. The vaccine provides effective protection against infection with high-risk HPV types and significantly reduces the risk of developing cervical precancerous lesions and cervical cancer [3–6]. In Uganda, HPV vaccination was first introduced through demonstration projects in 2006, which explored both age-based and school-based delivery strategies [12]. Following the success of these pilot programmes, HPV vaccination was integrated into the national routine immunisation schedule in 2015 [13].

Despite the availability of HPV vaccination services, completion of the recommended two-dose vaccination schedule remains a challenge in many low-resource settings. Although uptake of the first dose (HPV1) is generally high, a substantial proportion of eligible girls fail to complete the second dose (HPV2), thereby limiting the effectiveness of vaccination programmes [14, 15]. Several factors have been associated with poor completion rates, including weak follow-up systems, inadequate record management, vaccine stock-outs, insufficient healthcare personnel, misinformation, and limited community engagement [14, 15].

Rukiga District has consistently reported low HPV2 completion rates compared with national targets [16]. Full protection against HPV infection requires completion of the recommended two-dose vaccination schedule among girls aged 9–14 years [15]. Rukiga District was therefore selected for this study because of its documented low HPV2 completion rates, substantial dropout between vaccine doses, and limited outreach coverage.

Health facilities play a central role in the success of immunisation programmes through vaccine storage, transportation, administration, monitoring, and follow-up of eligible beneficiaries. However, systemic challenges such as logistical constraints, inadequate cold-chain infrastructure, operational inefficiencies, shortages of healthcare workers, and weak outreach systems may compromise the effectiveness of vaccine delivery and reduce completion rates [12–16].

Previous studies conducted in Uganda and other sub-Saharan African countries have reported that health system barriers remain among the most important determinants of HPV vaccine uptake and completion [12–16]. However, evidence specifically examining health facility-level barriers to HPV vaccine completion in rural Ugandan settings remains limited. Understanding these barriers is essential for informing interventions aimed at strengthening immunisation services and improving vaccine coverage.

Therefore, this study aimed to assess health facility-level factors influencing completion of the HPV vaccination series among adolescent girls aged 9–14 years in Rukiga District, southwestern Uganda. The findings provide important evidence for policymakers, programme managers, and healthcare providers seeking to strengthen HPV vaccination programmes and reduce the burden of cervical cancer in rural and resource-constrained settings.

Methods

Study design and setting

A mixed-methods cross-sectional study was conducted in Rukiga District, southwestern Uganda, between June and September 2022. The study employed both quantitative and qualitative approaches to provide a comprehensive understanding of health facility-level barriers influencing completion of the Human Papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination series among adolescent girls aged 9–14 years.

The study was conducted in selected public health facilities, including Health Centre II (HC II), Health Centre III (HC III), and Health Centre IV (HC IV) facilities, as well as private health facilities providing HPV vaccination services. These facilities play a central role in the delivery of routine immunisation services, including HPV vaccination, to eligible adolescent girls within the district [1, 3].

The mixed-methods approach was selected to enable triangulation of quantitative and qualitative findings and to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the factors influencing HPV vaccine completion [2, 4].

Study population and sampling

The study population comprised caregivers (parents and guardians) of adolescent girls aged 9–14 years, healthcare workers, and Village Health Team (VHT) members involved in immunisation service delivery.

Quantitative component

The quantitative component included caregivers of adolescent girls aged 9–14 years residing in Rukiga District. A total of 292 caregivers were selected using systematic random sampling from a sampling frame of approximately 1,200 households with eligible adolescent girls. Households were selected proportionately across villages to ensure adequate representation of the study population [10, 15].

The sample size was calculated using the single population proportion formula based on an estimated HPV vaccine uptake of 5%, a 95% confidence level, and a margin of error of 5% [16]. The initial sample size obtained was 385 participants. A finite population correction factor was subsequently applied using the estimated source population of 1,200 households, resulting in a final sample size of approximately 292 caregivers.

Qualitative component

For the qualitative component, 21 key informants comprising 11 healthcare workers and 10 VHT members were purposively selected. Participants were chosen based on their direct involvement in HPV vaccination activities, immunisation programme implementation, and community mobilisation efforts. Their experiences and perspectives were considered essential for understanding contextual barriers affecting HPV vaccine uptake and completion within the district [2, 4].

Outcome variable

The primary outcome variable was completion of the HPV vaccination series (HPV2), defined as receipt of the recommended two doses of the HPV vaccine among adolescent girls aged 9–14 years.

Vaccination status was verified using vaccination cards whenever available. In instances where vaccination cards were unavailable, caregiver reports were used to determine vaccination status. Participants were categorised as either having completed or not completed the HPV vaccination series based on receipt of the second vaccine dose [5].

Data collection instruments and procedures

Quantitative data collection

Quantitative data were collected using a structured interviewer-administered questionnaire. The questionnaire was developed following a review of published literature on HPV vaccine uptake, vaccine hesitancy, and factors associated with HPV vaccination completion in Uganda and other sub-Saharan African countries [6, 7, 17].

The tool was adapted from questionnaires previously used in HPV vaccination studies and modified to suit the local context [7, 18]. Prior to data collection, the questionnaire was reviewed by experts in public health and immunisation to assess content validity, relevance, and clarity.

The questionnaire included sections on socio-demographic characteristics, household characteristics, caregiver knowledge of HPV infection and cervical cancer, awareness of HPV vaccination, attitudes and perceptions towards vaccination, and health system factors influencing vaccine access and completion [6, 7, 18]. Questions included dichotomous (Yes/No), multiple-choice, and Likert-scale response options.

The questionnaire was pretested among caregivers in a neighbouring district with characteristics similar to those of the study area. Feedback obtained during the pretest was used to refine question wording, sequence, and structure to improve comprehension and data quality [19].

Qualitative data collection

Qualitative data were collected using a semi-structured interview guide developed from the study objectives and existing literature on HPV vaccination barriers and facilitators [2, 4, 20].

The interview guide explored participants’ experiences regarding HPV vaccine service delivery, community perceptions and misconceptions about HPV vaccination, barriers affecting vaccine uptake and completion, health system constraints, and recommendations for improving vaccination coverage.

Interviews were conducted in private settings at health facilities or community locations convenient for participants. Each interview lasted approximately 30–60 min and was conducted either in English or the local language according to participant preference. With participants’ consent, interviews were audio-recorded and supplemented with field notes to ensure completeness and accuracy of information. Audio recordings were subsequently transcribed verbatim and translated into English where necessary before analysis [20].

Data analysis

Quantitative data analysis

Quantitative data were entered, cleaned, and analysed using statistical software. Descriptive statistics were generated to summarise participant characteristics and study variables. Frequencies and percentages were used to describe categorical variables.

Bivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to examine associations between health system factors and HPV vaccine completion. Variables with a p-value of less than 0.20 at bivariate analysis were included in the multivariable logistic regression model.

Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent factors associated with HPV vaccine completion while controlling for potential confounding variables. Adjusted Odds Ratios (AORs) with corresponding 95% Confidence Intervals (CIs) were calculated. Statistical significance was considered at a p-value of less than 0.05 [21].

Qualitative data analysis

Qualitative data were analysed using thematic analysis. Interview transcripts were read repeatedly to facilitate familiarisation with the data. Coding was conducted to identify meaningful patterns, concepts, and recurring issues related to HPV vaccine uptake and completion.

Codes with similar meanings were grouped into categories and subsequently organised into broader themes and subthemes. The analysis focused on identifying health facility, individual, and community-level barriers affecting HPV vaccine completion.

Findings from the quantitative and qualitative components were integrated through triangulation to enhance the credibility, validity, and interpretation of the study findings [22].

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Department of community at Kabale University under registration number 2018/MPH/1659/W. Further ethical approval was granted by office of District Health officer Rukiga District after getting final clearance for data collection obtained from the Uganda National Council for Science and Technology (UNCST), reference number MUST-2022-511. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their involvement in the study.

Written informed consent was obtained from all study participants before enrolment into the study. Participants were informed about the objectives of the study, the voluntary nature of participation, their right to withdraw at any stage without consequences, and the measures implemented to ensure confidentiality.

To protect participant privacy, unique identification numbers were used in place of personal identifiers. All study records were stored securely and were accessible only to authorised members of the research team. The study was conducted in accordance with accepted ethical principles governing research involving human participants [1].

Results

HPV vaccine completion

Among the 292 caregivers interviewed, the HPV vaccine completion rate (HPV2) among eligible adolescent girls was 23.49%, indicating low coverage compared with district and national immunisation targets [16]. This finding suggests a substantial dropout between receipt of the first and second doses of the HPV vaccine.

The most frequently reported challenges were lack of staff motivation for outreach activities (73.6%) and untimely transportation of healthcare workers to vaccination sites (73.3%). These findings suggest that several health system challenges may influence HPV vaccine uptake when examined individually.

Table 3 presents adjusted associations between health system factors and HPV vaccine completion. After controlling for confounding factors, only vaccine shortages and cold chain challenges (AOR = 1.75; 95% CI: 1.04–2.93; p = 0.004) and understaffing of healthcare workers (AOR = 1.97; 95% CI: 1.05–3.68; p = 0.006) remained statistically significant predictors of HPV vaccine completion.

Other variables, including limited knowledge among health workers, lack of government programmes targeting out-of-school girls, poor records management, untimely transportation of staff, and low staff motivation, were not statistically significant in the adjusted model. Overall, the results indicate that structural health system constraints, particularly vaccine supply and staffing levels, are the key determinants of HPV vaccine completion in the study area.

Health facility-level barriers identified during interviews included vaccine stock-outs, inadequate cold-chain systems, staff shortages, transportation difficulties, and weak outreach services. Participants reported that these challenges frequently disrupted vaccination activities and reduced opportunities for eligible girls to receive the second vaccine dose.

Individual-level barriers included limited caregiver awareness, fear of injections, school absenteeism, and frequent relocation of adolescents. These factors often contributed to missed vaccination appointments and poor follow-up.

Community-level barriers included cultural and religious beliefs, rumours regarding infertility, and misconceptions that HPV vaccination encourages early sexual activity. Such beliefs contributed to vaccine hesitancy among caregivers and adolescents.

The qualitative findings complement the quantitative results by demonstrating how systemic health facility constraints, particularly vaccine shortages, inadequate cold-chain infrastructure, and shortages of healthcare workers, interact with individual and community-level barriers to influence HPV vaccine completion in rural settings.

Discussion

This study assessed health facility-level barriers influencing completion of the Human Papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination series among adolescent girls aged 9–14 years in Rukiga District, southwestern Uganda. The findings show that HPV vaccine completion remains substantially low at 23.49%, far below national targets. The study further identified vaccine stock-outs, cold-chain challenges, and understaffing of healthcare workers as the key health system barriers significantly associated with HPV vaccine completion. Qualitative findings complemented these results by highlighting additional barriers including inadequate outreach services, limited provider capacity, caregiver misconceptions, and community-level influences.

The HPV vaccine completion rate observed in this study is considerably lower than national expectations and reflects persistent gaps in immunisation programme performance in rural settings. Similar low completion rates have been reported in other Ugandan districts, where dropout between the first and second HPV vaccine doses remains a major challenge [14, 15]. Similar concerns regarding HPV vaccination awareness and factors influencing vaccine acceptance have also been reported in European settings, including Poland, where knowledge and attitudes toward HPV vaccination were found to influence support for vaccination programmes and preventive interventions [23]. This indicates that while initial vaccine access may be achieved, sustaining adherence to the full vaccination schedule remains problematic, potentially limiting the long-term effectiveness of cervical cancer prevention efforts.

In the multivariable analysis, vaccine stock-outs and cold-chain challenges were significantly associated with reduced HPV vaccine completion. These findings are consistent with evidence from Uganda and other low- and middle-income countries, where disruptions in vaccine supply chains negatively affect immunisation coverage [24, 25]. Reliable vaccine availability is essential for ensuring continuity of services, and frequent stock-outs may discourage caregivers from returning for subsequent doses, thereby contributing to dropout between HPV1 and HPV2.

Similarly, understaffing of healthcare workers was a significant predictor of low HPV vaccine completion. Health workers are central to vaccine delivery, mobilisation, follow-up, and record keeping. When staffing levels are inadequate, particularly in rural settings, the capacity to conduct outreach and ensure timely follow-up is reduced. Comparable findings from other African contexts indicate that human resource shortages are strongly associated with reduced immunisation coverage and missed vaccination opportunities [25–27]. Strengthening the health workforce is therefore critical to improving HPV vaccine completion.

Although limited healthcare worker knowledge and the absence of government programmes targeting out-of-school girls were not statistically significant in the multivariable model, they remained important contextual barriers identified in qualitative findings. Evidence suggests that knowledgeable healthcare workers are more likely to provide accurate information, recommend vaccination, and address caregiver concerns effectively, thereby improving uptake [20, 26]. This observation is supported by findings from Poland, where healthcare providers with greater knowledge and awareness of HPV and HPV vaccination demonstrated stronger engagement in vaccine promotion and prevention activities [23]. Inadequate provider knowledge may therefore indirectly contribute to suboptimal vaccine completion, even if not independently significant in the adjusted model.

The absence of structured programmes targeting out-of-school girls also remains an important equity concern. School-based vaccination strategies have been effective in improving HPV vaccine coverage in many settings; however, adolescents outside the school system are often missed. Qualitative findings from this study confirmed that school absenteeism, dropout, and relocation frequently disrupt vaccination schedules, contributing to incomplete vaccination. Similar challenges have been documented in other low-resource settings where out-of-school girls consistently have lower vaccination coverage [19, 28].

Qualitative findings further highlighted the interaction between health system, individual, and community-level barriers. Caregiver awareness, misconceptions, and limited knowledge about HPV vaccination contributed to missed opportunities for completion. Misconceptions regarding infertility and beliefs that vaccination promotes early sexual activity were also commonly reported. Such misinformation has been widely documented in other African and global contexts and continues to undermine vaccine acceptance despite strong evidence of vaccine safety and effectiveness [29, 30]. Similarly [28], reported that patient awareness, perceptions, and attitudes toward HPV-related disease prevention play an important role in the acceptance of HPV preventive interventions.

Overall, the findings demonstrate that HPV vaccine completion is primarily constrained by structural health system weaknesses, particularly vaccine supply chain disruptions and inadequate staffing. These supply-side challenges are further compounded by demand-side factors such as misconceptions and limited awareness, highlighting the need for integrated interventions. Evidence from patient-centred studies further demonstrates that improving public awareness and understanding of HPV-related diseases is essential for strengthening vaccine acceptance and uptake [28].

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this study is the mixed-methods design, which allowed triangulation of quantitative and qualitative findings and provided a comprehensive understanding of HPV vaccine completion barriers. Inclusion of caregivers, healthcare workers, and Village Health Team members enhanced the depth and credibility of findings.

However, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference between identified factors and HPV vaccine completion. Additionally, reliance on caregiver self-report may introduce recall bias. The study was conducted in a single rural district, which may limit generalisability to other settings. Despite these limitations, the findings provide important evidence to guide HPV vaccination programme strengthening in similar rural contexts.

Conclusion

HPV vaccine completion in Rukiga District remains substantially below national targets and is mainly influenced by health system constraints. Vaccine stock-outs, cold-chain challenges, and understaffing of healthcare workers were the only significant predictors of low HPV vaccine completion. These structural barriers are further compounded by limited outreach services, inadequate provider capacity, and community misconceptions. Strengthening vaccine supply chains, improving staffing levels, enhancing healthcare worker capacity, and expanding outreach and community engagement strategies are essential for improving HPV vaccine completion.
