Inhaler use and their carbon footprint in Germany: a 10-year analysis (2013–2022)
Authors: Sarah Blau, Martin Mücke, Michaela Hesse, Julia Sellin, Gabriele Gradl, Martin Schulz, Gülay Ateş
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInhalers are essential for managing asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease; however, their environmental effects vary significantly. Pressurised metered-dose inhalers (pMDIs) contain potent greenhouse gases (GHGs), resulting in a much higher carbon footprint (CF) than non-propellant inhalers (NPIs). Consequently, reducing the use of pMDIs is seen as an important contribution to reduce the healthcare sector’s effect on climate change. This study analyses inhaler dispensing trends in Germany, estimates their resulting CF and quantifies the potential GHG savings from increased NPI use.MethodsDispensing data at the expense of statutory health insurances, covering nearly 90% of the German population, were analysed from 2013 to 2022 across three age groups. Annual dispensing shares and CF estimates based on life cycle assessment-derived CF values were calculated for four inhaler types: pMDIs with hydrofluorocarbon (HFC)-134a, pMDIs with HFC-227ea, dry powder inhalers (DPIs), and soft mist inhalers (SMIs). Two scenario calculations estimated the potential GHG savings.ResultsBetween 2013 and 2022, the total number of dispensed defined daily doses of inhalers increased by 14%, with no significant shift towards lower-emission inhalers (2013, 55% NPIs; 2022, 52% NPIs). Consequently, the total CF increased from 459 kilotonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent (kt CO2eq) in 2013 to 525 kt CO2eq in 2022 (+14%). More than 95% of the inhaler-related CF was attributable to pMDIs. A GHG-saving scenario assuming 85% NPI use among patients aged 10–79 years projected an annual CF reduction of 55% (288 kt CO2eq).ConclusionDespite climate neutrality goals, inhaler-related CF has continued to rise because of stable pMDI usage rates. The substantial potential for GHG reduction highlights the necessity and feasibility of a sustainable change in clinical prescription practice. Our insights could support the promotion of climate-friendly inhalers across other European countries with similar prescription patterns.

Introduction

The climate crisis is seen as the biggest global health threat of the 21st century, placing a crucial responsibility on healthcare professionals to respond to this challenge.1 The German healthcare sector is a major contributor to climate change, accounting for 6% of the national greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions.2 3 Within primary care, 60% of the emissions originate in the supply chain of pharmaceuticals.4

Pulmonary diseases, such as asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), which are both caused and exacerbated by environmental and climate-related factors, are highly prevalent in Germany.5 6 In treating these conditions, inhalers are the most commonly used pharmaceuticals, primarily prescribed by general practitioners.7 8 However, frequently prescribed pressurised metered-dose inhalers (pMDIs) disproportionately contribute to climate change because of their use of hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs) as propellants.9 Previously, chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) were used; however, these were phased out under the Montreal Protocol in 1987 owing to their harmful effects on the ozone layer.10 The HFC propellants currently used in pMDIs, HFC-134a (global warming potential (GWP) = 1430) and HFC-227ea (GWP=3220), still result in a substantial carbon footprint (CF) caused by pMDIs.11 The relative effects of HFCs on global warming are quantified using GWP, a standardised measure that compares the warming effect of GHGs to carbon dioxide (CO₂) over a 100-year period.

Based on study outcomes and the availability of alternatives, there are ongoing global efforts aimed at significantly reducing HFC-containing pMDIs. In particular, pMDIs with HFC-227ea have a very high GWP and can be easily replaced by non-propellant inhalers (NPIs) or if necessary, by pMDIs with HFC-134a.12

Moreover, numerous studies have confirmed that dry powder inhalers (DPIs) and soft mist inhalers (SMIs), summarised as NPIs, have no clinical disadvantages and may even contribute to improved control of asthma and COPD in most patients.13–20 However, pMDIs may be more suitable for patients with severely restricted inspiratory flow, such as very young and very old patients and patients with an exacerbation, as they can be used with spacers.12 18 21 Switching to inhalers with a lower CF can reduce inhaler-related GHG emissions by >85% and is a low-threshold change, particularly since studies have shown that patients often prioritise climate-friendly alternatives if they are clinically feasible.18 20 22 Moreover, switching to NPIs is considered cost neutral or potentially more cost-effective.17 23 Additional strategies to reduce GHG emissions include promoting inhaler recycling, reducing unused doses, replacing current pMDIs with low-GWP-propellant pMDIs and optimising clinical therapy.24 25 Contrary to approaches taken by the UK and Australia, Germany has not yet implemented nationwide return and recycling programmes for inhalers.12 26–28

Acknowledging the responsibility of healthcare professionals in mitigating climate change, the German Society of General Practice and Family Medicine introduced a guideline in 2021 that was updated in 2024, promoting climate-friendly inhaler prescription.12

Notably, the market shares of pMDIs and DPIs vary significantly between countries. In Sweden, pMDIs account for only 13% of inhalers, whereas in England, pMDIs make up 70%, contributing to 3.5% of the National Health Service’s CF.29 30

A robust data-based analysis of current and past de facto usage and potential for improvements in Germany is lacking. This study aimed to quantify the contribution of inhalers to Germany’s healthcare emissions. Therefore, this study analysed dispensing numbers as an estimate for pMDI and NPI usage and calculated the resulting CF between 2013 and 2022 in Germany based on life cycle assessment-derived CF values. Additionally, this study analysed three potential GHG reduction scenarios, focusing on groups aged 10–79 years, in which NPIs can be used most widely.12 18 21

Methods

Study design, population, and data collection

In this retrospective secondary data analysis, prescription trends of inhalers were assessed. The database of the German Institute for Drug Use Evaluation containing anonymised dispensing data at the expense of the statutory health insurance funds from 2013 to 2022 was used for this almost nationwide large-scale real-life data study. Data were obtained from a representative sample covering >80% of German community pharmacies up to June 2019 and >95% from July 2019 onward. Data were extrapolated using regional factors to 100% of the population with statutory health insurance.31 In Germany, nearly 90% of the population is insured by the statutory health insurance (online supplemental table 1).32 33 Data from private health insurances or from inhalers dispensed in hospitals were not included. This study considered aggregated dispensing data of inhalative drugs for obstructive airway diseases, classified under ATC-Code R03A (adrenergics and inhalants) and R03B (other drugs for obstructive airway diseases and inhalants).

The data included information on the following age categories: 0–9, 10–79 and ≥80 years.

Inhaler use was measured as defined daily doses (DDDs) and packs dispensed. Based on dosage form, inhalers were further classified into nebulisers, DPIs, SMIs, pMDIs with HFC-134a and pMDIs with HFC-227ea. For a comprehensive insight, inhaler types were also grouped as NPIs, comprising DPIs and SMIs, and pMDIs, including pMDIs with HFC-134a and pMDIs with HFC-227ea. Nebulisers were excluded from the analysis, as they are not a feasible alternative to pMDIs.

Ethics statement

The Ethics Committee of the Medical Faculty of Rheinisch-Westfälische Technische Hochschule Aachen has reviewed and approved this study (EK 23 304).

Outcomes and statistical analysis

Quantification of outcomes

The outcome measures included the number of dispensed DDDs, share of dispensed DDDs, CF and relative contribution to CF per inhaler type, which were analysed over a 10-year period. The number and share of dispensed DDDs were calculated using sum indices of DDDs. The CF was estimated using sum indices of dispensed packs considering pack size (see formula for CF calculation).

Carbon footprint estimation model

A comprehensive literature review was conducted to extract CF values by inhaler type. Only CF values that were derived from the life cycle assessment using internationally accepted standards and were published as primary data were considered. These values had to be published until February 2024 and detailed CF values per actuation were required (online supplemental table 2).

A literature review was also performed to summarise the methodologies used in previous studies for calculating the CF (detailed findings in online supplemental table 2).

For the most accurate CF estimate, we used an adapted calculation model based on the study by Nagasaki et al, which also considered multi-inhaler packs.35 Therefore, the methodology was adjusted by multiplying the CF per actuation by the number of actuations per pack and the quantity of packs dispensed for each inhaler type in the database:

CF=CF per actuation×actuations per pack×packs dispensed

This approach based on life cycle assessment-derived CF values differs from other studies that calculated the CF by multiplying the mean CF per inhaler by either the number of inhalers dispensed, or the total number of packs sold.

Scenario analyses of potential annual greenhouse gas savings

The annual total CF and saving scenarios were calculated using minimum, mean and maximum CF values per actuation according to table 1.

Annual saving scenarios analysing changes in prescription behaviour were projected based on the same quantity of dispensed DDDs as in 2022 for the group aged 10–79 years. In the first scenario, the CF was estimated under the assumption that all pMDIs with HFC-227ea were replaced by NPIs, as pMDIs with HFC-227ea are considered obsolete owing to their high GWP.12 The second scenario assumes an NPI/pMDI ratio similar to Sweden (85% NPIs and 15% pMDIs with HFC-134a), with no further use of pMDIs with HFC-227ea (online supplemental figure 1).29

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Microsoft 365 Excel (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA) and IBM SPSS Statistics version 29 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). First, a nationwide calculation of shares and estimated CFs per year was conducted, followed by a subgroup analysis based on age groups. Finally, the annual saving potential was estimated as explained.

By contrast, in 2013, approximately 10 million packs of pMDIs with HFC-134a were dispensed, equivalent to 475 million DDDs (pMDIs with HFC-227ea, 507 thousand packs; DDDs, 24 million). This number rose by 109 million to 584 million DDDs of pMDIs with HFC-134a in 2022 (pMDIs with HFC-227ea, 407 thousand packs; DDDs, 30 million).

The total number of inhaler dispensing in DDDs has steadily increased by 14% over the past decade (figure 1a, table 2, online supplemental table 6). This rise is notable for SMIs (+226%), pMDIs with HFC-134a (+23%) and pMDIs with HFC-227ea (+26%). Only the number of DPIs dispensed has decreased by 4%. The share of inhaler types was analysed to assess potential shifts in prescription behaviour independent of the total increase in DDDs

This analysis revealed a continuously decreasing share of NPIs; however, the share of SMIs was increasing starting from a very low level. Hence, the decline in NPI usage is caused by decreased DPI dispensing. Although the share of pMDIs with HFC-134a increased, the share of pMDIs with HFC-227ea remained relatively stable ( figure 1c , table 2 ).

Comparing the percentage point changes between 2013 and 2022, the trend in DPI distribution (−8% p) was decreasing, and the SMIs were increasing (+5% p). This resulted in an overall reduction in NPI usage, along with an increase in pMDIs with HFC-134a (+3% p). The share of NPIs to total pMDIs in 2022 ranged from 52% to 48%, which was less climate-friendly than in 2013 with a share of 55% to 45% (table 2).

By examining figure 1b, the 10-year trend of the estimated CF in Germany can be observed in relation to the number of inhaler use (figure 1a). The dispensed NPIs emitted 16 kt CO2eq (estimated range, 11–26 kt CO2eq) in 2013. These estimated NPI emissions remained relatively stable at a low level, consistent with the 10-year dispensing trend. The estimated CF of pMDIs exhibited a different trend. There was an upward trend in the CF of pMDIs with HFC-134a from 386 (340–442) kt CO2eq in 2013 to 463 (407–530) kt CO2eq in 2022. An increase in the total number of inhalers dispensed and a decrease in the proportion of climate-friendly inhalers resulted in a 14% increase in the total CF of inhalers from 2013 to 2022, reaching a total CF of 525 (463–603) kt CO2eq in 2022 (figure 1b, online supplemental table 7).

Figure 1d shows that >95% of the estimated annual total CF attributed to inhalers was caused by pMDIs. Hence, there was a disproportionate contribution of pMDIs compared with their share of dispensing (table 3). This discrepancy can be attributed to the high GWP of pMDIs with HFC-134a (GWP=1430) and pMDIs with HFC-227ea (GWP=3220). As the overall share of inhaler types became less climate-friendly, there was also a slight increase in the relative contribution to CF by pMDIs.

Potential greenhouse gas-saving scenarios

In the following step, inhaler dispensing in 2022 in Germany was examined among groups aged <10, 10–79 and ≥80 years. The age groups were defined based on the assumption that a transition from pMDIs to NPIs is likely feasible for a considerable proportion of patients aged 10–79, depending on their clinical condition. Therefore, we assumed that it is possible to reduce the CF of patients aged 10–79 years.

In 2022, most inhalers were dispensed to groups aged 10–79 years, followed by those aged ≥80 years (figure 2a). As expected, pMDIs were prescribed predominantly for young children aged 0–9 years (figure 2b). However, owing to their lower consumption of DDDs, their CF remained relatively small, as shown in figure 2c.

In the first scenario, a minimum GHG saving was calculated by replacing only pMDIs with HFC-227ea with NPIs. This simple substitution would have resulted in a CF reduction of 40 kt CO2eq for 2022.

In the second scenario, the estimated saving potential for patients aged 10–79 years was based on an inhaler prescription regimen according to the current practice in Sweden: if 85% of all inhalers were prescribed as NPIs, the estimated GHG reduction in 2022 would have been 288 kt CO2eq. Since the total CF among all age groups in 2022 was 525 kt CO2eq, this scenario would have resulted in a 55% CF reduction.

If this prescription practice were implemented in Germany, based on 2022 data, an estimated annual saving of 288 kt CO2eq could be achieved. This would be equivalent to 514 000 individuals switching to a vegetarian diet, 134 000 individuals using climate-neutral mobility, or the CO2 storage of 100 000 ha of forest in 1 year (figure 4, calculations in online supplemental material).36 37

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate trends in inhaler dispensing to assess prescription practices and their resulting CF in Germany. This analysis of large-scale real-life data revealed an increase in dispensed inhalers and a corresponding rise in the estimated CF, whereas the prescription shares of NPIs and pMDIs remained relatively stable. Our 10-year analysis of inhaler dispensing patterns underscores the urgent need for enhanced initiatives to promote the prescription of climate-friendly inhalers. Achieving the German Society of General Practice and Family Medicine’s goal of climate neutrality by 2030 will clearly require a sustainable change in prescription practices, alongside targeted interventions to promote sustainable alternatives for healthcare providers and patients.38

Based on a Swedish prescription practice for the group aged 10–79 years and 2022 dispensing data, approximately 288 kt CO2eq could be saved annually. Therefore, prioritising the reduction of pMDIs in this age group as a nationwide strategy would have an immediate positive effect, resulting in a 55% average reduction of the country’s estimated CF of inhalers. The potential GHG savings would be even higher if the overall number of inhalers dispensed increases compared with 2022.

This study revealed three unexpected findings. First, the dispensing of pMDIs containing HFC-227ea has not decreased since 2013, indicating a lack of awareness that these inhalers are obsolete and can be easily replaced. Second, the NPI/pMDI ratio in the group aged 10−79 years is surprisingly less climate-friendly than with those aged ≥80 years, again underlining the potential shifts that can be made in the group aged 10–79 years. Third, the most significant change was the increase in SMI dispensing (+226%; +5% p in 10 years), which demonstrates a potential for climate-friendly inhaler usage. To maximise this potential, SMIs should replace pMDIs, particularly as relievers in asthma therapy (short-acting muscarinic antagonist (SAMA) / short-acting β2-adrenergic receptor agonists (SABA) SMIs) and for dual bronchodilation in COPD treatment (long-acting muscarinic antagonist (LAMA) / short-acting β2-adrenergic receptor agonists (LABA) SMIs), rather than displacing DPIs, as appears to be happening.

In an international comparison, Sweden leads with the most climate-friendly prescription practice, as only 13% of inhalers are pMDIs.29 Japan follows closely, with 23% pMDI usage, total emissions of 203 kt CO2eq in 2019 and limited savings potential owing to already climate-friendly prescribing practices.35 Germany and the Netherlands have comparable outcomes, with 48% and 50% pMDI use, respectively, and similar potential annual savings adjusted for population size: 288 kt CO2eq in Germany (84.4 million inhabitants) vs 63 kt CO2eq in the Netherlands (17.5 million inhabitants).23 The UK has the least climate-friendly prescription practice, with 70% pMDI use, a high annual savings potential of 550 kt CO2eq and inhalers accounting for 4% of the healthcare sector’s emissions compared with 1% based on our own calculations for Germany.29 30 Thus, the Netherlands and the UK also have significant potential for GHG savings. This suggests similar opportunities in other European countries. Promoting climate-friendly inhaler prescription could have a major effect on achieving greater GHG reductions across Europe.

In this study, data from 2013 to 2022 were used to analyse the number of inhalers dispensed in pharmacies, providing an indirect reflection of prescription practices in Germany. To our knowledge, no other study in this field has analysed trends in prescription practice over a decade. The strength of using a pharmacy claims-based database for drug utilisation studies is that it provides nationwide access to nearly 90% of the German population. Only inhalers dispensed in clinics and to patients with private health insurance were not included. The division into four inhaler types enabled a detailed analysis and allowed a more precise calculation of the CF. Another strength is the adoption of a precise calculation method for estimating CF, supported by comprehensive literature research. Despite calculating only estimates, a CF range was calculated using mean, minimum and maximum values to accommodate uncertainty. Furthermore, our conservative focus on the group aged 10–79 years likely under-estimates the true saving potential. This is particularly significant, as recent evidence demonstrates that most primary school-aged children (5–11 years old) can successfully use DPIs with appropriate training and support.39

A limitation of this study is the use of aggregated data on inhaler dispensing, which precludes the calculation of average per capita GHG emissions or savings. Furthermore, the dosage of inhalants for obstructive airway diseases varies based on individual factors such as the specific medical indication and patient age. Consequently, the dispensing values calculated in DDDs may differ slightly from actual usage. In addition, we did not quantify the potential risks and costs associated with large-scale inhaler switching. Successful transition for non-clinical reasons requires careful individual assessment, adequate education and training in inhalation technique and ongoing clinical monitoring. Without such support, there is a risk of worsening disease control, which could paradoxically increase GHG emissions.40 41

This analysis of aggregated data raises further questions, as it does not allow for multifactorial explanations of the observed trends. Additional research is required to understand the reasons and considerations that guide physicians in their inhaler prescription practices, as well as the extent to which inhaler availability affects inhaler dispensing. However, there are no indications that inhaler availability has significantly affected prescription behaviour. While switching to NPIs offers the greatest emission reduction, complementary strategies remain necessary for patients who still clinically require pMDIs. This includes the proper disposal of pMDIs through pharmacy return systems, which are currently lacking in Germany. Additionally, once available, pMDIs formulated with low-GWP propellants could provide a more climate-friendly alternative.24

The potential for change in prescription practices has already been demonstrated in a pilot study in a German pulmonology practice. Within 1 year, the share of DPIs increased from 49% to 78% without a significant increase in costs.13 It is now crucial to disseminate knowledge and reduce uncertainties concerning climate-conscious inhaler prescription, particularly as patients prioritise ‘environmentally-friendly inhalers’.22 Patients newly commencing inhaler therapy represent a particularly suitable target group for NPI prescription, as they require education on inhalation technique regardless of the inhaler type. In a shared decision-making process between the patient and the prescriber, it is essential to provide information on the CF of inhalers.18 This study can facilitate such efforts by providing and illustrating the first estimated CF data for Germany.

Our findings highlight both the necessity and feasibility of sustainable changes in clinical practice and policy to achieve climate neutrality. The German Medical Association’s professional code states that physicians must help preserve “the natural foundations of life with regard to their importance for human health”.42 Our results demonstrate a potential for GHG savings, a step that is small but significant and easy to implement. We stress the importance of disseminating knowledge and promoting climate-friendly inhaler prescribing, which also integrates climate change and its health risks into doctor–patient communication, as recommended by the WHO.43 This is one of the many ways healthcare professionals can contribute to climate action through clinical practice, research and policy engagement.
