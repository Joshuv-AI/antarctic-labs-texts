Optimization and prediction of paver block properties with ceramic waste as fine aggregate using response surface methodology
Authors: G Uday Kiran, Nakkeeran G, Dipankar Roy, George Uwadiegwu Alaneme
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe ceramic industry produces a significant volume of ceramic waste (CW), representing around 20–30% of its the entire output. The waste mostly comes from challenges noticed in the manufacturing process, overproduction, and damage to products. Considering the substantial worldwide production of ceramics, it is crucial to efficiently handle and recycle this waste to promote sustainability efforts. This study explores the conversion of ceramic waste into fine aggregates suitable for the production of paver blocks. Currently, a variety of assessments are being conducted to determine the effectiveness of these enhanced paver blocks. The evaluations involve aspects like compressive strength, water absorption (WA), dry density, flow table measurements, ultrasonic pulse velocity (UPV), and rebound hammer tests. The results indicate that replacing natural aggregates with up to 30% CW significantly improves compressive strength (CS) and Rebound results from tests. This study provides useful information into optimising the content of CW in paver blocks, contributing to the development of sustainable and economical construction materials. Furthermore, it focusses on minimising landfill waste and preserving natural resources.Keywords: Ceramic waste, Paver block, Sustainability, Alternative new building materials, RSM, Optimization and predictionSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Methodology.

In the absence of data autoscaling and standardization, the performance of machine learning models can be greatly influenced by the scale of the values present in the dataset. When features exhibit different scales, those with greater values can unduly affect the model, resulting in biased or distorted outcomes. This occurs due to the reliance of numerous machine learning algorithms on distance metrics or the assumption that features play an equal role in the prediction process. Autoscaling (normalizing feature values to a common scale) and standardization (transforming data to have a mean of zero and a standard deviation of one) are essential preprocessing steps that ensure all features contribute equally, thereby enhancing the model’s accuracy and performance.

Materials

Ceramics were collected from disposal grounds21. In the beginning, an iron rod had been used for splitting larger ceramic parts determining approximately 18 mm, into smaller amounts measuring around 6 mm. Once the size was reduced to below 3 mm using a ball mill machine, the outcome’s parts were suitable for use as a fine aggregate replacement. After being crushed, the ceramic specimens were sifted. the study, noticed the particles that were captured by a 2.36 mm sieve and passed through a 4.75 mm sieve. Following the grinding stage, ceramic elements were incorporated into the mortar as a substitute for the FA. By incorporating CW, there were significant advancements in compressive strength and water absorption capabilities, while also contributing to environmental responsibility22.

The methods used in crushing and sieving ceramic waste (CW) can greatly influence the ultimate characteristics of paver blocks. Various crushing techniques can influence the distribution of particle sizes, the texture of surfaces, and the morphology of ceramic particles, subsequently impacting the workability, compaction, and strength of paver blocks. For instance, smaller particles can enhance compressive strength by occupying voids, whereas larger, angular particles might improve interlocking but could compromise workability. Assessing how various crushing techniques influence these elements can enhance the effectiveness of CW as a replacement for fine aggregate.

In addition to compressive strength, water absorption, and various mechanical properties, multiple variables may play a vital role in the practical use of paver blocks. These factors include resistance to wear, which is essential for areas with heavy footfall, and the ability to conduct heat, significant in harsh weather conditions. Moreover, the ability to withstand freeze-thaw cycles could be crucial in colder climates, while resistance to chemicals might be important in industrial environments. Incorporating these properties in upcoming investigations would enhance the overall comprehension of the long-term performance of paver blocks under diverse conditions.

Response surface methodology (RSM)

RSM involves a range of arithmetical and scientific approaches that are utilized to enhance, refine, and optimize procedures30,31. In RSM, the CCD is a widely used method that employs partial factors approach to explore the connections between various variables and the resulting reactions. The efficacy of the model is evaluated by determining the R2, adjusted R2, and predicted R2 values. The effect of numerous variables is determined by computing the F-value. A higher F-value demonstrates greater importance of the component of the study’s results32,33. When the P-value is below 0.05, the model or its variables are deemed important.

Following the completion of the research, the data that was gathered underwent thorough analysis. This analysis involved the creation of multivariate regression formulas, which served as mathematical models34,35. These models were then utilized to make predictions about specific properties, Show in Fig. 4. A number of factors explain the 30% ceramic waste (CW) replacement-improved compressive strength. The rough, angular texture of CW particles improves the mechanical attachment in the cement matrix and the aggregate-cement paste bond. Fine CW particles can fill mix gaps, minimizing porous and expanding micro structures. CW’s substance may increase pozzolanic activity, improving cement hydration and durability. The cement matrix and CW interaction greatly increase compressive strength.

Result and discussion

Previous investigations on ceramic waste as fine aggregate in concrete and other construction materials showed promising sustainability and performance. Ceramic waste from building demolition and ceramic industry can reduce aggregate production’s ecological impact, according to research. Many studies have found that ceramic waste has mechanical properties equivalent to conventional aggregates, with some even stronger due to angular shapes. Medina et al.46 found that ceramic waste could replace natural sand in mortar mixtures with similar compressive strength. According to Kara et al.47 found that ceramic aggregates performed better in freeze-thaw cycles, making them suitable for climate-controlled structures. Ceramic waste also increases compressive strength, improving long-term performance. A few study results have been negative. Due to its angular shape and surface texture, ceramic waste may reduce mortar mix workability, according to some studies. Thus, admixtures or mix optimization are often recommended to mitigate this effect. Pacheco-Torgal and Jalali48 also stressed the need for extensive research on ceramic waste’s long-term durability and environmental impact, particularly in extreme conditions.

Limitations of ceramic waste

While there are promising advantages to incorporating ceramic waste as fine aggregate in construction, various challenges must be addressed. A significant issue is the inconsistency in the characteristics of ceramic waste, which can influence the uniformity and quality of the end construction material. Moreover, ceramic waste might have elevated water absorption rates in comparison to natural aggregates, potentially affecting the workability and strength of the PB. The existence of specific additives in the waste may result in compatibility challenges with conventional binders. Additionally, it is essential to conduct a comprehensive examination of the long-term durability and performance of structures that utilize ceramic waste to confirm their compliance with building standards and safety regulations. Overcoming these limitations is essential for effectively incorporating ceramic waste into construction practices.
