A Cross-Species Hydroquinone Model Replicates Smoking-Related Corneal Endothelial Oxidative Injury and Enables Therapeutic Screening of FGF10
Authors: Bryanna J. Lee, Devansh Agarwal, John H. Huang, Anastasiia Ivanova, Jennifer J. Bu, Christian A. Hill, Takako Noguchi, Jong Hwa Jun, Helen P. Makarenkova, Karl J. Wahlin, Peter X. Shaw, Natalie A. Afshari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo establish an in vitro model of smoking-associated oxidative injury to the corneal endothelium and evaluate an injury-mitigating factor.MethodsPrimary human and porcine corneal endothelial cells (CEnCs) were exposed to hydroquinone (HQ; 0–150 µM, 48 hours). Outcomes included viability (ATP-based assay), reactive oxygen species (ROS), and apoptosis (TUNEL). Bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) profiled HQ (Nrf2 and Hippo–Yap pathways) and HQ ± fibroblast growth factor 10 (FGF10) responses under moderate stress (cadherin/Wnt programs). FGF10 dose–response (1–100 ng/mL) identified 30 ng/mL for subsequent HQ ± FGF10 studies. Western blotting provided protein validation.ResultsHQ caused dose-dependent loss of viability with increased ROS and apoptosis (EC50: 191.2 µM human; 151.2 µM porcine). RNA-seq showed induction of Nrf2 antioxidant/detoxification genes with attenuation of Hippo–Yap signaling. At 150 µM HQ, total Yap decreased and the pYap/Yap ratio increased, consistent with Yap inactivation. FGF10 improved viability across doses (1–100 ng/mL; plateau 30–100 ng/mL) and required cotreatment during HQ exposure. Using 30 ng/mL, FGF10 improved viability across HQ doses, reduced ROS at 100 to 150 µM, and decreased apoptosis at 50 to 100 µM. At 50 µM HQ, FGF10 increased cadherin/Wnt-related transcripts, while N-cadherin protein decreased and β-catenin remained stable, consistent with junctional remodeling rather than full endothelial–mesenchymal transition. Key injury and rescue phenotypes were consistent across species.ConclusionsOur study supports HQ as a translatable in vitro model of smoking-related oxidative stress in CEnCs. FGF10 shows injury-mitigating and pro-regenerative effects, supporting further preclinical evaluation to preserve endothelial integrity and potentially lessen reliance on transplantation in corneal endothelial dystrophies.

The corneal endothelium maintains corneal transparency through its barrier and pump functions but has limited regenerative capacity, making it vulnerable to oxidative injury and endothelial disorders such as Fuchs endothelial corneal dystrophy (FECD).1 FECD affects approximately 7.33% of the population and is the leading cause of corneal transplant worldwide. With only one donor cornea available for every 70 patients, therapies that preserve endothelial function and reduce dependence on transplantation are urgently needed.1–6

Oxidative stress is characterized by an imbalance between reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and antioxidant defenses. In corneal endothelial cells (CEnCs), this imbalance drives DNA damage, endothelial–mesenchymal transition (EndMT), extracellular matrix (ECM) dysregulation, and cell death.2 Cigarette smoke is a recognized risk factor for endothelial loss and advanced FECD,7,8 yet smoking-related oxidative injury in CEnCs remains underexplored. Hydroquinone (HQ), an abundant constituent of cigarette smoke with potent redox-cycling activity,9 has been used to model dysfunction in retinal pigment epithelium (RPE),10–12 but applications to CEnCs remain limited.13,14 In RPE and other ocular models, HQ-induced oxidative stress activates the nuclear factor erythroid 2–related factor 2 (Nrf2) antioxidant pathway, and loss of Nrf2 exacerbates HQ-mediated DNA damage and apoptosis.10,15–17 Although HQ and Nrf2 interactions have not yet been defined in CEnCs, Nrf2-regulated antioxidant defenses are essential for CEnC survival under oxidative stress from ultraviolet A irradiation and tert-butylhydroquinone, and they are impaired in settings such as diabetes and FECD, which are associated with endothelial vulnerability.18–20

The Hippo–Yes-associated protein (Yap) pathway is a key regulator of cell fate, proliferation, and apoptosis, integrating mechanical and stress signals to control tissue homeostasis.21,22 Direct studies of Hippo–Yap signaling in CEnCs are lacking. However, in lens epithelial cells, oxidative stress suppresses Hippo–Yap signaling and reduces Yap-dependent transcriptional programs, including factors involved in proliferation and antioxidant defense.23,24 These findings support a broader role for crosstalk between ROS and Hippo–Yap signaling in ocular tissues. HQ also perturbs growth factor signaling in ocular models, including altering the balance between vascular endothelial growth factor and pigment epithelium-derived factor in RPE.25,26 Together, these observations support the concept that targeting growth factor pathways may be an effective strategy to counteract HQ-mediated ocular injury.

In this study, we establish and validate an HQ-induced oxidative stress model in human, porcine, and murine CEnCs to investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying smoking-induced endothelial damage and to identify potential therapeutic interventions. We focus on fibroblast growth factor 10 (FGF10), which has established roles in ocular development,27–29 corneal repair and protection,30,31 and protection against oxidative stress.32 Together, this multispecies platform provides a clinically relevant tool to study smoking-induced corneal endothelial injury and to screen candidate therapies, such as FGF10, to reduce endothelial loss and address the global shortage of donor corneas.

Research-Grade Human Corneoscleral Tissues

Donor corneas considered unsuitable for transplantation were obtained from the San Diego Eye Bank (San Diego, CA, USA) under UC San Diego Institutional Review Board approval and in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Sixty-three corneas from 43 donors with a mean age of 62.8 ± 15.2 years (range, 21–77 years) were used within 2 weeks. Of these, 38 corneas were from male and 25 from female donors (Supplementary Table S1). Basic donor demographics (age, sex, and selected clinical characteristics) were available, but donors were otherwise deidentified.

To increase human CEnC (hCEnC) yield while maintaining biological diversity, donors were combined into independent donor pools of two to three donors. Pooling was performed at the time of primary hCEnC isolation (P0) or after the first passage (P1) by combining low-yield primary cultures, aligning with published protocols for establishing primary cultures from elderly donors.33 Donor tissue was allocated to pools based on tissue availability and to mitigate the influence of any single donor. While pooling introduces biological variability related to donor age and other factors, using multiple independent biological replicates (n = 3) from distinct donor pools reduces the impact of individual donor characteristics on observed responses.

Each experimental replicate (n = 3) represents an independent biological preparation derived from a distinct donor pool. Technical replicates represent repeated measurements performed on the same pooled preparation.

Cell Isolation and Culture

The hCEnCs were isolated using a previously described two-step peel-and-digest method with a dual media culture system.34,35 Briefly, Descemet membrane (DM)–endothelium was stripped, pooled, digested in collagenase A (2 mg/mL, 2–4 hours), and plated on FNC-coated 12-well plates in “maintenance medium” (Human Endothelial Serum Free Medium, 4% FBS, 1% PS) overnight. Cells were expanded in “proliferative medium” (Opti-MEM-I, 8% FBS, 5 ng/mL EGF, 10 µM Y-27632, 100 µg/mL BPE, 0.5 mM L-ascorbic acid 2-phosphate, 200 mg/L CaCl2, 0.08% chondroitin sulfate), then returned to maintenance medium ≥3 days before subculture.

Porcine eyes from 3- to 4-month-old pigs (Premier BioSource Farms, Ramona, CA, USA) were processed within 12 hours postmortem. After disinfection, corneoscleral buttons were prepared, the endothelium was exposed to 0.5% TE for 5 minutes at 37°C, and CEnCs were gently scraped from DM and plated on FNC-coated 12-well plates in maintenance medium.

Subculture used Accumax single-cell dissociation (≤15 minutes for hCEnCs, ≤45 min for porcine CEnCs [pCEnCs]). Cells were replated at ≥1.5 × 104 cells/cm2 on FNC-coated plates. The hCEnCs were used at passage 2 and pCEnCs at passage 1.

Mouse Models and Immunocytochemistry

Fgf10CreERT2:Rosa26tdTomato mice were generated by crossing Fgf10CreERT2 (RRID:IMSR_JAX:033807)36,37 with Rosa26tdTomato (RRID:IMSR_JAX:007909). For inducible Cre activation, mice received intraperitoneal tamoxifen on 2 consecutive days, 1 week before tissue collection. Eyes were harvested, fixed in 2% PFA/PBS for 60 minutes on ice, and cryopreserved in isopentane cooled with liquid nitrogen. Cryosections (10 µm) were cut using a Hacker/Bright (Mannedorf, Switzerland) OTF5000-LS004 cryostat. Sections were blocked for 1 hour in 5% FBS in TBS (150 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.6) containing 0.05% Tween-20. Primary antibody E-cadherin (1:50; cat. 610182, BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA; RRID: AB_397581) was applied overnight at 4°C. After washing, sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor–conjugated secondary antibodies (1:200; cat. 21136, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 45 minutes at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, mounted using Fluoromount-G, and imaged on a Zeiss LSM 880 confocal microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). For fibroblast visualization, triple transgenic Fgf10CreERT2:Rosa26tdTomato:PDGFRαeGFP mice were generated by crossing with PDGFRαeGFP (RRID: IMSR_JAX:007669). Tamoxifen induction was performed as above. Corneas were dissected, processed for frozen sectioning, and immunostained using the same protocol described above.

Immunocytochemistry and Morphometric Analysis of Cultured Corneal Endothelial Cells

Confluent P1–P2 CEnCs were fixed in 4% PFA, permeabilized in 0.3% Triton X-100, blocked for 30 minutes at 4°C in PBS-T + 1% Blocker BSA, and incubated with primary antibodies Na+/K+-ATPase (1:200; cat. 23565, Cell Signaling Technologies, Danvers, MA, USA), ZO-1 (1:200; cat. 61-7300, Thermo Fisher Scientific), N-cadherin (1:200; cat. 13116, Cell Signaling Technologies), or Yap (1:200; cat. sc-15407, Santa Cruz Biotechnologies, Dallas, TX, USA) overnight in 4°C. After washing, cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor–conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500; cat. A21202 and A21206, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 1 hour at 37°C. Cells were mounted in Vectashield with DAPI and imaged on an Eclipse TS2 Microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Cell Viability, CellROX, and TUNEL Assays

CEnCs were treated with HQ (0–500 µM) for 48 hours at 37°C. Viability was measured with CellTiter-Glo at 100 µL per well (n = 3 per treatment, total samples = 18) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, plates were incubated for 10 minutes at room temperature and transferred to a black clear-bottom 96-well plate, and luminescence was measured with a Molecular Devices (San Jose, CA, USA) SpectraMax M5 using SoftMax Pro software (v5.4) with a 1-second integration time. To define the working dose and timing, cells were exposed to 190 µM HQ and treated with FGF10 (1, 10, 30, or 100 ng/mL) either concurrently during HQ exposure or as a 2-hour pretreatment prior to HQ, followed by CellTiter-Glo quantification at 48 hours. This protocol was repeated for 0 to 150 µM HQ ± FGF10 at 30 ng/mL (n = 3 per treatment, total samples = 9).

ROS and apoptosis were assessed after treatment with 0 to 150 µM HQ for 48 hours. Cells were stained with CellROX Green at 5 µM and DAPI for 30 minutes (n = 3 wells per condition, 9 wells total), and TUNEL labeling was carried out with the In Situ Cell Death Detection Kit, TMR Red according to the manufacturer's instructions. Cells were fixed in 4% PFA, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and blocked with 2% BSA. The TUNEL mixture (1:10) was added to each sample for 1 hour at 37°C. DNase I–treated cells were positive controls.

Imaging was performed on an Eclipse TS2 microscope (Nikon) and analyzed using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) software. For each well, three nonoverlapping images at 10× or 20× were acquired, yielding a minimum of 3000 or 600 cells, respectively, per condition per experiment for analysis. Acquisition settings (exposure time and gain) were kept constant across all conditions within an experiment. For CellROX, images were converted to 8-bit and background-subtracted, and an intensity threshold was defined using the untreated control and then applied uniformly to all images in the experiment. For TUNEL, a global threshold was similarly established from the DNase I–treated positive control and applied to all images, and TUNEL-positive nuclei were quantified using the “Analyze Particles” function. Image analysis was performed unblinded to treatment condition, with predefined acquisition parameters and fixed threshold settings applied uniformly across all samples.

Single-Cell RNA Sequencing Analysis

Public human cornea single-cell RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data were obtained from Català et al.38 The processed dataset (GEO accession: GSE186433) and metadata were downloaded from DataVerseNL (https://doi.org/10.34894/X7ZSDZ). FGF10 and FGFR2 expression were visualized across clusters using violin and UMAP plots.

Bulk RNA Sequencing and Analysis

Total RNA was isolated from human or porcine CEnC cultures using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) after 48 hours in 0 to 150 µM HQ ± 30 ng/mL FGF10 (n = 8 conditions, n = 3 each, total samples = 24). Quantity and quality were assessed on a Nanodrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 4200 TapeStation (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Only samples with an OD 260/280 ratio from 1.9 to 2.1 and an RNA integrity number >7 were used. Libraries were prepared with the NEBNext UltraExpress RNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA; cat. E3330L) and sequenced on a NovaSeq X Plus (100-bp paired-end reads; Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) at the UCSD Institute for Genomic Medicine (IGM) Core to obtain 25 million reads per sample.

Adapters were trimmed from FASTQ files using TrimGalore! (v0.6.10), and reads were mapped to GRCh38.p13 (human) or Sscrofa11.1 (pig) using HISAT2 (v2.2.1). Gene counts were quantified using FeatureCounts within the Subread package (v2.0.8). Differential expression across treatment groups was performed in DESeq2 (Galaxy v2.11.40.8) using the median of ratios method for normalization. Principal component analysis was performed using DESeq2 (Galaxy v2.11.40.8). Hierarchical cluster maps were generated with Seaborn (v0.13.2) and Matplotlib (v3.9.2) in Python (v3.12.7). Volcano plots were generated using the Sanbomics package (v0.0.7). Significant differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as normalized counts >10, |log2FC > 1|, and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) used GSEApy (v1.1.5) against Reactome 2022 and Panther 2016 to identify significantly enriched pathways (FDR < 0.05). Enrichment results were visualized as −log10 (FDR) bar charts using Seaborn and Matplotlib. Heatmaps for oxidative stress–related pathways identified by GSEA plotted treatment means (n = 3 replicates per treatment) using Seaborn/Matplotlib based on DESeq2 normalized gene counts.

Western Blotting

The hCEnCs were lysed in RIPA buffer. Proteins were separated on NuPAGE Bis-Tris Mini Protein Gels (4%–12%), transferred to PVDF membranes using the Trans-Blot Turbo Transfer System (Bio-Rad), blocked with 3% Blotting-Grade Blocker, and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against GAPDH (1:200; cat. 2118S, Cell Signaling Technologies), β-actin (1:200; cat. 66009-1-Ig, Proteintech, Chicago, Illinois, USA), Sod1 (1:200; cat. 37385T, Cell Signaling Technologies), Hmox1 (1:200; cat. 26416T, Cell Signaling Technologies), N-cadherin (1:200; cat. 13116T, Cell Signaling Technologies), β-catenin (1:200; cat. sc-7199, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), Yap (1:200; cat. sc-15407, SCBT), or phospho-Yap (1:200; cat. 13008S Cell Signaling Technologies). Membranes were then incubated for 1 hour at room temperature with a secondary antibody (anti-rabbit IgG or anti-mouse IgG, HRP-linked antibody, 1:1000; cat. 7074S/7076S, Cell Signaling Technologies). Signals were developed with Clarity ECL Western Blotting substrates, imaged on an Azure 400 Imager (Azure Biosystems, Dublin, CA, USA), and quantified with ImageJ software.

Results

Hydroquinone Induces Dose-Dependent Oxidative Stress and Apoptosis in hCEnCs

Primary hCEnCs maintained hexagonal morphology and membrane localization of ZO-1, Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, and N-cadherin across passages 0 to 2 (P0–P2), confirming preserved tight junctions, pump function, and cell–cell adhesion (Fig. 1A). P2 hCEnCs exposed to HQ for 48 hours (0–500 µM, in triplicate) showed a dose-dependent loss of viability with an EC50 of 191.2 µM. Cell viability remained above 75% through 100 µM but declined sharply at higher doses (Fig. 1B). Subsequent studies used 0 to 150 µM HQ for 48 hours to induce injury without total cell loss. ROS increased at 50 µM (*P < 0.05), 100 µM (***P < 0.001), and 150 µM (****P < 0.0001) compared with control (Fig. 1C), and apoptosis (TUNEL-positive cells) increased at 50 µM (****P < 0.001) and 100 µM (**P < 0.01) compared with control (Fig. 1D).

Transcriptomic Analysis of Hydroquinone-Induced Oxidative Stress

Bulk RNA sequencing of hCEnCs (0–150 µM HQ, 48 hours, in triplicate) showed dose-separated clustering by principal component analysis (Supplementary Fig. S1A) and graded shifts in differentially expressed genes (DEGs, Fig. 2A). At 150 µM, upregulated DEGs included canonical Nrf2 antioxidants (Hmox1, Srxn1), integrated stress and endoplasmic reticulum repair genes (Atf3, Chac1, Sel1l3), detoxification factors (Cyp1b1, Cyp1a2), metabolic adaptors (Hkdc1, Slc6a9, Lyplal1-dt), and regulatory mediators (C2cd4a, Epgn, Sh2d5, Sectm1, Mnral2p) (Supplementary Fig. S1B). GSEA against REACTOME_2022 showed activation of oxidative stress pathways comparing control and 150 µM HQ, including the response of EIF2AK1 (HRI) to heme deficiency (−log10 (FDR) = 5.15), ATF4 activation in endoplasmic reticulum stress (−log10FDR = 2.30), and PERK regulated gene expression (−log10 (FDR) = 1.83, Fig. 2B). These results were corroborated by an increase in normalized expression counts of integrated stress response (ISR) targets Atf4, Ddit3, Atf3, and Ppp1r15a (Supplementary Fig. S1C). Nrf2-centered pathways were also prominent, including nuclear events mediated by NFE2L2 and the KEAP1-NFE2L2 pathway (−log10FDR = 3.39 and 2.67), along with biological oxidations (−log10 (FDR) = 3.63) and phase I functionalization of compounds (−log10 (FDR) = 2.61), consistent with coordinated translational control, antioxidant defense, and detoxification.

A heatmap of significant Nrf2 pathway genes showed a dose-dependent induction of Nfe2l2 and glutathione–biosynthetic enzymes Gclc and Gclm (blue boxes); phase II detoxification genes Nqo1, Srnx1, Gstp1, and Pgd (purple boxes); and redox-buffering/transport components Slc7a11, Sod1/2, and Hmox1 (orange boxes, Fig. 2C; Supplementary Fig. S2A–C). To validate Nrf2 pathway activation at the protein level, we performed Western blotting for Hmox1 and Sod1 as representative antioxidant targets. Hmox1 is a well-established direct Nrf2 target,39 and Sod1 is a key superoxide scavenging enzyme whose expression was increased in our Nrf2 gene panel. Western blotting confirmed Hmox1 and Sod1 protein upregulation at 100 and 150 µM HQ relative to control (Figs. 2D, 2E).

In parallel, HQ modulated the Hippo–Yap pathway. A heatmap of significant core components revealed dose-dependent, gene-specific changes (Fig. 2F; Supplementary Figs. S3A–C). Among the core transcriptional effectors (gray boxes), Yap1, Tead1, Tead2, and Tead4 decreased with increasing HQ, Tead3 increased at the highest dose, and Wwtr1 (Taz) remained unchanged. Upstream kinases and scaffolds (pink boxes) demonstrated decreased Lats2 and Mob1a with increased Sav1 and Stk4 at higher doses. Membrane-associated and regulatory proteins (green boxes) Amotl1, Amotl2, Nf2, and Wwc1 were predominantly reduced, while Amot increased at lower concentrations and fell at higher concentrations.

As Yap is regulated predominantly through posttranslational modifications, including phosphorylation, subcellular localization, and protein stability,40,41 we performed Western blotting for phosphorylated Yap (pYap) at Ser127, as a marker for inactivated Yap, and for total Yap to assess functional Yap activity. The relative pYap/Yap ratio increased with HQ exposure and was significantly elevated at 150 µM HQ compared to control (*P < 0.05, Fig. 2G). Consistent with this, immunohistochemical staining showed reduced total Yap in hCEnCs treated with 150 µM HQ relative to control (Fig. 2H).

Cross-Species Validation of a Hydroquinone Oxidative Stress Model in Corneal Endothelium

The pCEnCs preserved membrane localization of ZO-1, Na+/K+-ATPase, and N-cadherin (Supplementary Fig. S4A). Viability declined with increasing HQ dose (0–500 µM HQ, 48 hours, in triplicate), yielding an EC50 of 151.2 µM (Supplementary Fig. S4B). Similar to hCEnCs, RNA-seq with GSEA (0–150 µM HQ, 48 hours, in triplicate) against REACTOME_2022 enriched core oxidative stress response pathways, including nuclear events mediated by NFE2L2 (−log10 (FDR) = 5.92) and the KEAP1-NFE2L2 pathway (−log10 (FDR) = 4.24, Supplementary Fig. S4C).

A heatmap of significant Nrf2 pathway genes (Supplementary Fig. S4D) revealed a lower concentration peak in pCEnCs at 50 to 100 µM HQ compared to hCEnCs. Nfe2l2 and Nqo1 were highest around 50 to 100 µM in both species. In pCEnCs, glutathione synthesis and recycling genes (Gclc, Gclm, Gsr, blue boxes) were most elevated at 100 µM, whereas the corresponding increase in hCEnCs occurred at 150 µM. Phase II and redox enzymes Nqo1, Txnrd1, Srxn1, Pgd, and Me1 (purple boxes) were significantly induced at 100 µM, similar to hCEnCs. Redox buffering and transport components Slc7a11, Sod1, Sod2, and Hmox1 (orange boxes) rose at 100 µM and were sustained or increased at 150 µM in both species.

Species-specific differences were confined to auxiliary genes. Gstp1 was significant in hCEnCs but not in pCEnCs, and Akr1c1 and Aldh1a1 were significant in pCEnCs. Additional increased hCEnC genes included Akr1b10, Abcc3, Slc2a1, Slc2a12, Fth1, and Ftl. Overall, core antioxidant modules were conserved between human and porcine CEnCs, and pCEnCs showed a lower activation threshold.

FGF10-Mediated Protection Against Hydroquinone-Induced Damage

Given that HQ disrupts growth factor signaling in ocular models25,26 and FGF10 has established roles in ocular development27–29 and corneal repair,30,31 we investigated whether FGF10 signaling is altered by HQ and whether exogenous FGF10 can rescue hCEnCs from HQ-induced injury. Following HQ exposure in hCEnCs, FGF10 was significantly decreased at all HQ concentrations (*P < 0.05, ***P < 0.001, Fig. 3A), and FGFR2 was decreased at 100 and 150 µM HQ (**P < 0.01, Fig. 3B). Public human corneal single-cell RNA-seq from eight donor corneas38 showed FGF10 enriched in endothelium compared to stromal cells and nearly absent in epithelium, immune cells, and vascular endothelium (Fig. 3C; Supplementary Fig. S5A). Notably, approximately 80% of endothelial cells exhibited detectable levels of FGF10 transcripts (data not shown), indicating widespread expression within this population. FGFR2 was strongly expressed in endothelial cells and also present in stromal and epithelial clusters, suggesting a potential paracrine axis in which stromal FGF10 could influence the epithelium via FGFR2-mediated signaling.

To validate the conservation and functional relevance of FGF10 in the endothelium across species, we utilized the FGF10CreERT2;Rosa26tdTomato mouse model36,37 (Fig. 3D), as FGF10 antibodies for in vivo studies and immunostaining are currently unavailable. Two- and 4-month-old mice were administered tamoxifen on 2 consecutive days (Fig. 3E). Confocal imaging of frozen sections (Fig. 3F) and whole mounts (Fig. 3G) showed FGF10-TOM+ stromal cells and uniform labeling of corneal endothelial cells, with E-cadherin (ECAD) marking the corneal endothelium. In crosses with PDGFRαeGFP (platelet-derived growth factor receptor α-GFP) reporter mice (Fig. 3H), FGF10 expression was restricted to a subset of PDGFRαGFP+ stromal cells most consistent with fibroblasts and stromal keratocytes, supporting their mesenchymal identity and capacity to adopt a fibroblast-like phenotype under pathologic conditions. Consistent with our previous findings above, FGF10 was highly expressed in corneal endothelial cells.

Based on our HQ dose–response experiments, 190 µM HQ (EC50 for HQ-induced viability loss) was selected as the standardized stress condition to generate and FGF10 dose–response curve. The hCEnCs exposed to 190 µM HQ were cotreated with increasing FGF10 doses (0, 1, 10, 30, and 100 ng/mL). All FGF10 concentrations significantly improved viability compared with HQ alone (****P < 0.0001), with maximal rescue achieved by 30 ng/mL and no additional benefit at 100 ng/mL (Fig. 3I). Accordingly, 30 ng/mL was selected as the working concentration for subsequent experiments as the lowest dose associated with maximal rescue. Using 30 ng/mL FGF10, cotreatment across HQ doses (0, 50, 100, 150 µM) significantly increased viability compared with HQ alone (Fig. 3J), whereas 2-hour FGF10 pretreatment did not prevent the dose-dependent decrease in viability with increasing HQ (Supplementary Fig. S5B).

FGF10 also reduced HQ-induced ROS at 100 and 150 µM (*P < 0.05, ****P < 0.0001, Supplementary Fig. S5C) and decreased TUNEL-positive apoptosis at 50 and 100 µM (*P < 0.05, Supplementary Fig. S5D).

Hydroquinone-Induced Transcriptomic Remodeling Is Partially Mitigated by FGF10

GSEA of hCEnCs (0–150 µM HQ, 48 hours, in triplicate) showed that at 50 µM, FGF10 significantly enriched extracellular matrix organization by REACTOME_2022 (−log10 (FDR) = 1.58) (Fig. 4A) and cadherin (log10 (FDR) = 5.74) and Wnt (−log10 (FDR) = 5.24) signaling by PANTHER_2016 (Fig. 4B). At higher HQ doses, FGF10 did not significantly upregulate pathways.

Cadherin pathway heatmaps (Fig. 4C) showed that 50 µM HQ alone downregulated N-cadherin (Cdh2), Gja1, Vcl, and Cdc42, as well as reduced endocytic and recycling factors (Cav1, Arf6) with broad suppression of protocadherins. Cotreatment with FGF10 at 50 µM HQ restored these core junctional and trafficking genes to at or above baseline and elevated multiple protocadherins. For Wnt pathway genes (Fig. 4D), 50 µM HQ broadly suppressed transcripts, with reduced expression of the canonical target Axin2 and the key effector and mediator Tcf7l2. The addition of FGF10 at 50 µM HQ shifted expression toward canonical Wnt engagement with induction of Axin2, Tcf712, Crebbp, Srcap, Wnt7b, and Wnt8b. As HQ dose increased, Wnt gene expression was broadly attenuated.

To assess cadherin/Wnt pathway changes at the protein level, we performed Western blotting for N-cadherin and β-catenin, as they are central mediators of adherens junction integrity and canonical Wnt signaling, respectively.42,43 In the presence of FGF10, N-cadherin protein levels showed a downward trend with increasing HQ concentration and reached statistical significance only at 50 µM HQ + FGF10 (**P < 0.01), despite transcriptional restoration of N-cadherin at this dose. In contrast, β-catenin protein levels remained stable across HQ conditions with and without FGF10 (Figs. 4E, 4F).

Regarding the Hippo–Yap pathway, in the presence of FGF10, baseline expression at 0 µM + FGF10 modestly increased multiple Hippo–Yap components, including Yap1, Tead1, Mob1A, Lats2, Amot, Wwc1, and Nf2, compared with untreated cells. This upregulation was partially preserved at 50 µM HQ + FGF10 but largely lost at 100 to 150 µM HQ + FGF10 (Supplementary Fig. S5E). These transcript-level changes did not reach statistical significance. Consistent with this, Western blot analysis of the pYap/Yap ratio showed no FGF10-dependent differences at any HQ concentration (Supplementary Fig. S5F).

Discussion

In this study, we established a physiologically relevant in vitro oxidative stress model in primary hCEnCs using HQ, a major oxidative component of cigarette smoke. This model recapitulates key features of smoking-induced oxidative injury and provides a robust platform for investigating molecular mechanisms of corneal endothelial dysfunction, with direct relevance to smoking-associated disease and FECD.9 Using this platform, we evaluated FGF10 as a potential therapeutic agent to attenuate HQ-induced damage.

HQ elicited dose-dependent endothelial injury with early increases in ROS, apoptosis at moderate doses, and sharp viability loss at higher exposures, mirroring oxidative stress–mediated degeneration in FECD and related disorders.2,44 At the highest HQ doses, the decline in TUNEL-positive cells despite rising ROS likely reflects a shift from organized apoptosis to more rapid nonapoptotic death, such as necrosis or pyroptosis, or extensive DNA–protein crosslinking that limits detection of fragmented DNA by TUNEL.45,46 In FECD, antioxidant defenses are impaired, including downregulation of Nrf2-regulated antioxidant genes, which reduces the capacity to counter ROS.9,47 Under basal conditions, Keap1 targets Nrf2 for ubiquitination and degradation. With oxidative stress, Keap1 is modified, allowing Nrf2 accumulation, nuclear entry, and induction of antioxidant genes.9 In our model, Nrf2 pathway genes, including Hmox1, Nqo1, and genes for redox homeostasis and glutathione synthesis, increased with dose, consistent with coordinated antioxidant defense.13,48

The ISR also increased, with Atf4, Ddit3, Atf3, and Ppp1r15a induced while Eif2ak1, Eif2s1, and Eif2b remained stable, a pattern consistent with regulation through eIF2⍺ phosphorylation and stress adaptive translation.49 These changes align with compensatory responses observed in experimental models and patient-derived tissues.44,50 Together, the observed oxidative burden, cell death, and stress pathway engagement parallel the FECD injury cascade, where oxidative DNA damage, mitochondrial dysfunction, and apoptosis are central to disease progression. The failure of FECD corneal endothelial cells to activate a sufficient Nrf2-mediated antioxidant response under oxidative stress leads to heightened cellular vulnerability, mitochondrial dysfunction, and progressive cell loss.44,48,50,51 These findings underscore the central role of Nrf2 in corneal endothelial homeostasis and support the potential utility of this model as a platform to explore therapeutic strategies that enhance Nrf2 activity in the context of oxidative stress and FECD pathogenesis.

The HQ concentrations used in this study (EC₅₀ ≈ 190 µM, with experimental doses up to 150 µM) are consistent with prior in vitro models of acute oxidative injury in ocular and vascular cells.12,52 These doses are higher than estimated steady-state HQ levels in biological fluids, where reported plasma concentrations after environmental, occupational, or topical exposure are generally in the sub-micromolar to low-micromolar range, and direct measurements in aqueous humor of smokers have not been characterized.53,54 Thus, this dosing strategy is best viewed as a model of acute, high-level oxidant stress rather than a direct reflection of chronic in vivo exposure. This paradigm was chosen to reliably induce oxidative stress, cell death, and pathway activation over a short experimental window, providing a mechanistic window into the cellular response to severe oxidative insult. Future work using lower, repetitive dosing or mixed oxidant exposures will be needed to more fully capture chronic exposure scenarios relevant to human disease.

In parallel, HQ reduced Hippo–Yap signaling, suggesting reduced capacity for endothelial survival and regenerative competence.21,22 In this cascade, Mst1/2 activate Lats1/2 to phosphorylate Yap1 and Wwtr1 (Taz) and restrict nuclear entry. When the pathway is inactive, unphosphorylated Yap/Taz enter the nucleus and partner with Tead transcription factors to drive repair programs.55 With HQ, the relative preservation of Taz with loss of Yap-driven transcription aligns with the dominant role of Yap in proliferation and cell survival.56 Upstream changes favored inhibition, with increased Sav1 and Stk4 despite decreases in Lats2 and Mob1a, suggesting that oxidative stress shifts the pathway away from regeneration. These changes align with reduced proliferation and increased apoptosis seen in FECD endothelium.57 These changes were corroborated by our findings that the pYap/Yap ratio was increased at the maximum dose of 150 µM HQ, indicating that severe oxidative stress both inactivates Yap through Ser127 phosphorylation-mediated cytoplasmic retention and promotes its degradation through phosphodegron-mediated proteasomal pathways. This pattern is consistent with reports that oxidative and metabolic stress drive Yap phosphorylation, cytoplasmic retention, and proteasomal loss, linking Hippo pathway activation to reduced proliferative capacity.58–60 Together, these findings support a model in which HQ-driven oxidative stress disables a key survival and repair node, leaving corneal endothelial cells vulnerable to apoptosis and limiting their ability to regenerate.

The pCEnCs are highly conserved with hCEnCs and are widely recognized as a practical alternative when human donor corneas are limited.61,62 Both species showed dose-dependent loss of viability in response to HQ and a conserved Nrf2-centered program that increased glutathione synthesis and cystine import (Gclc, Gclm, Slc7a11), phase II detoxification and thiol repair genes (Nqo1, Txnrd1), and redox buffering genes (Hmox1, Sod1, Sod2), with the master regulator, Nfe2l2, increased. The pCEnCs exhibited a slightly lower EC50 and reached their strongest Nrf2 responses at 50 µM HQ, whereas hCEnCs did so at 100 µM, suggesting a lower activation threshold that may reflect baseline antioxidant capacity. Species-specific differences were confined to auxiliary detoxification and transport genes, which may influence metabolism and adaptation to stress. Such divergence is documented in comparative studies63,64 and highlights the importance of confirming key findings in human cells before clinical translation. Despite these differences, the shared viability profiles and conserved pathways support pCEnCs as a scalable and physiologically similar system when human donor tissue is limited.

Furthermore, public human corneal single-cell RNA sequencing data38 and our mouse lineage tracing studies show FGF10 and FGFR2 highly expressed in corneal endothelial cells across species, indicating an evolutionarily conserved role for FGF10-FGFR2 signaling in maintaining endothelial integrity, supporting cell survival, and potentially mediating regenerative responses. These findings reinforce the physiological relevance of our model and underscore the translational potential of targeting this pathway for therapeutic intervention.

FGF10, a growth factor previously implicated in tissue repair and regulation of oxidative stress,65–67 emerged as an injury-mediating candidate within our framework. Our FGF10 dose–response curve under EC50 HQ stress showed significant rescue at all concentrations and a plateau between 30 and 100 ng/mL, consistent with receptor-saturable growth factor signaling, which supports a relatively broad therapeutic range, with 30 ng/mL representing the lowest maximally effective dose.68 These data demonstrate that the protective effect of FGF10 is not idiosyncratic to a single concentration but reflects a reproducible dose–response relationship. The timing experiment further refined this by demonstrating that cotreatment during HQ exposure, but not 2-hour pretreatment, conferred protection, suggesting that FGF10’s cytoprotective effects require a concurrent presence during oxidative injury rather than prior conditioning. This timing-dependent protection is consistent with a model in which FGF10 acts primarily through real-time activation of antioxidant and survival pathways rather than inducing long-lasting transcriptional reprogramming.65,70,71 The lack of benefit seen from pretreatment likely reflects the rapid and partly irreversible nature of HQ-induced oxidative damage combined with the corneal endothelium's particular vulnerability due to its postmitotic state, high metabolic activity, and lifelong exposure to environmental stressors.2,71 Under these conditions, FGF10 must be present to prevent oxidative damage as it occurs, and these data suggest that, in corneal endothelium, FGF10 functions predominantly as a real-time cytoprotective factor rather than a long-lasting preconditioning agent.

Consistent with this interpretation, cotreatment of HQ-treated hCEnCs with 30 ng/mL FGF10 improved viability across HQ doses, reduced ROS at higher doses, and decreased apoptosis at moderate doses, thereby preserving viable cells and improving endothelial monolayer integrity. These findings align with prior studies showing that FGF10 accelerates wound healing, enhances mitochondrial function, and upregulates antiapoptotic proteins.31,69

Transcriptomically, FGF10 cotreatment increased cadherin/Wnt pathway signatures, suggesting induction of a junctional repair program at lower doses. Cadherin signaling is primarily mediated by N-cadherin, which complexes with β-catenin to maintain cell–cell adhesion and barrier integrity. β-Catenin is also a central effector in the canonical Wnt pathway, driving genes that support proliferation, phenotype maintenance, and regenerative responses.72,73 At 50 µM HQ, FGF10 enriched ECM organization and increased cadherin and Wnt signaling, consistent with FGFR2 and PI3K/Akt activation.31,74 In contrast, the weaker cadherin/Wnt pathway transcriptional response at higher HQ concentrations may reflect a shift toward alternative cytoprotective mechanisms, as FGF10 has been shown to activate concentration-dependent signaling cascades that can diverge substantially based on ligand dose.75 Above 50 µM HQ, FGF10-mediated cell survival may rely more heavily on antioxidant defense pathways, such as NQO1 upregulation, rather than junctional repair programs that predominate at lower stress levels.76

Despite increased N-cadherin transcripts with FGF10 cotreatment, N-cadherin protein decreased and reached significance at 50 µM HQ + FGF10, while β-catenin remained stable across HQ conditions with and without FGF10 (Figs. 4E, 4F). This RNA–protein discordance may reflect FGF10-induced transcriptional activation of junctional repair programs occurring simultaneously with accelerated N-cadherin turnover rather than accumulation of full-length junctional protein.77,78 FGF signaling has been shown to enhance matrix metalloproteinase–mediated N-cadherin cleavage, reducing detectable full-length protein despite elevated pathway signatures.42 Functionally, stable β-catenin supports preserved regenerative signaling capacity during selective junctional remodeling and may reflect redistribution from membrane-bound junctional pools to cytoplasmic or nuclear pools that sustain proliferative programs, rather than a full pathologic EndMT.43,79 Consistent with this, FGF10 has been reported to promote corneal endothelial wound repair while limiting EndMT-associated protein expression,80 contrasting with the more EndMT-prone effects described for FGF2.81

Hippo–Yap pathway heatmaps showed modest FGF10-associated upregulation of several components at baseline and 50 µM HQ that was progressively lost at higher HQ doses, a pattern that is biologically consistent with regulation of Hippo–Yap signaling by oxidative stress and growth factor input, although these transcript-level changes did not reach statistical significance. Under severe oxidative stress, multiple mechanisms converge to phosphorylate and degrade Yap, so growth factor input alone may be unable to restore Yap activity when Yap protein is being actively inactivated and removed.58,82 In this context, the lack of Yap protein change with FGF10 treatment supports a model in which FGF10 exerts its protective effects largely through Yap-independent mechanisms, such as enhanced antioxidant defense69 and support of cell survival,68 rather than through reactivation of Hippo–Yap driven proliferative programs.

As oxidative burden increased, transcriptomic rescue waned, and FGF10’s protective effect was dominated by viability rescue and ROS reduction. This may suggest a limited capacity of FGF10 to initiate pathway-level repair at high doses and a cytoprotective mode that is partially nontranscriptional, potentially involving mitochondrial stabilization, preservation of junctional proteins, inhibition of apoptosis, and enhanced antioxidant defense.31,69 These patterns highlight the importance of therapeutic timing and dosage in the development of FGF10-based interventions and underscore the need for complementary or combinatorial approaches in severe oxidative injury.

While our study supports the utility of HQ-induced oxidative stress modeling and the protective role of FGF10, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, HQ models a single acute oxidant and does not capture mixed agents or chronic, low-level stress in vivo. Future work should incorporate chronic and mixed-oxidant exposures with varied schedules to better approximate disease biology and examine delayed or repeated FGF10 delivery to evaluate its efficacy under ongoing oxidative stress.44,48,83 Second, off-target effects of FGF10 were not systematically examined, and studies should define specificity, optimize dose and timing, and assess tolerability. Third, future work should incorporate FGFR inhibition and downstream signaling readouts to define the canonical or noncanonical pathways through which FGF10 acts. Fourth, we did not test whether exogenous FGF10 rescues HQ-induced corneal endothelial injury in vivo, and future mouse studies will be important to determine whether FGF10 delivery can protect or restore endothelial function in the intact eye. Finally, cross-species analysis supported conservation of core Nrf2 responses, but differences in antioxidant thresholds and secondary genes may effect translation.

Conclusions

This study provides a disease-relevant, cross-species framework to study smoking-induced oxidative injury and cytoprotection in the corneal endothelium. Our reproducible HQ model captures core features of endothelial stress biology, enables interrogation of injury mechanisms and therapeutic responses, and identifies FGF10 as a promising therapeutic candidate. Together, these results provide a path toward pharmacologically preserving endothelial function and lessen the dependence on donor corneas.
