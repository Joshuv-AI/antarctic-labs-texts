Genital leech infestation presenting with vaginal bleeding in a 55-year-old Ethiopian woman: Case report and literature review
Authors: Ahmed Siraj Mohammed, Kelil Hussein Jibicho, Kedir Negesso Tukeni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Genital leech infestation is a rare but clinically significant condition encountered primarily in tropical and subtropical rural communities where untreated surface water is commonly used for drinking, bathing, or personal hygiene [1], [2], [3]. Leeches are segmented hermaphroditic annelids belonging to the phylum Annelida and class Hirudinea, comprising more than 700 described species worldwide [4], [5]. Although most species are free-living predators, several freshwater species are obligate hematophagous parasites of vertebrates. Among these, Limnatis nilotica (family Praobdellidae) is considered the species most frequently associated with human mucosal infestations in Africa, the Middle East, and parts of the Mediterranean region, whereas Tyrannobdella rex has been described as another mucosal leech capable of infesting humans [6], [7], [8]. Unlike medicinal leeches that attach externally to the skin, L. nilotica preferentially attaches to mucosal surfaces of the nasopharynx, larynx, gastrointestinal tract, urinary tract, and female genital tract after entering body orifices during exposure to contaminated freshwater [5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10].

The life cycle of freshwater leeches consists of egg, juvenile, and adult stages. Eggs are deposited in protective cocoons in moist aquatic environments, from which juvenile leeches emerge and mature directly without an intermediate host [5], [6]. Human infestation occurs accidentally when juvenile or adult leeches enter natural body openings during bathing, swimming, drinking, or washing with untreated water [5], [6], [7]. Once attached to the mucosa, the parasite feeds intermittently on blood while secreting a complex mixture of bioactive substances, including the potent anticoagulant hirudin, vasodilators, hyaluronidase, and local anesthetic compounds. These secretions facilitate painless attachment, inhibit local coagulation, prolong feeding, and cause persistent bleeding even after the parasite detaches, potentially resulting in significant blood loss, anemia, or hypovolemic shock [5], [7], [9], [10], [11], [12].

Case presentation

A 55-year-old postmenopausal Ethiopian woman presented with painless, moderate vaginal bleeding for one week and symptomatic anemia. No history or signs of trauma or systemic illness were found. Upon evaluation, she was a well-looking woman with stable vital signs apart from an increased pulse rate, 116 bpm, and pale conjunctivae. Investigations revealed moderate anemia with hemoglobin of 8.9 g/dl, while all other investigations were non-revealing (Table 1).

Management and follow-up

After stabilizing the patient's hemodynamic status, she was placed in the dorsal lithotomy position. Approximately 100 ml of Normal Saline was instilled vaginally at the site of leech attachment, allowing the leech to be removed intact with ring forceps shortly after. The attachment site was inspected with no active bleeding. The patient was discharged in stable condition with a prescription for ferrous sulphate 325 mg three times a day and hygiene advice. Subsequent visits showed no further bleeding episodes, and she is healthy.

Discussions

Leech infestation is an uncommon but important parasitic condition that remains endemic in tropical and subtropical regions where untreated freshwater is frequently used for drinking, bathing, or personal hygiene [1], [2], [3]. Among the more than 700 described leech species, Limnatis nilotica (family Praobdellidae) is the species most commonly implicated in human mucosal infestations in Africa and the Middle East because of its predilection for attaching to moist mucosal surfaces rather than intact skin [5], [6], [7]. Human infestation occurs accidentally when juvenile or adult leeches enter natural body orifices during exposure to contaminated water. Once attached, they feed on blood while secreting biologically active substances including hirudin, calin, histamine-like vasodilators, hyaluronidase, and local anesthetic compounds, allowing painless attachment and prolonged bleeding that may persist for hours after detachment [5], [6], [7], [9], [10], [11], [12].

Although leeches have been reported in the nasopharynx, larynx, gastrointestinal tract, rectum, urinary tract, and female genital tract, vaginal infestation remains exceptionally rare [1], [2], [3], [9], [10], [11], [12]. The majority of reported genital cases originate from rural communities in Ethiopia, Eritrea, Rwanda, Iran, and Malaysia, where exposure to untreated streams or ponds is common [1], [2], [3], [5], [6], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20]. Our patient represents another rare case of postmenopausal vaginal bleeding secondary to genital leech infestation and further highlights that this condition should remain an important differential diagnosis in women residing in or returning from endemic rural settings.

The clinical presentation depends on the site of attachment and duration of infestation. Persistent painless vaginal bleeding is the hallmark of genital infestation and results primarily from the anticoagulant activity of hirudin and other salivary proteins rather than direct tissue injury. Consequently, patients may develop symptomatic anemia or, in severe cases, hypovolemic shock despite relatively small mucosal lesions [1], [2], [3], [10], [15], [19], [20]. Published cases consistently demonstrate that genital leech infestation affects patients across a wide age spectrum, from young children to elderly postmenopausal women, although most cases originate from rural areas where untreated freshwater is routinely used. As summarized in Table 2, nearly all patients presented with painless vaginal bleeding, while the severity of anemia varied according to the duration of infestation and blood loss. Mechanical extraction following direct visualization was uniformly successful, and the overall prognosis was excellent when diagnosis and treatment were not delayed.

In postmenopausal women, such bleeding understandably raises immediate concern for gynecologic malignancy. Therefore, careful history taking—including recent exposure to untreated freshwater—and meticulous genital examination are essential to establish the diagnosis while avoiding unnecessary invasive investigations.

Published reports consistently demonstrate that direct visualization remains the cornerstone of diagnosis [1], [2], [3], [18], [19], [20]. Speculum examination, when feasible, usually identifies the live parasite attached to the vaginal mucosa. Laboratory investigations are valuable for assessing the severity of anemia and excluding coagulopathy or systemic disease, whereas pelvic ultrasonography assists in excluding structural gynecologic pathology, particularly in postmenopausal patients. Our patient's diagnostic evaluation followed this approach, enabling prompt identification of the leech and exclusion of alternative causes of vaginal bleeding.

Treatment consists of complete removal of the leech while minimizing trauma to the attachment site. Gentle irrigation with normal saline, followed by extraction using non-toothed forceps, remains an effective and safe technique, as demonstrated in our patient. Excessive traction should be avoided because rupture of the parasite may leave the oral sucker attached, potentially prolonging bleeding or provoking local inflammation. Adjunctive measures such as saline, topical anesthetics, or other detachment agents may facilitate removal when direct extraction is difficult, particularly in children or when preservation of hymenal integrity is important [18], [20]. Supportive management, including iron replacement, intravenous fluids, or blood transfusion, should be individualized according to the severity of blood loss.

An additional consideration after leech removal is the potential risk of secondary bacterial infection. Freshwater leeches harbor symbiotic bacteria within their digestive tract, predominantly Aeromonas species, which facilitate digestion of ingested blood but may occasionally be transmitted to the host during feeding or removal [5], [21]. Although most evidence derives from medicinal leech therapy in reconstructive surgery, Aeromonas hydrophila has been implicated in cellulitis, wound infection, bacteremia, and, rarely, sepsis following leech exposure. Consequently, the attachment site should be examined carefully during follow-up for evidence of local or systemic infection. Routine antibiotic prophylaxis after uncomplicated accidental leech removal is not universally recommended. However, antibiotics with activity against Aeromonas species—such as fluoroquinolones or third-generation cephalosporins, selected according to local antimicrobial susceptibility patterns—should be considered in patients with significant mucosal injury, persistent bleeding, immunocompromising conditions, or clinical features suggestive of secondary bacterial infection [5], [21].

Although persistent bleeding remains the most frequently reported complication of mucosal leech infestation, clinicians should recognize several additional potentially serious consequences. Secondary bacterial infection, particularly due to Aeromonas species carried as endosymbionts within the leech digestive tract, may result in localized wound infection, cellulitis, bacteremia, or, rarely, sepsis if not promptly recognized [5], [21]. Allergic or hypersensitivity reactions have also been described, ranging from localized inflammation and pruritus to rare systemic manifestations, including urticaria and anaphylaxis, likely resulting from exposure to salivary proteins introduced during feeding [5], [21]. Furthermore, leech attachment within the nasopharynx, larynx, or tracheobronchial tree may present with epistaxis, hemoptysis, dysphagia, hoarseness, stridor, or acute airway obstruction requiring urgent endoscopic removal to prevent respiratory compromise [1], [2], [3], [10], [15], [19], [20]. Similar infestations involving the gastrointestinal or urinary tract may cause hematemesis, melena, hematuria, or urinary retention depending on the site of attachment [5], [6], [7]. Consequently, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for leech infestation whenever unexplained bleeding follows recent exposure to untreated freshwater, regardless of the anatomical site involved.

Treatment of mucosal leech infestation is based exclusively on prompt mechanical removal of the parasite and appropriate supportive care. Unlike helminthic or protozoal infections, there is no specific pharmacologic antiparasitic therapy for leech infestation because leeches are annelid ectoparasites and are not susceptible to commonly used antiparasitic agents such as albendazole, mebendazole, ivermectin, or praziquantel [5], [21]. Direct visualization followed by careful extraction using non-toothed forceps remains the treatment of choice. Gentle irrigation with normal saline, topical anesthetics, or other detachment techniques may facilitate removal while minimizing trauma, particularly when the parasite is firmly attached or when preservation of surrounding mucosa is important [18], [20]. Excessive traction should be avoided because rupture of the leech may leave the oral sucker attached, resulting in continued bleeding or local inflammation. Following successful removal, supportive management should be individualized according to the severity of clinical presentation and may include local pressure to achieve hemostasis, iron supplementation, intravenous fluid resuscitation, blood transfusion for significant anemia or hemodynamic instability, and clinical follow-up to detect persistent bleeding or secondary bacterial infection, particularly due to Aeromonas species [5], [21]. In most reported cases, including the present patient, complete recovery is achieved following successful removal of the parasite and appropriate supportive care without the need for antiparasitic medications.

This case underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for genital leech infestation in patients presenting with unexplained vaginal bleeding in endemic settings. Early recognition, careful removal of the parasite, correction of anemia, appropriate follow-up, and counseling on avoidance of untreated freshwater exposure usually result in complete recovery while preventing unnecessary investigations and potentially life-threatening complications.

Conclusion

Genital leech infestation, prevalent in tropical and subtropical rural areas reliant on untreated water, poses significant health risks. This case report of a 55-year-old Ethiopian woman highlights the consequences, including painless vaginal bleeding and symptomatic anemia caused by a leech. After successful removal, she recovered, underlining the dangers of severe anemia and hypovolemic shock in resource-limited settings. The report urges public health initiatives for safe water access and awareness about untreated surface water risks, emphasizing the link between rural living, untreated water, and leech infestations, especially among vulnerable populations like women and children, and the critical need for clinical recognition for proper diagnosis and treatment.
