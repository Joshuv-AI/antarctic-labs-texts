Effectiveness and safety of tacrolimus in systemic lupus erythematosus with various clinical manifestations: a retrospective real-world study
Authors: Wei Bai, Hui Luo, Huaxiang Wu, Xinwang Duan, Jian Xu, Cheng Zhao, Qinghua Zou, Xiaofei Shi, Yuehong Huo, Zhen Chen, Jiuliang Zhao, Xinping Tian, Qian Wang, Xiaomei Leng, Yanhong Wang, Yan Zhao, Mengtao Li, Xiaofeng Zeng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study evaluated the real-world effectiveness and safety of tacrolimus as a steroid-sparing maintenance therapy alone without additional biologics or immunosuppressive medications in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), particularly in mild, non–new-onset cases, with a focus on glucocorticoid-sparing effects and multi-organ remission.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted using data from 679 SLE patients in the Chinese SLE Treatment and Research Group (CSTAR) cohort. Patients received tacrolimus for ≥ 4 weeks alongside glucocorticoids and/or hydroxychloroquine were included. Patients on other immunosuppressants or biologics at baseline were excluded. The primary endpoint was the changes in SLE Disease Activity Index 2000 (SLEDAI-2 K). Secondary endpoints included SLE Responder Index-4 (SRI-4) response at weeks 4, 24, and 48 (≥ 4-point SLEDAI-2 K reduction with no new major flare), Physician Global Assessment (PGA) scores, remission of organ-specific disease manifestations, reduction in daily glucocorticoid dose, and safety.ResultsAt baseline, 84.39% (573/679) of patients had mild disease (SLEDAI-2 K ≤ 6), 11.49% (78/679) moderate, and 4.12% (28/679) severe. Statistically significant reductions in SLEDAI-2 K (baseline: 3.3 ± 4.1; week 48: 1.7 ± 2.2, p < 0.001) and PGA scores (p < 0.001) were observed. Glucocorticoid doses significantly decreased from 20.0 ± 18.3 mg/day (baseline, n = 590) to 10.0 ± 9.9 mg/day (week 48, n = 233; p < 0.001). SRI-4 response rates were 59.06% (75/127), 49.15% (58/118), and 48.75% (39/80) at weeks 4, 24, and 48, respectively. Organ remission rates ≥ 50% were achieved for mucocutaneous (78.95% [45/57]), serositis (88.89% [8/9]), arthritis (82.61% [19/23]), and leukopenia (71.87% [23/32]). Adverse events occurred in 7.36% (50/679) of patients, with only 0.74% (5/679) deemed treatment-related; serious AEs were rare (n = 3, none treatment-related).ConclusionTacrolimus demonstrated effectiveness in reducing disease activity, enabling significant glucocorticoid tapering, and inducing multi-organ remission in extrarenal manifestations. Its favorable safety profile supports its use as a promising sparing maintenance therapy alone, potentially obviating the need for additional biologic or immunosuppressive agents in SLE.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13075-025-03661-1.Keywords: Systemic lupus erythematosus, Tacrolimus, Glucocorticoid-sparing, Real-world study

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic autoimmune disorder affecting various organs such as the mucocutaneous, musculoskeletal, renal, neuropsychiatric, pulmonary, cardiac, gastrointestinal, and hematologic systems [1, 2]. The standard treatments for SLE includes glucocorticoids and antimalarials, often complemented by immunosuppressants (e.g., cyclophosphamide [CYC], methotrexate [MTX], azathioprine [AZA], cyclosporine [CsA], and mycophenolate mofetil [MMF]) and biologics (e.g., belimumab and telitacicept) [3, 4]. In addition, the Treat-to-Target (T2T) strategy in SLE, which has surfaced in recent years, underscores the criticality of swiftly attaining remission and curbing disease activity [5]. This approach is instrumental in substantially mitigating organ damage, diminishing the likelihood of recurrence, and enhancing the long-term quality of life [5]. These advancements have led to the 5-year and 10-year survival rates elevated to 94% and 89% respectively [6]. However, the maintenance therapy remains a necessity for SLE patients. The side effects of long-term exposure to glucocorticoids are a major concern in the field, since this can cause Cushingoid effects, disrupt the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, and independently lead to irreversible organ damage in lupus [7, 8]. Therefore, strategies to minimize steroid use while maintaining disease control are crucial.

Tacrolimus is a calcineurin inhibitor that directly suppressing T-cell activation, indirectly inhibiting B-cell activity through disruption of T-helper signaling, and modulating production of various cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6) pivotal to SLE disease pathogenesis [9]. Its notable efficacy in LN, for reducing relapse rates and managing refractory cases [10], is widely recognized [11–18] and endorsed by multiple professional bodies including the European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) [3], Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) [19], and the Asia-Pacific League of Associations for Rheumatology (APLAR) [20]. In addition, promising efficacy has also been observed in SLE patients with manifestations including refractory thrombocytopenia and severe cutaneous vasculitis [21–23].

However, the role of tacrolimus as steroid-sparing maintenance therapy alone without additional biologics or immunosuppressive medications in clinically mild, established (non–new-onset) systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) remains insufficiently characterized, and clarifying its value as a steroid-sparing agent in this patient subset is critical for refining long-term management strategies consistent with treat-to-target principles. Systematic reviews conclude that most high-quality data for TAC address nephritis rather than global disease control [24]. Outside the kidney, evidence is confined to small, heterogeneous real-world cohorts. A prospective single-center study (n = 21) reported improvements across diverse manifestations but sample size is small [25]. Adjunct low-dose tacrolimus has been shown to effectively manage minor lupus flares (e.g., mild arthritis, cutaneous flares, or hematological exacerbations) without necessitating glucocorticoid escalation [26]. Notably, one retrospective study of patients with mild-to-moderate SLE flares found that adding tacrolimus achieved clinical improvement comparable to an increase in prednisone, thereby maintaining disease control while keeping steroid doses low [27]. A Chinese CSTAR analysis highlighted steroid-sparing with 40% of patients achieving remission/LLDAS at 6 months [23]. Collectively, these data underscore a clear knowledge gap: evidence for tacrolimus as maintenance therapy in mild, longstanding SLE—aligned with treat-to-target goals of remission or LLDAS—has yet to be generated. Given the cumulative risks associated with prolonged glucocorticoid therapy, even at low to moderate doses, there is a compelling rationale to evaluate alternative immunomodulatory therapies that can maintain disease control while minimizing steroid burden.

To address this gap, we conducted a large-scale retrospective study using data from the Chinese SLE Treatment and Research Group (CSTAR) cohort, which includes 64 registration sites across China, to assess the real-world effectiveness and safety of tacrolimus across a spectrum of SLE disease severity, ranging from mild to severe cases. Demonstrating such an effect would support treat-to-target goals in SLE management—achieving durable low disease activity with minimal or no glucocorticoid use—and could broaden the scope of safe, long-term therapeutic options for SLE patients.

Methods

Study design and patients

This was a retrospective study based on the CSTAR cohort. Inclusion criteria were (a) age ≥ 18 years; (b) diagnosed with SLE based on the 1997, 2012, or 2019 diagnostic criteria; (c) received a tacrolimus regimen for ≥ 4 weeks post-baseline, with data from at least one follow-up visit. Patients were permitted to use glucocorticoids and hydroxychloroquine, but not biologics or other immunosuppressives agents. Exclusion criteria were: (a) Had received the concomitant medication at baseline (e.g., taking other traditional immunosuppressive agents and disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs [DMARDs] such as CYC, leflunomide, MTX, AZA, MMF, CsA, and Tripterygium glycosides, unless these were discontinued at least 4 weeks prior to baseline, with additional cholestyramine washout for patients treated with leflunomide; or targeted biologics including rituximab, belimumab, and telitacicept; or receiving intravenous immunoglobulin or plasmapheresis, which were not allowed within 6 months prior to baseline); (b) severe liver function abnormalities, gastrointestinal ulcers, cardiovascular diseases, malignant tumors, severe infections, etc. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Peking Union Medical College Hospital (Nos. S-478 and JS-2038).

Patient and public involvement

No patient or public involvement was incorporated in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans of this research.

Endpoints and assessments

The primary endpoints were SLEDAI-2 K scores at the respective time points (baseline, week 4, week 24, and week 48). Key secondary endpoints included SRI-4 response rate at weeks 4, 24, and 48, changes from baseline in PGA, remission of organ involvement, glucocorticoid daily dose required for controlling the disease and safety.

SRI-4 response was defined based on meeting all of the following criteria: a ≥ 4-point reduction from baseline in SLEDAI-2 K score, no new disease activity measured by a new British Isles Lupus Assessment Group (BILAG) grade A or >1 BILAG grade B score, and no worsening in the PGA score (i.e., an increase of < 0.3 points from baseline) [28]. Organ involvement was determined by evaluating affected organ systems using the baseline SLEDAI score, with remission of organ involvement achieved when subsequent SLEDAI evaluations showed disappearance of corresponding items from the SLEDAI scoring system (i.e., signifying that active disease was no longer present in that organ). Complete remission (CR) of thrombocytopenia was defined as a platelet count of ≥ 100 × 109/L, while partial remission (PR) was defined as a platelet count of ≥ 30 × 109/L with at least a two-fold increase from baseline. Safety was evaluated by calculating the incidence of adverse events (AEs).

Data were collected at baseline and from follow-up at weeks 4, 24, and 48 after initiation of tacrolimus therapy, including demographics, clinical manifestations, disease activity, and laboratory test results. Disease activity was assessed according to the SLEDAI-2 K [29] and PGA. Immunologic indices included anti-dsDNA antibodies and low complement, defined as complement component 3 (C3) < 0.73 g/L or complement component 4 (C4) < 0.1 g/L. Additionally, subgroup analysis was performed according to disease severity at baseline (mild, SLEDAI-2 K ≤ 6; moderate, SLEDAI-2 K 7–12; or severe, SLEDAI-2 K >12).

Patients ineligible for follow-up due to dropout, incomplete data registration, or use of alternative anti-SLE treatments were excluded from the analyses. If patients concurrently began using any of the medications mentioned in the exclusion criteria, that visit was considered as the last follow-up. The number of patients at each follow-up time point was 283, 369, and 233 at weeks 4, 24, and 48, respectively (Supplementary Table 1).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SAS 9.4 software (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Clinical characteristics of patients are summarized using mean with standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables, and as count (percentages) for categorical variables. The Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank test was used to evaluate changes in SLEDAI-2 K, PGA, and glucocorticoid dosage compared to baseline. McNemar’s test was used to assess changes over time in the SRI-4 response rate, organ involvement, and immune indices. A two-tailed test with p < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. Missing values were not imputed, and variables with a missing data rate exceeding 50% were excluded from subsequent statistical analyses.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 3,433 patients were screened for eligibility, and 679 patients who received tacrolimus treatment between March 2014 and August 2024 from 64 hospitals across China were finally included in the study (Fig. 1).

The majority (94.55%) were female, and the average age was 34.1 years (Table 1). The majority (n = 573, 84.39%) of subjects had mild disease activity, with 11.49% (n = 78) experiencing moderate and 4.12% (n = 28) experiencing severe disease activity at baseline. The mean scores for SLEDAI-2 K and PGA at baseline were 3.3 and 1.01, respectively. Clinical manifestations at baseline included LN (19.44%), mucocutaneous involvement (13.84%), thrombocytopenia (12.52%), leukopenia (9.57%), and arthritis (6.19%). With regard to serological parameters, 36.08% of patients exhibited hypocomplementemia and 76.73% were positive for anti-dsDNA antibodies. 68.78% of patients were on glucocorticoid therapy (with an average daily dose of 20.0 ± 18.3 mg) and 58.91% were being administered hydroxychloroquine (Table 1). This was an established disease cohort with average disease duration was 69.3 months. Of note, the mild group had a longer duration of SLE compared with the moderate and severe groups (72.0 ± 65.2 vs. 55.0 ± 62.2 vs. 52.1 ± 59.6 months; Supplementary Table 1).

Effectiveness results

SLEDAI-2 K, PGA scores, dosage of glucocorticoid and immunologic indices

At week 4, the average SLEDAI-2 K score showed a substantial decrease from 4.1 ± 4.7 at baseline to 2.2 ± 2.7 (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2), indicating a rapid reduction in disease activity, while the average PGA score also improved from 1.16 ± 0.57 to 0.95 ± 0.46 (p < 0.001), reflecting an early improvement in physician-assessed disease severity. By week 24, the average SLEDAI-2 K score further declined to 1.7 ± 2.2 (p < 0.001). Similarly, the average PGA score also showed continued improvement, decreasing to 0.76 ± 0.48 (p < 0.001). The trend persisted at week 48, a decrease on both average SLEDAI-2 K score and the average PGA score were observed (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2). Improvements were observed as early as week 4 and continued through weeks 24 and 48, suggesting a sustained clinical benefit. Subgroup analysis indicated that these improvements were observed across all severity levels, including mild (Fig. 2), moderate (Supplementary Fig. 1), and severe disease (Supplementary Fig. 2).

With tacrolimus treatment, the dosage of glucocorticoid (prednisone) required to control the disease showed a statistically significant reduction at week 4 (21.1 ± 15.5 mg/day vs. 24.8 ± 20.1 mg/day at baseline, p < 0.001), week 24 (10.6 ± 9.8 mg/day vs. 17.3 ± 16.4 mg/day, p < 0.001), and week 48 (10.0 ± 9.9 mg/day vs. 18.4 ± 17.6 mg/day, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2). Subgroup analyses revealed a consistent steroid-sparing effect across all disease activity levels (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Figs. 1–2). In patients with mild SLE, prednisone tapering followed a similar trajectory, from 21.3 ± 18.4 mg at baseline to 18.5 ± 14.4 mg at week 4, 11.9 ± 7.3 mg at week 24, and 9.5 ± 8.9 mg at week 48 (all p < 0.001). Comparable reductions were observed in patients with moderate and severe disease activity (Supplementary Figs. 1–2). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that tacrolimus as steroid-sparing maintenance therapy alone without additional biologics or immunosuppressive medications facilitates a rapid (within 4 weeks) and sustained (up to 48 weeks) reduction—approximately halving—of glucocorticoid exposure in the majority of patients treated.

The proportion of patients seropositive for anti-dsDNA antibodies exhibited only minor fluctuations at weeks 4 and 48, with no statistically significant changes (Fig. 2A and C). A slight increase in was detected at week 24 (Fig. 2B). Subgroup analysis indicated that no significant changes were observed over time for any group (Fig. 3 & Supplementary Figs. 1–2).

Improvements in hypocomplementemia were observed at all time points relative to baseline. The proportion of SLE patients with hypocomplementemia decreased to 33.22% at week 4 (p = 0.003), 24.93% at week 24 (p < 0.001) and 27.04% at week 48 (p = 0.091) (Fig. 3). Subgroup analysis indicated particular improvement in the moderate and severe disease subgroups (Supplementary Fig. 1&2).

The SRI-4 response rates for all patients were 59.06% (75/127) at week 4, 49.15% (58/118) at week 24, and 48.75% (39/80) at week 48 (Table 2). Patients with severe disease activity had SRI-4 response rates of 94.74% (18/19), 76.92% (10/13), and 100% (5/5) at each time point (Table 2).

Safety results

The incidence of AEs was low, with all-causality AEs reported in 50 (7.36%) patients and treatment-related AEs in five (0.74%) patients. The most frequently reported AEs were infection (14 patients, 2.06%), abnormal renal function (six patients, 0.88%), bone marrow suppression (four patients, 0.59%), abnormal hepatic function (three patients, 0.44%), and gastrointestinal reactions (three patients, 0.44%) (Table 3). Serious adverse events (SAEs) were reported in only three patients, consisting of two cases of infection and one case of rash (Supplementary Table 3). None of these SAEs were considered related to the treatment. Of the two infection-related serious adverse events, one was fatal, while the other required hospitalization, temporary discontinuation of tacrolimus, and later showed a favorable outcome. Another SAE involving rash also led to extended hospitalization and was managed with an increased tacrolimus dose, resulting in clinical improvement. 58% (29/50) of AEs had either resolved or improved, especially after dose reduction or if administration was suspended or discontinued. Regarding severity, the majority of AEs (30/50) were mild, 16 were moderate, two were severe, and the severity of the other two occurrences was unreported. In addition, a doubling of creatinine level from baseline occurred in one patient at each of 4 weeks and 24 weeks post-treatment, respectively.

Discussion

This study represents the first large-scale clinical investigation to evaluate the effectiveness and safety of tacrolimus as maintenance therapy in SLE, with a patient population predominantly comprising clinically mild, non–new-onset cases. Our findings demonstrated that tacrolimus treatment resulted in significant clinical improvements, evidenced by robust SRI-4 response rates and statistically significant reductions in both SLEDAI-2 K and PGA scores at weeks 4, 24, and 48. Concurrently, daily glucocorticoid dosages were effectively reduced. Additionally, in patients with moderate-to-severe disease, tacrolimus induced a remarkable remission in extrarenal organ manifestations by week 4 and this was maintained until week 48. Tacrolimus was generally well tolerated, with a low incidence of adverse events and infections.

In our study, with tacrolimus treatment, a statistically significant reduction in SLEDAI-2 K and PGA scores was observed, as well as a significant reduction in daily glucocorticoid dosages, at all follow-up time points. SRI-4 response rates of 59.06% at week 4, 49.15% at week 24, and 48.75% at week 48 were observed. These treatment responses were achieved with tacrolimus alone, in contrast to belimumab, which required additional therapies such as CYC or MMF for each patient. Together, these findings suggest that tacrolimus may be a highly effective and tolerable standalone therapy to enhance clinical outcomes in SLE patients. Our results are consistent with several network meta-analyses suggesting that, compared with MMF, AZA, and CYC, tacrolimus had the highest probability of being the best treatment for patients with LN, with a higher remission rate and a more favorable safety profile, based on benefit/risk assessment [10, 30–32] in patients with moderate-to-severe SLE.

Clinical significance is crucial when interpreting treatment effects, especially in patients with mild SLE. While a reduction in SLEDAI-2 K of ≥ 4 points is often considered meaningful, this threshold is inherently challenging in mild SLE with low baseline disease activity. The statistically significant reduction in the overall cohort’s SLEDAI-2 K score was largely driven by improvements in moderate/severe subgroups. However, patients with mild SLE achieved a substantial and sustained reduction in their daily glucocorticoid dose (from 21.3 to 9.5 mg/day). Reducing the daily steroid requirement from above 20 mg to below 10 mg is a major therapeutic achievement. This reduction can translate to a lower risk of long-term glucocorticoid-related organ damage, metabolic complications, and other adverse effects. Furthermore, the post-treatment SLEDAI-2 K scores in the mild group were very low, indicating that most patients achieved remission/LLDAS. Attaining this treat-to-target goal with concurrent steroid tapering is highly clinically relevant, as it reflects successful disease control while minimizing treatment toxicity. Therefore, tacrolimus provides clinical benefits for patients with mild, non-new onset SLE in the real-world setting, aligning with the principles of T2T strategies.

For patients with moderate to severe SLE, clinical benefits were observed in this study as evidenced by SRI-4 response rate. Notable, for patients with severe disease activity in our study, the SRI-4 response rate exceeded 75% throughout the observation period. It has been shown that SRI is more sensitive than the British Isles Lupus Assessment Group-based Composite Lupus Assessment (BICLA) in detecting clinically significant improvement, particularly in SLE patients with multiple organ involvement at baseline [33]. Moreover, SRI was used as the primary outcome measure in two successful phase III trials (BLISS-52 and BLISS-76) that led to regulatory approval of belimumab [34, 35]. In the BLISS trials, 10 mg/kg belimumab combined with standard therapy achieved the primary efficacy endpoint, showing a significantly higher SRI response rate at week 52 compared with placebo (43.2% vs. 33.5%, p = 0.017) [34, 35].

Despite the absence of a continuous trajectory for tacrolimus within the same cohort (i.e., from baseline through weeks 4, 24, and 48), our data revealed a marked early surge in SRI-4 response rate by week 4 with sustained responses at weeks 24 and 48, as evidenced by both the full-cohort and subgroup analyses. This pattern reflects a rapid onset of the therapeutic effect of tacrolimus and a stabilizing effect during the period from week 24 to week 48. While previous studies have suggested clinical efficacy of tacrolimus in LN [24, 25, 36] and hematologic manifestations of SLE [23, 26], few have reported its efficacy in terms of inducing remission across other SLE manifestations. To the best of our knowledge, therefore, the present results demonstrate significant remission in skin, mucocutaneous, and arthritis involvement in SLE patients for the first time. To our knowledge, this study is the first to demonstrate in a large cohort that tacrolimus can induce and sustain remission in diverse extrarenal organ systems (particularly skin, joints, and serosal tissues) in SLE. In sum, our findings substantially broaden the therapeutic scope of tacrolimus, showing that its benefits in SLE extend beyond renal disease to encompass a range of organ-specific manifestations.

Tacrolimus alone (i.e., without the need for additional biologics or immunosuppressive medications) potentially contributed to a more favorable safety profile. We observed a low incidence of AEs (7.36% for all-causality AEs and 1.3% for treatment-related AEs) and the majority were mild in severity. Only three SAEs were reported, none of which were considered definitely related to tacrolimus. This is consistent with the findings that Tacrolimus used as a single agent—i.e., without concomitant cytotoxic immunosuppressants or biologics—has repeatedly shown a low frequency of AEs in both renal and extrarenal SLE cohorts. In a Chinese CSTAR analysis that enrolled 110 patients with diverse manifestations, total AE incidence was 8.2% and only 1.8% were treatment-related, mirroring the 7.4% and 1.3% observed in the present study [23]. A five-year post-marketing surveillance programme in Japan (TRUST; >1,400 patient-years) confirmed that tacrolimus maintenance led to no new safety signals; the overall AE rate (13.6%) and serious infection rate (0.8 per 100 PY) were lower than historical series of MMF or cyclophosphamide [18]. Long-term follow-up (median 10 years) of Japanese LN patients on tacrolimus demonstrated a persisting favourable safety profile and stable estimated GFR, alleviating concerns about chronic calcineurin nephrotoxicity at the doses used in SLE [37]. Collectively, these data suggest that tacrolimus as steroid-sparing maintenance therapy alone without additional biologics or immunosuppressive medications for SLE delivers disease control with a safety margin at least comparable to standard-of-care immunosuppressants, while simplifying the regimen and facilitating rapid prednisone reduction.

TRAEs affecting symptoms such as fatigue have the influence on the quality of life in patients with SLE. While this real-world study did not specifically assess fatigue under tacrolimus treatment, previous clinical evidence suggests that tacrolimus is not commonly associated with significant fatigue [25, 38, 39]. Nevertheless, the lack of systematic assessment of patient-reported outcomes such as fatigue remains to be further addressed, as it may offer further insights into daily function and well-being. Future studies incorporating structured measures of fatigue and other patient-centered outcomes are warranted to complement these clinical findings.

This study has some notable limitations, however. First, our findings were based on follow-up data as far as 48 weeks post-treatment, and extending this period might benefit the integrity of the results. Second, the retrospective nature of the study resulted in incomplete longitudinal effectiveness data for individual patients on tacrolimus. Third, there was a proportion of patients without follow-up who were excluded from this study. This is common in large real-world registries like CSTAR and often due to administrative reasons. The baseline characteristics (Supplementary Table 4) indicated that patients who discontinued follow-up tended to have longer disease duration and higher baseline disease activity. Such differences may introduce attrition bias, although they do not necessarily imply a lack of efficacy or safety of tacrolimus. Further prospective studies that systematically capture the reasons for discontinuation will be needed. Fourth, owing to gaps in clinical laboratory data, quantitative comparisons were infeasible for standard SLE serological markers (i.e., anti-dsDNA antibodies, complement C3 and C4, and 24hUPro). Our analysis relied on the qualitative presence of anti-dsDNA antibodies and hypocomplementemia to infer therapeutic effectiveness for tacrolimus, allowing us to discern variations in the seropositivity rate but not the precise levels of anti-dsDNA antibodies, limiting the conclusions that may be drawn [23, 36].

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study indicated that Tacrolimus is promising for serving as an effective maintenance therapy, achieving multi-organ remission while simultaneously allowing significant steroid reduction.
