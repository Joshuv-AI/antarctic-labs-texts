Polymicrobial bloodstream infection caused by three carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales species in a neutropenic cancer patient: Clinical and genomic characterization
Authors: Ana Paula Cury, Karen Alessandra Rodrigues, Edy Ribeiro, Evangelina da M Araujo A de Araujo, Marcello Mihailenko Chaves Magri, Adriana Satie Gonçalves Kono Magri, Odeli Nicole Encinas Sejas, Edson Abdala, Amanda Yaeko Yamada, Daniel de Sena Miranda, Pedro Smith Pereira Ferraro, Karoline Rodrigues Campos, Marlon Benedito Nascimento Santos, Claudio Tavares Sacchi, Carlos Henrique Camargo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCarbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales (CPE) represent a major global health threat due to limited therapeutic options and high mortality. While isolates harboring a single carbapenemase are increasingly common, polymicrobial bloodstream infections involving multiple carbapenemase-producing species remain rare and pose substantial diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. We describe a case of polymicrobial bloodstream infection in a neutropenic patient with relapsed acute myeloid leukemia. Blood cultures yielded three distinct Enterobacterales species. Identification was performed by MALDI-TOF MS, antimicrobial susceptibility testing followed CLSI guidelines, and carbapenemase production was screened by immunochromatographic assay and PCR. Whole-genome sequencing was conducted for all isolates, with in silico multilocus sequence typing, resistome characterization, plasmid replicon analysis, and phylogenetic reconstruction. A literature review (2015–2025) was performed to contextualize this finding. Three carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales were simultaneously isolated from blood cultures: Citrobacter freundii producing NDM-1, Klebsiella michiganensis producing IMP-16, and Enterobacter hormaechei producing KPC-2. One isolate harbored mcr-9 gene. The patient developed septic shock but was successfully treated with a targeted combination therapy of ceftazidime-avibactam plus aztreonam, supplemented with amikacin, alongside catheter removal and supportive care. Follow-up blood cultures were negative, and no secondary transmission was detected. We report a case of a polymicrobial bloodstream infection caused by three different carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales species, each expressing a distinct carbapenemase. This case underscores the clinical value of rapid molecular diagnostics, highlights the need for strengthened surveillance and tailored antimicrobial strategies in high-risk patients facing extreme antimicrobial resistance scenarios.Keywords: Enterobacterales, Carbapenemase, Bloodstream Infection, Whole Genome Sequencing, KPC, NDM, IMP

Introduction

Carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales (CPE) represent one of the most critical threats to global public health, being associated with high morbidity and mortality rates, prolonged hospital stays, and severely limited therapeutic options [1]. These enzymes hydrolyze almost all β-lactams, including carbapenems, often regarded as last-line agentes, and can disseminate across bacterial species via mobile genetic elements, particularly plasmids [2].

The most clinically relevant carbapenemases belong to three main moleculares classes: class A (e.g., KPC), class B metallo-β-lactamases (e.g., NDM, VIM, IMP), and class D oxacillinases (e.g., OXA−48-like). While the isolation of Enterobacterales producing different carbapenemases is increasingly common in hospital settings, the simultaneous occurrence of multiple carbapenemase-producing species in the same patient remains rare and carries significant clinical and epidemiological implications [3], [4].

Such polymicrobial infections pose major challenges for clinical management, as they involve pathogens with broad and heterogeneous resistance profiles, limiting therapeutic options and increasing the risk of horizontal transfer of resistance genes among species. Moreover, laboratory detection can be complex when multiple resistance mechanisms coexist in the same clinical specimen.

In this report, we describe the first documented case of a polymicrobial bloodstream infection in an immunocompromised patient involving three distinct Enterobacterales species; Citrobacter freundii, Klebsiella michiganensis, and Enterobacter hormaechei, each producing a different carbapenemase (NDM−1, IMP−16, and KPC−2, respectively). To our knowledge, this is the first clinical report of the concomitant isolation of these three carbapenemase-producing species from a single blood culture.

We present the patient’s clinical course, microbiological and genomic findings of the isolates, and discuss the clinical and epidemiological significance of this event in the context of the recent literature (2015–2025), highlighting the risks associated with the simultaneous circulation of multiple resistance determinants within a single host.

CASE

A 56-year-old male from Brazil with relapsed acute myeloid leukemia was admitted for febrile neutropenia during chemotherapy. He had a long-term central venous catheter and multiple prior hospitalizations. On hospital day 7, he developed septic shock. Blood cultures were collected using the BD BACTEC™ system, and empiric meropenem plus vancomycin was initiated.

Two blood culture sets became positive within 18 h, revealing polymicrobial growth of three Gram-negative species identified by MALDI-TOF MS: Citrobacter freundii, Klebsiella michiganensis (K. oxytoca complex), and Enterobacter hormaechei (E. cloacae complex). All were recovered simultaneously from different bottles, confirming concurrent polymicrobial bacteremia. Isolates were designated C. freundii−380A, K. michiganensis-380C, and E. hormaechei−380B.

Initial AST (Vitek 2 XL, CLSI 2025) showed all isolates were carbapenem non-susceptible, prompting addition of colistin. C. freundii and K. michiganensis were also resistant to cephalosporins, piperacillin-tazobactam, aztreonam, fluoroquinolones, and aminoglycosides. E. hormaechei had a similar profile but was colistin-resistant (MIC >4 µg/mL), while the others remained susceptible (MIC 0.5 µg/mL). Only amikacin was active against E. hormaechei (MIC 4 µg/mL). NG-Test CARBA 5™ detected NDM, IMP, and KPC in C. freundii, K. michiganensis, and E. hormaechei, respectively.

Whole-genome sequencing was performed on an Illumina NextSeq platform. Assemblies were generated using CLC Workbench and annotated with Prokka. C. freundii (ST1303) harbored blaNDM−1 and multiple resistance genes including blaOXA−1, blaTEM−1, blaCMY−150, dfrA14, sul1/2, tet(A), and qnrB38. K. michiganensis (ST645) carried blaIMP−16, blaCTX-M−15, blaOXA−1, blaOXY−5, and additional resistance genes including aac(3)-IIa, qnrE1, and mph(A), with absence of mcr−9. E. hormaechei (ST113) carried blaKPC−2, blaACT−15, and mcr−9, consistent with colistin resistance, along with qnrB2, fosA, and aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes. No hypervirulence genes were detected (Table 1).

Despite initial therapy, the patient deteriorated after 48 h. On hospital day 9, treatment was switched to ceftazidime-avibactam plus aztreonam, targeting both serine and metallo-β-lactamases. Amikacin was added based on susceptibility. The patient became afebrile within 72 h, and blood cultures were negative by day 11. Therapy was continued for 14 days, with neutrophil recovery by day 15.

The patient was discharged after clinical stabilization. The central venous catheter was removed, with tip culture yielding C. freundii and E. hormaechei, suggesting it as the infection source. No secondary transmission occurred. Although the patient later died from leukemia-related complications, the polymicrobial carbapenem-resistant bacteremia was successfully treated.

This case highlights successful management of an unprecedented polymicrobial bloodstream infection caused by three distinct carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales, enabled by rapid diagnostics and targeted combination therapy.

Discussion

This case report describes an unprecedented polymicrobial bloodstream infection by three different carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales in a single patient. The patient’s status as a hematological cancer patient with prolonged neutropenia and prior healthcare exposures likely created a “perfect storm” for acquiring multiple resistant organisms. Each isolate in our case was genetically unrelated yet highly resistant, reflecting probable independent acquisitions from the hospital environment or endogenous colonization. The concurrent isolation of C. freundii (NDM−1), K. michiganensis (IMP−16), and E. hormaechei (KPC−2) emphasizes the diverse arsenal of carbapenemases that can co-circulate in a healthcare setting. To our knowledge, no prior clinical report has documented three distinct CPE species causing a simultaneous infection in one patient, making this an extreme illustration of the challenges posed by antimicrobial resistance.

Our experience highlights several important clinical and public health implications for this case. (i) Diagnostic vigilance: Routine methods identified the organisms, but detecting their carbapenemase genes required molecular and genomic tools. In polymicrobial CRE infections, there is a risk of missing uncommon enzymes if only phenotypic tests are used. For example, the K. michiganensis in our case produced IMP−16, a less common metallo-β-lactamase that might not be covered by some commercial PCR panels focused on NDM/VIM. Comprehensive resistance gene profiling by WGS proved invaluable, it revealed the exact mechanisms and guided the choice of combination therapy. Rapid detection using immunochromatographic testing or use of multiplex PCR assays (covering KPC, NDM, OXA−48, VIM, IMP) should be considered early when facing unusual resistance patterns [5]. Early identification allows timely optimization of treatment and infection control. (ii) Therapeutic strategy: Treating infections involving multiple carbapenemase producers is extraordinarily difficult. Monotherapy with even the newest β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor agents may fail if not all enzymes are inhibited. In our case, ceftazidime-avibactam alone would cover KPC−2 but not NDM−1 or IMP−16; conversely, newer agents like cefiderocol might cover NDM/IMP but not KPC adequately. We employed a double-carbapenemase inhibition strategy (ceftazidime-avibactam targets KPC, while aztreonam bypasses MBLs) based on literature support and in vitro studies [6]. Resistance to ceftazidime-avibactam-aztrenam remains low, as only 0–1.8% of non-selected Enterobacterales isolates were resistant to this antimicrobial option [7]. This combination has been reported as successful in treating infections due to organisms co-producing KPC and NDM or other MBLs, including in cases of dual-carbapenemase K. pneumoniae [6]. Our patient’s favorable response reinforces the concept that customized combination therapy is essential for such complex resistance profiles. Addition of a third agent (amikacin) provided coverage redundancy and was justified given the high mortality risk. Still, outcomes depend on more than antibiotics, source control (catheter removal) and immune recovery (neutrophil engraftment) were likely pivotal in this patient’s clearance of infection. (iii) Infection control: This case underscores the need for rigorous infection control, especially in high-risk units. Once the lab identified CPE in this patient’s blood, our hospital initiated contact precautions, dedicated equipment, and screening of contacts. Fortunately, no secondary cases occurred, but the incident prompted a review of our antimicrobial stewardship and infection control policies. The fact that three different carbapenemase-producers infected one patient suggests a high burden of environmental or colonizing CPE. It is possible the patient was colonized in the gut with these organisms (perhaps selected by prior antibiotic pressure), which then translocated into the bloodstream during neutropenia. This aligns with findings from colonization studies, e.g. a study in an OXA−48 endemic hospital found 15% of patients colonized with CPE had more than one carbapenemase type present (sometimes detected serially or concurrently) [8]. Routine rectal screening on admission might have identified our patient’s carriers early; however, given the rarity of triple-colonization, this case may represent an outlier scenario. Nonetheless, it serves as a warning that infection control must prepare for polymicrobial resistant threats, not just single organism outbreaks. (iv) Genomic insights: The genomic data from our isolates offer a window into how such a co-infection might develop. The C. freundii and K. michiganensis each carried multiple plasmid-borne resistance genes in addition to their carbapenemase, indicating they likely came from environments (or donors) rife with multi-drug resistance. The E. hormaechei had the mcr−9 colistin resistance gene, which is concerning as colistin is often a last-resort drug for CRE. Plasmids carrying mcr−9 (often along with KPC, as reported in some Enterobacter spp.) could spread silently. The combination of blaKPC−2 + mcr−9 in ST113 E. hormaechei in our hospital is reminiscent of strains reported in the United States and China, where IncHI2 plasmids have been noted to carry mcr−9 gene [9]. While we did not fully characterize plasmids here, the parallel is worrisome: if a single Enterobacter plasmid carries both colistin and carbapenem resistance, it could readily transfer to other Enterobacterales in the gut. Our K. michiganensis (IMP−16 producer) and C. freundii (NDM−1) did not have mcr genes, meaning colistin could have been effective for those, but had the E. hormaechei been the dominant pathogen, colistin therapy would have failed. Genomic surveillance in our facility was thus intensified after this case to screen for mcr and additional carbapenemases in our CRE isolates.

Conversely, our study has limitations. As a single-case report, broad conclusions are limited by the unique circumstances. The patient’s outcome, while positive, may not be generalizable; many patients with similar infections may not survive even with aggressive therapy, especially if comorbidities are present. We also lacked direct evidence of whether these three organisms were present as colonizers prior to infection (no pre-infection rectal swab was available), so the exact sequence of acquisition is unclear. Nonetheless, the temporal clustering suggests nosocomial acquisition. Another limitation is that we did not perform conjugation experiments or long-read sequencing to delineate the plasmid structures; such analyses would be valuable to understand if any transfer of resistance genes occurred between the isolates (for instance, could the C. freundii and K. michiganensis have exchanged plasmids? Both carried blaOXA−1 and blaCTX-M−15, hinting at possible common lineage of resistance elements). Future studies should investigate the molecular epidemiology of these isolates and related strains in the unit to trace sources.

In conclusion, we describe the first reported case of a triple-carbapenemase polymicrobial bloodstream infection involving three different Enterobacterales species in a neutropenic cancer patient. The isolates, C. freundii (NDM−1), K. michiganensis (IMP−16), and E. hormaechei (KPC−2), exemplify the convergence of diverse resistance mechanisms within a single host. Successful management required a precision medicine approach, combining rapid diagnostics and tailored combination therapy (ceftazidime-avibactam plus aztreonam; and amikacin), along with rigorous supportive care. This case highlights the clinical impact of emerging multiple-resistant threats and underscores the importance of global surveillance for unusual carbapenemase combinations. Infection control measures remain essential to prevent such multi-resistant organisms from establishing epidemic peaks or endemic levels in healthcare settings. In summary, as carbapenemase genes continue to disseminate, clinicians must be prepared for rare but formidable scenarios like this one, where only a creative and aggressive antimicrobial strategy could result in a favorable (albeit temporary) outcome against a triple-headed resistant infection.
