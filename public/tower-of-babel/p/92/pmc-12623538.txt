Description of the Protocol Investigating Whether Remote Care Can Reduce Clinic Attendance in Lung Transplantation
Authors: Rebecca J. Varley, Rebecca Borton, David Van Dellen, Karthik Santhanakrishnan, Colin Edwards, Elana Pizzo, John F. Blaikley
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPatients undergoing lung transplantation require regular monitoring to ensure that the transplanted lung(s) stay healthy. This is usually achieved through a combination of home spirometry combined with regular reviews in hospital outpatient clinics. Administering questionnaires remotely could reduce the need for regular hospital clinic visits. This study aims to investigate whether a remotely administered questionnaire combined with home spirometry can reduce the number of hospital clinic visits.MethodsPatients will be randomised to the intervention arm (questionnaire and home spirometry) or standard of care arm (home spirometry + clinic visit). Quality of life (St George’s Respiratory Questionnaire), health economic data and usage acceptability will be collected at the beginning and end of the study period.Planned OutcomesThe primary outcome is to evaluate whether the number of hospital clinic visits are reduced in the intervention arm compared to the control arm. Secondary outcomes will evaluate the intervention influence on healthcare usage, the associated costs of service delivery and its environmental impact.Trial Registration NumberClinicalTrials.gov NCT05916495.

Key Summary Points

Introduction

Lung transplantation extends and enhances the quality of life for patients with end-stage lung disease [1]. Outcomes for this lifesaving intervention are generally inferior to other types of solid organ transplant, with lung transplant recipients having a median survival of 6.2 years [1, 2]. As a result, patients are intensely monitored in outpatient clinics following lung transplantation, with the frequency of visits ranging from weekly to every 4 months dependent on clinical need [3]. These visits are often unnecessary if no transplant-related complications are detected, yet they still incur costs for the patient (e.g. travel and time) and place a strain on clinical resources within the department.

Two potential solutions to this problem, telemedicine [3, 4] and home spirometry [5–7], have already been investigated in the context of lung transplantation. Telemedicine is effective in reducing travel time if the patient goes to a clinical facility near their home [3]. The consultation, however, often has to take place during working hours since both the patient and clinician have to be present. This interrupts patients and carers’ lives whilst also reducing clinical capacity. The other alternative, home spirometry, is widely used in lung transplant programs. Trials have shown that it improves patient outcomes owing to its capability of early detection of lung transplant problems [7]. It only records information about lung function, preventing it from detecting other changes in a lung transplant recipients health. Therefore, patients are still seen on a regular basis in the lung transplant unit despite the use of home spirometry to detect this change. This could potentially be avoided if a patient-reported outcome measure (PROM) was administered alongside home spirometry. Use of a pre-clinic questionnaire, a type of PROM, in other contexts separate from lung transplantation has already been shown to improve patient satisfaction and engagement with clinical teams [8]. This questionnaire could also record symptoms and medication, allowing the clinical team to triage the need for a clinic visit. To investigate this a randomised pragmatic trial was undertaken comparing clinic visit frequency between patients who completed a PROM and home spirometry against those who underwent home spirometry without PROM-based triage.

Methods

The project is a prospective single-centre interventional study evaluating the impact of a hybrid model of care where patients’ scheduled visits would be triaged with the use of home spirometry and PROM measure which record clinical symptoms, patient wishes and medication.

Objectives

The objective of this study is to investigate whether hybrid care (PROM + home spirometry) can be effective in reducing the requirement for clinic visits for lung transplant recipients.

Selection of Participants

One hundred participants who have undergone lung transplantation and are looked after at Manchester University NHS Foundation Trust will be screened. Eligible patients who meet the inclusion/exclusion criteria will then be invited to participate in the trial having been approached in clinic.

Study Design

The study is an unblinded randomised controlled study with parallel arms (Fig. 1). Participants will be randomly allocated to either hybrid care arm or the standard of care arm. Randomisation will occur when results from their baseline visit are available and suitability for remote follow-up is confirmed. Participants will be randomised using a random sequence, random block number randomisation schedule provided by an independent centralised electronic randomisation service (Sealed Envelope Ltd). Randomisation will be in a 1:1 ratio and stratified according to their normal follow-up interval. A screening log will be kept to identify what proportion of the transplant population is suitable for remote care and also how many patients refuse to take part in the study.Fig. 1Study protocol flow chart. At baseline, clinical and health-economic data will be collected and used as part of the study including medical history, current medication, most recent lung function tests and demographics. These data will be collected from Electronic Patient Records, clinical notes, patientMpower platform and study-specific data collection documents.

All participants have been provided with a portable handheld spirometer (Spirobank Smart, MIR, www.spirometry.com) linked to an electronic health application (patientMpower, www.patientMpower.com) and are trained in its use as part of the standard of care in Manchester University Foundation Trust. Patients will be asked to perform spirometry measurements 1 day/week. All data recorded via the patientMpower (pMp) app will be visible to the patient and to the healthcare provider in real-time via a secure browser-based portal. Alert thresholds for abnormal spirometry are set by senior physicians in the treatment team. Alerts are then actioned by the clinical team according to local standard operating procedures (SOPs); these actions are recorded on the electronic healthcare record.

Patients will be asked to complete a patient-reported measures, St George’s Respiratory Questionnaire (SGRQ) [9] at baseline and 6 months (Table 1). At each clinical appointment, all patients will be asked to complete some questions to assess symptom burden, medication adherence and if they wanted to see a doctor. All clinical observations, appointments, and investigations which occur during the time the patient is participating in the study will be recorded in an electronic case report form (eCRF) and analysed.

Baseline Visit

All patients are seen in hospital outpatient clinics. During the clinic visit a history is taken and lung function (spirometry) is performed along with routine blood tests (haematology, biochemistry and immunosuppression levels). If they do not need immediate investigations then they will be randomised into standard of care or hybrid arms (Fig. 1).

Standard of Care Arm

Patients will be seen in the hospital outpatient clinic for two visits. During these visits hospital spirometry will be performed, blood tests (haematology, biochemistry and immunosuppression levels) will be taken and they will be seen by both a nurse and physician. A nurse will then call them back in the evening or the following day to discuss results with them (Fig. 1).

Hybrid Arm

Patients who are allocated to the hybrid care arm will be evaluated at the next visit (visit 1) by home spirometry and blood tests combined with a clinical questionnaire. The blood tests (haematology, biochemistry and immunosuppression levels) will be performed at the hospital or locally to the patient, according to patient preference. The results will then be remotely reviewed by a physician who will ascertain whether the patient needs to be seen in person in hospital outpatient clinics. The clinical questionnaire in the first section asks three questions to assess respiratory health regarding exercise tolerance, shortness of breath and whether they have any infections. The second section contains questions regarding general health, medication compliance and if they have had problems with home spirometry. Finally two open-ended questions are included, one asking if they have been well and a second asking if they would like to tell the doctor anything. The final question asks whether the patient would prefer to be seen in clinic.

It is anticipated that subjects will need in-person outpatient review if they have abnormal home spirometry, they report a change in symptoms or express a desire to be seen in hospital outpatient clinics. All patients will receive a phone call from the transplant nurse with the results of the blood tests once immunosuppressant levels have been reported. The subsequent visit will take place as per standard of care (Fig. 1).

Outcomes

The primary outcome will be measured by comparing the number of clinic visits between the hybrid care arm and the standard of care arm during the observation period.

Sample Size

A power calculation estimates that the sample size of 100 (50 per arm) has a 95% power to detect a 20% reduction in outpatient attendance at the 5% significance level. This was calculated using the assumption that the primary outcome measure is a reduction in outpatient attendance. The power calculation was performed on 42 patient/arm as we anticipate that 15% (approx. 8 patients) will be admitted to hospital or have an unplanned clinic visit.

Data Collection and Management

All clinical data will be collected in an eCRF provided by REDCap hosted by the University of Manchester. Participating healthcare providers will be given access to the eCRF. Data will be stored during the duration of the project.

All data will be handled in accordance with the Data Protection Act 2018. Trial identification number (ID) will be used for identification. A data processing agreement is in place to support data sharing under the Data Protection Act. CRFs have been designed by the study steering committee and the final version approved by the ethics committee. All documents will be stored safely in confidential conditions. All investigators will have access to the anonymised data set for the purpose of analysis only and it will be stored by the sponsor.

Data Analysis Plan

The primary analysis is to determine the number of clinic visits between the hybrid care arm and the usual care arm during the observation period with statistical significance being ascertained by a t test (or Mann–Whitney U test if non-parametric data). This endpoint will be assessed at the end of the observation period. Secondary endpoints will be assessed using appropriate statistical tests.

An economic evaluation to compare the costs and outcomes of the intervention (hybrid care) and usual care will be performed using the standard methods and adopting the perspective of the National Health Service (NHS). The cost of the hybrid care will be assessed and compared to the cost of the treatment as usual arm. This will include the cost of the remote monitor tool, the cost of clinical staff time, the cost of the blood tests taken, the cost of outpatient appointments and clinic visits and subsequent hospital admissions to manage complications etc. Data on healthcare resources used in both arms are collected within the trial and published unit costs data will be used to assess the costs (e.g. PSSRU, NHS tariffs etc.). The total cost of both interventions will then be compared to the clinical outcomes of the two arms.

Results of the economic evaluation will be presented in terms of incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) to show the difference in costs and effectiveness of the hybrid treatment compared to the treatment as usual.

Safety Reporting

No significant safety concerns are anticipated in relation to any measurements carried out as part of this trial. All adverse events will be recorded and closely monitored until resolution or stabilisation and reported to the chief investigator.

Public and Patient Involvement

Representatives of the New Start Charity have been consulted during the design of the study. Patient information materials including the content of the patient questionnaire in the hybrid arm will be reviewed by the charity. Study findings will be communicated to New Start Charity and nationally through collaboration with other patient group networks.

Strengths and Limitations

A key strength of this study is the prospective randomised controlled trial (RCT) design. This is recognised as the gold standard in trial design, minimising allocation bias and increasing internal validity of the findings. A limitation of this design is the potential imbalance in clinical factors between groups at enrolment. For example, one arm may include patients undergoing more frequent follow-up than the other. To address this, a stratified approach was adopted, assigning participants to one of three strata on the basis of the interval since their last clinical review.

A key concern of hybrid care is the increased burden on healthcare professionals of dealing with alerts, and maintenance of the system. Therefore, a strength of the study is the included economic evaluation, via electronic healthcare record analysis, during the clinic visits and the interactions before and after the visit. The inclusion of this economic evaluation will be able to identify the cost of the additional burdens of running hybrid care and if this outweighs any detected advantages from an economic perspective.

Patient acceptability is perhaps the most important part of the project, since if the intervention is not acceptable to patients then it will be difficult for it to be adopted into clinical care. Patient acceptability will be evaluated by co-design of the remotely delivered clinic questionnaire, a health technology acceptability checklist and also patient compliance with the system. This will therefore provide confidence to any institution which adopts this care model what impact it will have on patients.

The study has three principal limitations. First, the use of the SGRQ rather than the EQ-5D-5L limits the precision of quality-adjusted life year (QALY) estimations. The SGRQ was chosen for its specific relevance to respiratory disease which is important in lung transplant recipients. This was crucial in this study since the clinic questionnaire used in the hybrid visit had not been previously validated.

Second, the study employs a non-validated tool to assess clinical outcomes. To our knowledge, no validated lung transplant-specific instrument currently exists that can serve as a surrogate for face-to-face clinic visits and therefore we had to develop our own. Accordingly, a novel questionnaire was developed in collaboration with clinical staff and patients at the study site. Although not formally validated, data obtained using this tool will be compared against clinic records, with any discrepancies closely monitored throughout the trial to ensure patient safety. The questionnaire primarily focused on pulmonary symptoms; however, if proven effective, future versions could be adapted to capture symptoms associated with the underlying disease that necessitated transplantation, such as cystic fibrosis.

Finally, this is a single-centre study, which may limit external validity. However, given the standardised models of care and national key performance indicators governing transplant services in the UK, the findings may be generalisable to other UK centres operating under similar frameworks.

Ethics

The study has received ethical approval from the National Research Ethics Committee Manchester and the Health Research Authority (23/NW/0086). All participants will confirm their Informed consent in writing. The trial will be conducted in line with the Helsinki declaration.

Dissemination

Data will be anonymised, with group-level data being used for presentation and publication. The results from this study will be presented to the charity representing patients at the lung transplant centre. Preliminary findings will be presented to conferences in abstract form, whilst the final findings will be submitted for publication in accordance with UK Research Council policies. Aggregated data which will be anonymised will be shared on reasonable request if it is in accordance with the ethical approval (23/NW/0086).

Conclusion

This randomised controlled pragmatic trial evaluates a hybrid model to determine whether alternate in-person appointments can be effectively replaced with remote spirometry combined with patient-reported outcomes. It is hypothesised that this novel intervention will not negatively impact patient outcomes but reduce the requirement on transplant patients to attend hospital. This will provide significant benefits for patients—such as reduced time in clinical settings, decreased travel requirements, and greater flexibility in engaging with healthcare providers. It also will provide advantages for healthcare providers, including releasing capacity so that urgent patients can be seen whilst also reducing healthcare delivery costs. These potential advantages, if approved, should ensure that this approach will be investigated/adopted in other transplant centres.
