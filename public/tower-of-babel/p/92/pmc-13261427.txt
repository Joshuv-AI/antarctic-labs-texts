Phenolic Compounds with PCSK9 Secretion Inhibitory Activities from the Fruits of Liriope spicata
Authors: Runjiao Zheng, Min-Hee Seo, Chan-Woong Park, Ga Min Ryu, Jungmoo Huh, Young Hee Choi, Wan Kyunn Whang, Young-Won Chin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bioactivity-guided isolation of the fresh fruits of Liriope spicata afforded two new megastigmane glucosides (1 and 2), and one new depside (3), together with 24 known compounds (4–27). The structures of all compounds were elucidated by extensive analysis of their UV, HR-MS, NMR spectroscopic data, ECD calculations, gauge-including atomic orbital (GIAO) NMR calculations. Among the isolated compounds, (+)-platyphyllarin A (5) and (3S)-3,5,4′-trihydroxy-7-methoxy-6-methylhomoisoflavanone (8) showed potent effects in reducing PCSK9 secretion, accompanied by a decrease in PCSK9 expression at both transcriptional and protein levels and enhanced uptake of 1,1′-dioctadecyl-3,3,3,3′-tetramethylindocarbocyanine perchlorate-labeled low-density lipoprotein (DiI-LDL). Notably, compounds 5 and 8 also attenuated atorvastatin-induced PCSK9 upregulation, highlighting their potential to complement and enhance atorvastatin-based lipid-lowering therapy.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the primary cause of death worldwide, is attributed to elevated levels of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) in the blood. , LDL-C levels are mainly regulated by low-density lipoprotein receptors (LDLRs) expressed on hepatocytes, which mediate the clearance of circulating LDL-C. While statins such as atorvastatin are widely prescribed to lower LDL-C levels, their clinical efficacy varies among individuals, and some patients exhibit inadequate responses or intolerance to statin therapy. Moreover, statin treatment has been reported to upregulate the expression of proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9), a hepatic protein that binds LDL receptors (LDLR) and promotes their lysosomal degradation, thereby inhibiting LDLR recycling and LDL-C clearance. − In 2015, monoclonal antibodies that disrupt the PCSK9–LDLR interaction were approved by the U.S. FDA for the management of hypercholesterolemia, followed by the approval of an siRNA-based therapy targeting PCSK9 mRNA in Europe in 2020.

Recently, since statin therapy has been reported to upregulate PCSK9 expression, combination therapy with PCSK9 inhibitors and statins has been proposed as an effective approach for treating hypercholesterolemia. This strategy is also recommended in the 2023 China Guidelines for Lipid Management, which advocate the combination of statins and PCSK9 inhibitors when LDL-C targets are not achieved with statin therapy alone. Consistent with this concept, our group recently identified small molecules with potential to complement statin therapy and continues to explore additional PCSK9-modulating natural products.

Liriope spicata Lour. (Asparagaceae) is commonly known as “Shan Mai Dong” or “Hubei Mai Dong” in Traditional Chinese Medicine. The roots of L. spicata have been widely used in traditional medicine to nourish yin, promote body fluid production, moisten the lungs, clearing the heart, and relieve symptoms such as fatigue, insomnia, and constipation. L. spicata is also a key component of “Sheng Mai San” and “Guan Xin Sheng Mai oral liquid”, well-known Chinese herbal formulas traditionally used to treat cardiovascular diseases and listed in the 2020 edition of the Pharmacopoeia of the People’s Republic of China. Previous studies on the phytochemistry of L. spicata have identified steroidal saponins, homoisoflavonoids, and polysaccharides, which are associated with activities against inflammation, diabetes, and cardiovascular disorders. However, few studies have focused on L. spicata fruits and their potential for treating hypercholesterolemia and hyperlipidemia, which are well-established risk factors for cardiovascular diseases.

In an initial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)-based screening, the ethyl acetate and chloroform fractions of the methanolic extract of fresh L. spicata fruits significantly reduced PCSK9 secretion in HepG2 cells. Accordingly, bioactivity-guided isolation was performed on these two fractions to identify compounds responsible for PCSK9 downregulation. As a result, three previously undescribed compounds (1–3), together with 24 known analogues (4–27), were isolated from L. spicata (Figure ). The compounds 5 and 8 were further evaluated for their activities to enhance LDL-C uptake and to attenuate the atorvastatin-induced upregulation of PCSK9.

Materials and Methods

General Experimental Procedures, Chemicals, and Reagents

Optical rotation, UV/CD, IR, NMR, and HRMS data were obtained as previously reported, , using a JASCO P-2000 polarimeter, Chirascan CD spectrometer, JASCO FT/IR-4700 spectrometer, JEOL 500 MHz and Bruker AVANCE 800 MHz NMR spectrometers, and an Agilent Q-TOF 6530 mass spectrometer. Analytical and preparative HPLC were performed on a Gilson system, and MPLC separations were conducted using a Biotage Isolera One instrument. Column chromatography was carried out on C18 or silica gel, with size-exclusion chromatography using Sephadex LH-20. Solvents and reagents were obtained from commercial suppliers as previously reported. ,

Plant Material

The fruits of L. spicata were collected at Gwanak Mountain (37.4429° N, 126.9610° E), Seoul, Republic of Korea in September 2021. The plant material was taxonomically authenticated by Professor Wan Kyunn Whang, College of Pharmacy, Chung-Ang University, Seoul, Republic of Korea. A voucher specimen (CYWSNU-KP0027) has been deposited at the College of Pharmacy, Seoul National University, Republic of Korea.

Extraction and Isolation

Fresh fruits of L. spicata (12.4 kg) were extracted with 40 L of MeOH at room temperature for 24 h (three times) to afford a crude methanolic extract (1.2 kg). The crude extract was suspended in water and successively partitioned with n-hexane, chloroform, ethyl acetate, and n-butanol, yielding the corresponding fractions: n-hexane-soluble (20.2 g), chloroform-soluble (5.7 g), ethyl acetate-soluble (3.5 g), and n-butanol (98 g). The chloroform-soluble extract (LC, 5.7 g) was applied on silica column chromatography and eluted with stepwise gradients of n-hexane–ethyl acetate (20:1 → 15:1 → 10:1 → 5:1 → 3:1 → 2:1 → 1:1, v/v) and chloroform–MeOH (20:1 → 15:1 → 10:1 → 5:1 → 3:1 → 2:1 → 1:1, v/v) to afford 12 subfractions (LC1-LC12). Fraction LC5 (14.2 mg) was performed on semipreparative HPLC using YMC-Pack Pro-C18(2)­100A-LC, 250 × 10 mm2 column, eluted with mixture of (MeCN–H2O, 40:60) to yield 7 (1.0 mg, t R 42.5 min). Fraction LC6 (35 mg) was applied on semipreparative HPLC, eluted with mixture of (MeCN–H2O, 38:62) to obtain 8 (0.8 mg, t R 46.0 min), 10 (0.8 mg, t R 60.8 min), and 9 (1.0 mg, t R 66.7 min). Fraction LC10 (72 mg) was separated by semipreparative HPLC, eluted with mixture of (MeCN–H2O, 25:75) to give 15 (3.0 mg, t R 28.5 min), 16 (13.0 mg, t R 30.8 min) and 18 (3.0 mg, t R 34.0 min). Fraction LC11 (770 mg) was subjected to the Sephadex LH-20 (eluted with MeOH) to obtain 6 fractions (LC11A-LC11E). Fraction LC11A (45 mg) was conducted on semipreparative HPLC, eluted with mixture of (MeCN–H2O, 15:85) to obtain 2 (4.0 mg, t R 47.5 min). Fraction LC11D (15 mg) was performed on semipreparative HPLC, eluted with mixture of (MeCN–H2O, 20:80) to yield 19 (0.7 mg, t R 62.0 min) and 17 (1.0 mg, t R 68.2 min).

The ethyl acetate-soluble extract (LE, 3.5 g) was subjected to reversed-phase MPLC using a MeOH–H2O gradient (5:95 to 80:20, v/v) to give 12 subfractions (LE1 to LE12). Fraction LE4 (90 mg) was applied to semipreparative HPLC using OptimaPak C18, 250 × 10 mm2 column, eluted with mixture of (MeCN–H2O, 18:82) to give 6 (12.0 mg, t R 20.3 min), 4 (3.5 mg, t R 21.5 min) and 26 (2.0 mg, t R 24.0 min). Fraction LE6 (100 mg) was separated by semipreparative HPLC, eluted with mixture of (MeCN–H2O, 13:87) to obtain 21 (2.5 mg, t R 34.5 min), 20 (15.0 mg, t R 41.0 min), 1 (14.0 mg, t R 74.5 min), 3 (1.0 mg, t R 100.0 min), and 14 (5.0 mg, t R 115.0 min). Fraction LE7 (300 mg) was separated by Sephadex LH-20 CC (eluted with MeOH) to give six subfractions (LE7A to LE7F). Fraction LE7B (100 mg) was performed on semipreparative HPLC, eluted with mixture of (MeCN–H2O, 18:82) to give 24 (23.0 mg, t R 23.5 min) and 13 (2.0 mg, t R 59.0 min). Fraction LE7D (150 mg) was separated by semipreparative HPLC, eluted with mixture of (MeCN–H2O, 18:82) to yield 23 (8.0 mg, t R 18.7 min), 22 (75.0 mg, t R 24.2 min), 25 (10.0 mg, t R 31.9 min), 5 (10.0 mg, t R 38.5 min) and 12 (7.0 mg, t R 67.5 min). Fraction LE8 (430 mg) was fractionated by MPLC with a gradient system of MeOH–H2O (10:90 to 60:40, v/v) to give five subfractions (LE8A to LE8E). Fraction LE8C (180 mg) was applied on Sephadex LH-20 with MeOH to obtain seven subfractions (LE8C1 to LE8C7). Fraction LE8C5 (10 mg) was performed on semipreparative HPLC, eluted with mixture of (MeCN–H2O, 30:70) to give 27 (2.0 mg, t R 30.0 min). Fraction LE9 (55 mg) was subjected to semipreparative HPLC, eluted with mixture of (MeCN–H2O, 30:70), to give 11 (2.0 mg, t R 20.0 min).

Determination of the Absolute Configuration of Sugar Groups

The monosaccharides (1, 0.7 mg; 2, 0.6 mg) obtained after hydrolysis were reacted with l-cysteine methyl ester hydrochloride (0.5 mg) in pyridine (0.1 mL) at 60 °C for 1 h. The resulting mixture was then further treated with o-tolyl isothiocyanate (0.5 mg) in pyridine (0.1 mL) and heated again at 60 °C for an additional 1 h. Authentic d- and l-glucose were treated in the same manner. Subsequently, retention time data obtained from HPLC chromatograms were used for comparison of the reaction mixtures. (Figure S80).

NMR Calculations

Possible diastereomers of compounds 1 and 2 were constructed using ChemBio3D Ultra 13.03. Conformer generation was performed with the MMFF94 force field in Spartan’16. Conformers accounting for 95% of the Boltzmann population were selected for further geometry optimization, which was carried out at the B3LYP/6–31G­(d) level using Gaussian 16. The GIAO method was applied to calculate magnetic shielding tensors at the mPW1PW91/6–31+G­(d,p) level with PCM (methanol). The calculated values of the predominant conformers were Boltzmann-weighted and subsequently analyzed using Sarotti’s Excel sheet to obtain DP4+ probabilities.

ECD Calculations

Possible diastereomers of compounds 1, 2, 1a, and 2a were built in ChemBio3D Ultra 13.03. Conformational sampling was performed in Spartan’16 employing the MMFF94 force field, and conformers contributing up to 95% of the Boltzmann population were selected for further optimization. Geometry optimizations were carried out at the B3LYP/6–31G­(d) level in the gas phase with Gaussian 16. ECD calculations were subsequently conducted by time-dependent density functional theory (TDDFT) using the B3LYP, CAM-B3LYP, and ωB97X-D functionals with the 6–311+G­(d,p) basis set for compounds 1 and 2, and the B3LYP/6–311+G­(d,p) level for compounds 1a and 2a, incorporating a PCM solvation model for methanol. Boltzmann-weighted ECD spectra were generated using SpecDis 1.62.

Results and Discussion

Structural Elucidation of Isolated Compounds

Compound 1 was obtained as a yellow amorphous solid. The molecular formula of C21H30O9 was established by the HRESIMS data (m/z 444.2228 [M + NH4]+, calcd for C21H34NO9, 444.2232). IR absorptions indicated the presence of hydroxy (3398 cm–1) and carbonyl (1750 cm–1) functional groups. The 1H NMR spectrum of 1 (Table ) showed the presence of two olefinic protons at δH 5.96 (1H, q, J = 1.1 Hz, H-14) and δH 6.05 (1H, m, H-10), two oxygenated methines at [δH 3.98 (1H, dt, J = 12.7, 3.5 Hz, H-3) and 4.15 (1H, d, J = 3.5 Hz, H-4)], a pair of methylene protons at [δH 1.90 (1H, t, J = 12.7 Hz, H-2a) and 1.68 (1H, ddd, J = 12.7, 3.5, 1.5 Hz, H-2b)], and four methyl groups at [δH 1.07 (3H, s, H3-12), 1.13 (3H, s, H3-13), 1.72 (3H, d, J = 1.1 Hz, H3-14) and 2.25 (3H, d, J = 1.5 Hz, H3-15)], indicating the presence of a megastigmane moiety. In addition, the signals belonging to a glucopyranose moiety were observed at δH 4.45 (1H, d, J = 7.7 Hz, H-1′), 3.25 (1H, dd, J = 9.2, 7.7 Hz, H-2′), 3.39 (1H, t, J = 9.2 Hz, H-3′), 3.34 (1H, overlapped, H-4′), 3.32 (1H, dd, J = 5.2, 2.3 Hz, H-5′), 3.85 (1H, dd, J = 11.8, 2.3 Hz, H-6′), 3.69 (1H, dd, J = 11.8, 5.2 Hz, H-6′). The coupling constant (J = 7.7 Hz) of anomeric proton (H-1′) suggested β-orientation. The 13C NMR and DEPT spectra of 1 (Table ) displayed 21 carbon resonances, including one ester carbonyl group [δC 171.4 (C-11)], one quaternary carbon [δC 38.4 (C-1)], six olefinic carbons [δC 132.6 (C-5), 137.0 (C-6), 110.0 (C-7), 152.3 (C-8), 157.2 (C-9) and 117.3 (C-10)] including two protonated ones (C-7 and C-10), two oxygenated methines [δC 76.8 (C-3) and 71.5 (C-4)]; one methylene [δC 38.1 (C-2)], four methyl groups [δC 20.2 (C-14), 27.7 (C-12), 30.0 (C-13) and 11.8 (C-15)], and six carbon signals ascribed to a glucopyranose moiety (Figure ).

1H NMR and 13C NMR Spectroscopic Data (800 and 200 MHz) Recorded in Methanol-d 4 for Compounds 1 and 2

Detailed interpretation of 1H NMR and 13C NMR data (Table ) of 1 revealed close similarities to those of vignoside A, a megastigmane glycoside previously isolated from adzuki beans, but differed in the signals for the side chain moiety. The side chain group of 1 was determined to be a γ-methylidene β-methyl α,β-unsaturated γ-lactone moiety, which was the same as the side chain of a previously isolated megastigmane glycoside, hederhomoside A, as supported by the HMBC correlations from H-10 to C-8/C-9/C-11/C-15, from H-15 to C-8 and from H-7 to C-9 (Figure ). A trimethyl cyclohexene moiety was confirmed by the 1H–1H COSY correlations between H-2/H-3/H-4 combined with the HMBC correlations from H-12/H-13 to C-1/C-2/C-6, from H-4 to C-3/C-5/C-6 and from H-14 to C-4/C-5/C-6 (Figure ). Furthermore, the HMBC cross-peaks from H-7 to C-1/C-5/C-6 indicated that the side chain was located at C-6 of the cyclohexene moiety (Figure ). The sugar moiety was connected to C-3 via an ether linkage based on the HMBC correlation from H-1′ to C-3 (Figure ). The sugar moiety of 1 was obtained by enzymatic hydrolysis to determine its absolute configuration and confirmed as d-glucose, with identification based on HPLC analysis of its thiocarbamoyl thiazolidine derivative and comparison of retention times with authentic d-glucose and l-glucose standards (Supporting Information, Figure S80).

Key HMBC (blue solid arrow) LR-HSQMBC (red dashed arrow) and 1H–1H COSY (bold) correlations of compounds 1–3.

The axial orientation of H-3 and the equatorial orientation of H-4 was determined based on the coupling constants of H-2a (t, J = 12.7 Hz), H-2b (ddd, J = 12.7, 3.5, 1.5 Hz), H-3 (dt, J = 12.7, 3.5 Hz) and H-4 (t, J = 3.5 Hz), which were also supported by the NOESY correlations between H-3/H-2b/H-4/H3-12 and between H-2a and H3-13. The NOESY cross-peak of H-7/H3-15 indicated that the Δ7 double bond in 1 took Z-geometry (Figure ). Therefore, there were two possible absolute configurations of 1, including (3R,4S)-1 and (3S,4R)-1. To determine the absolute configuration of 1, calculations of the gauge-including atomic orbital (GIAO) 1D NMR data and DP4+ probability analysis were performed for two plausible candidates at the PCM/mPW1PW91/6–31+G­(d,p)//B3LYP/6–31G­(d) level of theory using MeOH as the solvent, giving the results of (3R,4S)-1 with a 100% probability in DP4+ with the experimental values (Figure A). To further confirm the absolute configuration of 1, the ECD calculation of (3R,4S)-1 and (3S,4R)-1 of compound 1 was performed at three different functionals [B3LYP/6–311+G­(d,p), CAM-B3LYP/6–311+G­(d,p) and ωB97X-D/6–311+G­(d,p)] level in MeOH with the PCM model (Supporting Information, Figure S87). However, across all three functionals, the calculated ECD curves of the two diastereomers were essentially indistinguishable. Consequently, the aglycone (1a) of compound 1 was obtained by using enzymatic hydrolysis to enable ECD reassignment. The ECD calculation of 1a was performed at the B3LYP/6–311+G­(d,p) level in MeOH with the PCM model. The experimental ECD spectrum of 1a was consistent with the calculated spectrum for (3R,4S)-1a (Figure A). Therefore, compound 1 was determined as shown in Figure , an undescribed megastigmane glucopyranoside, and was named lirioposide A (Figures and ).

Effects of Fractions and Isolated Compounds on PCSK9 Secretion

Given that PCSK9 binds to the extracellular domain of LDLR and promotes its lysosomal degradation, inhibiting PCSK9 secretion is a critical factor in maintaining LDLR levels on the cell surface, which facilitates efficient LDL-C uptake. Therefore, the effects of various fractions and isolated compounds from L. spicata on PCSK9 secretion were evaluated using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) in HepG2 cells. To assess the effect of the L. spicata fractions (20 μg/mL) on HepG2 cell viability, a WST-8 assay was conducted, revealing no significant cytotoxicity in any of the fractions (Supporting Information, Figure S83). Based on these findings, the inhibitory effects on PCSK9 secretion were subsequently evaluated at the same concentration. The results showed that the ethyl acetate (LE) and chloroform (LC) soluble fractions significantly reduced PCSK9 secretion, with decreases of 61.0% and 85.1%, respectively, compared to the negative control (Figure ). These findings suggest that ethyl acetate and chloroform soluble fractions may contain bioactive compounds with PCSK9 secretion inhibitory properties, making them promising candidates for further isolation and purification.

Effects of Compounds 5 and 8 Derived from L. spicata on PCSK9 Transcription and Expression

Statins are widely used as primary therapies for hypercholesterolemia, and some statins, including atorvastatin (ATV), have been reported to promote the transcriptional expression of PCSK9. This phenomenon may contribute to the insufficient LDL-C-lowering effects observed in some patients, even when using maximum doses of statins. In this study, we investigated the impact of compounds 5 and 8 on PCSK9 mRNA expression and examined the variations in PCSK9 mRNA levels when these compounds were coadministered with ATV using HepG2 cells, employing qRT-PCR for analysis (Figure A). The results indicated that compounds 5 and 8 significantly reduced PCSK9 mRNA expression by 69.6% and 75.8%, respectively, compared to the negative control. This effect was consistently observed with coadministration of ATV. A roughly 3-fold upregulation of PCSK9 mRNA was observed in HepG2 cells following treatment with 10 μM ATV. However, when 20 μM of compounds 5 and 8 were coadministered with 10 μM of ATV, PCSK9 mRNA expression was significantly reduced compared to treatment with ATV alone. Specifically, cotreatment with compound 5 and ATV decreased PCSK9 mRNA expression by 64.3%, returning it to levels similar to the negative control. Co-treatment with compound 8 and ATV decreased PCSK9 mRNA expression by 93.1%, resulting in a 79.3% reduction relative to the negative control.

Additional qRT-PCR analyses were performed to investigate the effects of compounds 5 and 8 on various transcription factors, including SREBP1/2, HNF1α, LXRα, and FOXO1/3 (Figure B). SREBP activates PCSK9 transcription by interacting with sterol regulatory elements (SREs) in the PCSK9 gene promoter. Notably, SREBP2 is a key regulator of PCSK9 expression, modulating its levels in response to sterol concentrations. When sterol levels are low, SREBP2 becomes activated and promotes PCSK9 expression. Furthermore, HNF1α plays a critical role in regulating PCSK9 expression in hepatocytes by cooperating with SRE to control its transcription. Together with SREBP2, HNF1α activates the PCSK9 promoter, and their combined actions contribute to the precise regulation of PCSK9 expression. − However, the results of this study indicated that treatment with compounds 5 and 8 led to similar or increased mRNA levels of SREBP1/2 and HNF1α compared to the negative control group. Compound 8 significantly increased the mRNA levels of HNF1α, SREBP1, and SREBP2 by 1.9-fold, 1.5-fold, and 1.6-fold, respectively, when administered alone. When combined with ATV, the mRNA levels of HNF1α and SREBP1 were further elevated by 2.2-fold and 3.8-fold, respectively, compared to ATV treatment alone. Additionally, ATV is known to increase the activation and nuclear translocation of SREBP2, ultimately leading to an upregulation of PCSK9 mRNA expression. In this study, treatment of HepG2 cells with 10 μM ATV increased SREBP2 mRNA levels by approximately 2-fold compared to the negative control. Co-treatment with compounds 5 and 8 slightly reduced SREBP2 mRNA levels compared to ATV treatment alone; however, this reduction was not statistically significant. These findings suggest that the reduction in PCSK9 expression induced by compounds 5 and 8 occurs through mechanisms distinct from the known pathways involving SREBP1/2 and HNF1α.

LXRs are known to enhance PCSK9 transcription, and direct activation of LXR with agonists increases PCSK9 expression. The present study demonstrated that compound 5 treatment led to a slight reduction in LXRα mRNA levels compared to the negative control, indicating a potential role of LXRα downregulation in mediating the compound’s effect on PCSK9 expression. However, given that the degree of LXRα downregulation is minor relative to the reduction in PCSK9 expression, alternative pathways for regulating PCSK9 expression may exist, warranting further investigation. In contrast, compound 8 significantly increased LXRα mRNA levels by approximately 1.7-fold when administered alone and 1.6-fold when combined with ATV compared to the control. These findings imply that the mechanism by which compound 8 reduces PCSK9 expression operates through a pathway distinct from the conventional LXRα-associated pathway.

FOXO1, a tumor suppressor gene, is often suppressed in cancer cells via PI3K/Akt-dependent phosphorylation, followed by ubiquitination and degradation via the proteasome pathway. A small molecule, 7030B–C5, has been reported to reduce PCSK9 expression in HepG2 cells and mice primarily through the FOXO1 and HNF1α pathway. Additionally, FOXO3 is known to bind to the HNF1α binding site in the PCSK9 promoter region, competitively inhibiting the upregulation mediated by HNF1α and interacting with the insulin response element (IRE) within the PCSK9 promoter to recruit the histone deacetylase sirtuin-6 (Sirt6), thereby suppressing PCSK9 gene expression. In this analysis, treatment with compound 5 resulted in FOXO1 and FOXO3 mRNA levels that were similar to or marginally reduced compared to the negative control, whereas treatment with compound 8 significantly increased these levels by 2.8-fold and 1.9-fold, respectively. A comparable trend was observed with the cotreatment of compound 8 and ATV. These results suggest that the elevation of FOXO1/3 expression through compound 8 may contribute to the observed suppression of PCSK9 expression.

Furthermore, to examine the effects of compounds 5 and 8 on LDLR and PCSK9 protein expression, additional experiments were conducted using Western blot analysis (Supporting Information, Figure S85). The data revealed that compound 5 resulted in a marginal increase in LDLR protein expression, whereas compound 8 exhibited a 28.1% increase in LDLR protein expression compared to the negative control. Furthermore, PCSK9 protein expression levels were observed to decrease by 50.7% and 70.4% upon treatment with compounds 5 and 8, respectively. Importantly, when coadministered with ATV, compounds 5 and 8 significantly reduced PCSK9 protein expression levels by 34.6% and 91.7%, respectively, compared to ATV treatment alone. These findings suggested that compounds 5 and 8 have the potential to effectively regulate cholesterol levels when used in conjunction with ATV, indicating their strong potential as PCSK9 inhibitors.

Enhancement of LDL Uptake by Compounds 5 and 8

To confirm whether compounds 5 and 8 enhance LDL-C uptake, additional assays were conducted using LDL labeled with 1,1′-dioctadecyl-3,3,3,3′-tetramethylindocarbocyanine (DiI) to observe changes in intracellular LDL-C levels (Figure ). This experiment quantitatively analyzed the LDL-C uptake improvement effects of compounds 5 and 8, revealing that compound 5 exhibited approximately 11.3-fold and compound 8 approximately 6.61-fold increases in LDL uptake compared to the negative control. These effects may be attributed to the upregulation of LDLR expression by compounds 5 and 8, coupled with the downregulation of PCSK9 expression, suggesting their potential as therapeutics in the treatment of hypercholesterolemia.

Conclusions

This study systematically evaluated the cholesterol metabolism–modulating potential of compounds isolated from L. spicata by assessing their effects on PCSK9 secretion and expression. Among the tested compounds, compounds 5–8 significantly inhibited PCSK9 secretion, with (+)-platyphyllarin A (5) and (3S)-3,5,4′-trihydroxy-7-methoxy-6-methylhomoisoflavanone (8) exhibiting the most pronounced inhibitory effects. Further mechanistic investigations demonstrated that both compounds effectively downregulated PCSK9 at the mRNA and protein levels, resulting in a marked enhancement of LDL uptake in HepG2 cells. Notably, compounds 5 and 8 also attenuated atorvastatin-induced PCSK9 upregulation, highlighting their potential to complement and enhance atorvastatin-based lipid-lowering therapy.
