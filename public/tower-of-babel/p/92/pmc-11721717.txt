Introduction

Tactical athletes are a unique population whose dedicated physical training and occupational responsibilities are focused on a selfless service to their communities and countries in support of critical missions.1 Military personnel are tactical athletes tasked with maintaining proficiencies and being postured for various physical stressors. Atrial fibrillation (AF) symptom intolerances prevent military personnel from maintaining many occupations, being ready for deployments, and working in austere environments.1 The management of AF in the tactical athlete is nuanced and complex, partly due to medical therapy limitations imposed by side effects and occupational restrictions. Rate control therapies are often not well tolerated due to low baseline heart rates and adverse effects of fatigue and reduced exercise capacity.2 Pursuing a rhythm strategy for military patients with AF is of particular importance, as symptoms and sequelae of AF can be duty limiting, affect quality of life, and impact critical mission goals.1 Antiarrhythmic drug (AAD) therapy may be limited by adverse effects as well as by occupational policies. The impact of AF catheter ablation on AADs has not been previously described in military personnel and tactical athletes. This study aims to describe active-duty military personnel receiving AADs for AF and compare drug treatment durations between personnel who received AF catheter ablation and those receiving standard medical therapy.

Methods

A cohort analysis of identified active-duty individuals in the U.S. Armed Forces (Army, Air Force, and Navy military branches) with AF from January 2004 to June 2019 in the San Antonio Regional Military Health System was completed. Patients were included if they had a confirmed 12-lead-electrocardiographic diagnosis of AF.

Baseline demographics, AAD prescriptions, time from AF diagnosis to initial AAD prescription, duration of AAD therapy, ablation rates, time from diagnosis to first AF ablation, and their associations were analyzed and compared. Data analysis was performed using JMP statistical analysis software version 15 (SAS Institute).

This retrospective research protocol was approved by the institutional review board of San Antonio Military Medical Center, which waived the need for informed consent because the research involved no more than minimal risk to participants and the waiver did not adversely affect their rights and welfare. This study complies with the Declaration of Helsinki and was performed according to ethics committee approval.

The EnSite Precision cardiac mapping system (Abbott) or CARTO mapping system (Biosense Webster) were used for 3-dimensional electroanatomic mapping. A Pentaray multipolar mapping catheter or decapolar Lasso catheter (Biosense Webster) was paired with the CARTO mapping system. These mapping systems and associated catheters were used to generate left atrial and pulmonary vein anatomy. Operators throughout the study period utilized either TactiCath Quartz (St. Jude Medical), TactiCath SE (St. Jude Medical), ThermoCool Navistar (Biosense Webster) or Celsius 4 mm/8 mm (Biosense Webster) as ablation catheters. Alcohol ablations were not performed.

Results

A total of 150 (78%) were prescribed a class IC agent and 76 (40%) a class III agent (Figure 2). A total of 49 (25%) received pill-in-pocket therapy. Those who received pill in pocket tended to be younger (mean age 34 ± 8 years vs 36 ± 9 years, P = .0377) and have paroxysmal AF (P = .0045 vs persistent).

The average time from diagnosis to initial prescription was 11 ± 21 months with a mean duration of 27 ± 40 months. A total of 23% of personnel remained on AAD therapy at 24 months.

A total of 51 (27%) transitioned to a second AAD. The second AAD prescription averaged 30 ± 38 months with a cumulative average AAD duration of 37 ± 44 months. There was an average cohort follow-up of 55 ± 54 months after initial AF diagnosis.

A total of 120 (63%) underwent pulmonary vein isolation without additional empiric ablation or flutter lines. The mean time from diagnosis to initial ablation was 22.5 ± 26.6 months. The mean duration of AAD therapy prior to ablation was 16 ± 22 months. Those who underwent ablation received significantly shorter AAD treatment (23 ± 4 months vs 40 ± 6 months, P = .006) (Figure 3). A total of 45 (38%) patients managed with ablation underwent initial procedure prior to receiving an AAD. Procedural complications occurred in 4 (3.3%) patients. These included pulmonary vein stenosis, cardiac tamponade secondary to atrial perforation, arteriovenous fistula, and pseudoaneurysm. There were no fatalities.

Discussion

This study describes a cohort of U.S. active-duty military servicemembers managed with AADs. This population is unique in that it includes a subset of tactical athletes whose occupations require a minimum standard of physical fitness and whose careers may be altered by a diagnosis of AF, particularly if long-term medical therapy is necessary.

There is increasing evidence to support rhythm control strategies with a goal of preventing atrial remodeling, recurrences, and progression to persistent AF. The EAST-AFNET 4 trial demonstrated improved clinical outcomes with early rhythm control, regardless of symptoms, particularly if initiated within 1 year of diagnosis.3 Studies such as the CABANA and RECORD-AF trials have demonstrated improved quality of life with a rhythm control strategy as opposed to rate control, corresponding with reduced AF burden.4,5,6

Even though our studied patient cohort would not typically have been enrolled in the CABANA, RECORD-AF, and EAST-AFNET 4 trials, catheter ablation may not only decrease AF burden, but also alleviate the burden of medical therapies in the tactical athlete. Patients receiving ablation in this military population received significantly shorter durations of AAD therapy, and we theorize that AADs were able to be discontinued with reduced AF burden afforded by ablation. Further prospective study is required to objectively evaluate quality of life, AF burden, and long-term clinical and occupational outcomes in this special population.

Limitations of this study include the retrospective nature. TRICARE insurance–covered AAD prescriptions were verified; however, exact dates of medication use and verification of compliance were not possible. We believe that AAD use verification would be similarly affected across groups and would not account for the statistical difference between those who received ablation vs those who did not. The study spans a period of 15 years, and we recognize that technology and AF management recommendations evolved during this time frame and thus could have influenced the timing of ablations throughout the study period. Despite these limitations, our data are thought-provoking and provide a basis for future prospective study.

Conclusion

Military personnel who receive AF catheter ablation require shorter durations of AAD treatment. It may be reasonable to consider AF ablation in patients with tactical athletic occupations not amenable to chronic AAD therapy.
