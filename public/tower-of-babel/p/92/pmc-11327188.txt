Aromatic residues in N-terminal domain of archaeal trehalase affect the folding and activity of catalytic domain
Authors: Takumi Sakoh, Nagisa Satoh, Yumeka Domon, Maho Nakamura, Masao Kawakita, Masayoshi Sakaguchi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractConsidering the structure of the bacterial GH15 family glucoamylase (GA), Thermoplasma trehalase Tvn1315 may be composed of a β-sandwich domain (BD) and a catalytic domain (CD). Tvn1315 BD weakly binds to insoluble β-glucans, such as cellulose, and helps fold CD. To determine how aromatic residues contribute to proper folding and enzyme activity, we performed alanine scanning for 32 aromatic residues in the BD. The study did not identify a single residue involved in glucan binding. However, several aromatic residues were found to be involved in BD or CD folding and in modulating the activity of the full-length enzyme. Among those aromatic residue mutations, the W43A mutation led to reduced solubility of the BD and full-length protein and resulted in a full-length enzyme with significantly lower activity. The activity of W43F and W43Y was significantly higher than that of W43A. In addition, Ala substitutions of Tyr83, Tyr113, and Tyr17 led to a reduction in trehalase activity, but Phe substitutions of these residues could be tolerated, as these mutants maintained activities similar to WT activity. Thus, these aromatic residues in BD may interact with CD and modulate enzyme activity.Key points• Aromatic residues in the BD are involved in BD and CD folding.• Aromatic residues in the BD near the CD active site modulate enzyme activity.• BD interacts with CD and closely modulates enzyme activity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00253-024-13205-3.

Introduction

Trehalase (trehalose-glucohydrolase, EC 3.2.1.28) hydrolyzes trehalose to yield two glucose molecules, and plays important roles in modulating the concentration of trehalose in living cells. Trehalose functions as a source of carbon energy and as a protector against various stress conditions, such as desiccation, osmotic stress, and oxidation (Elbein et al. 2003). In addition, this disaccharide is widely applied in the field of pharmaceuticals and the food and cosmetic industries (Argüelles 2000; Higashiyama 2002; Ohtake and Wang 2011), because trehalose induces mTOR-independent autophagy (Sarkar et al. 2007), inhibits the aggregation of pathogenetic factors of Alzheimer’s disease (AD), such as β-amyloid 40 and tau protein, and reduces their cytotoxicity in vitro (Liu et al. 2005; Kruger et al. 2012). Trehalases belonging to the glycoside hydrolase 37 (GH37), GH65, and GH15 families (Carbohydrate-Active enZyme database, CAZy, http://www.cazy.org/) have been noted. Recently, we found two archaeal trehalose-hydrolyzing enzymes that belong to the GH15 family, i.e., Tvn1315 and Ta0286, which originate from the thermoacidophilic archaea Thermoplasma volcanium and Thermoplasma acidophilum, respectively (Sakaguchi et al. 2015). The archaeal trehalases show higher similarity to GH15 family bacterial glucoamylase (GA) than GH37 and GH65 enzymes. Most GH15 family enzymes has additional domain(s) at the N-terminal and/or C-terminal sides of their own catalytic domains (CDs). The GH15 family bacterial GA [α-(1 → 4)-D-glucan glucohydrolase, EC 3.2.1.3] which acts on α-(1 → 4) glucosidic linkages to release β-D-glucose from the nonreducing end of substrates possesses an N-terminal domain with a β-sandwich structure (BD) and a C-terminal catalytic domain (CD) with an (α/α)6-barreled structure (Aleshin et al. 2003). Based on the sequence similarities to bacterial GA and models built using an Alphafold2 protein structure prediction method (Jumper et al. 2021, Fig. S1), archaeal GH15 trehalases may have an N-terminal BD and a C-terminal CD. A CD with an (α/α)6-barreled structure is a common feature of GH15, GH37 and GH65 trehalases.

There are a few reports on the function of the N-terminal BD in bacterial GH15 family enzymes. The N-terminal BD in bacterial GA from Thermoanaerobacter tengcongensis is involved in folding the CD, eliciting GA activity, and thermal stability (Li et al. 2014). We also demonstrated that parts of the N-terminal disordered loop region, especially the XaaXaaTrp sequence, in bacterial GA from Clostridium sp. play similar roles (Sakaguchi et al. 2017). In archaeal trehalase, N-terminal Tyr9 in Tvn1315 may be involved in the correct folding of putative N-terminal BD and/or C-terminal CD, and we suggested that correct BD folding may assist soluble and active CD folding (Sakaguchi et al. 2021).

Most carbohydrate-active enzymes possess one or more noncatalytic carbohydrate-binding modules (CBMs) linked to a CD (also known as the catalytic module (CM)). CBM classification is also provided in the CAZy database, which includes 97 CBM families at present, and the β-sandwich fold is the dominant fold among CBM structures (Hashimoto 2006). For example, GH15 Aspergillus GAs contain a starch binding domain (SBD) at the C-terminal side of the N-terminal CD with an (α/α)6-barrel. The SBD has been assigned to the CBM20 family and adopts a β-sandwich fold (approximately 100 residues long) with two carbohydrate-binding sites (Sorimachi et al. 1996; Janeček et al. 2011). Most CBMs usually have one or a few aromatic amino acids as the ligand-binding site (Boraston et al. 2004). Since there is no obvious sequence similarity in the BDs between fungal and bacterial GAs, as well as archaeal trehalases, the functions of archaeal BD in GH15 trehalases remain unclear except for the function of BD in CD folding, as described above.

The predicted BD of Tvn1315 shows no significant similarity to known CBMs, but the BDs of archaeal GH15 trehalases contain several conserved and nonconserved aromatic and hydrophobic residues in their primary sequences (Fig. 1), and these conserved residues may be involved in recognizing carbohydrates and/or in helping the CD folding into soluble form. Although the involvement of Tyr9 in the folding and formation of functional C-terminal CD has been noted (Sakaguchi et al. 2021), the possible roles of aromatic residues in BD are still poorly understood. In this study, to expand our knowledge on the functions of the N-terminal BD in binding to polysaccharides and the relationship between the N-terminal BD and C-terminal CD, we constructed site-directed BD mutants of Tvn1315 trehalase in which aromatic amino acid residues are replaced with Ala or other amino acid residue(s). Materials and methods

Chemical reagents

The sources of chemical reagents, Bacto tryptone and Bacto yeast extract were as previously described (Sakaguchi et al. 2021).

Expression plasmid construction

The Escherichia coli strain, media, and ampicillin were used as described previously (Sakaguchi et al. 2015). The expression plasmid vectors containing the trehalase gene (Tvn1315) with a C-terminal hexahistidine affinity tag (His-tag) for expression as a wild type in E. coli and for PCR as a template were used (Sakaguchi et al. 2021). Plasmids encoding full-length Tvn1315 with BD mutations were constructed by using the ODA-PCR method (Mutan-Super express Km; TaKaRa BIO, Table S1) (Sakaguchi et al. 2015) or the two-step overlapped-extension PCR method as follows: a first PCR was performed using the template described above and oligonucleotide primer set (Fw primer and Rv mutation primer or Fw mutation primer and Rv primer, Table S1-5) for elongation. Then, a second PCR was performed using an amplified 1st PCR product as a template and the Fw and Rv primers listed in Table S1. To construct plasmids encoding the putative BD (amino acid residues 1–213) of Tvn1315 containing a mutation, PCR was performed using an appropriate mutated template and the oligonucleotide primer set (Fw primers and BD Rv primers) listed in Table S1. The amplified second PCR products were treated with NdeI and SalI and then cloned and inserted into the pET21a vector (Novagen Inc., Madison, WI, USA) to give appropriate expression vectors with C-termnally His-tagged full-length and BD mutant genes. All primers used were purchased from Eurofins Genomics (Tokyo, Japan), and the nucleotide sequences were verified by DNA sequencing (Eurofins Genomics).

Protein expression, purification, activity measurement, and protein assay

Protein expression, purification, and protein assays were performed as described in a previous report (Sakaguchi et al. 2021). Briefly, proteins including BD, BD mutants, and full-length Tvn1315 with a BD mutation (referred to hereafter as Tvn1315 mutants) were expressed in E. coli BL21(DE3) without isopropyl β-D-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) induction, because protein expression without IPTG induction could substantially improve the quantities of soluble proteins recovered in the supernatants for Tvn1315 mutants and Sulfolobus trehalase gene products (Sakaguchi et al. 2021; Yuasa et al. 2018). Expressed proteins were purified as described previously to apparent homogeneity to generate practically a single band on an SDS‒PAGE gel after CBB R-250 staining (Sakaguchi et al. 2021). The fractions were dialyzed against 20 mM Tris–HCl and 0.5 M NaCl (pH 7.5), pooled at 4 °C, and used for subsequent experiments. Protein concentrations were measured with a microassay method (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) using bovine serum albumin as a standard protein.

The enzyme activities of full-length mutants were measured at 30 °C in McIlvaine buffer (pH 3.7) with 100 mM trehalose as a substrate (standard activity assay). McIlvaine buffer is convenient for performing activity measurements under continuous pH changes in the range of pH 2.5–8.0. The amount of glucose liberated from trehalose was estimated as described previously (Sakaguchi et al. 2015). For full-length Tvn1315 with trehalose hydrolyzing activity, 1 unit of enzyme was defined as the amount of the enzyme that hydrolyzed 1 µmol of trehalose in 1 min. Kinetic parameters, Vmax and KM values, for trehalose hydrolysis were estimated using the DeltaGraph version 6 graphics software package (Nihon Poladigital K.K.) for nonlinear regression. The apparent kcat values were estimated using a molecular mass of approximately 72.3 kDa.

Analysis of the binding of BD mutants to polysaccharides

The binding tests for BD or BD mutants to insoluble cellulose were performed and assessed using SDS‒PAGE with a separating gel (12% acrylamide concentration) as described previously (Honda et al. 2017).

pH dependence of activity and thermal stability of each mutant

The pH dependence of the activities of Tvn1315 or Tvn1315 mutants was measured using McIlvaine buffer (pH 3.0-pH 4.5) at 30 °C with 90 mM trehalose. The thermal stability of each mutant was examined as follows: Tvn1315 or Tvn1315 mutants were incubated for 1 h at 70 °C. After the mutants were cooled on ice, the remaining activity was measured under standard conditions (90 mM trehalose, pH 3.7, 30 °C).

Results

Substitutions of aromatic residues in the β-sandwiched N-terminal domain (BD)

To investigate the significance of aromatic residues in the BD of archaeal trehalase (Tvn1315) in carbohydrate binding, we performed individual mutagenesis of aromatic residues in the putative BD and constructed 31 BD mutants (Fig. 1) in which a target residue was replaced with alanine. These mutants as well as wild-type BD (BD-WT) were expressed and purified and are shown in Fig. 2a-2d. Most of the constructs tested were expressed and purified as efficiently as BD-WT, but BD-W43A, BD-F55A, BD-Y66A, BD-F107A, and BD-F142A were not recovered sufficiently, similar to the previous result obtained with BD-Y9A (Sakaguchi et al. 2021). The amounts of these mutants recovered in soluble fractions were significantly reduced compared to those observed in total cell homogenates (Fig. S2a-S2d), suggesting that these amino acid residues may be involved in BD folding into soluble structures. When leucine substitutions instead of alanine in BD-W43, BD-F55, BD-Y66, BD-F107, and BD-F142 were performed, the solubility of all the tested BD mutants except for BD-F55L was improved (Fig. 2e). The cellulose binding properties of alanine-substituted BD mutants are shown in Fig. 2f and Fig. S2e. These results indicate that the cellulose binding ability of every BD mutant recovered in a soluble form was not significantly different from that of BD-WT, indicating that any aromatic residue in the BD alone does not play a central role in cellulose binding. Substitutions of aromatic residues in the BD affect full-length enzymes

To investigate how substitutions of aromatic residues in the BD affect CD folding and trehalose hydrolyzing activity, we tested the expression of full-length proteins with a BD mutation in small-scale culture (25 ml) (Fig. 3a-3d). Most of the expressed full-length mutants could be recovered as soluble proteins and efficiently purified, but the levels of F67A, Y83A, and F173A were decreased to an even lower level than previously described Y9A (Fig. 3b-3d, Fig. S2f). The modeled structure suggests that Tyr83 may protrude toward the CD side, and this mutation may negatively affect CD folding, likely due to perturbations in surface interactions between the BD and the CD (see discussion below). In contrast, the side chains of Phe67 and Phe173 may be oriented into the inside of the BD based on the modeled structure (Fig. S3). The BD mutants at these residues are soluble as long as the putative BDs alone are expressed. It is unclear why these mutations in the BD cause the full-length protein to become insoluble. It is also interesting that W43A, F55A, Y66A, F107A, and F142A, which lead to insoluble BD without CD, could result in soluble protein in the full-length context and be purified efficiently. It has been reported that helix conformations inhibit protein aggregation and β-sheet formations lead to protein aggregation (Naeem et al. 2016; Abidi et al. 2017). To investigate the properties of the Tvn1315 mutants, we measured the trehalose-hydrolyzing activities under the standard assay conditions (Fig. 3e). The activities of some mutants were decreased. Among them, W43A showed only marginal activity, and the activities of Y17A, Y83A, and Y113A were reduced to less than approximately 20% that of WT. To further investigate how these amino acid residues (Tyr17, Trp43, Tyr83, and Tyr113) near the domain boundary may affect the characteristics of Tvn1315, these residues were mutated to amino acids other than alanine. We then expressed these mutants in a large-scale culture (250 ml or 500 ml cultures) and examined their properties.

Substitution of Trp43 in the full-length enzyme

To investigate the functional significance of the Trp residue at the 43rd position in the BD of Tvn1315, we constructed, expressed, and purified 6 full-length mutants, W43F, W43Y, W43L, W43I, W43V, and W43A (Fig. 3f). The amounts of purified proteins tended to decrease gradually from Trp (WT) to the Ala (W43A) mutant in the above order. This result indicates that a bulky aromatic side chain at this position is favored when soluble forms are constructed. Regarding trehalose-hydrolyzing activity, W43F exhibited similar activity, and W43Y showed 20% activity compared with WT, but other mutants were almost inactive, even though substantial amounts were recovered as soluble proteins (Fig. 3g). These results also emphasize that the aromatic side chain at the 43rd position is important for activity. Figure 4a compares the pH dependence of trehalase activity of W43F, W43Y, and W43A with that of WT at 30 °C. At pH 4.0 and pH 4.2, W43F was only approximately 70% as active as the WT, whereas at pH 3.5 or below, the activity was almost equal to that of the WT. It is likely that replacing an indole ring side chain with a phenyl ring lowered the pKa value of the catalytic residue, a general acid catalyst. As a result, the optimal pH for W43F activity was moved toward the acidic pH side to approximately pH 3.5. The same tendency was more pronounced after substitution with a phenol ring in W43Y, and its optimal pH was further lowered to pH 3.3; compared to the WT under the same conditions, the specific activity of W43Y was 66%. Assessments of amino acid residues in the CD interacting with Trp43 in the BD in full-length enzyme

GH15 family GAs possess five conserved regions (CRs) in the CD, and these CRs constitute the enzyme active sites, of which two Glu residues in CRs 3 and 5 have been identified as catalytic residues in GH15 family GAs (Ohnishi et al. 1994; Sierks et al. 1990; Frandsen et al. 1994; Aleshin et al. 2003). Since GH15 trehalases also possess similar regions, we tentatively assigned two Glu residues, Glu408 and Glu571, in CRs 3 and 5, respectively, to the catalytic residues of Tvn1315 (Sakaguchi et al. 2015, Fig. S1). According to the modeled structure, Trp43 is positioned at the boundary face between the BD and the CD, is close to the CR1 (Fig. S3a), and seems to form a hydrogen bond with the main chain carbonyl group of Pro263 in the CR1 of the CD (Fig. S4a). Therefore, we investigated three Tvn1315 mutants, the P263A mutant and the W43A/P263A and W43A/P263W double mutants. P263A was recovered in the soluble fraction and showed activity similar to WT. On the other hand, W43A/P263A and W43A/P263W mutants were almost insoluble, and those recovered in the soluble fraction were inactive. (Fig. S5a and S5b).

According to a prediction obtained by the Protein Interactions Calculator (PIC) server (http://pic.mbu.iisc.ernet.in/) using the modeled structure, Trp43 may interact with Arg280 in CR1 of the CD with a cation-π interaction (Fig. S4a). R280K and R280A were inactive at a pH range of 3.0 to 3.7 and exhibited a reduced level of activity at approximately pH 4.5 (Fig. 4b). The behaviors of R280 mutants were different from those of Trp43 mutants described above. It seems that an aromatic residue at position 43 is important in eliciting activity on the higher pH side, while an Arg residue at position 280 is essential for activity on the lower pH side.

Substitution of Tyr17 in the full-length enzyme

To investigate the functional significance of Tyr17 in the BD of Tvn1315, we constructed full-length mutants, Y17F, Y17L, and Y17A. These mutants were expressed and purified in sufficient yields, and the activity of Y17F was comparable to WT; however, those of Y17L and Y17A at pH 3.7 were decreased to approximately 10% and 60% of WT, respectively (Fig. 4c). These results indicate that the 17th position likely needs to be occupied by an aromatic side chain rather than a hydrophobic side chain. The reason why Y17A retained more activity than Y17L remains unknown at present. Y17L showed a reduced kcat value (Table 1), consistent with the result obtained for its specific activity. PIC predicts that Tyr17 may interact with Tyr342 in CR2 of the CD through aromatic-aromatic interactions (Fig. S4b). Y342F exhibited comparable activity to that of the WT, while Y342A was practically inactive (Fig. 4d), indicating that an aromatic side chain should be present at the 342nd position to exhibit sufficient catalytic activity.

Substitution of Tyr83 and Tyr113 in the full-length enzyme

We constructed, expressed, and purified full-length mutants (Y83F, Y83L, Y83A, Y113F, Y113L, and Y113A) to investigate how side chains at the 83rd and the 113th positions in Tvn1315 affect enzyme activity. For the Y83 mutants, Y83F activity was slightly higher than that of the WT, but Y83L and Y83A showed lower activity (Fig. 4e). Tyr83 must be replaced by an aromatic side chain to maintain full enzyme activity. PIC predicted that Tyr83 may interact through cation-π interactions with Arg243 in a linker helix between BD and CD (Fig. S4c). However, the activities of R243A, R243T, and R243K were similar to that of WT with a slight increase. These results suggest that the interaction between Tyr83 and Arg243 predicted by PIC may not occur. In addition, other factors should explain the low activities of Y83L and Y83A as well as the demand for an aromatic group at the 83rd position.

For the Y113 mutants, the Y113F and Y113L mutants exhibited similar activities to WT, but Y113A showed approximately 50% WT activity (Fig. 4f). Thus, a hydrophobic side chain at this position may better induce activity. Although the main chain of Tyr113 is predicted to interact with the OH group of Ser111 from the PIC prediction (Fig. S4d), S111A exhibited no effect on the catalytic activity (Fig. 4f).

Thermostability of Tyr17-, Trp43-, Tyr83-, and Tyr113-mutants

Fusing a CBM to a single domain protein and fusing a CBM to a foreign CD (CBM exchange) sometimes improve the catalytic efficiency and/or thermal stability of CD (Koizumi et al. 2009; Jang et al. 2019; Jun et al. 2009; Neeraja et al. 2010; Shoseyov et al. 2006). To investigate how the aromatic residues in BD contribute to the thermostability of full-length enzymes in regenerating the active site structure after heat treatment at 70 °C, we tested the heat stability of several mutants. As shown in Fig. 5, Phe mutants (Y17F, W43F, Y83F, Y113F) maintain some activity after heat treatment, whereas the activity of Leu mutants except Y83L decreased. Discussion

Archaeal GH15 trehalases likely consist of a BD (or CBM) and a CD based on the crystal structure of prokaryotic GH15 family member enzymes (Aleshin et al. 2003; Mizuno et al. 2004) and on a structure model predicted by the AlphaFold2 method.

CBMs of carbohydrate-metabolizing enzymes are divided into the following types: Type A, type B, and type C. Type A CBMs bind insoluble crystalline polysaccharides on flat binding faces, type B CBMs recognize an internal region of the oligosaccharide unit of polysaccharides on cleft-shaped binding sites, and type C CBMs typically recognize terminal regions of glycans on pocket-typed sites (Boraston et al. 2004; Gilbert et al. 2013). The BD of archaeal trehalase may belong to type A CBM because it binds to insoluble crystalline β-glucans such as cellulose and chitin but not to soluble saccharides (Sakaguchi et al. 2021).

Several aromatic residues are found at the surface of a modeled structure; thus, we hypothesized that these residues may contain a part of the binding site for insoluble saccharides because the involvement of aromatic residues in carbohydrate binding has been suggested for several CBMs (Boraston et al. 2004). This consideration led us to investigate the possible involvement of aromatic groups of the Tvn1315 BD in insoluble saccharide binding. We examined the effect of individually substituting aromatic residue to Ala, but none of the single amino acid substitutions led to a significant loss of saccharide binding activity. The aromatic side chain may not be involved in insoluble saccharide binding, or multiple residues may be involved in cellulose binding in a cooperative manner. For instance, the binding of cellulose to the type-A CBM (CBM63) (Georgelis et al. 2012) of bacterial expansin BsEXLX1 from Bacillus subtilis, which can bind and loosen the plant cell wall, was suggested to be mediated through three aromatic residues, Trp125, Trp126, and Tyr157, in its D2 domain. The cellulose binding abilities of the W125A and W126A mutants, Y157A and the triple mutant were reduced to half, 80% and 30% that of WT, respectively (Georgelis et al. 2011).

The BD-mutants were insoluble when aromatic residues, such as Trp43, Tyr66, Phe107, and Phe142, were substituted by Ala but were soluble when they were replaced by Leu. These residues are located inside the BD, and hydrophobic effects may be sufficient for maintaining correct folding, as previously described (Sakaguchi et al. 2021).

When BD-mutants were connected to the CD to generate full-length Tvn1315 proteins with mutated BD, the folding, catalytic properties, and stability were found to be significantly affected in several aromatic residue-mutants.

There are many reports that CBM truncation from whole carbohydrate hydrolyzing enzymes results in significant decreases in the catalytic activity of the enzymes. In addition, the presence of CBMs is important for obtaining catalytic activity of CDs. To date, reports on amino acid residues in CBM that affect the catalytic activity of these enzymes have been limited. Kim et al. (2016) reported that a single substitution of a conserved Trp residue (Trp483) in CBM3c of Clostridium cellulovorans EngZ, a GH9 endoglucanase (Cel9 enzyme), resulted in a reduction in the catalytic activity of EngZ without affecting substrate binding ability. The CBM3c that binds to the flat surface of crystalline cellulose belongs to the type A CBM and is attached to the C-terminal side of the EngZ CD. The Trp483 residue is well conserved in other Cel9 enzymes and is located close to the CD surface based on the modeled structure of EngZ. Kim et al. (2016) also pointed out that Trp483 has an important role in moving active sites to the right location. In Clostridium sp. GA (CGA), a GH15 family bacterial GA, the N-terminal 26XaaXaaTrp28 sequence in the N-terminal domain of CGA is important for CGA activity, and the Trp residue is responsible for interactions with an element of the active site (CR1) in GA (Sakaguchi et al. 2017).

Trp43 in the BD of Tvn1315 is also positioned at the boundary face between the BD and CD and is well conserved among GH15 trehalases (Fig. S3a, S3b, and Fig. 1). Trp43 may form a hydrogen bond with the main chain carbonyl group of Pro263 in CR1 of CD (Fig. S4a). Based on the PIC prediction obtained with the modeled structure, Trp43 may also interact with Arg280 in CR1 of CD with a cation-π interaction (Fig. S4a). P263A retained activity similar to that of WT, indicating that the presence of Pro at the 263rd position is not essential for the activity under the standard condition. The introduction of the Trp-Pro and Ala-Trp pair in the reverse direction in the W43P/P263W and W43A/P263W mutants did not recover trehalase activity. The W43/P263 interaction, if it occurs, did not clearly determine the behaviors of Trp43 mutants.

W43F and W43Y mutants retained significant activity at lower pH ranges, but the activities were rapidly lowered toward higher pH ranges. In contrast, R280K and R280A exhibited low but significant activity at approximately pH 4.5. An aromatic residue at position 43 may be important in eliciting activity on the higher pH side, while an Arg residue at position 280 is essential for activity on the lower pH side. Mutation at one of the Trp43-Arg280 pair may cause the necessary interactions between the two residues to be lost and could lead to an alteration in the BD-CD interaction, which may result in a profound change in the pH dependence of trehalase activity. According to the modeled structure, the Arg280 side chain is oriented outside of the putative active center. Therefore, the guanidium side chain may not interact with the catalytic site directly. The loss of activity of Arg280 mutants at a lower pH range may result from unidentified indirect interactions with catalytic sites, probably with catalytic bases. The guanidium side chain at the 280th position seems to play an important role in maintaining the active site from the external side. For Trp43, the indole ring may be substituted by a phenyl ring, but the substitution caused a slight reduction in trehalase activity at higher pH values. The reduced bulkiness and/or the absence of N atom in the phenyl group may have led to the slightly modified interaction with the active site, which resulted in a slightly modified pH profile. Since the amino acid residue that interacts with the side chain at the 43rd position remains unknown at present, additional studies are needed to clarify the significance of substitutions at this position.

Interestingly, the KM values of the W43 mutants for trehalose were almost constant at acidic pH values, whereas those of Tvn1315WT gradually decreased from pH 3.3 to pH 3.7. This suggests that in the WT enzyme, environmental pH conditions may lead to changes in the pKa value of some acidic residues in the active site, which could subsequently affect substrate recognition. Since these changes were not observed in W43 mutants, the interaction network in the active site may be somehow perturbed when the indole ring is substituted as described above.

Notably, Tyr17 in the BD and Tyr342 in the CD had to be substituted by aromatic residues for their mutants to retain enzyme activity similar to the WT enzyme. This result suggests that an aromatic-aromatic interaction between the 17th and 342rd positions occurs, as PIC analysis predicts, and that the interaction may be important for maintaining trehalase activity.

The present study revealed that several aromatic residues in the BD of GH15 trehalase may help the BD and/or the CD fold properly and maintain the active site structure of the CD. In particular, interactions between Trp43 in the BD with residues in the CD may be important, and perturbations of these interactions may profoundly affect the catalytic properties of the enzyme. Further studies on the interactions between the BD and the CD in eliciting and modulating enzyme activity are needed to extend our knowledge on the GH15 family as well as carbohydrate-active other family enzymes.
