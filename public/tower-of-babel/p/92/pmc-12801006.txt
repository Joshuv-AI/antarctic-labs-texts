Technique for Creating a Bone–Hamstring Tendon–Bone Graft for Anterior Cruciate Ligament Reconstruction
Authors: Vinh Pham Dinh, Toan Nguyen Huy, Loi Do Van, Anh Tang Ha Nam, Quyen Nguyen Quang Ton
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHamstring tendon autograft are commonly used in anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction; however, they are classified as soft-tissue grafts, which exhibit slower graft-bone integration compared with bone-patellar tendon-bone grafts. To address this, we describe a technique to incorporate a bone block at the tibial end of the hamstring tendon graft, with the aim of enhancing graft integration and minimizing tunnel widening.

In recent years, there has been a notable increase in the incidence of anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injury, as a result of the advancements in sports,1 leading to the popularity of ACL reconstruction surgery. This surgical intervention helps patients return to preinjury activities and mitigates the risk of developing knee osteoarthritis.2 However, choosing the optimal graft for operation has proven to be challenging and controversial. The ideal graft requires rapid and complete integration inside the tunnels, long-term preservation of its viscoelastic properties, minimal morbidity at the donor site, adequate dimensions in terms of length and diameter, minimal impact on the extensor mechanism, absence of rejection or disease transmission risks, cost-effectiveness, and easy accessibility.

Autogenous hamstring tendon bone grafting was reported by Zein et al.3 in 2017 and Zhou et al.4 in 2021. However, both authors designed a bone plug in one side. In this Technical Note, we describe a way to create bone–hamstring tendon–bone autograft.

Surgical Technique

A 4-cm oblique incision is made proximal to the pes anserinus insertion (Table 1). The sartorial fascia is incised, and the semitendinosus and gracilis tendons are identified, released, and harvested using an open tendon stripper (Table 2). The tibial insertion site of the hamstring tendons is exposed. Using an osteotome, a bone block measuring approximately 12 × 5 × 3 mm is harvested en bloc with the attached tendons (Fig 1). The bone portion is divided apart equally with connected gracilis and semitendinosus tendon (Fig 2).

Discussion

Several grafts are being used in ACL reconstructive surgery: bone-patellar tendon-bone, quadriceps tendon bone, and hamstring tendon graft. There are multiple factors involved in the selection of autografts,5 with the tissue-bone healing process within the tunnel being considered as the foremost significant aspect. The process of tendon-to-bone healing after surgery typically involves 4 phases, namely an initial inflammatory phase, a proliferative phase, a remodeling phase, and ultimately a mature phase. The outcome of this process is significantly influenced by factors such as age, surgical method, graft materials, tunnel condition, and weight-bearing activity. The duration of the tendon-to-bone healing process typically spans a period of approximately 6 to 9 months, impacting the daily activities of those undergoing ACL reconstruction. Therefore, in order to facilitate the recovery, tendon-bone grafts have been implemented, and yielded favorable clinical outcomes.

Bone-patellar tendon-bone or quadriceps tendon-bone grafts have been commonly chosen because they are an appropriate length and diameter comparable with the human ACL and possess histologic characteristics similar to human ACL. However, bone-patellar tendon-bone graft has been associated with various disadvantages, including the occurrence of anterior knee pain, high risk of patellar fracture and tendonitis, the development of joint stiffness, and the presence of a large cicatrix. Quadricep tendon-bone also exhibits drawbacks that impact knee extensor mechanism and scarring at the anterior knee. Therefore, we believe that the use of a bone–tendon–bone hamstring autograft would resolve the aforementioned issues. In addition, this method accelerates the recovery process within the bone tunnel through bone-to-bone healing mechanism. This method is proposed to be more efficient compared with the traditional hamstring graft, which relies on tendon-bone healing because this also reduces the risk of widening the bone tunnel during ACL reconstruction.6 More studies are needed to evaluate clinical outcomes and biomechanical performance. However, this method possesses uncommon risks such as tibial fracture. We recommend refraining from applying this technique for patients who have not yet attained bone maturity because bone-tendon graft harvesting may potentially damage the cartilaginous joints.
