Current use of conventional tobacco and novel products among students aged between 13 and 15 years old in Panama nationwide, Bocas del Toro health region, and Ngäbe Buglé and Guna Yala indigenous health regions: Global Youth Tobacco Survey, 2023
Authors: Hedley Knewjen Quintana, Flavio Figueroa, Cecilio Niño, Roger Montenegro, Fulvia Bajura, Reina Roa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Panama has the lowest overall prevalence of tobacco use in the Americas. However, rates were higher in 3 health areas compared with the national average. Since most people who smoke started during adolescence, this population must be a target of health interventions. This study assesses the current tobacco use prevalence in Bocas del Toro, Ngäbe-Buglé, Guna Yala and nationwide. The national prevalence of any tobacco product consumption was 8.5% (95% confidence intervals: 6.9%–10.6%). Ngäbe-Buglé health region had the largest prevalence of any current tobacco use 16.9% (95% confidence intervals: 14.2%–19.9%), while both in Bocas del Toro and in Guna Yala was 10.9%. There is still a need for more indigenous-specific data to improve tobacco control and reduce tobacco and novel products use among vulnerable populations.

1. Introduction

Tobacco use among adolescents remains a critical public health challenge globally, with profound implications for long-term health outcomes. Early initiation of tobacco consumption significantly increases the risk of lifelong addiction and tobacco-related morbidity[1,2]

While global trends show mixed progress in reducing tobacco use, Panama has emerged as a notable success story in the Americas, achieving the region’s lowest adult smoking prevalence through comprehensive tobacco control measures.[3] Tobacco control achievements in Panama stem from early adoption of World Health Organization Framework Convention on Tobacco Control measures, as the Law 13/2008 that implemented smoke-free environments, advertising bans, and graphic health warnings.[4] Furthermore, Law 315/2022 banned sales and advertisement of novel products (e-cigarettes and heated tobacco products), as well as their use where conventional products were also banned.[5] Later in July 2023, the Executive Decree 24/2023 was enacted to formalize and clarify the implementation of Law. A year later, Law 315/2022 was declared unconstitutional; however, since its inception, it was never properly implemented: advertisement and sales of novel products were available nationwide while data for this study was collected.

Analysis of 34 countries implementing the Global Youth Tobacco Survey (GYTS) between 2012 and 2020 reveals that more than 60% of sites showed no significant change in tobacco use rates. Among Latin American countries included in comprehensive studies, Argentina, Nicaragua, and Peru showed no significant changes in overall tobacco use, while Paraguay demonstrated a concerning increase of 1.8 percentage points.[6]

Emerging evidence suggests significant disparities in tobacco use patterns in adults between national prevalence of current consumptions and 3 health regions: Bocas del Toro and the indigenous regions of Guna Yala and Ngäbe Buglé.[7] In a previous report, the national prevalence of current tobacco products consumption of students aged between 13 and 15 years old was higher than the Guna Yala indigenous region Guna Yala.[8]

The aim of this analysis is to describe the prevalence of current conventional tobacco and novel products use in students aged between 13- and 15-years students in Panama nationwide and in Bocas del Toro health region, and Ngäbe Buglé and Guna Yala indigenous health regions. According to data from the National Health Survey of Panama 2019 (ENSPA in Spanish language), these 3 health regions have the highest prevalences of tobacco consumption in adults aged 15 years or more in Panama.[9]

2. Materials and methods

We assessed tobacco usage in adolescents in Panama based on the GYTS. The GYTS provides a global standard for monitoring youth tobacco to track key tobacco control indicators. It is a school-based survey of secondary school students aged 13 to 15 years, using a standardized protocol and a Spanish version of the adapted questionnaire.[10] In 2023, the GYTS was undertaken in Panama using a 3-stage cluster sample design to produce a nationally representative sample, as well as representativeness for Bocas del Toro and Ngäbe-Buglé Indigenous Health Region among school grades 8th to 10th where most students between aged 13 to 15 years are enrolled. We used the GYTS protocol and questionnaire to undertake a census of students aged 13 to 15 years (n = 1164 students) in Guna Yala Indigenous Health Region from 102 schools which are included in the national sample frame (for Guna Yala, each student weights 1). While most students in Guna Yala schools are from the Guna community, 1 school in Puerto Obaldía (in the eastern part of the territory) includes a significant proportion of nonindigenous students. To maintain appropriate ethical standards and confidentiality, we included all 13- to 15- year- old students in the survey and did not distinguish based on school or indigenous status. It was conducted in Spanish with 5761 students from 231 schools throughout Panama. Out of them, 1691 participants study in 75 Bocas del Toro schools; and 1354 participants study in 29 Ngäbe-Buglé schools. All schools with students aged 13 to 15 years were included in the sampling frame. Both the Guna Yala and Ngäbe-Buglé versions of the GYTS were administered in Spanish, but questions were clarified in their respective indigenous language whether necessary.

2.1. Geography of Bocas del Toro

Bocas del Toro is a northwestern province of Panama. It has an area of 4657 km2 and a population of 159,228 persons in 2023.[11] Out of them, 5822 were students enrolled in grades 8th to 10th (Statistics provided by the Ministry of Education Planning directorate).

Bocas del Toro comprises the Bocas del Toro Archipelago, Almirante Bay, The Chiriquí Lagoon, and nearby continental territory. The Bocas del Toro City or Town is its capital, placed in Colon Island[11] part of the Archipelago. Bocas del Toro province (or Health Region) has 4 districts: Changuinola, Almirante, Bocas del Toro, and Chiriquí Grande (see Fig. 1, source[11]).

Second level division of Bocas del Toro Health Region. Source: Análisis de Situación de Salud de Bocas del Toro. Ministerio de Salud.[11]

Spanish language is the official language; however English is also spoken. Ngäbe-Buglé is mainly spoken by indigenous population. Persons living in Changuinola, Almirante and Chiriquí Grande are indigenous people who have banana crop for exporting to United States and Europe. People living in Bocas del Toro Archipelago are mainly Latino and Afro-Panamanian which its economy is based on fishing and tourism.

In December 2020, a Naso Tjër Di Indigenous District was installed, but for administrative purposes, it belongs to Changuinola District.

Bocas del Toro is the health region with the top current tobacco use prevalence in adults aged fifteen years old or more.[9]

2.2. Geography of Ngäbe-Buglé

Ngäbe-Buglé Indigenous Health Region has an area of 6968 km². It borders with the Caribbean Sea in the north, the eastern part of Chiriqui and Veraguas in the South, Veraguas in the east and with Bocas del Toro and Chiriquí in the west (Fig. 2, source[12]). The estimated population in 2024 was 247,990 persons.

Second level division of Ngäbe-Buglé Health Region. Source: Analisis de Situación de Salud de Ngäbe-Buglé. Ministerio de Salud.[12]

This indigenous territory has the second to the top highest current tobacco products consumption in Panama.[9]

Guna Yala has already been described in previous publication.[8] In brief, Guna Yala is an indigenous territory in northeast Panama, most of its population belongs to the Guna indigenous community.

2.3. Indicators

Key tobacco indicators were constructed using core questions from the Panamanian version of the GYTS questionnaire.[10] Selected tobacco control indicators from the tobacco “use” and “access and availability” components of the 2015 GYTS Analysis and Reporting Package were used to generate the following indicators.

The weekly money to spare was asked in the following ranks: in general, I lack any money to spare; US$0.50 or less, US$0.51 to US$1.00, US$1.01 to US$1.00, US$2.01 to US$3.00, US$3.01 to US$4.00, US$4.01 to US$10.00, and more than US$10.00. These ranks were grouped in tertiles as a proxy for socioeconomic status.

2.4. Statistical analysis

The prevalences of tobacco product groups, as well as specific products were estimated respondents from the studied health regions and nationwide, after excluding missing data, taking account of complex sampling weights and uncertainty, 95% confidence intervals (CI). Prevalences were compared using chi-square tests with Rao and Scott correction for complex sampling.

Unconditional crude and adjusted logistic regression models predicting specific tobacco products consumption were assessed for age, sex, weekly money to spare and health region taking into account complex sampling design. Observations with missing data were excluded.

Analyses were performed using R version 4.3.3 (Angel Food Cake; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Complex sample analyses were performed using the survey package 4.4.2.

2.5. Ethic statement

The study was approved by Hospital del Niño, José Renán Esquivel Institutional Review Bord stated on DM-N-468. Prior to conducting the application of the survey, a notification note was sent to the parents of students who can potentially participate in the study. They were allowed to ask questions and deny participation of the study for each potential student.

These students were approached by the surveyors responsible for each of the classrooms selected in each school participating in the study in our country. Prior to conducting the self-administered surveys, the interviewer will orally give information about the study, and they will have the opportunity to ask questions and express any concerns. Potential participants were allowed to deny their participation at this time. After the administration of the survey, it is impossible to identify individual participants or in which school he/she is enrolled.

4. Discussion

The national prevalence of tobacco use was 8.5%. In this version of the GYTS, the consumption of heated tobacco was queried for the first time, contributing to tobacco products. In the prioritized health regions prevalences of heated tobacco, as well as conventional products, were higher than the rest of the country.

We earlier reported, using the GYTS methodology, that indigenous students from Guna Yala had a higher prevalence of current tobacco products consumption than those in the rest of country,[8] the current report shows that such figures have not changed since the last time reported. The Bocas del Toro health region prevalence for each tobacco product was similar to Guna Yala and lower than Ngäbe Buglé indigenous health region. There are scarce reports on the current consumption of tobacco products by indigenous teens in other Latin American countries. Although there are few reports on Latin American indigenous teens, our findings in Ngäbe Buglé indigenous health regions are like reports from Chileans[19] and those from Bocas del Toro and Guna Yala are similar to current figures from Guatemalan teens.[20]

In contrast to tobacco products, current consumption of electronic cigarettes heads towards the opposite direction. The prevalence of electronic cigarette consumption was the lowest in Guna Yala and Ngäbe (2.5% and 3.5%) indigenous health regions, it increased to 5% in the Bocas del Toro health region and 8.5% nationwide. There are few reports both for Latin American teens as well as Indigenous teens living there. In Panama, a Law in the mid 2023 was approved to ban e-cigarette sales, it was later repealed. Decree 34/2025 implements a e-cigarette, heated tobacco and alike products ban on advertisement and sales to minors has begun to be implemented a year after the data collection.

4.1. Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, GYTS is a cross-sectional study design and causality should not be inferred. Secondly, data of this study were self-reported; however, students give their responses with a high level of confidentiality as required by the GYTS protocol. Socioeconomic status variables were not assessed, but the weekly money to spare was used as a proxy. In the other hand, GYTS is an international and robust methodology to assess several key indicators of tobacco control.

5. Conclusion

Enforcement of the Law 13/2008 and Decree 34/2025 that bans on tobacco and novel products are needed to decrease consumption nationwide, with emphasis in Bocas del Toro and indigenous areas.[4,21]
