Validity and reliability evaluation of the Chinese version of the attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder stigma questionnaire
Authors: Xixi Yang, Lixian Chen, Luyao Chi, Yan Wang, Tao-Hsin Tung, Dongjun Bi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPsychological problems among children and adolescents are increasing, and people with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) face varying degrees of stigma as they grow up. Such perceptions of stigma can prevent them from seeking psychological help and adhering to treatment. However, a standardized instrument for assessing ADHD-related stigma in Chinese populations is lacking. This study assessed the validity and reliability of a Chinese version of the Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder Stigma Questionnaire (ASQ).MethodsA total of 508 adolescents with ADHD, mostly aged 11–13 years (mean age = 13.68 ± 2.12 years), participated in this study. Among them, 51.8% were junior high school students, and the distribution of male and female participants was relatively balanced. Data were collected from two Chinese hospitals between September 2023 and September 2024. The ASQ was translated and culturally adapted into Simplified Chinese. Psychometric evaluation included exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses, assessment of internal consistency, test–retest reliability, and examination of content and construct validity.ResultsConfirmatory factor analysis supported a three-factor structure for the study, demonstrating good model fit ( \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\chi^2$\end{document}χ2/df = 1.325; goodness-of-fit index (GFI) = 0.900; comparative fit index (CFI) = 0.977; root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) = 0.036). Convergent validity was supported by average variance extracted values above 0.50 and composite reliability values above 0.70. The scale showed excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.951) and strong test–retest reliability (r = 0.958).ConclusionsThe Chinese version of the ASQ is a valid and reliable measurement tool that can be used for assessing ADHD-related stigma in Chinese adolescents. This survey tool can assist healthcare professionals in this field in identifying and addressing stigma more effectively, thereby improving treatment adherence.

Introduction

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a common chronic disorder of neurodevelopment with characteristic onset in childhood, which can lead to cognitive, behavioral, emotional, and social disorders (Posner, Polanczyk & Sonuga-Barke, 2020). ADHD diagnosis involves observing symptoms related to inattention or hyperactivity–impulsivity (or both), while considering symptom duration and contexts in which symptoms manifest, and evidence of functional impairment. Symptoms must be observed in at least two distinct settings (e.g., home, school, workplace, or social environments) and should not be confined to a single context (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). ADHD has a largely negative impact on the academic, occupational, and social lives of those living with it (May et al., 2023). The global prevalence of ADHD in school-aged children is 5–7% (Cortese et al., 2022). In adulthood, symptoms persist in approximately 60% of people with ADHD (Biederman et al., 2012). In 2022, an epidemiological study showed that the prevalence of mental disorders in children and adolescents in China was 17.5%, with an ADHD prevalence of 6.4%. Furthermore, the most common comorbid mental disorders associated with ADHD include anxiety disorders (4.7%), oppositional defiant disorder (3.6%), and major depressive disorder (2.0%) (Li et al., 2022).

Children diagnosed with ADHD may experience stigma associated with mental illness (Mueller et al., 2012). Moreover, as ADHD symptoms are persistent, they may lead to academic failure, strained relationships with family members and peers, and low self-esteem (Meza et al., 2019). Individuals living with ADHD exhibit higher rates of substance abuse, violent behavior, suicide, and automobile accidents in adulthood compared with the general population (Lebowitz, 2016; Schoeman & Voges, 2022). These elevated risks contribute to the experience of stigma, social isolation, and discrimination among individuals with ADHD as they grow up (Miller, 2020). Other studies suggest that people with ADHD face social discrimination throughout their lives (Canu et al., 2008; Mueller et al., 2006).

A systematic review found that stigma hinders children and adolescents from seeking mental health care and may be harmful to their overall physical and mental health and quality of life (Lebowitz, 2016).

A high level of stigma increases the risk of mental illness and reduces treatment adherence among children and adolescents (Kaushik, Kostaki & Kyriakopoulos, 2016). Despite increased public awareness of mental health, stigma has not decreased (O’Connor et al., 2022). Misconceptions about ADHD can prevent people from seeking psychological help and may exacerbate the effects of stigma (Lau et al., 2016).

Cultural differences should be considered when examining stigma in Eastern and Western populations (Abdullah & Brown, 2011). Most Asian cultures share common values, including adherence to social norms, emotional self-regulation, a collectivist orientation, and the pursuit of family recognition and filial piety through personal achievements. Consequently, conditions that deviate from these norms—including mental illness—are often perceived as sources of shame and are subject to stigmatization (Yang et al., 2007). A systematic review has shown that individuals of Asian descent with mental illness are often perceived as dangerous and aggressive. Social acceptance of mental illness in Asian societies remains relatively low, and stigma experienced by family members is prevalent (Zhang et al., 2019). Individuals with mental illness in Asian societies may face challenges in attaining culturally valued achievements, such as academic and professional success. Their inability to achieve familial recognition through personal accomplishments may further contribute to experiences of stigmatization (Abdullah & Brown, 2011). Children and adolescents with ADHD often exhibit poor academic performance due to difficulties in sustaining attention, as well as impairments across social, physical, and emotional functioning, which may also contribute to stigma (American Psychiatric Association, 2022). In addition to people with ADHD, their parents also experience varying degrees of stigma (Jelinkova et al., 2024). This dual impact underscores the importance of examining stigma not merely as an individual experience but as a systemic challenge affecting family ecosystems. The Universal Stigma Scale has been used to measure adolescent stigma (Dikeç et al., 2021). However, no stigma scale specific to people with ADHD is available in China, and research examining stigma in individuals with ADHD is lacking.

Kellison et al. (2010) assessed social disapproval associated with ADHD using the Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder Stigma Questionnaire (ASQ). In their study, a sample of 301 adolescents living in the community (including both high-risk and low-risk individuals with ADHD) was used, and the questionnaire supported a three-factor structure, with a total Cronbach’s α of 0.93. Rim, Jang & Park (2018) translated the scale into Korean, and 673 adults in South Korea completed an online questionnaire; the Cronbach’s α for this 26-item scale was 0.940, supporting a three-factor structure. Kıransal & Kaya (2022) created a Turkish version and surveyed a sample of 300 teachers; the overall Cronbach’s α was 0.92, indicating high internal consistency, and the unifactorial structure demonstrated cultural compatibility with the Turkish context, with factor loadings ranging from 0.435 to 0.735. Therefore, we adapted this scale, investigated its psychometric properties, and verified its applicability in China for assessing ADHD-related stigma.

Materials and Methods

Participants

This study employed a convenience sampling method to recruit adolescent–caregiver dyads. Adolescents aged 11–19 years with a formal diagnosis of ADHD and their primary caregivers were recruited from the outpatient clinics of the Departments of Child Psychiatry/Psychology at Yuhuan Second People’s Hospital and Taizhou Rehabilitation Hospital. All adolescent participants were diagnosed by attending physicians in accordance with the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) criteria (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). The diagnostic assessment comprised a semi-structured interview, a thorough review of medical history, and the parent-reported Swanson, Nolan, and Pelham Rating Scale (SNAP-IV). Consistent with DSM-5 age-specific guidelines, individuals aged 11–16 were required to meet at least six symptom criteria of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity, while those aged 17 and above were required to exhibit at least five. All patients were able to communicate effectively. A primary caregiver was defined as a family member who had been providing daily care for the adolescent for more than 6 months and was capable of effective communication.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: (1) Adolescents aged 11–19 years with a physician-confirmed DSM-5 diagnosis of ADHD; (2) presence of a primary caregiver as defined above; and (3) both the adolescent and caregiver having provided informed assent/consent.

Exclusion criteria: Adolescents were excluded if they: (1) had significant cognitive impairments that would preclude valid communication or assessment; (2) had ambiguous diagnostic status upon independent review of medical records by the research team; or (3) if the adolescent or caregiver declined to participate.

Data collection was conducted from September 2023 to September 2024 using paper-based questionnaires administered in face-to-face settings. Two qualified researchers supervised all assessments—a principal investigator with over 10 years of clinical experience in mental health and psychology, and a licensed psychotherapist experienced in psychological assessment. Uncertainties regarding scale items were clarified immediately to minimize missing responses. The adolescents and their caregivers completed the demographic information questionnaire, and the adolescents completed the stigma questionnaires.

Based on the sample size recommendation, the minimum sample size for Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) was estimated at 130–260 (Costello & Osborne, 2005; Kline, 2016), and for Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA), a minimum sample size of 200 is recommended for SEM to ensure statistical adequacy (Cai et al., 2024). A total of 560 questionnaires were distributed, and after excluding 23 responses with missing data, four with outlier values and 25 incomplete responses, 508 were included in the study. Fifty research subjects were included in the current study to assess retest reliability, and each participant completed the same questionnaire 2 weeks apart. Raw scores from the scales were used for analysis.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Yuhuan Second People’s Hospital (NO 2307) and Taizhou Rehabilitation Hospital (No. K20240510). We explained the purpose and the meaning of the investigation to the adolescents and their caregivers, and the questionnaire was then provided upon obtaining informed consent from the research subjects. Adolescent participants and their caregivers signed the informed consent forms.

Research tools

General demographic information on ADHD

The questionnaire covers key demographic characteristics of the ADHD participants, including sex, age, and current educational level, as well as caregiver-related family information such as age of the primary caregiver, place of residence, educational attainment, and household average monthly income per capita.

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder stigma questionnaire

The original questionnaire was developed by Kellison et al. (2010) to assess the stigma experienced by people diagnosed with ADHD. The 26-item scale, adapted from the 40-item HIV stigma scale, includes three dimensions: disclosure concerns, negative self-image, and concern about public attitudes. Items were rated on a four-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 = “do not agree at all” to 4 = “fully agree.” The total possible score was 104, with higher scores indicating a more pronounced sense of shame. The scale has good reliability and validity.

The ASQ used in this study was completed by adolescents with ADHD themselves, not by their caregivers. This self-report approach ensured that we captured the subjective experiences of stigma directly from the affected individuals. The general demographic questionnaire, however, was completed by both adolescents with ADHD and their caregivers, collecting relevant background information from each of their respective perspectives.

Translation process

First, we obtained the authorization of Dr. Regina Bussing, author of the original 2010 study. The original scale (Kellison et al., 2010) was standardized according to the Brislin translation forward–backward model (Jones et al., 2001). It was independently translated from English to Chinese by a native Chinese speaker with a master’s degree in psychology, and by a master’s degree nursing student. Next, research team members with extensive experience in bilingual translation conducted a comparative analysis of the two versions. They identified issues pertaining to Chinese wording and accuracy, and discussed them with the two translators involved to reach a consensus. In the back-translation phase, the consensus version was independently back-translated into English by a Doctor of English and a Doctor of Nursing. The forward and back translations were carefully analyzed and reconciled by the research team, leading to a final Chinese version of the ASQ that was both accurate and easily understood.

Cultural adaptation

To address cultural discrepancies, the translated version was reviewed and finalized in consultation with specialists in humanities medicine from the hospital. The final version was then sent to the author for confirmation and approval.

The final Chinese version and original scale items were reviewed by eight experts, including three psychiatrists, two psychiatric nurses, one chief physician specializing in pediatric medicine, one nursing manager, and a nursing educator. They evaluated the Chinese version for conceptual, semantic, and content equivalence relative to the original scale. Minor revisions were made to the wording. For example, when modifying the fourth item, it was translated as “People with ADHD will lose their jobs when the employer finds out.” The experts suggested that since this scale was investigated in a sample of adolescents living with ADHD, it would be more appropriate to change it to “People with ADHD will lose some learning or job opportunities because they are found to have the disorder.” Moreover, they believed that the meaning of item 10 of the back-translated version, “Most people think the reputation for people with ADHD is damaged,” and “reputation” was one-sided and suggested its deletion. They also thought that “blame” in the 17th item of the back-translation (“People with ADHD worry that others may blame or judge them for having ADHD”) was not appropriate and was inconsistent with the original expression and therefore recommended its removal.

Data analysis

Two people checked the input data, and the IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0, Mplus Demo Editor 8, and Amos 24.0 statistical software were used for the data analysis.

Descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, range, kurtosis, and skewness) were computed, and multivariate normality was verified using the procedure in Mardia (1970), where a criterion of p > 0.05 was applied. Specifically, the skewness values were within the range of −3 to +3, and the kurtosis values fell between −10 and +10 (Kline, 2016). Item analysis was performed using both the correlation coefficient and critical ratio methods. Test–retest reliability and Cronbach’s α were used to test reliability. Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach’s α. Values that were equal to or exceeded 0.70 were regarded as meeting satisfactory criteria (Streiner, 2003). Content validity was considered adequate if the content validity index (CVI) exceeded 0.8 (Lee, Kwon & Seo, 2021). The total sample (n = 508) was randomly split into two subsamples using a random number generator. One subsample (n = 254) was designated for EFA, whereas the other (n = 254) was reserved for CFA. An EFA was performed on the initial sample (n = 254) to determine its underlying factor structure. Prior to the EFA, the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy was calculated with thresholds set at >0.6 for KMO and >0.5 for factor loadings (Costello & Osborne, 2005). In the EFA, principal axis factoring was employed as the extraction method to allow for correlation among the factors coupled with an oblique rotation (specifically, Oblimin) and scree plots for exploratory factor analysis (Kline, 2016). The correlation matrix demonstrated multiple associations, with coefficients surpassing the 0.30 threshold (Tabachnick & Fidell, 2013). Parallel analysis was also conducted to determine the number of factors to retain by comparing the observed sample eigenvalues with the mean (or 95th percentile) eigenvalues from parallel analysis (Zwick & Velicer, 1986).

CFA (n = 254) was conducted to evaluate the model fit of the hypothesized subdomain structure and its construct validity. Model fit was assessed using multiple indices, including comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis index (TLI), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\chi^2$\end{document}χ2/df, in accordance with established criteria: CFI ≥ 0.90 (acceptable) and ≥0.95 (excellent), RMSEA ≤ 0.05 (close fit), 0.05–0.08 (acceptable), and ≥0.10 (poor) (Hu & Bentler, 1999).

Convergent validity was assessed by examining the average variance extracted (AVE) and composite reliability (CR). An AVE value > 0.5 and a CR value > 0.7 are considered acceptable indicators of convergent validity. Discriminant validity was assessed by comparing the square root of the AVE for each construct with the corresponding inter-construct correlation coefficients. When the square root of AVE exceeds the corresponding correlation value, it indicates good discriminant validity.

Reliability

The Cronbach’s α of the total Chinese version of the ASQ was 0.951, and for each dimension (negative self-image, disclosure concerns, and concern about public attitudes), the coefficients were 0.889, 0.881, and 0.934, respectively (all >0.70), indicating good internal consistency of the scale (See Table 5).

Fifty of the total 508 participants with ADHD were selected to complete the scale again 2 weeks later, and the temporal stability of the scale was assessed by calculating the correlation coefficient between the total scores obtained from the two administrations. This result showed that the retest reliability was 0.958.

Validity evaluation results

Content validity

Content validity was evaluated using the CVI. The results showed that the scale-level CVI of the Chinese version of the ASQ was 0.875–1.000, indicating good content validity.

Structural validity

Results of the EFA (n = 254) indicated that the KMO was 0.938, exceeding the recommended threshold of >0.7. Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant ( \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\chi^2$\end{document}χ2 = 3,853.91, df = 325, p < 0.001), supporting the suitability of the data for EFA (Lim et al., 2020). Considering that the constructs of ADHD symptoms and stigma were expected to be correlated, an oblique rotation (Oblimin) was selected for the EFA using principal axis factoring. We conducted the analysis without pre-specifying the number of factors to retain. After six iterations, a stable three-factor solution was identified with eigenvalues greater than 1.0, providing robust evidence for a three-factor structure. These three factors accounted for 53.10% of the total variance (see Fig. 1). Parallel analysis was also performed using Mplus software, which further confirmed the three-factor structure (See Fig. 2 and Table 6). When compared to the 95th percentile parallel analysis eigenvalues, the sample eigenvalues from factors 1, 2, and 3 were greater than the 95th percentile eigenvalues. However, the sample eigenvalues from factors 4 and 5 were lower than the 95th percentile eigenvalues. These findings indicate that a three-factor solution is most appropriate. Table 7 presents the pattern matrix of the Chinese version of the ASQ. Factor loadings ranged from 0.517 to 0.768, all of which exceeded the recommended threshold of >0.4. Three factors were extracted from the scale: Factor (1) is concern about public attitudes (items 4, 7, 10, 12, 14–16, 20–22, and 24–26); factor (2) negative self-image (items 1, 2, 6, 8, 9, 11); and factor (3) disclosure concerns (items 3, 5, 13, 17–19, and 23).

Construct validity

Model estimation and evaluation

As shown in Table 8, the goodness-of-fit index (GFI) of different models were analyzed using CFA (n = 254). The single-factor model exhibited unsatisfactory fit, as indicated by suboptimal values across all fit indices (GFI = 0.705, adjusted goodness-of-fit index (AGFI) = 0.654, CFI = 0.824, TLI = 0.809, RMSEA = 0.097, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\chi^2$\end{document}χ2/df = 3.366). The original three-factor structure was then tested as a model for verification. This model produced the following fit indices: GFI = 0.866, AGFI = 0.841, CFI = 0.946, TLI = 0.941, RMSEA = 0.054, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\chi^2$\end{document}χ2/df = 1.733. To improve model fit, parameters were respecified in a stepwise manner based on modification indices, beginning with the highest MI value. Based on the largest theoretically defensible MI, a covariance between the residuals of e9 and e13 (MI = 20.793) was added, as both items reflect caution in disclosure (Items 13 & 23), sharing variance specific to the disclosure concerns dimension. This process was repeated 12 times, with each step guided by both statistical relevance (MI values) and theoretical meaningfulness (see ESM 1), resulting in a well-fitting final model.

Discussion

This study aimed to verify whether the Chinese version of the ASQ can measure stigma in people with ADHD. The translation and validation of the ASQ into Simplified Chinese involved psychometric evaluations, including EFA, CFA, and internal consistency analysis. Based on these results, we found clear evidence supporting the reliability and validity of the Chinese version of ASQ. Our findings also suggest that this Chinese version can serve as a reliable and valid instrument for assessing societal stigma related to ADHD in China.

The findings support the reliability of the Chinese version of ASQ. The overall Cronbach’s α of 0.951, together with subdimension values all above 0.7, suggests improved reliability compared with the original, Korean, and Turkish versions. In addition, satisfactory test–retest reliability indicates good temporal stability.

The Chinese version of the ASQ demonstrated strong content validity, as evidenced by high CVI scores. This is a critical first step in establishing the scale’s psychometric properties within the Chinese cultural context, ensuring that the items adequately reflect the target construct. Good content validity provides an essential foundation for the subsequent investigation of structural validity and supports the overall rigor and interpretability of the translated scale.

Exploratory factor analysis of the Chinese version of the ASQ supported its structural validity. A three-factor solution was retained, which aligned with the theoretical underpinnings of the original scale, accounting for 53.1% of the total variance. Parallel analysis also verified the three-factor structure, representing a satisfactory level for an initial validation study in a new cultural context. All items loaded saliently (≥0.40) on their intended factors, indicating a clear and interpretable factor structure. Furthermore, evidence of convergent and discriminant validity complemented the factor analysis, supporting that the Chinese version of the ASQ adequately captures the intended construct and distinguishes it from related concepts.

Exploratory factor analysis supported the structural validity of the Chinese ASQ, retaining a three-factor solution consistent with the original theoretical model (Kellison et al., 2010). The replication of the three-factor structure in our sample—comprising individuals with direct, contextual experience related to ADHD—aligns with most prior validations (for example, Rim, Jang & Park, 2018), but it contrasts with a Turkish adaptation that reported a unidimensional structure among teachers (Kıransal & Kaya, 2022). This discrepancy may be attributable to sample characteristics. The Turkish sample comprised teachers, who may have had less direct experience with ADHD, whereas our participants were situated within a collaborative healthcare-education-family ADHD care context. This exposure likely fostered a more nuanced understanding of the stigma constructs measured by the ASQ, facilitating differentiation between the distinct factors. Therefore, our findings support the structural validity of the Chinese ASQ and highlight how sample characteristics (e.g., familiarity with the construct) can influence the observed factor structure in stigma assessment tools.

Practice implications

Healthcare professionals can use this scale to measure stigma toward people with ADHD. Stigma is an important issue for adolescents with ADHD, and adolescence is a developmental period in which identity, self-reliance, and social acceptance are significant. Therefore, adolescents at this stage of growth are more likely to internalize negative public stereotypes and develop feelings of shame (Catthoor et al., 2015). Measures to address stigma and mitigate detrimental outcomes widen understanding of the underlying factors that contribute to stigmatization. When the use of this scale shows high levels of perceived stigma in adolescents with ADHD, we should provide adolescents and their families with accurate information about ADHD, its neurobiological basis, and the realities of living with the condition (Evans et al., 2018). This can help dispel misconceptions and reduce feelings of shame and self-blame. Consequently, we can implement targeted psychological interventions, such as cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT), to effectively address stigmatizing cognitions and foster self-compassion (Sibley et al., 2023).

Limitations

The limitations of this study should be considered: (1) The two-hospital sample limits the generalizability of the results, as they may be restricted by regional demographic and clinical characteristics. Therefore, while the conclusions are robust within this setting, they require validation in broader, multi-center studies. (2) The sample size was limited to one city within a province, and may not capture variations between the northern and southern parts. (3) Participants were mainly from rural areas and towns, rather than cities, which might have affected the evaluation of the entire scale. (4) We only conducted validation for the single-factor structural model and the three-factor structural model, and did not test the second-order factor model (higher-order confirmatory factor analysis). (5) Our research did not exclude ADHD participants with learning disorders or psychological disorders (anxiety, depression) when screening participants, which increased clinical representativeness, but limited our ability to attribute findings solely to ADHD. Future studies with stratified samples are needed to disentangle these effects. (6) This research employed a cross-sectional design, which implies that its capacity to monitor temporal modifications is restricted. Future research should include larger samples to improve the applicability of the Chinese version of ASQ in people with ADHD in China. Finally, relying on one subjective measure for perceived stigma in a clinical setting represents a limitation, as it was not supplemented with additional items that may be relevant in an ADHD population.

Conclusions

The results indicates that the 26-item Chinese version of ASQ provides a robust and valid instrument for investigating stigma among individuals diagnosed with ADHD. To our knowledge, this is among the first studies to examine stigma among children and adolescents with ADHD in China. The findings may inform strategies to provide people diagnosed with ADHD with different treatments and early interventions to help reduce stigma. Continued use of the Chinese version of ASQ in assessing people with ADHD may facilitate accurate assessment of the degree of stigmatization they experience.
