Phosphoribosyl pyrophosphate amidotransferase: Novel biomarker and therapeutic target for nasopharyngeal carcinoma
Authors: Yuki Kitagawa, Satoru Kondo, Masaki Fukuyo, Kousho Wakae, Hirotomo Dochi, Harue Mizokami, Shigetaka Komura, Eiji Kobayashi, Nobuyuki Hirai, Takayoshi Ueno, Yosuke Nakanishi, Kazuhira Endo, Hisashi Sugimoto, Naohiro Wakisaka, Atsushi Kaneda, Tomokazu Yoshizaki
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCancer cells show a dynamic metabolic landscape, requiring a sufficient supply of nucleotides to proliferate. They are highly dependent on de novo purine biosynthetic pathways for their nucleotide requirements. Phosphoribosyl pyrophosphate amidotransferase (PPAT), catalyzing the first step of de novo purine biosynthesis, is highly expressed in various cancers. We observed an increased expression of PPAT in nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC). Moreover, our ribonucleic acid sequencing analysis showed high PPAT expression in Epstein–Barr virus‐positive NPC, which was supported by in vitro analysis. Through a gene knockdown study, we showed that the suppression of PPAT expression reduced the proliferation and invasion of NPC cells. We also demonstrated the regulation of PPAT by glutamine, a cosubstrate for PPAT. A glutamine antagonist, 6‐diazo‐5‐oxo‐L‐norleucine, blocked glutamine‐mediated induction of PPAT and reduced NPC cell proliferation. Immunohistochemical analysis of PPAT in NPC tissues revealed increased expression of PPAT with disease progression, which was significantly associated with poor prognosis. In summary, this study highlighted the biological function of PPAT in NPC, establishing its potential as a novel prognostic biomarker for aggressive NPC and a promising therapeutic target.Keywords: Epstein–Barr virus, glutamine, nasopharyngeal carcinoma, PPAT, purine biosynthesis

INTRODUCTION

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma is a unique cancer in which EBV is implicated in carcinogenesis. 1 , 2 , 3 Epstein–Barr virus establishes a latent infection characterized by the expression of a limited subset of viral genes known as latent genes, such as EBV nuclear antigens, latent membrane proteins, and small RNAs. We have previously reported that LMP1 activates multiple cellular signaling pathways, eventually developing NPC. 1 , 4 , 5 , 6 Although the prognosis of patients with NPC has improved due to recent advances in radiation therapy and chemotherapy, some NPC cohorts still show poor prognosis with a high potential for metastasis. Therefore, an in‐depth exploration of effective targets for NPC treatment is necessary to improve patient outcomes. In addition to the carbon shift of the Warburg effect, 7 , 8 the nitrogen shift plays a key role in the malignant development of cancer. 9 , 10 Phosphoribosyl pyrophosphate amidotransferase is a key regulator of the shift in nitrogen utilization from glutamine, introducing nitrogen into the de novo purine biosynthesis pathway to sustain the proliferation of cancer cells. 11 , 12 , 13 Normal cells fulfill their purine and pyrimidine requirements through the salvage pathway. Cancer cells are speculated to meet these requirements by activating the de novo purine pathway. 14 , 15 Previous studies have shown that PPAT expression is upregulated in lung, thyroid, gastric, and colorectal cancers, promoting tumor cell proliferation and invasion. 16 , 17 , 18 A meta‐analysis further revealed that PPAT shows the strongest correlation with malignancy among all metabolic enzymes. 9 However, the clinical significance and biological role of PPAT in NPC remain unclear. In this study, we confirmed increased expression of PPAT in NPC. Subsequently, we investigated its effect on the malignant phenotype of NPC cells and the associated mechanisms. Furthermore, we established a correlation between high PPAT expression and a poor prognosis in patients with NPC. Additionally, a potential association between EBV infection and PPAT expression in NPC was suggested.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patient samples

All participants provided informed consent before the study, and clinical investigations were carried out after approval by the Ethics Committee of Kanazawa University (IRB#2016‐033). This study included 49 patients with NPC who were diagnosed at the Department of Otolaryngology, Head and Neck Surgery, Kanazawa University Hospital between 2005 and 2020. The diagnosis was based on the histological examination of primary lesion biopsies. All patients completed radiation and cisplatin‐based chemotherapy as their primary treatment. Clinical data were retrospectively collected from medical records, and all cases were staged according to the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer/UICC Stage Classification.

RNA sequencing

We previously developed mRNA expression sequencing data of NPC samples using publicly available data from the Gene Expression Omnibus database. 19 Briefly, all of the gene expression database was used to query all publicly available data regarding NPC (https://aoe.dbcls.jp) using the keywords “nasopharyngeal carcinoma” or “nasopharyngeal cancer.” After the review, four datasets (121 NPCs and 10 normal nasal mucosa) were utilized in our study. Details of each dataset includes GSE102349, 20 GSE118719, 21 GSE134886, 22 and GSE164544. 23 Sequenced reads were aligned to human chromosomes (hg19) using HISAT2 and Cufflinks and were used for transcript assembly. Gene expression levels were represented as fragments per kilobase of exon per million mapped sequence reads (FPKM). The PPAT expression in normal nasal mucosa and NPC was analyzed.

In situ hybridization of EBER

In situ hybridization for the detection of EBER was undertaken using the EBER PNE/fluorescein and PNA ISH detection kit (Dako) according to the manufacturer's instructions, as described previously. 24 Specimens with dark‐blue signals appearing within the nucleus were considered positive. 25

Reagents and cell culture

HK1, EBV‐negative NPC cells (RRID: CVCL_7084) (a kind gift from Dr. George Tsao, Hong Kong University), were maintained in 10% FBS RPMI‐1640 in a 5% CO2 cell culture incubator. Akata‐EBV‐eGFP cells (Cat#JCRB1764, RRID: CVCL_VN88) were purchased from JCRB and cultured in 10% FBS–RPMI‐1640 with 800 μg/mL G‐418 (Cat#09380; Nacalai Tesque). The HK1 cells were authenticated using short tandem repeats (BEX) and were not passaged more than 20 times.

L‐glutamine and DON were purchased from Sigma‐Aldrich. As described previously, 26 , 27 , 28 for glutamine studies, cells were washed twice with serum‐free, glutamine‐free medium, replaced with glutamine‐free RPMI‐1640 containing 10% FBS, and incubated for 48 h. The corresponding glutamine‐replete media were prepared by adding the indicated concentrations of glutamine. For DON (a glutamine analog) studies, we added 5 μM DON to the cells, which were already in 10% FBS + 2 mM Gln + RPMI‐1640 medium. 29 , 30

Epstein–Barr virus infection

The EBV‐negative NPC cell line, HK1, was reinfected with EBV using neor EBV‐infected Akata cells and eGFP neor EBV‐infected Akata cells, respectively, as described previously. 19 , 31

Immunoblot analysis

Western blotting was undertaken as previously reported 32 using anti‐PPAT Ab (1:1000, Cat#TA504559S; OriGene Technologies) and anti‐α‐tubulin Ab(1:5000, Cat#sc‐5286; Santa Cruz Biotechnology) as primary Abs, and goat anti‐mouse HRP conjugate (1:2000, Cat#1705047; Bio‐Rad Laboratories) as a secondary Ab. Whole cells were lysed with RIPA buffer. Proteins were transferred to nitrocellulose membranes, and the resulting immunoblots were visualized using ECL Plus (GE Healthcare). Protein signals were detected using a Bio‐Rad ChemiDoc XRS (Bio‐Rad Laboratories). All experiments were repeated at least three times, and representative data are shown.

RNA extraction and qRT‐PCR

cDNA was synthesized by reverse transcription of 500 ng total RNA from HK1‐GFP and HK1‐EBV‐eGFP cells using the ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Master Mix (Toyobo). Then qRT‐PCR was carried out using THUNDERBIRD SYBR qPCR Mix (Toyobo) using the A3000P Real‐Time qPCR system (Agilent Technologies): holding at 95°C for 1 min; 40 cycles of denaturation at 94°C for 15 s, annealing at 60°C for 30 s, and extension at 72°C for 30 s; and one cycle of 95°C for 1 min, 55°C for 30 s, and 95°C for 30 s. Forward and reverse primers for PPAT were 5′‐GCGATTGAAGCACCTGTGGATG‐3′ and 5′‐CGGTTTTTACACAGCACCTCCAC‐3′, respectively, as described previously. 18 The PPAT mRNA expression levels were normalized using GAPDH as the reference.

Immunohistochemical analysis

The PPAT expression was immunohistochemically examined in NPC biopsy specimens during the first medical examination. Sections (3 μm thick) were cut from paraffin blocks of the primary lesion. Sections were deparaffinized, treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide, and incubated with a protein blocker (Agilent Technologies). The sections were then incubated at 4°C overnight with rabbit anti‐PPAT Ab (1:100, 15401‐1‐AP; Proteintech). After washing with PBS, the sections were exposed to EnVision+ secondary Ab (Agilent Technologies) and detected using 3,3′‐diaminobenzidine + chromogen (Agilent Technologies). Sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. Stained sections were independently evaluated by two investigators (Y.K. and S.K.) who were blinded to the clinical data. The PPAT expression was considered positive only if distinct immunoreactivity was present and cytoplasmic staining was diffuse in the absence of nuclear staining.

siRNA transfection

Knockdown was achieved using a PPAT gene‐specific siRNA (Cat#J‐006003‐06; Dharmacon). siRNA targeting EGFP (Cat#EHUEGFP; Sigma‐Aldrich) was used as the siControl RNA. HK1‐EBV‐eGFP was seeded into 6‐well plates at 3.5 × 105 cells per well on the day before transfection. The MISSION siRNA Transfection Reagent (Cat#S1452; Sigma‐Aldrich) was used for transfection at a final siRNA concentration of 30 nM for 48 h. The cells were then assessed by western blotting.

Cell proliferation assay

A total of 2.0 × 103 cells were seeded into a 96‐well plate and cell proliferation was assessed using the CellTiter 96 Aqueous One Solution Cell Proliferation Kit (Cat#G3580; Promega), as described previously. 33 Three samples from each group were used for the analysis.

Matrigel invasion assay

The Matrigel invasion assay was carried out according to the manufacturer's protocol. The density of HK1‐EBV‐eGFP cells was adjusted to 1 × 106/mL, and 500 μL was added to the Transwell insert. Four random fields were selected, and the invading cells were counted. Invaded cells were counted in three different visual fields at 400× magnification from three independent experiments, and the average number of invaded cells was reported.

Statistical analysis

Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All reported p values were two‐sided. All in vitro experiments were carried out in triplicate unless otherwise stated. All statistical analyses were undertaken using EZR (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University), a graphical user interface for R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). 34

RESULTS

PPAT expression upregulated in NPC tissues

We performed RNA‐seq analysis to detect PPAT expression in 121 NPC and 10 normal nasopharyngeal tissue samples. The expression of PPAT mRNA in NPC tissues was significantly upregulated compared to that in normal tissues (p = 0.00002; Figure 1A).

PPAT expression was elevated in EBV‐positive NPC tissues and cells

Next, we focused on the relationship between latent EBV genes and PPAT expression. Our RNA‐seq analysis revealed significantly increased PPAT expression in certain groups with high latent EBV gene expression, including EBER1, EBNA1, and LMP2A (Figure 1B–D). These results suggested that several latent EBV genes affected PPAT expression. To verify whether PPAT expression in NPC cells was associated with the presence of EBV, we undertook qRT‐PCR and immunoblot analyses to detect the expression of PPAT mRNA and protein in EBV‐positive and EBV‐negative NPC cells. PPAT mRNA and protein expression in EBV‐positive HK1 cells was significantly higher than that in EBV‐negative HK1 cells (Figure 2A,B).

(B) Western blot analysis showed the PPAT protein levels in HK1‐GFP, HK1‐EBV‐eGFP, and A549 adenocarcinoma cell lines as positive control.

PPAT promoted cell proliferation and invasion in NPC

Next, we examined whether PPAT expression affected biological functions such as cell proliferation and invasion. For this purpose, we performed knockdown experiments using specific siRNA for the PPAT gene. Knockdown efficiency was confirmed by immunoblot analysis (Figure 3A). PPAT knockdown resulted in a significant decrease in cell proliferation (Figure 3B) and reduced cancer cell invasion as measured by Boyden chamber Matrigel invasion assays (Figure 3C), suggesting that PPAT promoted both NPC cell growth and invasion.

(B) PPAT knockdown HK1‐EBV‐eGFP cells showed reduced cell proliferation. The experiment was repeated three times to confirm similar results. (C) Boyden chamber Matrigel invasion assay. Glutamine induced PPAT expression in NPC cells

Glutamine, a cosubstrate of PPAT, is a key nitrogen source for purine biosynthesis. Therefore, we tested the effect of glutamine on PPAT expression. Glutamine deprivation significantly reduced PPAT expression and proliferation of HK1 cells (Figure 4). Furthermore, treatment of HK1 cells with the glutamine antagonist, DON, reduced PPAT expression and cell proliferation, even in the presence of glutamine, to levels comparable to those of glutamine‐starved controls.

Inhibition of glutamine (Gln) metabolism in nasopharyngeal carcinoma cells results in reduced phosphoribosyl pyrophosphate amidotransferase (PPAT) protein levels and cell proliferation. (A) Western blot analysis of PPAT in HK1‐EBV‐enhanced GFP (eGFP) cells following treatment with no glutamine, glutamine (2 mM), and 6‐diazo‐5‐oxo‐L‐norleucine (DON; 5 μM) after 48 h. (B) Growth curves of HK1‐EBV‐GFP cells following treatment with no glutamine, glutamine (0.5 and 2 mM), and DON (5 μM). The experiment was repeated three times to confirm similar results.

PPAT overexpression predicted poor survival in NPC

To identify the relationship between PPAT expression and the clinicopathologic characteristics of NPC, we undertook immunohistochemical staining for PPAT in 49 NPC samples (Figure 5A). PPAT expression was significantly correlated with T stage (p = 0.009) and EBER (p = 0.03). However, no significant association was observed between PPAT expression and age, sex, N stage, or TNM stage (Table 1). Finally, we evaluated the association between PPAT expression and the survival of patients with NPC. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis and log‐rank tests revealed that patients with high PPAT expression had significantly shorter overall survival (p = 0.02; Figure 5B). Multivariate analysis (with age, gender, and T stage) indicated that PPAT was independently associated with poor prognosis in NPC (hazard ratio 5.42; 95% confidence interval, 1.01–29.1; p = 0.048; Table 2).

DISCUSSION

As a key regulatory enzyme for purine biosynthesis, PPAT plays a critical role in carcinogenesis and cancer progression. 11 , 12 , 13 Previous studies have reported the upregulation of PPAT in various cancers, promoting cancer cell growth in lung cancer, thyroid cancer, gastric, and colorectal cancers. 16 , 17 , 18

In this study, we validated a significant upregulation of PPAT expression in NPC tissues through RNA‐seq analysis for the first time. Subsequently, we focused on investigating the relationship between latent EBV gene expression (EBER1, EBNA1, and LMP2A) and PPAT expression. This was based on the established association between NPC pathogenesis and EBV infection, as determined in our previous studies. 1 , 4 , 5 , 6 Our RNA‐seq data revealed a correlation between high PPAT expression and the expression of these latent EBV genes. Additionally, in vitro experiments confirmed that PPAT expression was significantly higher in EBV‐positive cells than in EBV‐negative cells. These results suggested that latent EBV gene expression promotes PPAT expression in NPC cells.

Our study also confirmed that PPAT knockdown could significantly reduce the proliferation and invasion of NPC cells, suggesting that PPAT contributes to the malignant transformation of NPC cells by enhancing tumor growth and invasive potential, which is consistent with previous reports on other cancers. 16 , 17 , 18 , 35

De novo purine biosynthesis utilizes glutamine as a cosubstrate by PPAT in the first step. Although nonessential for normal cells, glutamine becomes essential for neoplastic transformation. Most cancer cells show glutamine addiction and cannot survive in its absence. 36 , 37 , 38 Therefore, the targeting of glutamine to inhibit de novo purine biosynthesis is a promising therapeutic strategy. In lung cancer, inhibition of PPAT expression using DON, a glutamine antagonist, has been reported. 18 We observed that the induction of PPAT expression in NPC cells was mediated by glutamine, and this induction was inhibited by DON. Additionally, the growth of NPC cells was significantly reduced following treatment with DON, suggesting that targeting glutamine addiction could be a potential strategy for treating NPC.

Our study also showed that high PPAT expression correlated with advanced T stage and poor prognosis in patients with NPC, suggesting that PPAT expression might contribute to NPC tumor progression. Finally, PPAT can function as a biomarker for poor prognosis of NPC.

Our findings have several clinical implications. First, PPAT expression levels in NPC biopsy specimens can help in assessing the malignancy of NPC. Patients with high PPAT expression may require more intensive treatments. Second, if validated, PPAT could emerge as a viable individual therapeutic target for NPC. The downregulation of PPAT expression could be an effective strategy to eliminate cancer cells or enhance their efficacy when combined with existing cancer therapies. Moreover, given our results indicating a potentially stronger PPAT dependence in cellular metabolism in EBV‐positive NPC compared to EBV‐negative NPC, we anticipate that PPAT‐targeted therapy could be highly effective in treating EBV‐positive NPC.

However, this study had some limitations. First, although our results suggested that the expression of some latent EBV genes promotes PPAT expression in NPC cells, the underlying mechanism remains unclear. Further studies are required to elucidate the exact mechanisms of PPAT overexpression induced by EBV infection in NPC. Next, the glutamine antagonist, DON, was used to inhibit glutamine metabolism in NPC cells, which showed potent antineoplastic effects in vitro; however, incorporating them in clinical settings was difficult because of neurotoxicity, gastrointestinal toxicity, and myelosuppression. 36 , 39 Further investigations to circumvent toxicity issues are necessary for clinical applications. In conclusion, we confirmed the increased expression of PPAT in NPC. Moreover, we showed that PPAT can function as a novel prognostic biomarker for aggressive NPC, showing potential as a therapeutic target.
