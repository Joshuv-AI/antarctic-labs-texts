Survival following intentional overdose of veterinary insulin: A case report
Authors: Elsa Sandeno, Sergey Karachenets, Kourtney Zehlke, Raza Khan, Michael Kalinoski, Pravin Meshram, James V Harmon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 40-year-old man presented to the emergency department within 6 h of a massive overdose of 500 units of long-acting feline glargine insulin intravenously and an estimated 50 mg of clozapine. Upon initial assessment, his Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) was 15 and his blood sugar level was 45 mg/dL. The patient was successfully managed with dextrose infusions and close monitoring of blood sugar and electrolytes. To date, no cases have been documented in the literature describing an overdose of insulin formulation intended for veterinary use. Cases of overdose with animal-prescribed human insulin analogs can be managed similarly to previously reported cases of human insulin overdose.Keywords: Veterinary insulin, Overdose, Glargine insulin, Blood glucose, Dextrose infusion

Background

Reports of intentional and accidental insulin overdose, while still uncommon, are becoming increasingly recognized in the literature [3], [1], [2]. When used inappropriately, this hormone has the potential for significant harm, potentially leading to coma, seizures, electrolyte abnormalities, cardiac arrhythmia, and death [4]. In a retrospective analysis of the National Inpatient Sample (NIS) database (2016–2022), 4664 hospitalizations were identified with a diagnosis of intentional self-harm due to poisoning by insulin and oral hypoglycemic agents using ICD-10-CM code T38.3X2A. In-hospital mortality was recorded in 85 cases (1.8 %), with 4571 patients (98.0 %) surviving to discharge. A review of the current literature, however, does not find a single recorded case of overdose with insulin formulations intended for non-human use.

We report an intentional feline insulin glargine overdose, which is a formulation of human insulin glargine marketed specifically for veterinary use in cats [5]. However, this equivalence is not universal among insulin formulations designed for non-human use. The structure of endogenous feline insulin is nearly identical to that of human insulin, with a difference of only four amino acids in its alpha and beta chain sequences. While these substitutions alter the tertiary structure of feline insulin, they permit binding to other mammalian insulin receptors [5]. Both human and porcine insulin formulations have been found to be biologically active in cats and have been used to achieve glycemic control in cases of feline diabetes [5], [6]. Human insulin analogs, including insulin glargine, insulin detemir, and short-acting regular insulin are commonly prescribed by veterinarians for extra-label use due to their safety and efficacy in felines [5], [6], [7], [8]. Regardless of the intended use in human or non-human recipients, insulin is standardized in units/mL. In veterinary applications of human insulin, the dosage and timing are adjusted to suit the species [8].

In addition to veterinary applications of human insulin, insulins designed specifically for feline and canine use also exist. Vetsulin, a porcine-derived insulin, was the first FDA-approved veterinary insulin for the treatment of animal diabetes [5], [9]. While it is more similar to canine and feline insulin in the structure of its beta chain, the alpha chain more closely mimics that of humans [5]. In fact, porcine insulin was the first widely used insulin therapy in humans before the development of recombinant human insulin [10], [11]. Though less allergenic than bovine insulin, it is still inferior to recombinant human insulin, which has the lowest prevalence of allergic reactions, about 2 % [10].

Case report

A 40-year-old man with a history of panic disorder, generalized anxiety disorder (GAD), and depression presented after a suicide attempt by self-administration of 500 units of feline insulin glargine. He had been experiencing increased psychosocial stress related to his employment and reported daily panic attacks and major depressive symptoms, including suicidal ideation, for the past several days. On the morning of admission, around 4 a.m., the patient reported administering 500 units of feline insulin glargine into a peripheral vein along with ingestion of an unknown quantity of clozapine, estimated at 50 of 1 mg tablets. He was found by a friend who rapidly alerted emergency medical services (EMS).

Per the EMS report, the patient was lethargic and somnolent, however, responsive to stimuli. Initial glucose was 45 mg/dL and improved to 120 mg/dL with a bolus of 10 % dextrose. On emergency department (ED) arrival, GCS was 15 with a blood glucose of 87 mg/dL (Fig. 1). Labs were otherwise notable for hypokalemia (3.1mEq/L) and lactate (6.3 mmol/L). An electrocardiogram (EKG) demonstrated normal sinus rhythm.

ED management initially began with a 10 % dextrose infusion and a 2 L bolus of normal saline. Despite initial improvement, glucose trended downward, eventually necessitating IV administration of multiple ampules of 50 % dextrose and subsequent escalation to continuous infusions of 12.5 % and 20 % dextrose. Oral potassium replacement was given for hypokalemia. Throughout his stay in the emergency department, the patient remained alert and interactive. The patient was admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) as escalating treatment and frequent point-of-care glucose measurement warranted a higher-acuity setting.

ICU management included a 20 % dextrose infusion at 200 mL/h to maintain a goal serum glucose of > 100 mg/dL. The patient experienced 10 episodes of glucose below this threshold over the first 24 h, which prompted an additional 50 mL 50 % dextrose boluses. Despite this management, his blood glucose levels continued to remain labile, which prompted the addition of intermittent 50-mcg boluses of octreotide every 6 h. Over the next two days, glucose administration was calibrated to the patient’s serum glucose, with a 20 % dextrose infusion tapered to 5 % dextrose and discontinued by hospital day three. The patient maintained a regular diet throughout the hospital course. On the third day of hospitalization, he was maintaining adequate blood glucose levels with oral intake alone and supplementary glucose treatment was discontinued. Potassium and insulin levels were closely monitored throughout hospitalization. Dextrose infusions were continued for a total of 45 h.

Psychiatric consultation was obtained, providing counseling regarding discharge recommendations and psychiatric medication reconciliation. On hospital day 5, the patient was voluntarily transferred to an inpatient psychiatric hospital.

Discussion

Our case report shows that an overdose with a human formulation of feline insulin glargine can be managed similarly to previously reported cases of insulin overdose. In this case, the patient administered the human analog glargine, which had been prescribed to his cat. As previously discussed, the biological formulation of this medication prescribed for feline use is the same as the human version, with adjusted dosage recommendations.

Glargine insulin is a long-acting basal insulin analog that takes effect within 1–2 h and remains at a steady concentration for approximately 24 h. The prolonged duration of action can make this particular form of insulin especially dangerous in overdose. This was evidenced by the continuously labile blood glucose measurements the patient over the first 48 h of hospitalization, requiring continuous 20 % dextrose infusions and frequent 50 % dextrose boluses. Previous cases of glargine insulin overdose report similar hospital courses, with patients requiring continuous dextrose infusions for up to a week, depending on the number of units of insulin administered [13], [12], [14].

While continuous glucose infusion with IV dextrose remains the mainstay of treatment for hypoglycemia from insulin overdose across numerous studies [3], [1], [12]–18], less common therapies, such as octreotide, glucagon, and hydrocortisone infusion have shown benefit in refractory cases [3], [1], [15]. In the case of our patient, blood glucose levels continued to fluctuate despite continuous dextrose infusion and bolus, prompting an endocrinology consultation. Octreotide was started at their recommendation, with the proposed benefit of suppressing endogenous insulin production.

To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of insulin overdose with a non-human insulin. Despite our patient using a human insulin analog, veterinary insulin formulations can be derived from alternative sources, including those purposefully formulated for use in non-human mammals [5], [8]. While porcine insulin has historically been used with relative safety and efficacy in humans, it has been shown to have a greater likelihood of inducing immune reactions [11]. This report underscores the importance of determining the type of insulin injected to anticipate additional side effects and develop an appropriate treatment plan. Future studies are needed to determine the true incidence of non-human insulin overdose and further characterize the possible clinical presentations, complications, and effective treatments in these patients.

Conclusion

Physicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for insulin overdose when evaluating and treating patients presenting with acute-onset hypoglycemia that is refractory to sustained correction with dextrose administration. Nationally, low mortality has been reported for patients who receive appropriate medical management. Although extremely rare, attempted overdose with non-human insulin prescriptions does occur. Similar to human insulin overdose, these cases benefit from rapid recognition and initiation of medical intervention, interdisciplinary collaboration, and frequent monitoring. Determining the form of insulin administered is important to anticipate additional complications and to treat patients safely and effectively.
