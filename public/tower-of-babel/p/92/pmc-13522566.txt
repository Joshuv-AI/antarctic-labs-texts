Diurnal testosterone oscillations gate drug delivery via phase separation of ZO-1 at the blood-testis barrier in mice
Authors: Zhaoyang Wang, Qihong Wu, Xun Tang, Lu Li, Jingxian Deng, Rufei Huang, Wanqing Lin, Yadong Huang, Yan Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The blood-testis barrier is essential for sperm development but also blocks the delivery of non-hormonal male contraceptives and other germ-cell-targeting therapies. However, how this barrier opens and closes in a controlled manner remains poorly understood. Here we show that the barrier exhibits time-of-day-dependent changes in permeability, allowing drugs to enter selectively during the light phase while remaining sealed during the dark phase. This gating mechanism is driven by daily fluctuations in testosterone. When testosterone rises at night, it activates calcium channels in supporting cells of the testis, leading to dephosphorylation of a key scaffolding protein called zonula occludens-1. This triggers the protein to undergo liquid-liquid phase separation, forming condensates that tighten the barrier. During the day, when testosterone drops, the condensates dissolve and the barrier becomes more permeable. Manipulating this calcium pathway pharmacologically enhances the entry of a contraceptive candidate without causing permanent toxicity. A specific disordered region within zonula occludens-1 is identified as the core driver of this testosterone-responsive condensation. Our study establishes diurnal hormone oscillations as a physiological rheostat for biomolecular condensate formation, offering a chronotherapeutic strategy to optimize drug delivery across biological barriers.

Introduction

In the mammalian testis, the blood-testis barrier (BTB) is formed by tight junctions between adjacent Sertoli cells, which are essential for normal spermatogenesis because it prevents harmful substances from entering the sperm development area and provides an optimal environment for spermatogenesis1,2. However, this protective function poses a formidable challenge for the development of nonhormonal male contraceptives, as it restricts drug delivery to the adluminal compartment. For instance, the efficacy of adjudin is limited by its poor BTB penetration3. This selective permeability is a critical consideration for the development of reversible male contraceptives that target spermatogenesis4. Although TJ dynamics are known to involve cytoskeletal remodeling and junctional protein turnover1,5, the molecular mechanisms governing cyclical BTB “opening” and “sealing” during germ cell transit remain poorly defined. Emerging evidence has suggested that biological barriers, including the blood-brain barrier and gut barriers, exhibit circadian-regulated permeability6,7, yet whether such temporal control operates at the BTB—and how it could be harnessed therapeutically—remains unexplored. Deciphering these mechanisms is therefore critical for advancing contraceptive strategies that require targeted BTB modulation, offering a promising approach to overcome the delivery barrier that has hindered the development of effective nonhormonal male contraceptives.

The BTB is composed of both tight junctions (TJs) and adherens junctions (AJs)1, with zonula occludens (ZO) proteins acting as linkers between the TJs and AJs integral membrane proteins5. The formation and permeability of these junctions depend on ZO proteins8, which belong to the membrane-associated guanylate kinase homolog (MAGUK) protein family9,10. ZO-1, the first identified TJ protein, has two homologs, ZO-2 and ZO-311. ZO-1 interacts with the ZO-2/3 proteins, TJ proteins, and the cytoskeleton through multiple domains, contributing to the formation of the TJ complex12,13. It serves as a molecular switch to make the barrier “open/leaky” or “closed/tight.” Loss of ZO-1 expression leads to Sertoli cell BTB disruption or disassembly, making the barrier leaky5. On the other hand, increased of ZO-1 expression promotes BTB integrity, making it “tighter.”14 Additionally, the regulation of BTB dynamics, specifically disassembly and reassembly, involves TGF-β3, P-glycoprotein, laminin, and mTORC115–18. However, the precise molecular mechanisms and molecules responsible for regulating BTB dynamics are still not well understood. Recent studies highlight that liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) is frequently associated with dynamic cellular processes that undergo temporal variation. ZO proteins undergo self-organization into biomolecular condensates via LLPS, facilitating TJ formation19.

LLPS arises from molecular interactions, resulting in the aggregation of molecules into significant droplets or liquid domains. This phenomenon has been extensively studied in various disciplines, including cancer, neurodegenerative diseases, inflammation, and other areas20,21. The ZO-1 protein exhibits multiple domains that facilitate protein-protein interactions and possesses distinct and elongated intrinsically disordered regions, which form the basis for its capacity to undergo LLPS22. A recent study has revealed that ZO-1 plays a role in driving the formation of tight junctions through LLPS in MDCK cells. During the LLPS of ZO-1, multiple proteins are recruited to the tight junctions, contributing to the ZO-1 dynamic changes19,23. However, the formation of ZO-1 condensates largely depends on the cell type. The precise mechanism behind the formation of ZO-1 condensates involved in regulating the BTB dynamics remains unclear.

In our study, we found that adjudin administered to male mice during the light phase (ZT6) exhibited significantly higher efficacy in inducing germ cell depletion compared to dark phase dosing (ZT16). Mechanistically, testosterone oscillations were found to gate BTB permeability by regulating ZO-1-dependent phase separation. Specifically, when testosterone levels were elevated at night, it facilitated the assembly of BTB tight junctions, thus hindering the transport of adjudin across the BTB. Conversely, light-phase testosterone decline triggered ZO-1 condensate dissolution, loosening TJs and facilitating drug transport. Structural dissection identified the intrinsically disordered PSG + C domain as the core module enabling testosterone-responsive phase separation, while proteomics reveals temporal recruitment of integrins and extracellular matrix regulators to ZO-1 condensates. Crucially, pharmacological mimicry of “light-phase-like” low testosterone states enhances adjudin delivery, establishing chronotherapy as a viable strategy to circumvent BTB resistance. These findings redefine biological barriers as time-of-day-dependent systems, where hormone oscillations encode temporal permeability through phase separation—a mechanism with broad implications for drug delivery and regenerative medicine.

Results

Diurnal variations regulate BTB permeability and adjudin entry

To investigate the chronopharmacology of nonhormonal contraceptives, we administered adjudin—a testis-targeted drug with limited blood-testis barrier (BTB) penetration—to mice at the light phase (ZT6) or the dark phase (ZT16) (Fig. 1A). Strikingly, light phase dosing triggered germ cell desquamation (Fig. 1B) and significantly impaired sperm quality, as evidenced by reduced epididymal sperm viability (54.67% ± 7.126% vs. 38.63% ± 4.00%, p < 0.001) and an increased abnormal sperm morphology rate (30.41% ± 6.33% vs. 43.43% ± 4.87%, p < 0.01) (Fig. 1C–F). Importantly, this germ cell loss was not due to apoptosis (Supplementary Fig. 1A), nor did it involve changes in the diameter of the epididymal tubules (Supplementary Fig. 1B–D). In contrast, dark phase administration showed no spermatogenic disruption, mirroring saline controls. To further understand the underlying mechanisms of these time-of-day-dependent effects, we used biotin-SA to examine the permeability of the BTB at the light phase and the dark phase. Biotin was observed in the seminiferous tubules at the light phase, but was not observed at the dark phase (Fig. 1G). The ultrastructure of the BTB region was examined using TEM. During the dark phase, the BTB was characterized by the coexistence of TJs and basal ectoplasmic specializations (ESs) structure between two adjacent Sertoli cells (SCs) at the basement membrane (green arrow). During the light phase, the structure of the BTB region became indistinct, and localized disassembly was observed (red arrow) (Fig. 1H). At the molecular level, the expression of connexin 43 (CX43), a key adhesion junction protein, was significantly lower during the light phase than during the dark phase (Fig. 1I, J), whereas claudin-11 displayed a similar decreasing trend (Supplementary Fig. 1E, F). These observations suggested that the light phase makes the BTB “open/leaky,” while the dark phase makes the BTB a closed/tight state. The efficacy of adjudin correlated with BTB status—light-phase leakage enabled drug entry, while the dark phase sealing blocked permeation. These data establish diurnal fluctuations as a physiological gatekeeper of BTB permeability and drug delivery.

Nocturnal testosterone elevation seals the murine BTB

In the mouse testis, testosterone secretion exhibits circadian variation24. In our study, we observed significantly higher testosterone levels during the dark phase (ZT16) compared to the light phase (Fig. 2A). To investigate whether BTB permeability is modulated by testosterone levels, we pharmacologically manipulated hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis activity using the luteinizing hormone (LH) receptor agonist goserelin (gos) and antagonist cetrorelix (cet), with saline-injected mice serving as controls (Fig. 2B, C). Following ZT0 administration, serum testosterone measurements at ZT16 revealed that cetrorelix significantly suppressed testosterone levels, whereas goserelin induced a marked elevation compared to vehicle-treated controls (Fig. 2D). Cetrorelix-induced testosterone depletion triggered BTB “leakiness,” allowing biotin tracer penetration into seminiferous tubules. Conversely, goserelin-maintained high testosterone preserved BTB integrity, blocking tracer entry (Fig. 2E). To investigate whether goserelin antagonizes adjudin’s effects, we co-administered goserelin with adjudin (adjudin + gos) and assessed physiological and structural outcomes (Fig. 2G). Mice receiving combined treatment exhibited significantly elevated serum testosterone levels compared to adjudin monotherapy (Fig. 2H). Histological analysis via H&E staining revealed that adjudin alone induced germ cell damage, whereas goserelin co-treatment attenuated this phenotype, preserving germ cell integrity (Fig. 2I). Ultrastructural examination by TEM showed that adjudin alone caused BTB disassembly, but the adjudin + gos group maintained an intact, continuous BTB architecture with preserved tight junction morphology (Fig. 2J). Concomitantly, compared to adjudin single treatment, the combination significantly increased epididymal sperm counts while reducing abnormal sperm morphology rates (Fig. 2K–M). These improvements translated to enhanced fertility, as testosterone-replete mice exhibited higher mating success and larger litter sizes, manifestations of restored reproductive capacity (Fig. 2N, O). Collectively, these data establish that elevated testosterone sustains a functionally tight BTB configuration, thereby counteracting adjudin-induced permeability changes. This hormone-gated molecular switch illuminates a chronotherapeutic strategy for male contraception, where drug efficacy can be optimized by aligning administration with physiological barrier-opening phases.

To further investigate the role of L-type calcium channels in BTB regulation, mice received continuous NIT treatment for 14 days (Fig. 3H). Biotin tracer assays revealed that chronic nitrendipine administration significantly compromised BTB function, increasing biotin tracer penetration (Fig. 3I) and reducing sperm counts and quality (Fig. 3J and Supplementary Fig. 2A–C). Fertility was severely impaired, with litter numbers dropping to 4 vs. 7 in controls (Fig. 3K, L). Remarkably, 21-day (day 35) nitrendipine withdrawal restored BTB integrity and fully recovered reproductive parameters (sperm motility and litter size) (Supplementary Fig. 2D–I). These data demonstrate that L-type calcium channels act as reversible regulators of BTB plasticity, where pharmacological inhibition creates transient “therapeutic windows” for drug delivery without irreversible toxicity.

Nitrendipine-mediated calcium channel inhibition enables reversible BTB opening for contraceptive delivery

To establish a pharmacological strategy for transient BTB modulation, we combined the L-type calcium channel inhibitor NIT with adjudin (adjudin + NIT) in mice (Fig. 4A). Serum testosterone measurements revealed no significant changes in either the adjudin monotherapy or adjudin + NIT groups compared to control (Fig. 4B), confirming that both agents act independently of androgen signaling. Testis weights remained unchanged across all experimental groups (Fig. 4C), ruling out systemic toxicity. Histological analysis revealed basal compartmental gaps in adjudin-treated testes, with the combination group exhibiting exacerbated structural disorganization and occasional seminiferous tubule necrosis (Fig. 4D). Sperm count (Supplementary Fig. 3A) and motility assays demonstrated a significant reduction in the adjudin group (p < 0.01 vs. control), with further decline in the adjudin + NIT group (p < 0.001 vs. adjudin alone), while abnormal sperm morphology rates followed a parallel trend (Fig. 4E, F, and Supplementary Fig. 3C), indicating synergistic impairment of spermatogenesis. To assess male fertility, each treated or control male mouse was paired with one progesterone-primed female mouse (1:1) for 3 consecutive days. Importantly, both treated and untreated groups were tested over the same defined 3-day mating period. Pregnancy outcomes were recorded, and litter size was determined after birth. We found that the conception rate in adjudin + NIT group (0% pregnancy vs. 14.28% in adjudin monotherapy) under synchronized estrus conditions (Fig. 4G, H). Remarkably, 3-week drug withdrawal restored testicular architecture, sperm count (Supplementary Fig. 3B) and motility, and reproductive capacity, demonstrating full functional reversibility (Fig. 4I–M and Supplementary Fig. 3C). Collectively, these findings indicate that intermittent NIT treatment (administered only on days 1, 4, and 7) significantly potentiates adjudin-induced infertility. Notably, despite this reduced dosing frequency compared to the monotherapy regimen, the combination strategy achieved complete infertility (0% pregnancy rate), an efficacy not seen with adjudin alone. Testosterone-driven barrier dynamics correlate with calcium-dependent ZO-1 condensate-like assembly

To explore the molecular mechanism by which testosterone regulates the BTB, we treated TM4 cells with T and examined the expression of ZO-1, a protein closely related to BTB formation. We found that T did not affect the expression of ZO-1 (Fig. 5A, B). Intriguingly, immunofluorescence assays revealed that T could induce the formation of ZO-1 droplet-like condensates at the TM4 cell membrane (Fig. 5C), and the formation and disruption of these condensates were influenced by PEG 8000 (polyethylene glycol 8000, 5%, a molecular aggregating agent) and 1,6-hexanediol (1,6-HD, 5%, a condensate-disrupting agent) (Supplementary Fig. 4A). Moreover, live imaging showed that adjacent EGFP-tagged full-length ZO-1 (EGFP-ZO-1) droplets fused within 90 s—a hallmark of LLPS fluidity (Fig. 5D and Supplementary Video 1). Intriguingly, T was found to promote the fluidity of ZO-1 condensates, as evidenced by the gradual dissipation of ZO-1 condensates after T removal and the shortened fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) recovery time following T treatment (Supplementary Fig. 4B–D and Supplementary Videos 2–5). Crucially, calcium deprivation (Ca²⁺-free medium) delayed condensate fusion by 3.77-fold (480 ± 90.92 s vs. control 127 ± 14.46 s, p < 0.001) and reduced FRAP recovery to 63% (vs. 41% in Ca²⁺-free cells, p < 0.001), while TEER values dropped by 77.5% (p < 0.01) (Fig. 5E–G, Supplementary Videos 6–9, and Supplementary Fig. 4E, F). Since calmodulin (CaM) is a key mediator of calcium signaling, we investigated the localization of endogenous CaM and ZO-1 by immunostaining after testosterone exposure. Under basal conditions, CaM was distributed throughout the cytoplasm and nucleus, whereas ZO-1 was primarily enriched at the cell periphery. Testosterone enhanced ZO-1–CaM colocalization, an effect abolished by Ca²⁺ chelation or CaM inhibition. After testosterone treatment, ZO-1 formed discrete foci that were predominantly detected near the cell periphery, and CaM showed significant colocalization within these foci, indicating a testosterone-facilitated interaction. This colocalization was disrupted by reducing intracellular Ca²⁺ levels or inhibiting CaM with NIT (Fig. 5H). Immunoprecipitation (co-IP) further confirmed that testosterone strengthened ZO-1–CaM binding (Fig. 5I, J). Moreover, treatment of TM4 monolayers with NIT or the calmodulin antagonist W-7 decreased TEER, counteracting the testosterone-induced increase in TEER (Fig. 5K). These results demonstrated that testosterone activated L-type channels, increased intracellular Ca2+ levels, and promoted the general propensity of ZO-1 to assemble into condensate-like puncta, which was associated with enhanced tight junction organization at the BTB. Conversely, inhibition of Ca2+–CaM signaling reduced Ca2+ levels and attenuated ZO-1 puncta/condensate formation, concomitant with increased BTB permeability (Fig. 5L).

Calcium-dependent dephosphorylation enhances the propensity of ZO-1 to form condensate-like assemblies and supports barrier function

The phosphorylation status of ZO-1 is tightly regulated by specific kinases (Casein Kinase 2, CK2) and phosphatases (protein phosphatase 1, PP1) to control barrier function19,25,26, and we predicted its phosphorylation sites in UniProt (Fig. 6A). Because intracellular Ca²⁺ can stimulate enzymes, such as protein kinases and phosphatases, we examined whether testosterone-induced Ca²⁺ signaling impacts the phosphorylation state of ZO-1. We observed decreased ZO-1 phosphorylation (reduced p-Ser signal) following testosterone treatment, whereas the calcium channel inhibitor NIT increased p-Ser levels (Fig. 6B, C). These findings suggested that testosterone may influence junctional organization and barrier function by regulating ZO-1 phosphorylation. To test how phosphorylation status affects ZO-1 assembly behavior, TM4 cells were treated with PP1 or CK2, and ZO-1 puncta/condensate formation was assessed. PP1 markedly increased the number of ZO-1 puncta/condensates detected within the cell footprint compared with controls, whereas such assemblies were rarely observed in the CK2 group (Fig. 6D). Live-cell fluorescence microscopy further revealed that within 40 min, PP1 induced the emergence of large EGFP-ZO-1 foci, whereas CK2 treatment yielded only small and transient EGFP-ZO-1 foci (Fig. 6E and Supplementary Videos 10, 11). FRAP analysis demonstrated that PP1 enhanced ZO-1 assembly dynamics with increased fluorescence recovery, whereas CK2 impeded recovery and antagonized the effect of testosterone (Fig. 6F, G, and Supplementary Videos 12–17). Consistently, testosterone and PP1 increased droplet area, with the combination further enlarging droplet size, whereas CK2 decreased droplet size and abolished the testosterone-induced increase (Supplementary Fig. 5A, B). ZO-1 proteins comprise several crucial domains, namely PDZ, SH3, GUK, and a C-terminal region (ZU5). Sequence analysis using IUPred revealed that PSG + C (amino acids 425–1745) was intrinsically disordered, meaning it lacks a stable 3D structure (Fig. 7I). The PSG + C domain of ZO-1 played an essential role in the formation of ZO-1 puncta induced by testosterone, which was crucial for the establishment of the BTB.

Temporal recruitment of integrins and ECM regulators to ZO-1 condensates supports diurnal barrier integrity

To determine the selective recruitment of proteins by ZO-1 condensates, we performed immunoprecipitation followed by quantitative mass spectrometry. Lysates from TM4 cells, TM4 cells treated with testosterone, and TM4 cells deprived of Ca2+ were used for the immunoprecipitations. The immunoprecipitants were then analyzed using mass spectrometry for quantitative analysis (Fig. 8A). Compared to the control group, testosterone treatment resulted in significant changes in the ZO-1 complex for 84 proteins. Among these proteins, 61 were upregulated, and 23 were downregulated. In contrast, Ca2+ deprivation in TM4 cells led to significant changes for 300 proteins in the ZO-1 complex. Among them, 56 proteins were downregulated, and 244 proteins were upregulated compared to the control sample (Fig. 8B). A Venn diagram analysis depicted the proteins with significant changes between testosterone treatment and Ca2+ depletion, revealing that 16 proteins were shared between the two treatments. Among these, 12 were significantly upregulated, and 4 were downregulated (Fig. 8C, D). Further analysis using Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment showed that among the upregulated proteins, 6 were enriched in integrin binding (important for cell adhesion, migration, and signaling), 3 were enriched in fibronectin binding (involved in cell adhesion and immune response), 3 were enriched in extracellular matrix binding, and 2 were enriched in C-X3-C chemokine binding (Fig. 8E). In terms of molecular function (MF), 5 upregulated proteins were enriched in cell junction organization and 2 were enriched in cell-cell adhesion mediated by integrin (Fig. 8F). Although the BTB is fundamentally a cell-cell adhesion structure, these findings suggest that cell-matrix adhesions synergize with cell-cell contacts to fine-tune barrier dynamics. Integrins, by binding to ECM components, can transmit mechanical signals and orchestrate actin cytoskeleton reorganization—a process critical for the assembly and stability of tight junctions27. The recruitment of integrins and ECM factors to ZO-1 condensates may thus reinforce the “sealed” BTB by providing structural anchorage and stabilizing the junctional architecture. These proteins were associated with tight junctions and intracellular vesicular transport. Notably, we found that calreticulin (Calr) belongs to the core ZO-1 network. Calr is a calcium-binding protein that is primarily located in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) of cells. Its function was to regulate calcium ions, maintaining calcium ion balance by absorbing and releasing them within cells28. Additionally, it binds to other proteins and assisted in their proper folding and assembly, ensuring their functionality and stability29. Taken together, these results suggest that the nocturnal rise in testosterone activates L-type channels and elevates cytosolic Ca²⁺, coinciding with increased ZO-1 phase-separation propensity and the formation of ZO-1 condensate-like assemblies. Proteomic analyses further indicate enhanced association of ZO-1 with integrin- and fibronectin/ECM-related factors during this phase, consistent with a tighter BTB state. In the light phase, reduced Ca²⁺ signaling is accompanied by diminished ZO-1 assemblies and redistribution of junction-associated components, correlating with transient increases in BTB permeability that may facilitate germ-cell transit and improve drug access. This diurnal switching of ZO-1 assembly state and associated interactome changes—linked to testosterone–calcium crosstalk—highlights biological barriers as dynamic, temporally regulated molecular systems (Fig. 8G).

Discussion

The established role of testosterone in spermatogenesis has been extensively documented30. Here, we identify a non-transcriptional testosterone-responsive pathway that is consistent with calcium–calmodulin (Ca2+–CaM)-dependent regulation of ZO-1 phase separation/condensate-like assembly. These findings provide a potential framework for understanding how the BTB can maintain overall stability while accommodating cyclic germ-cell transit.

During nocturnal testosterone elevation, activation of L-type channels increases cytosolic Ca2+, which is accompanied by CaM-dependent dephosphorylation of ZO-1 and an increased propensity of ZO-1 to form condensate-like assemblies. This molecular “sealing” mechanism coincides with physiological demands for immune protection, effectively compartmentalizing post-meiotic germ cells31. Interestingly, our interactome analysis revealed that testosterone promotes the association of ZO-1 with proteins involved in integrin and ECM binding. Although the BTB is primarily a cell-cell adhesion structure, this finding supports the concept of “junctional crosstalk.” Integrins, by binding to ECM components, can transmit mechanical signals and orchestrate actin cytoskeleton reorganization—a process critical for the assembly and stability of tight junctions27. The recruitment of integrins and ECM factors to ZO-1 condensates may thus reinforce the “sealed” BTB by providing structural anchorage and stabilizing the junctional architecture. Conversely, light-phase testosterone troughs dissolve condensates via CaM dissociation, creating transient “leaky windows” that enable adjudin penetration and spermatid movement, thereby aligning barrier permeability with spermatogenic staging.

Our findings also contribute significantly to the understanding of phase separation, as phase separation is a dynamic process commonly observed in cellular processes that undergo temporal variation32. Previous studies have primarily focused on phase separation occurring in steady or homogenized cellular contexts, such as stress conditions or unidirectional shifts during cell differentiation and development33,34. Our results revealed that dispersion of ZO-1 condensates promoted the disassembly/dissolution of the BTB, making the barrier “open,” while the formation of ZO-1 condensates promoted the assembly of the BTB, making the barrier “sealed.” Importantly, although ZO-1 ultimately functions at the plasma membrane to support tight junction organization, our imaging detected droplet-like ZO-1 condensate-like assemblies that were frequently observed near the cell periphery under testosterone stimulation (Fig. 5C), together with functional readouts indicating strengthened barrier properties. We therefore interpret testosterone-driven dephosphorylation as increasing the phase-separation/condensation propensity and assembly dynamics of ZO-1, which is associated with improved junctional organization and barrier integrity. We note that the present study does not directly track a cytoplasm-to-membrane transfer or fusion process of ZO-1 assemblies; resolving the precise trafficking route and the relationship between intracellular puncta and junctional condensates will require future work using dedicated membrane markers and higher spatiotemporal resolution. Together, our results support a model in which testosterone-induced dephosphorylation enhances ZO-1 condensation/assembly behavior, which is linked to increased BTB integrity.

In addition to the role of testosterone in ZO-1 phase separation, our study has elucidated a mechanistic link between testosterone signaling and calcium homeostasis. Our findings revealed that testosterone elevates intracellular Ca²⁺ levels. We identified calreticulin as a component enriched within ZO-1 condensates. As a Ca²⁺-buffering chaperone, Calr potentially links calcium signaling to junctional stability, although its precise functional contribution requires further investigation. By mimicking physiological light-phase Ca²⁺ troughs through NIT administration, we have successfully created therapeutic windows that not only significantly enhance the contraceptive efficacy of adjudin but also maintain a favorable safety profile. This breakthrough addresses the challenge in the field of nonhormonal male contraception by eliminating irreversible toxicity concerns. Beyond male contraception, our discovery of this diurnal LLPS paradigm opens up new and exciting therapeutic possibilities for testicular cancer treatment. By precisely timing drug delivery to coincide with the BTB-opening phases, we may be able to overcome the drug resistance mediated by the BTB. This approach offers a dual benefit: it enhances the efficacy of chemotherapy while providing targeted protection for spermatogonial stem cells, thus preserving fertility during cancer treatment. NIT could be a promising candidate for male contraception and may also serve as a means of delivering nonhormonal male contraceptives and other drugs through the BTB, offering a new approach to overcome the challenges posed by the BTB in drug delivery.

It is worth noting that NIT treatment alone also induced spermatogenic impairment, but this required a continuous daily dosing regimen (14 days), likely due to sustained disruption of BTB integrity that reduced fertility rates from 66 to 50%. In contrast, the combination strategy (adjudin + NIT) achieved complete infertility (0%) with only intermittent NIT exposure (days 1, 4, and 7). If the mechanism were solely additive, this drastic reduction in NIT dosage would theoretically lead to a milder outcome. The fact that minimal NIT exposure triggered such a profound contraceptive effect suggests that NIT functions primarily as a “gatekeeper.” By transiently modulating the BTB, NIT likely maximizes the local bioavailability of adjudin within the adluminal compartment, allowing for high efficacy with reduced systemic drug exposure. Thus, this strategy has the potential to effectively optimize male contraceptive delivery while maintaining full reversibility.

At the molecular level, our research has delved into the mechanism of ZO-1 phase separation. Previous reports have highlighted the critical importance of the central region, which included the SH3, U5, GUK, and U6 domains, for targeting ZO-1 to junctions9,35,36. Notably, there was a highly flexible intrinsically disordered “HOOK” region that separated the SH3 and GUK domains9,37. When there was no intermolecular interaction, the HOOK region functioned to conceal protein-protein interaction sites in the SH3 region. However, in response to elevated intracellular Ca2+ levels, the intrinsically disordered HOOK region of the SH3 and GUK domains interacted with the Ca2+-binding protein calmodulin (CaM)38. While mapping these domains, we noticed that the isolated PSG fragment (aa 425–794)—which lacks the N-terminal PDZ modules required for membrane anchoring—frequently accumulated in the nucleus; this passive localization is expected for a soluble EGFP fusion and does not reflect the native membrane targeting of full-length ZO-1. Importantly, our data demonstrate that the PSG + C region (aa 425–1745), not PSG alone, is both necessary and sufficient for Ca²⁺/testosterone-driven condensation and BTB assembly. Consequently, future small-molecule strategies should focus on the intrinsically disordered PSG + C domain to achieve tissue-specific barrier modulation while minimizing off-target effects.

It is important to note that while our study has made significant progress in understanding the role of testosterone in ZO-1 phase separation and BTB regulation, the mammalian diurnal rhythm is regulated by complex interlocked positive and negative feedback loops and can be influenced by external cues. Further research is needed to fully elucidate the coordination between phase separation and the diurnal rhythm.

In conclusion, our study has provided insights into how diurnal testosterone fluctuations regulate the permeability of the BTB through ZO-1 phase separation. This mechanism has important implications for male contraception, testicular cancer treatment, and drug delivery. Targeting ZO-1 phase separation could be a viable strategy for enhancing the transport of contraceptive drugs across the BTB or treating male infertility by controlling the “open/leaky” and “closed/tight” states of the BTB. Based on the diurnal variations of ZO-1 condensates revealed in this study, careful consideration of the timing of diagnosis and therapeutic interventions will be crucial in future clinical applications, paving the way for the development of more effective and targeted treatments.

Methods

Animal

All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Jinan University (Approval No: [20230301-14]). We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use. Eight-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were obtained from the Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center. Mice were group-housed (6–8 mice per cage) in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility under a strict 12-h light/12-h dark cycle (lights on at 07:00, defined as Zeitgeber Time 0 (ZT0), lights off at 19:00, ZT12). The room temperature was maintained at 22 ± 2 °C with a relative humidity of 50 ± 10%. Mice were provided with standard laboratory chow and water ad libitum. After 1 week of acclimation in the experimental animal facility, the animals were randomly divided into different experimental groups.

Adjudin administration

According to Cheng et al.39,40. Briefly, adjudin (15 mg/kg, AF-2364, MedChemExpress) or an equal volume of the vehicle (0.05% methylcellulose) was administered via oral gavage. The administration time points were ZT6 (light phase) or ZT16 (dark phase), which correspond to 13:00 and 23:00, respectively, under the lighting conditions of this study. Administrations were performed on days 1, 4, and 7, and the mice were sacrificed at ZT16 on day 16 for sample collection.

Testosterone modulation

Mice were randomly allocated into two groups (n = 7 per group). The Goserelin group received intraperitoneal injections of Goserelin (Gos, 5 mg/kg, MedChemExpress, HY-13673), while the Cetrorelix group received intraperitoneal injections of Cetrorelix (Cet, 5 mg/kg, MedChemExpress, HY-P0009). Both drugs were administered at ZT0, and the mice were sacrificed at ZT16 for sample collection. The drugs were dissolved in saline, and control mice received an equivalent volume of saline (see Fig. 2C).

Nitrendipine administration

Mice were randomly divided into two groups (n = 14 per group). The NIT group received daily oral gavage of Nitrendipine (NIT, 10 mg/kg, BAY-E-5009, MedChemExpress, Shanghai) at ZT6 for 14 consecutive days. The control group received an equivalent volume of the vehicle (0.05% methylcellulose). Subsequently, 7 mice were randomly selected from both the control and NIT groups for a 3-day fertility test, and they were sacrificed at ZT16 immediately afterward. The remaining 7 mice from each group continued without drug administration for 21 days (day 35), followed by a 3-day fertility test, and were sacrificed at ZT16 (see Fig. 3H).

Combination therapy

Mice were randomly assigned to three groups (n = 14 per group): control, adjudin, and adjudin + goserelin (or adjudin + nitrendipine). The adjudin group received oral gavage of adjudin (15 mg/kg). The adjudin + goserelin group received oral gavage of adjudin combined with an intraperitoneal injection of goserelin (5 mg/kg, ICI 118630, MedChemExpress). The adjudin + nitrendipine group received oral gavage of both adjudin and Nitrendipine (10 mg/kg). Control mice received an equivalent volume of the respective vehicles. All administrations were performed at ZT6 on days 1, 4, and 7. Following this, 7 mice were randomly selected from the control, adjudin, and adjudin + goserelin (see Fig. 2G) or adjudin + nitrendipine (see Fig. 4A) groups for a 3-day fertility test and were sacrificed at ZT16. The remaining 7 mice in each group were maintained without drug administration for 21 days (day 28), subjected to a 3-day fertility test, and then were sacrificed at ZT16.

Fertility tests

To rigorously evaluate contraceptive efficacy within a defined therapeutic window, we utilized an estrus-synchronization protocol to maximize mating probability. Sexually mature C57BL/6 female mice were primed with subcutaneous injections of estradiol benzoate (0.02 mg/mouse; MedChemExpress; HY-B1192) 24 h prior, followed by progesterone (500 μg/kg; MedChemExpress; HY-N0437) 4 h before the trial. This ensured all females were in estrus at the time of pairing. Treated male C57BL/6 mice (n = 7 per group) were individually paired (1:1 ratio) with these primed females for a 3-day intensive mating period. Following cohabitation, males were removed. Females were housed separately and monitored daily. Fertility outcomes were quantified by pregnancy rate (presence/absence of pregnancy) and litter size (number of pups delivered). This protocol allows for a precise evaluation of male fertility potential independent of female cycle variations.

Biotin tracer assay

Upon completion of the animal experiments, mice were anesthetized using 1% sodium pentobarbital. A 10 mg/mL solution of EZ-Link Sulfo-NHS-LC-Biotin (Biotin-SA, 21335, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was injected in situ into the right testis of each mouse at a volume of 20 μL via a microinfusion pump (LSP01-1A, LongerPump), while an equal volume of physiological saline was injected into the left testis as a control. After allowing 1 h for biotin diffusion, the mice were euthanized, and the testes were collected. The testes were embedded in Tissue-Tek® O.C.T. Compound and sectioned into 5 μm thick frozen sections. The sections were fixed with acetone for 20 min, blocked with 5% BSA at room temperature for 1 h, and incubated in the dark with Alexa Fluor 568-conjugated streptavidin (2 mg/mL) for 1 h. This was followed by incubation with DAPI staining solution for 10 min. Imaging was performed using a Nikon microscope.

Histological staining

The testes were embedded in OCT compound (American Cherry Blossom freezing embedding medium) and sectioned into 5 μm thick slices using a cryostat. The slices were then fixed with acetone and blocked with an immunostaining blocking solution for 1 h. Primary antibodies (ZO-1 1:100, CaM 1:50, CX43 1:200, claudin11 1:200) were added, and the testes were left to incubate overnight at 4 °C. The following day, the testes were exposed to fluorescent secondary antibodies (1:200) for 1 h. DAPI staining was performed for 10 min to visualize the nuclei, and images were captured using a Nikon Eclipse fluorescence microscope with a 20× objective lens (Nikon Eclipse). Quantitative evaluation of immunofluorescence signals (e.g., ZO-1/CaM intensity) was conducted in a blinded manner using ImageJ software (version 1.8.0.345). At least 5 random selected fields of view per sample from ≥3 independent experiments were analyzed. Positive signals were defined as fluorescent puncta with intensity >2-fold background (threshold set via auto local threshold plugin). For Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining, the testes were detached and fixed in Bouin’s (G1121-15ML, Servicebio, Wuhan) fixative for 24 h. Subsequently, the samples were sent to Wuhan Seville Company for HE staining and imaged using a Nikon microscope equipped with a 20× objective.

Quantitative analysis of epididymal tubule diameter

Quantitative morphometric analysis of epididymal tubule diameter was performed using transverse sections and longitudinal sections of the cauda epididymis. Only tubules displaying a nearly circular profile were included to avoid artifacts caused by oblique sectioning. For each animal, at least 20 randomly selected transverse-sections and longitudinal-sections epididymal tubules were analyzed. Tubule diameter was measured across the minor axis (shortest axis) of the tubular cross-sectional profile using ImageJ software, following established morphometric criteria. Tubules that appeared distorted, collapsed, or obliquely sectioned were excluded from analysis. Measurements were performed under identical magnification settings for all samples (20× objective). A total of 7 mice per experimental group were included for quantitative analysis. This measurement strategy is consistent with previously reported morphometric analyses of tubular structures in the male reproductive tract41,42.

Testosterone detection

Blood samples were collected at the appropriate time points. Serum were separated by centrifugation at 3500 × g for 15 min. The serum testosterone content was then detected using a testosterone test kit (S10940093, Beijing Northern Biotechnology Research Institute Co, Ltd). Specifically, 50 μL of mouse serum were mixed with 100 μL of rabbit anti-T antibody and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The following day, 125I-T of 100 μL was added to the mixture and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. After that, 500 μL of donkey anti-rabbit immunoassay solution was added to the mixture, and the mixture was centrifuged at 4 °C for 3500 × g for 15 min. The supernatant was then removed for machine detection. The content of testosterone in the sample was calculated based on the test results of the T standard by drawing a fitting line.

Quantification and evaluation of sperm parameters

After euthanisation, the animals were dissected, and the cauda epididymis were collected from male mice and immediately placed in 1 mL of pre-warmed M2 medium (Sigma-Aldrich, M7167). The tissues were minced and incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere for 10 min to allow sperm to swim out. For concentration assessment, a 10 µL aliquot of the raw sperm suspension was loaded into a pre-warmed Makler counting chamber (Sefi-Medical Instruments). Sperm concentration (in millions per milliliter) was objectively determined by counting sperm heads within a defined volume under a phase-contrast microscope. Based on the raw concentration, the suspension was adjusted to approximately 2–5 × 10⁶ sperm/mL for optimal computer-assisted sperm analysis (CASA). For CASA, a 10 µL aliquot of the adjusted suspension was loaded into a standardized counting chamber (Leja slide, 20 µm depth) pre-warmed to 37 °C. Analysis was performed using the ML-500JZ CASA system (Nanjing, China). System settings were calibrated for mouse sperm as follows: images were captured with a 10× objective lens at an acquisition rate of 60 frames per second; a minimum of 200 cells per sample were analyzed; and the temperature of the analysis area was maintained at 37 °C. Sperm motility was classified as progressive (VAP > 25 μm/s), nonprogressive (VAP ≤ 25 μm/s), or immotile (VAP = 0 μm/s) based on predefined kinetic criteria (straight-line velocity, VAP)43.

Sperm morphology assessment

Sperm was stained using the Papanicolaou staining kit (Solarbio, G2571). In brief, sperm were resuspended in 1 mL of M2 medium, wash them with PBS, resuspended, dilute the concentration to 2 × 10⁶ sperm/mL, and then take 10 µL was used to prepare smears. Prior to staining, the air-dried smears were fixed in 95% ethanol and processed through a standard staining protocol: rehydration through a graded ethanol series, nuclear staining with hematoxylin staining solution (5 min), differentiation in acid ethanol, bluing, and cytoplasmic staining with Orange G6 (2 min) and Modified EA50 (5 min) solutions. After dehydration through a graded ethanol series, slides were cleared in xylene and mounted. A minimum of 200 sperm per sample were examined under a light microscope for abnormalities in the head, midpiece, and tail. Characteristic staining patterns were used to distinguish the acrosome (light blue), postacrosomal region (deep blue), and midpiece (slight red). Abnormalities were defined as structural deviations: head (duplicated, headless, malformed), midpiece (swollen, broken), and tail (coiled, duplicated, short). These were quantified in a blinded manner using ImageJ on ≥10 random selected fields of view per sample (covering ≥200 sperm).

Sperm sample preparation for scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Following the same sperm collection procedure, the sperm suspension was adjusted to a final concentration of 2 × 10⁶ sperm/mL, a 500 µL aliquot of the sperm suspension was added to 9500 µL of physiological saline and gently mixed. The diluted suspension was centrifuged at 2000 × g for 10 min. After discarding the supernatant, the sperm pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of electron microscopy fixative (G1102-100ML, Wuhan Cyagen Biosciences) and fixed at room temperature for 1 h. Subsequent SEM processing, including critical point drying and sputter-coating, as well as imaging, were performed by Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd.

Cell line and culture conditions

The mouse Sertoli cell line (TM4, Procell-CL-0456) and human embryonic kidney (HEK293T, Procell-CL-0005) cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM, 21013024, Gibco), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, 10091148, Gibco). These cells were incubated at 37 °C in a cell incubator with a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

Plasmid and transfection

Homologous recombination reagents (pEASY®-Basic Seamless Cloning and Assembly Kit, CU201-02) were employed to recombine the full-length ZO-1 (XM_036152895.1) into the pEGFP-C1 plasmid at the EcoRI site. Briefly, RNA was extracted from the TM4 cell line and converted into cDNA, which served as a template for amplifying the full-length ZO-1. The constructed plasmids were then transformed into E. coli DH5α (B528413-0010, Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai). Monoclonal identification and sequencing were carried out, and colonies with complete sequencing were amplified and cultured. Subsequently, the plasmids were extracted using an endotoxin-free plasmid extraction reagent kit (DP118-02, Tiangen Biotech Co. Ltd.; Beijing) for subsequent experiments. Similarly, different truncated domains of ZO-1 were cloned into the pEGFP-C1 plasmid at the EcoRI site, following the same method mentioned above. Primer details are provided in supplementary information (SI-Table 3).

The EGFP-ZO-1 plasmid was utilized along with Lipofectamine 3000 (L3000075, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Shanghai) to transfect 293T or TM4 cells. Briefly, the plasmid (1 µg) and P3000 reagent were diluted in Opti-MEM (31985070, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Shanghai), and Lipofectamine 3000 was mixed with it and left to incubate for 10 min. The mixture was then slowly added to the cells. After 48 h, the cells were treated according to the specific requirements of the experiment.

Immunofluorescence assay

TM4 cells were seeded into 20 mm confocal dishes at a density of 1 × 105 cells per dish. The next day, the cells were treated with Boldenone undecylenate (T, 10 μM, S3715, Selleck), Nitrendipine (NIT, 10 μM) or calcium-free medium (Ca2+ free, YC-2067, Shanghai) for 12 h. Then the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (4% PFA, G1101-500ML, Servicebio, Wuhan) for 10 min and washed with PBS, and then the cells were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA, A801320, Macklin, Shanghai) at room temperature for 1 h. Subsequently, anti–zonula occludens-1 antibody (ZO-1, ab221547, Abcam, Beijing, dilution 1:200) or calmodulin (CaM) primary antibody (MA3-917, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Shanghai, dilution 1:200) was incubated overnight at 4 °C. The next day, the cells were again washed with PBS. After that, the cells were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h with Alexa Fluor® 594 (ab150116, Abcam, Beijing) or Alexa Fluor® 488 (ab150077, Abcam, Beijing) secondary antibody (dilution 1:200), and the cell nuclei were stained with DAPI (ab285390, Abcam, Beijing). Finally, the cells were imaged using a laser scanning confocal microscope (LSM900, Carl Zeiss, Germany).

Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) assay

The EGFP-ZO-1 plasmid was transfected into TM4 and 293 T cells. After 48 h, the cells were treated according to the experimental requirements. Subsequently, FRAP experiments were conducted using a confocal laser scanning microscope equipped with a 63× oil immersion objective. Briefly, the EGFP-ZO-1 droplets within the cells were subjected to photobleaching by exposing them to a laser intensity of 100% at 488 nm for 1 s, following the acquisition of three previous time points. Fluorescence recovery was then monitored at a time interval of 2 s for a total of 120 time points. For each sample, images with dimensions of 1024 × 1024 pixels were captured at a frequency of every 2 s over the course of 120 time points. Fluorescence intensity analysis was performed using ZEN blue software (version 3.9.4). The fluorescence changes of at least 4 droplets per field were quantified. To conduct the time-lapse experiment, the confocal dish was located on the stage at a temperature of 37 °C with 5% CO2. The interval time and number of image acquisitions were set according to the experimental requirements for continuous interval shooting.

Calcium ion content detection assay

TM4 cells were seeded into 12-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well. The following day, the cells were treated with T at concentrations of 10 nM, 1, or 10 μM, NIT (10 μM), or a combination of NIT (10 μM) and T (10 μM) for 12 h. The calcium content was then measured using a calcium detection assay kit (40704ES50, YEASEN, Shanghai) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, the supernatant was removed, and the cells were washed with PBS. TM4 cells were treated with Fluo-4 AM (dilution 1:1000) for 30 min at 37 °C. Finally, photographs were taken using a fluorescence microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) at an excitation wavelength of 488 nm, and the fluorescence intensity of intracellular calcium indicators (Fluo-4 AM) was quantified on a per-field basis using ImageJ software. Images were opened and converted into an RGB stack. Under the image > adjust > threshold menu, the positive signal range was uniformly defined by setting the lower threshold level to 60 and the upper threshold level to 255. Measurements were then executed via analyze > measure, and fluorescence intensity was calculated and recorded based on the integrated density value for each image. A minimum of 10 random fields per experimental group from at least 3 independent experiments were analyzed. All image acquisition parameters were kept strictly consistent across all compared samples to ensure valid intensity comparisons.

Measuring transepithelial resistance

TM4 cells were seeded into transwell chambers with a pore size of 0.4 μm, which were placed in 12-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well. The cells were then treated for 12 h with one of the following conditions: T at 10 nM, 1 μM, or 10 μM and NIT (10 μM); a combination of NIT (10 μM) and T (10 μM); the calmodulin antagonist W-7 hydrochloride (W-7, 10 μM, HY-100912, MedChemExpress, Shanghai); a combination of W7 and T; protein phosphatase 1 (PP1, 10 μM, P0753S, NEB, Beijing); casein kinase 2 (CK2, 10 μM, P6010S, NEB, Beijing); a combination of PP1 and T; or a combination of CK2 and T. The integrity of the cell monolayer was assessed by measuring the transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) using a Millicell Electrical Resistance System (Millipore, Hong Kong, China) according to a previously described method44. The measured resistance value was adjusted by subtracting the blank value, and then converted to the unit area of resistance by multiplying it by the effective surface area of the transwell filter insert. The resistance of a unit area was calculated as resistance (Ω) multiplied by the effective membrane area (cm2). Three independent experiments were performed to validate the results.

Western blot assay

TM4 cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 3.0 × 105 cells per well. After overnight incubation, cells were subjected to the indicated treatments, harvested, and lysed in RIPA buffer (P0013C, Beyotime, Shanghai) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (1×, P1008, Beyotime, Shanghai). Cell lysates were homogenized using a tissue homogenizer (SRT-24, Sailt Biotechnology) at 60 Hz for 90 s and subsequently centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The protein concentration was determined using the BCA kit (A53225, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Shanghai). The proteins were then separated by SDS-PAGE at 8%, transferred onto a PVDF membrane (IPVH00010, Millipore, Guangzhou), and blocked with 5% nonfat milk at room temperature for 1 h. The membranes were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies and subsequently with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. The target proteins were visualized using ECL (35050, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Shanghai). Band intensities of each protein were quantified using Quantity One software (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) and normalized to β-actin levels.

Animal tissue: following the completion of drug administration in mice, the testes were dissected and placed into RIPA lysis buffer. A homogenizer was used to thoroughly homogenize the testes, and the resulting mixture was subjected to centrifugation at 13,000 × g for 20 min. The supernatant, containing the extracted proteins, was carefully collected. The protein concentration was then measured using the BCA protein assay method to ensure consistency. Finally, the expression of the target protein was assessed using Western blot analysis.

Immunoprecipitation (co-IP)

TM4 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 3.0 × 10⁵ cells per well and cultured until they reached 80–90% confluence. The cells were then treated for 12 h under the following conditions: 10 μM T, 10 μM NIT, or calcium-free medium (Ca2+ free). After treatment, the cells were washed with ice-cold PBS and lysed with IP lysis buffer (87787, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Shanghai) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitor. The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected. Total protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay. For the experiment, 50 μg of protein was allocated as the Input sample, while 500 μg was used for the immunoprecipitation (IP) group. DynaGreen™ Protein A/G Magnetic Beads (80106G, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Shanghai) were incubated with ZO-1 primary antibody (ab221547, Abcam, Beijing, dilution 1:50) at room temperature for 1 h to form antibody-bead complexes. The antibody-conjugated beads were then incubated with the IP protein samples overnight at 4 °C with gentle rotation. The Input sample was processed using plain beads without antibody. The next day, the beads were collected magnetically, washed four times with ice-cold wash buffer, and resuspended in 1× loading buffer. After boiling at 100 °C for 10 min, the supernatant was collected for subsequent Western blot analysis.

Proteomics analysis

TM4 mouse testicular Sertoli cells (3.0 × 10⁵ cells per well in 6‑well plates) were treated for 12 h with 10 μM testosterone or Ca²⁺‑free medium. ZO‑1‑containing protein complexes were immunoprecipitated from cell lysates using a ZO‑1‑specific antibody coupled to DynaGreen™ Protein A/G Magnetic Beads, followed by extensive washing and elution of bound proteins. Eluted proteins were reduced with 0.5 M TCEP (37 °C, 1 h), alkylated with 1 M iodoacetamide (dark, room temperature, 40 min), precipitated with acetone, and digested with trypsin (1:50 enzyme‑to‑protein ratio) in 100 mM TEAB at 37 °C overnight. The resulting peptides were desalted on C18 tips, dried, reconstituted in 0.1% formic acid, and spiked with iRT peptides. LC‑MS/MS analysis was performed on an EASY‑nanoLC 1200 coupled to an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos mass spectrometer. Peptides were separated on a C18 column (75 μm × 25 cm) at 250 nL/min with a 60‑min gradient from 4 to 50% solvent B (80% acetonitrile/0.1% formic acid). MS1 scans were acquired at 120,000 resolution (350–1200 m/z), followed by DIA MS2 with 60 variable isolation windows (overlap 1 m/z), 30,000 resolution, and normalized collision energy of 30%. Raw data were processed with Spectronaut 17 (Biognosys) using default settings, searching against the UniProt mouse proteome (21,992 entries). Trypsin was specified with carbamidomethylation of cysteine as a fixed modification, and oxidation of methionine and N‑terminal acetylation as variable modifications. FDR was set to 1% at both precursor and protein levels using a mutated decoy database. Label‑free quantification was performed using the MaxLFQ algorithm after local normalization. All LC‑MS/MS and data processing were performed by Shanghai Apexbio Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

Statistics and reproducibility

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 8.0 (GraphPad Software). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), as indicated in the figure legends. The number of biological replicates is denoted by n. Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For normally distributed data, parametric statistical tests were applied, including two-tailed Student’s t test, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), or two-way ANOVA, as appropriate. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

A minimum of three independent biological replicates was used for each experiment, based on preliminary data generated in our laboratory, to ensure adequate statistical power. Mice were randomly assigned to treatment and control groups to minimize selection bias. All treatments and assessments were conducted in a consistent manner. Animals were housed under identical conditions, and cage positions were rotated weekly to reduce potential environmental effects. Data analysis was performed in a randomized manner to enhance the objectivity of the study.
