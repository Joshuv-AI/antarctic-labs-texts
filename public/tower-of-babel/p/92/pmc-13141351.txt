Suicidal ideation comes to surface: a Suicide Stem Completion Measurement for implicit suicide risk
Authors: Yu Liu, Qingqiu Ai, Xueping Wang, Chunhua Kang, Weijian Li, Lingfeng Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSuicidal ideation tends to be hidden, limiting the ability of current assessment tools to reliably identify at-risk individuals. Based on the spreading activation model, this study developed the Suicide Stem Completion Measurement (SSCM) as an implicit assessment tool. First, the stem was determined and evaluated by experts. Subsequently, 2,250 adolescents were recruit from two middle schools to completed the SSCM and other relevant measures. Finally, an exploratory study with a small sample compared participants based on interviews and questionnaires, both of which confirmed the presence of self-injurious thoughts and behaviors. The results showed that: (1) The single-dimensional structure of the SSCM was good; (2) The scale showed acceptable internal consistency and split-half reliability; (3) Different clusters were effectively distinguished, and good correlations with various criterion validity were demonstrated; (4) The ROC curve analysis showed that SSCM had an AUC of 0.76 for identifying suicide risk; (5) Analyses based on both interview and questionnaire data revealed that participants with self-injurious thoughts and behaviors scored significantly differently on the SSCM compared to the control group. However, no significant difference was found between the two at-risk groups. These findings indicate that the SSCM demonstrates potential utility as an effective tool for assessing suicide risk.Keywords: Suicide Stem Completion Measurement, Adolescents, Suicide risk, Implicit measures, Reliability, Validity

Introduction

Suicide has become a growing global public health crisis, with global suicide deaths rising to 727,000 in 2021 compared to 2019 [1]. Recent data revealed that 1 in 5 adolescents experience suicidal ideation during their lifetime, while 7% will attempt suicide [2]. In China, more than 15% of children and adolescents report suicidal ideation [3, 4]. Evidence suggested suicide risk can be mitigated through appropriate screening and assessment [5]. However, despite nearly five decades of research since 1965, the predictive accuracy for suicide risk has shown little improvement [6]. Current self-report screening tools exhibit notable limitations, particularly in assessing the complexity and hidden nature of adolescent suicide risk [7, 8]. This makes the complete elimination of suicide risk an extraordinarily difficult challenge. Therefore, there is an urgent need for screening tools capable of effectively and objectively identifying individuals at high risk of suicide.

Self-report methods are widely used for suicide risk screening and assessment [6]. However, several factors have been identified that limit the validity and accuracy of these methods. Firstly, individuals may conceal or distort their suicidal ideation due to social desirability effect and stigma associated with suicide [9, 10]. Researches revealed that 78% of clinical patients who died by suicide explicitly denied having suicide ideation in their final clinical interview [11]. Secondly, recall bias is a well-recognized limitation of self-reporting method. Individuals may overestimate or underestimate their suicide risk due to fluctuating emotional states or memory distortions when retrospectively evaluating past experiences [12]. Klimes-Dougan et al. [13] found that 39% of participants who reported recent self-injury at baseline subsequently denied any lifetime history of such behaviors during follow-up assessments.

Beyond the limitations mentioned above, a key concern with existing self-report suicide risk scales is that most contain explicit references to “suicide” and “death”. For example, the Beck Scale for Suicide Ideation includes 19 items, 18 of which explicitly reference suicide [14]. Emerging evidences indicate that directly asking about suicide can trigger acute distress [15]. About one-fifth report clear signs of discomfort when recalling painful experiences, especially among those with prior attempts [16]. Empirical findings demonstrated that participants administered the Beck Scale for Suicide Ideation exhibited an 80% elevation in negative affect compared to controls completing the Quality-of-Life Measurement [17]. In China, deep cultural taboos around death and beliefs about children’s cognitive immaturity can further limit parents’ acceptance of direct suicide-related questions, potentially hindering effective risk detection.

Recently, based on the framework of automatic and controlled antecedents of suicidal ideation and action model, it has been emphasized that suicide susceptibility may arise simultaneously from both controlled pathways and automatic pathways [18]. Traditional self-report suicide scales typically measure general suicide-related cognition, which involves reflective and deliberative processes [19]. In contrast, implicit suicide ideation operates primarily at an automatic processing level, reflecting rapid and involuntary activation of suicide-related representations with minimal conscious control [18]. Several computer-based paradigms have been developed to assess individuals’ unconscious processing of suicide-related stimuli through, such as the implicit association test and attentional bias tasks [20, 21]. However, these paradigms often require multiple trials, making them time-consuming and unsuitable for large-scale screening. Additionally, emotional vocabulary or graphic materials are often used, but not all individuals who experience emotional distress decide to suicide [22]. Evidences suggest that compared to blog posts by individuals with depression and suicidal ideation, suicide notes consistently exhibit more positive emotional language [23]. Cha et al. [24] found that attentional bias toward suicide-related words significantly predicted suicide risk six months later, unlike bias to negative emotion words. However, using suicide-related words in implicit measures may also increase the risk of exposure.

The Word-Stem Completion Task (WSCT) accesses unconscious mental content by the words individuals generate to complete stems [25]. This task not only addresses the shortcomings of self-report measures but also enables the assessment of automatic associations related to implicit suicidal concepts. According to the spreading activation model, activating a concept in a semantic network automatically triggers related nodes, with stronger connections increasing this effect [26, 27]. Based on this theory, when completing the WSCT, individuals at higher risk for suicide are more likely to have suicide-related concepts repeatedly activated in their minds, making them more likely to complete stems with suicide-related words. Furthermore, the WSCT requires competition among multiple lexical candidates, and the preferential completion with suicide-related content reflects the relative accessibility of underlying suicide schemas in memory. For example, given the stem “han___”, they are more likely to generate a suicide-related word (e.g., “hang”) rather than a neutral word (e.g., “hand”). In summary, this measurement approach may capture a form of cognitive readiness toward suicidal interpretation under ambiguous conditions, representing vulnerability in preconscious processing systems. This interpretation aligns with theoretical frameworks distinguishing automatic and controlled antecedents of suicidal ideation and behavior. Therefore, the WSCT is a potentially valuable, non-intrusive tool for assessing implicit suicidal ideation.

To date, limited studies have employed the WSCT in suicide-related research, all of which were conducted within Western alphabetic systems. Gasiorowska [28] used a word-stem task to show that monetary primes increase implicit death-thought accessibility. Kheibari and Cerel [29] utilized the WSCT as a validated measure to evaluate the effectiveness of mortality salience manipulations, while concurrently examining the interaction between death cognition and self-esteem. This finding suggests that WSCT is an effective method for assessing implicit death-related thoughts. However, the stems employed in these studies may not be directly applicable in China due to cultural and linguistic differences. Notably, suicide methods differ significantly between China and Western countries, with firearms being a common means of suicide in the West, but prohibited in China [30]. As a result, the suicide-related stems developed in Western contexts may not accurately capture the suicide risk profiles of individuals in Chinese populations. Therefore, if these tools are to be used in China, they will require cultural adaptation.

In conclusion, the current situation of adolescent suicide remains deeply concerning, highlighting the urgent need for validated tools that can assess suicide risk from implicit perspective. While the WSCT has shown promising validity as an implicit assessment paradigm, its cultural adaptation and empirical validation within the Chinese context remain limited. To address this gap, the present study developed culturally appropriate stems and materials for a Suicide Stem Completion Measurement (SSCM) and validated its screening effectiveness in a large sample of Chinese adolescents, providing a novel and culturally sensitive approach for the early detection of suicide risk.

Method

Participants

Sample1: Convenience sampling was used. 17 psychology students, specifically specializing in the field of suicide research, were recruited to serve as an expert review panel. Among them, 6 were male and 11 were female; 3 were doctoral candidates, 12 were graduate students, and 2 were undergraduate students. The average age is 24.41 ± 3.32 years. This sample was utilized for the construction of the questionnaire dimensions.

Sample2: Cluster sampling was used. 2250 participants were recruited from junior high and high school in Zhejiang province of China. The data retention rate was 100%, as the online data collection method prevented any missing data, and a preliminary check revealed no abnormalities. Among them, 1,258 were male and 992 were female; 299 were in 7th grade, 287 in 8th grade, 270 in 9th grade, 503 in 10th grade, 471 in 11th grade, and 420 in 12th grade. The average age is 15.14 ± 1.60 years. Conduct reliability and validity tests on the questionnaire, including exploratory factor analysis, confirmatory factor analysis, internal consistency reliability analysis, split-half reliability analysis, criterion validity analysis, and known-groups validity analysis, based on this large-sample dataset.

Sample3: Convenience sampling was used. A total of 23 participants were recruited. Participants were divided into three groups. The risk group 1 (n = 5) comprised individuals with a hospital diagnosis of suicide risk, confirmed via interview to verify self-injurious thoughts and behaviors (SITB). The risk group 2 (n = 9) included individuals who screened positive for SITB on questionnaires and reported a suicide attempt within the past year. The control group (n = 9) consisted of individuals with no lifetime or past-year suicide attempts. Exclusion criteria were age over 18 years. Among them, 9 were male and 16 were female. The average age is 14.24 ± 1.59. Given the challenges associated with recruiting participants with a history of self-injury, the sample size for this subgroup was limited; nevertheless, an exploratory validation of the SSCM was still conducted.

Development of a SSCM

First, literature was reviewed to determine the word-stem content and the dimensionality of the scale. Previous studies have shown that international researchers often use terms related to both “suicide” and “death” in WSCT. In English, besides “suicide”, commonly used words include “murder”, “coffin”, “corpse” and “killed” [28, 31, 32]. However, “suicide” specifically refers to self-injury behaviors, whereas “death” is broader and can include accidental or passive deaths, such as “accidents” [33]. Sohn [34] found that the Implicit Association Test for Death (IATD) was less effective than the Implicit Association Test for Suicide (IATS), which uses specific suicide-related words and has been shown to better predict suicide attempts within six months [35]. This indicates that individuals process “suicide” and “death” differently. Therefore, this study aimed to develop a tool specifically for assessing suicide risk by retaining only suicide-related words and excluding general death-related terms, resulting in a unidimensional structure.

To ensure cultural relevance in the Chinese context, additional literature and everyday language were examined. In addition to “suicide”, Värnik et al. [36] noted that word stems in such tasks often include the most common suicide methods, such as “pendre” (to hang), “corde” (rope), and “veine” (wrist), which correspond in Chinese to terms like “上吊” [hanging] and “割腕” [wrist-cutting]. These items were added to the assessment lexicon. Furthermore, 62 additional suicide-related terms were collected from social media (e.g., Weibo), news reports, and spoken language, including “投井” [drowning in a well], “殉情” [dying for love], and “中毒” [poisoning], forming a comprehensive word pool. After an initial screening, highly explicit or emotionally intense stems (e.g., “坠亡” [falling to death], “绝命” [ending one’s life], “勒死” [strangulation]) were excluded, leaving 16 suicide-related for further evaluation.

20 neutral word stems were additionally selected to improve measurement precision. These stems could only form non-suicidal words, aiming to mask implicit suicidal thoughts and reduce participants’ awareness of the task’s purpose [37]. Consequently, they were excluded from later scale indicators. To avoid interference from extraneous factors [38], the stemming task was administered first. Although we also developed word stems related to death, subsequent analyses showed poor structural validity, so these results are not reported.

The final 36 selected words were evaluated by 17 experts. The vocabulary was evaluated in groups, categorized into four categories: two groups of suicide-related words and two groups of neutral words. Each suicide group contained eight stems, while each neutral group included ten stems. The task of the experts was to review the stems and select from each group 3 to 8 stems that best represent the theme. The top 3 stems from each suicide group were selected, resulting in a total of 6, e.g., “跳___” (The stem can form the suicide-related word, which means suicide by jumping from height.); the top 5 stems from each neutral group were selected, resulting in a total of 10, e.g., “答___” (The stem can only form the neutral-related word, such as meaning answer.). The final scale was composed of 16 word-stems, which can be found in Supplementary Materials S1.

The scoring method for the SSCM is as follows: For each of the six suicide-related word stems, such as “腕” [wrist], one point is awarded if the completed word forms a suicide-related phrase, such as “割腕” [wrist-cutting]. If the completed word is unrelated to suicide (e.g., “手腕” [wrist part of the hand] or “脚腕” [ankle]), zero points are assigned. The total score ranges from 0 to 6, with higher scores indicating greater suicide risk [39].

Measurements

Suicidal behaviors questionnaire-revised

The scale was developed by Osman [40], with the Chinese version revised by Shi et al. [41]. The scale consists of 4 questions, such as “Have you ever thought about or attempted to kill yourself?” The total score ranges from 3 to 18, with higher scores indicating an increased risk of suicide. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the scale in this study was 0.80.

Suicide attitude

The scale was derived from the General Social Survey conducted by the National Opinion Research Center (NORC) [42]. The scale consists of 4 questions, such as “Do you believe a person can end his or her life if he or she has an incurable disease?” The scale was scored a 2-point format, where 0 represents “disagree with the item statement” and 1 represents “agree with the item statement”. The total score ranges from 0 to 4, with higher scores indicating more pronounced suicidal attitudes. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the scale in this study was 0.72.

Patient health questionnaire-9

The scale was derived from the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (4th edition) [43, 44], with the Chinese version verify by Wang et al. [45]. The scale consists of 9 questions, such as “Little interest or pleasure in doing things.” The questionnaire was scored by Likert 4 points, ranging from 0 (none) to 3 (almost every day). The total score ranges from 0 to 27, with higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the scale in this study was 0.80.

Deliberate self-harm inventory

The scale was developed by Bjärehed [46] to assess the frequency of self- injury. The scale consists of 10 questions, such as “cutting own wrists, arms, or other body parts”. The scale was scored by Likert 7 points, ranging from 0 (never) to 6(more than five times). The total score ranging from 0 to 60 with higher scores indicating a greater frequency of self-injury. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the scale in this study was 0.82.

Positive psychological Capital Questionnaires

The scale was developed by Zhang [47], which referred to the scale dimensions compiled by Luthans [48]. The scale consists of 26 questions. It encompasses 4 dimensions: self-efficacy, resilience, hope, and optimism. The questionnaire was scored by Likert 7 points, ranging from 1 (poorly met) to 7 (well met). The total score ranges from 26 to 182, with higher scores indicating a more positive mindset and emotional state. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the scale and its dimensions in this study were 0.89, 0.90, 0.41, 0.71, and 0.90, respectively. Resilience dimension was excluded from the subsequent analysis because of its low reliability.

Procedure and statistical analysis

All data were collected through online questionnaires. All test administrators were psychology graduate students who read standardized instructions before the assessment and obtained informed consent from all participants. The study was approved by the ethics committee of the Ethics Committee of Researcher’s University.

The statistical analyses in this study were conducted using SPSS and Amos software. Initially, descriptive statistics and a test of variance were performed on the collected data. Subsequently, exploratory factor analysis and confirmatory factor analysis were conducted. The model’s fit indices satisfied the recommended criteria (CFI > 0.90, NFI > 0.09, IFI > 0.09, TLI > 0.90, SRMR < 0.08, RMSEA < 0.08) [49]. Then, reliability and validity analyses were performed, along with ROC curve analysis. Finally, the differences in the scores of the SSCM among the three groups of participants were tested.

Result

Common method bias

Harman’s single-factor test was conducted to assess common method bias [50]. An exploratory factor analysis was performed on all questionnaire items. The results showed that the first factor accounted for 31.85% of the total variance, which is below the recommended threshold of 40% [51]. This indicates that common method bias is not a significant concern in this study.

Project differentiation

The item-total correlation coefficient was used to assess item discrimination [52]. All items demonstrated significant correlations with the total scale score (r = .50 ~ .76, ps < 0.001), indicating satisfactory discriminative power. Detailed results are presented in Table 1.

An exploratory factor analysis was conducted on the data, yielding a KMO value of 0.805 and a significance level of less than 0.001 for Bartlett’s test of sphericity. The factors were extracted using principal component analysis, revealing one factor with an eigenvalue greater than 1, without the removal of any questionnaire items. This factor exhibited an eigenvalue of 2.62 and accounted for a cumulative variance of 43.61%.

Measurement reliability

The internal consistency reliability test of the SSCM revealed that the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the scale was 0.74, while the split-half reliability was 0.70. These results indicate that the scale demonstrates adequate internal consistency reliability.

ROC analysis

The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted to assess the diagnostic value of the SSCM. Firstly, participants were classified as 0 (non-risk) or 1 (risk) based on the cutoff score of 9 on the Suicide Behavior Questionnaire [54]. Secondly, the SSCM score was used as the test variable and the Suicide Behavior Questionnaire result was used as the state variable to plot the ROC curve, in order to determine the optimal cutoff point, the ROC curve, and the area under the curve (AUC). The results showed that the AUC was 0.76 (95% CI = [0.73, 0.79], p < .001), indicating acceptable discriminative validity. The optimal cutoff point determined by the maximum Youden Index was 1, yielding a sensitivity of 0.73 and a specificity of 0.73. Details are shown in Fig. 2.

Non-parametric comparisons

The Kruskal-Wallis test was conducted to examine differences in SSCM scores among three groups: Group 1: risk group 1 (interview screening for individuals with self-injury thoughts and behaviors); Group 2: risk group 2 (questionnaire screening for individuals with self-injury thoughts and behaviors) and Group 3: control group. The results indicated a significant difference among the groups (χ² = 9.77, p < .01). Subsequent pairwise comparisons using the Mann-Whitney U test revealed no significant difference between Group 1 (Mdn = 1.0, IQR = 1.0–2.0) and Group 2 (Mdn = 1.0, IQR = 0.0–4.0)(p > .05), whereas significant differences were observed between Group 1 and Group 3 (Mdn = 0.0, IQR = 0.0–0.0)(p < .01), and between Group 2 and Group 3 (p < .05).

Discussion

This study developed the SSCM based on the spreading activation model to assess the implicit suicide ideation. And its reliability and validity were assessed in a large sample of Chinese adolescents. The results indicated that the SSCM has a single-dimensional structure, exhibiting acceptable reliability and validity. Furthermore, it demonstrated favorable overall measurement properties and promising screening validity for suicide risk. The SSCM avoids directly mentioning sensitive words, which reduces participants’ resistance and response bias. Overall, our findings suggest that the SSCM may serve as a practical screening instrument that addresses some of the limitations of conventional self-report measures and traditional computer-based implicit tasks.

Development and structure of scale

The psychological content measured by a scale is influenced by its structural dimensions and the number of items included. This study aims to assess and screen an individual’s overall risk of suicide, rather than focusing on specific dimensions such as attitudes toward suicide and negative perceptions of it. Consequently, a one-dimensional overall suicide risk assessment scale was developed for the stem questionnaire. According to the spreading activation model, words with similar concepts are stored within the same node. The SSCM for this single dimension can effectively activate nodes related to that dimension. This concentration enables researchers to measure vocabulary activation related to this dimension more accurately. If multiple dimensions are included, associations may become too fragmented, potentially reducing the accuracy of measuring vocabulary activation within each dimension.

In selecting suicide-related stem, this study referenced internationally recognized terms such as “自杀”[suicide] and “割腕”[wrist-cutting] [36]. These terms have been extensively utilized in prior research, thereby ensuring the tool’s feasibility. Simultaneously, the vocabulary related to suicide has been locally adapted to align with China’s cultural and linguistic context. For instance, terms that reflect Chinese characteristics, such as “轻生”[another expression of suicide] have been incorporated. While these words are seldom found in Western studies, they hold significant relevance within the context of Chinese culture.

In terms of statistical indicators, the dimensions and items of this questionnaire were well evaluated: An evaluation of item discrimination revealed that the correlation coefficients between item scores and total scores exceeded 0.5, indicating effective discrimination across all items. An exploratory factor analysis of the sample data identified a single factor with an eigenvalue greater than 1, which accounted for 43.61% of the cumulative variance. This finding supports the validity of the measure with respect to its dimensionality and refutes the hypothesis that there are redundant items. Conducted in conjunction with a confirmatory factor analysis, the results demonstrated that the model exhibited a satisfactory fit and met the established criteria. In summary, both exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses strongly support the one-factor structure of the self-administered SSCM.

Reliability of scale

Regarding the internal consistency of the implicit measure, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.74 for its six items is considered acceptable, while the split-half reliability test yielded a coefficient of 0.70. This value aligns with prior research on similar implicit instruments; for instance, in the Implicit Positive and Negative Affect Scale developed by Wang et al. [55], the test-retest reliability (ICC) for each factor was 0.72 and 0.73. Furthermore, according to Greenwald and Lai [56], such values are regarded as relatively high for implicit measures. Nonetheless, this level of reliability suggests the presence of non-negligible measurement error. In the current study, the relatively modest reliability may be attributed to the inherent volatility of implicit measurements, which capture more transient and context-dependent cognitive processes. Crucially, as argued by Borsboom et al. [57] and Tuerlinckx et al. [58], a measure can be valid (i.e., caused by the to-be-measured attribute) even when it is not reliable. Therefore, the current findings may actually underestimate the true relationships between variables. This attenuation bias underscores the necessity for future research to refine and improve the measurement of implicit constructs, particularly in adolescent populations.

Validity of scale

Conducted for the purpose of validity testing, the study employed both known-group validity and criterion validity tests. The results from the t-tests and chi-square tests showed the ability of the SSCM to distinguish between different groups. Girls scored significantly higher than boys on the SSCM, and on-campus residents reported higher scores than off-campus peers. While these demographic differences are consistent with prior findings [59, 60], they also highlight the potential role of social-environmental factors, such as peer interactions and bullying—in shaping implicit suicidal ideation among adolescents. adolescents with a family history of suicide or mental illness scored significantly higher. The vulnerability of individuals to environmental influences, coupled with the concept that suicidal behaviors can be “contagious” within individuals or groups, emphasizes the potential for interactions with suicidal individuals to reinforce their perceptions of suicide and promote the assimilation of related concepts [61]. A clear distinction is observed in the prevalence of peer self-injury across different demographic groups. In this context, Syed [62] have found a significant positive correlation between the suicides of friends and adolescents’ own suicidal ideation. Furthermore, the results of the criterion validity testing revealed a significant correlation between SSCM score and each of the criterion. Specifically, a strong positive correlation was observed between the scores of the SSCM score and Suicidal Behaviors. These findings suggest that heightened suicidal ideation is strongly associated with an increased likelihood of future suicidal behaviors, supporting the well-established notion that suicidal ideation is a key predictor of suicide attempts [63]. Additionally, a positive correlation between SSCM score and Suicidal Attitudes was identified. An intervention study found that adolescents who viewed suicide prevention videos showed improvements in their suicidal attitudes and a reduction in suicidal ideation [64]. Depression has been positively correlated with SSCM score. This relationship may be attributed to the fact that individuals with depression often experience a sense of hopelessness about the future, leading them to view suicide as an escape from overwhelming negative emotions [65]. The present study also found that scores on the SSCM were significantly positively correlated with deliberate self-harm. This finding is consistent with previous empirical research, which has shown that the combination of different functions of self-harm, as well as its frequency and variety, can effectively predict suicidal ideation [66]. The negative correlation between SSCM scores and Positive Psychological Capital suggests that adolescents with fewer psychological resources (e.g., lower self-efficacy, optimism) may be more vulnerable to implicit suicidal ideation. This finding underscores the potential value of strength-based interventions in suicide prevention [67]. Taken together, these findings suggest that the validity indicators of the SSCM are statistically significant.

Screening accuracy for suicide risk

The results of the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis indicate that the optimal cut-off score for the SSCM among Chinese adolescents was determined to be a score of 1. With a cutoff score of 1, sensitivity and specificity were both 0.73, reflecting a balance between identifying at-risk individuals and minimizing false positives. This cutoff suggests that participants who generate one or more suicide-related word stems may be at heightened risk for suicidal behavior and warrant further assessment. Given the moderate accuracy, it is important to emphasize that the SSCM is not a diagnostic tool; scores at or above this threshold should prompt follow-up clinical interviews to confirm risk rather than being interpreted as definitive evidence of suicidality. However, in school-based screening settings, any word stem recognized as related to suicide should be taken seriously. Our finding indicates that when participants generate one or more stems associated with suicide, they may be at risk for suicidal behavior and should be promptly assessed. This approach may substantially improve the efficiency and accuracy of suicide risk screening.

To examine the clinical validity of the SSCM, we further conducted an exploratory study with a small sample. The results preliminarily showed that both the self-injury and suicidal ideation groups scored significantly higher on the SSCM compared to the control group, providing initial evidence that the scale may effectively distinguish individuals at risk for self-injury or suicidal thoughts from those without such risks. Given the small sample size, these findings should be interpreted with caution, and further studies with larger samples are needed to confirm the scale’s discriminative validity and refine its clinical applicability.

Novelty and contributions

The novelty of this study can be articulated from both theoretical and practical perspectives. First, at the theoretical level, this study advances the conceptualization of implicit suicidal ideation by operationalizing it within a structured psychometric framework. Grounded in dual-process perspectives on suicidal cognition, we translate an experimental WSCT into a measurable construct embedded within a formal scale. This approach strengthens the theoretical integration between implicit cognitive processes and suicidal ideation research, providing a clearer framework for future hypothesis-driven investigations. Second, at the practical level, this study develops and validates a concise, user-friendly instrument suitable for large-scale screening, particularly in school-based adolescent populations. The absence of explicit suicide-related wording reduces potential response bias and exposure effects, enhancing acceptability in sensitive contexts. Its demonstrated reliability and validity in school settings underscore its applicability for early identification and mental health assessment in real-world environments.

Limitations and further directions

The present findings support the validity of the SSCM; however, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study sample was limited to adolescents, which constrains the generalizability of the results. Although the current study exploratorily examined the applicability of the instrument in a small clinical sample, the effects of medication use, comorbidities, and other variables in these clinical participants were not strictly controlled due to the difficulty of sample collection. Future research should validate the reliability and validity of the scale in larger and more representative samples (e.g., college students, social workers, and clinical patients) while accounting for potential confounding factors, thereby enhancing the robustness and external validity of the findings. Second, the study relied solely on an explicit self-report measure for criterion. Given the concealment of implicit measurement of suicide, the correlation between self-report and implicit measures may be suboptimal. Future studies are encouraged to incorporate additional implicit assessment tools to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of suicidal ideation. Third, this measurement was developed within a specific Chinese linguistic context, and its items incorporate culturally specific elements, which pose limitations for cross-cultural measurement. Therefore, when applied to other linguistic or cultural groups, systematic cultural adaptation and revalidation of its psychometric properties are necessary to ensure measurement equivalence.

Conclusion

This study developed the SSCM based on the spreading activation model as an implicit tool to assess suicide risk. The SSCM demonstrated a reliable and valid unidimensional structure in an adolescent sample. Participants scoring above 1 were classified as potentially at risk, indicating the scale’s effectiveness in identifying individuals with implicit suicide risk. Overall, the SSCM is a creatively designed paper-and-pencil instrument developed from an implicit perspective. It offers a psychometrically valid measure that enhances suicide research and holds promise for practical applications.
