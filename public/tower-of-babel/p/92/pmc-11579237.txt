Increased visual alpha‐band activity during self‐paced finger tapping does not affect early visual stimulus processing
Authors: C Gundlach, M M Müller
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlpha‐band activity is thought to be involved in orchestrating neural processing within and across brain regions relevant to various functions such as perception, cognition, and motor activity. Across different studies, attenuated alpha‐band activity has been linked to increased neural excitability. Yet, there have been conflicting results concerning the consequences of alpha‐band modulations for early sensory processing. We here examined whether movement‐related alterations in visual alpha‐band activity affected the early sensory processing of visual stimuli. For this purpose, in an EEG experiment, participants were engaged in a voluntary finger‐tapping task while passively viewing flickering dots. We found extensive and expected movement‐related amplitude modulations of motor alpha‐ and beta‐band activity with event‐related‐desynchronization (ERD) before and during, and event‐related‐synchronization (ERS) after single voluntary finger taps. Crucially, while a visual alpha‐band ERS accompanied the motor alpha‐ERD before and during each finger tap, flicker‐evoked Steady‐State‐Visually‐Evoked‐Potentials (SSVEPs), as a marker of early visual sensory gain, were not modulated in amplitude. As early sensory stimulus processing was unaffected by amplitude‐modulated visual alpha‐band activity, this argues against the idea that alpha‐band activity represents a mechanism by which early sensory gain modulation is implemented. The distinct neural dynamics of visual alpha‐band activity and early sensory processing may point to distinct and multiplexed neural selection processes in visual processing.Keywords: early visual sensory gain, EEG, finger tapping, neural oscillations, steady state visual evoked potentials, vision

INTRODUCTION

Meaningful interactions with the environment rely on the integration of information across senses and sensory cortices and the interaction between sensory and the motor system. Processes such as perception and action are thought to be tightly interrelated and interact at different processing stages rather than sequentially following each other (Pezzulo et al., 2018). In such an interactive framework, sensory information (i.e. from proprioception, somatosensation, vision, etc.), internal predictions, as well as motor signals, interact at various timescales to allow for optimal tuning of ongoing effective behavior (Scott et al., 2015) as well as active sensing (Davidson et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2016) or predicting sensory consequences of movements (Crapse & Sommer, 2008; Schröger, Kotz, et al., 2015; Timm et al., 2014).

One neural signature that has been linked to orchestrating the flow of information is alpha‐band activity (Fiebelkorn & Kastner, 2019; Gaillard et al., 2020; Helfrich et al., 2019; Schroeder & Lakatos, 2009). Distinct alpha‐band activity is measurable in different sensory cortices and presumed relevant for visual, auditory, and somatomotor processing (Haegens et al., 2015). Modulations of alpha‐band activity have been linked to changes in neural excitability with lower amplitudes, representing increased neural excitability and higher amplitudes representing lower neural excitability and potentially leading to suppressed or inhibited stimulus processing (Iemi et al., 2016; Jensen & Mazaheri, 2010; Klimesch et al., 2007; Morrow et al., 2023; Samaha et al., 2020). With regards to movements, it is well established that motor alpha‐band activity is modulated by motor activity. Amplitude decreases, so‐called alpha event‐related‐desynchronization (ERD), before and during each movement are associated with motor preparatory activity (Babiloni et al., 2008; Brinkman et al., 2014, 2016; Brovelli et al., 2004; Deiber et al., 2012; Klostermann et al., 2007; McFarland et al., 2000; Pfurtscheller & Neuper, 1997; Stolk et al., 2019; Toro et al., 1994) and thought to be relevant for the modulation of excitability and information flow in the motor system. Following the movement, motor alpha‐band activity usually depicts a transient amplitude rebound with an increase above baseline levels, i.e. an event‐related‐synchronization (ERS). Such an ERD‐ERS pattern is also measurable for motor beta‐band activity and both frequency bands are, due to comparable spatio‐temporal dynamics, often grouped to form the somatomotor mu‐activity (Cuevas et al., 2014; Hari, 2006; Miller et al., 2010; Tiihonen et al., 1989).

Excitability changes in the somatomotor system are often associated with changes in alpha‐band activity in different brain regions. During (often lateralized) movements, such as simple finger taps or the processing of somatosensory stimuli, the alpha‐ERD pattern over somatomotor regions is often accompanied by an non‐lateralized ERS of visual alpha‐band activity in both hemispheres (Haegens et al., 2012; Pfurtscheller, 1992, 2003; Pfurtscheller & Silva, 1999). Such an antagonism may depict the selection of information between modalities, relevant for the current task and was as such even found to be behaviorally relevant in a somatosensory task (Haegens et al., 2012). Alpha‐band modulations are often seen in the context of attentional selection. Here alpha‐band amplitudes are usually higher in regions representing the unattended stimulus as compared to regions representing the attentionally selected stimulus (Foster & Awh, 2018; Foxe & Snyder, 2011; Morrow et al., 2023; Van Diepen et al., 2019). In tasks involving the selection of information from multiple sensory streams, analogous modulations of alpha‐band amplitudes are often found as well. Attentional selection of one modality over the other, that is, attending to auditory while ignoring visual stimuli, results in an increase in alpha‐band amplitudes over the unattended as compared to the attended modality, aiding the overall selection of relevant information (Bauer et al., 2012; Foxe et al., 1998; Keil et al., 2016; Mazaheri et al., 2014). The reported antagonistic modulation of somatomotor and visual alpha‐band activity during movements may thus be seen as an attenuation of potentially irrelevant visual information by increased visual alpha‐band activity during periods with increased motor activity.

Crucially, the functional relevance of such alpha‐band modulations is still not well understood. Some studies showed that signatures of local neural processing, such as local fMRI‐BOLD activity, are decreased with increased alpha‐band amplitudes (Goldman et al., 2002; Ritter et al., 2009) and local gamma‐band activity is more prevalent during low‐amplitude phases of alpha‐band activity (Osipova et al., 2008; Roux et al., 2013; Spaak et al., 2012; Voytek et al., 2010). These findings suggest that sensory stimulus processing should be altered by visual alpha‐band activity (see Iemi et al., 2019). On the other hand, recent studies found that sensory gain modulation, a robust signature of attention‐modulated stimulus processing (Hillyard et al., 1998), is in fact unaffected by alpha‐band activity: Amplified sensory gain modulations in early visual areas during spatial visual attention are not affected by trial‐by‐trial amplitude changes of alpha‐band activity (Antonov et al., 2020; Gundlach, Moratti, et al., 2020; see also Keitel et al., 2019; Zhigalov & Jensen, 2020). In a recent study, we examined the potential interaction between lateralized visual alpha‐band activity and Steady‐State‐Visual‐Evoked‐Potential (SSVEP) amplitude modulations as a marker of early visual sensory gain (see below) in a spatial attentional cueing task (Gundlach, Moratti, et al., 2020). While paying attention to either a left or right flickering ring resulted in an increase in SSVEP amplitude for the cued as compared to the uncued stimulus as well as lower alpha‐band amplitudes contralateral to the cued as compared to the uncued stimulus, both modulations were unrelated. Sorting trials by the amount of alpha‐lateralization did not affect SSVEP amplitude modulations and we concluded that alpha‐band amplitude modulations do not implement early sensory gain. These results demonstrate that early sensory consequences of alpha‐band modulations are still not well understood.

Here, we aimed to study the role of visual alpha‐band activity on early sensory processing. Crucially, we wanted to examine whether our previous finding that alpha‐band and early sensory gain modulations are unrelated (Gundlach, Moratti, et al., 2020) extends beyond the context of a spatial attention task. For this purpose, we took advantage of the extensive and antagonistic alpha‐band modulations in motor and visual regions during simple voluntary movements, with somatomotor alpha‐band decreases being accompanied by increases in visual alpha‐band activity, to study the effects of visual alpha‐band activity on early visual stimulus processing. In an EEG experiment, participants performed a self‐paced finger‐tapping task while task‐irrelevant flickering dots were presented and participants were instructed to passively view these stimuli. Flickering stimuli evoke the SSVEP, an early neural response with its sources in earl visual cortex that depicts sustained modulation through early sensory gain by an amplitude increase for attended as compared to unattended frequency‐tagged stimuli (Boylan et al., 2023; Di Russo et al., 2001; Kim et al., 2007; Müller & Hillyard, 2000; Müller, Picton, et al., 1998; Müller, Teder‐Sälejärvi, et al., 1998). We expected motor alpha (and beta‐) band amplitudes to display an ERD‐ before and during and an ERS pattern after single voluntary finger taps. This pattern should be accompanied by an ERS of visual alpha‐band amplitudes before each finger tap. Given our previous findings, we expected that visual alpha‐band modulations do not affect early sensory processing, and SSVEP amplitudes are not modulated by single finger taps.

METHODS

Participants

A total of 20 participants were invited, took part in the experiment, and either received class credits for their psychology classes or were financially reimbursed for their participation (10 Euros per hour). The sample size was determined based on available economic and time resources (no statistical a priori power analysis was conducted). One participant was discarded from further analysis, as button presses throughout the experiments were not recorded correctly due to technical issues with the keyboard. Overall, 19 participants (12 females, mean age 26.11 years; range 19–38) entered the analysis. Participants gave written informed consent and were informed about the nature of the experiment before the experimental session. The study was designed and conducted according to the declaration of Helsinki and the local ethics committee.

Stimuli, procedure, and task

Stimuli were created with custom scripts using the Psychophysics toolbox 3 (Brainard, 1997; Kleiner et al., 2007) implemented in Matlab R2016 (The MathWorks, Natick, MA) running in a Linux environment and were presented on a CRT monitor with a resolution of 1024‐by‐768 pixels and a refresh rate of 85 Hz situated 80 cm in front of the subject. The stimulus display consisted of a rectangular Random‐Dot‐Kinematogramm (RDK), presented centrally with a width of 16.9° and a height of 12.7° of visual angle on a gray background in addition to a fixation cross (see Figure 1a). The RDK was comprised of 100 bright gray squares with a length of 0.49° of visual angle moving around in random Brownian motion and flickering between the background gray (30% of maximum luminance) and bright gray (80% of maximum luminance) at a rate of 14.167 Hz.

After introducing the aim and nature of the experiment and EEG preparation, participants were seated comfortably in an electromagnetically shielded and acoustically damped chamber. The main experiment consisted of a self‐paced finger‐tapping task. Participants were instructed to tap the arrow‐down button on a keyboard about every 8 s. They should refrain from mentally counting the seconds. Previous studies have pointed towards a relationship between counting as a cognitive operation and the somatomotor system, finger representations and related alpha‐band activity modulations (Domahs et al., 2010; Klein et al., 2011; Salehzadeh et al., 2023). As we were interested in the modulatory role of “pure” motor and motor preparatory neural activity (see below), we aimed at reducing any contamination of these signals by potential modulations associated with counting as a potential strategy to tap with an appropriate timing. Before electrophysiological recordings, subjects practiced this pace with a white circle on the computer screen that was briefly flashed red every 8 s. During the recordings, after each block participants got feedback from the experimenter on their average inter‐tap interval within the preceding block. While doing the finger‐tapping task, participants were instructed to keep their eyes open and to fixate on the central fixation cross on the screen. In addition, they were told that a task‐irrelevant display of randomly moving and flickering dots would be presented to probe early visual processing. The experiment consisted of six blocks each with a length of 6 min separated by brief breaks.

Electrophysiological data recording and preprocessing

EEG was recorded from 64 Ag/AgCl electrodes mounted in an elastic cap with an ActiveTwo Amplifier (BioSemi, Amsterdam) at a sampling rate of 512 Hz with a high‐pass filter of 0.16 Hz and a low‐pass filter of 100 Hz and stored for offline analysis. To measure horizontal and vertical eye movements as well as blinks two electrodes were placed horizontally at the canthi of both eyes and two electrodes vertically above and below the right eye. Markers for time points of finger taps as well as onsets of every 85th flicker‐cycle were saved in the EEG file to allow aligning physical presentation, behavior, and EEG signals across trials.

For offline data analysis, the EEGLAB toolbox (Delorme & Makeig, 2004), as well as custom Matlab scripts (The MathWorks, Natick, MA), were used. As a first step, continuous data was resampled to 256 Hz. Since the SSVEP is an oscillatory response evoked by and tightly time‐locked to a flickering visual stimulation, the extraction of the signal across different trials (i.e., finger taps) and subsequent averaging requires the exact synchronization of the flicker onset of a certain cycle with the time point of the keyboard press. To this end, for each finger tap the closest time point was estimated that corresponded to the next “on” frame of visual flicker, and this time point was taken as a reference or “zero” point to extract epochs of ±3.5 s for analysis. Given the oscillatory nature of the signal at about 14 Hz, time gaps between finger taps and flicker onsets were in the range of ±35.3 ms. All epochs with blinks in a time range from ±3.5 s around the finger tap were discarded. Based on an automated procedure, blinks were identified as time points for which amplitudes in the high pass filtered (f = 0.5 Hz), bipolarized vertical eye channels exceeded 100 μV. This resulted in an average number of 146.11 (SD = 49.90) trials that entered the analysis for each subject. These initial blink‐free time windows (±3.5 s around finger taps) were larger than the later analyzed time windows (±3.25 s around finger taps, see below) to minimize any contamination of EEG signals by blink‐related dynamics and to discard edge artifacts of the implemented Gabor‐transform (see below).

Data analysis

Behavioral analysis

Finger taps were registered as button presses. For each participant inter‐tap intervals were calculated. To test for potential variations of finger taps across the time course of the experiment, the six experimental blocks, that is, 36 min in total, were divided into 60 bins (each 36 s long), and the number of finger taps in each time bin was estimated. To test whether finger taps were uniformly distributed across the experiment we used a permutation‐based approach (Ernst, 2004; Maris & Oostenveld, 2007), to create an empiric uniform distribution of finger taps across the experiment. For each subject, the bins (1–60) with the measured number of finger taps were shuffled leading to a permuted count of finger taps in each bin across subjects. Repeating this 10,000 times leads to an empiric distribution of finger taps in each bin created by chance which is, thus, uniform across the time course of the experiment, that is, across all bins. The actual recorded number of finger taps within each bin was then compared to an empiric confidence interval (CI) of 95% derived from the permuted data.

Analysis of electrophysiological data

The main aim was to examine temporal dynamics of neural activity during self‐paced finger taps in somatomotor as well as in early visual cortex. Do single voluntary finger taps lead to the expected modulations of somatomotor and visual alpha‐band activity? And are the expected amplitude increases of visual alpha‐band activity mirrored by a decrease in SSVEP amplitudes as a marker of early sensory processing?

To extract SSVEP amplitudes, we used a spatial filtering approach by which the SSVEP signal is extracted from an optimally weighted sum of all EEG sensors (Cohen & Gulbinaite, 2017). This procedure derives spatial filters with an optimized representation of the (SSVEP‐) signal over noise individually determined for each subject. For the implementation of this procedure, the signal component was centered at our SSVEP frequency (14.167 Hz) and had a bandwidth of 0.5 Hz while noise was defined as spectral components ±2 Hz apart from the signal and had a bandwidth of 2 Hz. For a more numerically stable estimation of the spatial filter estimation, the covariance matrix was regularized by adding 1% of the mean of its eigenvalues to its diagonal (Gulbinaite et al., 2019; Schettino et al., 2020). Individually filter‐projected epoched data (see Figure 2 for a topographical representation of the filter weights) was detrended for the entire length of the epoch (−3.5 to 3.5 s) to remove any general drifts and then averaged across trials. With a Gabor‐filter‐based transformation of the data (bandwidth of filter: FWHMfrequency = ±1 Hz; FWHMtime = ±220.636 ms) individual mean SSVEP amplitude time courses around finger taps −3250 to 3250 ms were then analyzed. Such a wide window was selected for the analysis to encompass extensive ERD and ERS modulations across several seconds associated with voluntary movements (see Pfurtscheller & Lopes da Silva, 1999). To test for systematic modulations of SSVEP amplitudes across time, Gabor‐filtered SSVEP amplitude time courses were first baseline corrected by dividing each amplitude value by the average SSVEP amplitude measured between 3250 and 3000 ms before the actual finger tap. This time window was selected as it depicted a presumably neutral state of the motor system (and thus the visual system) 3 s before and around 5 s after each finger tap executed with a designated inter‐tap interval of 8 s. SSVEP time courses were thus depicted as the change of amplitude relative to this pre‐tap baseline amplitude in percent and allowed for the analysis of modulations potentially unfolding over a longer time window. Modulations between −3000 and 3000 ms relative to finger tap were tested with a running t test against zero. To control for false positive results, results of the running t test were cluster‐corrected with a cluster mass approach (Bullmore et al., 1999; Gundlach, Moratti, et al., 2020; Maris & Oostenveld, 2007). Here point‐by‐point t tests were first thresholded at a p < .05. A series of time points with significant changes from zero formed a cluster and for each cluster, the t‐values of each statistical test were summed. These t‐sum values were then compared with a surrogate distribution of random t‐sum values. This random t‐sum‐value distribution was created by first shuffling the data label (SSVEP modulation vs. pre‐tap baseline vector of zeros; treating them as coming from one single distribution), calculating a running t test and extracting the largest t‐sum value from significant clusters and repeating this 10,000 times. For each cluster of the real data the respective t‐sum value was compared to this distribution of surrogate t‐sum values expected by chance and an empiric Monte‐Carlo p‐value was derived. Cluster Cohen's D values are calculated by dividing the average modulation for each cluster by the standard deviation of cluster‐averaged modulations across participants and reported as suggested by Meyer et al. (2021).

In addition to the analysis of SSVEPs as a marker of early visual processing, we analyzed the dynamics of ongoing neural oscillatory activity in the alpha and beta frequency bands associated with the voluntary finger taps. This analysis was also based on flicker‐aligned trials, as described above. Since the SSVEP signal had a frequency peak at 14.167 Hz (plus harmonics) we wanted to minimize the influence of SSVEP signals when analyzing alpha‐ and beta‐band activity. This was achieved by again using the calculated spatial filters of the SSVEP‐optimization procedure described above. In contrast to the SSVEP analysis, we now selected all but the RESS component depicting the SSVEP signal. By excluding the RESS component and back‐projecting all other components, the contribution of SSVEPs to other frequency bands was minimized (Cohen & Gulbinaite, 2017). Subsequently, data was transformed to reference‐free scalp current densities (SCDs), to yield more spatially distinct signal maxima (Ferree, 2006; Gundlach, Moratti, et al., 2020; Kayser & Tenke, 2006; Müller et al., 2018). For the extractions of oscillatory activity from different frequency bands, single‐trial data (i.e. induced activity) at each electrode was transformed via multiple single Gabor filters with frequencies from 3.67 to 35.167 Hz in 0.25 Hz steps with the same filter bandwidth as described above for the SSVEPs, yielding the same temporal resolution for the time courses of all signals of interest. The resulting amplitude time courses for each frequency and electrode were then averaged across trials, yielding mean individual amplitude time courses for 3.67–35.167 Hz around finger taps. To analyze visual and motor‐related oscillatory dynamics associated with the finger tap, time‐frequency data were averaged for an electrode cluster depicting left motor activity contralateral to the right index finger (electrodes C3 and CP3) and an electrode cluster depicting visuo‐occipital activity (electrodes P9, P10, PO7, PO8, PO3, PO4, Poz, O1, O2, Oz, I1, I2, Iz). For testing temporal modulations of motor alpha and beta‐band as well as visuo‐occipital alpha‐band activity, time‐frequency transforms from the respective electrode clusters were averaged across the respective frequency range: alpha‐band from 8 to 13.167 Hz and beta‐band from 15.167 to 30 Hz. As the FWHM of the employed Gabor filters was 1 Hz, a frequency range of ±1 Hz around the SSVEP frequency was excluded from the analysis of alpha and beta‐band activity to reduce potential leakage from the SSVEP signal into the analyzed data. As described above, the time courses of these clusters were baseline corrected relative to the period 3250–3000 ms before the finger tap, respectively and the data, thus, depicted changes in amplitudes in percent relative to the time window before the finger tap (see above). For each time by frequency point changes different from zero were tested with a t test against zero and cluster‐corrected, as described above.

In an exploratory post hoc analysis, we examined the potential relationship between and comodulation of visual alpha‐band activity and SSVEPs by analyzing SSVEP modulations in a time window for which the abovementioned analysis revealed a significant modulation of visual alpha‐band activity. For each participant SSVEP and visual alpha‐band modulation values were averaged across the respective time window. Whether or not these averaged modulations differed from zero was tested with a t test against zero complemented by a Bayesian t test in order to evaluate the evidence in favor of the alternative (i.e., modulations differ from zero) or the null hypothesis (i.e., modulations do not differ from zero). Bayesian t‐test equivalent were implemented by using the BayesFactor package (Morey et al., 2015) and the function ttestBF running in R (R Core Team, 2016) and implementing a standard JZS prior of 1 (Rouder et al., 2009). Evidence in favor of the null hypothesis is indicated by a Bayes Factor BF01 > 3 and evidence in favor of the alternative hypothesis by a Bayes Factor BF10 > 3 (Jeffreys, 1998; Raftery, 1995). In a last step we calculated the between subject Spearman rank correlation between alpha‐band and SSVEP modulations of this specific time window implemented in R with function SpearmanRho of package DescTools (Signorell, 2024). This correlational analysis was again complemented by a Bayesian equivalent based on van Doorn et al. (2020) evaluation evidence in favor of the null hypothesis (i.e., BF01 > 3; there is no correlation different from zero) or the alternative hypothesis (i.e., BF10 > 3; there is a correlation different from zero).

RESULTS

Finger tapping

Overall, participants were able to tap with the instructed interval of 8 s. On average we counted 253.263 taps per experiment (SD = 22.330). The median of the inter‐tap interval of 8.234 s and the 95%‐interval stretched from 5.357 to 13.235 s (see Figure 1c). As revealed by permutation tests, in all time‐bins of 36‐s length distributed across the experiment, the number of finger taps fell within the 95% CI as expected from a random distribution of finger taps across the time course of the experiment (see Figure 1b). This suggests that finger taps were uniformly distributed across the time course of the experiment for all participants.

Temporal dynamic of early visual sensory gain modulation indexed by SSVEPs

The spatial filter weights derived via RESS (Cohen & Gulbinaite, 2017) revealed an occipital topographical representation in line with previous findings pinpointing sources of SSVEPs to the early visual cortex (see Figure 2b). The time‐resolved analysis of SSVEP amplitude modulations revealed no significant differences compared to zero for the entire epoch (see Figure 2a,c).

Dynamics of ongoing neural oscillatory activity

The time‐resolved analysis of alpha and beta‐band activity, derived from an electrode cluster above somatomotor regions (Figure 3b), revealed clear modulations in the vicinity of the finger tap (Figure 3a). Activity in the alpha‐ (8–13.167 Hz) and beta‐band (15.167–30 Hz) showed distinct pre‐ and post‐tap modulations centered over the left somatomotor regions (Figure 3c). We found motor alpha‐band desynchronization from −1412 before to 494 ms after the finger tap (M = −6.456%; SD = 10.026; p cluster = .009; D cluster = 0.644). This was followed by an increased amplitude from 1314 to 2705 ms after the finger tap (M = 8.785%; SD = 9.560; p cluster = .012; D cluster = 0.919) before it returned to baseline (see Figure 3c top). Somatomotor beta‐band activity decreased from −1334 before to 229 ms after the finger tap (M = −5399%; SD = 6.152; p cluster = .015; D cluster = 0.878) and was then increased from 385 ms to the end of the analysis time window (3000 ms) (M = 13.585%; SD = 9.294; p cluster < .001; D cluster = 1.462), depicting a sharp amplitude rise followed by an amplitude drop off towards the pre‐tap baseline values (see Figure 3c bottom). Overall, somatomotor alpha and beta‐band activity depict the well‐known ERD‐ERS pattern associated with voluntary movements (Pfurtscheller & Lopes da Silva, 1999).

Exploratory analysis on the relationship between SSVEPs and alpha‐band activity

For the time window between −1014 and 229 ms relative to the finger tap, for which visual alpha‐band activity showed a positive modulation, the potential modulation of SSVEPs was analyzed exploratively. As visible in Figure 5, no systematic and significant SSVEP modulation was measurable in that time window (M = −1.122%; SD = 6.400; t(18) = 0.764, p = .455, D = 0.175). The results of a complementary Bayesian t test (JZS prior of 1) are in favor of the null hypothesis (BF01 = 4.339), that is, no modulation different from zero. Visual alpha‐band modulations are increased (M = 3.386%; SD = 3.466; t(18) = 4.258, p < .001, D = 0.977; BF10 = 71.616). Spearman's rank correlation for SSVEP and visual alpha‐band modulations across participants and the respective Bayesian equivalent found no evidence in favor of any correlation: ρ(18) = 0.365, p = .12, 95% CIρ = [−0.107 0.703], BF10 = 0.718.

DISCUSSION

We tested whether an amplitude increase in visual alpha‐band activity associated with motor alpha‐ERD before and during voluntary finger taps affects early visual stimulus processing of passively viewed irrelevant flickering stimuli on a screen. Self‐paced finger taps led to the well‐known motor alpha and motor beta‐band activity modulations with an alpha and beta‐band ERD before and during each finger tap followed by a rebound ERS for both frequency bands. Motor‐alpha‐band dynamics were mirrored by an antagonistic modulation of visual alpha‐band activity which is seen in an amplitude increase before and during each finger tap. This visual alpha‐band modulation was, however, not accompanied by a change in early sensory processing as there was no modulation of SSVEP amplitudes that were elicited by the flickering RDK. Post‐hoc analysis in the specific time window for which visual alpha‐band amplitudes were increased, found evidence in favor of the absence of any SSVEP modulation. Furthermore, there was no evidence for any correlation between SSVEP and visual alpha amplitude modulations.

Motor alpha‐band activity depicted well‐known amplitude dynamics repeatedly reported for the motor system (Pfurtscheller, 1992; Pfurtscheller & Aranibar, 1977; Pfurtscheller & Lopes da Silva, 1999; Pfurtscheller & Neuper, 1997, 2003; Toro et al., 1994). The event‐related desynchronization (ERD, i.e., amplitude decrease) before and during the actual movement is thought to represent excitability changes in neural populations relevant for preparing and executing the specific motor program (Brinkman et al., 2014, 2016; Deiber et al., 2012; Stolk et al., 2019). As in our data, this ERD is often followed by an event‐related synchronization (ERS, amplitude decrease) seen in a transient rebound of activity to levels even higher than before the actual movement, potentially functionally signifying an idling and less excited state of the motor system (Pfurtscheller et al., 1996). Motor beta‐band activity displayed comparable modulations (Kilavik et al., 2013; Pfurtscheller et al., 2005; Pfurtscheller & Neuper, 2003; Spitzer & Haegens, 2017). In fact, motor alpha‐ and beta‐band activity are often grouped as one mu‐rhythm with comparable spatio‐temporal dynamics during movements (Cuevas et al., 2014; Hari, 2006; Miller et al., 2010; Tiihonen et al., 1989). Yet, recent work suggests distinct contributions to motor and cognitive processes (Brinkman et al., 2014, 2016; Stolk et al., 2019). For instance, only beta‐band activity was found to effectively modulate corticospinal neurons (Baker et al., 1997; Hussain et al., 2018, 2019; Mima & Hallett, 1999). In general, motor beta‐band activity has been associated with signaling the status quo of sensorimotor activity and beta‐band dynamics should, thus, represent a change thereof during the execution of motor actions (Engel & Fries, 2010; Jenkinson & Brown, 2011; Joundi et al., 2012; Leunissen et al., 2022; Spitzer & Haegens, 2017). Overall, we replicated these motor alpha‐ and beta‐band modulations that formed the prerequisite to examine antagonistic visual alpha‐band modulations.

For visual alpha‐band activity, an opposite amplitude modulation was observed: amplitudes over occipital regions showed an increase (i.e., an ERS) before and during voluntary finger taps. This opposite pattern suggests that visual and motor alpha‐band activity indeed stem from different neural sources and represent different neural processes. A similar occipital ERS pattern was also previously reported to occur after simple movements such as finger taps or somatosensory processing (Haegens et al., 2012; Pfurtscheller, 1992, 2003; Pfurtscheller & Silva, 1999). In general, modulations of alpha‐band activity have been linked to changes in neural excitability with lower amplitudes representing increased neural excitability and higher amplitudes representing lower neural excitability and potentially leading to the suppression/inhibition of incoming stimuli during that period (Iemi et al., 2016; Jensen & Mazaheri, 2010; Klimesch et al., 2007; Morrow et al., 2023; Samaha et al., 2020). Based on these functional implications, alpha‐band activity is also discussed to play a role in attention as with attentional allocation alpha‐band amplitudes are often higher in regions representing the unattended stimulus as compared to regions representing the attentionally selected stimulus (Foster & Awh, 2018; Foxe & Snyder, 2011; Morrow et al., 2023; Van Diepen et al., 2019). The spatial attentional focus for instance can be depicted by the topographical pattern of alpha‐band activity with amplitudes typically higher for neural populations representing unattended versus attended spatial locations (Di Russo et al., 2007; Moratti et al., 2023; Müller, Picton, et al., 1998; Müller, Teder‐Sälejärvi, et al., 1998). Such modulation is also observable in the context of intermodal attention: paying attention to information from one sensory modality such as vision may increase alpha‐band activity in other sensory areas (such as auditory regions) relative to visual alpha‐band activity (Di Russo et al., 2007; Moratti et al., 2023; Müller, Picton, et al., 1998). This alpha amplitude increase is thought to be related to the attenuation of irrelevant or even distracting information. In this vein, increased alpha‐band amplitudes of our study may be seen as a marker of attentional reallocation or selective processing. To perform regular self‐paced finger taps, it seems quite logical that passively viewed visual stimuli are irrelevant or perhaps potentially disturbing to keep up with the tapping rhythm. As a consequence, attention may be withdrawn from this modality depicted by the amplitude increase in alpha‐band activity. At the same time, motor‐related processing is prioritized leading to the ERS pattern in motor regions.

Albeit this modulation of visual alpha‐band activity, there was neither evidence for a modulation of SSVEP amplitudes and thus early sensory processing in the temporal vicinity of voluntary finger taps nor evidence for a correlation between visual alpha‐band modulations and synchronous SSVEP amplitude modulations across participants. Previous work linked SSVEPs to early neural stimulus processing with neural generators encompassing regions from V1 to V5 (Boylan et al., 2023; Di Russo et al., 2007; Moratti et al., 2023; Müller, Picton, et al., 1998). It has repeatedly been shown that early stimulus processing is altered with attention, leading to a sensory gain modulation seen in increased SSVEP amplitudes for the attended stimulus (Di Russo et al., 2001; Gundlach, Moratti, et al., 2020; Kim et al., 2007; Müller & Hillyard, 2000; Müller, Picton, et al., 1998; Müller, Teder‐Sälejärvi, et al., 1998) or the attended feature (Andersen & Hillyard, 2024; Forschack et al., 2017; Müller et al., 2006). As described above, occipital alpha‐band activity, with thalamic and cortical generators (Bollimunta et al., 2011; Buffalo et al., 2011; Halgren et al., 2019; Kerkoerle et al., 2014; Saalmann et al., 2012; Silva et al., 1991; Vijayan & Kopell, 2012), seem to represent attentional modulations as well. Yet, previous work found that these two neural measures, alpha‐band activity and SSVEPs that are even measurable in comparable regions, seem independent as trial‐by‐trial variations in sensory gain were unrelated to trial‐by‐trial variations of alpha‐band activity (Antonov et al., 2020; Gundlach, Moratti, et al., 2020; Zhigalov & Jensen, 2020). It is thus questionable whether alpha‐band activity represents a direct top‐down controlled mechanism to alter stimulus processing (see also Jensen, 2024). We found again that SSVEPs and visual alpha‐band amplitudes do not depict matching dynamics. Therefore, it seems unlikely that alpha‐band activity represents a mechanism by which early sensory gain modulation is implemented. It is rather likely that they depict different levels of neural processing at which (attentional) selection is instantiated. Alpha‐band modulations have been specifically tied to the routing of information between cortical regions as seen in comodulated changes of connectivity measures (Alamia et al., 2023; Alamia & VanRullen, 2019; Gundlach, Müller, et al., 2020; Leske et al., 2015; Mayhew et al., 2013; Mo et al., 2013; Scheeringa et al., 2012; Weisz et al., 2014) and local amplitude modulations may be relevant for the readout of sensory information (Chaumon & Busch, 2014). As such alpha‐band modulations may depict or be associated with the selection of or modulation of information processing at later stages of neural processing (see also Zhigalov & Jensen, 2020).

In a previous study, Steady‐State‐Evoked Auditory Responses (SSAR) to auditory stimuli were, however, found to be modulated by finger taps (Makeig et al., 1996). In this study, the complex ERP response depicted by the complex time series of the SSAR in the frequency domain was modulated before and after the motor response. A drop in amplitude of the SSAR signal was found right after the finger movement, which was accompanied by phase shifts of the signal before and after the button presses (1 ms slowing of the evoked response before and 2 ms speeding after the finger tap). In the same vein, it was repeatedly found that voluntary movements lead to modulations of neural signals elicited by tones (Schafer & Marcus, 1973; Schröger, Kotz, et al., 2015; Timm et al., 2013, 2014). This idea has been discussed in light of the prediction of potential sensory outcomes by self‐initiated movements. Such a prediction forms an important aspect of the sense of agency by allowing the differentiation between consequences of one own's movements leading to sensory input and independent incidents leading to sensory input (Frith et al., 2000; Schröger, Marzecová, et al., 2015; Timm et al., 2014; Yang et al., 2016). Corollary discharges, that is, the routing of motor‐command‐related information to the sensory system form the basis thereof and have been proposed as a mechanism for motor‐sensory interactions (see Crapse & Sommer, 2008; Schröger, Marzecová, et al., 2015). They may also explain the modulation of SSARs by movements that were found by Makeig et al. (1996). While such patterns of corollary discharge have been associated with various sensory systems, including the visual system, for motor‐vision interactions most findings focus on situations in which movements and visual input are dependent. In such studies, eye movements (saccades) or head movements were studied, and they led to changes in the retinal input (Barczak et al., 2019; Crapse & Sommer, 2008; Ross et al., 2001; Sommer & Wurtz, 2008). Motor‐visual interactions may thus depend on scenarios, in which the movement has a direct impact on the current or future visual input. Crucially in our task, participants were asked to look at the fixation cross throughout the experimental blocks while tapping with the right finger and thus their gaze was kept constant and the visual input was not altered by the finger tap. In addition, visuo‐perceptual modulations were found for movements or planned movements for which the visual information represented a task‐ and motor‐action‐relevant stimulus that was for instance aiding or even necessary for completing a task such as grasping an object successfully (Gutteling et al., 2011, 2013; Wykowska et al., 2009). The passive task‐irrelevant presentation of visual stimuli in our task may thus have attenuated visuo‐motor interactions. It remains an open question whether SSVEP signals as a marker of early sensory processing may be modulated when they stem from stimuli that either contain action‐relevant information or that can potentially be altered by motor movement. Experimental designs in which stimuli and task and, thus, visual processing and behavior are more tightly coupled may be an interesting avenue to test a potential interaction.

Despite evidence for a modulation of visual alpha‐band modulations and an absence of SSVEP amplitude modulation, there is neither clear evidence in favor nor clear evidence against a relationship between both measures across participants. While in previous work, no such relationship was found either (see supplemental of Gundlach, Moratti, et al., 2020), here more confirmatory evidence may be needed for weaker effects to be detected (see Peylo et al., 2021). In addition, as no unitary alpha rhythm exists and distinct sources and generators are found in different cortical areas (Haegens et al., 2015) as well as layers (Bollimunta et al., 2008, 2011) with cortical and subcortical pace‐makers (Buffalo et al., 2011; Halgren et al., 2019; Saalmann et al., 2012; Vijayan & Kopell, 2012), some of which may not even be accessible to EEG measurements, their potential different interaction and relationships with neural processing is not yet fully understood.

Overall, our findings showed that single voluntary finger movements resulted in extensive modulations of motor alpha‐ and beta‐band activity before and after single taps. These motor‐preparation‐ and motor‐activity‐related changes are accompanied by an attenuation of visuo‐occipital alpha‐band activity before each finger tap. Such reduced visual alpha‐band activity may index an attenuated readout of potentially distracting visual information, aiding the pure motor task. The analysis of SSVEP time courses, however, revealed that the early sensory representation of task‐irrelevant visual information is unaffected by the task and associated dynamics in information processing. Again, visual alpha‐band activity and early sensory processing were found to be independent and may likely be associated with selective neural processing at different levels within processing hierarchies (Buschman & Kastner, 2015; Ferguson & Cardin, 2020; Luo & Maunsell, 2015; Maunsell, 2015).
