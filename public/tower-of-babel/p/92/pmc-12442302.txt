Evaluation of a modified one-stage technique for repair of third-degree perineal laceration and rectovestibular fistula with rectal pull-back technique in 12 mares: a retrospective case series
Authors: Ahmed Sharshar, Amal Hammad, Mohamed Salem, Mohamed El-Sunsafty
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AimThis study was carried out to evaluate a rectal pull-back one-stage technique, which is a modification of the one-stage Goetz technique, for treating third-degree perineal laceration or rectovestibular fistula.Materials and methodsThis study was performed on 12 mares, ten of whom suffered from third-grade perineal laceration, and two suffered from rectovestibular fistula and were included in this study. After incision of the vestibular shelf horizontally, the dissected rectal flap was pulled caudally and sutured to the anal sphincter during its reconstruction at the final stage. While the vestibular shelf/vaginal flap was reconstructed using size 2 polyglactin 910 suture material in six-bite suture pattern. Post-oparative follow up included monitoring the wound healing and recording the complications. ResultsNeither necrosis of the rectovestibular shelf nor formation of rectovestibular fistula has been recorded. Two mares showed partial dehiscence of the perineal body and one had partial dehiscence of anal sphincter which healed successfully.ConclusionRectal pull-back technique can be a good alternative for the treatment of third-grade perineal laceration and rectovestibular fistula in mares.

Introduction

Perineal lacerations and/or rectovestibular fistula are among the common reproductive disorders reported in different animal species, especially in mares [1–7]. It mostly occurs during their first parturition because of the prominence of the vestibulo-vaginal sphincter and remnants of the hymen. especially when the. It also may occur as a result of violent expulsive forces, particularly when it is accompanied by excessive manipulation during assisted delivery, or when the fetus is oversized, or presented with malposition [3, 8–10].

Perineal lacerations are classified according to their extent into first, second, and third-grade. Second and third-grade lacerations demand surgical correction otherwise, the athlete and the reproductive performance of the animal will be greatly influenced. In such cases, the genital organs become more vulnerable to inflammation resulting from fecal contamination and wind suction during exercise as a result of loss of the barrier between the vestibule and the rectum [2, 11–14].

Immediate and delayed repair of third-grade perineal laceration have been described by many authors. Although some of them have recommended immediate repair [15, 16], the outcome is questionable. Therefore, delaying the correction for at least 4–6 weeks may be the optimal solution to allow complete and efficient healing of the injured tissues with contraction and epithelization [1, 2, 13, 17].

Surgical repair of third-grade perineal laceration and rectovestibular fistula was initially accomplished using the one- or two-stage procedures that have been developed by Goetz and Aanes, respectively [8, 18]. In the upcoming years, various modifications have been reported with differences in the number of surgical stages, the suture material used, and the pattern of stitching. The main principles of all are to achieve sufficient tissue apposition with minimal or no tension via reconstruction of recto-vestibular shelf and apposition of the rectal mucosa with or without direct suturing [1, 3, 13, 17, 19–27]. Formation of fistula between the rectum and the vagina has been frequently reported as a main post-operative complication [1, 18, 25, 27]. Necrosis of the rectovestibular shelf during the early postoperative period was attributed to excess tension or from fecal matter impaction at rectal mucosal wound [25, 28, 29]. To our knowledge, none of them has performed a rectal pullback technique for the repair of third-grade perineal laceration and/or rectovestibular fistula. We hypothesized that such a technique would be easy to perform and would improve the quality of the repair and the outcome. This study aims to describe and evaluate a modified one-stage surgical repair technique that was used for treatment of 12 cases of delayed third-grade perineal laceration and rectovestibular fistula, and to rate the postoperative outcome and complications. The used technique is a modification from one-stage Goetz technique in which the rectovestibular shelf together with vestibular mucosa were sutured using six-bite pattern while the rectal mucosa was dissected to a suitable depth, pulled backwards, and sutured at the anal sphincter during its reconstruction.

Materials and methods

Animals

The present study included 12 mares (3–5 years old) with a history of third-degree perineal laceration or rectovestibular fistula occurred during parturition and were referred to surgery clinic at faculty of veterinary medicine, university of Sadat City, Egypt, between January 2018 to October 2023. The animals were privately owned by individuals. In all animals, the perineum was examined, and the degree perineal injury was assessed and classified as a third-degree laceration (where the perineal body was completely severed) or rectovestibular fistulation (where the perineal body was intact or partially injured with presence of a fistula connecting the rectum and the vagina). One mare had one unsuccessful surgery, while the remaining 11 mares were subjected to surgery for the first time. In all recent cases, surgical repair was delayed for at least 6 weeks until second intention healing of the rectovestibular wound had occurred. Informed consent was taken from the owners to use the animals in this study.

Surgical technique

All procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee, University of Sadat City–Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Sadat City, Egypt. All animals were fed alfalfa with bran mash for 1 week before surgery. Food was withheld from 24 h before surgery and up to 48 h after surgery, during which the animals were administered intravenous fluid therapy, including dextrose and Ringer’s lactate. Feeding of the operated animals started on the third day after surgery, at which time the animals were allowed to feed alfalfa hay and bran mash for 14 days. Mineral oil was administered to animals that exhibited signs of constipation or straining during defecation. All animals received a preoperative prophylactic dose of antitetanic serum, intravenous nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (flunixin meglumine, 1 mg/kg), and a broad-spectrum antibiotic, Penicillin (20,000 IU)- Dihydrostreptomycin sulfate (250 mg) (Pen & Strep, Norbrook Laboratories. Administration of the anti-inflammatory and the antibiotic was continued for 7 successive days post-operatively.

Surgery was performed in standing, in which the animals were controlled in a stanchion. All mares were initially sedated with xylazine (Xylaject 20 mg/mL, ADWIA Co, 10th of Ramadan city, Egypt) given as an intravenous bolus of 0.55 mg/kg followed by a constant rate infusion (CRI) of 1.08 mg/kg/hour [30]. During the procedure, when sedation was judged to be insufficient, a bolus of xylazine (0.14 mg/kg, IV) was given or the CRI was doubled. Caudal epidural anesthesia was achieved by injecting a combination of xylazine (0.17 mg/kg) and lidocaine (0.25 mg/kg: Debocaine 20 mg/mL, Al-Debeiky for pharma industries, Obour City, Egypt) into the first intercoccygeal space.

The rectum was evacuated manually, and a wet cotton tampon soaked with diluted povidone iodine was placed cranially within the rectum to avoid fecal contamination of the surgical site, and the tail was wrapped and suspended dorsally. The surgical field including perineum, vestibule and rectum was cleaned with saline then with diluted povidone iodine solution. In cases of rectovestibular fistula, the fistula was converted to third-degree perineal laceration directly before the surgery. The vulvar lips were retracted and fixed to increase exposure of the surgical filed using a single stay stitch at each side that was held by an assistant or anchored into the lateral skin.

A horizontal incision was made at the level of the rectovestibular shelf along the scar tissue at the junction between the vestibule/vagina and rectum. The incision started at the skin caudally to the intact rectovestibular shelf cranially to form two separate rectal and vaginal flaps. At both lateral aspects, the incision was deepened to 3–4 cm, while at the cranial aspect of the rectovestibular shelf, the incision was deepened with both sharp and blunt dissection to a proper depth to allow a smooth backward pull of the rectal flap. The dissection was judged to be sufficient when the rectal flap was easily pulled without undue tension to reach the anal sphincter (Fig. 1).

The dissected rectal flap was gently grasped backward and upheld by an assistant using Alli’s tissue forceps. The vestibular shelf and the vaginal flap were reconstructed from cranial to caudal in a 6-bite pattern using size 2 polyglactin 910 suture material. Placing of the 6-bite sutures was carried out on the same basis as described by [1, 13] except for the rectal flap. For proper obliteration of the dead space, the submucosal layer of the rectal flap was anchored during suture placement at the vestibular shelf/vaginal flap without penetration of the rectal lumen (Fig. 2). By reaching the level of the perineal body, the caudal end of the rectal flap was sutured in an inverting manner at the anal sphincter during its reconstruction (Fig. 3).

Reconstruction of the perineal body was completed using Caslick’s procedure in which the subcutaneous layer was sutured in a simple continuous pattern using size 1 polyglactin 910 absorbable suture material. Perineal skin was sutured in a simple interrupted manner using size 1 polypropylene non-absorbable suture material (Fig. 4). All animals received a preoperative prophylactic dose of antitetanic serum, intravenous nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (flunixin meglumine, 1 mg/kg), and a broad-spectrum antibiotic, Penicillin (20,000 IU)- Dihydrostreptomycin sulfate (250 mg) (Pen & Strep, Norbrook laboratories. Administration of the anti-inflammatory and the antibiotic was continued for 7 successive days post-operatively.

The operated animals were observed postoperatively for the first 5 days for evidence of defecation or straining. Manual evacuation of the rectum was performed in case of constipation. A warm rectal enema was performed in case of straining. Daily dressing of the perineal wound was performed by cleansing the wound with saline, followed by application of iodine ointment. Wound healing, recovery, and complications were recorded.

Results

In all 12 mares, perineal laceration occurred during parturition. The animals included 7 Arabian and 5 crossbreeds. Out of the refereed mares, ten had third-grade perineal lacerations. In two of them, the laceration was recent, and the lacerated tissues were inflamed, edematous, and contaminated (Fig. 5). In both cases, perineal wounds were cleaned and frequently dressed. The surgery was delayed until second intention healing took place 6–8 weeks later (Fig. 6). The remaining two mares had rectovestibular fistula. In the first one, the fistula resulted primarily from dystocia, in which the dorsal vulvar commissure was the only intact part while the vestibular shelf and the remaining perineum were completely lacerated. In the second one, the fistula resulted from partial wound dehiscence after primary surgical correction of a third-degree perineal laceration (Fig. 7).

No post-operative fistula formation has been reported in any of the operated mares. In 9 mares, the first intention healing of the perineal skin wound was complete (Fig. 8A). One of them showed partial prolapse of the rectal mucus membrane at the second postoperative day as a result of tissue edema (Fig. 8B). The prolapsed mucus membrane was treated via worm fomentation with diluted povidone iodine solution. Post-operative complications were reported in 3 mares. It included partial dehiscence of the perineal skin wound in 2 cases at the 5th day post-operation. The wound was freshened and sutured. Healing by first intention was obtained in both animals. Partial dehiscence of anal sphincter with pneumorectum was reported in one mare at the 4th day post-operation (Fig. 9A). Complete healing of the wound by second intention occurred 15 days later with partial deformation of the anal sphincter (Fig. 9B). No post-operative fistula formation has been reported in any of the operated mares. The post-operative follow-up of the breeding history of 6 mares was missed due to a lack of owner compliance. The remaining 6 mares were bred 3–4 months post-surgery, two of them didn’t conceive and became repeat breeders. The remaining 4 mares delivered normally without complications.

Discussion

Different surgical techniques have been described for repair of third- grade perineal laceration and rectovestibular fistula in different animal species [5, 6, 13, 17, 20, 23, 26]. The present study described a different approach for the management of such an obstacle in mares. Instead of apposition of the rectal mucosa with direct or indirect suturing, it was dissected from the underlying vaginal mucosa, pulled backward, and sutured at the anal sphincter during its reconstruction. Fixation of the rectal mucosa in such a way reduced the overall complication rate and improved the outcome.

Immediate repair of third-grade perineal laceration has been reported by Hope (1992) [31], it is not recommended by many other authors [8, 16, 19, 32, 33]. The presence of inflammation and edema in addition to contamination and devitalized tissue at the wounded area, increases the chance of wound dehiscence during the postoperative period and probably complete failure of the operation. In the present study, all mares were treated at least 6 weeks after initial injury. This period was sufficient to subside the inflammation, healing of the lacerated wound by second intention, and formation of healthy tissues that can withstand suturing [25].

Reconstruction of third-grade perineal laceration was performed under the effect of general anesthesia while restraining the animal in controlled dorsal recumbency [16, 34, 35] or under the effect of caudal epidural analgesia in standing position [7, 25, 26]. In this study, in addition to performing caudal epidural analgesia using xylazine/lidocaine combination, the mares were kept sedated under the effect of a constant rate infusion using xylazine. This protocol proved to be effective for such kind of surgeries in standing mares. It provided satisfactory analgesia and sedation during the whole period of the operation with no need for additional injection of local anesthetic. In addition to providing adequate analgesia, controlling the animal in a standing position prevented tissue distortion, kept the damaged tissue in approximate anatomical conformation, and allowed for easy and smooth tissue apposition.

In previous reports, formation of rectovestibular fistula was the mostly reported complication during the postoperative period after reconstruction of third-grade perineal laceration [18, 25–27]. It usually occurs due to wound dehiscence probably resulting from excess tension over the sutured tissues or from impaction of the fecal matter in between the lips of the rectal mucosal wound [25, 28, 29]. The key point in the current technique to overcome such complications was to perform deep dissection, especially at the cranial aspect of the rectovestibular shelf to form a large and thick, easily movable rectal flap. The formed rectal flap was smoothly pulled backward without tension and sutured at the anal sphincter instead of placing sutures inside the rectal wall. We believe that the main advantage of this technique over the previously reported ones by [2, 17, 25, 26] is that pulling and suturing of the rectal flap in such a way provides an intact mucosal surface covering the area of laceration. Besides, it excluded the possibility of fecal matter leakage and impaction within the rectal wound hence reducing the chance of wound dehiscence and fistulation.

Surgical repair of recto-vestibular fistula is challenging because of the limited access to the whole length of the lacerated wound, which may result to improper suturing, giving a chance for failure. In previous reports by Colbern et al. (1985), Belknap & Nickels, (1992) and O’Reilly et al. (1998) [1, 19, 27], the authors recommended converting the rectovestibular fistula to a third-grade laceration before reconstruction for better visualization of the operation area. In cases of recto-vestibular fistula (two mares), the perineal body was incised at the time of operation. This gave complete access to the wound and facilitated its closure. The rectovaginal shelf and the perineal body were reconstructed using polyglactin 910, a multifilament absorbable suture material, it provided good wound apposition, and no complications of its use have been reported. Similar results have been previously reported [3, 27, 36] with neither contamination of the deep tissue nor formation of post-operative fistula.

Several complications have been reported after reconstruction of third-grade perineal laceration. ranging from 0% [3, 26] to 25% [13, 18, 21, 25, 27]. It included bleeding from the rectum or the vagina, anal sphincter incompetence, tenesmus, and constipation, partial or complete dehiscence of perineal skin or rectovaginal shelf with formation of rectovestibular fistula. Although the post-operative complication rate, in 3 out of 12 (25%) mares recorded in this study was compatible with previous studies [21, 25, 27], neither necrosis of rectovestibular shelf nor formation of rectovestibular fistula have been recorded. Primary healing rate of 75% (9/12) and a final healing rate 92% (11/12). The reported complications were partial dehiscence of perineal skin and anal sphincter which were treated successfully. Although the breeding history of 50% (6/12) of the treated cases could not get known due to lack of owner compliance, 4/6 (67%) of the remaining mares delivered normally without any complications. These results were in accordance with previous reports by [3, 25]. The limitations of this study were the small sample size, lack of a comparison group, Absence of long-term follow-up evaluation of the breeding history of 6 (50%) cases due to lack of owner compliance.

Conclusion

Based on results obtained from the study, it can be concluded that rectal pull-back technique can be a good alternative for treatment of third-grade perineal laceration and rectovestibular fistula in mares instead of suturing of the rectal mucosa, regardless the used suturing pattern for closure of the vestibule-vaginal shelf. It provides excellent results without post-operative recurrence or fistula formation. However, it needs furthermore investigation on large number of cases before it is fully recommended as an optional treatment procedure for the management of third-grade perineal laceration in mares.
