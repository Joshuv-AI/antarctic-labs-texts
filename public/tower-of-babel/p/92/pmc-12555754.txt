Clinical implications of taurine in breast cancer
Authors: Shih-Hong Chen, Linyi Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTaurine, an amino acid with documented anti-cancer properties, has shown potential in breast cancer treatment. This review focuses on taurine's role in inhibiting tumor growth and metastasis by influencing cellular metabolism and enhancing immune responses. Taurine affects energy production pathways and intracellular amino acid levels, leading to reduced tumor growth. It also detoxifies reactive oxygen species in mitochondria and enhances the activity of antioxidant enzymes. Additionally, taurine modulates the immune system by increasing antitumor cytokines like IFN-γ and decreasing tumor-promoting cytokines such as IL-10. Furthermore, taurine enhances the effectiveness of CD8+ cytotoxic T cells and other effector T-cell subsets, while altering the immunological profile of CD4+CD25+ regulatory T cells (T regs) that suppress immune defenses. This combined immune modulation and metabolic influence of taurine promote tumor cell apoptosis by upregulating pro-apoptotic proteins (PUMA, Bax) and downregulating anti-apoptotic proteins (Bcl-2), potentially preventing metastasis and improving outcomes for breast cancer patients. Factors such as extracellular osmolarity, electrolyte imbalances, and nutrient levels (such as folate) can influence taurine uptake. Our review shows taurine's potential as an adjuvant therapy in breast cancer treatment and advocates for more clinical trials to validate its therapeutic benefits. Further research is necessary to determine its clinical efficacy, optimal dosage, and long-term safety.Keywords: Taurine, Cancer metastasis, Breast cancer, Immune modulation, Taurine homeostasis

Introduction

Breast cancer remains a major global health concern, with approximately 1.9 million new cases reported in 2023, incidence rates varying between 27 and 97 per 100,000 individuals, and the incidence of breast cancer is influenced by lifestyle factors, socioeconomic status, and else [[1], [2], [3]]. Generally, considerable public health efforts have been made to combat breast cancer in terms of the early detection of breast cancer and the development of improved treatment protocols. Fortunately, from 2019 to 2020, breast cancer–related mortality decreased by 1.5 %, mainly due to advances in treatment approaches. Early screening, diagnosis at the early stage using high resolution equipment, and treatment with advanced technology according to current recommendations have substantially improved survival rates. In normal breast tissue, luminal cells lining the lactiferous ducts and stromal cells respond to hormonal stimuli, such as estrogen and progesterone. Hormone receptors, including ER [4], PR [5], and HER2 [6], are expressed on the surface of luminal cells. In breast cancer cells, ER and PR are predominantly expressed in approximately 75 % of patients. The disease exhibits substantial morphological and molecular heterogeneity, particularly with respect to the expression of receptors on the cell surface, such as estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) [7]. Numerous mechanisms contribute to the progression and metastasis of breast cancer, with the tumor microenvironment (TME) playing a critical role. In addition, studies have demonstrated that the TME regulates the growth, differentiation, and apoptosis and alterations in the extracellular matrix, fibroblasts, myoblasts, immune cells, and other components promote the survival of malignant epithelial cells [8,9].

Taurine, also known as 2-aminoethanesulfonic acid, is a naturally occurring amino acid ubiquitously found in all mammalian tissues. This nonproteinogenic amino acid plays a critical role as a major component of bile, and taurine also plays a substantial role in the digestion and absorption of lipids [10,11]. Taurine is predominantly distributed in the large intestine of most animals, and this amino acid accounts for approximately 0.1 % of human body weight [12]. In its zwitterion form, the sulfonic acid group of taurine has a low pKa; thus, it is ionized to sulfonate at intestinal pH in humans [13]. Although the body synthesizes taurine from methionine and cysteine, classifying it as a nonessential amino acid, research highlights its essential roles in the nervous system [14], retinal neural cells [15], and cardiac and muscular cells [16]. In addition to its fundamental role in the normal function of cells, taurine has several benefits, including anti-inflammatory [17], antioxidation [18], and an ability to ameliorate the effects of glucose dysregulation [19]. Furthermore, taurine has potential therapeutic applications, and taurine supplementation has been demonstrated to ameliorate various diseases and organ dysfunction [20]. The anti-breast-cancer effects of taurine have been demonstrated in both in vitro and in vivo models. Despite these documented benefits, the mechanisms through which taurine exerts its anticancer effects remain unclear. Elucidating these mechanisms is crucial for verifying the therapeutic potential of taurine for cancer.

Chronic inflammation is a known facilitator of cancer progression, whereas antioxidants play a crucial role in protecting cells from oxidative damage that contributes to tumorigenesis. For instance, immune responses can either enhance or inhibit tumor growth, as observed in the case of taurine-infused beverages [21]. Taurine improves immune responses by enhancing the efficacy of anti-programmed death-1 (PD-1) antibodies, primarily by promoting the cytotoxic function of CD8+ T cells [22]. Immune responses can either enhance or inhibit tumor growth, as observed in a study examining taurine-infused beverages [21].

Additionally, taurine upregulates the expression of tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs), such as TIMP-1 and TIMP-2—endogenous glycoproteins that serve as natural inhibitors of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs)—while downregulating the expression of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and MMPs, thereby effectively suppressing breast cancer cell migration. [23,24]. This amino acid can also induce the apoptosis of cancer cells [25]. Taurine also promotes the apoptosis of breast cancer cells by upregulating the expression levels of the p53 upregulated modulator of apoptosis (PUMA) protein, subsequently up- and downregulating the expression levels of BCL2 Associated X (Bax) and B-cell lymphoma 2 (Bcl-2) proteins and leading to the activation of caspase-3 [26]. Therefore, cancer cells may suppress taurine levels by modulating gene expression. Our recent finding demonstrated that triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) reduces the expression of GAD1—a key enzyme in an alternative taurine biosynthesis pathway—resulting in enhanced tumor growth and metastatic potential, particularly under prolonged morphine treatment [27]. Furthermore, taurine reverses the downregulated expression levels of p53 and caspase in 7,12-dimethylbenz[a]anthracene (DMBA)-induced breast cancer animal model [28]. Taurine's potential therapeutic applications remain underexplored. Taurine's multifaceted mechanisms make it a candidate for further research in oncology.

Malignant cells, such as breast cancer cells, are known to enhance glycolysis and glutamine metabolism, a phenomenon referred to as the Warburg effect [29]. This metabolic reprogramming leads to excessive lactate accumulation and acidification of the TME, which in turn promotes cancer invasion and metastasis [30]. Taurine has been shown to mitigate lactate production and acidification by stabilizing intracellular calcium homeostasis and restoring glucose levels [31]. Through its modulation of intracellular electrolyte balance, taurine contributes to the maintenance of pH and glucose levels in cancer cells, thereby potentially inhibiting cancer cell proliferation and metastasis.

As a well-documented anticancer amino acid, taurine has potential application as an adjuvant therapy based on its ability to support cellular health and mitigate the side effects of conventional cancer treatments. However, the precise anti-breast cancer mechanisms of taurine remain unclear, and taurine's treatment efficacy has not yet been established. Continual research into the mechanisms of action of taurine is crucial for establishing its therapeutic efficacy and safety. Clinical trials and preclinical studies must continue to explore the biochemical interactions between taurine and cancer cells, its optimal dosage for therapeutic effects, and the potential side effects associated with long-term supplementation with taurine. Integrating taurine into cancer treatment protocols and utilizing it as a nutrient supplement may improve outcomes and quality of life in breast cancer patients. This review summarizes the current state of research into the potential anti-breast-cancer effects of taurine and its potential use as an adjuvant in breast cancer treatment.

Mechanism through which taurine inhibits tumor growth and metastasis

Taurine inhibits breast cancer growth through metabolic modulation

Angiogenesis, nutritional support, and the inhibition of tumor suppressers, such as tumor suppressor genes, are required for the growth of breast cancer cells. Metabolism in breast cancer cells considerably differs from that in normal cells [32], and these metabolic changes are a major factor contributing to tumor growth [33]. In the TME, the decrease in glucose levels and the increase in lactate levels lead to the activation of glutamine metabolism and glycolysis. This process, which is known as the Warburg effect, enhances ATP production and provides essential nutrients for the proliferation of cancer cells [29]. The accumulation of lactate causes the acidification of the TME, which promotes cancer invasion and metastasis [30,34]. This acidification induces intracellular sodium inflow, followed by calcium exchange through the sodium-calcium exchanger (NCX), resulting in intracellular calcium overload [35]. Taurine activates the taurine transporter and inhibits calcium exchange through the Na+-Ca2+ channel, leading to the alleviation of intracellular Ca2+ overload. By maintaining Ca2+ homeostasis, taurine mitigates glucose dysregulation and restores the pH of the TME (Fig. 1). Overall, taurine has the potential to normalize excessive lactate and glucose levels, thereby preventing intracellular calcium overload [31]. Nonetheless, the underlying molecular mechanism remains undetermined.

Mechanism of intracellular taurine modulation

Taurine plays a crucial role in modulating intracellular apoptosis in breast cancer cells while promoting stability in normal cells. It supports normal cellular function by enhancing mitochondrial antioxidative protein expression, facilitating the synthesis of N-bromotaurine in response to hypobromous acid, and suppressing inflammatory responses via inhibition of the JAK1/STAT3 pathway. Additionally, taurine inhibits tumor growth and metastasis by inducing intrinsic apoptosis, promoting tumor cellular toxicity, and preventing intracellular calcium overload.

Breast cancer alters the patient's cellular amino acid profile due to changes in protein metabolism [36]. Taurine has been demonstrated to inhibit cancer cell proliferation by altering the levels of amino acids [37]. For example, Chen et al. found that supplementation with 2 % taurine decreased the intra-tumor levels of glucose, valine, leucine/isoleucine, trimethylamine oxide, methionine, glutamate, creatine, taurine, and arginine [31]. These metabolic alterations induced by taurine treatment can markedly affect the proliferation of breast cancer cells. For example, after taurine treatment, decreases were noted in the serum and tumor levels of creatinine, which is synthesized from arginine, glycine, and methionine. Other studies have reported that creatinine levels are related to cancer cell proliferation and cancer metastasis [38]. The decrease in plasma creatinine levels may be related to the effect of taurine on creatinine related enzymes, such as glycine aminotransferase. A study [39] reported different expression levels for metabolites, including proline, α-ketoisocaproic acid (involved in leucine metabolism), and 1,2-keto-isovaleric acid (involved in valine metabolism), in a mouse model of breast cancer, with the expression levels of the metabolites being either upregulated or downregulated. Following taurine treatment, the plasma levels of fumarate, malate, citrate, α-ketoglutarate, and pyruvate decreased substantially. Enrichment analysis revealed metabolic changes in protein biosynthesis, the urea cycle, the Krebs cycle, ammonia recycling, and alanine metabolism. These metabolic pathways are involved in energy production, and taurine may inhibit the growth of breast cancer cells by limiting their energy supply. Taurine inhibits breast cancer through the suppression of oxidative stress

For decades, research has indicated the antioxidant activity of taurine. Plasma taurine can serve as a strong bulwark against oxidative stress [40], and low plasma taurine levels are associated with oxidative stress–related diseases such as liver disorders, Alzheimer's disease, and growth retardation [41]. Although the precise mechanisms underlying the antioxidant effects of taurine are not fully understood, researchers have proposed several potential pathways through which taurine protects against oxidative stress.

In normal mitochondria, taurine conjugates with uridine at the wobble position of mitochondrial tRNALeu(UUR) to form 5-taurinomethyluridine [40]. This modified base enables the tRNA to efficiently recognize both UUG and UUA codons through accurate base pairing with AAU anticodons [42]. In contrast, tRNALeu(UUR) lacking 5-taurinomethyluridine can only decode the UUA codon, leading to impaired translation of UUG-dependent mitochondrial proteins. This deficiency compromises the synthesis of critical components of the mitochondrial respiratory chain [43,44]. Consequently, mitochondrial ATP production is also diminished as respiratory function deteriorates. This mechanism may offer protection against oxidative stress and apoptosis in mitochondria [45,46]. Taurine also detoxifies hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), hydroxyl radicals (.OH), and nitric oxide (NO), but not through traditional mechanisms involving reactive oxygen species scavenging [40]. In addition to detoxifying hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), hydroxyl radicals (•OH), and nitric oxide (NO), taurine also mitigates the production of superoxide anions by stabilizing the mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC), thereby reducing the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [42]. Moreover, taurine enhances the activity of antioxidant enzymes, such as superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase, facilitating ROS detoxification. These mechanisms collectively contribute to taurine's cytoprotective effects through both suppression of ROS formation and enhancement of antioxidant defenses. Taurine exerts antioxidant effects not only in mitochondria but also in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) [47] (Fig. 1). Taurine has been implicated in exerting antioxidant effects in both mitochondria and endoplasmic reticulum [47], likely through increasing glutathione levels [48,49].

Taurine may also serve as an antioxidant through indirect mechanisms. Taurine derivatives, such as N-bromotaurine (TauBr) or N-chlorotaurine (TauCl), are produced from the reaction between taurine and hypobromous acid (HOBr) or hypochlorous acid (HOCl) [50,51]. These derivatives protect phagocytic cells from self-oxidative injury [52,53] (Fig. 1). TauCl enhances the expression of antioxidant enzymes, notably heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) [54], inhibits lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced ERK activation in macrophages, and protects phagocytic cells from hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)-induced cell death [55]. Additionally, both TauCl and TauBr suppress the phagocytic activity of immune cells, a mechanism that reduces oxygen consumption and attenuates the respiratory burst [56]. Previous studies have demonstrated that chronic inflammation and oxidative stress contribute significantly to carcinogenesis [57]. Therefore, protecting phagocytic cells from oxidative damage and suppressing chronic inflammatory responses may potentially mitigate cancer progression. On the other hand, taurine has also demonstrated protective effects in various systemic diseases. For example, taurine exerts beneficial effects on the cardiovascular system by modulating the renin–angiotensin axis [17,27].

Several lines of evidence have hinted at the mechanisms underlying the anticancer effects of taurine in breast cancer. Studies have demonstrated that taurine supplementation leads to reductions in mitochondrial lipid peroxidation and enhances the activity of mitochondrial antioxidant enzymes, including superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), glutathione peroxidase (GPx), glutathione S-transferases (GST), and glutathione reductase (GR), in animals with DMBA-induced breast cancer [58,59]. Taurine-mediated increases in glutathione, vitamin C, and vitamin D levels also play a critical role in cellular protection. The mechanisms associated with taurine supplementation enhance mitochondrial electron transport chain activity, providing an overall protective effect against oxidative damage.

Oxidative stress leads to the damage of normal tissue, and taurine suppresses oxidative stress through multiple pathways. The antioxidant properties of taurine aid in repairing damage to the electron transport chain in mitochondria and cytoplasmic reactions. However, the direct mechanisms linking the antioxidant effects of taurine to its antitumor activity remain unclear. Further research is required to elucidate the detailed mechanisms through which taurine exerts its protective effects against breast cancer and beyond. Taurine modulates immune response

Immune responses play a crucial role in the proliferation of breast cancer cells and the metastasis and inhibition of breast cancer through diverse mechanisms. Immunity involves the maturation, proliferation, and migration of immune cells, along with their interactions, antibody production, and cytokine release. Although studies have aimed to elucidate the related antitumor mechanisms, the effects of taurine on immune responses against breast cancer cells remain unclear. The inhibitory effect of taurine on cancer is mediated in conjunction with the immune response; for example, immunodeficiency during taurine supplementation may result in adverse outcomes in breast cancer [60]. Cytokines, as major drivers of immune responses, can promote or inhibit breast cancer, and taurine can reduce the levels of primary inflammatory cytokines that are produced in response to infection, including NO, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and interleukin-6 (IL-6) [35]. Certain cytokines, and their respective concentration levels, have been identified as prognostic biomarkers in specific cancer types. For example, elevated IL-6 can be associated with shorter survival in breast cancer [61], whereas IL-10 may serve as an indicator of poor prognosis in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) under particular TME conditions [62,63]. Taurine may enhance the release of antitumor cytokines from immune cells, contributing to its cytotoxic effects. It is worthy to note that while taurine has been shown to increase antitumor cytokines, such as INF-γ and TNF-α, in certain cancer models [22,64], other studies indicate that taurine can reduce specific proinflammatory cytokines in different disease contexts [60]. These divergent outcomes likely result from variations in disease type, immune status, and dosage, suggesting that taurine's immunomodulatory effects are highly context-dependent. Further research is needed to delineate the precise conditions under which taurine enhances versus suppresses inflammatory or cytotoxic cytokine release. When combined with PD-1 treatment, these effects of taurine can considerably enhance tumor toxicity [22]. Through a diverse and complex range of mechanisms, taurine modulates immune responses, involving various types of immune cells. For example, taurine affects CD4+CD25+ regulatory T cells, which have been reported to reduce the efficacy of chemotherapy [65]. A recent study [66] demonstrated that taurine supplementation increased the proportions of CD3+CD4+ helper T cells and CD3+CD8+ cytotoxic T cells in tumor-bearing mice, while reducing the proportions of CD4+CD25+ regulatory T cells (Tregs) and CD11b+Ly6G+ myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs). Tregs and MDSCs are known to suppress antitumor immune responses. Consequently, lowering their levels can enhance cytotoxic T-cell activity and improve immune-mediated tumor cell elimination, thereby contributing to taurine's overall antitumor effects (Fig. 1). Additionally, flow cytometry findings revealed that taurine increases the numbers of inter-tumoral CD8+ T cells with minimal impact on other immune cells, including FOXP3+CD4+ T cells, macrophages, dendritic cells, neutrophils, monocytes, NK cells, B cells [60]. These findings jointly suggest that taurine modulates immune responses and cytokine release, thus indirectly enhancing tumor cytotoxicity. However, the mechanisms underlying these effects of taurine remain unclear and warrant further investigation.

Taurine inhibits the proliferation of cancer cells through its influence on intracellular signal transduction and gene expression. By influencing intracellular calcium levels, taurine inhibits several signaling pathways, such as Ca2+-PKC-NF-κB and Ca2+-CaM-CaN-NFAT, attenuating the release of inflammatory factors [35]. Cao et al. [60] demonstrated that taurine supplementation inhibits JAK1-STAT3 signaling and suppresses activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4) expression. Taurine deficiency in CD8+ T cells lead to ER stress, elevates ATF4 transcription, and subsequently activates immune checkpoint genes, contributing to T-cell exhaustion. Therefore, taurine supplementation, through suppression of ATF4 transcription, could help mitigate immune dysfunction associated with T-cell exhaustion. It is important to note that the detailed mechanism underlying ATF4 pathway inhibition in the context of T-cell proliferation has not yet been fully elucidated and requires further investigation. In gastric cancer cells, taurine deficiency mediated by SP1-SLC6A6 can reduce immune tumoral toxicity. Taurine was discovered to inhibit the metastasis of triple-negative breast cancer by upregulating the expression of phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN), which inhibits PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, modulates the immune system, and finally, increases the M1/M2 macrophage ratio [67] (Fig. 1).

The antitumor effects of taurine have been found to be based in the modulation of immune responses; thus, accumulating evidence supports the use of taurine as an adjuvant in immune therapy. Nevertheless, current evidence regarding the immunomodulatory effects of taurine within the immunosuppressed TME remains inconsistent. Therefore, additional clinical and preclinical studies are essential to clearly determine the optimal protocol, dosage, and treatment regimens for taurine supplementation. Taurine induces cancer cell apoptosis

Apoptosis, a mechanism of programmed cell death, is essential for maintaining cellular homeostasis and supporting the cycle of cellular regeneration [68]. Under the evasion of apoptosis signals from the immune system or hypoxia, breast cancer cells undergo proliferation and invasion [69]. Apoptosis is categorized into extrinsic and intrinsic pathways based on the stimuli, and caspase proteins are involved in both pathways [70].

The intrinsic pathway of apoptosis is initiated by intracellular stressors within the TME, such as heat, radiation, hypoxia, viral infection, DNA damage, and increased intracellular calcium levels [71]. In the intrinsic pathway, the initial step is the activation of the BCL-2 protein, leading to mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP), which is considered the “point of no return” in intrinsic apoptosis. Subsequently, cytochrome c is released from mitochondria into the cytosol through a mitochondrial permeability transition pore [72]. The release of cytochrome c leads to the formation of the apoptosome, which causes the activation of caspase proteins, which mediate apoptosis.

The extrinsic pathway involves the systemic regulation of the extracellular environment through ligand–receptor binding. Tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily (TNFRSF), a transmembrane death receptor with an intracellular “death domain” protein, induces the extrinsic pathway. TNFRSF members, including FS-7associated surface antigen receptor (FasR), tumor necrosis factor receptor-1 (TNFR1), tumor necrosis factor-related apoptosis-inducing ligand 1 (TRAILR1), tumor necrosis factor-related apoptosis-inducing ligand 2 (TRAILR2), tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily 25 (TNFRSF25), tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily 21 (TNFRSF21), ectodysplasin A Receptor (EDAR), and p75 neurotrophins receptor (p75NTR), form the death-inducing signaling complex (DISC) at the intracellular docking site. DISC activates caspase proteins (caspase-8 and -10), which mediate apoptosis [70]. Cross-talk between the extrinsic and intrinsic pathways occurs during apoptosis. When the extrinsic pathway is triggered by specific ligand–receptor binding, activated caspase-8 cleaves BH3-interacting domain death agonist (BID) into truncated-BID (tBID). tBID activates Bax and BAK proteins in the outer mitochondrial membrane, inducing the intrinsic apoptosis pathway [73]. BID serves as a signaling intermediary between the intrinsic and extrinsic pathways, promoting the apoptosis of cells with considerable damage, such as DNA injury.

Taurine plays a multifaceted role in regulating apoptosis (Fig. 1). While it inhibits apoptosis in response to normal cell damage, taurine deficiency in mouse cardiomyocytes reduces the glutathione/oxidized glutathione (GSH/GSSG) ratio, leading to membrane potential collapse. Consequently, cytoplasmic cytochrome c levels increase, subsequently leading to apoptosis [74]. Conversely, taurine inhibits the proliferation of breast cancer cells through the modulation of certain proteins. For example, taurine upregulates PUMA expression, which, in turn, upregulates Bax expression and downregulates Bcl-2 expression, collectively promoting the apoptosis of breast cancer cells [26]. Taurine mediates apoptosis by increasing p53, Bax, caspase-3, and caspase-9 expressions and directly decreasing Bcl-2 expression [28] (Fig. 1). Such modulation of proteins by taurine leads to the activation of the intrinsic apoptosis pathway. For protect normal tissues from oxidative stress, taurine scavenges reactive oxygen species and reduces lipid peroxidation, mitigating damage to non-malignant cells during stress conditions [39]. Studies showed that taurine enhances T-cell viability, modulates cytokine release, improves the cytotoxic activity of CD8+ T cells against tumor cells, and reduces apoptosis in these immunocompetent cells [66]. Taurine has a role in calcium homeostasis and regulatory effect on apoptotic pathways, and it protects non-malignant cells from death [31]. Unlike tumor cells, which have dysregulated signaling pathways, normal proliferating cells are less sensitive to taurine-driven apoptosis. Therefore, the taurine-induced activation of apoptosis not only promotes breast cancer cell apoptosis but also prevents breast cancer metastasis [75]. Factors affecting the cellular uptake of taurine in breast cancer

The intracellular level of taurine is influenced by both its synthesis within the cell and its uptake from the extracellular environment. Taurine is naturally synthesized from cysteine through several enzyme-catalyzed steps, involving enzymes such as glutamate decarboxylase and cysteine dioxygenase [76]. The efficiency of the cellular uptake of taurine fluctuates due to variations in the extracellular microenvironment and molecular differences. In addition to endocytosis for the cellular uptake of taurine, the taurine transporter—a type of sodium/chloride-dependent transporter expressed in the cell membrane—plays a crucial role in regulating intracellular taurine levels. Active taurine transport from the extracellular environment is regulated through protein kinase C (PKC) phosphorylation, which is ATP-dependent [77]. A variety of mechanisms regulate the expression of the taurine transporter in the cell membrane. For example, the tumor suppressor gene p53 represses the expression of the taurine transporter in the cell membrane [77].

Taurine uptake depends on the concentration of extracellular Na+. Substituting choline for Na+ reduces taurine influx, with choline supporting only 3.3 % of the sodium influx. Conversely, replacing Cl− with NO3− supports 20 % of the total sodium influx [78]. Volume-activated Cl− channel inhibitors suppress taurine efflux [61], although inconsistent results have been obtained across studies [79]. Previous research has demonstrated that taurine transport is ion-dependent. Osmolarity is another key regulator of the direction of taurine transport. Increased extracellular osmolarity promotes cellular taurine uptake; this phenomenon is not observed for the uptake of other amino acids such as leucine and carnitine [80]. By contrast, extracellular hypoosmolarity stimulates taurine efflux, which is likely related to Cl− channel activity [81,82].

Extracellular molecules also substantially influence intracellular taurine levels. For instance, extracellular 17β-estradiol increases taurine uptake through Na+-dependent taurine transport in ER-positive (ER+) breast cancer cells [78]. Nutritional factors, such as folate, and methionine restriction can reprogram cancer cell metabolism, decreasing intracellular taurine levels [83,84]. Because methionine and folate are common nutrients and important dietary supplements, their deficiency can affect cellular taurine levels and consequently influence breast cancer progression. Certain medications also affect taurine levels. Tamoxifen, a hormone therapy for breast cancer, not only binds to ER but also inhibits taurine efflux under extracellular hypo-osmolar conditions [82]. Valproic acid, which is commonly prescribed for epilepsy, may be a new breast cancer treatment, as it downregulates the taurine/hypotaurine metabolic pathway [85]. Thus, based on these findings, the cancer microenvironment, depending on the physiological and biochemical status, markedly affects taurine levels in breast cancer cells (Fig. 2).

Clinical application of taurine in breast cancer

Although clear evidence supports the antioxidant properties of taurine and its potential therapeutic application for breast cancer, its clinical application remains limited. Some clinical trials have identified taurine as a potential biomarker of breast cancer [86,87]. The dysregulation of amino acids, including taurine, occasionally occurs in breast cancer tissue, and the levels of these amino acids are a useful indicator of the presence of cancer [88]. Detecting taurine levels in breast tissue can aid in differential diagnosis; thus, taurine detection may a valuable tool for cancer diagnosis in clinical settings [89,90]. The breast cancer microenvironment is highly complex, involving immune responses, angiogenesis, hypoxic zones, cytokine release, and other factors. Any variation in these elements can affect both the intracellular and extracellular concentrations of taurine. Consequently, monitoring taurine levels may provide insights into cancer cell proliferation and invasion. To this end, a study demonstrated that higher taurine concentrations in postoperative tissue samples from breast cancer patients who had undergone neoadjuvant chemotherapy were associated with higher long-term survival rates [91]. Nonetheless, this effect remains to be verified by additional clinical studies.

Current preclinical research indicates that taurine's antioxidative and cytoprotective properties may reduce toxicity in normal tissues, as described previously. Taurine potentially enhances chemotherapy efficacy by modulating cytokine production [64], inducing apoptosis in tumor cells, and regulating immune responses against cancer [66]. Clinically, taurine serves not only as a neoadjuvant but also as a predictive marker for chemotherapy responsiveness [63,91]. Furthermore, given its immunomodulatory effects, taurine supplementation may augment immune checkpoint therapy. Recent clinical reports support the beneficial role of taurine supplementation in enhancing the efficacy of PD-1 immune checkpoint therapy [22].

Conventional cancer therapies often trigger systemic inflammation and oxidative injury, leading to significant adverse effects that constrain therapeutic potential. Taurine's anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties may mitigate systemic toxicity, protecting normal tissues. Preclinical studies have demonstrated favorable outcomes with taurine supplementation [92,93], and clinical studies have similarly supported taurine's role in reducing treatment-related adverse effects [94]. Cancer-associated cachexia significantly impairs patients' quality of life and daily activities due to progressive skeletal muscle wasting. Taurine's anti-inflammatory activity may prevent muscle loss, making taurine supplementation particularly valuable for patients at risk of cachexia [95].

Despite promising preclinical data, formal clinical trials evaluating taurine supplementation protocols as an adjunctive therapy in standard cancer treatment regimens remain limited. Dose-ranging studies are required to define optimal regimens, verify safety, and confirm that taurine's protective effects do not inadvertently compromise therapeutic efficacy against cancer cells. Standardization of taurine administration—covering dosage, timing, and duration relative to chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy—is essential.

In our study, we highlight multiple mechanisms through which taurine may enhance cancer therapy outcomes and provide protection to normal tissues. Consequently, it is crucial to conduct multi-center clinical trials and prospective cohort studies to establish evidence-based taurine supplementation protocols. Integrating taurine into supportive care guidelines may help personalize cancer therapy, particularly benefiting patients at higher risk for toxicity or those with compromised nutritional or immune status.

Conclusion

The multiple roles of taurine in cellular metabolism, oxidative stress modulation, and apoptosis induction and its interaction with the immune system highlight its potential as a therapeutic agent for breast cancer. Taurine's ability to modulate metabolism in breast cancer cells by normalizing glucose and lactate levels, preventing intracellular calcium overload, and altering amino acid profiles indicates its influence on tumor growth and proliferation. The observed metabolic changes in taurine-treated breast cancer models, such as decreased levels of creatinine and pyruvate, suggest that taurine may limit the energy supply of cancer cells, thus inhibiting tumor progression. Additionally, the antioxidant properties of taurine offer marked protective effects against oxidative stress, a known factor in cancer development. By enhancing mitochondrial function, reducing lipid peroxidation, and normalizing glutathione levels, taurine protects against cellular damage.

Although reactive oxygen species (ROS), such as superoxide anion (O2−), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), and hypochlorous acid (HOCl), generated within the phagosome are essential for pathogen elimination, their excessive production can damage the phagosomal membrane, allowing ROS leakage into the cytoplasm. This leakage subsequently induces oxidative stress and apoptosis in phagocytic cells. Additionally, phagocytic cells normally produce various inflammatory mediators, including nitric oxide (NO), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin (IL)-6, IL-8, IL-12, and others. Overproduction of these mediators can result in interactions leading to the formation of toxic reactive nitrogen species, such as peroxynitrite (ONOO−). Taurine derivatives mitigate oxidative stress by inhibiting excessive ROS generation and modulating inflammatory mediator production through multiple mechanisms, such as inhibition of PMA-induced phosphorylation of p47phox [96] and suppression iNOS expresison [97]. The formation of taurine derivatives, such as N-bromotaurine and N-chlorotaurine, further extends its antioxidant properties, protecting phagocytic cells from self-oxidative injury. The effects of taurine on the immune system also contribute to its anticancer effects. By modulating the production of cytokines and enhancing the activity of cytotoxic immune cells, taurine acts enhances defenses against cancer. The increase in the number of CD8+ T cells and the decrease in the number of regulatory T cells (CD4+CD25+) and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (CD11b+Ly6G+) indicate that taurine can enhance antitumor immunity, potentially improving the efficacy of existing immunotherapies. Furthermore, taurine induces apoptosis in breast cancer cells through the upregulation of proapoptotic proteins such as PUMA and Bax and the downregulation of antiapoptotic proteins such as Bcl-2; this is a direct mechanism through which taurine mediates programmed cell death. Through this mechanism, taurine not only reduces tumor cell viability but also prevents metastasis, thereby enhancing the overall prognosis of breast cancer patients. The clinical application of taurine as a biomarker and therapeutic adjuvant remains to be verified. For this, additional investigations should be conducted to determine the optimal dosage, treatment regimen, and long-term safety for taurine. Current evidence indicates that higher taurine concentrations in postoperative tissue samples from breast cancer patients are correlated with higher long-term survival rates. This correlation implies the potential benefit of taurine supplementation for enhancing patient outcomes following conventional treatments such as surgery. Conversely, certain mechanisms of the antitumor effects of taurine depend on synergistic immune responses. In the context of immune deficiency, taurine supplementation has been shown to promote tumor growth in animal studies. Therefore, before the prescription of taurine supplements, the immune status in candidate breast cancer patients should be carefully evaluated.

In clinical practice, taurine supplements are readily available in tablet form. However, insufficient evidence is available as to the efficacy and safety of daily supplementation, as well as the appropriate dosage, frequency, and dosing duration. Further research is needed to determine the optimal dosage, frequency, and dosing duration of taurine supplements. Particular attention should be paid to patients with immunosuppression, such as those undergoing chemotherapy or those with acquired immunodeficiency syndrome.

In summary, taurine's diverse biological activities and its potential to support conventional cancer therapies and enhance their efficiency make it a candidate for further research and clinical application in breast cancer treatment. Future studies should elucidate the precise mechanisms underlying the anticancer effects of taurine, and clinical trials should be conducted to validate its efficacy and safety for breast cancer patients.
