Predicting stillbirth and identifying key maternal risk factors using machine learning
Authors: Merga Abdissa Aga
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStillbirth remains a major public health concern, particularly in low-income and middle-income countries. Identifying maternal and obstetric determinants is essential for prevention and targeted interventions. Logistic regression offers a baseline predictive model, while machine learning (ML) methods, such as Random Forest (RF) and Extreme Gradient Boosting, can improve predictive accuracy and highlight key risk factors through feature importance. This study aimed to predict stillbirth and identify influential maternal and obstetric predictors among pregnant women in Ethiopia using ML models.MethodsA retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted using maternal and obstetric records from Bishoftu General Hospital, Ethiopia. Predictors included maternal age, weight, gravidity, gestational age at admission and delivery, history of pre-eclampsia, antenatal care visits, pregnancy complications, multiplicity, previous abortion and mode of delivery. Data were split into training (70%) and testing (30%) sets. RF, Gradient Boosting Machines, Support Vector Machines and logistic regression were applied. Model performance was evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall, balanced accuracy and receiver operating characteristic-area under the curve (ROC-AUC). Feature importance and SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) supported interpretability.ResultsAmong 549 pregnancies, 17 stillbirths occurred. RF outperformed other models, achieving 92% accuracy, 0.95 ROC-AUC and 0.94 balanced accuracy. Maternal age was the strongest predictor, followed by mode of labour, maternal weight, gravidity and delivery mode. Pregnancy complications and antenatal care visits showed moderate importance, while history of pre-eclampsia, previous abortion and multiplicity contributed minimally. SHAP analysis confirmed these findings and explained variable-specific effects on risk.ConclusionsMaternal age emerged as the dominant determinant of stillbirth, with labour and delivery factors and maternal characteristics also contributing. ML models, particularly RF, effectively identified high-risk pregnancies and provided interpretable predictions through SHAP analysis. These findings underscore the potential of ML to support targeted prenatal care and reduce stillbirth risk in low-resource settings.

Introduction

Adverse birth outcomes, particularly stillbirth, remain a major public health concern worldwide. Globally, over two million stillbirths occur annually, and approximately 2.4 million neonates die within the first month of life, highlighting the urgent need for effective strategies to prevent and manage these outcomes.1 2 Despite advances in maternal and neonatal care, many countries—especially low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs)—continue to face challenges in reducing stillbirth rates.

In Africa, maternal and neonatal health outcomes are particularly concerning. High maternal mortality ratios and neonatal mortality rates reflect gaps in access to quality healthcare, limited utilisation of antenatal services and insufficient capacity for early identification and management of high-risk pregnancies.3 4 Factors such as maternal age, parity, pregnancy complications and health system limitations contribute to elevated stillbirth rates across the continent.

Ethiopia faces similar challenges, with a maternal mortality ratio of 412 per 100 000 live births and a neonatal mortality rate of 27 per 1000 live births,5 6 which is slightly higher than the African regional average of 23 per 1000 live births and above the global average of 17 per 1000 live births.7 Stillbirths are influenced by a complex interplay of maternal, obstetric and clinical factors. Key maternal determinants include age and weight, while obstetric history—such as parity, gravidity and previous abortions—also plays a significant role. Additionally, gestational age (GA) at admission and delivery, antenatal care (ANC) utilisation, pregnancy complications, multiplicity and mode of delivery have been repeatedly identified as important predictors.810

Traditional epidemiological studies have often relied on logistic regression or survival analysis to quantify the effects of these determinants.4 11 While these methods provide valuable insights, they have limitations: they assume linear relationships, may not adequately capture interactions among predictors and are less effective for high-dimensional datasets. Moreover, most studies have been conducted in high-income countries, limiting the generalisability of their findings to LMICs such as Ethiopia, where health system constraints and sociocultural factors play a critical role.

In recent years, machine learning (ML) techniques have emerged as powerful tools for predictive modelling in healthcare. ML algorithms, such as Random forests (RFs), gradient boosting machines and support vector machines (SVMs), can model non-linear relationships, handle complex interactions and identify key predictors without strong parametric assumptions.12 13 Several studies have successfully applied ML to predict adverse birth outcomes, demonstrating improved accuracy and the ability to uncover novel risk factors.7 14 However, the application of ML to maternal and neonatal health in Ethiopia remains limited, with few studies using comprehensive hospital-based datasets to predict stillbirth.

Most existing studies on stillbirth prediction have been conducted in high-income countries. In Ethiopia, very few studies have investigated birth outcomes, and none have applied ML techniques; those that exist often focus on a limited set of predictors or specific subpopulations.5 6 In contrast, our study integrates a wide range of maternal, obstetric and clinical variables from hospital records. The increasing availability of hospital records and electronic health data in Ethiopia provides a unique opportunity to apply ML techniques to identify high-risk pregnancies and guide targeted interventions.

This study aims to fill this gap by applying ML approaches to identify determinants of stillbirth among pregnant women in Ethiopia. Using comprehensive maternal, obstetric and clinical data from Bishoftu General Hospital, we develop predictive models that estimate stillbirth risk and determine the most influential predictors. The findings are intended to guide evidence-based prenatal care strategies and targeted interventions to reduce stillbirth risk in Ethiopia and similar LMIC settings.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study employed a retrospective cross-sectional design, analysing maternal and obstetric records from Bishoftu General Hospital in the Oromia region of Ethiopia. The study population included pregnant women with complete records of maternal, obstetric and clinical variables, including maternal age, weight, gravidity, GA at admission and delivery, maternal blood pressure, ANC visits, history of pre-eclampsia, pregnancy complications, multiplicity of pregnancy, previous abortion and mode of delivery. Data were collected from hospital records between January 2023 and December 2023.

Participants

A total of 549 pregnant women who delivered at Bishoftu General Hospital, Ethiopia, between January 2023 and December 2023 were included in this retrospective study.

The study population consisted entirely of female participants, and ethnic group information was not recorded in hospital records. All eligible records that met the inclusion criteria were included in the analysis.

Outcome variable

The primary outcome variable was birth outcome, categorised as either alive or stillbirth. This binary classification allows for the application of supervised ML techniques suitable for classification tasks.

Predictor variables

Predictors included maternal and obstetric characteristics: maternal age, weight, gravidity (primigravida, multigravida), GA at admission and delivery, history of pre-eclampsia (no, yes), ANC visits (<4, ≥4), pregnancy complications (no, yes), multiplicity of pregnancy (singleton, twin), previous abortion (no, yes), mode of onset of labour (spontaneous, induced and elective) and mode of delivery (spontaneous vaginal delivery, vacuum, C-section). Continuous variables were standardised to improve model convergence, while categorical variables were encoded using one-hot or label encoding as appropriate.

Data preprocessing

Data cleaning included removing outliers and normalising continuous features.13 Correlation analysis was conducted to identify multicollinearity among predictors, and highly correlated variables were considered for exclusion or consolidation. Feature selection was guided by domain knowledge, univariate statistical significance and automated feature importance ranking from tree-based models.14 To address the class imbalance in the outcome variable, the training set was balanced using the Random Over-Sampling Examples technique, which generates synthetic minority-class examples while preserving the distribution of the majority class.15

The dataset was partitioned into training and testing sets using a 70–30 ratio, a widely used approach in ML to ensure sufficient data for both model learning and independent evaluation.16 To minimise the potential bias from a single random split, we further employed repeated stratified cross-validation, where the train-test split was repeated multiple times, and average performance metrics were reported. This approach provides more stable and reliable estimates of model performance. Performance was assessed using metrics appropriate for imbalanced data, including balanced accuracy and receiver operating characteristic-area under the curve (ROC-AUC).17

approaches

Firth’s penalised logistic regression is a statistical method designed to address issues in logistic regression models, particularly when dealing with small sample sizes, rare events or complete separation.18 This technique modifies the standard likelihood function to reduce bias in parameter estimates, leading to more reliable results in such challenging scenarios. In R, the logistf package is commonly used to fit Firth’s logistic regression models. This package implements the bias-reduced logistic regression method, which further improves prediction accuracy by adjusting for biases in predicted probabilities.19 In this model, the regression coefficient (β) represents the log-odds change in the probability of stillbirth per unit increase in the predictor. Model development and evaluation

The dataset was randomly split into training (70%) and testing (30%) sets. Models were evaluated based on accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score and AUC-ROC.13 14 Feature importance and SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values were computed to interpret model predictions and identify the most influential predictors of birth outcomes.2 7

Patient involvement

This study was retrospective and used anonymised maternal and obstetric records. Patients were not directly involved in the design, conduct or reporting of this research. All data were deidentified to protect patient privacy.

Results

Descriptive analysis of maternal and delivery characteristics

Mothers who attended four or more ANC visits had a higher proportion of alive births (524/540, 97.0%) compared with those with fewer than four visits (8/9, 88.9%). This suggests that adequate ANC attendance may be protective against stillbirth, highlighting the importance of regular prenatal care in improving birth outcomes. The mode of labour onset also influenced birth outcomes. Elective labour showed no stillbirths (13/13, 100%), while induced labour had slightly better survival (323/330, 97.9%) than spontaneous onset (196/206, 95.1%). This indicates that planned or medically supervised labour may reduce the risk of stillbirth. The type of delivery demonstrated variable outcomes. C-sections and spontaneous vaginal deliveries had high survival rates (98.1% and 97.2%, respectively). In contrast, vacuum-assisted deliveries had a higher stillbirth proportion (3/18, 16.7%), likely reflecting that this intervention is often used in high-risk or complicated deliveries (table 1).

Women with a history of previous abortion had 94.6% alive births (35/37), while those without previous abortion had 97.1% alive births (497/512). The difference is small, indicating that previous abortion was not strongly associated with birth outcome in this population. The birth outcomes were similar between primigravida (97.2% alive) and multigravida mothers (96.7% alive). This suggests that gravidity does not significantly influence the risk of stillbirth in this dataset. Mothers with a history of pre-eclampsia had slightly higher stillbirth proportions (3/39, 7.7%) compared with those without (14/510, 2.7%). Although the sample size is small, this finding suggests that pre-eclampsia may increase the risk of adverse birth outcomes. Women who developed complications during pregnancy had a stillbirth proportion of 6.3% (3/48), compared with 2.8% (14/501) among those without complications. This indicates that pregnancy complications are associated with a higher risk of stillbirth, highlighting the need for careful monitoring and management. Twin pregnancies showed a slightly higher stillbirth proportion (1/14, 7.1%) compared with singleton pregnancies (16/535, 3.0%). Although the sample of twins is small, the data suggest that multiple pregnancies carry a higher risk of stillbirth than singletons. Overall, factors such as adequate ANC visits, elective labour and C-section delivery appear protective, whereas vacuum-assisted deliveries, history of pre-eclampsia, pregnancy complications and twin pregnancies are associated with increased stillbirth risk. Despite variations across factors, most mothers had live births (>90%), reflecting good maternal care or a relatively low-risk population (table 1).

Maternal age was found to have a notable association with birth outcomes. Mothers aged below 30 years had a stillbirth rate of only 1.6%, compared with 6.0% among those aged 30 years and above. This indicates that advanced maternal age increases the risk of stillbirth (table 1). Maternal weight also showed an important relationship with birth outcomes. The stillbirth rate was 4.9% among mothers weighing 70 kg or more, while it was only 1.7% among those with lower weight. This finding implies that higher maternal weight, which may reflect overweight or obesity, is associated with an increased risk of stillbirth. GA at admission was another strong predictor. Mothers admitted before 37 weeks of gestation experienced a stillbirth rate of 7.5%, compared with 2.7% among those admitted at term or post-term. This suggests that preterm admission significantly increases the risk of adverse outcomes. Similarly, GA at delivery showed a marked effect on birth outcomes. Preterm deliveries (<37 weeks) had a stillbirth rate of 9.7%, whereas term or post-term deliveries had a much lower rate of 2.7% (table 1).

Descriptive analysis revealed clear differences in GA between groups. Both GA at admission and at delivery were lower among stillbirth cases compared with live births, with preterm pregnancies showing a higher proportion of stillbirths (table 1). While this underscores the clinical relevance of GA, these variables were not included in the ML models because they are only known late in pregnancy or at delivery, which limits their usefulness for prospective prediction and early intervention. Instead, the predictive analysis focused on maternal and clinical factors available before delivery. This approach allows the models to evaluate risk based on information accessible during pregnancy, thereby improving their potential utility for early identification of high-risk cases.

Firth logistic regression analysis

Firth’s logistic regression has been effectively applied in predicting stillbirth outcomes, particularly in datasets with small sample sizes or rare events. Reducing bias and improving model stability allows for more accurate identification of risk factors associated with stillbirth, thereby informing better clinical decision-making and interventions.

The results show that maternal age ≥30 years was significantly associated with increased odds of the adverse outcome. Mothers aged 30 years or older had about 2.5 times higher odds of experiencing the outcome compared with those younger than 30 years (OR 2.46, 95% CI 1.10 to 5.55, p=0.029). This suggests that advancing maternal age remains a critical risk factor, possibly reflecting the higher likelihood of comorbidities, reduced physiological resilience and increased obstetric complications in older mothers (table 2).

Similarly, maternal weight ≥70 kg was found to be a significant predictor, with an OR of 2.18 (95% CI: 1.01 to 4.65, p=0.048). This indicates that women with higher body weight had more than double the odds of the outcome compared with those weighing less than 70 kg. The association may be explained by the well-documented relationship between maternal overweight/obesity and complications such as gestational hypertension, pre-eclampsia and delivery difficulties, which can in turn contribute to adverse maternal and perinatal outcomes (table 2).

A history of pre-eclampsia was strongly associated with increased risk (OR 2.77, 95% CI 1.20 to 6.40, p=0.018). Women with prior pre-eclampsia had nearly three times the odds of the adverse outcome compared with those without such a history. This finding is consistent with previous literature, which highlights pre-eclampsia as a recurring and persistent risk factor due to its underlying vascular and metabolic consequences (table 2).

Finally, developing complications during pregnancy markedly elevated the risk (OR 3.89, 95% CI 1.49 to 10.15, p=0.006). Mothers who developed complications were nearly four times more likely to experience the outcome compared with those without complications. This underscores the importance of timely detection and management of maternal complications, as these events represent direct pathways to maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality (table 2).

ML model performance

Overall accuracy of the models ranged from moderate to high. RF achieved the highest accuracy at 92%, followed by XGBoost (82%), SVM (78%) and Firth logistic (71%). This indicates that RF correctly classified the majority of cases, outperforming other models in overall predictive performance. Sensitivity, which measures the ability to correctly identify stillbirth cases, was highest for RF (97%), while Firth Logistic achieved 81%. XGBoost and SVM had lower sensitivities (65% and 60%, respectively), meaning they missed about one-third to nearly half of true stillbirths. High sensitivity is critical for rare adverse outcomes like stillbirth to minimise missed cases (table 3).

Specificity reflects how well models identify alive births. RF again performed best (91%), followed by XGBoost (88%), SVM (78%) and Firth Logistic (70%). This shows that while Firth logistic detected most stillbirths, it produced more false positives among alive cases. Precision, which measures the proportion of predicted stillbirths that were actually true, was highest for RF (40%), indicating that about two-fifths of its stillbirth predictions were correct. XGBoost had moderate precision (24%), while Firth logistic (12%) and SVM (11%) produced many false positive predictions. This highlights that high sensitivity can come at the cost of low precision, particularly in rare-event settings.

Balanced accuracy, which accounts for both sensitivity and specificity, was highest for RF (94%), followed by Firth logistic (75%), XGBoost (76%) and SVM (69%). ROC-AUC, which indicates overall discriminative ability, was also highest for RF (0.95), demonstrating excellent separation between alive and stillbirth cases (table 3).

Overall, RF outperformed all other models across most metrics, balancing high sensitivity, specificity and precision. Firth logistic achieved good sensitivity but very low precision, making it prone to false positives. XGBoost and SVM were less effective, particularly in detecting stillbirths. For rare outcomes, RF provides the most reliable predictions among the models evaluated.

Discussion

This study identified maternal age as the most influential predictor of stillbirth in the RF model. Other important contributors included mode of labour, maternal weight, gravidity and delivery mode, while developing complications and number of ANC visits played a moderate role. In contrast, variables such as history of pre-eclampsia, previous abortion and pregnant multiplicity showed minimal predictive value. These findings were confirmed by SHAP analysis, which provided transparent explanations of the contribution of each feature to model predictions. To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies in Ethiopia to integrate ML with SHAP interpretation in predicting stillbirth, offering novel insights into complex relationships and rare outcomes.

Our results align with Ethiopian and international studies. Tesfay et al20 reported that advanced maternal age significantly increases the risk of stillbirth, while Melaku et al21 found labour-related factors to be important. Similarly, Laikemariam et al22 identified a 1.58-fold higher risk of perinatal mortality among older mothers, and studies by Dongarwar et al23 and Avagliano et al24 showed that women aged ≥40 years face markedly higher risk. These findings reinforce the conclusion that maternal age is a critical determinant of stillbirth and highlight the need for enhanced antenatal monitoring and timely interventions in older mothers.

Labour and delivery characteristics also contributed substantially. Mode of labour and delivery mode were important predictors, consistent with reports by Melaku et al21 and Tufa et al,25 which showed that obstetric complications and instrumental deliveries were linked with poor birth outcomes. Maternal weight ranked highly in terms of Gini importance, suggesting that nutritional status influences stillbirth risk even if its effect on overall model accuracy was more modest. Gravidity also played a significant role, supporting previous findings that reproductive history shapes pregnancy outcomes.

Moderate contributors included number of ANC visits and development of complications, both of which are clinically meaningful. Adequate ANC has long been associated with improved pregnancy outcomes, while pregnancy complications are established risk factors for adverse events.2527 Conversely, a history of pre-eclampsia, previous abortion and pregnant multiplicity contributed little to model performance, though they may still hold clinical relevance for individual patients.

Overall, the RF model emphasised maternal age as the dominant predictor, with labour characteristics, weight and gravidity also substantially influencing outcomes. While other covariates were less predictive in this dataset, they remain important from a clinical perspective. These results are consistent with both Ethiopian and global evidence2028 and underscore the need for targeted monitoring and timely intervention for older mothers and those with high-risk labour characteristics to reduce the burden of stillbirth.

Limitations

Despite these insights, several limitations should be noted. First, the data were collected from a limited number of health facilities, which may restrict the generalisability of findings. Second, the use of observational, secondary data introduces potential issues of information bias, missing data and unmeasured confounding. Third, although the RF model identified important predictors, ML approaches do not establish causality, and clinically relevant variables may appear to have low predictive importance. Fourth, the relatively small number of stillbirth events could have affected model performance and reduced the stability of estimates, particularly for rare covariates. Fifth, important factors such as socioeconomic status, maternal nutrition and quality of ANC were not included, although they may substantially influence birth outcomes.

Future research should build on these findings by using larger, multicentre datasets and incorporating additional maternal and contextual factors. Models that rely solely on prenatal variables available before delivery may provide greater value for prospective, real-time risk prediction and early intervention in high-risk pregnancies.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that maternal age is the most influential predictor of stillbirth in the Ethiopian context, followed by mode of labour, maternal weight, gravidity and delivery mode. Other variables, including the number of ANC visits and development of complications, contributed moderately, while factors such as history of pre-eclampsia, previous abortion and pregnant multiplicity provided minimal predictive value.

ML approaches, particularly RF, proved effective in identifying these determinants and offered interpretable insights through feature importance and SHAP analyses. To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies in Ethiopia to combine ML models with SHAP interpretation for stillbirth prediction, providing a novel, data-driven framework for understanding risk.

These findings support targeted prenatal and intrapartum interventions for older mothers and women with high-risk labour characteristics, which may help reduce the incidence of stillbirth. Future research incorporating additional maternal, socioeconomic and nutritional variables, and using larger, multicentre datasets, is recommended to further enhance predictive performance and generalisability.
