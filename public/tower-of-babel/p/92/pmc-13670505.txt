Glutamine synthetase genes family mediates nitrogen metabolism and disease responses in Kentucky bluegrass
Authors: Ke Wu, Tongsheng Liu, Shuning Li, Meizhu Chen, Zixian Wang, Xiaoyang Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKentucky bluegrass (Poa pratensis L.), an important cool-season turfgrass species, requires adequate nitrogen (N) fertilization for normal growth and development. It is also susceptible to infection by pathogens. The glutamine synthetase (GS) gene family, known to play central roles in plant N metabolism and disease responses across higher plants, has functions that remain uncharacterized in Kentucky bluegrass.ResultsWe identified and characterized four GS genes (PpGS1.1, PpGS1.2, PpGS1.3, and PpGS2) in Kentucky bluegrass. Phylogenetic analysis revealed a close relationship with perennial ryegrass (Lolium perenne), and structural analysis confirmed conserved functional domains and critical residues involved in substrate binding, phosphotransfer activity, thermostability, and cation coordination. Expression analyses showed that under low-nitrogen (LN) stress, PpGS1.1 was strongly upregulated (∼200%), while PpGS1.3 and PpGS2 showed modest increases (∼20%), and PpGS1.2 was unresponsive. Conversely, powdery mildew infection inhibits PpGS1.1 (∼79.4%), PpGS1.3(∼40.2%), and PpGS2 (∼76.2%) expression level, with PpGS1.2 unchanged. Transgenic tobacco overexpressing PpGS genes exhibited enhanced growth under LN stress, with higher N metabolism and antioxidant enzyme activities, reduced superoxide accumulation, and increased root length and fresh weight. However, upon tobacco mosaic virus (TMV) inoculation, these lines developed larger lesions and showed reduced antioxidant enzyme activities.ConclusionThe study reveals GS genes family mediates N metabolism and disease responses in Kentucky bluegrass. The findings provide a theoretical foundation for turfgrass N management and disease resistance breeding, which is critical for developing elite Kentucky bluegrass cultivars with LN input and durable disease resistance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09568-6.Keywords: Kentucky bluegrass, PpGSs gene family, Nitrogen metabolism, Disease responses

Introduction

Kentucky bluegrass (Poa pratensis L.) is an important cool-season turfgrass whose normal growth and development require adequate N fertilization [1]. Generally, LN stress induces chlorosis, leaf fading, slow growth, reduced turf quality, and increased susceptibility to rust [2]. Conversely, excessive N application promotes excessive shoot growth and wider leaves, while increasing the risks of powdery mildew and summer patch [3]. Moreover, different cultivars of Kentucky bluegrass vary in their growth responses to N concentrations [2]. Therefore, an efficient N metabolism system is essential for sustaining normal growth under LN conditions and preventing excessive elongation under high- N supply in Kentucky bluegrass.

In higher plants, GS is a key enzyme in the N metabolism pathway. Two forms of glutamine synthetase, GSI and GSII, exist in both monocotyledonous and dicotyledonous plants, with a limited number of family members [4]. Based on subcellular localization, GS is classified into two types: cytosolic GS1, which is distributed in the cytoplasm, and plastidic GS2, which is localized in chloroplasts or plastids [5]. GS1 in plants is encoded by a small gene family, primarily consisting of three to five GS1 genes, whereas GS2 is encoded by a single GS2 gene in most plant species [6]. In Arabidopsis thaliana, five GS1-encoding genes and one GS2-encoding gene have been identified [7]. Among cereal crops, there are generally three to five GS1 genes. For instance, rice (Oryza sativa L.) possesses three (GS1;1, GS1;2, and GS1;3), maize (Zea mays L.) has five (Gln1-1, Gln1-2, Gln1-3, Gln1-4, and Gln1-5), and Brachypodium distachyon contains three (Gln1-1, Gln1-2, and Gln1-3), wheat comprises three classes (GS1, GSr, and GSe) [5]. The nucleotide sequence identity of the GS1 genes in higher plants ranges from 76.5% to 82.3%, while the amino acid sequence identity ranges from 78.7% to 83.1%, with an average identity reaching 88.1%–93% [4]. Bayesian molecular clock dating 155 GS genes from 45 species revealed that GS2 diverged approximately 560 million years ago, while the two GS1 subtypes began to diverge around 700 million years ago [8]. Phylogenetic analysis of their GS protein sequences from Arabidopsis and six cereal species showed that the GS proteins of Arabidopsis and cereals clustered on distinct evolutionary branches, supporting the conclusion that the emergence of GS1 and GS2 genes occurred before the divergence of monocots and dicots [5]. It was found that GS1 proteins belonging to the same subgroup are located on different phylogenetic branches within the Panicoideae (maize), Pooideae (Brachypodium, and wheat), and Oryzoideae (rice) subfamilies. This indicates that the evolution of the cytosolic GS1 genes occurred prior to the diversification of the Poaceae family [5, 9].

The GS genes play a profound role in plant growth and development [10]. In maize, four GS genes located on chromosomes 1, 4, and 5 can enhance the activity of enzymes related to grain filling [11]. In wheat, a quantitative trait locus (QTL) for GS enzyme activity in the flag leaf was mapped to the TaGSr locus, which co-localizes with a QTL for grain N concentration [12]. Additionally, another wheat GS1 gene (TaGSe) has been associated with a locus for grain protein content [13]. In rice, Osgs1.1, Osgs1.2, and Osgs1.3 genes participate in amino acid homeostasis, plastid development, and seed development. Mutants of these genes often display dwarfism, growth retardation, and impaired grain filling [4, 14]. Functional specialization among isoforms exists within the GS gene family. Under LN conditions, Arabidopsis GLN1.1, GLN1.2, and GLN1.3 collectively mediate ammonium assimilation, exhibiting functional redundancy [15]. However, under high- N conditions, GS1.2 plays a dominant role, with its promoter being specifically activated in the root cortex and epidermis to enhance ammonium assimilation. Meanwhile, GS1.3 contributes minimally due to its low expression in Arabidopsis roots [16]. In Kentucky bluegrass, GS1.1 overexpression upregulates N metabolism-related genes and increases carbon and N content, however, the complete GS gene family in this grass species remains uncharacterized [3].

Glutamine synthetase (GS) genes exhibit a dual role in plant-pathogen interactions. During the early stage of wheat powdery mildew infection, the GS1 and GS2 expressions were suppressed, accompanied by a decrease in GS and glutamate synthase (GOGAT) activities, leading to a reduction in disease resistance [17]. Similarly, GhGLN1.5 knockout in cotton (Gossypium hirsutum L.) reduces arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) colonization, N uptake, and AM symbiosis-dependent resistance to Verticillium wilt [18]. Conversely, GS and its metabolic products can negatively increase host susceptibility by potentially nourishing pathogens [19]. In tomato (Solanum lycopersicum L.), overexpression of GS2 promotes seedling growth and biomass accumulation but also leads to decreased defense against Pseudomonas syringae pst DC3000 [10]. Meanwhile, the GS gene was heterologously expressed in tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum L.) to verify the role in pathogenic interaction. Overexpression of mulberry (Morus spp.) MaGS2 in tobacco reduces its resistance to Ciboria shiraiana, whereas knock down of MaGS2 enhances resistance to C. shiraiana, indicating that MaGS2 acts as a negative regulator of disease resistance [20]. Moreover, GS functional status is intricately linked to reactive oxygen species (ROS) metabolism. Overexpressing the wheat cytosolic GsTa4D.GSe enhances superoxide dismutase (SOD) and peroxidase (POD) activity and proline levels, mitigating oxidative damage [21]. Similarly, water starwort (Myriophyllum aquaticum) tolerance to high- N involves enhanced GS/GOGAT cycle capacity coupled with activated SOD defenses [22]. In tobacco, expressions of antioxidant and stress-responsive genes (Cu/Zn-SOD, MnSOD, and CBL1) were significantly elevated in plants transformed with the oat AsGS2 gene under drought conditions [23]. However, the relationship between the GS functional status and ROS metabolism has not yet been elucidated in Kentucky bluegrass.

As an important turfgrass species, functional role of GS genes in response to N metabolism and disease defense remains relatively unexplored [1, 24]. Based on this, we cloned the GS genes family in Kentucky bluegrass and analyzed their gene structures and phylogenetic relationships to determine the phylogenetic relationships between this species and other grass species. Subsequently, the expression levels of GS genes under different N levels and after pathogen infection were determined. Transgenic tobacco overexpressing GS genes was generated, and changes in N metabolism and antioxidant-related enzyme levels under different N treatments were measured. Finally, infection with TMV was conducted to investigate the role of GS genes in disease resistance in Kentucky bluegrass. Through this research, we aim to establish novel mechanistic foundations for understanding N metabolism and plant-pathogen interactions in Kentucky bluegrass.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

The P. pratensis ‘Maorshan’ cultivar was used for gene cloning and GS gene expression analysis, was independently bred and conserved by our laboratory [1]. Seeds were sown in growth substrate and transplanted to PVC pots (10 cm in diameter and 15 cm in height) filled with sand-vermiculite (2:1 v/v) mixture in a greenhouse in Qingdao Agricultural University (Qingdao, China; 120°39′E, 36°32′N). During the six- months growth period, plants were cut to 10 cm once a week, watered every five days, and fertilized weekly with 100 mL of Hoagland solution. Three weeks before treatment application, all pots were transferred to a growth chamber, with a 16-h photoperiod under 2000 µmol•m⁻²•s⁻¹ illumination at 25 ± 1 °C, followed by an 8-h dark phase at 23 ± 1 °C, and a relative humidity of 60 ± 5% [25].

Potted Kentucky bluegrass plants were rinsed with deionized water for one week prior to N level treatments. Based on our previous data, the low N treatment (LN) was set at 0.5 mM, while the normal N treatment (NN) was at 15 mM, using NO₃⁻ in Hoagland solution as the N source [2]. Plants were irrigated with 100 mL of the respective N solution every seven days and with deionized water twice weekly. Leaves were harvested after 20 days of differential N treatment.

The powdery mildew (Blumeria graminis) pathogen was previously isolated, purified, and identified by our laboratory [26]. Powdery mildew colonies were inoculated onto healthy seedlings and subjected to five successive generations for pathogen purification. Spores from infected Kentucky bluegrass leaves were dislodged into a 0.8% glucose solution to prepare a spore suspension. The suspension was then sprayed onto healthy leaves. After disease onset, leaves from infected plants were collected and stored at -80°C.

Wild-type tobacco (Nicotiana benthamiana) seeds used in the experiments were stored in our laboratory. Tobacco was selected as a model system due to: (i) its susceptibility to Tobacco mosaic virus (TMV), enabling standardized disease response assays; (ii) absence of functional redundancy complications inherent to the native Kentucky bluegrass GS family; and (iii) the inability to stably transform the plastid-localized PpGS2 gene in Kentucky bluegrass. Tobacco seeds were sown in nutrient soil and transplanted into seedling pots approximately 10–20 days later, followed by cultivation in a light‑controlled greenhouse. The growth conditions for tobacco were as follows: 16 h of light (2000 µmol•m⁻²•s⁻¹) at approximately 25 °C; 8 h of darkness at approximately 23 °C; and a relative humidity of about 60%.

Glutamine Synthetase (GS) genes identification

The full-length transcriptome and other second-generation transcriptome data generated by our laboratory were utilized as a local database in BioEdit software [1, 27]. The protein sequences of rice GS genes were used as the query sequences. The BLASTN program was selected with an E-value threshold of 1e− 20 to search the local database. The obtained sequences were subsequently subjected to BLASTX searches on the NCBI website for comparison, yielding candidate sequences (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi). Total RNA was extracted from leaves of P. pratensis ‘Maorshan’ and reverse-transcribed into cDNA. The reaction protocol was as follows: pre-denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s; followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s and annealing at 58 °C for 30 s. The primers used for amplifying PpGSs were listed in Table S1.

Bioinformatics analysis

To construct the phylogenetic tree, representative GS family sequences from Arabidopsis and other Poaceae species were first retrieved from the NCBI database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). A total of 673 protein sequences containing putative GS domains were obtained. After screening 42 representative species, including A. thaliana, O. sativa, Lolium perenne, and other plant species, one representative GS protein sequence was retained for each species based on sequence length, annotation completeness, and GS type priority for subsequent phylogenetic analysis. Functional motif validation and domain integrity verification of the 42 representative sequences were performed using the NCBI Batch CD-Search tool (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/bwrpsb/bwrpsb.cgi). Subsequently, multiple sequence alignment and phylogenetic tree construction of the filtered 42 GS protein sequences were conducted using MEGA12. Finally, the phylogenetic tree was visualized, annotated, and refined using the iTOL server (https://itol.embl.de/). Conserved motifs of the PpGSs genes were analyzed using MEME (https://meme-suite.org/meme/).

Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT-PCR)

QRT-PCR was conducted for PpGS1.1, PpGS1.2, PpGS1.3, and PpGS2, with the EF-1a gene used as the internal reference gene. The reaction protocol method for the assay was identical to that described in Sect. 2.2. Three biological replicates and three technical replicates were performed for each sample. The relative expression levels of the target genes were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method [28]. The primers used for qRT-PCR are listed in Table S2.

PpGSs vectors construction and genetic transformation

Recombinant pCAMBIA1300 vectors harboring PpGS1.1, PpGS1.3, and PpGS2 genes driven by the CaMV 35 S promoter were constructed. The recombinant plasmids were subsequently transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain LBA4404 via electroporation. Primer sequences used in this study are listed in Table S3. Wild-type tobacco seeds were stored in our laboratory. Healthy leaves from tobacco seedlings were excised, the midribs were removed, and leaves were cut into 0.5 cm² explants. These leaf discs were immersed in A. tumefaciens suspension (OD₆₀₀=0.6) for 5–10 min. Infected explants were transferred to antibiotic-free MS medium (T1), sealed with parafilm, and co-cultivated at 28 °C in darkness for 2–4 d. After co-cultivation, explants were transferred to selection medium (T2), with leaf edges slightly pressed into the medium to enhance selection pressure. Plates were sealed and cultured under a 16/8 h photoperiod (2000 µmol•m⁻²•s⁻¹) at 25–28 °C. Adventitious shoots (~ 1 cm in height) were excised after 2–3 weeks and transferred to rooting medium (T3). Primary roots emerged within 5–10 d, the medium formulas are listed in Table S4.

Transgenic plant screening was performed through PCR amplification of the bar gene. Controls included the corresponding overexpression plasmid (positive control), wild-type tobacco DNA (negative control), and ddH₂O (no-template control) [25]. The cloning method for the qRT-PCR was identical to that described in Sect. 2.2. PCR amplicons were separated via agarose gel electrophoresis, and visual band detection was recorded. All primers are detailed in Table S5.

Fresh weight, root length, nitrogen assimilation enzymes

Fresh weight and root length were measured on five individual plants using a balance and a ruler, respectively. For nitrate reductase (NR) and nitrite reductase (NiR) assays, 0.3 g of fresh leaves were homogenized under ice-cold conditions with 3 mL of 0.1 M potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) containing 7.5 mM cysteine, 1 mM EDTA, and 1.5% (w/v) casein NaNO₃ standard solution, 1% sulfanilic acid solution, and 0.2% α-naphthylamine solution [29]. The homogenate was centrifuged at 10,000 ×g at 4 °C for 10 min. NR activity was measured at 520 nm according to Högberg et al.20, and NiR at 530 nm following Aakanksha et al. [30, 31]. To assess glutamine synthetase (GS) and glutamate synthase (GOGAT) activities, 0.2 g of leaves were homogenized in 5 mL of 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), incubated at 4 °C for 30 min, and then centrifuged at 12,000 ×g for 20 min. The GS reaction mixture containing 0.7 mL of supernatant, 0.1 M Tris-HCl, and 80 mM hydroxylamine hydrochloride, supplemented with 0.7 mL of 40 mM ATP. After incubation for 15 min and centrifuged at 5,000 ×g, activity was assayed at 540 nm [32]. The GOGAT assay mixture included 0.3 mL of extract, 1.6 mL of 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.5), 0.4 mL of 50 mM L-glutamine, 0.5 mL of 50 mM α-ketoglutarate, and 0.2 mL of 0.6 mM NADH. Absorbance at 340 nm was recorded every 30 s for 5 min, with one enzyme unit defined as the amount of catalyzing the oxidation of 1 µmoL NADH per minute [33].

Superoxide (O2•−), and antioxidant determination

Fresh tobacco leaf tissue (0.5 g per assay) was homogenized in 2 mL ice-cold 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.8), centrifuged (12,000 × g, 15 min, 4 °C), and supernatants were then collected for analyses. Superoxide anion (O₂•⁻) content was quantified by reacting 1 mL supernatant with 1 mL of 10 mM hydroxylamine hydrochloride (25 °C, 20 min). The absorbance was read at 530 nm [34]. Catalase (CAT) activity was determined in a reaction mixture containing 0.2 mL supernatant, 1.5 mL of phosphate buffer (pH 7.8), 1 mL of distilled water, and 0.3 mL of 100 mM H₂O₂, and activity was calculated from H₂O₂ decomposition rate monitored at 240 nm [35]. Peroxidase (POD) activity was assayed using 0.1 mL of supernatant with 0.1 mL of 50 mM PBS (pH 6.0), 56 µL of guaiacol, 38 µL of 100 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.5) and 30% H₂O₂. The absorbance change at 470 nm was recorded [34]. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was measured in a reaction system containing 0.1 mL of supernatant, 0.3 mL of 50 mM sodium carbonate buffer (pH 10.2), 0.3 mL of 1.3 mM riboflavin, 0.3 mL of 13 mM methionine, and 0.3 mL of 75 µM NBT. Absorbance was read at 560 nm, with one unit defined as the amount of enzyme inhibiting 50% NBT reduction [36].

TMV inoculation in tobacco plants

Tobacco plants overexpressing PpGSs genes were cultivated in pots within a growth chamber maintained at 25 °C under a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod. A. tumefaciens cultures harboring TMV-GFP plasmid (adjusted to an OD600 of 0.8) were mixed in a 1:1 ratio and infiltrated into the lower leaves of plants at the four-leaf stage using a 1mL needleless syringe. GFP imaging was performed under ultraviolet light illumination, and photographs were captured using an OLYMPUS CAMEDIA E-10 digital camera [37].

Data analysis

The experimental data was analyzed using one-way ANOVA with Statistical Product and Service Solutions (SPSS) (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The significance was determined by a t-test with P < 0.05. Correlation analyses of individual growth parameters were performed using SPSS. Figures were made using GraphPad Prism v.9.00 (Graphpad Company, USA).

Results

PpGSs bioinformatics analysis in Kentucky bluegrass

We identified 4 GS genes (PpGS1.1, PpGS1.2, PpGS1.3, and PpGS2) in Kentucky bluegrass, were encoded by 356, 354, 363 and 430 amino acids, respectively. PpGSs sequence were listed in supplementary files. Phylogenetic analysis was performed on GS gene family members containing the glutamine synthetase (Glu-synt) domain from 42 plant species. The results showed that these species could be divided into five major evolutionary clades (Fig. 1A). Clade I contained 11 Poaceae species and A. thaliana, clade II contained 9 Poaceae species, clade III contained 5 Poaceae species, clade IV contained 8 Poaceae species, and clade V contained 8 Poaceae species. The glutamine synthetase protein of P. pratensis clustered within an evolutionary clade representing monocotyledonous Poaceae species. This clade included typical Poaceae crops, such as perennial ryegrass, oat, barley, and wheat. Notably, P. pratensis showed the closest phylogenetic relationship with L. perenne, whereas it exhibited marked evolutionary divergence from the dicotyledonous species A. thaliana and monocotyledonous species such as rice and maize.

Conserved domain and motif analysis of GS proteins from Kentucky bluegrass, rice, and Arabidopsis demonstrated that all evaluated GS proteins possess two typical conserved domains: an N-terminal Gln-synt_N domain (comprising motifs 1 and 6) and a catalytically active C-terminal Gln-synt_C domain (comprising motifs 2, 3, 4, and 7) (Fig. 1B). Regarding motif composition, most GS proteins share eight conserved motifs with identical arrangements (except for ATGLN1.5). Such highly consistent motif arrangements and domain compositions between monocot and dicot plants imply that these GS proteins have maintained conserved biological functions throughout evolution.

Multiple sequence alignment and secondary structure mapping of GS amino acid sequences from Kentucky bluegrass, Arabidopsis, and rice were performed alongside known crystal structures (maize PDB: 2D3B and another structure PDB: 4ISA) (Fig. 1C). The alignment revealed that these sequences share extremely high overall similarity, particularly within the secondary structure regions (α-helices and β-sheets). More importantly, the sequence alignment precisely localized multiple highly conserved key functional residues, including catalytic core residues involved in substrate binding (e.g., Gly127) and phosphotransfer activity (Arg311 and Arg332); residues participating in ammonia binding (Glu297 and Asp56) and cation coordination (e.g., Glu129), which form a conserved binding loop (Thr293-Ala299); as well as structural residues responsible for maintaining intersubunit hydrophobic interactions (e.g., Leu33) and thermostability (Ile161). The high conservation of these sites indicates that PpGS proteins adopt a catalytic mechanism consistent with classical crystal structures, thereby ensuring structural integrity and catalytic efficiency within complex intracellular environments.

PpGSs expression level under different N contents and disease infection

Expression levels of PpGSs were quantified in Kentucky bluegrass leaves. Under LN conditions, PpGS1.1 expression level increased approximately 200% relative to NN controls (Fig. 2A). PpGS1.2 expression exhibited no significant difference between LN and NN treatments (Fig. 2B). Meanwhile, PpGS1.3 and PpGS2 showed a coordinated increase of approximately 20% under LN compared with NN conditions (Figs. 2C, D).

Following inoculation with B. graminis, PpGS1.1, PpGS1.3, and PpGS2 were significantly downregulated at the transcriptional level (Fig. 2E, G, H). Pathogen stress led to reductions of 76.2%, 79.4%, and 40.2% in the expression of PpGS1.1, PpGS1.3, and PpGS2, respectively, compared with uninfected plants (CK). Notably, PpGS1.2 expression levels remained comparable to those in the CK group despite pathogen exposure (Fig. 2F).

Phenotype, root length, and fresh weight of PpGSs tobacco transgenic plants

Under NN conditions, discernible differences in root length and fresh weight were observed between wild-type (WT) and tobacco transgenic plants (Fig. 3A). Specifically, tobacco transgenic lines overexpressing PpGS1.1, PpGS1.3 and PpGS2 exhibited 26.3%, 5.5%, and 12.3% higher fresh weights, respectively, compared with WT plants (Fig. 3B). Concurrently, the average root length in tobacco transgenic lines reached 23 ± 1.5 mm, representing increases of 67.9%, 60.7%, and 71.4% relative to WT controls for PpGS1.1, PpGS1.3 and PpGS2, respectively (Fig. 3C).

Under LN stress, tobacco transgenic plants exhibited significant growth advantages over WT (Fig. 3D). Fresh weights of PpGS1.1, PpGS1.3 and PpGS2 remained stable at 22 ± 1.5 mg, representing increases of 63.4%, 62.9%, and 57.5%, respectively, compared with WT (Fig. 3E). Concurrently, root lengths in transgenic tobacco lines were significantly enhanced, measuring 171%, 147%, and 171% greater than the average WT root length for PpGS1.1, PpGS1.3 and PpGS2, respectively (Fig. 3F).

N assimilation enzyme activities analysis of PpGSs tobacco transgenic plants under N treatment

Under LN stress, NR activity in WT decreased significantly by 69.4% compared to that under NN conditions. Transgenic lines PpGS1.1 and PpGS1.3 and PpGS2 showed reductions of 22.4%, 20.5% and 28.7%, respectively (Fig. 4A). NiR activity in WT plants decreased by 56.3% under LN compared to NN, whereas transgenic tobacco PpGS1.1, PpGS1.3 and PpGS2 lines exhibited more moderate declines of 23.7%, 25.7%, and 23.8%, respectively (Fig. 4B). GS activity dropped by 51.9% in WT plants when shifted from NN to LN condition. In contrast, transgenic tobacco PpGS1.1, PpGS1.3 and PpGS2 lines experienced significantly smaller reductions of 20.9%, 18.9%, and 22.2%, respectively (Fig. 4C). GOGAT activity declined by 30.5% in WT controls under LN relative to NN treatment. However, PpGS1.1, PpGS1.3 and PpGS2 overexpressing lines maintained higher activity, with decreases limited to 12.8%, 15.6%, and 12.1%, respectively (Fig. 4D). Collectively, the enzyme activity reductions triggered by LN stress were markedly more pronounced in WT plants than in transgenic tobacco lines.

Antioxidant metabolism analysis of PpGSs tobacco transgenic plants under N treatment

Under LN stress, O₂•⁻ content increased significantly by 54.8% in WT compared to NN conditions. Conversely, transgenic PpGS1.1, PpGS1.3 and PpGS2 lines exhibited large increases of 30.9%, 18.5%, and 27.1%, respectively, under the same stress regime (Fig. 5A). Transgenic counterparts maintained significantly higher activity, with decreases limited to 13.9%, 22.0%, and 15.0% in PpGS1.1, PpGS1.3 and PpGS2 lines, respectively (Fig. 5D).

Antioxidant metabolism analysis under TMV infection of PpGSs tobacco transgenic plants

In WT, O2•− content increased by 18.5% following pathogen infection. Infected PpGS1.1, PpGS1.3, and PpGS2 transgenic lines exhibited O2•− increases of 38.3%, 12.9%, and 52.3% over uninfected controls, respectively (Fig. 7A). Infected WT plants showed a 6.2% decrease in POD activity versus uninfected controls. Discussion

A better understanding of the GS genes family is crucial for elucidating genetic relationship, nitrogen metabolism, and disease management of Kentucky bluegrass. In the study, we identified the PpGSs gene family of Kentucky bluegrass and conducted bioinformatics analysis in comparison with other species. Phylogenetic tree analysis GS proteins from 42 plant species showed that P. pratensis is more closely related to perennial ryegrass. Subsequent conserved domain analysis revealed that PpGSs gene, like those in rice and Arabidopsis, possess both N-terminal and C-terminal domains. The finding indicates that these domains play a significant role in the structure and function of GS, which aligns with the results reported by Mondal et al. [7]. Furthermore, sequence alignment demonstrated high similarity among the GS protein sequences of rice, Arabidopsis, maize, and Kentucky bluegrass, including specific conserved residues. The residues are essential for determining the structural stability of GS isozymes and for facilitating effective biochemical reactions in the presence of various internal metabolites and inhibitory compounds. Using tobacco as a heterologous system circumvented limitations associated with Kentucky bluegrass transformation and gene redundancy. Transgenic tobacco lines overexpressing PpGSs recapitulated conserved roles in N assimilation and antioxidant regulation under LN stress, confirming functional orthology. Critically, the tobacco-TMV pathosystem provided a standardized platform to resolve the trade-off between GS-mediated N metabolism and disease susceptibility.

Glutamine synthetase (GS) plays a central role in trade-off between growth and defense [10]. The cytosolic isoform GS1 is encoded by multiple genes, exhibiting functional redundancy and specific division of labor [6, 38]. In this study, members of the PpGSs gene family exhibited differential expression patterns under different N contents and powdery mildew infection. Under LN stress, the expression of PpGS1.1 was significantly upregulated, whereas PpGS1.3 and PpGS2 showed slight downregulation. This suggests that PpGS1.1 may play a core role in N starvation response, like the function of GS1 isozymes in maize under LN conditions [39]. Overexpression of the GS1;3 and GS2 genes has been confirmed to enhance N and carbon metabolism, thereby increasing biomass in rice and duckweed, respectively [14, 40]. In the study, overexpressing PpGS1.1, PpGS1.3, and PpGS2 tobacco exhibited significantly higher biomass (fresh weight) and root length compared to WT, with the growth advantage being more pronounced under LN stress. The cytoplasmic PpGS1.3 gene likely plays a crucial role in the variations observed among species in response to LN supply and was found to have physicochemical properties like PpGS1.1 and a comparable protein tertiary structure to PpGS2 in Kentucky bluegrass [2]. Furthermore, the overexpression of PpGS1.1 in wild-type Kentucky bluegrass resulted in increased carbon and N content [3]. All these results directly confirm the positive function of PpGSs in enhancing plant N- use efficiency, indicating that an efficient N metabolism system is an important physiological basis for normal growth under LN conditions in Kentucky bluegrass.

To elucidate the physiological mechanisms underlying LN tolerance in transgenic plants, key indicators of N metabolism and antioxidant enzyme systems were analyzed. Under LN stress, WT plants exhibited significantly reduced GS, GOGAT, NR, and NiR activities, while transgenic lines showed slight declines in these enzymatic activities, indicating that heterologous PpGSs overexpression lines not only directly enhanced GS activity but also stabilized N metabolism under stress. This well aligns with established views on GS’s central role in maintaining N assimilation homeostasis [6, 7]. Furthermore, LN stress triggered ROS accumulation and oxidative damage. In our study, transgenic plants accumulated less O₂•⁻ under LN conditions while maintaining higher CAT, POD, and SOD activities. Similarly, using a tobacco heterologous expression system, the oat GS gene was found to enhance the activity of antioxidant-related genes (Cu/ZnSOD, MnSOD, GR1, and GAPC) in response to drought stress [23]. The results indicate that PpGSs overexpression may sustain efficient N assimilation, thereby mitigating ROS bursts induced by metabolic disorders, while simultaneously enhancing antioxidant defense capacity to alleviate oxidative damage [41]. This coordinated adaptive response mirrors previously reported mechanisms in millet (Setaria italica L.), where preserved GS activity alleviates LN-induced oxidative damage [41].

Concurrently, PpGSs also play complex roles in biotic stress responses. The study found that following powdery mildew infection, the expressions of all three PpGSs (PpGS1.1, PpGS1.3, PpGS2) genes were significantly downregulated, with PpGS1.3 showing the greatest decrease. Meanwhile, PpGS1.2 expression did not differ to the control under disease resistance conditions. The findings suggest that pathogen infection may redirect N resources from growth to defense by inhibiting GS expression. Previous studies have indicated that impaired GS function often leads to increased susceptibility to disease, whereas enhanced GS activity can improve plant disease resistance [19]. In this experiment, tobacco plants overexpressing PpGSs exhibited larger lesion areas and reduced levels of antioxidant enzymes following TMV infection. This suggests that enhanced N metabolism may provide a richer N source and energy for viral replication, thereby increasing host susceptibility. Similarly, overexpression of MaGS2 in tobacco reduced its resistance to C. shiraiana, whereas knockdown of MaGS2 in mulberry via VIGS technology increased resistance to C. shiraiana [20]. The observation was consistent with the finding in tomato, where GS2 overexpression promotes growth while simultaneously inhibiting disease resistance responses [10]. The study further systematically deciphered the dual regulatory network of PpGSs under disease stress, thereby expanding the dimensions of GS functional research.

Collectively, the study systematically resolves the dual regulatory network of Kentucky bluegrass GS genes (PpGSs), demonstrating their essential role in enhancing N- use efficiency under abiotic stress while simultaneously acting as negative regulators of biotic resistance. GS activity stabilizes the GS/GOGAT cycle, reducing metabolic disruption under LN stress and minimizing ROS bursts. Conversely, during infection, N metabolites potentially fuel pathogen growth while dampening defense-related ROS signaling (Fig. 8). The trade-off between growth and defense underscores the need for context-specific manipulation of GS isoforms in turfgrass breeding programs.
