Age-Specific Associations Between Adiposity and Mortality in U.S. Adults, 1999-2018
Authors: Yuan Lu, Yunhan Mou, Yuntian Liu, Alissa S. Chen, Jeph Herrin, Erica Spatz, Mona Sharifi, Ania M. Jastreboff, Harlan M. Krumholz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundObesity is a leading risk factor for increased mortality. However, it remains unclear how this relationship varies across different age groups.ObjectivesThe purpose of this study was to evaluate whether the associations between adiposity and mortality are modified by age in a nationally representative sample of U.S. adults.MethodsWe analyzed data from 44,041 U.S. adults aged 18 to 79 years from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (1999-2018), linked to mortality data through 2019. Anthropometric measures included body mass index, waist circumference, weight, and waist-to-height ratio. Cox proportional hazards models were used to assess the interaction between age and adiposity on all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, adjusting for demographic, behavioral, and clinical covariates.ResultsOver a median follow-up of 10.1 years, there were 5,019 deaths (1,186 cardiovascular). Significant interactions between age and all 4 adiposity measures were observed (P < 0.05). Associations between adiposity and mortality were strongest among younger adults. For example, each 1-SD increase in body mass index was associated with a cardiovascular mortality HR of 1.49 (95% CI: 1.27-1.77) in adults aged 18 to 49 years, vs 1.15 (95% CI: 0.99-1.32) in those aged 70 to 79 years. Class 3 obesity was associated with a cardiovascular mortality HR of 4.37 (95% CI: 2.01-9.50) in younger adults. Underweight status was also associated with elevated all-cause mortality, particularly among younger individuals (HR: 2.04; 95% CI: 1.24-3.36).ConclusionsAge significantly modifies the relationship between adiposity and mortality. Younger adults experience greater mortality risks from both obesity and underweight, underscoring the need for early, age-tailored interventions.

Obesity has emerged as an urgent public health concern in the United States, affecting over 40% of adults and significantly contributing to the rising burden of cardiovascular disease and premature mortality.1,2 Extensive evidence highlights obesity as a major preventable cause of death, yet the extent to which age modifies the obesity-mortality relationship remains poorly understood.3, 4, 5, 6, 7 Biological changes throughout the lifespan—including alterations in body composition, metabolic shifts, and the accumulation of age-related comorbidities—may influence how both extremes of body weight impact mortality risk.8, 9, 10 Moreover, the interplay between obesity and metabolic disorders is likely to differ across various life stages, further underscoring the importance of quantifying age-specific risks associated with body weight extremes.11 Given the increasing prevalence of obesity and the emergence of effective weight management therapies, understanding age-related differences in the impact of adiposity on mortality is crucial for targeting public health interventions toward those most at risk.

Despite substantial evidence linking adiposity to mortality, significant gaps persist in our understanding of how this association varies by age. Previous research has primarily focused on body mass index (BMI), often overlooking other critical anthropometric measures such as waist circumference (WC), weight, and waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), which more accurately reflect central adiposity and cardiometabolic risk.12,13 Furthermore, many studies have concentrated on specific demographic subgroups, such as postmenopausal women or non-Hispanic White populations, limiting their generalizability to broader, more diverse populations.3,5 The inconsistent inclusion of various age groups, especially younger adults, further constrains insights into how obesity severity influences mortality risk across the lifespan.

To address these gaps, the current study comprehensively examined how age modifies the associations between multiple anthropometric indicators of adiposity—including BMI, WC, weight, and WHtR—and all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in a nationally representative sample of U.S. adults. Specifically, we investigate whether mortality risks associated with obesity vary by age group and severity, with emphasis on elucidating these relationships among younger adults. Leveraging nearly 2 decades of nationally representative data, this study aims to offer nuanced insights into age-related differences in obesity’s impact on mortality, ultimately informing targeted public health strategies and clinical interventions tailored to different age populations.

Methods

Study design and population

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 1999-2018 and the Linked Mortality Files released in 2019. NHANES is a series of cross-sectional surveys designed to provide nationally representative estimates of the noninstitutionalized U.S. population.14 For this analysis, we included data from 8 NHANES cycles spanning 1999 to 2000 through 2017 to 2018, with follow-up mortality data linked to the National Death Index (NDI) via Linked Mortality Files.

NHANES collects detailed information on participants' health and lifestyle through in-home interviews and standardized physical examinations conducted in mobile examination centers. Laboratory tests using blood and urine specimens are also obtained from a random subsample during the physical examination. On average, the time between the interview and the physical examination was approximately 2 weeks.

The Linked Mortality Files, provided by the National Center for Health Statistics, links NHANES survey data with NDI death records at the individual level, enabling the assessment of long-term mortality outcomes. The latest Linked Mortality Files release includes mortality data through 2019. By combining baseline NHANES data with follow-up mortality records, we constructed a longitudinal cohort to examine the interaction between age and anthropometric measures of adiposity (BMI, WC, weight, and WHtR) on mortality outcomes among U.S. adults.

From an initial sample of 54,947 adults aged 18 to 79 years across the selected NHANES cycles, we applied several exclusions. Individuals identifying as Asian, Alaskan Native, American Indian, or “other” race were excluded due to limited sample sizes. We also excluded pregnant women and participants with missing data for any of the anthropometric measures (BMI, WC, weight, or WHtR). After these exclusions, the final analytic cohort consisted of 44,041 individuals (Supplemental Figure 1).

Anthropometric measurements, age subgroups, and mortality

In the NHANES study, trained staff conducted standardized physical examinations to measure participants' weight, height, and WC. For this analysis, we utilized 4 anthropometric measures: BMI (kg/m2), WC (cm), weight (kg), and WHtR. Body fat composition was not included in the analysis because it was measured only for adults aged 49 years and under in NHANES.

BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared, while WHtR was calculated as WC divided by height. Per Centers for Disease Control and Prevention recommendations, BMI was categorized into 6 groups: underweight (BMI <18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (BMI 18.5-24.9 kg/m2), overweight (BMI 25.0-29.9 kg/m2), class 1 obesity (BMI 30.0-34.9 kg/m2), class 2 obesity (BMI 35.0-39.9 kg/m2), and class 3 obesity (BMI ≥40.0 kg/m2).15

To assess the impact of anthropometric measures on mortality risk across different age groups, we divided participants into 3 categories: younger adults (18-49 years), middle-aged adults (50-69 years), and older adults (70-79 years). Participants aged 80 years and older were excluded due to the high prevalence of mortality confounders, such as comorbidities, and their small sample size, which represented only about 4% of the cohort and could reduce statistical robustness and subgroup representativeness. All-cause mortality and cause-specific mortality were classified according to the International Classification of Diseases-10th Revision (ICD-10). Cardiovascular mortality was defined as any death caused by heart disease.

Covariates

We included multiple covariates in the analysis to account for potential confounding factors. These covariates were selected based on previous literature and clinical relevance and included sex (male or female), race and ethnicity (non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, or Hispanic), education level (less than high school, high school diploma, some college, or Bachelor's degree or higher), family income (categorized as high/middle income [≥200%] or low income [<200%] relative to the federal poverty limit, as defined by the Census Bureau), insurance status (insured or uninsured), smoking status (current, former, or never smoker), physical activity level (inactive, insufficient, or recommended), and self-reported history of cancer, myocardial infarction, and stroke. We included race and ethnicity as covariates to account for structural and systemic factors, such as racism and socioeconomic inequities, that contribute to differences in health outcomes and influence obesity prevalence, treatment access, and mortality risks.16

Consistent with previous studies,17,18 physical activity levels were categorized according to established guidelines19,20 as inactive (fewer than 10 minutes of moderate or vigorous physical activity per week), insufficient (10-149 minutes per week of moderate physical activity or 10-74 minutes per week of vigorous physical activity), and recommended (150 minutes or more per week of moderate physical activity or 75 minutes or more per week of vigorous physical activity). Diet quality, sleep, and mental health were not included in the model due to inconsistent measurement or missing data across NHANES cycles. Detailed definitions of all covariates are provided in Supplemental Table 1.

Statistical analysis

Our study adhered to established methods for analyzing structured survey data in accordance with NHANES guidelines.21 This included incorporating survey weights and strata to ensure nationally representative estimates. To evaluate the interaction between age and mortality, we conducted Cox proportional hazards regression analyses using 4 anthropometric measures—BMI, WC, weight, and WHtR—in relation to both all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. For example, in assessing the relationship between BMI (as a continuous variable) and all-cause mortality, our model included BMI, age (both continuous), an interaction term (BMI∗age), and other covariates in an additive form. The survival outcome was defined as the time to either all-cause or cardiovascular death. Given the high correlations among BMI, WC, weight, and WHtR, we modeled each anthropometric measure separately to avoid collinearity (Supplemental Table 2).

To further explore the association between anthropometric measures and mortality across age groups, we conducted subgroup analyses within the 3 defined age categories: younger (18-49 years), middle-aged (50-69 years), and older (70-79 years) adults. For instance, in analyzing the younger age group for BMI and all-cause mortality, we adjusted for age and included BMI and other covariates in the model. As our analysis focused on 3 distinct age groups, age was consistently included as a covariate in these subgroup analyses. We compared the coefficients and 95% CIs of the BMI term across age groups and presented these results using forest plots.

To better visualize the association between anthropometric measures and mortality across age subgroups, we applied a restricted quadratic spline approach within the Cox proportional hazards models. This method allowed for a clearer depiction of the nonlinear relationships between anthropometric measures and mortality. Additionally, we examined the varying impacts of underweight, overweight, and obesity on mortality by performing subgroup analyses within each age group, evaluating associations between the 6 BMI categories (underweight, normal weight, overweight, and class 1-3 obesity) and both all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, using normal weight as the reference.

We considered 2-sided P values <0.05 as statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R (version 4.2.2). This study was exempt from Institutional Review Board approval at Yale University, as NHANES data are publicly available and deidentified. The study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.22

Results

Among the 44,041 individuals included in our analytic sample, there were 5,019 deaths observed, of which 1,186 were attributed to cardiovascular causes. The median follow-up period was 121 months (IQR: 115). The weighted sample (N = 188,940,046) had a mean age of 44.75 years (SD: 16.16), and 50.5% (95% CI: 50.06 to 51.01) were female. Black individuals represented 12.21% (95% CI: 10.98-13.44), Hispanic/Latino individuals represented 15.42% (95% CI: 13.80-17.03), and White individuals represented 72.38% (95% CI: 70.34-74.41) of the sample. Compared with older adults (aged 70-79 years), younger (aged 18-49 years) and middle-aged (aged 50-69 years) adults had higher education levels. The middle-aged group also reported the highest family income, with 68.35% classified in the “high-/middle-income” category. Regarding physical activity, the younger age group was most active, with 45.71% classified as “active.” Notably, the older adult group had the lowest proportion of current smokers, comprising only 10.53%. Age differences in associations between anthropometric measurements and mortality

As shown in Table 2, significant interactions between age and all 4 anthropometric measurements were observed for all-cause mortality (P < 0.05). The Central Illustration showed the associations between anthropometric measures and mortality across different age groups. The strength of the association between each measure and mortality diminished with increasing age. For instance, compared with their nonobese counterparts, younger individuals exhibited a significantly higher HR for cardiovascular mortality per one SD increase in BMI (ages 18-49 years: HR: 1.49; 95% CI: 1.27-1.77; ages 50-69 years: HR: 1.32; 95% CI: 1.15-1.51) than older individuals (ages 70-79 years: HR: 1.15; 95% CI: 0.99-1.32), after adjusting for sex, race and ethnicity, education, income, health insurance, physical activity, smoking status, and history of cancer, myocardial infarction, and stroke. Similar trends were observed with WC, where younger individuals (ages 18-49 years: HR: 1.67; 95% CI: 1.41-1.99; ages 50-69 years: HR: 1.34; 95% CI: 1.15-1.55) had higher HRs for cardiovascular mortality per SD increase compared to older individuals (ages 70-79 years: HR: 1.23; 95% CI: 1.08-1.41). For WHtR, the HR was similarly higher among younger (ages 18-49 years: HR: 1.69; 95% CI: 1.40-2.03; ages 50-69 years: HR: 1.38; 95% CI: 1.19-1.61) than older individuals (ages 70-79 years: HR: 1.24; 95% CI: 1.10-1.41). Restricted cubic spline models were used to evaluate nonlinear relationships between body mass index (BMI), waist circumference (WC), weight, and waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) with all-cause and cardiovascular (CV) mortality, stratified by age groups (18-49, 50-69, and 70-79 years). A U-shaped pattern was observed, with higher mortality risk at both low and high levels of adiposity. Discussion

In this nationally representative sample, we identified significant interactions between age and adiposity concerning mortality risk. Younger individuals consistently exhibited higher mortality risks associated with multiple anthropometric measures of adiposity compared to their older counterparts. Notably, the increased cardiovascular mortality risk in younger adults was particularly pronounced among those with severe obesity. Interestingly, we also observed elevated all-cause mortality among younger adults classified as underweight, with HRs comparable to those associated with severe obesity. These findings suggest that both extremes of adiposity—underweight and severe obesity—are associated with increased health risks, albeit through potentially distinct mechanisms.

Several biological and social factors may explain these age-related differences in adiposity-associated mortality. Younger individuals with severe obesity likely experience a greater cumulative metabolic burden due to prolonged exposure to dyslipidemia, insulin resistance, hypertension, and systemic inflammation, directly elevating their cardiovascular risk.8,23 Conversely, the heightened mortality risk observed among younger adults who were underweight is somewhat unexpected, given that underweight status is more traditionally considered a risk factor in older populations.24 In younger individuals, being underweight may signal underlying chronic illnesses, nutritional deficiencies, socioeconomic hardships, or mental health issues, resulting in increased susceptibility primarily through noncardiovascular mechanisms, such as infections, malignancies, or frailty-related conditions. Recognizing these distinct pathways underscores the importance of adopting age-specific public health strategies to address both extremes of adiposity.

Our study builds on and extends prior literature in several key ways. Earlier research, often limited to BMI or to specific demographic subgroups,3,5,12,13 has consistently shown U-shaped associations between BMI and mortality, including large cohorts such as the UK Clinical Practice Research Datalink and the Canadian National Population Health Survey.25,26 Our findings confirm this pattern in the U.S. population while advancing the evidence base in 3 important respects. First, by analyzing multiple anthropometric measures—BMI, WC, WHtR, and weight—in a large, nationally representative sample, we provide a more comprehensive assessment of adiposity-related mortality risks across the life course. This approach is particularly relevant given recent evidence that BMI ≥30 is a valid marker of excess adiposity among adults younger than 60 years, but less reliable in older adults, for whom complementary measures such as WC remain essential.27,28 Incorporating additional anthropometric metrics therefore enables a more nuanced evaluation of age-specific risks. Second, by explicitly examining age interactions, we demonstrate substantial heterogeneity in adiposity–mortality associations, with particularly elevated risks among younger adults with severe obesity. Third, we identify significant associations between underweight status and all-cause mortality, especially in younger adults—an often overlooked finding in obesity-focused research that underscores the need for greater attention to undernutrition and its long-term health consequences.

Our findings have critical public health and clinical implications. The observed association between obesity and increased mortality risk in younger adults suggests additional attention to early and proactive interventions. Rising obesity rates in this demographic have long-term consequences,29,30 yet younger adults face barriers such as limited health care access and lower engagement with preventive services. Studies showed that preventive health visits in adults aged 35 to 44 years have declined, with weight-loss counseling dropping from 25% in 2016 to 16% in 2019, while rates remained stable in older adults.31,32 Young adults, particularly those aged 19 to 25 years, have the highest uninsured rates among all age groups and often prefer episodic care over primary care continuity.33 For instance, only 52% of Hispanic young adults reported having a primary care provider, compared to 94% of older adults.34 Addressing these gaps requires expanded insurance coverage, improved primary care access, digital health solutions, and culturally competent care to enhance engagement and reduce disparities in young adults.

Similarly, the elevated mortality risk among younger individuals who are underweight suggests the need for tailored strategies to address nutritional deficiencies, mental health disorders, or chronic diseases that may contribute to underweight status.35 Clinicians may incorporate assessments for undernutrition or underlying medical conditions in younger individuals presenting with low BMI, alongside strategies to manage obesity. Comprehensive care models that address both extremes of adiposity are essential.

While obesity was associated with increased cardiovascular mortality risk across all age groups, the magnitude of risk notably declined with advancing age. This attenuation in older adults may reflect survivorship bias, whereby older individuals with obesity who survive into later life may have protective characteristics—such as favorable genetics, healthier lifestyles, or better health care access—that mitigate cardiovascular risks. Additionally, shorter obesity duration or competing causes of mortality in older age could further reduce observed associations. Although our study found no significant increase in all-cause mortality related to adiposity among adults aged 70 to 79 years, we caution against interpreting this as evidence of a protective effect of obesity. Recent clinical trials, such as SELECT (Semaglutide Effects on Cardiovascular Outcomes in People With Overweight or Obesity; mean age of 60 years)36 and STEP-HFpEF (Semaglutide Treatment Effect in People With Obesity and Heart Failure With Preserved Ejection Fraction; mean age of 69 years),37 indicate that obesity treatment provides consistent benefits across older age groups, underscoring the need for continued research into optimal weight management strategies for older adults.

Study Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, its observational design precludes causal inference. Additionally, important covariates such as diet quality, sleep patterns, mental health, and medication use were not included due to inconsistent measurement across NHANES cycles. The absence of these factors may contribute to residual confounding or mediation, and results should be interpreted with this in mind. Furthermore, our demographic scope primarily includes non-Hispanic White, Black, and Hispanic populations, potentially missing important insights from other racial and ethnic groups. Another limitation concerns the accuracy of cause-of-death coding in the NDI, which has known issues of misclassification, potentially affecting cardiovascular mortality estimates.38 Our interaction analyses were conducted using multiplicative terms within Cox proportional hazards models, which assume proportional hazards. As a result, these tests may be insensitive to nonproportional hazards across age groups. Fifth, our age-stratified analyses may be subject to survivorship bias, as participants must live to enter older age groups, which could attenuate associations in later life. Finally, NHANES does not capture longitudinal adiposity trajectories; thus, our findings reflect average associations across individuals with varying durations of obesity rather than the effects of specific exposure histories. This limitation may introduce misclassification bias, particularly among younger adults whose weight patterns are more likely to change over time.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrates significant age-related differences in the associations of both obesity and underweight with mortality, with the highest risks observed among younger adults, particularly those with severe obesity. These findings, coupled with the persistently low rates of obesity treatment in the United States, highlight the importance of prioritizing early, proactive interventions to reduce long-term morbidity and mortality.
