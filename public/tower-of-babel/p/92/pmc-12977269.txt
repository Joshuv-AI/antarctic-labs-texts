Safety, immunogenicity, and effectiveness of the MAV/06 varicella vaccine: A comprehensive review of a new strain vaccine
Authors: Younchul Shin, Hyunji Ryu, Gwanglok Kim, Shinha Yang, Jaewoo Lee, BongKyoo Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMost of licensed varicella vaccines are based on the Oka strain, while MAV/06 strain vaccines were exclusively developed by GC Biopharma Corp. in South Korea. This paper aims to summarize and present more than 30 y of accumulated evidence from about 40 unpublished and published studies on safety, immunogenicity, and effectiveness of MAV/06 vaccines. The safety of MAV/06 vaccines has been confirmed through various clinical trials and post-marketing studies. Outstanding humoral and cell-mediated immunogenicity of the MAV/06 vaccines has been demonstrated in diverse clinical trials and real-world studies. For example, a seroconversion rate of the MAV/06 vaccine (BARYCELA inj.) was close to 100%. Moreover, several after-approval epidemiological studies in Korean and Filipino children have supported the effectiveness of MAV/06 vaccines, although it remains to be further confirmed in future studies. In terms of safety, immunogenicity, and effectiveness, MAV/06 strain varicella vaccines are comparable to Oka strain vaccines.

Introduction

Varicella-Zoster virus (VZV) is a type of herpesvirus with high infectivity, typically causing chickenpox in childhood and generally shingles in adulthood.1–6 VZV belongs to the Herpesviridae family and is a double-stranded DNA virus, with only one serotype but divided into five major genotypes (clades 1 to 5). Clades 1 and 3 are usually found in Europe and America, clade 2 in Asia, clades 4, 5 in Africa.4,5,7–9

The virus spreads through direct contact between individuals or inhalation of aerosol droplets from respiratory secretions of chickenpox patients, entering host cells via the upper respiratory tract or conjunctiva. After primary infection with VZV, the virus remains dormant in sensory ganglia and can later trigger shingles.4–6,10

VZV is globally distributed, and in countries without national immunization programs (NIP), most individuals become infected by adolescence, with the highest incidence observed in preschool or early elementary school children. Chickenpox also exhibits a strong seasonal pattern in temperate and some tropical climates, with the highest incidence occurring between winter and spring. Outbreaks can occur periodically in schools or daycare centers.4–6,11–13 Globally, chickenpox affects over 140 million individuals annually, resulting in serious complications requiring hospitalization in over 4 million cases and causing over 4000 deaths.5,6,14 Chickenpox significantly contributes to the burden of disease from secondary infections (e.g., group A streptococcal infections) among children.15 Most hospitalizations due to chickenpox occur in children, resulting in significant absenteeism from school and work for parents caring for sick children. Therefore, some countries have implemented vaccination programs during childhood to control chickenpox.5

The United States was the first country to introduce the one-dose varicella vaccine into its NIP program in 1995. Since then, several countries have been introducing the varicella vaccine as a universal vaccination program, with its effectiveness being demonstrated through various studies. In the United States, a two-dose program was introduced in 2006. After the introduction of the varicella vaccine in 1995, it became evident that one dose was insufficient to fully prevent chickenpox. Therefore, to further enhance immunity against chickenpox, maintain long-term immunity, reduce breakthrough infections, and prevent severe cases, the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP) recommended a 2-dose varicella vaccine schedule in 2006, which was subsequently integrated into the NIP. During 25 y after the introduction of the varicella NIP in the United States, the incidence of chickenpox has decreased by over 97% in all age groups, and particularly 99% in individuals under 20 y of age.5,16–18 In most countries where the varicella vaccine has been introduced into national vaccination programs, incidence and hospitalization rates have decreased by over 80%, indicating that vaccination is an effective method for preventing chickenpox. Therefore, the introduction of a varicella NIP has been recommended from a public health and economic perspective.5,14,19,20

However, there are still many countries that have not introduced the varicella vaccine as a NIP.16,19–28 In particular, most low-income countries in Africa lack a varicella NIP.16,19,29 And within low- and middle-income countries, generally, vaccination coverage is lower in the children with low socioeconomic background (household wealth and mother’s education level).30 Cost-effective varicella vaccines are an essential prerequisite for increasing vaccine coverage and reducing vaccination inequality particularly among the children in low- and middle-income countries. The cost-effectiveness of a vaccine is a crucial decision-making point for governments, especially those of low- or middle-income countries, to introduce the varicella vaccine into their NIPs and expand vaccination coverage.

The currently licensed vaccines for preventing chickenpox are the live attenuated vaccines, which include the Oka strain and the MAV/06 strain. Although both strains belong to clade 2 and are genetically very similar,31–34 the majority of varicella vaccines used worldwide are derived from the wild-type pOka (parental Oka, a wild-type clinical isolate of the VZV) virus. Oka strain varicella vaccines are produced by Biken, MSD, GSK, and other manufacturers in China, among others.5,16,35,36 On the other hand, varicella vaccines derived from MAV/06 strain were exclusively developed by GC Biopharma Corp. in South Korea.37,38 Among these, varicella vaccines from MSD, SK Bioscience, Sinovac, and GC Biopharma corp. are prequalified by the World Health Organization (WHO). Although more than 30 million doses of MAV/06 vaccines have been distributed internationally, there is no comprehensive review on safety, immunogenicity, and effectiveness of MAV/06 vaccines in the literature. In addition, despite some published papers,37,38,40–43 significant pieces of information on safety, immunogenicity, and effectiveness of MAV/06 vaccines have remained unpublished as internal documents of GC Biopharma Corp. As a result, the strengths and weaknesses of MAV/06 strain vaccines, a potential cost-effective alternative to Oka strain vaccines, have not been fully discussed and understood among global vaccine stakeholders.

The objective of this study is to provide a comprehensive review of the characteristics, safety, immunogenicity, and effectiveness of the MAV/06 varicella vaccine developed by GC Biopharma Corp., and qualified by the WHO for routine childhood immunization in many countries.

Methods

We conducted an extensive literature review of all peer-reviewed published articles during 1991–2023 about MAV/06 vaccines regarding safety, immunogenicity, and effectiveness. In addition, in our review, we included ten unpublished, but important and latest official documents of MAV/06 vaccines, issued by or submitted to regulatory authorities.

Attenuation of MAV/06 strain

To attenuate the obtained varicella viruses, several passages were carried out in normal human embryonic lung diploid cells (LuMa, normal human embryonic Lung diploid MogAm) and normal guinea pig lung diploid cells (GEL) through multiple cultivation steps (Table 3). To confirm the degree of attenuation, temperature sensitivity tests and cell susceptibility tests, which were previously used in the attenuation of the Oka virus strain44 were employed. Among the varicella viruses tested, the sixth virus (designated MAV/06 virus) exhibited the highest cell susceptibility and the lowest temperature sensitivity. The lowest virus infectivity ratio at 39°C/34°C since it best adapts to the temperature condition of 34°C and GEL cells, and at the same time, has the highest virus infectivity ratio in GEL/LuMA cells. When the MAV/06 was subcultured, the temperature sensitivity shown from p45 to p60 was 0.05 to 0.07, and this was at a level similar to the temperature sensitivity result of 0.03 of the Oka vaccine strain. In addition, the cellular susceptibility of the MAV/06 virus strain from p45 to p60 was 0.80 to 0.83, and this could be confirmed via references to be at a level similar to the result of 0.82 of the Oka vaccine strain. Subsequent sequencing studies of varicella vaccines revealed 42 known single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) occurring during the attenuation process from wild type to attenuated varicella viruses.31,33,45,46 In particular, open reading frame (ORF) 0 and ORF 62 of VZV genome have been reported to be associated with the vaccine attenuation.33,35,47 Read-through mutation at stop codon in ORF 0 is one of the attenuation genotypes of vaccine VZVs, which might be an important feature distinguishing vaccine strains from pOka strains.47 Also, during attenuation, genetic variants accumulated in ORF 62 which is essential for viral replication and spreading, are important in determining the level of attenuation of VZV vaccines.33 To confirm whether the MAV/06 vaccine was well attenuated, sequencing comparison studies were conducted with Oka-derived varicella vaccines. The results showed that levels of nucleotide sequence variations at the 42 SNP positions were similar between the MAV/06 vaccine and vOka-derived varicella vaccines such as VARILRIX and VARIVAX. For example, the nucleotide sequences of MAV/06 were the same as those of VARILRIX at 38 out of 42 (90.5%) SNP positions and those of VARIVAX at 30 out of 42 (71.4%) SNP positions. In addition, MAV/06 shared the same nucleotide sequences with VARILRIX and VARIVAX at all of the three SNP positions (560, ORF0; 106262, ORF62; and 107252, ORF62) known to be strongly associated with the vaccine attenuation.35 Based on these findings, it was confirmed that the MAV/06 vaccine was well attenuated.31,47

Product information on MAV/06 strain vaccines

There are two varicella vaccines that are derived from the MAV/06 strain: BARYCELA inj. and Varicella Vaccine-GCC inj. (Suduvax).

Varicella Vaccine-GCC inj. was developed by GC Biopharma Corp. using MAV/06 strain and LuMA cell line, which became the world’s second licensed varicella vaccine in 1993.34,47,48

The second generations MAV/06 varicella vaccine, BARYCELA inj., was developed by GC Biopharma Corp. using MAV/06 strain and MRC-5 cell line and licensed in 2020.49 BARYCELA inj. features an enhanced manufacturing process and greater stability compared to Varicella Vaccine-GCC inj. Antibiotic additives are generally used to prevent contamination of cells during a manufacturing process of vaccines. There are possibilities that these antibiotics may cause ADRs such as anaphylaxis in some people after administration.50 BARYCELA inj. is the world’s first and only antibiotic-free varicella vaccine that eliminates the risk of adverse reactions and assures improved safety.51–55 Varicella Vaccine-GCC inj. Immunogenicity

The gold standard test for measuring VZV antibodies is the Fluorescent Antibody to Membrane Antigen (FAMA) assay, developed in the 1970s. Additionally, the glycoprotein enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (gpELISA) is commonly used. Generally, an FAMA antibody titer of 1:4 or higher is considered seropositive, indicating protection against varicella.5,76,77

Effectiveness

After conducting clinical trial on immunogenicity and safety of MAV/06 vaccine in the Philippines in 2001, a follow-up effectiveness study was conducted for 5 y. Out of 299 healthy children enrolled in the previous clinical trial, 163 were able to be observed up to 5 y after the clinical trial. Based on the reviews of the follow-up post-vaccination clinical history and physical examination, none of them followed-up had experienced any significant skin lesions, varicella-like manifestations, or evidence of clinical varicella during the 5 y of post-vaccination, indicating 100% protection over the follow-up period.78

In addition, two separate groups of Korean researchers investigated changes in the varicella epidemiology in Korean populations before and after the introduction of a one-dose universal varicella vaccination program in 2005. They reported that varicella incidence significantly decreased by 60–67%, and the peak age of varicella occurrence also shifted toward older ages.85–87 Furthermore, according to a recent study,88 the incidence rate of complicated varicella cases among Korean children significantly (96%) decreased from 137 per 100,000 persons in 2010 to 6 per 100,000 persons in 2021. Considering that MAV/06 vaccine was predominantly distributed in Korea during this period, all of the above results indirectly support the effectiveness of the MAV/06 vaccine in Korean populations.

In a retrospective birth cohort study in 2011 in Korea where the vaccine coverage was higher than 95%, the incidence rate of break-through varicella cases in the cohort that received a single dose of MAV/06 vaccine was 27.1 per 1000 person-years during a follow-up period of 6 y.89,90 This rate was similar to the results of other studies conducted in the United States using the vOka-derived vaccine, which reported a range of 21.7–28.3 cases per 1000 person-years.91–94

GC Biopharma Corp. is currently conducting an effectiveness study with a follow-up period of 6 y in 1000 Korean children who received the BARYCELA vaccine.61 In addition, it is also conducting a randomized clinical trial on the first and second doses of the BARYCELA vaccine in healthy Thai and Vietnamese children with a follow-up period of 10 y in 474 healthy children (ClinicalTrials.gov ID; NCT07054099). We expect that the on-going studies will provide another good evidence of the effectiveness of the MAV/06 vaccines.

Conclusion

In this study, we conducted a comprehensive review of more than 30 y of accumulated evidence from unpublished and published studies on safety, immunogenicity, and effectiveness of MAV/06 strain vaccines. Our review indicates that in terms of safety, immunogenicity, and effectiveness, MAV/06 varicella vaccines are comparable to vOka strain varicella vaccines, while MAV/06 vaccines have been more cost-effective for low- and middle-income countries than vOka vaccines.

Through various clinical trials and post-marketing studies, including ones on strain-interchangeability, the safety of MAV/06 vaccines, has been confirmed. MAV/06 vaccines appeared to be safer than Oka varicella vaccines in the post-marketing comparison of adverse events (Table 9). However, it should be cautiously interpreted due to differences in pharmacovigilance reporting systems and reporting behaviors of healthcare professionals and consumers between countries. On the other hand, to the best of our knowledge, the recently developed BARYCELA inj. is the first antibiotic-free varicella vaccine in the world, eliminating any safety concerns associated with antibiotics included in varicella vaccines. Outstanding humoral (utilizing the FAMA assay and cross-reactivity test) and cell-mediated immunogenicity of the MAV/06 vaccines has been demonstrated in diverse clinical trials and real-world studies. Also, excellent cross-reactive immunity of MAV/06 vaccine against diverse wild-type varicella viruses (clades 1, 2, 3, and 5) has been demonstrated, based on high and similar FAMA antibody titers across the different clades. Moreover, several post-approval epidemiological studies in Korean and Filipino children have supported the effectiveness of MAV/06 vaccines.

There are a few limitations in this study. First, the evidence of effectiveness of MAV/06 vaccines summarized in this article has been based on observational cohort studies. There is yet no evidence for the effectiveness of MAV/06 vaccines from randomized clinical trials. We expect that the currently on-going (ClinicalTrials.gov ID; NCT07054099) and future trials will fill the knowledge gap. Second, 10 unpublished official documents as well as peer-reviewed published articles about MAV/06 vaccines were included in our review. Out of the ten unpublished documents, two were latest PBRER reports,48,61 three were market authorization documents,58–60 and five were reports of clinical studies.65,66,68,71,78 We think that the unpublished official documents, which carry minimal risk of bias, provided additional valuable information on the characteristics, safety, and immunogenicity of MAV/06 vaccines. All of the unpublished official documents will be available on request to the authors. Third, in this study, we reviewed and discussed the evidence of safety, immunogenicity, and effectiveness of one-dose MAV/06 varicella vaccination. However, such evidence for two-dose MAV/06 varicella vaccination remain to be tested and confirmed in future clinical studies.

MAV/06 varicella vaccines, which are developed by GC Biopharma Corp., have been distributed worldwide with more than 30 million doses over the past 30 y, ensuring stable vaccine supply to numerous countries. Varicella vaccine administration is the most effective method for preventing chickenpox. Economic barriers can increase inequality in vaccination, which highlights the importance of distributing cost-effective varicella vaccines. We expect that MAV/06 vaccines as cost-effective varicella vaccines make a significant contribution to improving global public health and health equality in children.
