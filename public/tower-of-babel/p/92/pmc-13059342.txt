Implementation of optimized kangaroo care for infection prevention and control in neonatal intensive care units (NeoIPC): Protocol for the implementation elements of a multicenter parallel cluster randomized hybrid type 2 implementation-effectiveness study

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHospital-acquired neonatal severe infections and resistant bacterial colonization in neonatal care are a world-wide challenge. Preventing the spread of resistant bacteria in neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) is specifically challenging, for example, due to multi-patient rooms, spaces being crowded with equipment, and high antibiotic use in these settings. Kangaroo care (KC), a practice that involves skin-to-skin contact between newborn infants and caregivers, is a promising, low-cost intervention that has been associated with reduced morbidity and mortality in low-birthweight infants. Despite these and other health benefits, KC has not been implemented systematically or consistently in NICUs following current WHO guidelines. The NeoIPC project aims to optimize KC practices in NICUs and to determine the effect on severe neonatal infections and resistant bacterial colonization among high-risk infants.MethodsWithin the NeoIPC project, NeoDeco is a multi-center, parallel-group, cluster-randomized type 2 hybrid effectiveness-implementation study with 24 NICUs from five European countries representing clusters. NeoImplement, comprising the implementation elements of NeoDeco, focuses on (1) providing implementation support to sites in the intervention arm of the study and (2) evaluating the implementation of optimized KC in intervention sites. Implementation support consists of core implementation strategies that are offered to all intervention sites as well as the co-design of tailored implementation strategies for each individual site. Innovative methods supporting this co-design process are presented in this protocol. The implementation evaluation comprises a mixed-methods longitudinal study evaluating barriers and facilitators and various implementation outcomes, including a comprehensive economic evaluation.DiscussionNeoImplement focuses on implementing optimized KC in participating NICUs by offering and co-developing strategies that can be sustained beyond the duration of the study. The accompanying implementation evaluation will provide insights into the effectiveness, feasibility, acceptability, sustainability, and cost-effectiveness of strategies targeting the implementation of optimized KC in European NICUs. Background

Approximately 6–10% of liveborn infants in high-income countries require admission to neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) [1, 2]. These infants, particularly preterm newborns, are highly vulnerable to hospital-acquired infections due to prolonged admission, invasive procedures, their immature immune systems and skin barriers, and devices such as central venous catheters or endotracheal tubes [3–6]. Hospital-acquired neonatal sepsis contributes substantially to mortality, morbidity, and long-term neurodevelopmental impairment [7–12]. Because signs of infection are non-specific, antibiotic use is often high, and rising bacterial resistance further complicates the management of neonatal sepsis [13, 14]. As bacterial colonization frequently precedes infection, its prevention is critical but challenged, for example, by multi-patient rooms, limited space around cots, and staff caring for several infants simultaneously [15].

Kangaroo Care (KC), as defined by the WHO, encompasses skin-to-skin contact (StSC) between infants and caregivers, promotion of exclusive breastfeeding, early discharge, and close follow-up [16]. Recommended even for unstable infants, KC has been shown to reduce neonatal mortality and sepsis [16–18]. Although evidence is limited, KC may also facilitate decolonization [19], likely directly through replacement of resistant flora with maternal non-pathogenic flora, and indirectly, by promoting breastfeeding. KC is therefore a promising, low-cost infection prevention and control (IPC) strategy in NICUs [17, 18, 20].

KC is widely practiced in Europe but inconsistently implemented in terms of duration, eligibility, and caregiver guidance [21]. Also, implementation approaches of KC vary across different hospital settings [22], for example, depending on whether the practice is embedded in a general family-centered care approach or whether it is driven by individual healthcare professionals who are championing the practice at their wards. Implementation is influenced by factors such as healthcare professionals’ acceptance, parental empowerment, medical concerns, cultural norms, and availability of private space [22]. Moreover, barriers to implementation include, for example, parental discomfort, time required to train parents, and communication gaps between parents and staff [23, 24].

Prior studies have mainly focused on the implementation of KC in low- and middle-income countries (e.g., 23,24). Moreover, there is a dearth of evidence on strategies supporting the implementation of KC in the NICU setting, especially regarding their feasibility, acceptability, sustainability and cost-effectiveness [25, 26]. Evidence regarding implementation determinants of behavioral interventions in the NICU setting in a broader context as well as a systematic evaluation of utilized implementation strategies are needed to derive recommendations on how to overcome barriers and effectively implement KC as an IPC practice in the NICU setting [26].

The NeoIPC project [27] aims to optimize KC practices in NICUs to reduce severe neonatal infections and resistant bacterial colonization among high-risk infants (born < 32 weeks’ gestational age). NeoDeco was developed as a hybrid type 2 implementation-effectiveness study, that (1) investigates the effects of optimized KC on clinical outcomes, such as neonatal sepsis and (2) evaluates a multifaceted implementation approach to optimized KC in 12 European NICUs. The NeoImplement activities reported here comprise the implementation elements of NeoDeco and center on (1) providing implementation support and (2) evaluating the implementation of optimized KC in intervention sites. NeoDeco is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05993442) [28]. Further details on the NeoDeco effectiveness protocol are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Methods

Study design

NeoImplement is part of the NeoDeco study, a multi-center, parallel-group, cluster-randomized type 2 hybrid effectiveness-implementation study with NICUs from different European hospitals as clusters. The main effectiveness objective of the NeoDeco study is to determine the effect of unit-level implementation of an optimized version of KC (see “intervention”) versus standard of care on hospital-acquired sepsis in high-risk infants (born < 32 weeks' gestational age) admitted to the NICU [28]. The study is registered at https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05993442.

NeoDeco includes 24 NICUs from Greece, Italy, Switzerland, Spain, and the United Kingdom which are randomized 1:1 to either implementing optimized KC during the study period (intervention arm) or continuing standard of care (control arm). For the implementation evaluation, sites are divided into the following groups: (1a) sites allocated to the intervention arm (“intervention sites”), (1b) a subgroup of five intervention sites (one per each country) selected for in-depth implementation evaluation (“in-depth evaluation sites”), and (2) sites allocated to the control arm (“control sites”). For in-depth evaluation, we will select sites that are as dissimilar as possible in relevant characteristics. To ensure an unbiased, maximum variation sample [29], exhibiting heterogeneity across key characteristics, the (dis-)similarity of sites will be measured using Gower’s distance. Gower’s distance examines the overall dissimilarity by computing the pairwise-similarity across different variables and is particularly suitable for estimations across different data types (e.g., binary, categorical). The computational selection will be based on data obtained from a survey distributed in the baseline phase and will incorporate organizational criteria (e.g., nurse-to-patient ratio, percentage of high-risk infants, number of ICU beds, existence of visiting hours and information on existing KC practice (two binary items on whether KC is practiced and KC training is offered at the unit). The site selection will be performed using RStudio 4.4.1 [readr; openxlsx; gower; dplyr].

The study includes a two-to-three-month baseline period followed by a two-month wash-in period. Randomization in two staggers to intervention or control arm is performed at the start of the wash-in period using restricted randomization [30] to improve balance in important characteristics between intervention and control arm. During the wash-in period, intervention sites tailor and initiate the implementation strategies, which are described in more detail below. This is followed by a nine-to-ten-month intervention period, during which implementation strategies are rolled out further and effects evaluated. The study concludes with a three-month sustainment phase for intervention sites that aims to determine the sites’ abilities to continue optimized KC and to employ implementation strategies supporting optimized KC beyond the trial period. In the same post-trial period, control sites will receive access to materials supporting the implementation of optimized KC (Fig. 1). Sites and participants

NICUs that provide routine care to extremely premature infants (< 28 weeks' gestational age), have a minimum number of 12 beds, and do not usually provide StSC for more than 18 h per day, are eligible to participate in NeoDeco.

Of the intervention sites, all NICU staff involved in patient care are eligible for implementation support and implementation evaluation. All infants admitted to intervention sites and their caregivers (parents or other designated carers) are eligible for optimized KC if the infant’s clinical condition permits or is expected to tolerate it. This applies regardless of gestational age, birth weight, complexity of care and whether they are admitted directly after birth or later. The provision of optimized KC will be offered irrespective of eligibility for being included in the effectiveness evaluation or provision of informed consent for the study.

Intervention

Optimized KC as defined in the NeoDeco study includes early, repeated, and sustained StSC between a caregiver and infant. The intervention aims to substantially increase the duration of StSC compared to current local practice, with site-specific targets determined during the baseline period. Sites already providing regular StSC are expected to achieve a meaningful increase from their baseline levels. StSC can be provided by the mother, father or other designated carers, while feeding is continued. A KC manual provides detailed guidance on StSC, including when StSC should not be initiated, or should be stopped, to ensure maximum safety of the infant.

Primary effectiveness outcomes include cumulative incidence of neonatal severe infection or sepsis in high-risk infants. Secondary effectiveness outcomes include resistant bacterial colonization, specific infection outcomes, antibiotic use and neonatal morbidity and mortality. During the study, sites collect clinical data of all high-risk infants at the unit, including the occurrence of infection episodes and antibiotic exposure. In addition, sites conduct weekly point prevalence surveys (PPSs) to define the level of receipt of StSC in high-risk infants. Finally, stool samples are collected weekly (baseline period) and monthly (intervention period) to define infants’ colonization with resistant bacteria.

Implementation strategies

We used multiple data sources to systematically plan implementation strategies to be used within the intervention arm of the NeoDeco study. First, we conducted a narrative review [25], followed by a systematic review [26] of the literature synthesizing implementation determinants and strategies for IPC in inpatient neonatal care. We analyzed implementation determinants found in these reviews using the updated Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR, [32]) and synthesized implementation strategies following Waltz et al.’s [33] taxonomy of the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC, [34]) compilation.

Second, we conducted a needs assessment survey [35] for the implementation of IPC measures among health professionals (n = 13) from NICUs in Estonia, Greece, Italy, Slovenia, South Africa, Spain, Switzerland, and the United Kingdom taking part in a feasibility study preceding the NeoDeco study [36] in 2022, where we assessed barriers and facilitators for implementing IPC measures in their units. To obtain a more specific impression of potential implementation challenges regarding the KC intervention, we also assessed current KC practices of units participating in NeoDeco, as well as anticipated barriers and facilitators for implementing KC via an online survey during the study’s baseline phase.

Third, we conducted a workshop with around 20 scientific collaborators in the NeoIPC project in a consortium meeting in 2022, where we explored anticipated barriers and facilitators to the planned optimized KC intervention. Involved collaborators held a wide-ranging expertise on neonatal clinical practice and research. Based on these data sources and informed by the ERIC compilation [34], as well as the CFIR-ERIC-matching tool [37], we developed a set of core implementation strategies that will be offered to all sites in the intervention arm of the NeoDeco study. In addition, we will support sites in developing a set of tailored strategies that meet their respective local conditions.

Core implementation strategies

Training materials and technical support on optimized KC

Upon randomization, intervention sites receive the KC manual describing the optimized KC intervention in detail and summarizing evidence from international guidelines. The manual lists commonly recognized exclusion criteria, as well as information on equipment and environmental factors. Additionally, a video for healthcare professionals illustrating the transfer of the neonate from cot to caregiver and vice versa is made accessible to the intervention sites.

During the wash-in-phase, sites are supported with their clinical questions in a call with KC experts scheduled within the week after randomization and a webinar that is offered to all intervention sites. In the implementation phase, site representatives are invited to bi-monthly clinical mentoring calls led by KC experts and open to all intervention sites. These calls also fulfill the function of peer support between site representatives.

Designation of implementation leads

In the wash-in-phase, intervention sites choose a local implementation lead (LIL) who is the primary contact to the NeoImplement study team. LILs are meant to be staff members who are involved in or have a good understanding of the daily care and nursing activities in NICUs, such as lead research nurses. They should attend all training sessions and calls that are included in NeoImplement.

Discussion of existing implementation

Following the concept of implementation mapping of existing implementation [38], the NeoImplement study team discusses and documents activities around the implementation of KC that already exist within intervention sites in a call directly following randomization. The team also discusses individual sites’ barriers and facilitators to KC that have been collected in an online survey during the study’s baseline phase (see below) as well as implementation strategies that are currently in place, such as the presence of a KC champion who is currently promoting the practice at the units.

Implementation training and networking

Approximately one month into the wash-in phase of each study cohort, 2–3 representatives from all intervention sites (the LIL and unit staff representing another profession, such as a neonatologist if the LIL is a nursing professional), the NeoImplement team and clinical experts from the NeoIPC consortium meet in person for a 1.5 day implementation workshop. The workshop serves both training and networking purposes. The overall goal of the workshop is for site representatives to look at their local contexts through an ‘implementation science lens’ to be prepared for developing tailored implementation strategies. It involves sessions on implementation science with a focus on implementation strategies, information around the clinical intervention as well as a detailed presentation of data collections serving the implementation and economic evaluation.

In an interactive format using an adapted version of system support mapping [39], site representatives identify implementation strategies that are tailored to the specific sites’ needs and that will be employed in addition to core strategies that are offered to all sites. In the adapted system support mapping exercise, participants from each hospital work together in small groups. Supported by a visual on a flip chart, they are asked to think about a particular challenge for implementing KC at their unit. Then, they discuss needs, existing resources, potential strategies and specific goals in relation to overcoming this challenge. A template that can be used as a handout or displayed on a flipchart to support the exercise is presented in Additional File 2. Following a knowledge brokering approach [40], workshop participants are encouraged to share their acquired knowledge and carry out the system support mapping exercise with colleagues at their hospitals. To facilitate information sharing, a short video summarizing the essentials of implementation support for KC at the hospital level is provided to intervention sites.

To support networking and peer support between sites, the workshop includes teambuilding activities that foster the site representatives getting to know each other as well as collaborative exercises that are carried out across sites.

Co-design of implementation blueprint

At the implementation workshop, site representatives work on short site-specific implementation blueprints that document their tailored implementation strategies and how they plan to include these in their everyday operations. The NeoIPC implementation blueprint templates are based on Proctor et al.’s recommendations [41] for specifying and reporting implementation strategies and presented in Additional File 3. After the workshop, site representatives discuss these blueprints and, if needed, adapt them together with colleagues at their NICUs. As new implementation strategies are developed, the blueprints are extended with descriptions of these strategies. The NeoImplement team is available for questions in quarterly progress calls (see also “implementation coaching”) and via e-mail as the implementation blueprints are put into action at local sites. The employment of implementation strategies is documented in an implementation log (see “implementation evaluation”).

Implementation coaching and facilitated peer support

During the implementation phase, quarterly progress calls are conducted with the LIL and other staff from intervention units. These calls are offered to all intervention sites from a stagger together, led by the NeoImplement team and accompanied by a clinical expert from the NeoIPC consortium. In preparation for the calls, the LILs fill out a progress sheet, detailing challenges and enablers to the implementation of the intervention that have occurred in previous weeks and listing their questions to the NeoImplement team. The NeoIPC progress sheet is presented in Additional File 4. Ongoing tailoring of implementation strategies [42] is supported by discussing current barriers and potential solutions, also supported by experiences from other sites. Implementation strategies that have been tailored by local implementation teams to their contexts, as well as potentially necessary adaptations, are also discussed.

Monitoring, benchmarking & feedback

Fidelity to the optimized KC intervention (hours of StSC provided per infant per day as collected in the study) will be assessed in intervention sites on a weekly basis. As a benchmarking strategy, the NeoImplement team presents anonymized intervention fidelity data from all intervention sites for the preceding months during the progress call. After the call, sites receive their individual fidelity data for the preceding months, including the percentage of neonates who have received and the median duration of optimized KC in their units.

Tailored implementation strategies

In addition to core implementation strategies that are offered to all intervention sites, a set of tailored implementation strategies will be developed and employed by each individual intervention site. The selection will be supported through an interactive session during the implementation workshop (see “implementation training & networking”) and discussed and ongoingly refined during each quarterly progress call (see “implementation coaching”). Based on the data guiding implementation support activities (see above), the NeoImplement team suggests a variety of implementation strategies that could fit individual site contexts. These include the designation of local KC champions [43], changes in site infrastructures [44] and the engagement of caregivers [45]. However, the local implementation teams at the intervention sites are responsible for the final selection and planning of tailored strategies.

Procedures & measures

Combining multiple data collection methods, this study will use a mixed-methods approach to evaluate implementation of optimized KC. We will collect data via online surveys and remote interviews. Moreover, participating units will designate an on-site investigator who documents the site’s implementation strategies in an implementation log. Most surveys, remote interviews and information provided through the on-site investigator will be collected in English. For staff surveys, professional translators will be involved in providing back-to-back translations of all study materials from English to other study languages (French, German, Greek, Italian, Spanish). DeepL, an artificial intelligence language translation tool, will also assist the process of translations. For in-depth implementation evaluation sites, we will conduct one site visit and additional remote interviews. At on-site interviews, local interpreters will support the study team. In the following, we describe operationalizations for implementation outcomes and other study variables.

Barriers & facilitators to implementing optimized KC

Implementation determinants that are likely to affect the implementation of optimized KC at the sites will be assessed in a baseline survey that is completed by all participating sites (intervention and control arm) via REDCap. With a vignette, we will ask participants to “Please imagine that from now on your unit offers continuous and prolonged skin-to-skin contact between caregivers and all newborns, provided that they are in a sufficiently stable condition.” In the following, participants are asked to respond in an open format to (1) “What would make the implementation of this practice in your unit difficult? [If the described situation is already established practice in your unit, please describe the challenges that your unit experienced with this practice]” and (2) “What would help the implementation of this practice in your unit? [If the described situation is already established practice in your unit, please describe the facilitators that your unit experienced with this practice]”. Similarly, we will assess barriers and facilitators to another IPC practice (surveillance of colonization of infants with resistant bacteria) in a second vignette. We will categorize named determinants according to the adapted CFIR framework [32] in a rapid approach [46] and create lists of determinants for each site, which will guide our implementation support.

In follow-up calls with the sites randomized to the intervention arm, we will discuss reported implementation determinants, especially in terms of their importance for the planning of implementation strategies. Barriers and facilitators will also be continuously assessed in progress sheets (see Additional File 4) and discussed with site representatives in progress calls.

Among sites selected for in-depth evaluation, we will also assess experienced barriers and facilitators for implementing optimized KC during on-site interviews. Ethnographic observations will help identify local contextual factors that are influencing local adoption and implementation of the intervention. Two rounds of interviews (one site visit 3 months into the implementation phase and remote follow-up interviews at the end of the implementation phase) with observations and interviews with prespecified key stakeholders involved in the clinical intervention, such as unit senior and frontline staff, infection prevention team members, and hospital management, will be carried out to understand the range of factors potentially affecting performance. Our site visits will follow the standards for site visits defined by Patton [47] and updated by Kenney et al. [48]. Data collection during site visits will be performed by researchers who are highly experienced in qualitative research methods.

Intervention fidelity

Intervention fidelity refers to the extent to which the clinical intervention (i.e., optimized KC) is delivered to individual high-risk infants as intended [49]. Intervention fidelity will be monitored in intervention sites during the weekly PPS and is defined in two ways: (1) the median duration of StSC received within the preceding 24 h by high-risk infants included in the PPS, and (2) the proportion of these infants who achieved the site-specific minimum expected target duration of StSC within the same period. In control sites, the median duration of StSC received will be assessed in a similar manner. In addition to quantitative assessments of the duration of StSC, we will assess the extent to which the practices proposed in the KC manual are implemented in our on-site interviews with NICU staff.

Implementation fidelity

Implementation fidelity refers to the extent to which the strategies to support the implementation of optimized KC in intervention sites will be delivered as intended [50]; ‘intended’ thereby implying the execution of tailored implementation strategies as defined by respective units. Based on the components of implementation fidelity defined by Carroll et al. [51], data collection of fidelity parameters in this study is conceptualized along the following dimensions: i. adherence (extent to which an implementation approach is delivered as intended), ii. exposure (dosage of implementation strategies (e.g., frequency, duration), iii. participant responsiveness (extent to which participants engage with implementation strategies), iv. quality of delivery (e.g., skillful conduction of strategies), v. program differentiation (the extent to which tailored implementation strategies differ from activities that are already in place at the units).

To assess implementation fidelity pertaining to adherence, exposure, and program differentiation, intervention sites will complete two sets of documents: implementation blueprints (see above) and an implementation log. Upon initiation, sites will outline their intended implementation strategies in their implementation blueprints, which will be iteratively expanded as additional strategies are developed during the study. As the study progresses, the sites will maintain the implementation log to document the actual strategies implemented. Information shared will include the starting date, the number and roles of individuals delivering the implementation strategy, the number and roles of individuals reached, the frequency and duration of the implementation strategies, preparation time, and material costs. Sites will categorize their strategies into three predefined types: passive, recurring, and single-time. Passive strategies require minimal effort to maintain once established, such as banners or flyers. Recurring strategies occur regularly, for example, weekly case discussions. One-time strategies happen only once, such as a single training session.

To ensure proper documentation for implementation evaluation, the research team will review and validate the implementation logs periodically by reaching out to intervention sites throughout the intervention period to ensure data consistency. The implementation logs will also be used to inform the health economic evaluation by estimating associated personnel costs, preparation time and material costs. The implementation logs have been developed using Microsoft Excel and will be shared with sites via Microsoft OneDrive. A schematic implementation log is provided as Additional File 5.

To evaluate implementation fidelity regarding the quality of delivery and participant responsiveness, observations and semi-structured interviews with healthcare professionals will be conducted. For the evaluation of program differentiation, completed implementation blueprints (see Additional File 3) detailing the planning of implementation strategies by sites will be reviewed.

Acceptability, feasibility and sustainability of the intervention and of implementation strategies

Acceptability captures how suitable the optimized KC intervention and implementation strategies are perceived to be by those delivering them, based on their experiences as well as anticipation (e.g. affective attitude, burden, self-efficacy) [52]. Feasibility is “the extent to which an implementation target can be successfully used or deployed within a given setting” [53], meaning that sites are able to perform the intervention and implementation strategies in the way these are intended. Sustainability concerns the potential continuation of implemented changes (e.g. behaviors, available resources) over an extended period of time, while sustainment refers to “the extent the innovation is in place or being delivered over the long-term” [54].

Acceptability, feasibility and sustainability of both the intervention and implementation strategies will be assessed in a staff survey that will be circulated to all NICU staff of intervention sites by the on-site investigator once the implementation phase has been halfway completed. The survey will include the Acceptability of Intervention Measure (AIM), and Feasibility of Intervention Measure (FIM) scales by Weiner et al. [55] as well as the Clinical Sustainability Assessment Tool by Malone et al. [56]. Acceptability, feasibility and sustainability will also be qualitatively explored during progress-calls and site visits at in-depth sites using semi-structured interviews. Sustainment of the optimized KC intervention will be measured in a staff survey circulated during the sustainment phase and complemented with information from telephone interviews with the on-site investigators of in-depth sites to evaluate the extent to which the clinical interventions have or will become institutionalized as a part of standard care.

Cost-effectiveness of implementation strategies for optimized KC

The cost-effectiveness evaluation will use the Time-Driven Activity-Based Costing (TDABC) method to assess resource utilization and costs associated with different implementation strategies for optimized KC in NICUs. Following the approach described by Cidav et al., TDABC quantifies costs by mapping processes, tracking personnel time spent on each activity, and assigning costs based on resource use [57]. This evaluation will be conducted from a health system perspective, considering costs incurred by hospitals and healthcare providers in implementing each strategy.

This approach aligns with the Proctor et al. framework, which describes implementation strategies in terms of key components, including actors (who), actions (what), temporality (when), and dosage (how often) [41]. Implementation logs, designed based on TDABC principles (see also “implementation fidelity”), will be used to systematically capture details on the healthcare professionals involved (e.g., nurses, neonatologists), and the time allocated to each strategy. These records will serve as the basis for estimating personnel and material costs and linking them to implementation fidelity. Figure 2 displays all data collection timepoints.

Data analyses

Quantitative data, e.g., on NICU characteristics, will be analyzed using R and SPSS. To describe and compare characteristics of intervention and control sites we will use descriptive statistics, chi2 tests (categorical variables) and t-tests (continuous variables). For intervention fidelity, we will assess changes within sites as the study progresses, and changes in-between intervention sites, as well as in-between intervention and control sites, at the end of the study period. Regarding fidelity to implementation strategies, we will calculate differences between the planned duration and attendance rates for appropriate strategies (e.g. training sessions) and their actual duration and attendance.

Cost-effectiveness analyses will focus on fidelity as the key implementation outcome, with additional analyses examining how acceptability, feasibility, and sustainability influence the long-term adoption and scale-up of optimized KC [58]. Sensitivity analyses will explore variations in these implementation factors to assess their impact on the overall cost-effectiveness of each strategy. If improvements in implementation fidelity are found to enhance neonatal outcomes—such as reduced neonatal infections or shorter hospital stays—an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) will be calculated to estimate the cost per neonatal infection averted. Further sensitivity analyses will be conducted to assess the robustness of the cost-effectiveness findings by varying key parameters, such as personnel time estimates, implementation costs, and the strength of the relationship between fidelity and clinical outcomes. Findings from this evaluation will help identify the most efficient strategy for scaling up optimized KC in NICU settings.

Qualitative data from open-ended questions in surveys will be analyzed using MAXQDA. Data on barriers and facilitators for optimized KC will be analyzed individually to understand local contexts and to plan implementation strategies. Interviews with staff from in-depth sites will be transcribed verbatim and included in qualitative thematic analysis [59] in MAXQDA. We will combine deductive (based on the updated CFIR framework) [32] and inductive approaches to ensure a comprehensive understanding of contextual characteristics. Regarding acceptability, feasibility and sustainability of the intervention and implementation strategies, interview data from in-depth sites will be used for a cross-case analysis to identify trends across centers.

Discussion

According to recommendations by the WHO [16], KC should be an established part of standard neonatal care. While manifold benefits of KC for neonatal health have been known for some time, such as supporting neonatal weight gain and neurodevelopment [17, 60], a growing number of studies also shows IPC benefits of KC in neonatal intensive care units [18, 20]. However, so far, benefits have been mainly described for low- and middle-income countries. Moreover, even in many contexts in which there is a strong focus on family-centered care, there are barriers to practicing KC as recommended. The NeoIPC project aims to optimize KC practices in European NICUs and to determine its effect on severe neonatal infections. NeoImplement focuses on sustainable implementation of KC by offering and co-developing strategies that can be feasibly maintained by participating sites beyond the duration of the study and that can be adopted by other hospitals that wish to strengthen their KC practices.

Hence, many of our planned implementation strategies focus on sustaining optimized KC beyond the duration of this hybrid study. For example, identifying local implementation leads with the responsibility for championing the practice aims at maintaining a continuous sense of this internal responsibility when external implementation support ends. Also, intervention sites gain ownership for implementation strategies when the tailoring of strategies to their contexts is led by themselves. For this purpose, practices, such as the adapted system support mapping exercise (see Additional File 2), foster capacity building in the participating units for implementation and tailoring.

Regular implementation coaching sessions during the study moreover support an “implementation mindset” in site representatives through facilitated discussions about the feasibility of tailored implementation strategies. Templates that we offer to sites, including system support mapping sheets and implementation blueprints (Additional File 3), can be used for planning further implementation strategies without external support. Also, networking between sites that will be established at the implementation workshop and maintained through regular progress calls helps build sustained partnerships for mutual support in implementing KC and exchanging experiences regarding clinical neonatal care in general.

Potential risks

Some practical and operational challenges are involved in carrying out the present study. To enable participation of site representatives from all participating countries, implementation support activities will be carried out and templates will be offered in English language. This leads to possible language barriers and misinterpretations of materials. To mitigate these risks for our implementation evaluation, our data collection instruments will be translated into local languages and site visits will be accompanied by professional interpreters.

Leaving responsibility for carrying out tailored implementation strategies with the sites comes with the risk of implementation activities being highly dependent on the competencies and motivation of on-site staff. Heavy clinical workloads involve the additional risk of on-site staff not finding the necessary time to engage in study activities. To deal with these risks, we offer regular mandatory coaching sessions with site representatives, in advance of which we ask sites to submit short progress sheets and implementation blueprints describing current implementation strategies. Also, discussing ways to incorporate implementation planning into everyday clinical practice will be an important part of our implementation coaching.

Concerning the statistical analyses, there are risks in multiple testing and small sample sizes as well as ceiling or flooring effects in survey responses. We address these risks with our mixed methods approach that includes several qualitative data assessments that add more depth to our quantitative results. For our in-depth evaluation, we only assess data from one site per country, which carries the risk of insufficient representativeness. Here, our method of selecting sites that are as diverse as possible aims to obtain the most informative data possible.

Limitations

The planned study comes with several limitations which warrant discussion, such as a limitation in sample size at the NICU level and the use of self-reported data for implementation fidelity. Also, our dual role as implementation support team and internal evaluators of implementation within the hybrid study means that we cannot rule out a certain bias in the interpretation of results. However, the biggest limitation of this study is that parents will not be involved in the discussion of implementation processes, even though their perspective as actual end-users of the intervention would be extremely valuable. To meet this limitation, we are planning an ancillary study in Switzerland and Italy with parents to assess their perspectives on barriers and facilitators for the implementation of KC. Also, we cannot include clinical health status of parents and infants into our analyses of intervention fidelity, which are likely to impact the implementation of optimized KC.

Strengths

This study is nonetheless likely to provide rich insights due to the use of multiple information sources using mixed methods to evaluate implementation of optimized KC, benefiting both from qualitative as well as quantitative methods, allowing for triangulation of data to validate or contrast findings from different sources. Another strength is the inclusion of a variety of settings (e.g. different cultures and health care systems), with a focus on European countries.

Conclusion

This multicenter parallel cluster randomized hybrid type 2 implementation-effectiveness study will provide in-depth insights into the effectiveness, feasibility, acceptability, sustainability, and cost-effectiveness of strategies targeting the implementation of optimized KC in European NICUs.
