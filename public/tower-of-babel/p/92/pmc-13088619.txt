Treatment of popliteal artery injuries: a comparison of application patterns and short-term outcomes between open surgical and endovascular repair
Authors: Qianqian Jiang, Yu Huang, Chong Li, Tianyi Zhu, Xiangdong Liu, Liang Li, Xianchao Zhang, Pengkai Cao, Fengkai Wang, Yaqi Wang, Jiatao Li, Yanrong Zhang, Yunsong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAfter femoral artery injury, popliteal artery injury (PAI) is the second most frequent form of arterial injury in the lower extremities. Its exceptionally high risk of limb loss has attracted a lot of attention in the field of acute lower extremity vascular damage. Nowadays, open surgical repair or endovascular surgical repair are the main methods used to treat popliteal artery damage due to developments in medical technology. The effectiveness and complication rates of these two surgical techniques are still hotly debated, though, and there is a lot of room for advancement in this field.ObjectiveThe purpose of this study was to compare the clinical effectiveness of endovascular surgery versus open surgery for PAI, list the benefits and drawbacks of each procedure, and offer recommendations for clinical decision-making.MethodsThis single-center retrospective cohort study (2022–2024) enrolled 140 patients with peripheral arterial injury (PAI) who underwent surgical treatment at our institution. Participants were stratified into two groups according to surgical approach: endovascular repair (n = 41) and open surgical repair (n = 99). The following parameters were analyzed and compared between groups: limb salvage rate, intraoperative blood loss, operative duration, preoperative and postoperative Visual Analog Scale (VAS) scores, complication rates, and secondary intervention rates. The prespecified primary endpoint was the 6-month limb salvage rate. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0, with a P value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.ResultsThe open surgery repair group experienced an intraoperative blood loss of 464.60 ± 120.78 ml and an average surgical length of 128.89 ± 34.65 min. The postoperative follow-up short-term patency rates at 1, 3, and 6 months were 90.91%, 85.86%, and 78.79%, respectively, and the preoperative VAS score was 6.99 ± 1.42 and the postoperative VAS score was 3.48 ± 1.12. The endovascular repair group experienced an intraoperative blood loss of 175.46 ± 138.33 ml and an average surgery duration of 104.34 ± 27.58 min. Both the preoperative and postoperative VAS scores were 7.29 ± 1.36 and 3.02 ± 1.33, respectively. Short-term patency rates were 87.80%, 82.93%, and 73.17% at 1, 3, and 6 months after surgery, respectively. Vascular short-term patency rates at six months did not differ significantly between the two groups (P > 0.05), although there were significant variations in surgical length, blood loss, and postoperative VAS ratings (P < 0.05). The open surgery group experienced an overall complication rate of 27.27% and an amputation rate of 10.10%. The endovascular surgery group experienced an overall complication rate of 21.95% and an amputation rate of 4.88%. The two groups did not differ significantly in terms of problems (P > 0.05).ConclusionThis study shows similar limb salvage and short-term patency between endovascular and open repair for popliteal artery injuries. Endovascular approach offers minimally invasive benefits without reducing complications. The retrospective design risks bias and limits follow-up, with open repair possibly including more complex cases. These findings should be confirmed through prospective multicenter trials with standardized outcomes.Keywords: PAI, Open surgery, Endovascular surgery, Limb preservation rate, Short-term patency rate

Introduction

Popliteal artery injury (PAI) is the second most common infrainguinal arterial trauma, accounting for approximately 10% of vascular injuries, with traffic accidents and falls from height being the predominant causes [1, 2]. Due to its anatomically constrained location within the popliteal fossa and limited collateral circulation, PAI poses a substantial threat to limb viability. Reported amputation rates vary widely, ranging from 9.7% to 53.8%, largely dependent on ischemia time, injury mechanism, and the severity of associated musculoskeletal trauma [3]. Therefore, prompt and accurate diagnosis—achieved through ankle-brachial index measurement, duplex ultrasound, CTA, or digital subtraction angiography—is critical for limb salvage [4].

Historically, open surgical repair has been the standard of care for PAI, enabling direct debridement, primary anastomosis, or interposition grafting. However, open repair is inherently invasive and may be associated with significant blood loss, extensive soft tissue dissection, and prolonged operative time, particularly in the setting of complex knee trauma [5]. In recent years, the increasing application of endovascular techniques has introduced a paradigm shift in the management of arterial injuries. Endovascular approaches—including covered stent placement, balloon angioplasty, and thrombolysis—offer potential advantages such as reduced operative trauma, shorter ischemia time, and faster postoperative recovery [6, 7]. Despite these theoretical benefits, the adoption of endovascular repair for PAI remains variable, and its comparative effectiveness relative to open surgery continues to be debated.

Several studies have compared open and endovascular repair in PAI, yet the existing evidence is largely derived from small, heterogeneous case series with inconsistent outcome reporting [8–10]. Key questions remain unanswered: Does endovascular repair achieve comparable limb salvage rates to open surgery? Does it reduce perioperative complications or improve long-term functional outcomes? Crucially, no study has systematically compared these two approaches with respect to both short-term endpoints (e.g., amputation rate, ischemia time, hospital stay) and long-term measures (e.g., graft patency, limb function, chronic pain) in a well-characterized cohort. Moreover, the influence of injury morphology—such as the distinction between hemorrhagic and ischemic presentations—on treatment selection and outcomes has not been adequately addressed.

To address this knowledge gap, the present study retrospectively analyzed 140 consecutive cases of PAI treated at our institution. We compared patients undergoing open surgical repair versus endovascular repair across multiple domains: amputation rates, postoperative complications, short-term vascular patency, and limb functional recovery. This study aims to provide evidence-based insights to guide clinical decision-making and optimize treatment strategies for this challenging injury.limb functional recovery, to provide evidence-based references for clinical decision-making.

Objects and methods

Clinical case data

Basic information

Retrospective case-control research is what this study is. We retrospectively gathered information on 140 patients who had surgical treatment at our institution between September 2022 and June 2024 after being diagnosed with lower limb PAI (as confirmed by imaging techniques) as a result of trauma, in accordance with the inclusion criteria, featuring popliteal artery injury segments ≤ 3 cm in length and without complete transection.

Ethical review

The ethics committee at our university gave its approval to this study. We requested an exemption from informed consent because this is a retrospective study utilizing pre-existing research data.

Inclusion criteria

(1) Patients diagnosed with popliteal artery injury based on clinical symptoms and physical signs, presenting with acute limb ischemia (characterized by one or more of the 5P signs: pain, pallor, pulselessness, paresthesia, paralysis), confirmed by ankle-brachial index (ABI) and/or imaging examinations (such as CTA or ultrasound); (2) Patients who underwent surgical intervention at our hospital within 24 h of injury (ischemia time was defined as the interval from the documented time of trauma—based on patient self-report—to the restoration of arterial blood flow in the operating room); (3) Patients aged ≥ 18 years and < 65 years, capable of understanding their condition and communicating effectively in normal language; (4) Patients with complete medical records and available follow-up data.

Exclusion criteria

(1) Patients with hemorrhagic manifestations without severe ischemia (e.g., active bleeding, expanding hematoma, or hemodynamic instability) at presentation; (2) Patients with evidence of long-segment popliteal artery contusion or complete popliteal artery transection on preoperative imaging; (3) Patients with life-threatening trauma, multiorgan failure, or irreparable concomitant injuries that could confound the assessment of popliteal artery repair; (4) Patients with prior ipsilateral popliteal surgery or major limb destruction precluding revascularization; (5) Patients aged ≥ 65 years; (6) Patients unable to complete scheduled follow-up.

Clinical data

Clinical information from 140 patients—111 men and 29 women—was included in this study. The average age was 42.93 ± 14.02 years, with a range of 18 to 65 years. Infection in 12 cases (8.57%), thrombosis in 8 cases (5.71%), blunt trauma in 136 cases, sharp trauma in 4 cases. Clinical Outcomes: postoperative bleeding in 10 cases (7.14%), and compartment syndrome in 23 cases (16.43%) were the causes of injury; of these, 12 cases (12.14%) required amputation because of severe muscle necrosis, and 12 cases (12.87%) underwent secondary intervention surgery. The distribution of gender, age, underlying conditions, injury type, and location was compared between the two groups using statistical analysis. The findings demonstrated that there were no significant differences between the two groups, making them equivalent (P > 0.05).

Surgical intervention

Preoperative preparation

Upon emergency admission, the medical team immediately initiated ECG monitoring and oxygen therapy for the patient, closely monitored vital signs, and commenced emergency fluid resuscitation. Open wounds were managed with bandaging for hemostasis. During this period, a detailed history of the injury and past medical history was obtained, and a routine physical examination was performed. To comprehensively assess the patient’s condition, routine preoperative tests were conducted, including electrocardiography, chest X-ray, complete blood count, coagulation profile, comprehensive metabolic panel, preoperative infectious disease screening (eight items), and blood typing. For patients with concomitant injuries to other regions such as the chest or abdomen, further specialized imaging such as CT scans was performed. If fractures were suspected, X-ray examinations of the affected areas were obtained. Additionally, emergency CTA or ultrasound of the bilateral lower extremity arteries was performed to determine whether popliteal artery injury was present, thereby providing evidence for developing a precise surgical plan. When patients presented with other medical conditions, a multidisciplinary collaboration mechanism was immediately activated, and relevant clinical departments were invited for joint consultation to exclude surgical contraindications and assess the patient’s tolerance for surgery. During this process, the Anesthesiology Department was consulted to optimize perioperative anesthesia evaluation. After completing all preoperative preparations, the patient was thoroughly informed of their condition and the associated surgical risks. Once the patient signed the surgical consent form, surgical treatment was promptly initiated.

Surgical management

The choice between open repair and endovascular repair was not determined randomly; rather, it was made by the attending vascular surgeons through an individualized clinical assessment, with each evaluation involving the participation of at least four vascular surgeons, each possessing over 10 years of experience in vascular surgery. Key considerations included anatomic features of the injury, severity of limb ischemia, time to presentation, presence of concomitant injuries requiring exploration, and patient-specific factors such as comorbidities and socioeconomic context.

Open surgical repair primarily employed end-to-end arterial anastomosis, often performed concurrently with the management of associated soft tissue, fracture, or nerve injuries. When necessary, vascular bridging was accomplished using prosthetic grafts or autologous great saphenous vein.

Endovascular repair involved procedures such as thrombus aspiration and stent placement (covered stents), offering a less invasive alternative for selected cases. In this study, thrombus aspiration was applied selectively based on intraoperative angiographic assessment. Mechanical thrombectomy (typically using the AngioJet system) was prioritized if angiography confirmed a significant acute thrombus burden in the main popliteal artery, which was anticipated to compromise proper stent apposition or distal flow. For lesions primarily consisting of localized intimal flaps, dissection, or pseudoaneurysms with preserved distal runoff, stent placement was generally performed without routine thrombus aspiration (Fig. 1).

Conversion from endovascular to open repair was a critical intraoperative safety decision. The criteria for conversion included:① Inability to pass a guidewire/catheter across the injured segment; ② Persistent extravasation or failure to restore flow on angiography after stent deployment; ③ Intraoperative identification of vascular injury extent far exceeding preoperative evaluation (e.g., long-segment severe contusion or multi-segment injury not suitable for endovascular repair); ④ Occurrence of major complications, such as iatrogenic vessel rupture, severe dissection at the proximal or distal stent margins, or acute stent thrombosis.

Postoperative management

After the patient returned to the ward, routine postoperative care was provided, and vital signs were monitored. Antibiotics were administered postoperatively to prevent infection, while anticoagulant, antiplatelet, and analgesic medications were used to prevent vasospasm. Electrolyte imbalances, if present, were corrected. The affected limb was immobilized with a plaster cast. Distal blood circulation in the affected limb was monitored daily, including assessments of skin temperature, skin color, sensation, and arterial pulsation. The wound was regularly dressed to maintain incision cleanliness. The drainage tube was removed on postoperative day 2. For patients who underwent preventive lower leg fasciotomy, delayed primary closure was performed electively. The patient remained on bed rest for two weeks and performed ankle pump exercises in bed to prevent deep vein thrombosis of the lower extremities, among other complications.

Observation indicators and evaluation criteria

In this comparative study, we systematically documented and analyzed multiple parameters across both treatment cohorts. The recorded intraoperative metrics included blood loss and procedure duration, while popliteal artery revascularization status was assessed through Doppler ultrasonography or manual palpation of dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial arterial pulses. Pain progression was evaluated using pre- and postoperative Visual Analog Scale (VAS) scores.

Our secondary outcome measures encompassed complication rates—specifically addressing postoperative hemorrhage, surgical site infection, popliteal artery thrombosis, and compartment syndrome—along with amputation incidence and reintervention rates. The latter was strictly defined as any secondary procedure necessitated by clinical deterioration or failure to achieve anticipated recovery targets.

All participants completed a standardized postoperative surveillance protocol with scheduled evaluations at 1, 3, and 6 months. We defined primary patency as maintained vessel lumen integrity without target-lesion reintervention, objectively verified through clearly palpable pedal pulses with corroborative Doppler confirmation. A dedicated vascular assessor performed all short-term patency evaluations at these predetermined intervals.

Among the 12 patients who underwent amputation, all achieved primary wound healing with functionally favorable residual limbs. The open repair cohort (n = 89) demonstrated successful limb salvage with complete wound healing in all cases, while the endovascular group (n = 39) showed no access-site complications. Our surveillance protocol emphasized serial assessment of pedal perfusion dynamics through pulse examination and duplex ultrasonography.

We established 6-month major amputation-free survival as the primary study endpoint. Major Amputation-Free Survival within 6 months was defined as the survival time from the post-operative period until the first occurrence of major amputation of the affected limb. Patients who did not undergo major amputation within 6 months were considered to be in a survival state. Major amputation was defined as an amputation performed at the level of the proximal tibia below the knee joint or the distal femur. This included amputations due to irreversible ischemia, gangrene, or severe infection (e.g., osteomyelitis) resulting from occlusion of the target vessel. Amputations performed for non-vascular reasons, such as non-union of fractures or soft tissue necrosis unrelated to vascular insufficiency, were excluded. For brevity, this endpoint is referred to as amputation in subsequent sections.

Secondary endpoints included 6-month short-term patency, in-hospital/30-day composite complications, operative duration, blood loss, and postoperative pain scores. To ensure comprehensive analysis, we employed multivariable logistic regression models adjusted for key covariates: age, biological sex, injury mechanism, injury pattern (open/closed), associated fractures/dislocations, concomitant venous/neural injuries, and documented ischemia duration.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 27.0 was used in this study to statistically analyze the data. First, descriptive statistics were run. Mean ± standard deviation was used to express normally distributed data, and independent samples t-tests were used to examine differences between groups. Nonparametric tests were used to assess data that was not normally distributed. Chi-square tests were used to evaluate intergroup differences, while case numbers and percentages were used to express qualitative data. For the time-to-event endpoints, including time to loss of short-term patency and major amputation-free survival, this study utilized the Kaplan-Meier method for survival analysis, with between-group comparisons performed using the log-rank test. Regarding the handling of censored data, censoring events included loss to follow-up (last known follow-up date), administrative censoring (end of study period without the event), and competing risks (death from non-target events). All censored observations were fully incorporated into the survival probability calculations up to their time of censoring, ensuring the complete utilization of available follow-up information and maintaining the unbiased nature of the results. To examine the benefits and drawbacks of various surgical techniques, patients were categorized based on their surgical procedures and compared with clinical signs. Statistical significance was indicated by a P-value < 0.05.

Surgery time

In the open surgery repair group, the average surgical time was 128.89 ± 34.65 min, whereas in the endovascular surgery repair group, it was 104.34 ± 27.58 min. Compared to endovascular surgery, open surgery took longer, and the difference was statistically significant (P < 0.05). In the open surgery repair group, the average intraoperative blood loss was 464.60 ± 120.78 milliliters, whereas in the endovascular surgery repair group, it was 175.46 ± 138.33 milliliters. There was a statistically significant difference (P < 0.05) in the amount of blood lost by the open surgery group compared to the endovascular surgery group. The open surgery repair group’s preoperative pain score was 6.99 ± 1.42, while the endovascular surgery repair group’s was 7.29 ± 1.36. The two groups did not differ statistically significantly (P > 0.05). In the open surgery repair group, the postoperative pain score was 3.48 ± 1.12, whereas in the endovascular surgery repair group, it was 3.02 ± 1.33. As indicated in Table 4, the open surgery repair group’s postoperative score was greater than the endovascular surgery repair group’s, and the difference was statistically significant (P < 0.05).

Postoperative survival prognostic analysis

A total of 140 patients with popliteal artery injury of the lower extremity were enrolled in this study. All patients completed the 6-month follow-up with no loss to follow-up. Discussion

PAI is the second most prevalent type of groin artery injury, accounting for 10.05% of vascular injuries [1], while peripheral vascular injury makes up 4%–6% of all traumas [11]. The calf blood vessels’ primary input channel is the popliteal artery, with a minor collateral circulation. Amputation rates range from 10% to 50%, and PAI can have major repercussions [12]. The amputation rate for PAI has been falling as a result of improvements in medical technology. Between 2007 and 2015, the amputation rate dropped from 7.1% to 5.1%, according to a retrospective analysis of 5,143 PAI patients from the National Trauma Data Bank (NTDB) [13]. Limb ischemia duration has decreased due to advancements in surgical techniques and the use of novel procedures, such as two-point access vascular stent technology and temporary catheter-based popliteal artery bypass [14, 15]. Currently, endovascular reconstruction and open surgical repair are the two main approaches used to treat PAI. Due to its high success rate, open surgical repair is still the most common method; however, as endovascular surgical techniques have advanced, the use of endovascular treatment has been growing yearly, going from 3.2% in 2007 to 7.6% in 2014 [16]. Reports on the two surgical techniques’ postoperative results are scarce, nevertheless. Therefore, this study performed a retrospective analysis of their various indicators to give reference for clinical treatment decisions in order to elucidate the variations in treatment efficacy between the two modalities.

The clinical data of 140 individuals who received simultaneous surgical therapy for PAI was gathered and statistically examined for this study. According to the findings, the rates of limb preservation, complications, and six-month short-term patency did not differ statistically significantly between open surgery repair and endovascular surgery repair. On the other hand, preoperative and postoperative VAS scores, intraoperative blood loss, and surgery time all showed statistically significant variations.The limb preservation rate in this study was 89.90%, meaning that out of the 99 patients who had open surgery for limb salvage, 89 had effective limb preservation. Twelve of these cases successfully preserved their limbs, out of the 13 that were converted to open surgery, resulting in a 92.31% limb preservation rate. 39 of the 41 patients who had endovascular treatment were able to successfully preserve their limbs, yielding a 95.12% limb preservation rate. The two surgical techniques did not significantly differ in terms of limb preservation rates, according to statistical analysis. Within the usual range, Yun-Huan Hsieh et al. reported a limb preservation rate of 84% for open surgery repair [17] while Chuli Jiang et al. reported a limb preservation rate of 89.1% for endovascular repair [18].

Even though there was no statistically significant difference in the limb salvage rates between the two surgical techniques in this study, endovascular repair had a little greater limb salvage rate. The higher observed limb salvage rate in the endovascular group may be partially attributable to the allocation of popliteal artery injuries of lower complexity to this treatment modality, while the open surgery group inherently included a higher proportion of severe trauma cases (such as open wounds, knee dislocations/fractures, and concomitant venous or nerve injuries), as reflected in the baseline characteristics. Moreover, the extended mending time in open surgery might be the cause of this outcome. According to studies, the amputation rate can approach 30% when limb ischemia lasts longer than 6 h [19], and the risk increases by 12% for every extra hour [20]. The ischemic time of the affected limb was somewhat prolonged by the conversion of 13 patients to open surgery as a result of unsuccessful endovascular surgery; however, the subsequent surgical repair time was somewhat shortened by the use of DSA imaging to clearly identify the extent and location of the injury.

This study demonstrated that the endovascular surgical repair group’s average operation time was less than that of the open surgery group, indicating the benefit of endovascular surgery in terms of a quicker recovery of the lower limb blood supply. According to related research, tibia-fibula fractures or dislocations and blunt trauma mechanisms are separate risk factors for amputation [13]. The longer operative time and greater intraoperative blood loss observed in the open surgery group likely reflect both the inherent technical demands of open vascular reconstruction and the characteristics of the more severe injuries allocated to this approach, which required more complex management. The majority of patients in the open surgery group in this study had venous and nerve injuries, open injuries, and knee dislocations or fractures, which may indicate that they experienced more severe traumatic injuries. The endovascular surgery group, on the other hand, saw less related injuries, which could be one factor contributing to its greater limb preservation rate. Additionally, the lower success rate and unique limitations of endovascular surgery may have caused an uneven distribution of cases between the two groups, which could also be one reason for the slightly higher limb preservation rate in the endovascular surgery group. This is because open surgery repair is currently the mainstream procedure.

Postoperative complications have an effect on patient outcomes in addition to limb preservation. Open surgery had a higher rate of fasciotomy than endovascular surgery, according to studies by Helen Potter et al. and Agastya Vaidya et al. [16, 21]. According to a study by William et al., endovascular surgery had a greater rate of thrombosis and open surgery had a higher incidence of compartment syndrome [22]. This suggests that the two surgical techniques differ in the frequency of postoperative problems. In particular, endovascular surgery had a higher risk of thrombosis but a reduced incidence of compartment syndrome.

Four surgical complications—bleeding, infection, thrombosis, and compartment syndrome—were statistically analyzed in this study, and no statistically significant differences were discovered. The two surgical techniques had comparable rates of postoperative bleeding. Anastomotic hemorrhage may occur during repair, however open surgery necessitates a 15 cm incision and careful anatomical restoration of damaged vessels. Stent installation poses a risk of fistula formation, which can also result in bleeding, and endovascular surgery involves puncturing the contralateral femoral artery, which may cause more harm and bleeding. Compared to the open surgical repair group, the endovascular repair group demonstrated a higher postoperative thrombosis rate but lower rates of postoperative infection and compartment syndrome. The large surgical incisions and open injuries commonly associated with open surgery elevate the risk of infection. Furthermore, a strong correlation exists between ischemia time and the incidence of compartment syndrome [23]. The longer operative duration of open surgery, coupled with potential iatrogenic tissue damage during the procedure, may contribute to tissue edema and subsequent compartment syndrome. The lower rates of infection and compartment syndrome in the endovascular group may be attributed to its minimally invasive nature and shorter ischemic time. Conversely, the observed trend toward higher thrombosis in the endovascular group may stem from the technical limitations of fully assessing vascular injury under fluoroscopy and the inherent thrombogenic potential of covered stents.

Both surgical techniques aim to improve prognosis outcomes, guarantee limb preservation, and quickly restore blood flow to the lower limbs. Patients’ quality of life is greatly impacted by postoperative popliteal artery short-term patency, which is also a crucial metric for assessing the effectiveness of treatment. Amputation rates are considerably greater for individuals who lose popliteal artery short-term patency within 30 days after surgery than for those who experience delayed short-term patency loss, according to studies [24]. Although endovascular surgery can attain comparable short-term patency rates with multiple treatments, open surgery has higher vascular short-term patency rates at one year [17]. The open surgery group’s short-term patency rate was somewhat greater than the endovascular surgery group’s, but there was no statistically significant difference, according to follow-ups conducted at one, three, and six months. The popliteal artery’s unique anatomical location, with a large bending angle that can cause stent deformation, narrowing, or displacement, the limited number of arteries below the knee, which can impair blood flow and increase the risk of thrombosis, and poor patient compliance, with irregular medication use after surgery, are some potential causes [17]. The maximum follow-up duration in this study was six months; therefore, the reported patency rates should be considered short-term outcomes. Given the hemodynamic stresses in the popliteal segment and the known long-term challenges of stents—particularly covered stents—in this location, such as intimal hyperplasia and in-stent restenosis, longer-term follow-up (e.g., at 1, 2, or 5 years) is required to evaluate their durability and long-term efficacy. Therefore, in order to fully examine this occurrence, more data collection and long-term follow-up research are required.

Furthermore, the role of endovascular repair as a bridging or damage control strategy—rather than definitive treatment—in the setting of acute polytrauma warrants consideration. In such scenarios, its primary objective is rapid hemorrhage control and perfusion restoration, with potential planned conversion to definitive open repair once the patient is physiologically stabilized. While our cohort did not specifically analyze this nuanced application, it represents an important dimension of modern trauma vascular surgery.

There are several important limitations to this study. First, the sample size is relatively small, which may limit the generalizability of the findings and reduce the statistical power to detect clinically meaningful differences between groups. This issue is particularly noteworthy because, in clinical practice, patients with open injuries are often prioritized for open surgery, which may have led to an overrepresentation of such cases in the open repair group. Second, this study lacks comprehensive long-term outcome measures. While short-term endpoints such as amputation rates were assessed, important indicators including chronic pain, degree of limb function recovery, long-term vascular patency, and other long-term complications were not systematically evaluated. This limits our in-depth understanding of the durable effects and functional impact associated with each treatment approach. Third, although baseline characteristics appeared comparable on aggregate measures, this was not a randomized controlled trial. Treatment allocation was based on the judgment of vascular surgeons, which may have introduced clinically significant differences in injury complexity between groups that were not fully captured in our comparative tables. For example, even if the binary presence of severe soft tissue disruption or complex fracture patterns appeared statistically similar between groups, the open surgery group may have included a higher proportion of such injuries; parameters such as hemodynamic status and injury severity were not stratified in detail due to incomplete data in existing medical records. These unmeasured nuances could confound the comparison of key outcomes such as amputation rates. Fourth, as a single-center retrospective study, the external validity of its findings may be limited. Variations in clinical practice guidelines, technical expertise, institutional protocols, and surgical resources across different centers may affect the applicability of our results to other settings or populations. Finally, confounding factors were not sufficiently controlled in this study. Perioperative management—including the type and dosage of postoperative anticoagulation and antibiotic therapy, as well as rehabilitation protocols—varied among patients. These factors may influence the recovery process and overall efficacy but were not rigorously standardized or adjusted for in the analysis. Furthermore, the study did not perform stratified analyses based on concomitant injuries or comorbidities. Given that popliteal artery injuries often coexist with other traumatic or systemic conditions, the absence of such adjustments may have obscured the true relationship between surgical approach and clinical efficacy.

In summary, within the limitations of this retrospective study, the observed associations suggest that endovascular repair for PAI can achieve limb salvage and short-term patency rates comparable to those of open surgery in selected patient populations, while offering advantages in operative time and intraoperative blood loss. Given the inherent selection bias and potential imbalance in injury severity between groups, our findings should not be interpreted as establishing therapeutic equivalence. The choice of technique should be individualized, with comprehensive consideration of injury pattern, patient physiological status, and surgeon expertise. Future research should prioritize the conduct of a large-scale, multicenter, prospective randomized controlled trial to definitively establish the comparative effectiveness of endovascular versus open repair for popliteal artery injuries. Such an initiative should aim to enroll a broad and heterogeneous patient population to minimize selection bias and enhance the generalizability of the results. A standardized set of outcome measures, including long-term patency rates and comprehensive limb function recovery, must be established prospectively to allow for robust comparisons. Furthermore, future studies should systematically evaluate the impact of specific technical approaches, perioperative management protocols, and surgeon experience on patient outcomes. The ultimate goal is to generate high-quality evidence that can refine treatment algorithms, optimize patient selection, and improve overall prognosis and quality of life for individuals suffering from these severe injuries.

Conclusion

The findings of this study indicate that, within the context of its methodological constraints, endovascular repair is associated with outcomes comparable to open surgical repair regarding limb salvage rates and short-term patency after popliteal artery injury. Owing to its minimally invasive nature, the endovascular approach is associated with reduced revascularization time and intraoperative blood loss. These observed advantages suggest a potential clinical utility in selected cases, although the endovascular approach did not significantly lower the overall incidence of postoperative complications.

However, several important limitations must be acknowledged. This study employed a retrospective design, and treatment allocation was based on clinical judgment, which may have led to baseline differences between groups due to variations in injury complexity. Furthermore, due to limitations in the available medical records, detailed classification of injury characteristics—such as hemodynamic status and injury severity—was not feasible. Unmeasured differences between the two groups regarding these parameters may have introduced confounding bias and influenced the observed outcomes. Although we analyzed the primary endpoint using the Kaplan-Meier method, no multivariable adjustment analysis was performed to control for known confounding factors (such as injury mechanism, ischemia time, and associated injuries), nor were in-depth sensitivity analyses conducted. This may result in observed differences in surgical parameters and potentially affect outcomes. Therefore, the observed intergroup differences should be interpreted with caution, as they may be influenced by residual confounding. Additionally, the relatively short follow-up period limits the assessment of long-term patency, stent durability, functional recovery, and quality of life. The lack of standardized long-term functional and patient-reported outcomes (such as quality of life measures) restricts a comprehensive evaluation of treatment efficacy. Future prospective studies or more sophisticated statistical adjustments are needed to validate these findings.

Therefore, the current conclusions should be interpreted as preliminary. We strongly advocate for future prospective, multicenter studies incorporating standardized endpoint definitions and long-term follow-up protocols to definitively validate the comparative efficacy of these two strategies in the management of popliteal artery injuries and to provide more robust, high-level evidence for clinical practice.
