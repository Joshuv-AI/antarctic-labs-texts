Molecular epidemiology of Campylobacter jejuni associated with Guillain–Barré syndrome
Authors: Shoma Hayat, Md Golam Mostafa, Md Abu Jaher Nayeem, Ruma Begum, Ayesha Akter Anjuman, Saima Akhter Sadia, Imran Hasan, Asaduzzaman Asad, Zhahirul Islam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCampylobacter jejuni is the leading cause of bacterial gastroenteritis and its post-infectious sequelae, Guillain–Barré syndrome (GBS). Disease epidemiology of campylobacteriosis differs between high- and low-income countries. The molecular epidemiology of GBS-associated C. jejuni strains has not been thoroughly studied to understand its population dynamics and unique genomic features. We investigated a genome-based epidemiological analysis of 90 GBS-associated C. jejuni strains from 10 different countries to elucidate bacterial genomic features and epidemiological patterns related to GBS induction. We found 11 clonal complexes (CC) and 25 sequence types (STs) with two prevalent lineages, CC-362 (ST-2993) and CC-22(ST-22). SNP-based phylogenetic analysis revealed, the first sequenced C. jejuni (CC-22) from USA was genetically associated with strains from Bangladesh, Mexico, and China. Lipo-oligosaccharide (LOS) class A was prevalent worldwide (63%). In Bangladesh, 32% (9/28) of sialyltransferase cstII-genes harbored a Threonine at 51st-position, compared to China (60%, 6/10), USA (100%, 2/2), and Netherlands (67%, 4/6). Type-VI secretion system was enriched in Bangladesh (11/38, 29%). Among C. jejuni-integrated elements, CJIE-1 was most common (27/90, 30%). Phase variation was significantly higher in CC-403 strains. Introduction

Campylobacter jejuni (C. jejuni) is the leading cause of bacterial gastroenteritis and is responsible for neurological post-infectious sequelae, such as Guillain–Barré syndrome (GBS)1. Currently, both intravenous immunoglobulin (0.4 g/kg for 5 days) and plasma exchange (usually five sessions at 200–250 ml/kg) are established standard-of-care treatments for patients with GBS. Over the last 10 years, C. jejuni incidence and prevalence have surged in both low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) and developed countries2. C. jejuni infections are frequently accompanied by a self-limiting disease marked by diarrhoea, abdominal cramps, and bloody feces2. However, only a small percentage (1 in 1000–5000 cases) of patients with C. jejuni causing enteritis develop GBS, depending on the variable serotype/genotype involved1,3,4. Lipooligosaccharides (LOS) on the cell surface of C. jejuni mimic the nerve gangliosides, leading to an autoimmune response that damages the peripheral nerves, leading to the development of GBS5. Globally, C. jejuni strains have manifested a wide range of genomic diversity, including their core and accessory genomes6. These strains vary in the surface structure, which includes variable genomic regions such as flagella, lipo-oligosaccharide biosynthesis region, lipo-polysaccharide biosynthesis region, and genes including flaA, cadF, racR, dnaJ, cdtA, cdtB, cdtC, wlaN etc. with putative virulence properties, such as invasiveness, toxin expression, iron acquisition, and sialylation7–9. Previously, comparative genomic studies of the GBS-associated C. jejuni from Japan, the Netherlands, China, South Africa, Mexico, and Peru have been conducted to identify novel genomic determinants of C. jejuni10–13. These studies reported that C. jejuni strains associated with GBS are mainly HS:19 and HS:41 using Penner heat-stable (HS) serotyping scheme14. Multi-locus sequence typing (MLST) analysis showed different C. jejuni sequence types (STs) are associated with GBS in different countries, such as ST-362 in South Africa15, ST-2993 in China and Peru16, ST-22 in the Netherlands17, and the USA. Moreover, cst-II, cgtA, and cgtB genes are involved in sialylation of LOS and linked with C. jejuni ganglioside mimicry expression18. Still, the genomic variations with epidemiological dynamics of GBS-associated C. jejuni strains are less studied. Technological advancements, particularly in next-generation sequencing (NGS) and advanced bioinformatics enable high-resolution analysis of C. jejuni genomes, their evolution and transmission. Therefore, using these cutting-edge technologies, identification of potential microbial markers in GBS-associated C. jejuni genome in locations across different regions is a prerequisite to explore clinical and therapeutic targets for improved disease control and prevention strategies in the future.

Existing literature suggests that disease epidemiology and genomic signatures of campylobacteriosis differ between LMICs and high-income countries19. Previous comparative genomic studies separately analyzed using a limited numbers of strains from GBS-associated pathogenic C. jejuni, from different geographical locations. With the emergence of high-throughput genomic sequencing, the number of C. jejuni sequences isolated from GBS patients in the publicly available database has expanded, providing a valuable resource for future genome-based research. However, no genome-based epidemiological study has been performed encompassing all publicly accessible GBS-linked C. jejuni isolates to explore the bacterial genomic features and epidemiological pattern across the globe to identify candidate microbial biomarkers in C. jejuni that may induce GBS. Therefore, we extensively investigated GBS-associated C. jejuni strains from Bangladesh and various geographical regions to provide a deeper insight into the pathogenic virulence features or pathways by which C. jejuni causes GBS in Bangladesh and worldwide, representing a genomic epidemiological landscape with various phenotypes and clinical manifestations. Thereby, this study provided a large dataset from various countries which may serve as an international epidemiological database of GBS-associated C. jejuni strains. This resource can help to understand the geographical basis of genomic diversity and similarity of C. jejuni strains.

Results

Epidemiology of intended C. jejuni-associated GBS

We conducted a comparative analysis of data from Bangladeshi patients with GBS and patients representing nine different countries. In Bangladesh, the majority of patients were male (n = 22/32) compared to female (n = 10/32) and had a history of diarrhoea (n = 24/32) before developing GBS. Acute motor axonal neuropathy (AMAN) was the prevalent (n = 23) subtype of GBS followed by acute inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (AIDP) (n = 2), acute motor and sensory axonal neuropathy (AMSAN) (n = 1) (Supplementary Data 1). Based on previously published studies, the epidemiology of C. jejuni-associated GBS exhibited marked geographic variation across the 10 countries included in this study. The incidence of GBS across the included countries ranged from 0.42 to 6.58 per 100,000 population, with the lowest incidence observed in Japan (0.42) and the highest in the USA (6.58)20,21. Diarrhoea was the predominant antecedent event reported in Bangladesh22, and Peru11, whereas upper respiratory tract infection (URTI) along with diarrhoea was common in China, the Netherlands and Germany23–25. Axonal subtype prevailed in Bangladesh, Peru, Mexico and Northern China (1990s)11,22,26,27. In contrast, the demyelinating subtype was predominant in the Netherlands, Japan, USA, UK, Germany, South Africa, and currently in both Northeastern and Southern China21,25,28–32 (Fig. 1). Among the pathogenic autoantibodies implicated in GBS, anti-GM1 antibodies are frequently detected in patients with the axonal subtype and are strongly associated with preceding C. jejuni enteritis through molecular mimicry mechanisms22. In all included countries, the majority of patients received either intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) or plasma exchange (PE) as specific treatment, except Bangladesh, where 85% of the patients could not afford either IVIg or PE. The outcome of GBS was favorable in most countries in contrast to Bangladesh, where substantially higher proportion of patients remained disabled at 6 months after disease onset, with approximately 30–40% unable to walk independently24 (Table 1).

MLST-based genetic diversity of C. jejuni

Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) analysis has shown that 90 C. jejuni isolates from 10 different countries (isolates: Bangladesh, n = 38 and other 9 countries, n = 52) were classified into 25 distinct sequence types (STs). Out of the 25 distinct sequence types, 22 belong to known clonal complexes (CCs), while 3 are not associated with any CCs (Fig. 2a). A minimum spanning tree showed that clonal complex CC-362 (32%, [29/90]) was the most prevalent lineage among the global C. jejuni strains, followed by CC-22 (20%, [18/90]), CC-21 (13%, [12/90]) and CC-403 (12%, [11/90]). The majority of the C. jejuni strains (77%) were assigned to one of these four CCs. Sequence type ST-22 (18%, [16/90]) was prevalently found in strains originating from six different countries (Fig. 2b). Two lineages, CC-362 (ST-2993) and CC-22 (ST-22), were found in 26% and 18% of the C. jejuni strains, respectively. C jejuni strains from Bangladesh were found to be highly diverse, with the unique clonal complex CC-403 identified exclusively in this region. Clonal complex CC-362 was dominant in strains isolated from Peru (100%, n = 22/22) and South Africa (100%, n = 3/3). In China and the Netherlands, most of the strains belong to CC-22 (54% and 66%, respectively), while in Japan and Mexico, the studied single strain isolated from each country also classified under clonal complex CC-22. Among the studied strains isolated from the USA (n = 4) had classified as clonal complex CC-21 and CC-22 in equal numbers, respectively (Fig. 2b).

Phylogenetic analysis of GBS-associated C. jejuni

The phylogenomic analysis of the C. jejuni isolates from 10 different countries (1983–2022) revealed a diverse and widespread distribution of strains with distinct clonal complexes, LOS class variations and lineage specific virulence genes (Fig. 3). In our study, the first C. jejuni strain isolated in the USA in 1983 that carried clonal complex CC-22 was closely associated with the isolated strains from Bangladesh BD-39 (2006), Mexico RM1510, and China HB93-13 (1993). LOS classes A (63% [57/90], A1 & A2), B (16%, [14/90], B1 & B2), & C were the common LOS classes as well as LOS classes D (D-4267), F (HB-CJGB-XWM), H (HB-CJGB-LC), M (Z211097SS & Z211097RS), P (HB-CJGB-LL), & W (BD-94) were also found. LOS class C strains were found in 10% (9/90) of all studied strains, and these strains also carried cstIII gene. Among the Bangladeshi strains, 74% (n = 28 [28/38]) carried the cstII gene. Of the cstII-positive strains, 32% (n = 9 [9/28]) had threonine and 68% (n = 19 [19/28]) presented with asparagine at the 51st position. All strains from Clade I were isolated from Bangladesh that carried an identical clonal complex CC-403 and belong to LOS class B1. Strains of this clade I possess 29% (n = 11/38) threonine at the 51st position of cstII gene, type VI secretion system (T6SS), antimicrobial resistance (AMR) marker gene gyrA T86I and integrated element CJIE3. In clade II, 21% (n = 19 [19/90]) of the strains showed diversified characteristics following clonal complexes CC-21, CC-61, CC-354, and CC-443. These strains also exhibited diversity in LOS classes, including A1, A2, C, D, F. However, 47% (n = 9/19) clonal complexes CC-21 in Clade II belong to LOS class C. Clade III contains the oldest strains across the international context. In sub-clade III-A, only two strains—one from Bangladesh and another from China, belonged to clonal complex CC-45. Sub-clade III-B, strains had the genomic association by carrying similar clonal complex CC-42, LOS class B2, T4SS, LOS genes (cgtA, cgtB, cstII [Thr51]), and CJIE1 while sub-clade III-C showed the genomic association with the strains from three continents (North America [USA], Europe [ the Netherlands] & Asia [Bangladesh & Japan]). All strains from sub-clades III-D & III-E were isolated in Bangladesh, however, strains in sub-clade III-D were isolated between 2007 and 2012, and strains in sub-clade III-E were isolated between 2020 and 2022. Two strains in sub-clade III-F were segregated from sub-clade G and possessed LOS class M along with cstIIAsn51 gene. Sub-clade G was another worldwide dissemination of GBS strains from China, South Africa, and Peru. Among the 90 C. jejuni strains analyzed, 53% (48/90) possessed antimicrobial resistance (AMR) determinants associated with β-lactam and quinolone resistance. The gyrA T86I mutation was the most frequently detected resistance marker, occurring in 53% (48/90) of the isolates.

Pangenome analysis of C. jejuni strains associated with GBS

A total of 4894 genes were identified among the 90 strains, of which 1167 were classified as core genes as their presence was ≥ 99% of strains (Fig. 4a). Core genes represented only 24% of the total pangenome. Soft-core genes, another subset comprised of 167 genes, covered 3.4% of the total genes. However, most of the genes were defined as accessory genes, comprising 73% of the pangenome, with 634 genes represented as shell genes (13% of the pangenome) and 2926 genes (60% of the pangenome) represented as cloud genes. Flower plots showed the core, accessory, and unique genes of the genomes (Fig. 4b). In Bangladesh, the accessory genes were found at a mean of 538.45 ± 82.39 (mean ± standard deviation) genes with unique genes at a mean of 15.95 ± 45.75. In China, the accessory genes were 505.23 ± 55.80 with unique genes of 15.46 ± 20.74. On the other hand, the mean accessory genes were decreased to 485.27 ± 58.47 and 472.50 ± 23.21, with unique genes of 9.91 ± 44.63 and 0.33 ± 0.82 in Peru and the Netherlands, respectively. Comparative analysis of accessory genes showed that strains from Bangladesh had a significantly higher gene abundance than those from Peru (p = 0.0048; Supplementary Fig. 1). Gene presence and absence matrix in 90 C. jejuni strains indicated that strains were clustered regionally, located by different branches (Fig. 4c).

Virulence factors in GBS-associated C. jejuni strains

Virulence genes showed no clustering regarding clonally or regionally. All isolates in this study carried the genes CadF, jlpA, porA, pebA, and ciaB, which encode proteins involved in bacterial adhesion and invasion of host epithelial cells (Fig. 5). CdtB, the active subunit of cytolethal distending toxin (CDT) with DNase activity on the host, was detected in 93% (n = 84) of the strains. In contrast, the remaining 6 strains lacking the cdtB gene were isolated from Bangladesh. Z221130RS, the only strain from Bangladesh, did not carry the cdtC gene. O-linked glycosylation genes (pseA-pseC, pseF-pseG), N-linked glycosylation genes (pglB, pglC, pglF-pglJ), flagellar genes (flgC-flgE2, flgG-flgI, flgK-flgL, flgR, fliA, fliD, fliF-fliI, fliL, fliM, fliR, fliY, motA, motB, pflA) were harbored in all strains. The flagellar filament genes, flaA and flaB were found at 38% (n = 34) and 42% (n = 38) of strains, respectively. The motility accessory factor (maf) family genes, maf1 and maf4 were absent in all strains except maf1, which was present only in the BD-22 strain from Bangladesh (Fig. 5). Flagella-related genes (fliQ, fliS, fliN, fliG, and flgB) showed a significant positive correlation (p < 0.001) with the N-linked glycosylation genes pglE and pglD, as well as with the O-linked glycosylation gene maf3 (p < 0.01). Furthermore, O-linked glycosylation LOS genes neuB2 & neuC2 had a significant (p < 0.001) positive correlation with the T6SS. Motility and export genes (ptmA and ptmB) and LOS genes neuB2 and neuC2 showed a negative correlation (p < 0.001) with metabolic gene cysc1, oxidation catalase sub unit protein ddhA, flagellar gene maf7, and cytotoxic subunit gene cdtB. Type IV secretion system genes—virB4, virB8, virB9 & virB10 also had a significant (p < 0.01) negative correlation with ptmA, ptmB, neuB2 and neuC2 (Supplementary Fig. 2). The LOS gene, cstII, was carried in 80% (72/90) of the isolates and was distributed across clonal complexes CC-21, CC-22, CC-362, CC-41, CC-42, and CC-61; whereas, cstIII was found only in CC-21 strains (Supplementary Fig. 3).

C. jejuni integrated elements and phages among GBS-associated strains

All five known C. jejuni integrated elements (CJIEs)—CJIE1, CJIE2, CJIE3, CJIE4, and CJIE5 were screened across the study strains and revealed their presence in 40% (36/90) of the strains (Supplementary Data 2). CJIE1 was found 30% of strains (n = 90, [27/90]) in which 59% (n = 27, [16/27]) CJIE1 carried strains were from Bangladesh, 11% (n = 3, [3/27]) were from South Africa, 7% (n = 2, [2/27]) were from USA & China respectively, 3% (n = 1, [1/27]) were from Germany, Japan and Netherlands respectively. Only 4 strains (3 from Bangladesh and one from Germany) carried CJIE2. CJIE3 was found in 12 strains, of which 11 were from Bangladesh and only one was from China. Furthermore, 8% (n = 7, [7/90]) strains carried multiple CJIEs. Strains from Peru did not carry any integrated elements, whereas 63% of Bangladeshi strains (24/38, n = 38) carried at least one integrated element. The most common phage in sequence in the CJIE1 was Pseudomonas phage PPpW & Escherichia phage vB_EcoM (Supplementary Data 2).

Phase variation in GBS-associated C. jejuni

The simple sequence repeat (SSR) driven phase variable (PV) genes of C. jejuni isolated from GBS patients were mainly capsular and flagellar genes. Comparative analysis revealed that Bangladesh possessed a higher abundance of SSRs than China (p = 0.0001), and Peru exhibited higher prevalence of SSRs than Netherland (p = 0.0106) and China (p ˂ 0.0001; Supplementary Fig. 4). Protein containing aminopeptidase—a core Phasome was the most prevalent (99%, [89/90]) in all strains followed by possible sugar transferase (98%, [88/90]), hemerythrin domain protein (94%, [85/90]), adenosine deaminase (66%, [60/90]), capsular polysaccharide export system protein (61%, [55/90]), O-methyl phosphoramidate capsule modification (57%, [52/90]), motility accessory factor (55%, [50/90]) (Fig. 6a). The most common type of SSR was poly-G tract (n = 2330), followed by poly-T (n = 152), poly-AT (n = 40), poly-TA (n = 22), and poly-CT (n = 15) (Fig. 6b). The length of the poly-G was commonly found at 9 bp (n = 1192), poly-T at 10 bp (n = 121), poly-AT at 7 bp (n = 32), poly-TA at 6 bp (n = 22), and poly-CT at 6 bp (n = 7) (Fig. 6c). Strains belonging to clonal complex CC-403 had significantly (p < 0.0001) higher numbers of SSR repeats than strains from clonal complexes CC-362, CC-21, and CC-22 (Fig. 6d).

Discussion

In this study, we aimed to unveil the genomic characteristics and epidemiological patterns of C. jejuni to elucidate the potential genomic features associated with GBS. This study reveals that strains were diverse both regionally and clonally, presenting clonal complex CC-22, the genetic lineage responsible for the global spread of GBS-associated C. jejuni. Particularly, strains from Bangladesh exhibited distinct genomic traits, including a significantly higher prevalence of the Type VI secretion system (T6SS), LOS class B variants, and multiple CJIEs, suggesting enhanced genomic plasticity. Pangenome analysis showed that strains from Bangladesh carried more accessory genes and phase-variable simple sequence repeats (SSRs), particularly within CC-403, suggesting increased genomic dynamism and adaptability. CJIE1 was detected in 30% of the strains, with the highest representation from Bangladesh (59%), followed by South Africa (11%), USA and China (7% each). Collectively, these features underscore the regionally distinct evolution of GBS-associated C. jejuni and highlight the key genomic factors that may contribute to its pathogenic potential in diverse epidemiological settings.

Several studies indicated that a substantial number of GBS cases (25–57%) have evidence of recent C. jejuni infection1,22,24,33. Epidemiological studies have reported an outbreak of 36 GBS cases linked to prior C. jejuni infections in China13. Similarly, in 2019, a GBS outbreak in Peru was also associated with C. jejuni11. The most recent outbreak of GBS in Pune (India) has described the link of a specific strain of C. jejuni with neurotoxic potential34, as one of the precedent microbes of GBS development, yet the detail genomic features have not been reported. The genomic content of microbe plays a very important role in outbreak investigation by predicting how the outbreak might evolve. Comparison of microbial genomics with known strains from other locations or reservoirs can identify the source of the outbreak. The current study described 90 strains from different geographical locations with 25 distinct sequence types (STs) and four major clonal complexes; CC-362, CC-22, CC-403, and CC-21, representing 77% of the isolates. Clonal complex CC-22 was the earliest lineage that was first found in C. jejuni strain isolated in the USA in 1983 from a GBS patient, though the stool culture and serum antibody titre confirmed the first C. jejuni-associated GBS patient was reported in 198235. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that this strain is genetically related to the strains first isolated from Bangladesh and China. Notably, 18% of the strains from six different countries carried this clonal complex. This suggests that clonal complex CC-22 is the earliest worldwide dissemination lineage of the GBS strains. The presence of CC-22, especially across diverse continents, aligns with its known role in post-infectious neuropathies36. However, CC-403 was only prevalent in Bangladesh with highly diversified genomic characteristics. These findings point to a unique epidemiological landscape, where local environmental or host-related factors may interact with specific C. jejuni strains to trigger the development of GBS. Furthermore, clonal complex CC-362 was found to be locally prevalent in both Peru and South Africa. This regional pattern indicates that GBS-associated C. jejuni strains have distinct geographic prevalence, indicative of localized outbreaks. These findings are further supported by the region-specific C. jejuni outbreaks reported in 2019 in Peru and in 2007 in China13,16. These findings underscore the importance of epidemiological tracking of such pathogens to enable early local detection and help prevent future GBS outbreaks.

Phylogenomic analysis further reinforced the geographic clustering of GBS-associated C. jejuni strains. Strains were clustered clonally irrespective of the isolation year and geographical distribution. Previous studies have exhibited that not all clades in a phylogenetic tree are commonly defined by the geographical distribution or year of isolation37. LOS class A is most prevalent among the strains, followed by class B and class C. Previous studies have reported that genes involved in the sialylation that mimics ganglioside are present in LOS classes A, B, C, and R, which trigger GBS38. Strains from Bangladesh clustered into unique clades (Clade I, III-D, and III-E), characterized by the presence of LOS class B1, CJIE3, and T6SS. These strains exhibited distinct genomic signatures, indicating long-term evolutionary divergence, driven by regional host–pathogen interactions and environmental factors.

Pangenome analysis revealed that strains from Bangladesh carried more accessory genes compared to the other regions of the world, which indicates evolutionary dynamics, possibly reflecting the adaptation in hosts under selective pressures. Accessory and cloud genes, particularly enriched in Bangladeshi isolates, were often associated with flagellar variation, glycosylation pathways, and antimicrobial resistance, consistent with high recombination rates in C. jejuni39.The role of virulence genes in the pathogenesis of C. jejuni-induced GBS remains incompletely understood. In this genomic study, we found that the number of virulence genes was consistently present in all strains. A previous study reported that these genes were associated with most clinical cases of C. jejuni-associated infection40. LOS-related genes (cgtaA, cgtB, cstII, cstIII, wlaN) were associated with the human-ganglioside-like structures, which might trigger GBS41. The positive correlation between LOS-related sialylation genes (neuB2 and neuC2) and the T6SS, along with their negative correlation with the T4SS, suggests that T6SS may play a role in ganglioside mimicry and potentially acts synergistically in virulence, a hypothesis previously proposed in experimental models42.

C. jejuni integrated elements (CJIEs), derived from prophages and mobile genetic elements, are thought to facilitate horizontal gene transfer and contribute to genetic diversity43. Nearly half of the analyzed strains harbored integrated elements, with CJIE1 being the most frequently observed type. Strains from Bangladesh accounted for the majority of CJIE1-positive isolates, highlighting the high genetic diversity of C. jejuni in that region. In contrast, strains from Peru lacked CJIEs entirely, which is supported by Quino et al., reflecting a more conserved genetic profile and a potentially uniform pattern in GBS pathogenesis10. Moreover, phase variation might play a vital role in persisting prolonged infection in the host, which might cause GBS autoimmunity. This is supported by Nayeem et al. who reported higher phase variation in the LOS region of GBS-associated strains compared to enteritis-associated strains44–46. However, the detection of phages such as Escherichia phage vB_EcoM and Pseudomonas phage PPpW suggests their potential roles as vectors for gene acquisition, modulation of virulence, and inter-strain competition, consistent with previous observations6,47. Clonal complex CC-403, which is specific to the Bangladesh region, harbors a significantly higher number of simple sequence repeats (SSRs) compared to CC-362 (prevalent in Peru and South Africa) as well as the worldwide distributed clonal complexes CC-22 and CC-21. This elevated SSR count underscores the enhanced potential of CC-403 strains for rapid phenotypic adaptation, highlighting the distinctiveness of C. jejuni populations in Bangladesh relative to other regions worldwide.

A major limitation of this study is the lack of comprehensive clinical data for all examined strains, which restricts our ability to fully assess the relationship between genomic characteristics and clinical epidemiology. Moreover, the scarcity of publicly available data on C. jejuni strains poses a significant challenge for global epidemiological studies. Furthermore, comparisons between GBS-associated and non-GBS-associated C. jejuni strains could provide deeper insights into the genomic and epidemiological factors underlying disease development.

In conclusion, our study implies the global molecular epidemiology of the C. jejuni strains isolated from patients with GBS. Clonal complex CC-22 was the global disseminator of the GBS pathogenesis. Moreover, strains isolated from Bangladesh are widely diversified compared to strains isolated from other regions of the world. This study emphasizes the urgent need for the strain-level variation of C. jejuni with clinical manifestation of GBS from different regions of the world to uncover the genomic determinants associated with GBS pathogenesis.

Materials and methods

C. jejuni strain isolated from patients with GBS in Bangladesh

In this study, we included 38 C. jejuni strains isolated from stool specimens and rectal swabs of 32 patients with GBS in Bangladesh between 2006 and 2023 using standard microbiological procedures (Supplementary Data 1). The selected C. jejuni isolates were enriched for 48 h at 42 °C under micro-anaerobic conditions (85% Nitrogen, 10% carbon dioxide, and 5% oxygen) in CHROMagar and blood agar with 5% sheep blood, respectively. The identified C. jejuni isolates were confirmed by species-specific PCR. The selected C. jejuni was enriched in Brain–Heart Infusion Broth (BHIB) and stored using 30% glycerol at − 80 °C until sequencing3,46,48,49. All experiments and methods were performed in accordance with the institutional guidelines and regulations of standard microbiological procedures and good laboratory practice.

Strain selection and worldwide distribution of the GBS-associated C. jejuni

We retrospectively explored the public genome databases, including the National Center of Biotechnology Information (NCBI), and the European Molecular Biology Laboratory (EMBL) to collect all of the genome sequences of C. jejuni associated with GBS. A total of 52 C. jejuni strains were found till 16 June 2025 from nine different countries and retrieved the sequences and annotation information, including Peru (n = 22), China (n = 13), The Netherlands (n = 6), USA (n = 4), South Africa (n = 3), Germany (n = 1), Mexico (n = 1), Japan (n = 1), United Kingdom (n = 1) (Supplementary Data 1). We compared the global epidemiology of the respective countries of the related strains (Table 1).

Genomic DNA extraction and sequence analysis of C. jejuni

The genomic DNA of 38 C. jejuni strains was extracted using the Wizard genomic DNA purification kit (Promega) as per instructions provided by the manufacturer48. The quality of DNA was checked using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA) and quantified using a Qubit 2.0 fluorometer (Life Technologies). The QuantiFluor ONE double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) system was used to fulfill the sample quality requirements (quantity, 10 μg; concentration, N50, < 200 ng/μg). All the C. jejuni strains were subjected to next-generation sequencing (NGS) using the NextSeq 500 system (Illumina platform). The high-quality sequencing library was prepared using the Illumina Nextera XT DNA library preparation kit (catalog number FC-131–1024). The NextSeq v2.5 reagent kit was used for high-throughput sequencing of C. jejuni strains. FastQC v0.12.1 was used to performed the quality checks on the paired-end sequencing reads (150 bp)50. Adapter trimming and quality trimming were conducted using fastp v0.23.4, respectively51,52. De novo genome assembly was performed using SPAdes v4.0.053,54. The genome sequences of the strains were annotated using NCBI Prokaryotic Genome Annotation Pipeline (PGAP) v5.155. Subsequent annotations were executed using Prokka v1.14.5 and RAST56,57.

Identification, characterization and phylogenetic analysis of C. jejuni

Sequence types (STs), and clonal complexes (CCs) were identified by submitting genome sequences of C. jejuni to the PubMLST server (https://pubmlst.org/)58. Virulence genes were determined by using the Virulence Factor Database (VFDB, http://www.mgc.ac.cn/VFs/)59 by using C. jejuni NCTC11168 as a reference strain. LOS (cgtA, cgtB, wlaN, cstII, and cstIII) genes were searched using the BLASTp tool60. LOS class was considered if ≥ 95% of the query sequences were mapped against reference LOS gene sequences and had a ≥ 95% sequence identity of the reference gene sequences. At the 51th position of the cstII gene, Asparagine (Asn) or Threonine (Thr) were confirmed by the BLASTp. Genomic islands containing C. jejuni integrated elements (CJIEs) were identified considering RM1221 as a reference, as RM1221 carried five (CJIE1, CJIE2, CJIE3, CJIE4, CJIE5) genomic islands. CJIE1, CJIE2, CJIE3, and CJIE4 were extracted according to the methodology described elsewhere6, and CJIE5 was extracted as described by Skarp et al.61. BLASTn was conducted to identify CJIEs from the strains. As integrated elements were segregated into different contigs, so quarry coverage was considered present if > 50% and had a > 90% sequence identity. To identify potential prophagic regions, PHASTER (PHAge Search Tool Enhanced Release) was used62 to predict the region, while intact regions were only considered. For identifying antimicrobial genes, sequence assemblies were screened by the NCBI AMRFinderPlus v3.12.863 and BacANt v3.3.364. SNP-based maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree was constructed using RAxML v865 in the GTR + G model. However, Interactive Tree Of Life (iTOL) v766 was used to visualize the phylogenetic tree.

Pangenomic diversity of C. jejuni

The pangenome of C. jejuni was constructed from 90 GBS-associated strains considering ICDCC7001 as a reference sequence using Roary v3.11.267 with a minimum BLASTp identity threshold of 90%. Pangenome visualization was performed using Phandango68. The figure was generated based on the gene presence and absence generated by Roary. Flower plots were subsequently constructed by the SRplot69 (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn).

Identification of phase variation in GBS-associated C. jejuni isolates

Phase variation (PV) in C. jejuni strains associated with GBS was analyzed using the PhasomeIt v1.1.5 tool for rapid identification of simple sequence repeats (SSR)-mediated PV with the location, type and repeat number of every SSR. Cut-off values were set to 7 6 0 5 5 to exclude trinucleotide repeats unlikely to induce frameshift mutations responsible for PV70. Additionally, all A/T tracts shorter than 9 base pairs were filtered out using the ‘-f W9’ parameter, as shorter A/T repeats are less prone to generating phase-variable effects.

Statistical analysis and visualisation

The geographical distribution of reported GBS-associated C. jejuni isolates was generated through the Tableau Public platform (https://www.tableau.com). Statistical differences across clonal complexes were examined and depicted based on phase variation using Mann–Whitney test in GraphPad Prism v9. A Pearson correlation matrix (− 1 represents the negative correlation and + 1 represents the positive correlation) was constructed to examine the associations among various virulence genes. Data cleaning and import were performed using the dplyr and readxl packages, correlation coefficients were calculated with the metan package, and visualizations were generated using base R (v. 4.4.1) plotting functions. Different repeat types and tract lengths were visualized in a heatmap using GraphPad Prism v9. P-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.
