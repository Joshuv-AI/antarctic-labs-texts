Transcriptome analysis reveals key pathways and regulatory networks involved in soybean’s early resistance to Peronospora manshurica
Authors: Biao Xu, Guangxun Qi, Xiaodong Liu, Cuiping Yuan, Yumin Wang, Yidan Li, Jian Zhang, Hongkun Zhao, Yingshan Dong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSoybean downy mildew (SDM), caused by the obligate parasite Peronospora manshurica (Pm), is a global fungal disease that significantly reduces soybean yield and quality by inducing early leaf drop and decreasing both 100-seed weight and seed oil content. Therefore, investigating the molecular mechanisms underlying early resistance to Pm infection in soybean, along with its key regulatory networks, is of paramount importance for developing effective strategies to combat Pm invasion.ResultsThis study employed transcriptome sequencing at six time points before and after Pm inoculation in a highly resistant (HR) soybean accession, Jiaohe xiaoheidou (JH), and a highly susceptible (HS) accession, Jilin 5 (JL). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in both accessions were assigned to 58 transcription factors (TFs) families, including the v-myb avian myeloblastosis viral oncogene homolog (MYB), WRKY domain-containing transcription factor (WRKY) and basic leucine zipper (bZIP). The mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway-plant and plant-pathogen interaction was significantly enriched in both the transcriptome analysis and the blue module of weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA). Additionally, isoflavonoid biosynthesis was also enriched in the transcriptome analysis, while flavonoid biosynthesis and phenylalanine metabolism were enriched in the WGCNA blue module, both of which are associated with the phenylpropanoid metabolic pathway. Furthermore, six hub genes were identified within the specificity module of WGCNA.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that soybean resistance to Pm involves multiple TF families and diverse metabolic pathways. These findings provide novel insights into the molecular regulatory network governing soybean resistance to Pm and lay the groundwork for further investigation of the molecular mechanisms underlying SDM resistance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07335-7.Keywords: Soybean, Peronospora manshurica, Transcriptome analysis, Early resistance, Key pathways and regulatory networks

Introduction

Soybean (Glycine max L.) occupies a central position in agricultural production as one of the most important protein and oilseed crops worldwide [1, 2]. However, its growth is frequently threatened by a wide range of pathogens, including bacteria, fungi and viruses, which severely affect both yield and quality. Among these, SDM caused by the fungus Pm, is a relatively common and destructive disease [3, 4]. SDM is prevalent across all soybean-producing regions globally, with particularly high incidence and severity reported in northeastern and northern China due to cool temperatures and high humidity.

Pm is an obligate biotrophic fungus that initiates infestation on soybean leaves. Under favourable environmental conditions, it produces sporocarps that spread to nearby plants by wind and rain. Additionally, the mycelium can infect the seeds, allowing the pathogen to be transmitted to the next generation along with the seeds [5]. Current control strategies for SDM include cultural practices, chemical treatments, and breeding for disease-resistant cultivars. For instance, application of potassium phosphite significantly suppressed SDM development, with disease severity decreasing linearly as the concentrations of P2O5 + K2O increased, compared to untreated and low-dose treatments [6]. However, long-term chemical use may lead to the development of pathogen resistance, reduced control efficacy, environmental contamination, and negative effects on future crops [7]. Therefore, the development of resistant soybean varieties has become an important approach for managing the disease sustainably.

Mining disease resistance genes remains a focal point in molecular breeding research to combat downy mildew. A wide array of resistance (R) genes, that recognise specific pathogen effectors, has been identified, highlighting their essential role in plant immunity [8]. In the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana, 149 proteins with nucleotide-binding site-leucine-rich repeat (NBS-LRR) domains have been identified, among which at least 28 confer resistance to Peronospora parasitica, referred to as Recognition of Peronospora parasitica (RPP) genes [9, 10]. In spinach, there exist six resistance loci (Resistance against Peronospora farinosa, RPF1-RPF6) that confer resistance to Peronospora farinosa [11]. In addition to R genes, TF families play critical roles in regulating plant immune response. For example, overexpression of VvWRKY1 enhanced downy mildew resistance in grapevine, transcriptome differential expression analysis revealed 160 DEGs involved in various defence-related pathways, including the jasmonic acid-signalling pathway [12].

Despite these advances, relatively few studies have focused on identifying key genes and elucidating the molecular response mechanisms of soybean to Pm infection. A hybrid population derived from the resistant cultivar AGS129 and the susceptible line Nakhon Sawan 1 (NS1) was used, and a dominant resistance gene (Rpmx) closely linked to SDM resistance was identified using random amplified polymorphic DNA (RAPD) markers. This gene is located within the 4.9–23.1 cM region [13]. RNA-seq was used to analyse the transcriptomic differences between resistant and susceptible genotypes, identifying 52 DEGs involved in defense responses against SDM [14]. They further analysed the expression of WRKY TFs and used yeast single hybridization (Y1H) assays to confirm that GmWRKY31 binds the W-box element in the promoter region of GmSAGT1. This suggested that GmWRKY31 may regulate the expression of the GmSAGT1 gene and participate in salicylic acid (SA)-mediated immune responses in soybean [15].

RNA-seq is a powerful high-throughput for method profiling RNA molecules in cells or tissues to reveal gene expression patterns [16]. WGCNA is a systematic biology approach used to construct gene co-expression networks from transcriptome data. It is widely applied in bioinformatics to identify modules and hub genes associated with specific traits [17]. Integrating RNA-seq with WGCNA enables the identification of key genes and signaling pathways involved in plant responses to biotic stresses, providing insights into the molecular regulatory networks underlying disease resistance [18]. This integrative approach has been successfully used to study resistance mechanisms in various crops, including resistance to soybean mosaic virus [19] and soybean rust [20], gray mold in woodland strawberry (Fragaria vesca) caused by Botrytis cinerea [21], and bacterial wilt in eggplant (Solanum melongena) [22]. However, the application of WGCNA to identify gene modules associated with resistance to Pm in soybean remains unexplored.

In this study, we employed the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to sequence the transcriptomes of a resistant soybean accession (Jiaohe xiaoheidou, JH) and a susceptible accession (Jilin 5, JL) at 0 h (control), 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h post-infection with Pm. A total of 36 cDNA libraries were constructed and sequenced. We combined RNA-seq with WGCNA to investigate the transcriptomic responses of JH and JL to Pm infection and to identify co-expression modules and hub genes associated with disease resistance. This approach may offer valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying Pm resistance in soybean and contribute to the development of strategies for the genetic improvement and effective management of downy mildew.

Results

Assessment of susceptibility in soybean accessions JH and JL to Peronospora manshurica

Disease symptoms were evaluated 14 days post-inoculation with Pm (Fig. 1). The resistant accession JH retained its green foliage with no visible symptoms, resulting in a disease index of 0. In contrast, the susceptible accession JL exhibited pronounced chlorosis and yellowing of leaves, with a disease index of 100. Based on these observations, JH was classified as highly resistant and JL as highly susceptible. To monitor the progression of early infection, phenotypic changes in JH and JL leaves were observed at 0 h (control), 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h post-inoculation (Fig. 2). No significant visual symptoms were observed in either accession from 0 to 48 h. However, by 72 h, slight chlorosis and discoloration became evident in JL leaves, while JH remained unaffected. These results suggest that the period from 0 to 72 h represents the early phase of soybean downy mildew infection.

Analysis of RNA-Seq data

RNA-seq analysis was conducted on leaf Samples from the soybean accession JH and JL collected at 0 h (control), 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h post-Pm inoculation. A total of 36 cDNA Libraries were constructed and sequenced to obtain 227.25 Gb of raw bases and 223.81 Gb of high-quality clean bases after data filtering. Each Sample generated more than 5.85 Gb of data, with GC content ranging from 40.62–45.2%, and Q30 values exceeding 95.43%, indicating high sequencing quality (Additional file: Table S1). Clean reads were aligned to the reference genome Glycine max (Williams82, v2.1), with overall mapping efficiencies ranging from 76.54–95.22%. Reads uniquely mapped to the reference genome accounted for 72.07–92.0% of the data and were used for downstream quantitative analyses (Additional file: Table S1). These results confirmed that the RNA-seq data were of high quality and suitable for further analysis.

Gene expression levels were quantified using Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads (FPKM), which normalize for both sequencing depth and gene length [23]. The distribution of FPKM values across all 36 samples was consistent and showed no major deviations, as illustrated by box plots (Additional file: Figure S1). Furthermore, Pearson correlation analysis among biological replicates showed squared correlation coefficients (R2) exceeding 0.945 (Additional file: Figure S2), surpassing the ENCODE project’s recommended threshold of R2 > 0.92 [24]. These results indicate high reproducibility among replicates and reliability of the experimental design.

Transcriptome analysis of soybean inoculated with Peronospora manshurica

The DEGs were identified in both accessions at different time points after inoculation with Pm compared to control plants that were not inoculated with Pm (JH_0h and JL_0h). Across five treatment time points (6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h post-inoculation), a total of 58,129 DEGs were identified in JH and 64,963 DEGs in JL. In JH, 26,290 genes were up-regulated and 31,839 were down-regulated, whereas in JL, 30,409 were up-regulated and 34,554 were down-regulated. In JH, the number of up-regulated DEGs at 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h was 5,481; 5,137; 7,779; 3,122; and 4,771, respectively. The corresponding numbers of down-regulated DEGs were 6,794; 6,647; 8,934; 4,090; and 5,374. In JL, 5,649; 5,776; 6,343; 6,734; and 5,907 genes were up-regulated at the respective time points, while 5,890; 6,250; 8,290; 6,539; and 7,585 genes were down-regulated (Additional file: Table S2).

Transcription factor analysis

TFs play a crucial role in the organism’s response to biological stress. By finely regulating gene expression, TFs help organisms to rapidly adapt to and resist external threats [25]. This study TFs were identified among DEGs across five post-inoculation time points in both JH and JL soybean accessions. A total of 17,677 DEGs were annotated as TFs, belonging to 58 TF families (Additional file: Table S3). Among these, 11 families including Basic Helix-Loop-Helix (bHLH), MYB-related transcription factor (MYB-related), NAM, ATAF1/2, CUC2 (NAC), Cys2His2-type zinc finger protein (C2H2), Ethylene Response Factor (ERF), WRKY, Cys3His-type zinc finger protein (C3H), Fatty Acid Reductase-related transcription factor (FAR), B3 domain-containing transcription factor (B3), bZIP, and MYB, each accounted for more than 3% of the total TFs. The remaining 44 TF families, including M-type MADS-box transcription factors (M-type MADS), Gibberellic Acid-Insensitive (GAI), Repressor of gal (RGA), and Scarecrow (SCR) transcription factors (GRAS), and G2-like transcription factors (G2-like), each contributed less than 3%. Notably, MYB, WRKY, and bZIP families, which are known to be involved in plant defense responses [26–28], were represented by 1,722, 930, and 626 TFs, respectively. These TFs are likely to play crucial roles in the response of soybean to Pm infection.

Differential gene expression in resistant and susceptible leaves under Peronospora manshurica infection

To elucidate the molecular basis of resistance and susceptibility to Pm, DEGs displaying contrasting expression patterns between the resistant accession (JH) and the susceptible accession (JL) were analyzed. Genes up-regulated in JH but down-regulated in JL were considered resistance-related, while those downregulated in JH and upregulated in JL were categorized as susceptibility-related.

To explore the biological function of Pm-induced DEGs induced in soybean susceptible accessions, Kyoto Encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was performed for up-regulated and down-regulated DEGs of JH and JL at five time points under Pm infection (Additional file: Table S4, S5). In JH, infection triggered the upregulation of numerous genes associated with immune activation and resistance. These DEGs were significantly enriched in key defense-related pathways, including the MAPK signaling pathway (gmx04016), plant-pathogen interaction (gmx04626), circadian rhythm-plant (gmx04712), and isoflavonoid biosynthesis (gmx00943). Conversely, in JL, many genes were downregulated following infection, potentially indicating a lack of proper defense activation. These DEGs which may also contribute to resistance through negative regulation of susceptibility, were enriched in pathways such as DNA replication (gmx03030), motor proteins (gmx04814), porphyrin metabolism (gmx00860), and biotin metabolism (gmx00780). In the susceptibility-related genome, DEGs in JL were co-enriched in the MAPK signaling pathway (gmx04016), plant-pathogen interaction (gmx04626), isoflavonoid biosynthesis (gmx00943), valine, leucine, and isoleucine degradation (gmx00280), endocytosis (gmx04144), and peroxisome (gmx04146). Additionally, JH-specific downregulation of motor proteins (gmx04814) was observed across all time points. This study highlights three core pathways, MAPK signaling pathway, plant-pathogen interaction, and isoflavonoid biosynthesis, as central components of soybean’s response to Pm infection.

Identification of DEGs involved in MAPK signaling pathway

The MAPK signaling pathway in plants plays a crucial role not only in response to various abiotic stresses but also in sensing pathogen attacks and activating downstream defense responses [29–31]. In JH up-regulation of DEGs was observed across all five time points following Pm infection (6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h post-inoculation). Specifically, 89, 80, 115, 47, and 75 genes were associated with the MAPK signaling pathway at these respective time points (Additional file: Table S4). A total of 31 DEGs were consistently involved in the MAPK signaling pathway across the five time points (Additional file: Table S6). Among them, 25 genes were significantly up-regulated in both JH and JL although expression levels differed between the accessions. The remaining six genes were either downregulated or showed no significant expression change in JL at one or more time points.

Identifications of DEGs involved in plant-pathogen interaction

In the resistant genotype JH, several differentially expressed genes (DEGs) associated with plant-pathogen interaction were upregulated at five time points following Pm infection. A total of 70, 65, 115, 41, and 83 DEGs were identified at each respective time point (Additional file: Table S4). Across these time points, 18 DEGs were consistently involved in the plant-pathogen interaction pathway (Additional file: Table S7). Among them, 11 genes were upregulated in both JH and the susceptible genotype JL, although the expression levels varied. The remaining seven genes were either downregulated or showed no significant difference in expression at one or more time points in JL.

In JL the upregulation of DEGs in response to Pm infection was also observed at the Same five time points, with 83, 72, 74, 100, and 82 genes involved in the plant-pathogen interaction pathway, respectively (Additional file: Table S5). A total of 43 DEGs were identified as commonly involved in this pathway across all five time points (Additional file: Table S7). Of these, 11 genes were upregulated in both genotypes, while 32 genes were either downregulated or not significantly expressed at one or more time points in JH. Notably, the gene Glyma.10G274400, encoding 3-ketoacyl-CoA synthase 6, was significantly downregulated in JH at 12 h post-infection. This gene may negatively regulate Pm resistance by influencing the biosynthesis or activity of the encoded enzyme. However, the precise role of this gene in plant defense requires further investigation.

Identification of DEGs involved in isoflavonoid biosynthesis

In JH, genes involved in the isoflavonoid biosynthesis pathway were also upregulated at all five time points following Pm infection. Specifically, 11, 14, 26, 10, and 19 DEGs were detected at 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h, respectively (Additional file: Table S4). Six DEGs were shared across all time points (Additional file: Table S8), with five showing consistent upregulation in both JH and JL, albeit with differing expression levels. In addition, Glyma.09G269600, encoding bifunctional dihydroflavonol 4-reductase/flavanone 4-reductase-like, exhibited significant upregulation at 48 and 72 h in JL, while no significant change was observed at earlier time points. This gene may play a role in the biosynthesis of defense-related secondary metabolites, contributing to resistance against Pm in JH.

In JL upregulated DEGs associated with isoflavonoid biosynthesis were also detected at the Same time points, with 11, 12, 14, 23, and 13 genes identified at 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h, respectively (Additional file: Table S5). Nine DEGs were found to be involved in the pathway across all time points (Additional file: Table S8). Of these, five were significantly upregulated in both genotypes, while four exhibited downregulation or no significant expression change in JH at one or more time points.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis

WGCNA was conducted on samples from both JH and JL across six time points (36 Samples total, 12 subgroups). The optimal soft-thresholding power was determined to be β = 10, with a scale-free topology fit index (R2) greater than 0.8 and average connectivity approaching zero (Fig. 3A, B), indicating suitability for constructing a scale-free network. A total of 33,255 genes were used to construct gene dendrograms and co-expression modules. Based on FPKM values, genes with similar expression patterns were grouped using dynamic tree cutting, resulting in 24 distinct modules (Fig. 3C), each represented by a unique color. The largest module, turquoise, contained 8017 genes, while the smallest, darkturquoise, included 86 genes. The remaining modules ranged in size from 90 to 4478 genes. Genes that could not be assigned to any specific module were grouped into the grey module, which contained 5 genes (Additional file: Table S9).

Significant correlation module screening and Gene ontology and KEGG enrichment analysis

Since genes within the same module exhibit similar expression patterns, they are likely to be functionally related. Based on correlation analyses between module eigengenes and sample traits across six time points of Pm infection, three modules were found to be significantly positively correlated with resistance in JH (p < 0.01) (Additional file: Figure S3). These included the purple module associated with the uninfected control (0 h) (r = 0.91, p = 0.00005), the dark red module correlated with 6 h post-infection (r = 0.90, p = 0.00007), and the blue module correlated with 24 h post-infection (r = 0.83, p = 0.00007). The genes in these modules may play critical roles in the activation of defense mechanisms in the resistant genotype JH following pathogen infection. Similarly, six modules were significantly positively correlated with the susceptible genotype JL (p < 0.01). These included the turquoise (r = 0.80, p = 0.002) and green (r = 0.71, p = 0.009) modules at 0 h (control), the cyan module at 6 h (r = 0.93, p = 0.00002), the Light-green module at 12 h (r = 0.73, p = 0.007), the Salmon module at 24 h (r = 0.94, p = 0.00004), and the grey module at 48 h (r = 0.74, p = 0.006). These modules likely contain genes contributing to the susceptible response observed in JL upon Pm infection. To identify key modules associated with Pm resistance in JH, we focused on the dark red (113 genes) and blue (4478 genes) modules, corresponding to 6h and 24 h post-infection, respectively (Additional file: Figure S4, S5).

Functional enrichment analyses were conducted to elucidate the biological roles of these two modules. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis (Additional file: Figure S6, S7; Table S10) revealed that genes in the dark red module were significantly enriched in terms related to intracellular protein transport, cellular protein localization, and macromolecule localization. Genes in the blue module were enriched in sequence-specific DNA binding functions.

Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis (Fig. 4; Additional file: Table S10) indicated that the blue module was significantly enriched in multiple pathways, including valine, leucine and isoleucine degradation (gmx00280), MAPK signaling pathway (gmx04016), plant-pathogen interaction (gmx04626), autophagy-other (gmx04136), propanoate metabolism (gmx00640), lysine degradation (gmx00310), beta-alanine metabolism (gmx00410), ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis (gmx04120), flavonoid biosynthesis (gmx00941), and phenylalanine metabolism (gmx00360), among others. No significant KEGG pathways were enriched in the dark red module.

Analysis of transcriptome and key pathways of WGCNA KEGG enrichment

To further investigate the regulatory mechanisms of KEGG pathways significantly enriched following Pm infection, we performed an integrated analysis of transcriptomic data and WGCNA. Genes commonly involved in enriched pathways were identified, with particular attention to those upregulated in the JH treatment group and either downregulated or not significantly altered in the JL treatment group at one or more time points. These genes were considered key candidates for further investigation.

In the MAPK signaling pathway (gmx04016), transcriptome analysis revealed that seven DEGs were upregulated in the JH group across five infection time points (Additional file: Table S6). In contrast, the same genes in the JL group were either downregulated or showed no significant expression change in at least one treatment condition. The WGCNA blue module was significantly enriched in this pathway (Additional file: Table S10), and three genes were co-identified in both analyses: Glyma.08G083300 encoding the LRR receptor-like serine/threonine-protein kinase (Flagellin sensing2, FLS2), functions as a pattern recognition receptor (PRR) that detects pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and initiates immune responses; Glyma.07G105700, encoding mitogen-activated protein kinase 9, is a critical component of the MAPK signaling cascade; and Glyma.04G147000, encoding EIN3-binding F-box protein 1, mediates crosstalk between ethylene and MAPK signaling by regulating EIN3 protein stability. These genes are likely to play central roles in soybean resistance to downy mildew through MAPK-ethylene pathway interactions.

In the plant-pathogen interaction (gmx04626), six DEGs were upregulated in the JH group across five infection time points (Additional file: Table S7). Similar to the MAPK pathway, these genes were either downregulated or unchanged in JL. The WGCNA blue module also showed significant enrichment in this pathway. Four genes were highlighted: Glyma.08G083300 FLS2 as a PRR; Glyma.12G133700 and Glyma.19G255300, encoding cyclic nucleotide-gated ion channels (CNGC4 and CNGC1), which likely enhance immune responses via calcium signaling; and Glyma.14G205600, encoding PTI6, a transcriptional activator of pathogenesis-related genes. These genes collectively contribute to defense signaling and immune activation against Pm.

Transcriptomic data also showed significant enrichment of upregulated DEGs in the JH group in the isoflavonoid biosynthesis pathway (gmx00943) during all five time points. Additionally, WGCNA revealed that the blue module was significantly enriched in flavonoid biosynthesis (gmx00941) and phenylalanine metabolism (gmx00360). These three pathways are closely related to the phenylpropane metabolic pathway. The phenylpropane metabolic pathway is a crucial secondary metabolic pathway in plants that provides the basis for the synthesis of a wide range of disease resistance-related compounds (e.g., lignans, flavonoids, and isoflavonoids), thus enhancing plant defences against pathogenic bacteria [32].

Hub gene screening

Genes with the highest intramodular connectivity (kWithin) were considered potential Hub genes. The top 10 hub genes from the dark red and blue modules were selected (Additional file: Table S11), the same method of screening hub genes was also adopted in the transcriptome analysis of two genotypes of pepper infected with mild mottle virus [33].

In the dark red module, the hub genes include Glyma.08G211700 (phospholipase D alpha 1), Glyma.09G285100 (MAP65/ASE1 microtubule-associated protein), Glyma.18G064900 (peptide transporter 1), Glyma.13G217900 (alpha-mannosidase 3), Glyma.06G292400 (glycosyl hydrolase), Glyma.16G107500 (pectinacetylesterase), Glyma.17G256500 (B3 transcription factor/AUX/IAA-related protein), Glyma.13G346000 (protein of unknown function), and two serine/threonine-protein kinases: Glyma.03G011000 and Glyma.04G205400.

In the blue module, the hub genes include Glyma.01G017700 (PLATZ transcription factor), Glyma.07G028600 (glycerol kinase), Glyma.01G002100 (B3 transcription factor/AUX/IAA-related), Glyma.08G065000 (octicosapeptide/phox/bem1p protein), Glyma.15G198900 (serine-rich protein), Glyma.09G185900 (aspartyl protease), Glyma.06G162200 (chloroplastic chaperone protein ClpD), Glyma.15G267700 (major facilitator superfamily protein), LOC112998759 (protein of unknown function), and Glyma.16G136600, which encodes a TIR-NBS-LRR class disease resistance protein with NB-ARC and TIR domains. These genes, with diverse molecular functions, may play essential roles in soybean response to Pm infection.

Validation of Hub gene expression via qRT-PCR

To validate the RNA-seq results, nine candidate hub genes from the dark red and blue modules selected based on their functional annotation, were subjected to qRT-PCR analysis (Fig. 5). The qRT-PCR results were consistent with RNA-seq findings, confirming the reliability of the transcriptomic data. Notably, Glyma.01G017700, Glyma.09G185900, Glyma.15G267700, and Glyma.16G136600 showed markedly higher expression levels in the JH group 24 h post-Pm infection compared to other time points and the JL group at 24 h. Similarly, Glyma.03G011000 and Glyma.04G205400 displayed significantly elevated expression in JH at 6 h post-infection compared to other periods and JL.

Discussion

Although numerous transcriptome studies have explored the interactions between pathogenic bacteria and plants, research specifically focusing on the transcriptomic responses of soybean to downy mildew infection remains limited. In recent years, the rapid development of high-throughput sequencing technologies significantly facilitated the application of RNA-Seq in identifying disease resistance genes and elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying plant-pathogen interactions [34].

In this study, RNA-Seq analysis was conducted at five time points post-Pm infection. A total of 5481, 5137, 7779, 3122 and 4771 DEGs were identified in JH, respectively, while 6794, 6647, 8934, 4090 and 5374 DEGs were down-regulated. In JL, 5649, 5776, 6343, 6734 and 5907 DEGs were upregulated, and 5890, 6250, 8290, 6539 and 7585 DEGs were down-regulated, respectively (Additional file: Table S2). These results reveal dynamic changes in gene expression over time, reflecting the temporal complexity of the plant's defense responses. For example, JH exhibited the highest number of upregulated 7779 DEGs at 24 h, suggesting a peak in defense-related gene activation at this time point. Conversely, the sharp decline to 3122 upregulated DEGs at 48 h may indicate a waning defense response or a shift in the plant's defense strategy. Downregulated DEGs also exhibited temporal variation, supporting the notion of a flexible gene regulation system post-inoculation. JL displayed a more stable pattern of DEG expression across time points, suggesting a distinct regulatory strategy compared to JH. Notably, JL had a higher number of upregulated 6734 DEGs than JH 3122 DEGs at 48 h implying possible differences in defense gene activation or stress tolerance mechanisms.

To identify key genes associated with resistance or susceptibility, DEGs exhibiting opposite expression patterns between JH and JL were analyzed. Upregulated DEGs in JH and down-regulated in JL were defined as resistance-responsive, while the reverse were considered susceptibility-responsive. KEGG enrichment analysis of these gene sets (Additional file: Table S4, S5) revealed the involvement of several critical pathways, including MAPK signaling (gmx04016), plant-pathogen interaction (gmx04626), isoflavonoid biosynthesis (gmx00943) and TFs, which are likely to form an integrated regulatory network during the immune response. These pathways will be discussed in more detail later.

TFs regulate gene expression by binding to specific DNA sequences, typically within gene promoters or enhancers, and play pivotal roles in biological process such as cell differentiation, proliferation, and response to stress [35]. Our analysis identified numerous DEGs annotated as TFs across all five time points in both JH and JL. These were categorized into several families, notably WRKY, MYB, and bZIP, highlighting the extensive and multifaceted role of TFs in plant defense regulation [36].

The WRKY family, in particular, is well known for its involvement in biotic stress responses. WRKY81, for example, has been reported to regulate soybean resistance to downy mildew by binding to (W-box) upstream of the GmSAGT1 transcription start site by transcriptomic sequencing and yeast monoclonal method [15]. Similarly, PtrWRKY73 from poplar was shown to enhance resistance to biotrophic pathogens while repressing responses to necrotrophs, suggesting involvement in SA mediated defense pathways [37]. MYB and bZIP TFs also contribute significantly to plant immunity. For example, GmMYB29A2 enhances soybean resistance to Phytophthora through the regulation of glyceollin biosynthesis [38], and GhMYB33 modulates the growth-defense balance in cotton [39]. Moreover, overexpression of GmbZIP15 in soybean improved resistance to Sclerotinia sclerotiorum and Phytophthora sojae by regulating antioxidant and hormone signaling pathways [28]. In this study, 1722 and 930 DEGs were identified as MYB and WRKY TFs (Additional file: Table S3), suggesting that these TFs play critical regulatory roles in the response to Pm infection. Future functional studies involving gene knockout and overexpression are necessary to validate their specific roles and provide a foundation for resistance breeding in soybean.

The WGCNA is a powerful systems biology method for identifying correlations between gene modules and phenotypic traits [40]. It is especially useful for dissecting complex regulatory relationships across multiple developmental stages or treatment conditions. Previous studies have used WGCNA to analysis, the resistance mechanism of resistant and susceptible sesame [41], Actinidia chinensis [42], and Aconitum carmichaelii [43]. Despite these advances, no WGCNA-based studies have been conducted specifically to identify Pm resistance-related genes in soybean.

In the WGCNA analysis of this study, the blue module was significantly enriched in 10 KEGG pathways, including valine, leucine, and isoleucine degradation (gmx00280), MAPK signaling pathway-plant (gmx04016), and plant-pathogen interaction (gmx04626) (Fig. 4; Additional file: Table S10). Importantly, MAPK signaling pathway (gmx04016) and plant-pathogen interaction pathways were also identified through comparative transcriptome analysis, reinforcing their central role in soybean defense against Pm. From these two modules, six hub genes were identified based on expression patterns functional annotations (Additional file: Table S11), and their expression was validated via qRT-PCR (Fig. 5). Glyma.01G017700, Glyma.09G185900, Glyma.15G267700, Glyma.16G136600 showed a significant expression increase in JH at 24 h, while Glyma.03G011000 and Glyma.04G205400 were upregulated at 6 h. These genes may be involved in early and time-specific immune responses. Their differential expression between resistant and susceptible varieties suggests a regulatory role in activating defense responses, and they represent promising targets for functional characterization and resistance breeding.

The MAPK signaling pathway is a complex signal transduction system that transmits and amplifies extracellular signals through a cascade of kinase interactions and phosphorylation events. It regulates plant growth, development, and responses to environmental stresses [44–46]. In this study, the number of DEGs involved in the MAPK signaling pathway varied between disease-resistant genotype JH and susceptible genotype JL across five time points. In JH, 89, 80, 115, 47, and 75 DEGs were identified, respectively (Additional file: Table S4), whereas in JL, 93, 83, 82, 95, and 90 DEGs were identified (Additional file: Table S5). This suggests temporal and genotype-specific recruitment of genes in this pathway, reflecting the dynamic and complex nature of plant defense. These findings are consistent with previous studies, such as the transcriptomic analysis of early resistance responses to Soybean Mosaic Virus in soybean [47].

The MAPK pathway responds to diverse upstream signals including pathogen infection and attack, phytohormones, cold, drought, osmotic stress, ozone, and wounding [31]. Notably, two primary MAPK-activating pathways in plant immunity include the flg22 and hydrogen peroxide pathways. FLS2, a pattern recognition receptor (PRR), detects PAMPs and activates the MAPK cascade upon forming a heterodimer with co-receptor BAK1 [20, 48]. This activation initiates downstream signaling that regulates the transcription of defense-related genes and induces antimicrobial compound production.

Transcriptome data showed significant enrichment of up-regulated DEGs in JH within both the MAPK signaling pathway (gmx04016) and the plant-pathogen interaction pathway (gmx04626). Consistent results were also obtained from WGCNA analysis, particularly in the blue module. Notably, Glyma.08G083300, encoding an LRR receptor-like serine/threonine-protein kinase (FLS2), was identified as significantly upregulated in JL at 24 and 72 h, potentially representing an early immune marker. Additionally, genes associated with ethylene (ET) signaling were enriched within the MAPK pathway. For instance, Glyma.07G105700 (MAPKK9) may activate downstream defense genes, and Glyma.04G147000, encoding an EIN3-binding F-box protein 1, likely modulates the stability of EIN3 via ubiquitin-mediated proteasomal degradation. These results suggest a potential crosstalk between MAPK and ET signaling pathways, collectively contributing to enhanced defense responses against downy mildew in soybean.

The plant-pathogen interaction pathway outlines the molecular mechanism governing pathogen recognition, signal transduction, and host defense. It includes two key innate immune systems: Pattern-triggered immunity (PTI) and effector-triggered immunity (ETI) [49, 50]. PTI is initiated by membrane-bound pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) recognizing PAMPs, whereas ETI is mediated by intracellular nucleotide-binding leucine-rich repeat (NLR) that detect pathogen effectors [51–53]. Numerous studies support the relevance of this pathway in various crops. For instance, Zhang et al. [54] analyzed pecan infected with pecan mosaic virus (PMV) by transcriptome and found 153 DEGs associated with plant-pathogen interaction pathways. Xiao et al. [55] demonstrated through transcriptome analysis that plant-pathogen interaction plays an important role in the response of eggplant to Ralstonia solanacearum. Wang et al. [49] revealed the underlying immune response mechanism of fungal (Penicillium oxalicum) disease through transcriptome analysis. Similar conclusions were also obtained in Gastrodia elata f. glauca. It also further elucidates the importance and universality of plant-pathogen interaction in plant response to pathogenic bacteria.

In this study, up-regulated DEGs in the disease-resistant genotype JH were enriched in the plant-pathogen interaction pathway at all five time points following Pm infection. A set of 18 DEGs were commonly upregulated at all time points (Additional file: Table S7). Among these, 11 genes showed differential expression in JH and JL, potentially due to differences in genotype-specific regulatory networks affecting disease resistance. Comparative transcriptome analysis revealed molecular mechanisms of peanut leaves responding to Ralstonia solanacearum and its type III secretion system mutant. The same result was obtained in the article [56].

Calcium (Ca2⁺) plays a vital role as a second messenger ion in eukaryotic signal transduction, of which Ca2⁺ inflow from the extracellular is the principal means of signal transduction [57]. For plants, the extracellular influx of Ca2⁺ is one of the first events to occur when a pathogen invades plant cells and is essential for plant immunity. In the system of plant immune responses, Ca2⁺ signals play a central role, especially in PTI and ETI responses [58]. In this complex immunomodulation process, Ca2⁺ transport and signalling are dependent on specific channels, and the CNGC is a potential class of Ca2⁺ channels in plants. Plant CNGCs, as tetramer cation channels composed of four subunits, are non-selective cation channels and molecular switches prevalent in plant cells and play an important role in plant growth and development, Ca2⁺ signaling and resistance to various stress responses [59]. Constitutive expresser of PR genes 22 mutant of Arabidopsis cpr22 (constitutive expresser of PR genes 22) mutant of Arabidopsis was identified as a chimeric mutant of AtCNGCI1 and AtCNGCI2, cpr22 plants also display spontaneous lesion formation, elevated levels of SA and heightened resistance to P. parasitica Emco5 [60]. The tomato SlCNGC16 gene negatively regulates the resistance of sclerotinia, while SlCNGC17 and SlCNGC18 genes negatively regulate the resistance of plants to necrotrophic pathogens [61]. Rice OSCNGC9 gene positively regulates resistance to rice blast [62]. In addition, calmodulin-like (CML) proteins are also important components in the Ca2⁺ signal transduction pathway, In A. thaliana, CML8, CML9 function as positive regulators in the defense against Pseudomonas syringae pv. Tomato DC3000 [63, 64]. CaCML13 plays a positive role in pepper immunity against Ralstonia solanacearum infection and forms a feedback loop with CabZIP63 [65].

In this study, transcriptome and WGCNA analyses revealed upregulation of two CNGC genes: Glyma.12G133700 (CNGC4) and Glyma.19G255300 (CNGC1), along with Glyma.14G205600, encoding PTI6, a transcriptional activator of pathogenesis-related genes. These genes likely contribute to defense signaling through calcium-dependent pathways and highlight potential targets for enhancing disease resistance.

Isoflavonoids are secondary metabolites synthesized via the phenylpropanoid pathway and are abundant in leguminous crops [66]. They play key roles in plant defense against biotic stresses. Isoflavonoid biosynthesis involves key enzymes such as dihydroflavonol 4-reductase (DFR), isoflavone synthase (IFS), and isoflavone reductase (IFR). DFR is involved in the production of anthocyanins and proanthocyanidins. Its overexpression enhances disease resistance in tobacco [67, 68]. For instance, RNA-seq analysis and comparative transcriptomic study of Asian soybean rust-resistant and susceptible soybean genotypes in 2023 found that a DFR gene, Glyma.17G173200, was upregulated in the sensitive material [20]. This result is similar to that of the present study. In this study, Glyma.09G269600 encoding a bifunctional dihydroflavonol 4-reductase/flavanone 4-reductase-like enzyme, was significantly upregulated in JL at 48 and 72 h, indicating a temporal role in the defense response. IFR enzymes contribute to isoflavone metabolism and are linked to resistance against pathogens such as Phytophthora sojae [69]. In this study, we identified glyma.11G070500 encoding an isoflavone reductase-like protein, as significantly upregulated at all five time points in JL, but not in JH at 6 h, suggesting possible genotype-specific regulation and a potential negative relationship with resistance to Pm.

IFS is an intermediate product of flavonoid biosynthetic pathway that catalyzes flavanones. It is an enzyme that initiates the first reaction of isoflavone biosynthetic pathway and is a key enzyme in this process. Previous studies have provided important insights into the functional role of IFS in soybean defense mechanisms. For instance, in 2005, research was conducted on the RNA interference effect of the IFS gene. Through the transformation of soybean cotyledon tissue by Agrobacterium rhizogenes, it was found that the IFS gene was silenced in the transformed root, the distal cotyledon was affected, isoflavone accumulation was inhibited, and soybean sensitivity to Phytophthora was enhanced [70]. This demonstrated that isoflavones contribute significantly to soybean disease resistance. Similarly, a specialized wheat-specific isoflavone synthase, TaCYP71F53, was identified in 2023 [71]. Its catalytic reaction is different from that of isoflavone synthase in legumes, revealing a new way of isoflavone biosynthesis. Isoflavone synthase (IFS) is a key enzyme in isoflavonoid synthesis and shares a common substrate with flavanone‐3‐hydroxylase (F3H) and flavone synthase II (FNS II). Moreover, CRISPR/Cas9-mediated multiple gene editing technology was used to simultaneously target GmF3H1, GmF3H2 and GmFNSII-1 in hairy roots and plants of soybean, and it was found that the increase of isoflavone content enhanced the resistance of leaves to soybean Mosaic virus [72]. In this study, the isoflavone synthase 1 Glyma.07G202300 was significantly upregulated at five time points in JL, while there was no significant difference in the expression levels at 6 and 12 h in JH. This expression pattern may be achieved by negatively regulating Pm.

In addition, the study also found that the gene encoding isoflavone-7-O-methyltransferase also negatively regulates Pm. For example, the gene encoding isoflavone-7-O-methyltransferase 6-like, glyma.20G213700, and the gene encoding isoflavone-7-O-methyltransferase 9, glyma.10G176500.

The blue module of WGCNA was significantly enriched for phenylalanine metabolism (gmx00360) and flavonoid biosynthesis (gmx00941) in KEGG pathway analysis (Fig. 4; Additional file: Table S10). Phenylalanine metabolism (gmx00360) is the upstream initiator of the phenylpropanoid metabolism pathway, whereby phenylalanine is deaminated and converted to cinnamic acid by phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) to provide the substrate for the entire phenylpropane metabolism pathway, which then drives subsequent metabolic reactions. Flavonoid biosynthesis (gmx00941) starts from the phenylpropane pathway and is based on the intermediate products produced by the phenylalanine metabolism. These intermediate products are used as raw materials, beginning with the generation of cinnamic acid from phenylalanine catalyzed by PAL, and then undergoing a series of complex enzymatic reactions to form various basic flavonoid compounds, such as flavonoids and flavonols. Isoflavone biosynthesis is an important branch of the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway [73]. Isoflavonoids are further synthesised under the action of specific enzymes using some of the intermediates of the flavonoid biosynthesis process as substrates. These flavonoid compounds, including flavonoids, flavonols, and isoflavones, are essential in plant life activities. They are widely involved in plant growth and development, and play a key role in enhancing plant resistance to stresses such as drought, low temperature, and other abiotic stresses, as well as disease resistance to biotic stresses such as pathogenic bacterial infestation. Although this study systematically characterized the transcriptome response of Pm, several important limitations remain: first, the study lacked functional validation experiments on key candidate genes (e.g., WRKY-family TFs and genes related to the MAPK pathway), which made it difficult to establish a direct causal relationship between these genes and disease-resistant phenotypes. second, the sampling time window (0–72 h) may have missed earlier or later key defense response events and did not integrate metabolomic or proteomic data to validate the actual activity of the relevant pathways. furthermore, the study only focused on host transcriptome changes, failing to analyze the dynamic expression of pathogen effector proteins in tandem or to take into account the potential impact of complex environmental factors in the field (e.g., soil microbiome).

Based on these limitations, future studies could be deepened in the following aspects: firstly, CRISPR gene editing or transgenic technology should be used to functionally validate the screened hub genes and comprehensively elucidate the molecular mechanisms of disease resistance by combining metabolomic and proteomic and other multi-omics data. secondly, it is necessary to extend the temporal resolution (e.g., 0–120 h) and carry out dual RNA-seq analyses to reveal the dynamic regulatory network of the host–pathogen interaction. Finally, the application value of these resistance genes should be evaluated under field conditions, and corresponding molecular marker-assisted breeding strategies should be developed to provide theoretical basis and technical support for the selection of soybean disease-resistant varieties.

Conclusions

Soybean exhibits a complex defense response upon Pm infection. In the early stages, PRRs detect PAMPs, initiating the plant’s PTI response, which involves ET signaling and the activation of multiple TFs. Meanwhile, NLR receptors recognize pathogen effectors, triggering ETI. These immune responses are accompanied by extensive metabolic and signaling changes, including the activation of isoflavonoid and flavonoid biosynthesis, phenylalanine metabolism, and plant-pathogen interaction pathways. Additionally, Ca2⁺ signaling and CNGC genes play key roles in defense regulation. Together, these processes form the molecular defense network of soybean against Pm invasion.

These findings provide new insights into the regulatory mechanisms of Pm resistance in soybean, contributing to a deeper understanding of the intrinsic molecular basis of downy mildew resistance. This knowledge will support the advancement of soybean disease resistance breeding and related research fields.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

In this experiment, the soybean accession Jiaohei xiaoheidou (JH) exhibiting high resistance (HR) to downy mildew and Jilin 5 (JL) showing high sensitivity (HS), were selected as experimental materials. Both accessions were obtained from the Soybean Research Institute, Jilin Academy of Agricultural Sciences. Plastic square pots (22 × 22 × 15 cm) were filled with 3 kg of soil, and two seeds were sown per hole in six holes per pot (12 seeds total). Prior to inoculation, plants were maintained under standard greenhouse conditions without strict environmental control.

Artificial inoculation of Peronospora manshurica

After the emergence of the first pair of true leaves, artificial inoculation was performed using a spore suspension of Pm (1.0 × 105/ml). After inoculation, soybean plants were covered with plastic bags for moisture retention and incubated in an RXZ intelligent artificial climate chamber (Gongzhuling). The environmental conditions were: 20–22℃ (day), 16–18℃ (night), 14-h photoperiod, and > 90% relative humidity. Leaf Samples were collected at 0 h (control), 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h post-inoculation. Each time point included three biological replicates, resulting in 36 total samples, which were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80℃.

RNA extraction and transcriptome library construction

RNA extraction, Library construction, and transcriptome sequencing were performed by Beijing Novogene Technology Co., Ltd. RNA quality was assessed using the Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer, and only samples with high integrity were used for library construction and transcriptome sequencing were performed [74]. The cDNA Library used for sequencing was constructed by Beijing Novogene Company according to the NEB general Library construction method or strand-specific library construction method, and the constructed library was sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform [75].

RNA-seq data quality control and gene expression analysis

Raw reads were quality filtered to remove adapters and low-quality sequences, resulting in clean reads. Q20 and Q30 values were calculated to assess sequencing quality [18]. Clean reads were aligned to the soybean reference genome Williams 82 (Glycine_max_v2.1) using HISAT2 (version 2.0.5). Featurecounts (version 1.5.0-p3) in the subread software was used to quantify reads mapped to each gene (including new predicted genes). Gene expression levels were calculated using FPKM method, and genes with FPKM > 1 were regarded as expressed genes [23, 76].

Differentially expressed gene analysis and functional enrichment

Differential expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2 [77], and genes with a |log2(FoldChange)|≥ 1 and adjusted p-value (padj) ≥ 0.05 were considered as the threshold standard. The resulting DEGs were subjected to GO enrichment analysis, KEGG pathway analysis, and TFs prediction to infer potential biological functions and regulatory pathways.

Analysis of gene co-expression network

The WGCNA analysis was performed on the Novogene cloud platform. Initially, low-expression genes were filtered out, and a soft-threshold power (β) was determined by raising the correlation coefficient to the power of N to construct a scale-free network. Using this threshold, a gene co-expression network was built by hierarchical clustering based on topological overlap. Modules were defined using the dynamic tree cut method, with the minimum module size set to 30 genes, and modules with correlation coefficient above 0.75 were merged to ensure genes with similar expression patterns were grouped together.

To identify modules specifically associated with disease resistance, correlation coefficients (r) and p-values were calculated between module eigengenes and infection time points. Modules with p < 0.01 were considered significantly associated with infection response [19]. The top ten genes with the highest intramodular connectivity (kWithin) in the selected modules were identified as hub genes for further functional analysis.

qRT-PCR validation of RNA-seq data

Soybean leaves were collected at 0, 6, 12, 24, 48 and 72 h post-inoculation, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80℃. Total RNA was extracted using EasyPure® Plant RNA Kit (ER301-01), and first-strand cDNA was synthesized using PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (RR047A Perfect Real Time, TaKaRa). The qRT-PCR primers were designed using the online tool primer 3 plus (https://www.primer3plus.com/) was used to design the (Additional file: Table S12). The housekeeping gene UKN2 (Glyma. 06G038500) was selected as the internal reference gene.

Reactions were conducted on a CFX48 ECO™ Real-Time PCR System (Illumina) using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (TaKaRa) in a 20 μL reaction volume, which included 0.2 µM of each primer, 60 ng of cDNA, and ultrapure water. Thermal cycling conditions were: 50℃ for 2 min, 95℃ for 5 min; followed by 40 cycles of 95℃ for 10 s, 55–60℃ for 30 s, and 72℃ for 1 min. All experiments were performed with three independent biological replicates. The relative expression levels of target genes were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method, and expression differences were analysed by GraphPad Prism8 software.
