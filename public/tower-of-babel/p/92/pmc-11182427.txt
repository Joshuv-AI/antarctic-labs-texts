Factors associated with 30-day mortality and morbidity in patients undergoing emergency colorectal surgery
Authors: Nahar A Alselaim, Muhannad Abdulrahman Alsemari, Mesnad Alyabsi, Abrar M Al-Mutairi
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUND: The 30-day period following emergency colorectal surgery (ECRS) is associated with high mortality and morbidity. There is a lack of data assessing factors associated with outcomes of ECRS in the Saudi population.OBJECTIVES: Assess factors associated with 30-day postoperative mortality and complications following ECRSDESIGN: Retrospective cohort studySETTING: Single tertiary care center, Riyadh, Saudi ArabiaPATIENTS AND METHODS: Demographic characteristics (age, sex, diagnosis, American Society of Anesthesiologists classification, pre-operative septic state, smoking, and comorbidities), operative characteristics (urgency, diverting ostomy, and procedure performed), and postoperative characteristics (length of stay, 30-day mortality, intensive care unit [ICU] admission, ICU length of stay, surgical site infection [SSI], readmission, reoperation, and complications) were collected from electronic medical records. Univariate logistic regression was used to evaluate association with the outcome measures (30-day mortality and postoperative complications). Multivariate logistic regression was applied to evaluate independent variables.MAIN OUTCOME MEASURE: Thirty-day postoperative mortality and morbiditySAMPLE SIZE: 241 patientsRESULTS: Among 241 patients, 145 (60.2%) were men, and 80 (33.2%) patients were between 50–64 years of age. The most common indication for surgery was malignancy 138 (57%). The overall complication rate was 26.6% and the 30-day mortality rate was 11.2%. Left hemicolectomy was the most commonly performed procedure, performed in 69 (28.6%) patients. Patients between the age of 65–74 had an increased odds of death within 30 days (OR 5.25 [95% CI 1.03–26.5]) on univariate analysis. Preoperative sepsis was associated with a fourfold increase in the likelihood of 30-day mortality (OR 4.44, 95% CI 1.21–16.24, P=.024) on multivariate analysis. The likelihood of hospital re-admission increased by fivefold in patients who developed a postoperative complication (OR 5.33, 95% CI 1.30–21.78, P=.02).CONCLUSION: Preoperative sepsis was independently associated with 30-day mortality in patients undergoing ECRS, while the likelihood of hospital readmission increased in patients with postoperative complications. Expeditious control of sepsis in the emergency surgical setting by both surgical and medical interventions may reduce the likelihood of postoperative mortality. Establishing discharge protocols for postoperative ECRS patients is advocated.LIMITATIONS: Retrospective design, small sample size, and single setting

INTRODUCTION

Colorectal disease comprises a wide variety of benign and malignant entities. In recent years, there has been an increased incidence of colorectal cancer, diverticular disease and inflammatory bowel disease among the Saudi population. The explanation for the higher incidence of colorectal cancer and inflammatory bowel disease has been partly explained by the adoption of Western diets in a growing globalized population.1–3

The term emergency colorectal surgery (ECRS) refers to a variety of circumstances wherein acute surgical intervention for the colon and rectum is required. ECRS comprises 15% of emergency general surgery performed in the acute care setting.4 In this patient population, preoperative optimization and control of comorbidities are usually limited due to the urgency of patient management. In the emergency setting, many colorectal conditions can be managed nonoperatively; however, patients overall condition and stability dictate the need for emergent surgery depending on indication.5,6

A common indication for ECRS is malignant large bowel obstruction from colorectal adenocarcinoma.7 In Saudi Arabia, colorectal cancer is the most prevalent malignancy in men and third among women according to 2018 national cancer registry data.8 National literature evaluating this topic reports that 30% of patients with colorectal cancer present initially in the emergency setting and subsequently require ECRS.9,10

Colonic perforation is another indication for ECRS. Perforation can occur secondary to long standing obstruction, or as a result of a primary inflammatory process such as inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) or diverticulitis.11,12 Other causes include iatrogenic perfo-rations, such as those caused by colonoscopy, or traumatic injuries.13,14 Colonic perforation results in contamination of the peritoneal cavity which consequently produces a disastrous cascade of septic shock followed by death.15

ECRS is associated with high mortality and morbidity rates.7 A multicenter study conducted in the United States reported morbidity rates of 30% and an in-hospital mortality rate of 13%.16 Factors responsible for postoperative death in ECRS have been studied, with conflicting results.7,17 Sepsis is a common cause of mortality in patients with colorectal emergencies, with some reports implicating it as the cause of death in 50% of cases.18 Sepsis can occur due to the primary disease process itself (e.g. diverticulitis) or secondary to perforation and intra-abdominal contamination.15

Surgical quality of care has garnered the interest of many stakeholders in healthcare, mainly due to the impact quality improvement initiatives have had on healthcare costs, patient reported outcomes, postoperative morbidity and mortality.19,20 Thirty-day mortality in the postoperative period is consistently used as an outcome measure to assess quality, safety and risk following surgical intervention.21

Saudi Arabia has undergone significant healthcare advancements in the last 70 years. Having a healthcare infrastructure that involves both public and private sectors, the Saudi healthcare system accommodates the needs of millions yearly. There remains, however, a dire need for data in order to estimate and improve quality of care.22 Because local data are lacking in this regard, assessment of local outcomes is imperative in understanding general and population-specific factors that are associated with postoperative complications and death. Our study aimed to assess factors associated with 30-day mortality and morbidity in patients undergoing ECRS at a single tertiary care center in Saudi Arabia.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

This was a retrospective cohort study based on a dataset from a previously published study.23 The data-set included 241 patients who underwent ECRS from July 2016 to July 2021 at King Abdulaziz Medical City (KAMC), Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. KAMC is a large tertiary care center that undertakes the care of National Guard affiliates across Saudi Arabia. Exclusion criteria included patients younger than 15 years of age, patients who underwent other major surgical procedures simultaneously, and patients who underwent elective colorectal surgery. A review of patient electronic medical records (BestCare) was done to collect patient demographic characteristics (age, sex, diagnosis, American Society of Anesthesiologists [ASA] classification, preoperative septic state, smoking, and comorbidities), operative characteristics (urgency, diverting ostomy, and procedure performed), and postoperative characteristics (length of stay, 30-day mortality, intensive care unit (ICU) admission, ICU length of stay, surgical site infection, readmission, reoperation, and postoperative complications). All patients presented to the emergency department and were evaluated by a surgical team. Patients either underwent immediate surgery (<48 hours) at presentation or delayed surgery (>48 hours). Thirty-day mortality was defined as death within 30 days of hospital presentation. Surgery was performed by either a specialized colorectal surgeon or a trained general surgeon. Procedures were performed at the discretion of the operating surgeon on an individualized basis. Left-sided lesions were treated by diversion alone, resection with end colostomy (Hartmann's procedure) or resection with anastomosis (left hemicolectomy). Similarly, right-sided lesions were treated with resection and anastomosis (right hemicolectomy) or diversion. Patients in which colonic salvage was not possible, a total or subtotal colectomy was performed. Procedures such as transverse colectomy, abdominal washout with anastomotic takedown, repair of rectal perforation were classified as “other”. Length of stay was calculated from time of admission until time of discharge or death. Postoperative complications included both medical and surgical complications.

Categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages and continuous variables as median and interquartile range. A binary logistic regression model was used to estimate the odds ratio (OR) of 30-day mortality and postoperative complications as dependent variables. Univariate logistic regression analysis was used to assess association of demographic, operative, and postoperative characteristics with 30-day mortality and postoperative complications (outcome variables). Variables that were found to have significant P-values and those with P value <.1 were then enrolled into a multivariable logistic regression analysis to adjust for confounders. A P-value of <.05 and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were used to report the statistical significance and precision of results. Data analysis was carried out using StataCorp. 2021. Stata Statistical Software: Release 17. College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC.

RESULTS

Of 241 patients who underwent ECRS, 145 (60.2%) were male. One-third of the patients (n=80, 33.2%) were between 50–64 years of age, the largest age class in the study (Table 1). The most common diagnosis was malignancy, with 138 (57.3%) patients requiring emergency surgery for cancer. Ninety-six patients (40.3%) were stratified as having an ASA score of III, and 191 (79.9%) patients had undergone surgery less than 48 hours from presentation. The most commonly performed procedure was left colectomy in 69 (28.6%) patients. Concurrent comorbid conditions were present in 199 (82.6%) of patients and postoperative complications occurred in 64 (26.6%) patients. Further, 50 (20.7%) underwent stoma creation as the primary procedure (Table 2).

Univariate logistic regression analysis revealed that increasing age was associated with the 30-day mortality outcome; patients in the 65–74 age group had a 5.25-fold higher odds of 30-day mortality as compared with younger patients (P=.045). Similarly, patients over 75 had a fivefold increased likelihood of death within 30 days of surgery (P=.037).

Among the diagnoses, ischemic colitis resulted in a six-fold increase in the odds of 30-day mortality (P<.001) whereas patients with malignancy had a 68% lower odds for that outcome (P=.01). Operative characteristics, including diversion with an ostomy and need for reoperation, were associated with a fourfold (P=.041) and twofold increased (P=.036) likelihood of 30-day mortality, respectively.

On multivariable logistic regression, preoperative sepsis was significantly associated with 30-day mortality, resulting in a fourfold (P=.024) increase in the likelihood of death within 30 days of surgery (Table 3). Additionally, Hartmann's procedure had a lower odds of 30-day mortality, by 88% (P=.04). The presence of postoperative complications conferred a fivefold higher odds of 30-day mortality independent of other factors (OR 5.058 [95% CI 1.64–15.57]). DISCUSSION

This study assessed factors associated with 30-day postoperative mortality and morbidity in patients undergoing ECRS. It revealed that the presence of sepsis in the preoperative setting increases the likelihood of 30-day mortality by 4.4 times. This association is independent of diagnosis, procedure type, time to operation and ICU admission. In the setting of colorectal emergencies, sepsis represents a fulminant state of gastrointestinal pathology, occurring commonly as a result of colonic perforation, with fecal contamination of the peritoneal cavity being the end result.24

Krutsri et al assessed ECRS in older patients. In that cohort, involving 92 subjects, septic shock was the most frequent cause of death, occurring in 50% of patients who died within 30 days. In their report, the median time from diagnosis to arrival at the operating room was 60 minutes. The main conclusion drawn from their analysis was that expeditious management of sepsis may improve outcomes.18 Our study shares the same view, as we found preoperative sepsis to be associated with 30-day mortality; however, in our study this association was independent of the time to intervention. Although emergency surgical management of the abdomen will always be advocated, our findings suggest that the disease process itself may at some point be irreversible despite damage control measures. Source control is a mainstay in the management of surgical emergencies. The Surgical Infection Society published guidelines with extensive recommendations on the surgeons role in managing intra-abdominal infections. Source control was endorsed to be undertaken no more than 24 hours after presentation, and more urgently for patients who are in septic shock.25 Therefore, initiatives directed towards expedited management protocols for septic surgical patients may aid in reducing mortality rates.

In our study, age greater than 65 was associated with a five times greater likelihood of mortality on univariate analysis (P=.045), but when controlling for other variables in multivariate analysis, age was not associated with mortality (P=.095). These findings are consistent with the report of Pacilli et al, which found no significant differences in morbidity and mortality of elderly patients compared to younger cohorts.26 These findings suggest that chronological age does not inherently confer an increased perioperative risk in ECRS. The higher risk in elderly patients could be because older patients tend to have more comorbid conditions which can impact postoperative outcomes. In a study assessing postoperative outcomes in elderly patients, Leong et al found that ASA status of III or IV predicted a higher mortality and morbidity rate in this population.27 Therefore, perioperative risk in older patients undergoing ECRS is dependent on comorbid profile rather than chronological age, and risk assessment should be individualized accordingly.

The presenting diagnosis was not associated with mortality or morbidity in our study. These findings are consistent with a recent study in which no association was found between postoperative complications and underlying diagnosis such as cancer, IBD or ischemic colitis.28 A possible explanation for this would be that the indications for ECRS remain uniform; features such as peritonitis, hemodynamic extremis and pneumoperitoneum indicate the need for ECRS regardless of the underlying pathology.

The choice of procedure appeared to be associated with outcome in our study. Cases in which an ostomy or diversion was created had a higher tendency for death (P=.03). However, this relationship is not causal, as patients who required diversion may have been in a state of hemodynamic decompensation intraoperatively compared to those who received an anastomosis, thus explaining this finding. Further, ostomies are used variably in the emergency setting, either as part of a staged procedure or for anastomotic protection.

There were no significant differences between right-sided and left-sided resections in regard to mortality and complications. Conversely, Hartmann's procedure was shown to have a protective effect, with a lesser odds for 30-day mortality (P=.042). These findings are in contrast to a recent study published by Bawa et al in 0.23 (0.10–0.501) 4.29 (1.99–9.24) which the primary procedure did not influence mortality.29 It is worthy to note that the procedure selected carries with it a vastly different complication profile. Patients in which an anastomosis was created are prone to anastomotic complications whereas creating an ostomy carries with it risks for ostomy-related complications. However, it remains imperative to employ damage control principles in ECRS.

There has been a recent interest in preventable hospital readmission as a quality of care indicator. Readmission was associated with patient dissatisfaction, increased health care costs and overall poor outcomes.30 The readmission rate in our cohort was 9.1% (n=22) and was significantly associated with postoperative complications (P=.020). In a review of over three thousand patients, Tevis et al demonstrated a readmission rate of 9% in patients undergoing general surgical procedures. Additionally, the presence of a postoperative complication was a risk factor for hospital readmission with early recognition, reducing the readmission rate dramatically.31 Therefore, in a population such as patients undergoing ECRS, who are complication prone, a lower threshold to investigate complications and a higher threshold for early discharge may reduce readmission rates. Thorough perioperative patient education and formulation of goal specific discharge criteria may improve outcomes.

The 30-day mortality rate in our sample was 11.2%. A multicenter trial conducted across the United States reported an in-hospital mortality rate of 13% among 469 patients who underwent emergency colonic resections with or without diversion.16 In the World Health Organization campaign for safe surgery in 2014, peri-operative mortality was endorsed as an indicator of quality of care.32 Compared to international data, mortality rates have been variably described. A large retrospective analysis derived from the American College of Surgeons National Surgery Quality Improvement Program (ACS NSQIP) reported mortality rates as low as 3.7% in emergency general surgery cases.33 To our knowledge, ours is the only published work evaluating local mortality rates in patients undergoing ECRS.

Various postoperative outcome prediction tools such as The Physiological and Operative Severity Score for the enUmeration of Mortality and morbidity (POSSUM) have been developed to estimate postoperative morbidity and mortality in surgical patients. In a study conducted by Al-homoud et al, a dedicated POSSUM scoring tool used for patients undergoing emergency surgery for cancer had an improved performance compared to ASA-based prediction models.34 Additionally, the ACS NSQIP risk assessment tool is being increasingly adopted for outcome estimation.35 In order to compare local outcomes to internationally reported mortality and morbidity rates, a robust database is needed. A collaborative national perioperative database such as the ACS NSQIP can provide a comprehensive understanding of the quality of perioperative care in Saudi Arabia. Population-specific quality-of-care improvement goals can be derived and resources can be directed toward early recognition and management of colorectal emergencies.

In our data, 57% of ECRS was due to colorectal cancer. This finding is similar to another study evaluating colorectal cancer within the Saudi population.36 Alsanea et al published the most robust epidemiological descriptions of colorectal cancer in the Saudi population.1 In their analysis, the age standardized rate of colorectal cancer increased during 1994–2010.37,38 The high incidence of colorectal cancer emergency presentations can be in part explained by the lack of a national screening program. Given the relatively low incidence of colorectal cancer in Saudi Arabia, modeling studies have shown that implementation of a nationwide screening program is cost-effective.39 In the emergency setting, complete cancer staging is not usually possible and acute presentations commonly denote more advanced disease. Efforts directed toward early identification may reduce the need for emergency surgical intervention and lead to better oncological outcomes in cancer patients.

Some of the limitations to our study include its retrospective design; a prospective design would enable the calculation of relative risks as opposed to odds ratios as measures of association. Further, these results represent data from a single tertiary center in the country and therefore may not be representative of the overall population. Another limitation is the broad definition of postoperative complications as it encompasses both medical and surgical complications. Further, outcome prediction scores such as the POSSUM were not calculated, limiting the ability to compare outcomes with international studies.

In conclusion, preoperative sepsis is associated with a fourfold increase in the likelihood of 30-day postoperative mortality in patients undergoing ECRS, independent of other preoperative and postoperative factors. Establishing local protocols targeting sepsis prevention in colorectal surgical patients may improve outcomes. Postoperative complications are associated with an increased likelihood of postdischarge readmission. Identification and early management of postoperative complications may reduce the rate of readmission. Malignancy is a common indication for ECRS in the Saudi population, but the need for ECRS in cancer treatment could be reduced by widespread implementation of screening initiatives.
