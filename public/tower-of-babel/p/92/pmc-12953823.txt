Differences in Psoas Major Muscle Properties Between Elite Athletes with and Without Chronic Low Back Pain
Authors: Emma Feng Ming Zhou, Arnold Yu Lok Wong, Guohui Lin, Jiebin Huang, Jianhui Fang, Wenting Liang, Xiongmao Cai, Siu Ngor Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite the proposed link between psoas major (PM) muscle dysfunction and low back pain (LBP), the relationship remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate the association between PM muscle properties, particularly stiffness, and chronic LBP in elite athletes.MethodsNinety-nine elite athletes (age: 10 to 32 years) from gymnastics and wushu (kung fu) teams were recruited. Fifty-three had chronic LBP. PM muscle stiffness (measured by ultrasound shear wave elastography), strength, and flexibility were measured on both sides. LBP severity was assessed with the Numeric Pain Rating Scale and Oslo Sports Trauma Research Center Questionnaire on Health Problems. ANCOVA and partial correlation were conducted to analyze the association between included outcome measures and LBP, with regression analysis identifying confounding factors. Statistical significance was set at P ≤ 0.05.ResultsIncreased PM stiffness of the affected side was significantly associated with LBP, especially when the pain was on the dominant side (17.2% increase; mean difference [MD] = 3.21 kPa; P < 0.001) and to a lesser extent on the non-dominant side (8.7% increase; MD = 1.65 kPa; P = 0.009). Higher PM stiffness correlated with greater LBP-related dysfunction (r = 0.349, P = 0.020). No associations were found between PM strength or flexibility and LBP (all P > 0.05).ConclusionPM muscle stiffness, but not strength or flexibility, is associated with chronic LBP and related dysfunctions. These findings highlight a potential link between PM stiffness and LBP in high-risk sports. Further longitudinal research is warranted to clarify the temporal and causal relationships.Keywords: Psoas muscle, Muscle stiffness, Low back pain, Shear wave modulus, Elite athletes

Background

Low back pain (LBP) is common among athletes, with a 1-year prevalence of up to 94% in high-risk sports like gymnastics [1]. Effective spinal stabilization, especially during dynamic movements, is essential for LBP prevention [2]. The psoas major (PM) muscle, attaching directly to the T12-L5 vertebrae, is vital for dynamic spinal stabilization, and its dysfunction is proposed as a factor in LBP [3–5]. In this regard, morphological studies of the PM muscle have yielded inconsistent results. Three studies have reported a reduced cross-sectional area of the PM in individuals with LBP compared to controls [6–8], while four other studies did not detect notable differences [9–12]. Similarly, evaluations of PM muscle strength (often assessed with the iliacus as part of hip flexor strength) have produced conflicting outcomes, showing both increased [13] and decreased [14] strength in those with LBP.

Notably, excessive PM tension has been implicated in vitro and in simulated models as contributing to heightened compressive and shear forces on the lumbar spine, particularly at the L5-S1 segment, potentially raising the risk of LBP [4, 15–17]. High muscle tension may arise from various factors such as reduced tissue elasticity or altered neuromuscular activation [18, 19]. These changes could modify the muscle’s mechanical behavior, potentially limiting its capacity to deform and transmit forces efficiently [20]. Consequently, the muscle’s ability to support dynamic stabilization of the lumbar spine during movement may be affected [2]. In this context, the relationship between PM tension and LBP has attracted research interest. The Modified Thomas test, which assesses hip extension range, has been employed to estimate PM muscle tension, but results have been varied [21–23]. It is noteworthy that hip extension range can be influenced by other factors, including the extensibility of the iliacus muscle and surrounding soft tissues at the hip joint [21, 24].

One recent study from Kitamura et al. [25] employed ultrasound shear wave elastography (SWE) to quantify PM stiffness at the groin region. SWE is a non-invasive imaging technique that quantifies shear-wave propagation within muscle tissue to derive the shear modulus [26, 27]. The shear modulus represents the material property of muscle tissue and correlates well with muscle stiffness (mechanical property) [28–30]; therefore, SWE is widely used as an indirect indicator of muscle mechanical behavior [31, 32]. In the current study, the term “muscle stiffness” refers to the shear modulus measured by SWE.

Kitamura et al. [25] found increased stiffness in swimmers with LBP compared to controls [25]. Notably, the measurement focused on the distal portion of the PM at the groin level, where a tendinous architecture has formed [5, 25]. The PM muscle exhibits a gradient in the distribution of type I and type II fibers from its upper to lower segments, corresponding to their distinct functions in postural support and joint motion facilitation [33, 34]. Previous studies have demonstrated that modulation of muscle stiffness among the different heads of the quadriceps and hamstrings varies according to their functional role and sport-specific loading [35, 36]. Therefore, stiffness of the PM at the upper segment (muscle belly in the lumbar region), which may be more closely associated with LBP, could differ from that at the distal tendinous junction. Conversely, chronic LBP may itself alter neuromuscular activation patterns, leading to secondary increases in PM stiffness [18, 19]. Whether an increase in PM stiffness at the lumbar segment also exists in individuals with LBP remains to be proven.

This study aimed to explore the associations between PM muscle properties, particularly stiffness, and LBP. Specifically, we compared PM muscle stiffness, strength, and flexibility between elite athletes with and without chronic LBP, and further investigated how these muscle properties relate to pain intensity and disability in symptomatic athletes. We hypothesized that (1) athletes with chronic LBP would exhibit greater regional PM muscle stiffness, reduced muscle strength, and decreased flexibility in comparison to their healthy counterparts; (2) increases in PM stiffness and decreases in strength and flexibility would be associated with greater pain intensity and dysfunction in those with chronic LBP.

Methods

Participant Recruitment and Procedures

This is a cross-sectional study. Full-time elite athletes were recruited from gymnastics and wushu (kung fu) teams at a provincial sports training center with the following inclusion criterion: (1) age between 10 and 40 years, (2) chronic LBP: pain located between the lower margin of the 12th rib and the buttock crease, with or without leg pain, persisting or fluctuating for more than 3 months, and with an average pain intensity of at least 3 over the past week on the Numeric Pain Rating Scale (NPRS) [37]. Exclusion criteria encompassed individuals with diagnosed systemic diseases, spinal pathologies, scoliosis, a history of spine trauma or surgery, or an inability to maintain a side-lying position for 30 min [38]. Athletes who had not experienced any LBP in the past 3 months were enrolled as the control group.

The study was approved by the Hong Kong Polytechnic University Institutional Review Board (Reference Number: HSEARS20220527005). Participants visited a sports hospital laboratory from May 7 to July 30, 2023, for data collection. All participants signed a consent form, and for those under 18, guardians provided written consent. After giving informed consent, participants completed a questionnaire detailing demographic information, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), training years, training hours per week, and dominant side (the leg used in kicking a ball).

Measurements

The SWE measurements were captured by a skilled physician, a registered musculoskeletal sonographer with three years of SWE experience. Physical tests were conducted by an experienced physiotherapist. Operators were blinded to the participants' LBP history and demographic information. All procedures were conducted on Sunday afternoons, approximately 30 h after the last scheduled team training session on Saturday morning. This timing ensured that all athletes were measured on non-training days, minimizing the influence of prior physical activity on PM stiffness.

Regional PM Muscle Stiffness

Ultrasound SWE (Supersonic Imagine’s Aixplorer® system with the UltraFast™ platform (Aix en Provence, France)) was used to measure PM muscle stiffness at the L4 region. The measurement details were thoroughly discussed in our earlier study [39]. In brief, athletes were placed in a lateral decubitus position with the spine and upper leg in neutral alignment. A wedge-shaped cushion was used to keep the torso perpendicular to the plinth, a cushion was placed between the legs to maintain hip neutrality, and a towel was added when necessary to support a neutral lumbar spine. Real-time SWE images of bilateral PM muscles were acquired using a 1–6 MHz low-frequency curvilinear probe positioned above the iliac crest along the mid-axillary line, then pivoted posteriorly to visualize the L4 vertebrae just beneath the PM muscle. SWE images of the PM were captured by aligning the ultrasound probe parallel to the long axis of muscle fibers (Fig. 1). Young’s modulus was assessed using a color-coded representation, and a 10-s real-time cine loop was recorded during breath-holding at the end of tidal expiration, starting from the right side and then the left.

Anatomical adjacencies and ultrasound shear wave elastography measurement of psoas major (PM)

For offline analysis, five well-defined regions of interest (ROIs) were selected from each 10-s video within the target muscle belly, avoiding fascial borders, visible artifacts, and acoustic shadowing [39]. ROIs were placed within homogeneous color-coded regions on the elastogram, indicating stable shear-wave propagation. Images with signal dropout or motion artifacts were excluded. A circled quantification box (Q-box) was drawn along the midline of each ROI, maximizing size without overlapping and maintaining a standard deviation within 20%. The mean Young’s modulus (in kPa) within the Q-box was averaged from five selected images in the 10-s video.

PM Strength Test

PM muscle strength was measured with a wireless microFET®2 digital Handheld Dynamometer (HHD) (Hoggan Scientific, LLC), operated by an experienced physical therapist. Participants were supine with a stabilized pelvis, and the leg positioned at 60 degrees of hip flexion, 30 degrees of abduction, and 20 degrees of external rotation, with the knee extended [40, 41]. The HHD was placed just proximal to the patella to minimize the rectus femoris recruitment, and resistance was applied via the HHD. Following an 80% maximum effort practice trial, participants completed three maximal voluntary contractions with a 60-s rest in between, and the peak force was recorded as the measure of strength.

Within-day intra-rater test–retest reliability was conducted and yielding high reliability for left (Intraclass Correlation Coefficients [ICCs]3,1 = 0.88, 95% Confidence Interval [CI] [0.67, 0.96]) and right (ICC3,1 = 0.86, 95% CI [0.64, 0.95]) sides.

Flexibility Test

The Modified Thomas test was used to assess PM muscle flexibility in when lying supine lying [21]. A Baseline® Plastic Goniometer (360 degree—12" HiRes™) was used to measure the hip extension range during the test. The fulcrum was placed on the lateral aspect of the greater trochanter, with the lateral midline of the femur as the distal arm and the lateral midline of the pelvis as the proximal arm to measure the true range of hip extension [42, 43].

Severity of LBP

For those athletes with chronic LBP, the pain location was recorded as unilateral (“left” or “right”) or “bilateral” (affecting both sides). Average LBP intensity over the past 7 days was evaluated by the NPRS from 0 (“no pain”) to 10 (“worst pain”) [44]. LBP-related disability specific to athletes was estimated using the Oslo Sports Trauma Research Center Questionnaire on Health Problems (OSTRC-H) [45]. The OSTRC-H includes 4 domains (4 questions): sports participation, training volume, sports performance, and pain. Each question has four answers, scoring 0, 8, 17, or 25, with higher total scores indicating greater severity.

Data Reduction and Statistical Analysis

Young’s modulus for PM muscle stiffness, PM strength normalized by body weight, and hip extension range were collected for both dominant and non-dominant sides. Athletes with LBP were categorized into LBP_dom (experiencing pain on the dominant side) and LBP_ndom (experiencing pain on the non-dominant side) groups. For control comparisons, Control_dom and Control_ndom indicate the dominant and non-dominant sides of the non-LBP athletes.

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, US). The normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Comparisons of PM stiffness, PM strength, and hip extension range of the affected sides to the corresponding sides of the control (LBP_dom and Control_dom, LBP_ndom and Control_ndom groups) were conducted with ANCOVA. Potential confounding factors were determined using independent samples t-tests (P < 0.1) for continuous demographic variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. Variables meeting these criteria were further examined using stepwise regression analysis. The values of the difference in PM stiffness were compared to the minimal detectable change with 95% confidence interval (MDC95) reported in our prior study [39]. Additionally, for those athletes with LBP, partial correlation was applied to explore the correlation between PM stiffness, strength, flexibility, and LBP severity. Correlation coefficients were interpreted as low (< 0.30), moderate (0.30–0.50), high (0.50–0.70), and very high (> 0.70), according to Hinkle et al. [46]. Statistical significance was set at P ≤ 0.05. In addition, the statistical power of the main ANCOVA analyses was calculated and reported to verify that the sample size was sufficient to detect meaningful between-group differences.

Comparison of PM Stiffness, Strength, and Flexibility in Athletes with and Without LBP

ANCOVA revealed a significant association between LBP and PM stiffness on the affected side under both conditions (LBP_dom, F = 22.13, P < 0.001, observed power = 0.996; LBP_ndom, F = 7.05, P = 0.009, observed power = 0.747), after adjusting for age and BMI as confounding variables. Pairwise comparisons demonstrated significantly higher PM stiffness of the affected side in LBP_dom group (by 17.2%; mean difference [MD] = 3.21 kPa > MDC95; P < 0.001) and LBP_ndom group (by 8.7%; MD = 1.65 kPa < MDC95; P = 0.009) compared to the non-LBP control group, respectively (Fig. 3a and b). Discussion

This study aimed to explore the associations between PM muscle properties and chronic LBP. We detected an increase in PM muscle stiffness on the affected side in athletes experiencing chronic LBP compared to controls. Additionally, greater stiffness of the PM muscle is related to more severe LBP-related dysfunctions among those with chronic LBP on the dominant side. However, as this study used a cross-sectional design, the directionality of this relationship cannot be determined; increased stiffness may either predispose individuals to LBP or result from pain-related adaptations.

Athletes with Chronic LBP have Increased PM Stiffness compared with Non-LBP Controls

Our finding of higher PM stiffness in the LBP group supports our hypothesis and is consistent with the results reported by Kitamura et al. [25] The observed increase in PM stiffness may reflect reduced muscle elasticity or elevated muscle activation [18, 19]. Such changes could alter the muscle’s mechanical behavior and potentially affect its ability to modulate movement and contribute to dynamic stabilization during hip actions [2, 20]. Since dynamic stabilization of the spine plays a key role in preventing LBP [2], greater PM stiffness may be associated with impaired dynamic stabilization of the lumbar spine, potentially contributing to LBP development. Furthermore, prior research, both in vitro and through biomechanical modelling, has demonstrated that excessive PM tension can increase anterior shear forces on the lumbar spine, particularly at the L5–S1 level, which may contribute to altered spinal loading and potentially to low back discomfort or dysfunction [4, 15–17]. Thus, it is plausible that increased PM stiffness may contribute to the development of LBP.

Alternatively, chronic LBP may induce reflexive muscle overactivation or altered neuromuscular control, contributing to secondary increases in PM stiffness. Co-contraction of PM and back extensors dynamically stabilizes the lumbar spine [3]. According to Lund et al.'s pain-adaptation model, chronic pain inhibits the α-motor neurons of the painful muscles and activates the antagonists [19, 47]. Pain perceived in the low back might trigger the antagonistic flexors, causing a PM muscle spasm and subsequently, a chronic increase in muscle stiffness [19]. In addition, the pain-induced motor adaptation model proposed by Hodges et al. [48] suggests a deliberate strategy to limit movement and safeguard the painful area, potentially leading to a redistribution of activity within and between muscles. This adaptation could enhance muscle activity and stiffness to maintain lower back stability [49]. In this context, LBP may indeed contribute to increased PM stiffness. However, due to the cross-sectional design of this study, the direction of causality between PM stiffness and LBP cannot be established. Future research could employ resting electromyography (EMG) to determine whether LBP is associated with PM spasms that elevate stiffness, or whether reducing muscle tension alleviates pain. Moreover, prospective longitudinal studies are warranted to clarify whether elevated PM stiffness precedes the onset of LBP or develops as a consequence. Such insights would be essential for developing effective preventive and management strategies for LBP.

The potential explanation for the increase in PM muscle stiffness is sports-specific loading. As the earlier research illustrated, activity-induced modulation of muscle stiffness might depend on multiple factors such as loading patterns, levels of activity, sports-specific requirement and involved muscle groups [36, 39]. Gymnastics and wushu athletes have similar PM muscle physical demands, frequently engaging in high-velocity trunk bending and extensive hip flexion and extension [50]. The PM muscle, functioning concurrently as a spinal stabilizer and hip flexor, may adapt under conditions requiring rapid or excessive hip flexion by shifting type I fibres toward fast-twitch type II fibers [34]. Although type II fibers are generally less stiff than type I fibers [51], such a shift may alter the muscle’s overall mechanical behavior and coordination of the PM muscle. Since type II fibers are more fatigable and less capable of sustaining low-level tonic activity required for spinal stabilization [34], this shift may impair the muscle’s stabilizing capacity during dynamic hip movements. To compensate for reduced contractile stabilization, the intramuscular connective tissue (IMCT) may undergo adaptive stiffening to meet functional demands [52]. Long-term mechanical loading has been reported to increase the synthesis of type I collagen fibers within the IMCT, thereby enhancing passive tissue stiffness [52–56]. Such stiffening may transiently aid static stabilization of the spine, but reduced muscle elasticity could compromise dynamic control, potentially contributing to the development of LBP.

During long-term training, the PM muscle on the dominant side may encounter relatively greater challenges and exhibit a more robust biomechanical response, especially in activities that require maintaining spinal stability and performing motions such as frequent high-speed kicking with the dominant leading leg [57, 58]. The alterations in muscle fiber composition and the increase in IMCT stiffness are expected to be more pronounced on the dominant side, where the physical demands are typically greater [52]. This may explain our finding of the more pronounced increase in PM stiffness when pain occurred on the dominant side.

Association Between PM Stiffness and Severity of LBP

Our study also delineated the association between PM stiffness and LBP-related dysfunction amongst athletes with chronic LBP. Greater PM stiffness is associated with higher OSTRC-H scores, indicating more severe dysfunction, though this association was not observed with pain intensity. Increased stiffness may act as a protective mechanism to limit movement, prevent further injury, and potentially reduce pain [48]. PM muscle contributes to trunk and hip flexion that are the fundamental movements for gymnastics and wushu athletes. This stiffness might lead to reduced sports participation, training volume, sports performance, and pain intensity. Therefore, the LBP-related dysfunction may be more directly influenced by muscle stiffness. The association was primarily observed when pain was on the dominant side, aligning with the greater sports demands placed on this side among athletes. These findings suggest an association between PM stiffness, LBP, and sports performance.

PM Strength and Hip Extension Range are not Associated with LBP

Our study did not detect significant differences in PM strength and flexibility between athletes with and without chronic LBP. Previous research using hip flexion to assess PM strength reported both increased and decreased strength in those with LBP [13, 14]. In the present study, we adopted a different approach that focused more specifically on the PM muscle rather than the entire group of hip flexors [40, 41]. Although height differences may influence moment arm length and consequently affect force generation, the PM strength values in this study were normalized to body weight. Therefore, the potential influence of height on the results is likely limited but should be considered in future studies.

Regarding hip extension range, the Modified Thomas test might be confounded by the condition of other tissues and joint function. In this connection, although improvements in range of motion were observed after stretching exercises, no change in regional muscle stiffness was noted [59, 60]. This suggests that changes in joint range might not be related to muscle stiffness of a single muscle.

Taking together, a regional increase in PM muscle stiffness, but not in strength or flexibility, was detected among elite gymnastics and wushu athletes with chronic LBP compared to controls. Future studies are warranted to determine whether modulating regional PM stiffness influences clinical outcomes or athletic performance in athletes with chronic LBP.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Firstly, being cross-sectional, it cannot establish cause-and-effect relationships. Whether increased muscle stiffness is a cause or consequence of LBP remains unclear, necessitating longitudinal or experimental research. Secondly, we only analyzed the pain-affected side due to the limited number of athletes with unilateral pain. Future studies with larger cohorts experiencing unilateral back pain could provide insights into changes in both affected and unaffected sides. Thirdly, although participants were classified as having LBP persisting for more than three months according to the chronic LBP definition, the exact duration of symptoms was not recorded. This may limit the interpretation of the association between symptom duration and psoas major stiffness. Lastly, our research only enrolled gymnastics and wushu athletes, who experience high loading on the lower back associated with extreme movements, which may limit the generalizability to other sports, such as weightlifting, where LBP is often related to compression.

Conclusion

Our study identified increased PM muscle stiffness, particularly on the dominant side, in elite gymnastics and wushu athletes with chronic LBP. Greater PM stiffness was associated with greater disability, whereas no such relationship was observed for muscle strength or flexibility. Future longitudinal and interventional studies are warranted to clarify whether changes in PM stiffness contribute to or result from LBP, and to explore whether modulating PM stiffness has any impact on clinical outcomes or athletic performance.
