The impact of energy‐based devices on sebum in acne vulgaris: A systematic review
Authors: Dana Jaalouk, Anika Pulumati, Yanci A Algarin, Jasmine Humeda, David J Goldberg
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcne vulgaris (AV) is a widespread inflammatory skin condition associated with increased sebum production, abnormal keratinization, bacterial overgrowth, and inflammation. Overactive sebaceous glands (SGs) produce excess sebum, promote Cutibacterium acnes growth, and affect acne development. Energy‐based treatments (EBDs), including light therapy, photodynamic therapy (PDT), lasers, and radiofrequency (RF) devices, have emerged as effective treatment options. As the use of EBDs becomes more widespread, it is imperative to understand their effects on skin parameters, such as sebum, in AV.MethodsSearches were conducted in Embase, PubMed, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library. The studies included were randomized and nonrandomized trials on facial AV that used EBDs and featured objective casual sebum level (CSL) measurements via Sebumeter. Data synthesis involved percentage reductions in CSL at follow‐ups compared to baseline.ResultsTwenty‐three studies were analyzed. PDT and RF consistently reduced CSL by 30%–40% and 30%–35%, respectively. Laser therapy showed lesser reductions, whereas light therapy varied significantly and studies had a high risk of bias. All EBD therapies were more effective than no treatment and PDT was superior to light monotherapy. Laser therapy combined with fractional microneedling radiofrequency (FMR) or as a standalone was more effective than laser alone.ConclusionNoninvasive sebum measurement provides valuable insights into AV treatment efficacy. PDT, lasers, especially the 1450‐nm diode laser, and FMR are promising for reducing sebum. Standardization of measurement techniques and further research are vital for enhancing treatment personalization, reducing side effects, and improving AV management.Keywords: acne vulgaris, energy‐based devices, sebum

INTRODUCTION

Sebum, produced by sebaceous glands (SGs) in the dermis, is essential for skin health, maintaining hydration, and protecting against bacteria. However, its overproduction can lead to acne vulgaris (AV), a common skin condition characterized by comedones, papules, and pustules. 1 AV's etiology includes increased sebum, abnormal keratinization, overgrowth of Cutibacterium acnes (C. acnes), and inflammation, with excess sebum being a major contributor to acne development. 1 , 2

AV management includes topical and oral medications and physical treatments such as light and lasers. Hormonal therapies and oral isotretinoin are the only treatments known to effectively reduce sebum production but are associated with significant risks, including feminization and teratogenicity, underscoring the need for safer sebum‐regulating treatments. 3

The field of energy‐based devices (EBDs), such as lasers, light therapies, photodynamic therapy (PDT), and radiofrequency (RF) devices is rapidly evolving. EBDs treat AV by targeting C. acnes bacteria, reducing inflammation, and/or impacting SG activity with variable efficacy. An early approach to AV with EBDs included the use of natural ultraviolet (UV) light, a technique within PDT. 4 Additionally, visible‐light lasers and intense pulsed light (IPL) devices have been employed to combat C. acnes, either as standalone treatments or in conjunction with PDT. 5 Infrared lasers and RF technologies utilize heat to target water in the skin surrounding the SGs, causing thermal destruction of the glands. Histological examinations have documented shrinkage in SG size, similar to the effects observed with oral isotretinoin usage. 6 A significant advancement is the FDA‐approved 1726‐nm laser, marketed under the brand names AviClear® and Accure®. This laser specifically targets and destroys SGs through selective photothermolysis, utilizing sebum as its primary chromophore. This innovation not only treats AV effectively by reducing sebum production but also results in fewer adverse events than earlier EBDs. Its development emphasizes the importance of SGs in AV pathology. As these EBDs become more prominent, understanding their impact on skin health and sebum production is crucial.

Noninvasive sebum measurements offer objective insights into AV treatment efficacy. 2 Key metrics include casual sebum level (CSL) and sebum excretion rate (SER), with CSL often preferred for its simplicity and efficiency in clinical trials. 2 However, research on the effects of EBDs on CSL in AV is limited, highlighting the need for comprehensive data analysis to gain insights.

This systematic review evaluates the effects of light, PDT, laser, and RF treatments on CSL in facial AV, comparing these therapies with other acne treatments and controls. It seeks to enhance the general knowledge of acne treatment outcomes, potentially guiding future therapeutic advancements and improving patient care.

METHODS

Eligibility criteria

Eligible studies included randomized and nonrandomized trials with at least two treatment arms, written in English, published in peer‐reviewed journals, and focused on facial AV. They must fit into one of the following therapy groups: light, PDT, laser, or RF. Objective CSL measurements in micrograms per square centimeter (μg/cm2) using a Sebumeter (Courage and Khazaka GmbH) were required. Sebumeter was selected because of its widespread use, reproducibility, and minimal uncertainty. 2

We excluded single‐arm trials, case reports, gray literature, non‐English articles, studies on other conditions, and those measuring sebum in nonfacial areas. Additionally, studies not utilizing a Sebumeter or specifying the measurement instrument were excluded.

Search strategy

We search Embase (December 12, 2023), MEDLINE (January 15, 2024), Web of Science (December 13, 2023), and CENTRAL (November 29, 2023) using key phrases such as “acne vulgaris” AND “laser,” “light,” “photodynamic therapy,” OR “radiofrequency” AND “sebum.” See the Appendix S1 for detailed search strategy.

Selection process

Search results were imported into the Covidence systematic review platform for screening. Two authors independently reviewed titles, abstracts, and full texts, assigning inclusion or exclusion statuses. Discrepancies were resolved by a third reviewer.

Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias assessment was conducted in Covidence using the revised Cochrane ‘Risk of Bias’ tool for randomized trials (RoB 2.0) 7 and the Cochrane Risk Of Bias In Nonrandomized Studies‐of Interventions (ROBINS‐I) tool. 8 The Risk‐of‐Bias VISualization (robvis) tool was used to create Traffic Light Plots and unweighted Summary Plots for visualization. 9

Data collection

Two independent authors collected data via Covidence. Study specifics (authors, year, country), sample demographics, and participant details (acne severity, Fitzpatrick skin type) were recorded. Additionally, research design, intervention details (type, duration, dose/parameters, timing), and sebum measurement techniques (premeasurement protocol, location) were documented. Baseline and follow‐up sebum measurements were recorded, with end‐of‐treatment (EOT) measurements used if no follow‐up was provided. When data was only available in graph form, it was extracted using WebPlotDigitizer. If necessary, the corresponding authors were contacted for missing data or clarification.

Effect measure

The primary effect measure was the percentage reduction in CSLs from baseline at follow‐up or EOT if no follow‐up was performed.

Synthesis methods

We reported the percentage reduction in CSL from baseline. For studies without percentage data, we calculated it from baseline and follow‐up values. When multiple facial locations were measured, the facial average was used.

The studies were grouped into four main intervention categories: light therapy, PDT, laser therapy, and RF therapy. They were subdivided into 18 potential groups based on the study's comparison framework and organized alphabetically for tabulation.

After searching four databases, 1600 studies were found, 1065 were screened, 66 were fully reviewed, and 23 were included (Figure 1). Four studies were organized into the Light intervention group, nine into PDT group, five into the Laser group, and five into the RF group. Study characteristics and demographics

Of the 23 studies included, 18 were randomized trials and five were nonrandomized, involving 559 patients (218 males and 307 females). The average participant age was 24.2 years (range 12–52 years). Acne severity varied from mild to severe, predominantly mild‐to‐moderate. Fitzpatrick skin types ranged from I to VI, mostly types III and IV. The studies were conducted in Asia, Europe, and North America. CSL measurements were obtained from 510 participants, primarily on the cheeks. Summary of study findings

Table S2 provides detailed results for each study.

Light versus Control

Ding et al. 1 observed a 60.9% CSL reduction (p = 0.008) after eight blue light treatments. Kwon et al. 10 reported a 19.9% CSL decrease after blue–red LED treatment at 12 weeks (p < 0.001). Zane et al. 11 found a 43% CSL reduction after eight red light treatments at the 12‐week follow‐up (p < 0.05). Control sites showed no reduction, and no quantitative data was provided. 11

Light versus Light

Wang et al. 12 evaluated the efficacy of combining IPL treatments with fractional carbon dioxide laser (FCL) sessions across three groups, differentiated by the number of IPL sessions received: four, five, or six (Groups A, B, and C). Posttreatment, all groups displayed significant reductions in CSL after IPL (A, 19.6%; B, 23.8%; C, 16.0%) and 12 weeks after FCL, except for Group C, where the reduction was not statistically significant. No significant differences were found between the groups. 12

PDT versus Control

Kwon et al. 13 found a gradual, significant CSL reduction of 32.6% at 12 weeks with ALA‐bu cream and daylight PDT compared to baseline (p < 0.05). Treatment also outperformed controls (p < 0.05). 13

PDT versus Light

Na et al. 14 found IAA with green light resulted in a 34.1% CSL reduction (p < 0.05), while green light alone showed a 27.7% nonsignificant decrease.

Song et al. 15 reported Chlorophyll‐a with blue–red LED significantly outperformed LED alone, with a 37.4% reduction at week 6 compared to 16.5% (p < 0.001). PDT also led to a faster CSL reduction, which was significant from 2 weeks onwards (p < 0.05). 15

However, Akaraphanth et al. 16 and Horfelt et al. 17 found no significant CSL reduction with 5‐aminolevulinic acid (ALA)‐PDT with blue light and methyl‐aminolevulinate (MAL)‐PDT with red light, respectively. Additionally, blue and red light monotherapy did not significantly reduce CSL. 16 , 17

PDT versus PDT

Jang et al. 18 compared indocyanine green (ICG)‐PDT with 805‐nm diode laser (DL) to IAA‐PDT with green light, noting 42.3% and 33.9% CSL reductions at week 5, respectively. Both treatments showed comparable efficacy. 18

At the 4‐week follow‐up, Kim et al. 19 found that St. John's Wort (SJW)‐PDT and IAA‐PDT using green–red LED significantly reduced CSL compared to baseline by 27.2% (p = 0.022) and 26.9% (p < 0.001), respectively. There were no significant differences between them. 19

Oh et al. 20 compared the effect of incubation length on the effectiveness of 20% ALA‐PDT using IPL. All groups showed significant CSL reductions at the 12‐week follow‐up: 31.5% for short incubation, 46.7% for long incubation, and 37.5% for IPL alone (p < 0.001). There were no significant differences between the groups. 20

Wangsuwan and Meephansan 21 compared riboflavin‐tryptophan (RT) gel to 13% ALA using blue light. Both treatments significantly reduced CSL starting from the first treatment, with RT‐PDT and ALA‐PDT showing reductions of 54.4% and 56.6%, respectively, at the 4‐week follow‐up (p < 0.001). No significant differences were observed between the treatments. 21

Laser versus Control

In assessing laser treatment efficacy compared to controls, Jung et al. 22 found a significant CSL reduction after three treatments (p = 0.001) and at follow‐up (p < 0.05) with dual‐mode 1064‐nm Nd:YAG laser combined with carbon suspension. There was a 27.7% CSL reduction at follow‐up compared to baseline (p = 0.025). 22

Kesty and Goldberg 23 evaluated the 650 μs 1064‐nm Nd:YAG laser versus a sham device, with the laser group showing a larger CSL decrease from baseline at the 4‐week follow‐up (18% vs. 9%).

Laser versus Light

Kwon et al. 24 compared the 1450‐nm DL combined with blue light to blue light alone. Combination therapy significantly reduced CSL compared to baseline and blue light monotherapy from the first treatment onwards, showing a 41% reduction versus an 11.4% increase with monotherapy at the 12‐week follow‐up (p < 0.05). 24

Laser versus PDT

Chen et al. 25 compared the efficacy of 2% ALA with red light on skin pretreated with the Q‐switch 1064‐nm Nd:YAG laser to ALA‐PDT alone. Combined therapy significantly reduced CSL compared to monotherapy at the end of treatment (27.7% vs. 9.2%) and week 12 (37.5% vs. 16.3%) (p < 0.05). 25

Laser versus Laser

Kwon et al. 26 compared the efficacy of dual‐mode versus stamp‐mode using the 1450‐nm DL. Both methods significantly reduced CSL after one treatment session (p < 0.05). By week 20, the dual‐mode laser achieved a 47.8% reduction in CSL (p < 0.05). The conventional laser side had a CSL increase at week 20 compared to week 9, with reductions of 21.7% versus 28%; however, this was still significantly lower than baseline (p < 0.05). The dual‐mode laser was significantly more effective than stamp‐mode from the second treatment onwards (p < 0.05). 26

RF versus Control

Choi et al. 27 found a significant 23.1% reduction in CSL after nonablative monopolar radiofrequency (NMRF) laser treatment compared to the control side at the 2‐week follow‐up (p < 0.017).

RF versus Laser

Kim et al. 28 found that combining fractional microneedle radiofrequency (FMR) with FCL significantly reduced CSL by 12.5% compared to FCL alone, which showed a 13.9% CSL increase (p < 0.05).

Kwon et al. 29 observed a significant CSL reduction with both 1450‐nm DL and FMR after two sessions compared to baseline. By week 20, FMR and DL showed 33.9% and 34.2% reductions, respectively (p < 0.05). Notably, FMR maintained better long‐term CSL reduction compared to DL (p < 0.05), which displayed reduced effectiveness at week 20 compared to week 9. 29

Shin et al. 30 found that both FMR and FCL treatment significantly reduced CSL at follow‐up compared to baseline, with reductions of 30.9% and 31%, respectively (p < 0.05). No significant differences were observed between the groups. 30

RF versus RF

Min et al. 31 compared FMR to bipolar radiofrequency (BR). At the 4‐week follow‐up, FMR significantly reduced CSL by 63.5% from baseline (p = 0.002), whereas the reduction with BR treatment was not significant (34.2%). At the final follow‐up, FMR reached a 65.4% reduction, whereas BR worsened to 30.9%. FMR significantly outperformed BR at both follow‐up visits (p < 0.001). 31

DISCUSSION

Previous research has shown that a 30%–50% reduction in sebum is needed to achieve a 50% decrease in acne severity metrics. 32 However, the effectiveness of traditional treatments in reducing sebum levels varies. Isotretinoin can achieve a sebum reduction of 60%–90%, while hormonal therapies may result in up to a 75% decrease. 32 Topicals, such as adapalene and benzoyl peroxide, show modest effects, with adapalene causing a 0.2%–7% decrease 33 and benzoyl peroxide showing variable impacts ranging from a 10%–25% increase 33 to a 20% decrease 34 in sebum production.

Regarding EBDs, PDT reduced sebum levels by 30%–40% in most studies, regardless of the photosensitizer used. RF devices also provided consistent reductions of 30%–35%. Laser therapy was typically less effective, except for 1450‐nm DL, which achieved 40%–48% reductions. However, light therapy exhibits more variable outcomes and studies had a higher risk of bias. This may be due to rapid C. acnes reproduction in overactive SGs after light therapy. 35

All therapies outperformed controls. PDT has excelled over light therapy. Laser therapy, particularly 1450‐nm DL, showed greater CSL reduction than light or PDT. FMR, alone or with laser, also outperformed laser monotherapy. Although these therapies did not achieve the reductions in sebum production seen with isotretinoin or hormonal therapies, most treatments still lead to a significant decrease.

When using EBDs for AV, selecting the correct treatment parameters is crucial. Effective treatment often requires high doses, prolonged contact, and high‐fluence exposure; however, some exceptions exist. For PDT, recent studies suggest that extended incubation time 20 , 21 , 25 and higher concentrations 21 , 25 may not be necessary to reduce CSL and treat acne lesions, highlighting the need for further research to optimize protocols.

Several factors must also be considered when measuring CSL. Sebum levels are highly sensitive to the external environment and can fluctuate rapidly, making accurate measurements challenging. 2 Allowing a 30–60 min acclimatization period before measurements can help control these variables. 2 While only three studies specified an acclimatization period, 1 , 12 , 16 most instructed participants not to wash their faces for 5–6 h premeasurement. Eight studies did not describe the measurement methods.

Furthermore, there is debate regarding the best location for sebum measurements. The T‐zone (forehead, nose, chin) has the highest concentration of SGs on the face and exhibits significantly higher CSLs than the U‐zone (cheeks). 2 The T‐zone has been recommended for measurements; however, some argue that measuring areas with high SG activity may lead to inaccuracies and greater variability. Recent research suggests that the U‐zone reliably reflects facial sebum levels and exhibits lower but stable sebum content, whereas the T‐zone, especially the nose, exhibits significant fluctuations, making it less suitable for accurate measurements. 2 Of the trials in this review that specified measurement location, 61% solely used cheeks.

The T‐zone requires a stricter degreasing protocol than the U‐zone. Forehead degreasing involves washing with detergent followed by alcohol wipes, whereas cheeks only require mild soap. 5 Additionally, a 5–6 h measurement delay postwashing provides more accurate results for the forehead. 5 This may explain the lack of efficacy on the forehead in Akaraphanth et al.'s16 study, which reported a brief acclimatization period postwash. Despite these considerations, the Sebumeter's simplicity and quick operation are beneficial and reduce risk of measurement errors. 5

A gap exists in studies measuring CSL in AV, especially for EBDs. Two‐arm studies are absent for devices such as the 585–595‐nm pulsed dye laser (PDL), 1540‐nm Er:glass laser, 1320‐nm Nd:YAG laser, potassium titanyl‐phosphate (KTP) laser, and others. Additionally, there is a lack of data on the 1726‐nm laser, and given its capacity to selectively target and destroy SGs, 36 , 37 , 38 future research should incorporate CSL measurements to comprehensively assess the laser's efficacy in reducing sebum. This evaluation could help broaden the potential of this laser for other conditions characterized by overactive SGs.

Ultimately, when selecting an EBD for AV treatment, it is crucial to prioritize proper hydration and sebum control to maintain the health of acne‐prone skin. Clinicians often focus primarily on reducing acne lesions, overlooking the importance of skincare in treatment efficacy. Acne treatments can lead to dry skin, compromised barrier function, and decreased hydration. 39 With the growing use of EBDs for AV, understanding their impact on skin health is essential.

LIMITATIONS

Regarding limitations, 52% of studies lacked numerical CSL data, relying on graphical representations, potentially introducing inaccuracies despite our standardized extraction approach. Data variability, which is crucial for result reliability, was rarely reported. Despite contacting the authors, no response was received. Given the inherent variability of sebum output, detailed variability reporting is essential.

Geographically, 87% of studies involved Asian populations, with 65% conducted in Korea, suggesting limited diversity. Future research should be conducted in diverse geographical locations.

CONCLUSION

Reliable noninvasive methods for assessing skin biophysical parameters such sebum, offer objective insights into skin health and treatment efficacy. When evaluating EBD therapies for AV, PDT and RF consistently reduced CSLs, whereas laser therapy showed slightly lower but significant reductions. Light therapy yielded inconsistent results and may be biased. Despite not reaching the sebum reduction levels of isotretinoin or hormonal treatments, EBDs still led to notable CSL reductions.

The review highlights challenges in CSL measurement, stressing the importance of site, environment, and methodology. Standardized protocols are crucial given the diverse measurement techniques and documentation gaps. Further research is necessary to understand the impact of EBDs on skin health in AV patients, enabling personalized treatment, minimizing side effects, and ensuring proper postprocedural care.
