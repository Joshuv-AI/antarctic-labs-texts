Falls and malnutrition are associated with in-hospital mortality in patients with cirrhosis
Authors: Nada Abedin, Moritz Hein, Alexander Queck, Marcus M Mücke, Nina Weiler, Anita Pathil, Ulrike Mihm, Christoph Welsch, Jörg Bojunga, Stefan Zeuzem, Eva Herrmann, Georg Dultz
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Hospitalized patients with end-stage liver disease are at risk of malnutrition, reduced body function, and cognitive impairment due to HE. This combination may have an impact on in-hospital falls and mortality. The purpose of this study was to identify factors associated with the risk of falls and to analyze the consequences regarding in-hospital mortality.Methods:We performed a retrospective analysis of patients hospitalized with liver cirrhosis between 2017 and 2019 at the Department of Gastroenterology at the University Hospital Frankfurt. Clinical data, laboratory work, and follow-up data were analyzed. Factors associated with the risk of falls and in-hospital mortality were calculated using a mixed effect poisson regression model and competing risk time-to-event analyses.Results:Falls occurred with an incidence of 4% (80/1985), including 44 injurious falls with an incidence rate of 0.00005/100 patient-days (95% CI: 0.00001–0.00022). In the multivariate analysis malnutrition (incidence risk ratio: 1.77, 95% CI: 1.04–3.04) and implanted TIPS (incidence risk ratio: 20.09, 95% CI: 10.1–40.1) were independently associated with the risk of falling. In a total of 21/80 (26.25%) hospitalizations, patients with a documented fall died during their hospital stay versus 160/1905 (8.4%) deaths in hospitalizations without in-hospital fall. Multivariable analysis revealed as significant clinical predictors for in-hospital mortality a Nutritional Risk Screening ≥2 (HR 1.79, 95% CI: 1.32–2.4), a falling incident during hospitalization (HR 3.50, 95% CI: 2.04–6.0), high MELD, and admission for infections.Conclusions:Malnutrition and TIPS are associated with falls in hospitalized patients with liver cirrhosis. The in-hospital mortality rate of patients with cirrhosis with falls is high. Specific attention and measures to ameliorate these risks are warranted.Keywords: Liver cirrhosis, falls, malnutrition, chronic liver disease, encephalopathy

INTRODUCTION

Liver cirrhosis is a progressive chronic disease with an increasing incidence ranking among the top 10 causes of death worldwide.1,2 Characterized by the formation of fibrotic tissue and destruction of liver cells, it leads to impaired liver function. 3 Causes include alcohol consumption, viral hepatitis, and metabolic disorders, including metabolic dysfunction–associated liver disease. Patients with liver cirrhosis are at a high risk of developing severe complications, such as variceal bleeding, HE, and ascites.2,4

Malnutrition has been linked to an increased risk of morbidity and mortality in patients with liver cirrhosis. Due to a catabolic state maintained by hypermetabolism, malabsorption, and inflammatory conditions, it can lead to muscle wasting, that is, sarcopenia and reduced immunity, and is further exacerbated by the disease itself.5,6 Additionally, it can be assumed that the combination of malnutrition and muscle wasting with altered mental status from HE may cause impairment in the mobility of patients with end-stage liver disease, which makes them prone to falls. Falls can cause serious injuries, including fractures and intracranial bleeding, which critically contribute to overall morbidity and mortality in these patients.7–9 Tapper and colleagues discussed falls as a significant complication of cirrhosis with potentially fatal complications and a lack of predictive factors.8,10 While the risk of falls in these patients is highest around the time of their diagnosis, the risk is consistently high. 11 Given the high number of patients being diagnosed after an initial event, ie, injury, diagnosing liver cirrhosis should prompt an assessment of other risk factors.

Decompensation can lead to recurrent hospitalization in patients with liver cirrhosis. This goes along with increasing malnutrition, the risk for in-hospital falls, and eventually an increased risk of in-hospital mortality.7,12 Furthermore, in-hospital falls are an important source of medicolegal liability. In-hospital falls, either caused by HE, medication, frailty or other reasons, are frequently reasons for legal claims and need to be avoided with all possible measures. 13

Surprisingly, while falls, sarcopenia, and malnutrition have been studied in the context of elderly populations,14–16 few studies have focused on chronic liver disease. 17 Although the impact of etiology, severity of liver disease, and comorbidities have been investigated as predictors of mortality in this population, the role of malnutrition and falls remains unclear. The importance of malnutrition assessment and adequate treatment has been highlighted by the current practice guidelines of the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) and Baveno VII consortium meeting in 2021.18,19 Nutritional support has been shown to improve the clinical outcomes of patients with liver cirrhosis and could be a valuable tool for reducing the in-hospital risk of falls and in-hospital mortality in patients with liver cirrhosis.10,20–22 Furthermore, early mobilization and regular assessments of patients’ risk of falling could help in the early detection of at-risk patients and implementation of preventive measures, respectively. 23 Accordingly, this study aimed to investigate the association between nutritional risk, falls, and in-hospital mortality in patients with liver cirrhosis. Identifying the factors associated with in-hospital mortality is crucial for improving patient outcomes.

METHODS

Patient selection

In this retrospective analysis, patients with liver cirrhosis hospitalized at the Department of Gastroenterology and Hepatology between 2017 and 2019 were included. Patients were identified using primary and secondary International Classification of Diseases codes, including liver cirrhosis. Data were extracted from the patients’ electronic files. We assessed the clinical data from admission to discharge, background information, laboratory work, and follow-up data. Both elective admissions and those through the emergency department were included, and the reasons for admission were assessed accordingly. All patients were above the age of 18 years.

Clinical data

Clinical data were obtained from the electronic files. In total, 156 variables were included. Next to the immediate reason for admission, the medical history of the patients was assessed, including comorbidities, regular medication as available on admission, risk and malnutrition assessment, vitals on admission, and routine laboratory values. We performed the MELD Score calculation and Child-Pugh score definition for the classification of disease severity in the included patients.

Malnutrition was routinely assessed on admission by Nutritional Risk Screening (NRS), as recommended by the European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN).24,25 The NRS includes the Body mass index, weight loss in the 3 months prior, reduced food intake in the week before, and an underlying severe disease.

Falling risk was also evaluated on admission for all patients according to the STRATIFY-scale,26,27 which includes different risk factors associated with a higher risk of falling. Finally, falling incidents were routinely documented by responsible nurses and doctors using a standardized form, including the falling incident, consequences, and potential injuries suffered by the patient.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Microsoft Excel, IBM SPSS Statistics Version 22 and R Version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) using the packages LME3, survival and cmprsk. Comparative analyses were performed between admissions with documented falls and no falls as well as cases with in-hospital death and regular discharge. Differences between groups of patients were determined using the nonparametric Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney U test and Chi-squared or exact Fisher test.

Univariate and multivariate regression analyses used a mixed effect Poisson regression model to analyze factors on the incidence of falling events accounting for the length of hospital stay and multiple admissions of the same patients. Furthermore, we analyzed the time to the first falling event taking transplantation and death as competing events and account for within patient cluster effects and a Fine and Gray weighted Cox regression analysis. Admissions with no event are counted as censored. Finally, factors associated with in-hospital mortality use time-to-event analysis taking transplantation as competing event and using falling events as time-dependent factors. Admissions with no event are counted as survived.

The MELD score was calculated using the formula: MELD=9.57×ln (creatinine, in mg/dL)+3.78×ln(bilirubin in mg/dL) + 11.20 × ln (INR) + 6.43. 28

Ethics committee’s approval

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Frankfurt University Hospital (Ethikkommission der Goethe-Universität Frankfurt, decision 20-260).

RESULTS

Study cohort

During the assessed time period, 2485 admissions of patients with liver cirrhosis were documented, with a total of 87 admissions with falling incidents. Five hundred of these, including 7 with fall documentations, were excluded according to the inclusion criteria of the study (age and secured diagnosis of liver cirrhosis). Table 1 summarizes the characteristics of the 1985 admissions included in the final analysis. The admissions refer predominantly to male patients (1401/1985, 70.6%), with a median age of 61 years (59.9±11.6). The length of stay included a wide range of 1–248 days with a median of 5 days. In a total of 181 admissions (9.1%), patients died during their stay. Malnutrition scores were available for 1701 cases with 18.4% suffering from malnutrition with an NRS≥2. The main reason for admission was acute decompensation, that is, ascites and/or pleural effusions (33.6%), followed by hepatic encephalopathy (15.7%), and infections (14.6%). Other comparable frequent reasons were hepatorenal syndrome (13.8%) and bleedings (10.4%). Note that multiple reasons were counted in some cases. Most cases are from patients with alcohol-associated liver cirrhosis (51.2%), followed by hepatitis C (15.2%) and metabolic dysfunction–associated liver disease (14.8%).

Falling incidents

During the evaluation period, 101 falling incidents occurred in 80 admissions. Falls were described as a slip to the ground in 30% of the cases (n=24), 25% (n=20) patients tripped, and slumped down in 13.8% (n=11). In 28.8% of cases (n=23), the cause remained unclear, and the patients were found lying on the floor. In 42 admissions with falling incidents, patients showed no signs of fall-related injury. However, in 22 cases patients had bruises and abrasions, and in 14 cases patients had analgesics-dependent pain. Severe fall-related injury was reported in 5 cases (2 nose bleedings, 2 head lacerations, and 1 subdural hematoma).

As could be expected, length of stay was significantly longer in admissions with a documented falling incident with an average of 21.3±20.8 days versus 8.6±13.7 days (p=0.035). In admissions with falling incidents, 32.9% had an NRS≥2 compared to 21% in the nonfall group (p=0.027); again, acute decompensation was the leading cause of admission (37.5%). There were no differences between the 2 groups in terms of etiologies or secondary diagnoses, that is diabetes or arterial hypertension. However, in admissions with falling incidents, patients had a significantly higher proportion of an elevated risk of falling on admission according to the STRATIFY risk scale in cases where this was documented, which considers different risk factors associated with falling incidents (98.5% vs. 89.2%, p=0.026). Moreover, a higher C-reactive protein (p=0.020), as well as a higher MELD Score (p=0.0031) upon admission were noted in cases with a documented falling incident. Finally, in admissions with falling incidents, a significantly higher proportion of patients had an implanted TIPS (78.8% vs. 17.5%, p<0.001).

The incidence risk of falling events and potential factors was also analyzed in univariable and multivariable poisson regression. Overall, 101 falling incidents during 18,062 patient days resulted in an overall incidence rate (IR) of 0.0004 falling incidents in 100 patient days (95% CI: 0.0001–0.001) when accounting for potential intrapatient correlations during multiple stays with a mixed effect model. Association with in-hospital mortality

Given the high mortality rate in patients with documented falls, we further investigated in-hospital mortality in patients with liver cirrhosis. As mentioned previously, patients died during their stay in 181 admissions (9.1%). The percentage of admissions with patients with malnutrition (NRS≥2) was higher in the group with deaths during their hospital stay (37.8% vs. 20.2%, p<0.001). In-hospital mortality was also associated with a longer period of stay, with an average of 19.9 vs. 8.0 days (p<0.001). Furthermore, there was a significant difference in admissions with patients with alcohol-associated liver cirrhosis and hepatitis C (p=0.0019 and 0.029, respectively). There were no differences in acute decompensation, but there was a significantly higher proportion of admissions with patients with infection in the group of admissions with in-hospital death (p<0.001). Finally, laboratory changes, including C-reactive protein elevation, higher creatinine levels, hyperbilirubinemia, leukocytosis, and a higher MELD Score, were observed in patients who died during their stay. Results summarized in Supplementary Table S1, http://links.lww.com/HC9/B61.

Looking at the possible associations between in-hospital mortality in patients with liver cirrhosis and the most important potential factors, a multivariable time-to-event analysis was performed, including falling incidents as time-dependent variable and transplantation as competing events (Table 3). Admissions for infections (p=0.020), an elevated MELD>15 (p<0.001), malnutrition (Fig. 3, p<0.001) and—as strongest association—falling incidents (Figure 3, p<0.001) were significantly associated with in-hospital mortality. Presence of TIPS at hospital admission was not significantly associated (p=0.284) with in-hospital mortality.

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, the present study is the first to investigate the association of malnutrition and falls with the morbidity and mortality of hospitalized patients with cirrhosis. The first major finding was an independent association between malnutrition and TIPS with falling incidents and with time to the first falling incident. Second, admission for infections, falls, and malnutrition but not TIPS were independently associated with in-hospital mortality.

Our study included 1985 admissions of 886 patients with liver cirrhosis hospitalized between 2017 and 2019, thereby offering a representative view of hospitalizations in patients with liver cirrhosis. A total of 70.6% of the cases were from male patients with a median age of 61 years, reflecting the currently higher number of male patients with liver cirrhosis and median age. 2 Most patients had alcohol-associated liver disease, followed by viral causes and metabolic dysfunction–associated liver disease, which is in line with the increasing prevalence of toxic liver disease compared to earlier years, during which viral hepatitis was the major driver of chronic liver disease. 29

In the final analysis, 101 in-hospital falls in 80 admissions were documented leading to an estimated overall incidence rate of 0.0004 falling incidents in 100 patient days. In-hospital falls were observed in patients with longer periods of stay, higher MELD Scores (and sub-scores, respectively), malnutrition, and finally HE on admission. Various factors influencing the risk of falling have been analyzed in previous studies, such as HE, sarcopenia, and frequent use of benzodiazepines. For example, Tapper et al30,31 found that deprescribing zolpidem is strongly associated with fewer falls and consequent complications. Our study confirmed malnutrition together with TIPS as factors independently associated with in-hospital falls. In the analyzed cohort, 18.4% had an NRS score of ≥2, which was significantly independently associated with in-hospital falls. The cutoff was previously published in the KIRRHOS study, which showed equal group sizes. 32 Although there was a significant difference between patients who fell and those who did not, body mass index was not used to assess malnutrition by itself, as it could not be validated in liver cirrhosis and is clinically influenced by acute decompensation leading to a higher weight. The hospital-wide use of NRS-2002 as a screening tool for malnutrition on admission for all patients was found to be reproducible.25,33 In our study, the association of NRS with falls was robust in cases with or without admissions for acute decompensation with ascites and/or pleural effusions.

Additionally, laboratory parameters such as bilirubin and creatinine, sub-values included in the MELD Score, were significantly associated with in-hospital falls, reflecting disease severity in these patients.

In line with overt HE as a major chief complaint on admission, an implanted TIPS proved to be an independent factor strongly associated with in-hospital falls. 34 TIPS is a powerful tool for the prevention of decompensation and secondary prophylaxis of variceal bleeding. Nonetheless, patients need to be evaluated carefully before TIPS implantation regarding safety, both in terms of liver function and whole-body status (sarcopenia, falling risk, malnutrition, and episodes of covert HE).34,35 Nevertheless, TIPS itself was not significantly associated with in hospital mortality in multivariable analysis. Falls are known to prolong hospital stays,7,36 which was also observed in this study.

Fall-associated complications have been shown to increase morbidity and mortality in these patients. Complications after falls include immediate injuries such as fractures or bleeding, longer hospital stays, and increased risk of in-hospital death. 12 The latter was validated in the present study. In a total of 181 admissions, patients died during their hospital stay, of whom 21 had fallen during their stay. While most studies on falls in patients with liver cirrhosis include both hospitalized patients and outpatients, our study focused only on hospitalized patients with liver cirrhosis. This could be the reason why fewer fractures were observed in this study than in previous studies. The patients included in this study were mostly decompensated and therefore less likely to slip or stumble, as described by Murphy et al 12 and Faulkner et al, 37 but rather slide down to the floor while standing up.

Finally, this study showed that in-hospital falls and malnutrition as well as high MELD are independently associated with in-hospital mortality, which is in line with previously published data.7,8,10 This underlines the need for consistent prevention and screening of patients at risk of in-hospital falls. While no studies have discussed the association between in-hospital mortality and in-hospital falls in patients with liver cirrhosis, geriatric cohorts have been examined in various studies36,38 and have shown higher mortality in patients after in-hospital falls. Our data have proven an association between in-hospital falls and in-hospital mortality in patients with liver cirrhosis. It is valid to assume that the morbidity of patients with geriatric and cirrhosis is associated with sarcopenia, malnutrition, and mental dysfunction. Other factors significantly associated with in-hospital mortality in this study were length of stay and a higher MELD score. It has been shown that prolonged hospital stay is associated with higher in-hospital mortality, 7 as mentioned above and observed in our cohort.

The MELD Score, which reflects disease severity in patients with liver cirrhosis, was significantly and independently associated with in-hospital mortality in our cohort. Tantai et al 39 have shown that in a recent meta-analysis. The mentioned study has also shown that patients with a MELD score>15 are prone to infections and HE, which have been drivers for admission in our cohort.

The importance of the NRS regarding mortality has been discussed by Hersberger et al, 40 highlighting an increase in in-hospital mortality with an increase in the NRS score. Given the significant improvement in survival with individualized therapy and nutritional support, 41 early assessment and preventive strategies are necessary. A recent study published by Román et al 22 proved that long-term interventions including nutritional support and a home exercise program both reduced the incidence of clinical events and hospitalizations.

Complications of portal hypertension, including decompensation with ascites, HRS and/or variceal bleeding, are the most frequent reasons for the implantation of a TIPS. Mortality in this fragile group of patients with advanced chronic liver disease and clinically relevant portal hypertension was 10.5%. 42 In our study, 20.3% of the patients with implanted TIPS died during their hospital stay. Considering the disease progression and the abovementioned risk factors for in-hospital mortality, these patients represent the sickest of all patients, supported by the higher MELD Score in this subgroup.

Overall, our study showed a high in-hospital mortality rate, as has been shown previously for hospitalized patients with liver cirrhosis.

The strength of this study is the representative size of the patients included. The cohort represents the current distribution of etiologies and is in line with previously published smaller cohorts, looking at in-hospital falls in patients with liver cirrhosis. Furthermore, the collective is homogeneous in the severity of disease, being treated at a major liver transplantation center, and including patients waiting for liver transplantation or suffering from acute-on-chronic liver failure. To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the association between malnutrition and in-hospital falls as well as in-hospital mortality.

The limitations of this study are its retrospective, single-center design. In addition, some important information assessing the risk of falling incidents as treatment with psychoactive medications with benzodiazepines, zolpidem or opioids are missing, as data were taken from the day of admission where not all information was available. Furthermore, the NRS-2002 as a screening tool for patients with liver cirrhosis has not been validated 33 ; nonetheless, it proves to be an easily applicable and reproducible tool, making it convenient to use on admission for all patients.

We identified 2 factors, malnutrition and in-hospital falls, that can be screened for and preventive measures taken. Individual nutritional risk screening should be performed for all patients with liver cirrhosis, ideally on a recurrent basis in the outpatient clinic but also every time on admission. In addition to the recurrent evaluation of required therapy adjustments regarding the complications of portal hypertension with beta-blockers and/or diuretics, there should be a continuous NRS assessment during outpatient visits.

Patients screened for malnutrition should receive individualized nutritionist support and regular follow-up to alleviate the risk of further complications as has been shown by Román et al, 22 above all in-hospital falls, which is the second major risk for the analyzed group of patients. 39 Falls have multiple causes that need to be addressed. Documentation of in-hospital falls should not be limited to paperwork; rather, it should lead to an investigation of patient treatment and improve it as there is a high mortality associated with falls. This is not only for medical, but also for medicolegal reasons. 13 Malnutrition, as mentioned before, needs to be addressed, as well as the immediate treatment of other risk-enhancing factors. Implanted TIPS has been shown to be an independently associated risk factor for both in-hospital falls and in-hospital mortality, and is an enhancing risk factor for HE. Therefore, patients should be carefully selected before TIPS implantation, considering other risk factors for in-hospital falls such as malnutrition or sarcopenia. In addition, close in-patient and outpatient monitoring and surveillance of patients with implanted TIPS regarding subclinical and clinical hepatic encephalopathy as well as nutritional risks is warranted. 11

Further studies are needed to evaluate specific measures of malnutrition and falls. A prospective study seems ethically difficult, putting patients at risk, but a comparison of interventions or combined treatments is urgently needed.
