Radial Wall Strain and the Stepwise Integration of Physiology and Vulnerability in Revascularization Decision Making
Authors: Ioannis Skalidis, Peter Wenaweser, Pascal Meier, Stephane Cook, Mario Togni
Published: 2026

We read with great interest the recent survey by Li et al1 evaluating the stepwise impact of Murray law-based quantitative flow ratio (μFR), radial wall strain (RWS), and optical coherence tomography (OCT) on revascularization decisions for angiographically intermediate lesions. The authors are to be commended for providing a granular depiction of how sequential physiologic and morphologic information reshapes therapeutic intent.

Several aspects of the study merit further reflection. First, the observed bidirectional modulation of treatment recommendations, with reduced revascularization following μFR disclosure and a subsequent increase after RWS and OCT availability, highlights the evolving paradigm from an ischemia-centered approach toward integrated assessment incorporating both coronary physiology and plaque vulnerability. It would be informative to further characterize lesions in which RWS prompted revascularization despite μFR >0.80. Were these predominantly located within the physiological gray zone, or did RWS exert an independent influence across the full μFR spectrum? Such stratified analysis could help clarify whether RWS refines physiologic ambiguity or introduces an orthogonal decision axis.

Second, the substantial proportion of cases transitioning from standard to intensive medical therapy after RWS disclosure suggests that vulnerability assessment may meaningfully affect pharmacologic intensification independent of percutaneous coronary intervention. This raises an important clinical question: should RWS be conceptualized primarily as a tool to guide revascularization, or rather as a risk-stratification metric to personalize preventive therapy? Clarifying this positioning may be central to future algorithm development.

Third, the discrepancy between the 28.6% decision change following qualitative OCT disclosure and the lower 16.1% change projected under strict lipid-to-cap ratio–based guidance is particularly intriguing. Does this difference reflect a tendency toward greater revascularization when plaque vulnerability is assessed visually rather than through predefined quantitative thresholds? If so, might quantitative indices, such as lipid-to-cap ratio or RWS, help align vulnerability-driven decisions with a more reproducible, risk-calibrated framework? Further analysis of interobserver variability in qualitative OCT interpretation vs quantitative metric–guided decisions would be highly informative.

Overall, this study provides important insight into how incremental integration of coronary physiology and plaque vulnerability reshapes revascularization decision making in intermediate lesions. The frequent discordance between physiologic and vulnerability signals underscores the need for a structured, evidence-based algorithm to guide their integration. Prospective outcome-driven studies evaluating discordant physiology–vulnerability phenotypes will be essential to determine whether such integration translates into improved clinical outcomes.
