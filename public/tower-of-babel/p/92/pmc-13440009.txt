Prognostic determinants of mortality and clinical outcomes in sepsis: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Chunlan Yang, Jianan Huang, Jieming Zhou, Wenjia Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo identify key prognostic determinants in sepsis patients by evaluating clinical, laboratory, and therapeutic factors associated with 30-day mortality, organ failure, and hospitalization duration.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study was conducted on 196 ICU-admitted sepsis patients meeting the Sepsis-3 criteria from June 2019 to May 2024. Baseline characteristics, organ dysfunction scores (qSOFA, MODS), laboratory markers (lactate, bilirubin, platelet count), and cardiac function parameters (ejection fraction, BNP, troponin) were analyzed. Primary outcomes included 30-day all-cause mortality, incidence of organ failure (defined as new-onset dysfunction of ≥ 2 organ systems lasting ≥ 48 h), ICU length of stay, and total hospital length of stay.ResultsElevated lactate levels (OR 1.320 per 1 mmol/L increase, 95% CI 1.134–1.566; p < 0.001) and reduced ejection fraction (OR 0.936 per 1% increase, 95% CI 0.893–0.977; p = 0.001) were independent predictors of 30-day mortality. Mechanical ventilation was significantly associated with prolonged ICU stay by an average of 5.950 days (95% CI 2.938–8.962; p < 0.001). Additionally, both ventilator use (β = 4.21 days, p = 0.009) and platelet count (β=-0.016 days per 10⁹/L increase, p = 0.022) were independent predictors of total hospital length of stay. However, no independent predictors of organ failure were identified in multivariable analysis.ConclusionLactate and ejection fraction are critical prognostic markers for 30-day mortality in sepsis, while mechanical ventilation and platelet count significantly influence hospitalization duration.

Introduction

Sepsis is a critical condition involving organ dysfunction resulting from an imbalanced host response to infection, and it continues to pose a significant global health burden, with mortality rates surpassing 20% globally [1, 2]. In the United States, sepsis accounts for over 200,000 deaths annually, with mortality rates reaching 14.9% in severe sepsis and 34.2% in septic shock. In China, the burden of sepsis is also substantial. A national cross-sectional survey reported that approximately 20% of ICU patients develop sepsis, with a 90-day mortality rate as high as 35.5%, highlighting the considerable disease burden in this population [3]. Furthermore, a large multicenter prospective cohort study involving 4,910 critically ill patients in China demonstrated that 42.5% developed sepsis or septic shock, with significantly increased mortality, particularly among patients with septic shock, underscoring the severity and clinical impact of sepsis in Chinese ICUs [4].

The pathophysiology of sepsis is complex and involves systemic inflammatory responses, immune dysfunction, and coagulation abnormalities [1]. Despite advances in antimicrobial and supportive therapies, sepsis outcomes remain highly variable, influenced by host factors, pathogen characteristics, and clinical interventions. In addition to bacterial infections, fungal pathogens have increasingly been recognized as important contributors to sepsis, particularly among critically ill and immunocompromised populations, and are associated with high mortality and prolonged hospitalization. Emerging evidence has highlighted the growing incidence and substantial clinical burden of invasive fungal infections in ICU settings [5–7]. Identifying modifiable prognostic factors—such as 30-day mortality, organ failure, total length of hospital stay (LOS), and ICU LOS—is crucial for risk stratification and personalized management.

Previous studies have identified several prognostic indicators of sepsis, including age, comorbidities, and inflammatory biomarkers (e.g., procalcitonin and C-reactive protein) [8, 9]. Scoring systems such as the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) and Sepsis-Induced Coagulopathy (SIC) scores have been strongly associated with mortality risk [10]. However, the optimal thresholds for these biomarkers and their dynamic changes during hospitalization remain uncertain.

Multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS) is a hallmark of severe sepsis, driven by persistent inflammation and immune suppression [11]. Factors such as delayed antibiotic administration, hypoalbuminemia, and genetic polymorphisms in immune-related genes (e.g., IL-6 and TNF-α) exacerbate organ damage [12, 13]. Additionally, post-intensive care syndrome (PICS), characterized by chronic inflammation and catabolism, may prolong organ recovery and increase long-term mortality [12].

Prolonged hospital and ICU stays are associated with complications such as delirium, inappropriate sedation, and secondary infections. Data from the MIMIC-III database indicate that age (50–65 years), high SOFA scores, and respiratory or abdominal infections are linked to extended ICU stays [14, 15]. However, the role of microbial factors, such as multidrug-resistant pathogens, in determining LOS remains underexplored. While previous study has investigated individual prognostic factors, most have focused on single endpoints (e.g., mortality) rather than integrating multiple clinical outcomes [16].

Although numerous studies have investigated prognostic factors in sepsis, their findings remain inconsistent across different populations and settings. For instance, biomarkers such as heparin-binding protein and D-dimer have been reported to predict sepsis outcomes [17], while other studies have developed predictive models, such as a 30-day sepsis risk model in patients with traumatic brain injury based on the MIMIC-IV database [18]. However, most existing evidence is derived from developed countries, and data from developing regions remain scarce, limiting the generalizability of these findings [4, 19–22]. Moreover, most studies have primarily focused on epidemiological characteristics or single clinical endpoints, while comprehensive analyses integrating multiple prognostic outcomes remain scarce.

To address these gaps, this retrospective cohort study aims to comprehensively analyze clinical, laboratory, and therapeutic factors associated with four key outcomes: 30-day mortality, organ failure, total LOS, and ICU LOS. By identifying modifiable prognostic factors, this study seeks to provide valuable insights for early risk stratification and improved clinical management of sepsis patients.

Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective cohort study consecutively included consecutive sepsis patients admitted to the Affiliated Hospital of Guangdong Medical University between June 2019 to May 2024. Inclusion criteria required: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) fulfillment of Sepsis-3 criteria [23] with both (a) confirmed or suspected infection validated by microbiological evidence or clinical diagnosis, and (b) Sepsis was defined according to the Sepsis-3 criteria as suspected or confirmed infection accompanied by acute organ dysfunction (an increase in SOFA score ≥ 2 from baseline). The qSOFA score was used only as a rapid screening tool rather than a diagnostic criterion; 3) availability of complete hemodynamic and laboratory data. Exclusion criteria included: (1) pregnancy or lactation status; (2) terminal illness with life expectancy < 24 h; (3) sepsis diagnosis established > 72 h before admission; (4) incomplete organ function assessment records; (5) active treatment limitations (e.g., do-not-resuscitate orders); (6) known severe chronic comorbidities (Class III/IV heart failure, Child-Pugh C cirrhosis, or metastatic malignancies).

Data collection

Baseline characteristics were collected within 24 h of ICU admission, including demographic data, vital signs (heart rate, respiratory rate, blood pressure), laboratory parameters (lactate, bilirubin, platelet count), and organ dysfunction scores (qSOFA, MODS). Cardiac function was assessed using echocardiographic ejection fraction and serum biomarkers (BNP, troponin). The qSOFA score was calculated based on three clinical criteria: respiratory rate ≥ 22 breaths/min, altered mentation (Glasgow Coma Scale < 15), and systolic blood pressure ≤ 100 mmHg [24]. MODS was determined by evaluating six organ systems (respiratory, renal, hepatic, cardiovascular, hematologic, and neurologic) according to the Marshall scoring system, with the worst recorded values in the first 24 h used as baseline [25]. Laboratory values (PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio, serum creatinine, bilirubin, platelet count) were obtained from standardized hospital assays, while cardiovascular dysfunction was quantified using pressure-adjusted heart rate calculations. Mechanical ventilation was defined as ventilatory support initiated during ICU stay, and patients who were already intubated prior to ICU admission may not have been fully captured. Mechanical ventilation included both invasive and non-invasive ventilation initiated during ICU stay.

Outcomes

The primary outcome (30-day mortality) was calculated from the time of ICU admission. Total hospital length of stay was defined as the duration from hospital admission (including emergency department presentation) to discharge or death.

Secondary outcomes included: (1) organ failure incidence, determined by new-onset dysfunction in ≥ 2 organ systems persisting > 48 h; (2) ICU LOS, calculated from ICU admission to discharge or death; and (3) total hospital LOS, measured from initial emergency department presentation to final discharge.

Statistical analysis

Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and all variables were found to be non-normally distributed. Therefore, continuous variables were expressed as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were analyzed using Pearson’s χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Univariate logistic regression was performed for binary outcomes, and linear regression was used for continuous outcomes to identify candidate predictors. Variables with a p-value < 0.05 in univariate analyses were included in multivariable regression models. Effect estimates were reported as odds ratios (ORs) or β coefficients, along with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Statistical analyses were conducted using R language 4.2.1, with significance set at two-tailed p < 0.05.

Results

The study included 196 sepsis patients, with no significant age difference between non-survivors (n = 95) and survivors (n = 101) (median 68 years, IQR 57–78 vs. 68 years, IQR 57–78; p = 0.937). Male predominance was observed in both groups (72.6% vs. 64.4%, p = 0.223) (Table 1).

Key differences in organ dysfunction markers were noted: non-survivors had significantly higher lactate [3.58 (2.15–6.38) vs. 2.08 (1.35–3.37) mmol/L, p < 0.001], bilirubin [16.30 (8.95–31.55) vs. 10.70 (7.40–19.90) µmol/L, p = 0.017], and MODS scores [9 (6–12) vs. 6 (4–9), p < 0.001]. Additionally, non-survivors exhibited higher cardiac biomarkers, including BNP [8,670 (2,333.5–35,000) vs. 2,410 (721-6,142) pg/mL, p < 0.001], creatinine [153 (99–336) vs. 120 (88–217) µmol/L, p = 0.020], and troponin [0.15 (0.05–0.45) vs. 0.04 (0.02–0.10) ng/mL, p < 0.001]. Survivors had better oxygenation (oxygen index 278.50 vs. 238.00, p = 0.002), higher Glasgow Coma Scale scores [14 (8–15) vs. 10 (7–14), p = 0.002], and preserved left ventricular function (ejection fraction 58% [53–60] vs. 55% [48–60], p = 0.001). Notably, mechanical ventilation was more frequently administered to survivors (56.4% vs. 20.0%, p < 0.001) (Table 1).

Primary outcome: 30-day mortality

Univariate analysis identified oxygen index, GCS score, creatinine, MODS score, lactate, BNP, ejection fraction, and ventilator use as factors associated with 30-day mortality. Multivariable logistic regression demonstrated that lactate (OR 1.320 per 1 mmol/L increase, 95% CI 1.134–1.566; p < 0.001) and ejection fraction (OR 0.936 per 1% increase, 95% CI 0.893–0.977; p = 0.001) were independent predictors of 30-day mortality (Table 2).

Secondary outcomes

Multivariable logistic regression did not identify any independent predictors for organ failure in sepsis patients (Table 3). Mechanical ventilation was associated with prolonged ICU stays by 5.950 days (95% CI 2.938–8.962; p < 0.001) (Table 4). For total hospitalization duration, both ventilator use (β = 4.21 days, 95% CI 1.04–7.37; p = 0.009) and platelet count (β=-0.016 days per 10⁹/L increase, 95% CI -0.030 to- 0.002; p = 0.022) were independent predictors (Table 5).

Discussion

The retrospective cohort design allowed for the evaluation of temporal relationships between baseline exposures and subsequent clinical outcomes. This study found that lactate levels and ejection fraction were independent predictors of 30-day mortality in sepsis patients. Elevated lactate levels were associated with an increased risk of mortality, whereas better left ventricular function correlated with improved survival outcomes. Additionally, mechanical ventilation was associated with prolonged ICU stays, and both ventilator use and platelet count influenced total hospitalization duration. The finding that mechanical ventilation was more administered in survivors than non-survivors appears counterintuitive and should be interpreted with caution. This phenomenon may be partially explained by selection bias and treatment decision-making processes inherent to retrospective studies. Although patients with documented do-not-resuscitate (DNR) orders were excluded, it is possible that some critically ill patients who eventually died did not receive mechanical ventilation due to early treatment limitations, family preferences, or rapid clinical deterioration prior to intubation. Additionally, variations in the timing of ventilation initiation and differences between invasive and non-invasive ventilation strategies may further contribute to this discrepancy. Therefore, this finding should not be interpreted as a protective effect of mechanical ventilation but rather as a reflection of underlying clinical complexity. These findings suggest that early recognition and management of circulatory dysfunction in sepsis patients are crucial for optimizing clinical outcomes.

This study, through multivariable logistic regression analysis, identified lactate levels and ejection fraction as independent predictors of 30-day mortality in sepsis patients. This finding is consistent with previous studies, highlighting the significance of lactate levels in the prognostic assessment of sepsis patients. Lactate is a critical biomarker of tissue hypoperfusion and organ dysfunction in sepsis. In septic conditions, infection-induced immune dysregulation can lead to shock, subsequently impairing both microcirculation and macrocirculation, resulting in lactic acidosis [26, 27]. Studies have demonstrated that persistently elevated lactate levels are strongly associated with poor prognosis in sepsis patients [28, 29]. Furthermore, early lactate clearance has been linked to improved outcomes in sepsis, making lactate levels and their dynamic changes key indicators for assessing disease severity and prognosis [30, 31]. Consequently, lactate monitoring has been incorporated into sepsis management guidelines.

Ejection fraction is an essential parameter for evaluating cardiac function, and its reduction may indicate underlying cardiac dysfunction in sepsis patients [32]. Impaired cardiac function can compromise systemic organ perfusion and oxygenation, thereby increasing the risk of mortality. Cardiac dysfunction is commonly observed in sepsis patients and is closely associated with the development of MODS [33]. The findings of this study underscore the critical role of lactate levels and ejection fraction in sepsis prognosis. Clinicians should closely monitor changes in these two parameters to more accurately assess disease severity and predict outcomes, thereby facilitating the development of more effective treatment strategies. Future research should further explore the combined application of lactate levels, ejection fraction, and other clinical indicators to enhance the accuracy of sepsis prognosis assessment.

Our study found that in patients with sepsis, mechanical ventilation is significantly associated with prolonged ICU length of stay, and both mechanical ventilation and platelet count are independent predictors of total hospitalization time. These findings provide important evidence for optimizing the treatment strategies for sepsis patients. Mechanical ventilation is a common therapeutic modality for sepsis patients, but its use is closely related to prolonged hospitalization. Previous research has shown that early initiation of mechanical ventilation may improve the prognosis of sepsis patients. A multicenter cohort study found that early mechanical ventilation (initiated on the first day of ICU admission) was associated with lower ICU mortality and shorter ICU length of stay [34]. This indicates that the timing of mechanical ventilation is of great importance to the prognosis of sepsis patients. However, mechanical ventilation may also lead to complications such as ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) and ventilator-associated lung injury (VALI), which can prolong hospitalization [34]. Therefore, clinicians need to identify patients who require mechanical ventilation at an early stage and optimize ventilation strategies to reduce hospitalization time.

Lower platelet counts were associated with prolonged hospitalization in our study, consistent with the established role of thrombocytopenia as a marker of disease severity in sepsis. Platelet count is an important prognostic indicator for sepsis patients. A study analyzing the trajectory patterns of platelet count in sepsis patients during the first four days after admission found that patients with a decreasing platelet count (DS trajectory pattern) had significantly longer ICU stays compared to those with an increasing platelet count (AS trajectory pattern) [35]. Furthermore, the study also pointed out that thrombocytopenia is a common clinical issue in ICU patients and is associated with increased morbidity and mortality [36]. These findings further support that thrombocytopenia is not only associated with short-term prognosis but also contributes to prolonged hospitalization in sepsis patients.

Thrombocytopenia may indicate an inflammatory response and microvascular dysfunction, which can affect the speed of patient recovery [37]. In sepsis patients, platelet activation can be directly induced by inflammatory mediators or microbial components, leading to the initiation of the coagulation cascade and microthrombosis formation, ultimately resulting in ischemia and multiple organ dysfunction [38]. Additionally, platelets play a crucial role in maintaining endothelial integrity and reducing vascular permeability, which may help regulate the inflammatory process and host defense mechanisms [39, 40].

The findings of this study highlight the importance of optimizing mechanical ventilation strategies and monitoring platelet count in sepsis patients. Future research could further explore how early intervention and optimized ventilation management can reduce the impact of mechanical ventilation on hospital length of stay while also evaluating the potential role of platelet count in disease prognosis. Additionally, studies could focus on the effects of different ventilation strategies (such as non-invasive vs. invasive ventilation) on hospital length of stay and how improving overall patient management can help shorten hospitalization duration. This study further confirms the significant role of mechanical ventilation and platelet count in the hospitalization duration of sepsis patients, providing valuable insights for clinical practice.

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration in the interpretation of the findings. First, its retrospective nature and single-center setting may constrain the external validity and limit the applicability of the results to broader populations and diverse clinical environments. The reliance on existing medical records could introduce potential biases due to inconsistencies in data recording and missing information. Secondly, the study did not account for the dynamic changes of biomarkers over time, which may be crucial for understanding their prognostic significance. Thirdly, genetic factors and individual variations in immune response, which may significantly influence sepsis outcomes, were not evaluated. Fourthly, the study did not explore the potential impact of non-invasive ventilation strategies on hospital length of stay, nor did it include long-term follow-up data beyond 30-day mortality. Additionally, the exclusion of patients with treatment limitations and end-stage comorbidities may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of our findings to the broader sepsis population. Furthermore, the unexpectedly lower rate of mechanical ventilation in non-survivors may reflect unmeasured confounders such as treatment limitations or incomplete capture of pre-ICU ventilation, representing a potential source of bias. Future investigations should overcome these limitations by employing prospective, multicenter designs, incorporating comprehensive data collection, and extending follow-up durations.

Conclusion

In this retrospective cohort study analyzing factors influencing prognosis in sepsis patients, lactate levels and ejection fraction were identified as independent predictors of 30-day mortality. Higher lactate levels were associated with an increased risk of mortality, whereas better left ventricular function correlated with improved survival outcomes. Additionally, mechanical ventilation was linked to prolonged ICU stays, and both ventilator use and platelet count influenced total hospitalization duration. These findings highlight the importance of early recognition and management of circulatory dysfunction in sepsis patients to optimize clinical outcomes.
