AQbD-driven RP-HPLC method for complete resolution of pantoprazole regioisomeric impurities with integrated multi-dimensional sustainability assessment
Authors: Thirupathi Reddy Gaddam, Chinna Gangi Reddy Nallagondu, Narendra Kumar Masani, Aparna Devi Kareti, Hanuman Reddy Vemulapati, Hemanth Kumar Chanduluru
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pantoprazole is a widely prescribed proton pump inhibitor, and accurate control of its related substances is essential to ensure drug quality and patient safety. However, existing pharmacopeial and reported chromatographic methods often fail to achieve adequate resolution between the critical regioisomeric impurities D and F, resulting in their co-quantification. This study aimed to develop a robust, stability-indicating, and environmentally responsible RP-HPLC method for the simultaneous determination of pantoprazole and its six related substances. A systematic quality-by-design approach was employed, integrating risk assessment and Central Composite Design to optimize critical chromatographic parameters. The optimized method achieved complete baseline separation of all impurities, including the challenging D/F regioisomeric pair, with resolution values greater than 2.0 and a total runtime of less than 12 min. Method validation, performed in accordance with ICH Q2(R2), demonstrated satisfactory accuracy (recoveries of 96.8–99.7%), precision (%RSD < 2.0%), and excellent linearity (r > 0.998). The environmental and operational sustainability of the method was confirmed using multiple assessment tools, including AGREE, MoGAPI, BAGI, RAPI and RGB12, indicating a favorable greenness and overall performance profile. Overall, the proposed method provides a reliable, efficient, and sustainable analytical solution for routine impurity profiling of pantoprazole, with improved selectivity and robustness compared to existing methods, supporting its application in pharmaceutical quality control and regulatory settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13065-026-01852-8.

Introduction

Pantoprazole (PTZ), a benzimidazole-based proton pump inhibitor, is widely used in the management of gastroesophageal reflux disease, peptic ulcer disease, and Zollinger–Ellison syndrome. By irreversibly inhibiting the gastric H⁺/K⁺-ATPase, PTZ provides potent and sustained suppression of gastric acid secretion. Its clinical importance, global consumption, and inclusion on the WHO Model List of Essential Medicines underscore the need for stringent control of its impurity profile to ensure quality, efficacy, and patient safety [1, 2].

Pharmaceutical impurities may arise from synthesis, degradation, process intermediates, or storage-related transformations. PTZ is known to generate six structurally related substances, each arising from distinct origins and possessing unique chemical properties (Table S1). Impurity A (Sulfone) and Impurity B (Sulfide) are process-related species formed during oxidative and reductive steps in synthesis. Impurity C (DBT derivative) can arise as a synthesis by-product or as a degradant. Impurity D and Impurity F are regioisomeric impurities arising from positional rearrangements during the coupling steps; due to their close structural similarity and nearly identical hydrophobicities, they pose severe chromatographic co-elution challenges. Impurity E results from side-chain modification during process quenching steps [3, 4].

The United States Pharmacopeia (USP) and European Pharmacopeia (EP) monographs for PTZ list these impurities; however, both pharmacopeias quantify Impurities D and F collectively because the monograph methods cannot resolve these two critical isomers. Several reported HPLC–UV methods similarly quantify only a subset of impurities (typically A–C), leaving the regioisomeric pair unresolved. A comparative assessment of published methods (Table 1) reveals limitations, including long runtime, incomplete impurity coverage, insufficient resolution between D and F, and inconsistent robustness across laboratories. This limitation emphasizes the need for improved analytical strategies for comprehensive impurity profiling [5–10].

Traditional chromatographic optimization, based on one-factor-at-a-time (OFAT) experimentation, fails to adequately explore multi-factor interactions, which are crucial in resolving closely eluting isomers. Analytical Quality-by-Design (AQbD) offers a systematic, science-driven approach that employs risk assessment, design of experiments (DoE), and response surface modeling to define critical method parameters, establish method understanding, and identify a robust analytical design space in accordance with ICH Q14/Q12 [11–15].

Equally important is the environmental impact of chromatographic methods. Routine impurity testing in quality control laboratories results in substantial solvent consumption, hazardous waste generation, and high energy use. Modern Green Analytical Chemistry (GAC) promotes sustainable analytical practices through principles such as solvent minimization, safer reagent selection, energy-efficient instrumentation, and waste reduction [16–18]. Recently developed multi-criteria evaluation tools, including AGREE, MoGAPI, BAGI, RAPI, and the RGB12 whiteness assessment, enable comprehensive quantification of environmental, economic, and operational sustainability.

In this context, this study focuses on developing an AQbD-optimized, stability-indicating RP-HPLC method capable of individually quantifying pantoprazole and its six related substances, while considering environmental factors, including complete resolution of the regioisomeric pair D and F with a significantly shortened analytical runtime. Using a Central Composite Design (CCD), the critical method parameters were optimized to achieve robust separation. The method was validated in accordance with ICH Q2(R2), evaluated under forced-degradation conditions, and subjected to a comprehensive greenness/whiteness assessment to establish its analytical superiority and environmental sustainability relative to existing methods. Experimental

Materials and reagents

PTZ (Purity:99.69%) sample, all related synthetic impurities such as related-compound-A (98.86%), compound-B (98.99%), compound-C (99.05%), compound-D and Impurity-F (99.42%), and compound-E (99.05%), were gifted from M/S Synpure Laboratories (Hyderabad, Telangana, India). Analytical grade Disodium hydrogen phosphate monohydrate (analytical grade, ≥ 99.0%), orthophosphoric acid (85%, analytical grade), and sodium hydroxide (analytical grade, ≥ 98.0%) were procured from Merck (Mumbai, Maharashtra, India). Acetonitrile and methanol (HPLC grade) were obtained from Rankem (Mumbai, Maharashtra, India). Hydrochloric acid (analytical grade, 37%) and hydrogen peroxide (analytical grade, 30%) were procured from Merck (Mumbai, Maharashtra, India). Ultra-pure water was obtained from a Milli-Q water purification system (Millipore, Bedford, MA, USA) and was used throughout the analysis.

Instrumentation

The chromatographic analysis was performed using an Agilent 1260 Infinity II high-performance liquid chromatography system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), equipped with an autosampler (G7129A), quaternary pump (G7111A), column oven (G7116A), and diode array detector (G4212B) based on a Max-Light cartridge flow cell with multiple-wavelength detection capability. Data acquisition and processing were carried out using Agilent ChemStation software (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Chromatographic separation was achieved using a Waters X-Bridge C18 column (150 × 4.6 mm, 3.5 μm; Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA). Experimental design and statistical optimization were performed using Design-Expert software (Version 13, Stat-Ease Inc., Minneapolis, MN, USA). An analytical balance (Mettler Toledo XPE205, Columbus, OH, USA) was used for weighing samples and standards. A hot-air oven (Bionics Scientific Technologies, Model BST/HAO-1124, Delhi, India) was used for thermal degradation studies.

Greenness and sustainability assessments were conducted using AGREE software (version 1.0), the MoGAPI, RAPI, and the BAGI evaluation framework, while the RGB12 whiteness assessment was calculated using established literature methodology. All data processing and graphical representations were performed using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA).

Preparation of buffer solution

An accurately weighed quantity of disodium hydrogen phosphate monohydrate was transferred to prepare a 0.05 M solution, and the pH was adjusted to 7.0 ± 0.02 using diluted orthophosphoric acid.

Preparation of diluent

A 50:50 (v/v) water: ACN mixture was used as the diluent to prepare all final solutions of the PTZ standard and impurity stock solutions.

Preparation of sample solution

Accurately weighed an amount equivalent to 10.0 mg of PTZ sample and transferred it into a 10 mL volumetric flask. The mixture was sonicated for 5 min to ensure complete dissolution. The flask was equilibrated to room temperature and diluted to volume with the diluent. The resultant solution was filtered through a 0.22 μm PTFE syringe filter before injection into the HPLC system.

Preparation of impurity stock solution

Approximately 5 mg of each impurity was accurately weighed and transferred into a 50 mL volumetric flask. The mixture was sonicated for 5 min to ensure complete dissolution. The flask was equilibrated to room temperature and diluted to volume with the diluent. The final stock solution of impurity was diluted to 1.0 µg/mL. The solutions were filtered through a 0.22 μm PTFE syringe filter before being injected into the HPLC system.

Forced degradation study of PTZ

The objective of the forced degradation studies was to establish the degradation mechanisms, including hydrolysis (acidic and basic), oxidation, and thermal degradation of the drug substance. Also, to differentiate the degradation products from the process-related impurities [19–21].

Forced degradation studies of PTZ were conducted under stress conditions, including acidic, basic, oxidative, and thermal conditions. Acidic and basic conditions were achieved by adding 5 mL of 5 M HCl and 5 M NaOH, respectively. Oxidative degradation was carried out with 5 mL of 30% H2O2 in the 25 mL volumetric flask. Thermal degradation was conducted at 105 °C for 5 days by spreading a thin layer of the solid PTZ drug substance on a Petri dish and placing the Petri dish in a hot-air oven.

AQbD workflow

Analytical target profile (ATP)

The Analytical Target Profile (ATP) was defined to ensure the development of a robust, stability-indicating RP-HPLC method capable of accurately and precisely quantifying pantoprazole and its six related substances. The method was required to achieve complete baseline separation of all impurities, particularly the critical regioisomeric pair Imp-D and Imp-F, with resolution greater than 2.0. Additional ATP criteria included acceptable peak symmetry (tailing factor < 1.5), high sensitivity (LOQ below specification limits), and a total runtime of less than 12 min. The method was also expected to be environmentally sustainable and suitable for routine quality control applications.

Identification of critical method attributes (CMAs)

Based on the ATP, Critical Method Attributes (CMAs) were identified as key performance indicators governing method quality. These included resolution between critical impurity pairs (PTZ–Imp-D, Imp-D–Imp-F, Imp-D–Imp-B, and Imp-B–Imp-E), peak symmetry, retention behavior, and sensitivity. Among these, the resolution between Imp-D and Imp-F was considered the most critical attribute, given their structural similarity and tendency to co-elute in existing methods.

Risk assessment

A systematic risk assessment was performed in accordance with ICH Q9 guidelines to identify and prioritize variables affecting CMAs. Initially, an Ishikawa (fishbone) diagram (Figure S1) was constructed to identify potential sources of variability, including method parameters (pH, organic composition, flow rate, temperature), instrument factors, sample characteristics, and environmental conditions. Subsequently, Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA) was employed to quantitatively evaluate the risk associated with each variable. Risk Priority Numbers (RPNs) were calculated based on severity, occurrence, and detectability. Variables with high RPN values were identified as Critical Method Parameters (CMPs). Based on this analysis, buffer pH, acetonitrile composition, flow rate, and column temperature were selected as CMPs due to their significant influence on chromatographic selectivity and resolution. The selected CMPs were chosen based on their strong mechanistic influence on chromatographic performance. Buffer pH directly affects the ionization state of pantoprazole and its impurities, thereby controlling selectivity. Organic phase composition governs elution strength and resolution, while flow rate and column temperature influence mass transfer kinetics and peak shape. The selected responses (Critical Quality Attributes (CQAs)) were resolution values between critical peak pairs, as these directly reflect method selectivity and regulatory suitability for impurity profiling. This selection ensures the optimization process focuses on achieving meaningful improvements in analytical performance.

Design space establishment and validation (MODR)

Response Surface Methodology (RSM) was employed to develop mathematical models describing the relationship between CMPs and CMAs. Three-dimensional response surface plots and desirability functions were used to identify optimal conditions. A Method Operable Design Region (MODR) was established, representing a multidimensional region within which all CMAs met predefined acceptance criteria. The MODR was validated by conducting confirmatory experiments at selected points within the design space, demonstrating consistent method performance. This design space provides operational flexibility and aligns with ICH Q14 recommendations for lifecycle management [22, 23].

Control strategy and method operability

A control strategy was established based on the identified MODR to ensure consistent method performance during routine application. Critical method parameters were controlled within defined ranges: buffer pH (± 0.1), organic composition (± 2%), flow rate (± 0.1 mL/min), and column temperature (± 2 °C). System suitability criteria, including resolution (> 2.0), tailing factor (< 1.5), and %RSD (< 2.0), were used to verify system performance prior to analysis. This control strategy ensures robustness, reproducibility, and regulatory compliance of the developed method.

Method validation

Validation was performed in accordance with ICH Q2(R2) [24] for specificity, linearity, accuracy, precision, limit of detection (LOD), limit of quantitation (LOQ), robustness, and system suitability. All results complied with regulatory acceptance criteria.

Results and discussion

The development of a stability-indicating and sustainability-aligned RP-HPLC method for PTZ and its six official impurities was pursued through a structured sequence of chromatographic evaluation, AQbD-assisted optimization, forced degradation testing, validation, and greenness/whiteness characterization. Each experimental step was guided by a deep understanding of PTZ’s physicochemical properties, the structural behavior of its impurities (A–F), and the chromatographic challenges documented in published literature and pharmacopeial monographs. The most significant analytical challenge was resolving the structurally similar regioisomeric pair, Imp-D and Imp-F, whose co-elution has limited the applicability of existing methods. The step-by-step procedure of the analytical method is presented below [25–27].

Preliminary chromatographic evaluation

Initial chromatographic trials were conducted using multiple stationary phases (C18, phenyl-hexyl, polar-embedded C18) and mobile phases varying in organic content, buffer composition, and pH. Most trial conditions provided acceptable retention of PTZ and early-eluting impurities; however, none achieved baseline separation between Imp-D and Imp-F. Chromatograms from preliminary experiments showed the persistent partial overlap of these regioisomers even under extended gradients.

Evaluation of buffer pH from 6.0 to 8.0 indicated that PTZ peak symmetry improved significantly at pH ≥ 7.0. Conversely, Imp-D and Imp-F resolution were highly sensitive to minor pH variations, suggesting that ionization plays a critical role in selectivity. Based on these observations, a 10 mM phosphate buffer at pH 7.5 was selected as the starting point. Among the tested stationary phases, the Waters X-Bridge C18 (150 × 4.6 mm, 3.5 μm) demonstrated superior selectivity and peak symmetry, and was therefore adopted for further experiments.

Pantoprazole-related impurities (A–F) exhibited distinct chromatographic behaviors based on their structural characteristics and polarity differences. Process-related impurities such as Imp-A and Imp-B eluted earlier. In contrast, structurally similar regioisomeric impurities, Imp-D and Imp-F, showed closely overlapping retention behavior, posing a significant separation challenge. The developed method successfully resolved all impurities with acceptable peak shape and resolution, confirming its suitability for comprehensive impurity profiling [28, 29].

AQbD-based risk assessment

An Ishikawa (fishbone) diagram (Figure S1) was constructed to identify factors potentially affecting CQAs: (i) PTZ and related compound A, (ii) PTZ and related compound D, (iii) Related compound D and related compound F, (iv) Related compound D and related Compound B, and (v) Related compound B and related compound E. Instrument-related, chemical, methodological, and environmental parameters were evaluated. After eliminating non-critical factors, four CMPs were selected for multivariate screening: buffer pH, organic proportion (ACN%), flow rate, and column temperature. These CMPs were further systematically evaluated using Design of Experiments (DoE) to quantify their individual and interaction effects.

Design of experiments (CCD) for screening of critical parameters

A CCD with 27 experimental runs was executed to quantify the individual and interactive effects of CMPs on CQAs. The design matrix and corresponding responses are summarized in Table 2. ANOVA results (Table 3) clearly demonstrated the statistical significance of the model and key factors affecting chromatographic responses. These statistical findings guided the selection of critical factors for further response surface optimization.

Chromatographic optimization and establishment of the MODR

Based on desirability analysis, optimal performance was achieved using a phosphate buffer (pH 7.5), an initial ACN composition of 20% with a gradient ramping to 40%, a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min, and a column temperature of 30 °C. The composite desirability function plot (Figure S3) supported these conditions by maximizing resolution (> 2.0), minimizing runtime (< 12 min), and ensuring acceptable tailing (< 1.5).

A MODR was generated using response surface maps (Fig. 2), illustrating the multidimensional “safe zone” within which all CQAs met the acceptance criteria. This MODR provides regulatory flexibility in accordance with ICH Q14, enabling minor adjustments without revalidation. Under optimized conditions, baseline resolution of all six impurities and clear separation of the D/F regioisomers were achieved, confirming the success of the AQbD-driven approach.

The proposed method was critically compared with previously reported HPLC methods (Table 1). Most reported methods either involve longer analysis times or fail to achieve complete resolution of all related substances, particularly the regioisomeric pair D and F. In contrast, the present method achieves complete baseline separation of all six impurities within a shorter runtime while maintaining robust system suitability. Furthermore, unlike conventional methods optimized using one-factor-at-a-time approaches, the present method employs an AQbD framework, ensuring improved robustness, reproducibility, and systematic control over critical method parameters.

Particular emphasis was placed on resolving structurally similar impurities, especially the regioisomeric pair Imp-D and Imp-F, which co-elute in conventional methods. Under the optimized chromatographic conditions, resolution values greater than 2.0 were consistently achieved for all critical peak pairs, confirming baseline separation. The method’s selectivity was primarily governed by buffer pH and organic composition, which significantly influenced the separation of closely eluting impurities.

PTZ was most susceptible to oxidative stress, producing elevated levels of Imp-A, consistent with sulfoxide/sulfone formation pathways. Acid and base hydrolysis generated characteristic degradants without interfering with PTZ or any known impurities. Thermal degradation showed moderate but distinct formation of Imp-B and Imp-E. Peak purity indices (PPI) from PDA analysis confirmed spectral homogeneity for PTZ and all impurity peaks. Mass balance remained within 95%-102% across all stressed conditions (Table 4), supporting the method’s stability-indicating capability.

Method validation

The optimized RP-HPLC method was validated in accordance with ICH Q2(R2) guidelines to confirm its suitability for the quantitative determination of pantoprazole and its six related impurities. The validation parameters included specificity, system suitability, linearity, accuracy, precision, limits of detection and quantification, robustness, and solution stability. All validation experiments were performed under the optimized chromatographic conditions defined within the established MODR. A consolidated summary of validation parameters is provided in Tables 5 and 6.

Specificity and peak purity

Specificity was demonstrated by injecting blank, placebo, individual impurities, mixed impurities, and the PTZ sample solution. No interference was observed at the retention times of PTZ or any impurities, confirming the method’s selectivity. Forced-degradation samples further established specificity: degradant peaks generated under acid, base, oxidative, and thermal conditions were well resolved from PTZ and its impurities (Fig. 4). Peak purity was assessed using photodiode-array (PDA) detection, and purity angle values were below the purity threshold for both PTZ and all impurity peaks, confirming the absence of co-eluting components. These findings demonstrate that the method is highly specific and capable of distinguishing analytes from degradation products and structurally similar impurities.

System suitability

System suitability was assessed by six replicate injections of a mixed standard containing PTZ and all impurities. The method consistently met stringent acceptance criteria: tailing factors < 1.5, theoretical plates > 5000 for PTZ, and resolution > 2.0 between all critical pairs, particularly Imp-D/Imp-F. Relative retention times were consistent, with %RSD < 1.0% (Table 5). Peak purity assessment using PDA detection confirmed spectral homogeneity for all analytes (purity angle < purity threshold), demonstrating the absence of co-eluting components. These findings indicate that the method operates reliably under routine analytical conditions.

Linearity, limits of detection (LOD) and quantification (LOQ)

Linearity was evaluated over concentration ranges appropriate for impurity qualification and quantification (typically from LOQ to 150% of the specification limit). Calibration curves exhibited excellent linear response, with correlation coefficients (r) greater than 0.998 for PTZ and all impurities. Residual plots showed no systematic deviation, confirming homoscedasticity and suitability for quantitative analysis. LOD and LOQ were estimated based on signal-to-noise ratios of approximately 3:1 and 10:1, respectively. All impurities demonstrated low detection limits, indicating the method’s suitability for trace-level impurity monitoring. LOQ values were sufficiently below ICH identification and qualification limits, ensuring regulatory compliance. In addition to correlation coefficients, the reliability of the regression models was further supported by the consistency of residual distribution and absence of systematic deviation across the calibration range. These observations confirm that the intercept values were negligible and did not significantly affect quantification within the studied range. The regression models demonstrated strong goodness-of-fit, indicating their suitability for accurate impurity quantification. Similar considerations regarding regression analysis parameters have been reported in recent studies [31, 32].

Accuracy (recovery studies)

Accuracy was determined using the standard addition method at three concentration levels (50%, 100%, and 150% of the target impurity levels). The results indicate satisfactory accuracy across all concentration levels (Table 6). No bias was observed between levels, demonstrating that the method accurately quantifies PTZ impurities across the analytical range.

Precision (repeatability and intermediate precision)

Repeatability was assessed through six replicate injections of spiked samples at specification levels; %RSD values for each impurity and PTZ remained below 2.0%. Intermediate precision, evaluated across different days and analysts, also exhibited %RSD < 2.0%, confirming the method’s reproducibility under varying experimental conditions. These observations align with robustness findings and support the method’s reliability for routine QC use.

Robustness

Robustness was evaluated by introducing deliberate variations into critical method parameters within the MODR boundaries: pH (± 0.1 units), flow rate (± 0.1 mL/min), organic phase (± 2% ACN), and column temperature (± 2 °C). No significant changes were observed in system suitability parameters, resolution, or relative retention times. Notably, the resolution between Imp-D and Imp-F consistently remained above 2.0. This result demonstrates that the MODR-based optimized conditions offer excellent operational flexibility.

Solution stability

Standard and sample solutions were evaluated for stability at room temperature and refrigerated conditions over 24–48 h. PTZ and all impurities remained stable, with a %difference < 2.0% relative to initial values and no additional degradant formation. These findings support the method’s suitability for routine batch analysis. The resolution between all critical peak pairs, particularly Imp-D and Imp-F, consistently exceeded 2.0, satisfying regulatory expectations for impurity profiling. Combined with PDA-based peak purity confirmation, these findings establish the method’s specificity and suitability for reliable impurity quantification in accordance with ICH guidelines.

Sustainability assessment and multi-colored analytical chemistry framework

The sustainability of the developed AQbD-based RP-HPLC method was comprehensively evaluated using a multi-dimensional analytical chemistry framework integrating green (environmental impact), blue (practical efficiency), red (analytical performance), and white (overall balance) profiles. This approach enables a holistic evaluation of analytical methods beyond conventional single-metric assessments and aligns with recent advancements in sustainable analytical science [33–38].

Rationale for multi-tool sustainability assessment

Single-metric evaluation is insufficient to fully characterize the sustainability of analytical methods, as environmentally favorable methods may still lack analytical robustness or operational feasibility. Therefore, multiple complementary tools, including AGREE, MoGAPI, BAGI, and RGB12, were employed to evaluate distinct yet interconnected dimensions of sustainability [39–42].

AGREE (analytical GREEnness) evaluation tool

The AGREE tool integrates all 12 principles of GAC into a single circular pictogram, with each segment representing a principle and the final score ranging from 0 (least green) to 1 (most green) [43]. The proposed RP-HPLC method achieved an AGREE score of 0.71, indicating strong compliance with GAC principles (Fig. 5a). This favorable score can be attributed to moderate solvent consumption (< 10 mL per run), elimination of derivatization steps, acceptable analysis throughput, and reduced waste generation. Although acetonitrile is classified as a moderately hazardous solvent, its controlled usage and shorter run time significantly mitigate its environmental impact. A detailed rationale for the AGREE scoring, principle-wise, is summarized in Table S2.

MoGAPI assessment tool

The MoGAPI tool extends the GAPI concept by incorporating additional criteria related to solvent hazard, waste generation, and energy consumption through a color-coded pictogram system. The proposed method demonstrated predominantly green and yellow zones, reflecting low solvent volume, minimal sample preparation, and efficient chromatographic separation [44]. The overall MoGAPI score of 70 classified the method as excellent green, confirming its suitability for routine quality-control analysis with minimal environmental burden (Fig. 5b, Table S2).

Blue analytical chemistry assessment (BAGI assessment tool)

The Blue Analytical Greenness Index (BAGI) emphasizes solvent safety, energy efficiency, and waste minimization, and serves as the blue axis within the RGB sustainability concept [45]. The proposed RP-HPLC method achieved a BAGI score of 75 (Fig. 5c), reflecting good environmental and operational sustainability. This score was primarily driven by reduced energy consumption, acceptable waste generation, and avoidance of highly toxic reagents.

Red analytical chemistry assessment using RAPI

The analytical performance of the developed RP-HPLC method was evaluated using the Red Analytical Performance Index (RAPI) [46], a recently introduced tool for assessing analytical methods based on validation-related criteria. RAPI evaluates ten critical parameters, including repeatability, intermediate precision, trueness, recovery, limit of quantification, working range, linearity, robustness, and selectivity, and represents the overall analytical performance through a cumulative score and a star-shaped pictogram. The RAPI pictogram (Fig. 5d) represents the analytical performance of the developed RP-HPLC method. The developed method achieved an RAPI score of 75.0, indicating a strong analytical performance profile suitable for impurity profiling and routine pharmaceutical quality control applications.

High scores were obtained for repeatability, trueness, recovery, linearity, robustness, and selectivity, reflecting excellent method reliability. Trueness was established through recovery studies, demonstrating acceptable accuracy across the studied concentration range. The method also exhibited strong linearity (R² > 0.99) and an adequate working range, ensuring reliable quantification of analytes. Robustness assessment, performed using a design of experiments approach, confirmed that the method is resilient to small variations in chromatographic conditions. The high selectivity of the method is particularly noteworthy, as complete baseline separation of all related substances, including the critical regioisomeric impurities D and F, was achieved. Moderate contributions to the overall score were associated with intermediate precision and reproducibility, as inter-laboratory validation was not performed. Overall, the RAPI assessment confirms that the developed method demonstrates well-balanced, reliable analytical performance, supporting its suitability for regulatory impurity profiling and pharmaceutical quality control.

The whiteness assessment tool RGB12 for the proposed HPLC method

Nowak et al. [47] proposed WAC as an advancement over the 12 principles of GAC, intended to foster safer and more sustainable analytical methodologies. The RGB12 Whiteness Assessment tool quantifies the overall sustainability of an analytical procedure by combining three color-coded groups: red, green, and blue. This work employs the RGB12 model to present a novel metric for evaluating quantification of PTZ-related compounds, merging sustainable development goals with analytical chemistry principles. The colors represent different attributes: red for analytical efficiency (R1-R4), green for environmental friendliness (G1-G4), and blue for economic viability (B1-B4). The WAC status classifies methods as poor white (0–25), average white (26–50), good white (51–75), excellent white (76–100), and extraordinary white (> 100).

The proposed HPLC analytical procedures achieved exceptional red color scores of 95% and 100%, indicating high accuracy and precision in quantifying PTZ-related impurities. Furthermore, the method achieved a notable greenness score of 97.5%, indicating minimal environmental burden and adherence to eco-friendly analytical practices. Efficiency evaluation yielded a blue score of 96.25%, confirming their superior operational and economic performance. As shown in Fig. 5e, the proposed HPLC method achieved an overall whiteness score of 96.3%, indicating excellent whiteness.

The comparative greenness assessment (Table S3) demonstrates that previously reported methods exhibit moderate environmental and analytical performance, as indicated by lower AGREE scores and less favorable multi-parameter profiles. In contrast, the proposed method achieved improved scores across AGREE, MoGAPI, BAGI, and RAPI metrics, reflecting reduced solvent consumption, shorter runtime, and enhanced analytical reliability. This confirms the superiority of the developed method in terms of integrated sustainability and performance.

Conclusion

The present study successfully demonstrates the development of an AQbD-optimized, stability-indicating, and environmentally sustainable RP-HPLC method for the simultaneous determination of pantoprazole and its six related substances. The method achieved complete baseline separation of all impurities, including the critical regioisomeric pair D and F, within a reduced runtime while maintaining excellent system suitability. Validation results confirmed the method’s accuracy, precision, sensitivity, robustness, and regulatory compliance. Furthermore, comprehensive sustainability assessment using AGREE, MoGAPI, BAGI, RAPI, and RGB12 demonstrated a well-balanced integration of environmental, analytical, and operational performance. Overall, the proposed method provides a robust, reliable, and sustainable analytical solution for routine impurity profiling of pantoprazole in pharmaceutical quality control.
