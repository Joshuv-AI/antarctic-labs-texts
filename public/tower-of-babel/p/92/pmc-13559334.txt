A bibliometric analysis of quantitative computed tomography in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease research based on Web of Science: trends, hotspots, and future directions (2005–2025)
Authors: Maimaitiming Mahemuti, Reyihanguli Wushouer, Yimiguli Yimin, Zulifeiya Mahemuti, Guzailinuer Keremu, Buweirebiyemu Tuersunaili, Haihong Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a heterogeneous lung condition not fully captured by spirometry. Quantitative computed tomography (QCT) enables objective characterization of emphysema, airway remodeling, and other structural abnormalities, playing key roles in early recognition, phenotyping, and prognosis. Despite growing literature in this field, no comprehensive bibliometric synthesis has mapped the intellectual structure, collaborative networks, or thematic evolution of QCT research in COPD. This study aims to fill this gap by providing a structured overview of the field over the past two decades.MethodsA systematic search was performed in the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC) using the topic formula: TS=((“quantitative computed tomography” OR “quantitative CT” OR “QCT” OR “CT quantification” OR “quantitative CT assessment”) AND (“chronic obstructive pulmonary disease” OR “COPD” OR “chronic obstructive pulmonary disease*”)). Publications from 2005 to 2025 were included, limited to English original articles and reviews. Titles and abstracts were independently screened by two reviewers; studies not primarily focusing on QCT-based quantitative analysis in COPD were excluded. Disagreements were resolved through discussion. Bibliometric and visual analyses were conducted using CiteSpace 6.4.R1, VOSviewer 1.6.19, and the R package bibliometrix.ResultsA total of 300 publications (279 original articles, 21 reviews) were included. The United States was the leading contributor in overall output and international collaboration. The University of Iowa was the most productive institution, Hoffman EA was the most prolific author, and the International Journal of Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease was the most productive journal. Keyword and thematic analyses revealed a clear evolutionary trajectory: early research (2005–2012) focused on technical quantification of emphysema and airway abnormalities; a transitional phase (2013–2018) emphasized “phenotypes” and disease heterogeneity; and the recent period (2019–2025) has seen rising attention to prognostic evaluation, mortality prediction, and artificial intelligence-assisted analysis.ConclusionsThis study confirms a shift from morphologic quantification toward clinically actionable imaging biomarkers. However, the existing literature suffers from several critical gaps: lack of standardized acquisition and analysis protocols, predominance of cross-sectional designs, and insufficient external validation of artificial intelligence models. Future research should prioritize multicenter prospective validation, integration with multi-omics data for endotyping, and development of open-source automated pipelines to facilitate clinical translation.

Key findings

• This bibliometric analysis of 300 publications (2005–2025) reveals that the United States is the most prolific contributor (36.9%) and the central hub of global collaboration. The University of Iowa and Hoffman EA are the most productive institution and author, respectively. The field evolved through three distinct phases: technical quantification (2005–2012), phenotyping and heterogeneity (2013–2018), and prognosis with artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted analysis (2019–2025). Emerging frontiers include “index”-based composite metrics and phenotypic classification, yet critical gaps persist in standardization and external validation.

What is known and what is new?

• Quantitative computed tomography (QCT) enables objective characterization of emphysema and airway remodeling in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), but the intellectual structure, collaborative networks, and thematic evolution of this field have never been systematically mapped.

• This first bibliometric synthesis dedicated to QCT in COPD identifies a US-centric collaboration network with fragmented international partnerships, a clear transition from morphologic measurement toward clinically actionable biomarkers, and a recent surge in prognosis and artificial intelligence-driven investigations.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The field must prioritize multicenter prospective validation of QCT-derived biomarkers, integration with multi-omics for precise endotyping, and development of open-source automated pipelines to reduce inter-scanner variability. Funding agencies and journal editors should encourage structured global consortia, particularly engaging underrepresented Asian and low- and middle-income regions, to ensure that future guidelines reflect worldwide COPD diversity and accelerate personalized management.

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a major global public health problem and one of the leading causes of chronic respiratory morbidity and mortality worldwide (1,2). According to the World Health Organization (WHO), COPD was the fourth leading cause of death globally in 2021, accounting for approximately 3.5 million deaths (3). In China, it affects about 8.6% of adults aged 40 and above, with risk factors such as smoking, rapid urbanization, and air pollution exacerbating its health and economic impacts (4).

Traditionally defined by spirometric airflow limitation, COPD is increasingly recognized as a heterogeneous lung condition resulting from interactions between genetic susceptibility and multiple lifelong environmental exposures, including biomass fuel, air pollution, infections, and impaired lung development (5,6). Although spirometry remains the gold standard for COPD diagnosis, it is limited in capturing early subclinical structural lesions and the full phenotypic heterogeneity of the disease (7,8). By contrast, chest computed tomography (CT) can identify emphysema, airway remodeling, and other structural changes even when spirometry is normal, providing key evidence for pre-COPD (9,10).

Against this evolving clinical background, quantitative computed tomography (QCT) has emerged as a robust and objective tool for characterizing lung structure, enabling detailed assessment of emphysema extent, airway wall thickness, and air trapping (11). QCT offers advantages for imaging-based phenotyping, disease severity evaluation, and prognostic assessment (9,12). With growing research attention focused on pre-COPD and early pulmonary structural impairment, QCT has become indispensable for advancing mechanistic research and translational clinical practice in the field of COPD.

As the literature on QCT in COPD grows rapidly, there is a need to move beyond traditional narrative or scoping reviews, which primarily synthesize and appraise study content, and instead adopt a bibliometric approach. Bibliometric analysis focuses on quantitative publication patterns, collaboration structures, citation networks, and keyword trends—questions that cannot be answered by conventional review methods. Specifically, bibliometric analysis can reveal how research foci have shifted over time, identify underrepresented areas, map international collaboration networks, highlight influential works, and detect emerging frontiers. These insights are complementary to but distinct from the findings of systematic or scoping reviews.

Nevertheless, a systematic bibliometric overview focusing on QCT applications in COPD remains absent. The global geographic distribution of publications, multi-institutional and cross-national collaboration patterns, inherent intellectual structure, and temporal evolution of research hotspots in this field have not been systematically explored. To address this research gap, the present study conducted a comprehensive bibliometric and visual analysis of QCT-related COPD literature in the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC) over the past two decades. Using CiteSpace, VOSviewer, and the R package bibliometrix, this study aimed to: (I) map the knowledge structure and major contributors; (II) identify collaboration patterns across countries, institutions, and authors; (III) track the dynamic evolution of research hotspots and emerging directions; and (IV) highlight gaps that may inform future scientific inquiry and clinical translation. We present this article in accordance with the BIBLIO reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0807/rc).

Methods

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in the WoSCC to identify publications related to QCT in COPD. The initial search was performed on August 6, 2025, using the following topic search formula: TS=((“quantitative computed tomography” OR “quantitative CT” OR “QCT” OR “CT quantification” OR “quantitative CT assessment”) AND (“chronic obstructive pulmonary disease” OR “COPD” OR “chronic obstructive pulmonary disease”)).

The search initially covered the period from January 1, 1900 to January 1, 2025, yielding 602 records. To capture the evolution of the field over the past two decades, the formal bibliometric analysis was restricted to publications from January 1, 2005 to January 1, 2025. After applying this time restriction, 555 records remained. After limiting to original articles and reviews in English, 480 records remained. Following manual screening of titles and abstracts, 300 publications were identified as the core dataset for all bibliometric analyses.

To provide a more current view of annual publication trends, we performed a supplemental raw search on June 2, 2026, covering January 1, 2025 to June 2, 2026. The search used the identical formula and the same document/language restrictions as the core search, but without manual relevance screening. The raw counts from this search are not displayed in any figure or used in any bibliometric analysis; they are mentioned only in the text of the Results section to indicate that the field remains active after 2025.

The WoSCC was chosen for its standardized citation records essential for bibliometric analysis. However, exclusive reliance on WoSCC introduces selection and language biases, as publications in other databases or non-English languages are excluded. This may underrepresent non-English-speaking contributions.

Eligibility criteria and screening

Only original research articles and review articles published in English were included. The following document types were excluded: conference abstracts, editorials, letters to the editor, case reports, technical notes, book chapters, and retracted publications. After restricting by these document types, 480 records remained.

Titles and abstracts of the 480 records were independently screened by two reviewers. Studies were excluded if they did not primarily focus on both QCT-related quantitative analysis and COPD. Specific exclusion criteria included: (I) articles addressing conventional visual CT interpretation without any quantitative analysis; (II) studies not centered on COPD (e.g., asthma, bronchiectasis, lung cancer alone); (III) methodological papers describing QCT techniques without application to COPD; (IV) publications in which the search terms appeared incidentally without constituting the main research topic. When the relevance of a study could not be determined from the title and abstract alone, the full text was retrieved and reviewed independently by both reviewers.

Disagreements between the two reviewers were resolved through consensus discussion, with a third reviewer consulted when necessary. After the screening steps, 300 publications were included in the final analysis. The literature screening process is illustrated in Figure 1.

Quality assessment

Consistent with standard bibliometric methodology, individual studies were not formally appraised for methodological quality, as the focus was on publication patterns rather than evidence synthesis.

Data extraction and bibliometric analysis

Full records and cited references were exported from WoSCC in plain text format. Bibliometric and visual analyses were performed using CiteSpace (version 6.4.R1), VOSviewer (version 1.6.19), and the R package bibliometrix (version 4.1.3). Summary of bibliometric indicators and definitions

In network visualizations, nodes represented countries/regions, authors, institutions, or keywords. Node size reflected the frequency or productivity of the entity (e.g., number of publications for a country, citation count for a reference), while edges indicated relationships such as co-authorship, co-citation, or keyword co-occurrence. Edge thickness represented the strength of the relationship. Collaboration among authors, institutions, or countries was defined by co-authorship on the same publication.

For CiteSpace, the parameters were set as follows: time span from 2005 to 2025, years per slice, 1; selection threshold, top 50 items per slice; other parameters were kept at default settings.

Analyses performed included annual publication trends, productivity rankings (countries/regions, institutions, authors, journals), collaboration networks (countries, institutions, authors), co-citation patterns, keyword co-occurrence and burst detection, and thematic evolution. Annual publication volume was used to assess overall research trends. Journal, author, and institutional analyses provided insights into research output quality, major influencers, and the distribution of resources and regional advantages. Keyword co-occurrence and burst detection were employed to identify research hotspots over specific periods and to predict emerging directions.

Analysis of countries/regions

The country collaboration network comprised 36 nodes and 131 edges. The United States was the most prolific contributor (107 publications, centrality 0.38) and formed the core of the network (Figure 3A). Germany, Japan, and China each contributed 38 publications but had much lower centrality (0.16, 0.12, and 0.02, respectively), indicating less bridging roles in international collaboration. Canada ranked third by centrality (0.24) with 36 publications.

Figure 3B displays the international co-occurrence network among 34 countries, organized into 9 clusters. The largest cluster centered on the United States, encompassing Canada, South Korea, China, Japan, and Germany. A European cluster was anchored by Germany with France, Italy, and Belgium, while Canada led another cluster with Norway, the Netherlands, Denmark, Iran, and Sweden. Additional clusters represented Central/Eastern European (e.g., Poland, Hungary, Latvia) and Southern European (Spain, Greece) partnerships.

Global publication distribution (Figure 3C) showed the US as the dominant contributor (36.9% of total output), followed distantly by South Korea (n=152), Germany (n=150), China (n=118), and Japan (n=103). North America accounted for 42.9% of total output, followed by Europe and Asia. Figure 3D illustrates multi-country publication (MCP) versus single-country publication (SCP). The US produced the most MCP (80, 26.7%), followed by China (40, 13.3%) and Japan (34, 11.3%). European countries exhibited high MCP ratios (e.g., Germany 52%, UK 54.5%), whereas major Asian producers had low MCP rates (China 10%, South Korea 15%), suggesting a stronger domestic research focus.

These findings reveal that the United States serves as the central hub of global collaboration, while Asian countries show high output but low international engagement, indicating a geographic imbalance in collaborative networks.

Figure 5B shows an overlay visualization of collaborative patterns and temporal trends among 59 institutions grouped into six clusters. Early research (2012–2014) was driven by North American (e.g., Harvard) and European (e.g., University of Bergen) centers. Keyword analysis

Using VOSviewer with a frequency threshold of 7, a keyword co-occurrence network was constructed (Figure 6A), consisting of 299 nodes and 1,675 edges.

Of all keywords, 88 met the threshold and were grouped into 6 clusters. Notably, “COPD”, “emphysema”, and “CT” emerged as central terms within their respective clusters.

A temporal keyword network (Figure 6B) shows the conceptual evolution from 2014 to 2020, with yellow nodes indicating recently emerging terms. Figure 6C lists the most frequent keywords: “COPD” (380 times), “emphysema” (198 times), and “computed tomography” (150 times).

Burst detection (Figure 6D) identified the top 25 keywords with sharp frequency increases. “High resolution CT” had the highest burst intensity (4.74), while “thin section CT” showed the longest burst duration (7 years). Notably, “index” and “phenotypes” began bursting in 2019 and 2020 (intensities 2.01 and 2.87), indicating recent research frontiers.

The thematic map (Figure 7A) places themes into four quadrants. “Phenotypes”, “flow limitation”, and “airway dimensions” lie in the motor themes quadrant (high centrality and density). “Collateral ventilation” and “lung volume reduction” are niche themes (high density, low centrality). “Emphysema”, “airway wall thickness”, and “predictors” fall into the lower-right quadrant (high centrality, low density), indicating they are conceptually important but underexplored. The trend topics analysis (Figure 7B) shows a clear progression from methodological development (e.g., thin-section CT, densitometry) toward clinical translation, with growing emphasis on phenotypes and personalized medicine.

Discussion

This bibliometric analysis provides the first structured overview of QCT research in COPD from 2005 to 2025. The field has grown steadily, with a rapid expansion between 2012 and 2018 followed by a consolidation plateau. This pattern indicates that QCT in COPD has moved beyond a purely technical exploration into a mature research phase, where efforts are now focused on clinical refinement, validation, and translation rather than method invention.

The rapid growth phase (2012–2018) coincides with the widespread availability of multidetector CT, automated airway analysis software, and the emergence of large cohort studies (e.g., COPDGene, ECLIPSE) (13,14). The subsequent plateau (2019–2024) shows a stabilization of annual output at 16–24 core publications per year, suggesting that the field has become an established research niche. Notably, the raw counts from the supplemental search indicate a marked increase in 2025 (54 raw publications) compared to 2024 (20 core publications), suggesting a possible resurgence. However, because the 2025–2026 raw data were not manually screened for relevance, it remains unclear whether this increase is driven by artificial intelligence (AI)-related topics or other factors. Future updates with full screening are needed to confirm any new growth trend.

The United States dominates both output and collaboration networks (36.9% of total, centrality 0.38). This likely reflects the presence of long-term, well-funded cohort studies (COPDGene, SPIROMICS), early adoption of QCT in clinical trials, and strong ties between academic radiology and pulmonary medicine (15,16). However, the US-centric network also raises concerns about generalizability: most evidence originates from North American and European populations, while COPD in low- and middle-income countries often involves biomass smoke exposure, recurrent infections, and different phenotypic expressions (17-19). The underrepresentation of Asian institutions among the top ten suggests that research capacity in regions with high COPD burden remains underutilized.

European countries show high MCP ratios (e.g., Germany 52%, UK 54.5%), probably driven by EU funding frameworks that encourage cross-border consortia. In contrast, Asian nations (China 10%, South Korea 15%) have low MCP ratios despite substantial output, indicating a domestic-oriented research culture.

Possible explanations include language barriers, funding policies that prioritize national journals, and less established international collaboration infrastructure. This geographic imbalance has implications for global health equity. Evidence from Asian populations, which exhibit unique COPD phenotypes (e.g., biomass-and tuberculosis-associated), remains underrepresented in the global research landscape that informs guidelines such as the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) reports (20,21).

Our temporal overlay network (Figure 5B) shows that Asian institutions began increasing their collaboration after 2014, but their participation remains peripheral compared to North American and European hubs. However, the persistence of isolated clusters (e.g., among top author groups) suggests that collaboration is still fragmented. For the field to achieve consensus on QCT acquisition protocols and validate biomarkers across populations, more structured multi-center initiatives are needed—a gap that funding agencies should prioritize (22-24).

The three-phase evolution (from technical quantification to phenotypes, and then to prognosis/AI) demonstrates a clear move from “what can QCT measure?” to “what does it predict for patients?” The recent emergence of “index” (e.g., parametric response mapping index) and “phenotypes” as burst keywords indicates that composite imaging metrics are now at the frontier. These shifts align with the broader precision medicine movement in COPD, where the goal is to subtype patients beyond forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1) (25). Notably, keywords such as “standardization”, “external validation”, and “multi-center” are absent from burst lists. This omission is striking because without harmonized acquisition protocols and independent validation, even the most promising imaging biomarkers will not reach clinical practice (26).

To our knowledge, this is the first bibliometric review specifically focused on QCT in COPD. Previous bibliometric studies have examined COPD biomarkers in general or CT imaging in respiratory diseases, but none have systematically mapped collaboration networks, thematic evolution, and research gaps for QCT (27). Compared with those broader analyses, our study reveals a more mature and clinically oriented field, with a concentration on phenotyping and prognosis rather than pure method development. The geographic concentration we observe (North American dominance) is similar to other COPD bibliometric reports, but the low Asian MCP ratios appear more pronounced in QCT research, possibly due to the high cost of CT equipment and analysis software.

Future research should prioritize several interconnected directions. First, harmonization efforts are needed, including the development of phantom-based calibration protocols and open-source analysis pipelines to reduce inter-scanner variability. Second, external validation should be required before claiming biomarker utility, meaning that independent multi-center validation becomes a prerequisite. Third, integration of QCT with multi-omics and patient-reported outcomes would help define clinically meaningful endotypes. Fourth, implementation science should study how to translate QCT biomarkers into routine care across different healthcare systems, including low- and middle-income countries. Finally, global collaboration must be encouraged, particularly inviting Asian and other underrepresented regions to join international consortia, ensuring that guidelines reflect the full diversity of COPD worldwide.

Strengths and limitations

Several strengths of this study can be highlighted. The integration of three complementary bibliometric tools (CiteSpace, VOSviewer, and bibliometrix) together with rigorous manual screening ensures that all included publications are topically relevant. This work provides the first bibliometric overview specifically focused on QCT in COPD, covering 20 years (2005–2025).

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, relying exclusively on the Web of Science Core Collection and English-language publications introduces database and language bias; non-English research (especially from China) is likely underrepresented, so the findings provide a structured overview of the WoSCC-indexed English literature, not an exhaustive global account. Second, consistent with bibliometric methodology, no formal quality appraisal of individual studies was performed; citation metrics reflect scholarly impact, not evidence quality. Third, citation-based indicators may undervalue very recent high-impact publications that have not yet accumulated sufficient citations. Fourth, the core dataset ends on January 1, 2025 (a fixed 20-year window); later 2025–2026 raw data were used only for trend illustration without manual screening. These limitations affect interpretation but do not invalidate the main conclusions.

Conclusions

In summary, QCT research in COPD has evolved from technical quantification toward clinically actionable imaging biomarkers. The field is now at a critical juncture, where further progress requires not only advanced algorithms but also standardization, external validation, and global collaboration. Future research should prioritize multicenter prospective validation of QCT-derived biomarkers, integration with multi-omics and patient-reported outcomes, and development of open-source automated pipelines. Our findings provide a roadmap for researchers, clinicians, and funding agencies to accelerate the translation of quantitative imaging into personalized COPD management.
