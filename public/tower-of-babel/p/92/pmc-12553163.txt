Long-term lipoprotein and lipid profiles and association with metabolic risk markers in youth with perinatally acquired HIV and matched controls: a 10-year comparative cohort study
Authors: Julie van der Post, Ben van Nieuwland, Samera Kassa Almu, Charlotte Blokhuis, Malon van den Hof, Dasja Pajkrt, Jason G van Genderen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundYouth with perinatally acquired HIV (PHIV) are at risk for cardiovascular disease (CVD) despite combination anti-retroviral therapy (cART). Longitudinal data on the impact of HIV and cART on lipid metabolism and CVD risk in PHIV youth is limited. We investigated lipid and lipoprotein levels in PHIV youth and matched controls over time and examined associations with cART and metabolic syndrome (MetS) markers.MethodsWe included 32 PHIV and 36 controls at three time points: 2013, 2018 and 2023. In 2023, we assessed lipid profiles cross-sectionally in a larger cohort of 53 PHIV participants and 45 controls. Measurements included lipoprotein (a) (Lp(a)), apolipoprotein B (ApoB), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), reduced high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) and total cholesterol (TC) and markers related to MetS risk.ResultsThe median age was 21.7 years (IQR 16.7–25.2) for PHIV participants and 21.2 years (16.8—22.3) for controls in 2023 for longitudinal assessment. No significant differences in lipid or lipoprotein levels were observed over time (p values > 0.05). TG levels were significantly higher in PHIV participants at second assessment (p = 0.043), but other levels were comparable (p values > 0.05). Higher Lp(a) levels were associated with higher LDL-C and ApoB levels, however associations were significantly weakened among PHIV participants. Furthermore, protease inhibitor (PI) use was associated with elevated TC, TG and LDL-C. During cross-sectional assessment median age was 17.4 years (IQR 12.7–22.4) and 19.1 years (IQR 15.0—21.8) for PHIV youth and controls. Lipid and MetS markers were comparable between groups (p values > 0.05).ConclusionPHIV youth on cART showed similar lipid and lipoprotein levels over time compared to matched controls. Lp(a) associations with lipid markers were weakened for PHIV youth and PI use was associated with lipid alterations. Our results imply that while lipid profiles, including Lp(a), are important components of cardiovascular health monitoring, the increased CVD risk observed in PHIV youth may be more substantially influenced by disease-specific or broader pathophysiological mechanisms related to HIV-infection and treatment.Trial registrationDutch clinical trial registration: Overview of Medical Research in the Netherlands (OMON) (ID: NL-OMON53727).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12944-025-02718-z.Keywords: Human immunodeficiency virus, Cardiovascular disease, Antiretroviral therapy, Lipoproteins, Lipid profiles

Introduction

With the introduction of combination antiretroviral therapy (cART), survival rates of children growing up with perinatally acquired HIV (PHIV) have drastically improved [1]. As PHIV youth are transitioning into adult care, the impact of HIV- and cART-related long-term complications and prevention strategies have become important areas of research [2, 3]. Despite successful viral suppression, PHIV youth remain at increased risk for possible long-term comorbidities, including cardiovascular disease (CVD) and metabolic complications later in life [4–7]. The global burden of HIV-related CVD has tripled in the past two decades and is expected to increase further [5, 6].

The pathophysiology of increased HIV-associated CVD risk possibly arises from HIV-specific mechanisms (e.g. chronic inflammation, immune activation, vascular dysfunction) and potential cardiovascular side effects of lifelong cART in combination with traditional CVD risk factors (e.g. smoking, hypertension) [8–11]. Research in HIV adults have clearly linked HIV to the development of CVD including premature coronary aging, atherosclerosis, diabetes, and heart failure [8, 12–15]. Although research in PHIV youth remains limited, studies suggest an increased risk of metabolic and subclinical cardiac abnormalities compared to HIV-negative controls (e.g. lipodystrophy, endothelial dysfunction and left ventricular hypertrophy) [16–18]. Other metabolic abnormalities observed in PHIV youth include features of metabolic syndrome (MetS) and dyslipidemia such as insulin resistance, elevated triglycerides (TG), elevated low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and reduced high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) [17–23]. MetS is strongly correlated with (pre)diabetes and increased CVD risk in the general population accompanied with characteristic changes in physical and laboratory measurements [24, 25].

Several cardiometabolic laboratory markers including glucose levels to assess insulin resistance, LDL-C, HDL-C, TG, apolipoproteins B and A1 (Apo B and ApoA1), and lipoprotein (a) ((Lp(a)) are applied to determine MetS and/or CVD risk in the general population [26–28]. Elevated Lp(a) is an independent risk factor for CVD, contributing to atherogenesis and thrombosis [29]. Lp(a) levels above 50 mg/dL (125 nmol/L) indicate increased CVD risk, levels between 30–50 mg/dL (75–125 nmol/L) indicate intermediate risk and warrant further assessment of additional CVD risk factors [28]. In children, Lp(a) levels above 30 mg/dL have been associated with elevated risk of arterial ischemic stroke [28]. In addition to plasma glucose levels, glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) is used to diagnose (pre)diabetes and MetS, though its accuracy in HIV is unclear and may underestimate HIV-associated MetS risk, thus warranting further investigation [30, 31]. Limited research on lipid levels in PHIV youth is largely cross-sectional and frequently conducted without a control group, but studies suggest higher prevalence of dyslipidemia with increased levels of TG, LDL-C and total cholesterol (TC) and lower HDL-C, occasionally linked to older protease inhibitors (PI)-based regimens.[19–23] Apolipoproteins are key players in lipid transport and are increasingly recognized as prognostic markers for CVD and MetS [32, 33]. Available literature shows elevated ApoB in treated HIV adults and lower ApoA1 in horizontally-infected HIV adolescents compared to controls [34, 35]. We previously reported higher Lp(a) levels and comparable apolipoprotein levels at baseline and similar lipid levels over time in PHIV youth on cART compared to matched controls.[36, 37] In PHIV youth, assessment of these lipid profiles, lipoprotein levels, and other MetS-related markers may provide valuable insights into the increased MetS and CVD risk. However, due to limited cross-sectional data and HIV- and treatment-specific factors, uncertainty remains regarding the long-term risks and the predictive value of these markers in PHIV youth who are growing into adulthood [18].

Despite emerging cardiometabolic complications in PHIV youth and the increasing burden in the aging HIV population, studies on lipid (bio)markers and their prognostic value — especially focusing on Lp(a) and MetS — remain scarce. To investigate the potential role of lipoprotein, lipid and MetS markers in early determination and long-term monitoring of increased MetS and CVD risk in PHIV youth, we longitudinally assessed changes in lipid and lipoprotein levels over a ten-year period in a cohort of cART-treated PHIV youth and matched controls. Furthermore, we explored associations between variation in Lp(a) levels, cART regimens and MetS risk related markers over time. Finally, we cross-sectionally compared lipid and lipoprotein profiles, including differences in Lp(a) and ApoA1 levels, between PHIV youth on cART and controls in a larger cohort.

Methods

Study population

This study was conducted as part of the NeurOlogical, cardioVascular, vIsual, and neuroCognitivE performance in pediatric HIV-1 (NOVICE) study. This is a prospective, observational dynamic cohort study that investigates long-term neurological, cardiovascular, neurocognitive, and ophthalmological performance in PHIV youth. [36–42]

First and second assessments of the NOVICE cohort were conducted between 2012–2014 and 2017–2018, respectively. [38–42] For the current study, longitudinal and cross-sectional participants were enrolled between December 2022 and October 2023. PHIV children aged 8 to 18 years, who were not previously enrolled, were recruited from the outpatient department of the Emma Children’s Hospital which is part of the Amsterdam University Medical Center (AUMC). HIV-negative controls, who were not previously enrolled, were recruited from similar communities and through licensed adoption organizations as the PHIV participants. Participants were matched for age, sex, ethnicity, socioeconomic status (SES) and adoption status in line with the second NOVICE study (NOVICE II), as described earlier. [38–40] We approached all NOVICE participants who had provided consent for follow-up assessment and included those who were willing to participate. Exclusion criteria included severe non-HIV-related neurological disorders, a history of intracerebral tumours, severe traumatic brain injury (with loss of consciousness exceeding 30 min), and severe psychiatric disorders. We obtained written informed consent from participants aged 12 years and older and from parents or legal guardians of participants younger than 16 years. Participants were included for longitudinal assessment when they had participated in at least two study assessments and for cross-sectional analysis when they participated during third assessment (details are described in Supplementary Figure S1). The study protocols adhered to tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and the ethics committee of the AUMC reviewed and approved the study protocol which was registered with the Dutch clinical trial registration: Overview of Medical Research in the Netherlands (OMON) (ID: NL81972.018.22 and NL-OMON53727).

Demographics, disease and treatment-related characteristics

We collected demographic data through standardized questionnaires and clinical records in line with previous assessments. [38–42] Collected demographic data included age, sex, ethnic background, country or region of birth, education level of the child, adoption status, SES and HIV- and treatment-related characteristics, as previously described in more detail. [38–42] SES was determined based on parental education level and occupational status: parental education was classified according to the International Standard Classification of Education (ISCED) and occupational status was defined by the number of caregivers with a paid job (0, 1, or 2) [43]. Physical measurements included body mass index (BMI), calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m2), and blood pressure (BP), measured in millimetres of mercury (mmHg). BMI Z-scores were calculated available for participants under 18 years old, using sex- and age-adjusted reference standards [44, 45]. Treatment with cART was defined as a regimen consisting of at least three antiretroviral drugs of at least two different drug classes. Clinical data included exposure to cART regimens, which were categorized into four groups: (1) non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NNRTIs), (2) protease inhibitors (PIs), (3) integrase strand transfer inhibitors (INSTIs), and (4) nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTIs). For controls, HIV-negative status was confirmed via serology at all assessments. For this study, we also included sociodemographic data on dietary intake and substance use, with smoking/vaping indicated as regular use, alcohol consumption as > 10 units/week, drug use as habitual or recreational, as reported individually by participants.

Sample collection and measurements

We collected non-fasting venous plasma and serum blood samples of each participant and stored these at –80ºC until further analysis. For longitudinal analyses, we assessed lipid profiles at three timepoints using non-fasting venous plasma blood samples. Plasma levels of C-reactive protein (CRP), TC, HDL-C were directly determined. LDL-C levels were calculated using the Friedewald formula. [46] During the first assessment, Lp(a) concentrations were measured in mg/dL. In subsequent assessments, an isoform-insensitive second-generation assay was used which reports concentrations in nmol/L. To allow for comparison over time in longitudinal analysis, values originally reported in nmol/L were converted to mg/dL using the following approximation: Lp(a) [mg/dL] ≈ Lp(a) [nmol/L] ÷ 2.15. This conversion factor is commonly used in literature for second-generation isoform-insensitive assays, still remaining an approximation due to residual variability in apo(a) isoform size and inter-assay calibration differences. [47] Supplementary Table S1 demonstrates all clinical laboratory assay details.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic and clinical characteristics of PHIV youth and controls. Normality of data distributions was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and residual plots and homogeneity of variances was evaluated with Levene’s test. For comparisons between two groups, unpaired t-tests were used for normally distributed data with equal variances, while the Mann–Whitney U test was applied for non-normally distributed variables. If normality was confirmed but variances were unequal, Welch’s t-test was applied. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test. Lp(a) levels were log-transformed to approximate normal distributions for parametric analyses. For longitudinal assessment we used linear mixed effects models to investigate the association between HIV status and lipid levels over time. This model accounts for repeated measures and accommodates missing data within individuals. [48]

Associations between Lp(a) or cART regimens and markers in literature associated with MetS were assessed using linear mixed models for longitudinal assessment. Details of selected MetS markers for association analyses are shown in Supplementary Table S2. [49–51] We adjusted all models for age and sex to account for their potential confounding effects. In models assessing Lp(a) we additionally adjusted for ethnicity, given known ethnic differences in Lp(a) concentrations. [50, 52] Statistical significance was set at a two-sided p < 0.05. All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.3.1, R Core Team, Vienna, Austria). No correction for multiple comparisons was applied, as analyses were considered exploratory.

Results

The mean follow up duration per participant for the PHIV participants was 9.7 years (IQR 5.8–9.9) and 9.7 years (IQR 5.5–10.0) for controls (p = 0.523). No significant differences were observed between PHIV participants and controls in terms of age, sex, ethnicity, SES, adoption status, follow-up rate or duration, BP, BMI or substance use (p values > 0.05).

Lipid and lipoprotein levels and MetS markers between groups in the longitudinal cohort

Lipid levels and MetS markers at three time points for PHIV and controls are shown in Table 2. Median Lp(a) levels at first 43.6 mg/dL (IQR 25.8–83.4), second 39.6 mg/dL (IQR 26.3–113.6) and third assessment 42.1 mg/dL (IQR 18.7–71.3) for PHIV participants and first 34.3 mg/dL (IQR 18.2–63.0), second 42.7 mg/dL (IQR 23.5–79.9) and third assessment 28.4 mg/dL (13.5–59.2) for controls. There were no significant differences observed between groups for all three assessments (p values > 0.05). At baseline during second assessment, PHIV youth showed significantly higher TG levels compared to controls (p = 0.043). Liver enzymes were also significantly higher in PHIV participants, specifically aspartate aminotransferase (ASAT) at second assessment (p = 0.043), alanine aminotransferase (ALAT) (p = 0.005 and p = 0.007) and gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) (p = 0.003 and p = 0.001) at first and second assessment (all values below target range for both groups). Of note, no co-infections of Hepatitis B or C were present in this cohort (data presented in text only). Serum creatinine was lower in PHIV participants compared to controls at first assessment, within target range for both groups (PHIV mean 47.3 µmol/L (SD 11.4), controls mean 56.0 µmol/L (SD 13.2), p = 0.024). Levels of HDL-C, LDL-C, TC, ApoB, ApoA1, HbA1c, glucose, and CRP did not differ between groups (p values > 0.05). Furthermore, no significant differences were observed between PHIV youth and controls in percentages above different CVD risk-reference thresholds for Lp(a) levels (p values > 0.05) as demonstrated in Supplementary Table S4.

Lipoprotein and lipid levels over time and associations in the longitudinal cohort

Over time, no differences were observed in changes of Lp(a), HDL-C, LDL-C, TC, TG and ApoB between both groups (p values > 0.05), as shown Fig. 1. Detailed data description for these comparisons are provided in Supplementary Table S5. Associations between Lp(a) levels and MetS markers are presented in Table 3. Across all assessments, higher LDL-C was associated with higher Lp(a) (β = 0.004, 95% CI: −0.0007–0.007, adjusted for age, sex and ethnicity p = 0.013) in the control group. However, this effect was weakened for PHIV youth (HIV interaction effect: β = −0.003, p = 0.049). Furthermore, higher ApoB was also associated with higher Lp(a) in controls over time (β = 0.092, 95% CI: 0.011–0.173, adjusted for age, sex and ethnicity p = 0.017), with a significant negative effect on this association in PHIV youth (HIV interaction effect: β = −0.099, p = 0.029). No other associations were observed between Lp(a) and other MetS markers (p values > 0.05). Associations over time between cART regimens and MetS markers for PHIV youth are detailed in Table 4. PI use was positively associated with higher TC (β = 0.476, p = 0.014), LDL-C (β = 0.400, p = 0.009), and TG (β = 0.241, p = 0.041) levels. No associations between NNTRI or INSTI use and MetS markers were observed (p values > 0.05). Characteristics of participants and lipid profiles and MetS markers of the cross-sectional cohort

Cross-sectional findings are presented in Supplementary Tables S7 and S8 to provide context for the longitudinal analyses. A total of 98 participants were included: 53 PHIV adolescents and 45 matched controls. Median age was 17.4 years (IQR 12.7–22.4) for PHIV and 19.1 years (IQR 15.0–21.8) for controls. No significant differences were observed between groups in age, sex, ethnicity, SES, adoption status, BP, BMI, or substance use (all p > 0.05) as shown in Supplementary Table S7. Median Lp(a) levels were 88 nmol/L (IQR 38–178) in PHIV and 54 nmol/L (IQR 24–123) in controls (p = 0.176), with 56.6% of PHIV and 44.4% of controls above the reference range (> 75 nmol/L, p = 0.310). PHIV participants had higher GGT levels (median 21.0 U/L, IQR 17–27) compared to controls (18.0 U/L, IQR 14–22, p = 0.022), though values remained within the reference range. No significant differences were found in Lp(a), HDL-C, LDL-C, TC, TG, ApoB, ApoA1, ApoB/ApoA1 ratio, HbA1c, glucose, ASAT, ALAT, creatinine, or CRP levels (all p > 0.05) as shown in shown in Supplementary Table S8.

Discussion

In this study we found comparable MetS risk-related markers between well-treated PHIV youth and matched controls over time. PHIV youth and controls have comparable L(a) levels above intermediate risk reference value. At second assessment, PHIV participants demonstrated higher levels of TG compared to controls, but this was no longer different at the third visit. We observed an association between higher Lp(a) and both higher LDL-C and ApoB over time. However, this association was significantly weakened in the PHIV group, suggesting that this relationship is less pronounced in PHIV youth. Use of PI-based cART regimens was associated with lipid profile alterations, specifically elevated TG, TC, and LDL-C over time.

Cross-sectionally, we observed comparable lipid profiles and MetS markers between groups, similar to those seen over time in the longitudinal data.

These findings indicate that lipid and lipoprotein levels, including Lp(a) and ApoB, remain stable over time in cART-treated PHIV youth compared to matched controls. This contrasts some cross-sectional findings in adults with HIV. One study found elevated Lp(a) levels in HIV adults on cART compared to controls and an association with endothelial dysfunction. [53] Two other studies in adults with HIV observed elevated Lp(a) levels on cART or increasing Lp(a) levels after cART initiation [54, 55]. One explanation for these discrepancies may be the suppressive effect of active HIV infection on Lp(a) levels. A study in adults showed that HIV disease activity influences allele-specific apo(a) concentrations, with higher CD4 + counts and lower viral loads linked to more atherogenic isoforms [56]. Paradoxically, this implies that well suppressed individuals or treated HIV may exhibit higher Lp(a) levels due to prior suppression by active disease, potentially leading to atherogenic lipid profiles [56]. Differences between youth and adults in cumulative cART exposure may also contribute: adults typically have longer treatment durations, which have been associated with dyslipidemia and elevated lipoprotein levels.[57] Another possible explanation for the lack of change in Lp(a) levels over time is that initially elevated levels at baseline subsequently normalize with cART treatment. These observations are consistent with cross-sectional studies in PHIV youth showing no significant metabolic differences and improved lipid profiles following cART initiation or change to contemporary regimens [58, 59].

Demographic and clinical differences may further explain the divergence from adult studies. Our cohort had a younger age distribution, matched ethnicity, low prevalence of comorbidities and shorter period of exposure to different cART regimens compared to adult populations [34, 53–56] Additionally, baseline lipid profiles in children and adolescents are influenced by developmental factors such as puberty-related changes, including transient decreases in LDL-C and TC [60]. Moreover, MetS in youth is difficult to determine due to age heterogeneity and the lack of universally accepted reference ranges or age- and sex-specific z-scores within national guidelines for BMI and BP for youth aged 18–25 years [61–63]. Since elevated BMI (≥ 25 kg/m2) and BP (≥ 140/90 mmHg) thresholds are based on adult criteria, they may not accurately reflect metabolic risk in 18-year-old adolescents [62, 63].

We observed a weakened association between higher Lp(a) levels and elevated LDL-C and ApoB in PHIV participants, indicating that HIV infection and/or its treatment may modulate this relationship. Although no significant differences between percentages above Lp(a) cutoff range in PHIV youth (61.9%, 67.9%, 68.8% longitudinally and 56.6% cross-sectionally) compared to controls (56.5%, 61.2%, 50.0% longitudinally and 44.4% cross-sectionally) were observed (p values > 0.05), the effect of HIV as an independent risk factor, in addition to elevated Lp(a) levels, should be considered when assessing individual CVD risk [28]. Moreover, the intrinsic pro-inflammatory properties of Lp(a) could also still amplify CVD risk in PHIV youth [64]. Furthermore, despite using cART, PHIV youth already experience persistent chronic immune activation and systemic inflammation, which contributes substantially to elevated levels of various pro-inflammatory proteins and immune dysregulation beyond Lp(a) alone, such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) or markers of monocyte activation.[18, 58] This chronic inflammatory state exacerbates endothelial dysfunction and atherogenesis, potentially amplifying the CVD risk linked to elevated Lp(a) in this population [53, 64]. Consequently, the overall CVD risk in PHIV youth may be driven by a complex contribution of chronic inflammation, induced immune dysregulation and treatment related dyslipidemia [18].

In our longitudinal analysis, use of PI based regimens was significantly associated with elevated lipid parameters, including TG, TC, and LDL-C. Our findings underscore the importance of careful regimen selection and ongoing monitoring of metabolic risk in PHIV youth receiving PI-based regimens. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting that a switch to more contemporary cART regimens in PHIV youth was associated with more favorable lipid profiles [59]. A recent review also highlights that PI-based regimens, particularly those including lopinavir, are commonly associated with hypercholesterolemia, elevated LDL-C, reduced HDL-C, and hypertriglyceridemia in PHIV youth [18]. Some contemporary regimens, such as atazanavir and darunavir among PIs and tenofovir among NRTIs, are associated with more favorable lipid profiles [18]. Literature reports mixed results on lower CVD risk associated with use of INSTIs and newer generation NNRTIs (such as rilvipirine) in HIV-infected adults [65, 66].

Our results underscore the importance of monitoring MetS risk related markers in PHIV youth on cART to identify early signs of dyslipidemia, MetS and thus CVD risk. Conventional risk-assessment tools for CVD and MetS have limited validity in this population due to their unique clinical and metabolic profiles. While updated models such as the Data-collection on Adverse Effects of Anti-HIV Drugs (D:A:D) study incorporate HIV-specific factors like CD4 count and cART regimen for adults [67], literature in PHIV youth investigating risk assessment and managing CVD risk is lacking. Thus, highlighting the importance of adapting cardiometabolic screening and risk assessment to reflect the specific needs of PHIV youth.

The increased CVD risk associated with HIV could justify cardiometabolic monitoring and CVD risk assessment, routine screening for MetS is not yet standard practice in PHIV care. Although statins have been explored as a treatment option for hyperlipidemia in HIV adolescents, early interventions are not well studied and thus not implemented [68, 69]. Future studies could explore the potential impact of routine MetS monitoring, targeted health education and investigate lipid-lowering interventions where appropriate. In addition, efforts could focus on developing PHIV specific CVD or MetS assessment tools, including HIV- or treatment specific markers combined with traditional parameters.

This study provides valuable longitudinal insights into lipid profiles in PHIV youth compared to matched controls, however, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the relatively small sample size reduces statistical power, increasing the risk of type II errors and potentially masking true differences. We acknowledge that non-fasting lipid and lipoprotein measurements is an important limitation of our study, particularly given the potential to overestimate cardiometabolic markers and mask subtle differences related to HIV status. However, recent evidence supports the clinical relevance and reliability of non-fasting lipid profiles for CVD risk assessment, especially when comparisons are made under consistent conditions across study groups [70, 71]. Large cohort studies and expert consensus statements have shown that non-fasting lipid levels are generally stable and predictive of CVD risk, with minimal impact on most lipid parameters. [70, 71] Nonetheless, to address this limitation and better capture subtle metabolic differences in PHIV populations, future studies should aim to standardize fasting status or include both fasting and non-fasting samples. Furthermore, the dynamic study design led to variation in follow-up across participants, and although linear mixed-effects models were used to address missing data and confounding, the lack of correction for multiple comparisons raises the possibility of Type I errors, meaning some borderline significant findings should be viewed as exploratory and require confirmation in larger studies. Although current cART regimens were recorded at each assessment, detailed tracking of regimen changes and switches was not feasible and may have provided further insight. These findings offer valuable preliminary insights, but should be interpreted with caution and seen as hypothesis-generating, highlighting the need for larger and generalizable studies to confirm our observations. Despite these limitations, the study also has notable strengths. These include a well-matched control group and a longitudinal design spanning a decade, allowing for the assessment of lipid profiles over a relatively long interval. This approach strengthens the internal validity and provides unique insights into development of lipoproteins over time in the context of growing up with PHIV and life-long cART.

Conclusion

Reassuringly, lipid and lipoprotein levels were comparable between PHIV youth and matched controls, with no significant changes observed over time. Furthermore, the associations between lipid markers and Lp(a) was weakened for PHIV participants over time, which may imply that metabolic risk in PHIV youth is influenced more by disease- and treatment specific or broader pathophysiological characteristics of HIV. Different cART regimens may play a role in the development of dyslipidemia, with PI-based regimens being particularly associated with lipid alterations. Given this potential, routine lipid and MetS assessments in combination with health education in PHIV youth on PI-based regimens are advisable. Our observations suggest that while lipoprotein levels, such as Lp(a), may serve as general marker for CVD risk, they may be insufficient and lack specificity for predicting increased long-term MetS and CVD risk in the PHIV population. Our study demonstrates the challenges in diagnosing and managing CVD risk in PHIV youth, due to the complex interplay of HIV- and treatment-related factors. Further longitudinal studies are warranted, investigating CVD risk assessment tools and incorporating HIV- or treatment specific factors in combination with traditional metabolic screening, to improve early identification and management of MetS and CVD risk in cART-treated PHIV youth.
