Coupled biogenic and soil emissions amplify heatwave-driven secondary pollution
Authors: Peng Wang, Wenxuan Yu, Zhaolei Zhang, Miao Feng, Meng Gao, Chunfeng Tian, Yiheng Wang, Alex Guenther, Yuqiang Zhang, Zhijin Li, Pingqing Fu, Gregory R Carmichael, Hongliang Zhang, Jianmin Chen, Renhe Zhang, Drew Shindell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlobal air pollution policies have drastically lowered anthropogenic emissions, yet climate change threatens regional air quality through poorly understood natural feedbacks. Extreme heat accelerates biogenic volatile organic compound and soil nitrogen emissions, fueling ozone and secondary organic aerosol formation. Although standalone impacts of these biogenic sources are documented, chemical interactions between co-elevated vegetation and soil fluxes remain unconstrained. Here we show that a temperature-driven synergistic mechanism between biogenic terpenoids and soil nitrogen emissions severely exacerbated secondary air pollution during China's unprecedented 2022 heatwave. Integrating ground observations, satellite data, and chemical transport modeling, we demonstrate that a massive surge in biogenic terpenoids enhances atmospheric oxidation capacity by generating reactive peroxy radicals. These radicals accelerate the conversion of soil nitric oxide to nitrogen dioxide without consuming ozone, driving a 21% regional ozone increase across the Yangtze River Basin and boosting secondary organic aerosol loads by up to 4 μg m−3. These findings reveal a potent natural feedback loop that counteracts anthropogenic mitigation gains, underscoring the necessity of integrating coupled ecological dynamics into future climate adaptation and pollution control strategies.Keywords: Biogenic emissions, Soil nitrogen, Heatwaves, O3

Introduction

Over recent decades, the effective implementation of air pollution control policies has substantially reduced anthropogenic emissions, resulting in an increasing relative role of biogenic emissions [1]. Biogenic emissions are important precursors of secondary organic aerosol (SOA) and ozone (O3) and are therefore strongly linked to secondary pollution [2,3]. Despite widespread reductions in fine particulate matter (PM2.5), intensified secondary pollution events were frequently reported across various regions, with a rising proportion of secondary components (e.g., SOA) in PM2.5 and elevated O3 trends [4,5].

Biogenic emissions primarily include volatile organic compounds (VOCs), such as terpenoids released by vegetation, and soil nitrogen oxides (e.g., NO, N2O). These emissions are strongly influenced by meteorological parameters such as temperature [6,7]. In particular, high temperatures drive a significant increase in both biogenic terpenoids and soil nitrogen emissions in both polluted and pristine environments [8]. Concurrently, these high temperatures accelerate the rates of most atmospheric chemical reactions, further exacerbating secondary pollution from the increased biogenic emissions. In studies that separately examined biogenic terpenoids and soil nitrogen during heatwaves, both types of emissions were found to contribute to elevated O3 [9,10]. Although the impacts of these individual biogenic emissions on air quality are well studied, and their relative importance has been evaluated [9,11,12], the interactions among them remain unclear. With more frequent heatwaves and droughts projected in the future, the impacts of climate change on biogenic emissions are expected to be more significant and complex [13,14]. For example, isoprene emissions (a key biogenic terpenoid) may slightly increase during moderate drought but decrease during extreme drought [14,15]. Soil nitrogen levels typically drop during drought but spike upon rewetting. These changes can considerably affect air quality [16,17]. Consequently, an improved understanding of the interactions between biogenic emissions and heatwaves is urgently needed.

In the summer of 2022, China experienced unprecedentedly high temperatures, with 13 provinces experiencing the highest number of high-temperature days since 1961 [18]. Such an extreme weather event provides a natural testbed for exploring the interplay between meteorological processes, biogenic emissions, and air quality. In this study, we investigate this interplay by comprehensive approaches including ground-based observations, satellite data, and modeling. Our results reveal a new synergistic mechanism among biogenic emissions that induced notable increases in O3 during heatwaves. Specifically, biogenic emissions increase markedly during heatwaves, which provides more O3 precursors. Among these emissions, biogenic terpenoids can enhance atmospheric oxidation by generating reactive radicals, thereby facilitating the conversion of NO to NO2, which in turn increases O3 concentrations. This mechanism, together with the direct contribution of biogenic terpenoids to SOA, leads to exacerbated secondary pollution. Our study elucidates the mechanism underlying the exacerbation of secondary pollution during extreme weather conditions, which should be considered in emission control strategies.

Materials and methods

Observation data sources

The meteorological observations, including temperature at 2 m, relative humidity, wind speed, and wind direction at 10 m, were from the National Centers for Environmental Information (https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/, last access: March 2024). Hourly pollutant observations of PM2.5 and O3 were from the China National Environmental Monitoring Center (http://www.mee.gov.cn/hjzl/dqhj/, last access: April 2023). The MDA8 O3 was calculated from hourly data, derived from the highest of 17 consecutive 8 h averages starting from 08:00 to 24:00 each day (requiring at least 14 valid 8 h averages). The formaldehyde (HCHO) and NO2 column data were obtained from the Tropospheric Monitoring Instrument satellite of the Sentinel-5P project (https://sentinel.esa.int/web/sentinel/missions/sentinel-5p/, last access: April 2023). More information on satellite data processing was provided in Supplementary Text S1.

WRF-CMAQ model system configuration

The Community Multiscale Air Quality (CMAQ) version 5.4 with the modified SAPRC-11 photochemical mechanism was applied with a horizontal resolution of 36 km × 36 km in June, July, and August from 2020 to 2022 (Supplementary Fig. S1–S2) [19]. Specifically, we calculated the average results from 2020 to 2021, defined as the 2020–2021 mean, for comparison with 2022. We used the Weather Research and Forecasting model (WRF) v4.2.2 to generate meteorological inputs, driven by initial and boundary conditions from the National Centers for Environmental Prediction Final Reanalysis Data with a high resolution of 0.25° × 0.25° (https://rda.ucar.edu/datasets/ds083.2/, last access: March 2023). The anthropogenic emissions were from the Multi-resolution Emission Inventory for China (MEIC) [20] (http://www.meicmodel.org, last access: March 2023) for 2020, and were adjusted to reflect emission changes in 2021 and 2022. Biogenic emissions were generated using MEGAN v2.1 with different scenarios in 2022 (Supplementary Table S1) [6]. The near-real-time daily wildfire emissions were from the Fire Inventory from the National Center for Atmospheric Research (https://www2.acom.ucar.edu/modeling/finn-fire-inventory-ncar, last access: March 2025). We also conducted a simulation without wildfire (NOFIRE, Supplementary Table S1) to better evaluate the role of biogenic emissions in the study (Supplementary Text S2 and Fig. S3). Observations from the National Air Quality Monitoring Network (http://www.cnemc.cn/, last accessed: March 2024) were used to evaluate the model performance. Hourly isoprene concentrations from 2020 to 2022 were evaluated using observational data obtained from the Chengdu Academy of Environmental Sciences (30.66° N, 104.05° E). Hourly isoprene concentrations (2020–2022) were evaluated using observations from the Chengdu Academy of Environmental Sciences (30.66° N, 104.05° E, Supplementary Fig. S4). In general, the WRF-CMAQ provided robust results for the analysis, and the detailed model validation and the relevant equations are provided in Supplementary Texts S3 and S4, respectively (also see Supplementary Table S2–S3).

We used the integrated reaction-rate analysis in CMAQ to determine the HOx budget to better understand the atmospheric oxidation capacity (Supplementary Text S5 and Table S4). Also, we selected three extreme high-temperature events in 2022 (June 25, 2022–June 29, 2022; July 23, 2022 –July 27, 2022; and August 12, 2022–August 16, 2022) to investigate the changes in emissions and air quality under extreme weather (Supplementary Table S5).

WRF simulations

We designed four sets of WRF experiments with different physical schemes to accurately simulate the extreme heat in 2022. The detailed parameters and model performance of these WRF experiments are shown in Supplementary Table S6–S7, respectively. For all simulations, the WRF land use statistics data were updated to the Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer Land Cover Type (MCD12Q1) Version 6 data product (https://ladsweb.modaps.eosdis.nasa.gov/search/order/1/MCD12Q1--6, last access: April 2023), and surface layer soil moisture initial data were substituted with NASA Soil Moisture Active and Passive Level-4 products (https://search.earthdata.nasa.gov, last access: April 2023). The URBAN case had better temperature performance than other simulations and fits the observations more closely in the YRB, where extreme heat occurred (Supplementary Fig. S5 and Table S2). Stations with extremely high temperatures (maximum daily average temperatures >35 °C) were further screened to evaluate their meteorological performance (Supplementary Fig. S6). The results showed that the Rapid Update Cycle Land Surface Model (RUC case) underestimated the temperature, while the Noah-Multiparameterization Land Surface Model (NOAHMP) overpredicted it. As a result, we selected the URBAN case to conduct the WRF simulation.

Adjustment of anthropogenic emissions

As the MEIC emissions inventory available for this study extends only to 2020, we used the 2020 data as the baseline for deriving 2022 emissions [21]. Despite the COVID-19 lockdown measures that began in late January 2020 and extended until March 2020, emissions had largely recovered to pre-pandemic levels by the summer months, making the 2020 data relevant for our adjustments. In this study, we conducted adjustments to three major anthropogenic sectors: transportation, industry, and power. For the transportation sector, we used passenger turnover and cargo turnover statistics (https://www.mot.gov.cn/tongjishuju/gonglu/index.html, last accessed: April 2023) to adjust its nitrogen oxides (NOx) and VOCs emissions (see Supplementary Table S8–S9 for adjustment factors). In the industrial sector, we applied the same adjustment ratio to VOC emissions and implemented a 2.0% reduction rate for NOx [22]. Biogenic emission scenarios in summer 2022

To better investigate the influence of extremely high temperatures in 2022 on biogenic emissions and air quality, we conducted three sensitivity scenarios: BASE, using the default MEGAN emission; DROUGHT, incorporating drought effects; and ADD5, using air temperature increased by 5.0 °C as MEGAN input while also incorporating drought effects (Supplementary Tables S1 and S10). The DROUGHT case is used to quantify drought/water-stress impacts via soil moisture parameterization in MEGAN, since biogenic terpenoid emissions are affected under combined heat-drought stress [6]. The ADD5 case is designed to estimate the response of biogenic emissions and air quality under higher temperatures and future warmer climates. Additionally, the selection of a 5.0 °C increase is based on the temperature changes under the Shared Socioeconomic Pathway 5-8.5 scenario [23,24]. This increment aligns with the projected global surface warming range of 3.3–5.7 °C by the period 2081–2100 relative to 1850–1900, as reported in the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change Sixth Assessment Report.

Results

Observational evidence of high-temperature and air quality changes

In summer 2022, temperatures increased significantly compared to the 2020–2021 mean, with the average temperature rising from 23.0 to 25.0 °C (maximum 46.4 °C, Supplementary Fig. S11). The most pronounced temperature increase was observed in the Yangtze River Basin (YRB), including the Sichuan–Chongqing region (CY), Yangtze River Delta (YRD), and the regions between them (Supplementary Fig. S1–S2), where the average temperature increased by up to 6.0 °C during extreme high-temperature events (Supplementary Fig. S12). The number of days with maximum hourly temperature ≥35.0 °C (FREQ35) also increased substantially nationwide, roughly doubling relative to the 2020–2021 mean (Supplementary Fig. S13). The geographical center of high temperatures shifted from South China to the YRB in summer 2022, where FREQ35 reached 25 days. FREQ35 increased by more than 200.0% in the CY and YRD regions within the YRB, with monthly changes described in Supplementary Text S7. Opposite patterns are observed in soil moisture during the same period (Supplementary Fig. S14). Regions that experienced higher temperatures are associated with decreased soil moisture, with drops of over 50.0% in portions of the YRB, further exacerbating drought risks [25].

Moreover, the high temperatures were accompanied by elevated O3 (approximately 5.0 ± 8.9% on average) in summer 2022 compared to the 2020–2021 mean, with similar spatial distributions (Fig. 1, Supplementary Fig. S15, and Supplementary Text S7). Compared to the 2020–2021 mean, the observed MDA8 O3 exhibits a regional average increase of 21.0 ± 9.5% across the entire YRB, while specific growth rates in the YRD and CY regions are 18.2 ± 9.3% and 22.0 ± 5.7%, respectively, coinciding with the significant temperature surge (Fig. 1). A more significant rise in O3 ground observation is noted during extreme high temperature events, with an increase exceeding 15.0 ppb in the YRB compared to the 2020–2021 mean (Supplementary Fig. S12), surpassing the average summer O3 increase of 1.9 ppb yr−1 from 2013 to 2019 [26]. A remarkable increase (>20.0%) in O3 is observed in the PRD during extreme high-temperature events, although the average MDA8 O3 in the PRD remains almost the same in summer 2022 as in the 2020–2021 mean (Supplementary Fig. S12 and S15). In contrast to O3 patterns, PM2.5 concentrations decreased across China, with the most pronounced decreases in the North China Plain (NCP) and Central China (up to −5.0 μg m−3, Supplementary Fig. S16). However, PM2.5 concentrations in some CY cities increased (>5.0 μg m−3) alongside O3, reflecting a co-occurrence of high O3 and PM2.5 due to rising temperatures, similar to that reported for North America [27]. This suggests that more stringent emission control strategies are needed in the CY region to combat the impact of extreme events.

Temperature-driven biogenic emissions and their combined impacts on O3

The effect of biogenic terpenoids on air quality was investigated, following previous reports that they play an important role during heatwaves [9]. Results show that high temperatures increased biogenic terpenoid emissions in summer 2022, as evidenced by both modeling analyses and observations (Fig. 2). Isoprene, a dominant terpenoid species (Supplementary Fig. S17), shows a significant increase in regions with notable O3 rises, such as the YRB and CY (Supplementary Fig. S18). In these regions, isoprene emission rates increased significantly, reaching 22.2 mol h−1 km−2 (relative increment >130.0%) compared to the 2020–2021 mean, and even exceeding 27.8 mol h−1 km−2 (relative increment >150.0%) during extreme high-temperature events (Supplementary Fig. S19). Besides, as an indicator of isoprene emissions, satellite HCHO column densities also increase in 2022 over the YRB (31.0 ± 8.5%), supporting the enhanced isoprene emissions observed in our model (Supplementary Fig. S20, Supplementary Text S1) [9,28]. From summer 2020 to 2022, elevated HCHO column densities persisted in the YRB (Supplementary Fig. S20–S21). The observed increases substantially exceed the random uncertainty of the satellite retrievals [29], confirming that the interannual trend is robust. Both model simulations and satellite observations consistently indicate marked increases in the YRB during summer 2022 relative to prior years (Supplementary Fig. S20). Detailed comparisons are provided in the Supplementary Text S1.

While simulated biogenic terpenoid emissions increase ubiquitously in the YRB, the CMAQ model used in this study underestimates the observed O3 in the central YRB (Supplementary Fig. S15). The severe drought in the YRB during the summer of 2022 likely reduced O3 removal by vegetation [30], a process not incorporated into the Wesley dry deposition scheme used in this study [31]. This may partially explain the underestimation of O3 [31,32], though likely not all of it, since the severe drought is widespread, whereas the underestimation was mainly in the central YRB [31]. This region, identified as a NOx-limited regime (NOx = NO2 + NO) based on HCHO to NO2 ratios (HCHO/NO2, FNR) [33] (Fig. 2, Supplementary Fig. S22, Supplementary Text S9), also exhibits an increase in observed NO2 column densities (31.0 ± 32.8% on average) that the CMAQ model failed to capture (Supplementary Fig. S23–S24). Similar to HCHO, a large portion of the uncertainties in NO2 column densities (typically −23.0% to −37.0%) are systematic, suggesting that the observed upward trend is real [34]. Consequently, the underestimated O3 is also due to the lower O3 production catalyzed by NOx emission (referred to as O3N, Supplementary Fig. S25).

In recent years, anthropogenic NOx emissions in China have steadily decreased due to the implementation of the Clean Air Action Plan [21]. Additionally, a decrease in anthropogenic NO2 emissions associated with lockdowns related to the omicron COVID-19 variant was reported in early 2022 compared with 2019–2021 (Supplementary Fig. S26) [35]. Furthermore, changes in the lifetimes of OH and NOx have been found to have a weak impact on tropospheric NO2 columns in summer, in contrast to winter [36,37]. Therefore, elevated NO2 in summer 2022 is highly likely to be linked to soil emissions. NO2 columns increase with temperature (Supplementary Fig. S27–S28), consistent with the pattern observed in soil NO emissions (Supplementary Fig. S29), further supporting this connection [6,38]. Like biogenic terpenoids, simulated soil NO emissions in the YRB show an increase of over 50% in summer 2022 compared to the 2020–2021 mean (Fig. 2). This additional NO is rapidly oxidized to NO2, and under the NOx-limited conditions typical of the region, the resulting NO2 enhancement drives increased photochemical O3 production. Despite this, their emissions might still be underestimated by the Model of Emissions of Gases and Aerosols from Nature version 2.1 (MEGANv2.1) used in this study, as reported previously [2,7,38], further leading to an underestimation in their contribution to secondary pollutants.

To better investigate the effect of biogenic emissions on air quality, we conducted multiple test cases in MEGAN (details in section 2.5). The co-occurrence of drought (DROUGHT case) reduced MDA8 O3 due to the lower isoprene emissions (Supplementary Fig. S30–S31) [14]. Future projections indicate that increasing drought intensity and duration may reduce emissions of isoprene and soil NO [16,17,39]. This further clarifies that the increases in these biogenic emissions are primarily driven by rising temperatures, and the drought effect can partially offset the pollution enhancement [14,40].

In contrast, higher temperatures in the ADD5 case elevated MDA8 O3 in the YRB region compared to BASE, attributed to increases in both BVOC and soil NO emissions (Supplementary Fig. S30–S32). Moreover, the ADD5 nearly eliminates the underestimation in O3 simulations in this region (Supplementary Fig. S33–S34). In the ADD5 case, enhanced biogenic terpenoid emissions lead only to a slight O3 increase (<2.0 ppb) in highly developed regions, including the NCP and Shanghai in the YRD, relative to the 2020–2021 mean; these regions are identified as transition or VOC-limited regimes (Supplementary Fig. S35). A more significant O3 increase (up to 5.0 ppb) is simulated in a large area in the YRB region due to the increased soil NO emissions. Consequently, this sensitivity study reinforces the conclusion that the combined impact of temperature-driven biogenic terpenoid and soil NOx emissions led to the increase in O3 across China during the summer of 2022.

Biogenic terpenoids fuel secondary pollution by enhanced atmospheric oxidation capacity

Enhanced atmospheric oxidation capacity is simulated in the summer of 2022, as indicated by the higher modeled HOx (HOx = HO2 + OH) concentration (Supplementary Fig. S36). Atmospheric oxidation capacity drives secondary pollutant formation and is influenced by a combination of emissions and meteorological changes [41]. In this study, the key oxidant concentrations (e.g., organic peroxy radicals (RO2) and HOx) are used to characterize the atmospheric oxidation capacity. In summer 2022, a notable increase in average HOx concentration (5.0–10.0 ppt) is simulated relative to the 2020–2021 mean in the YRB, with a spatial distribution similar to the O3 increase. Furthermore, in regions where HOx concentrations exceed 15 ppt, HOx is positively correlated with O3 formation, illustrating its important role in elevated O3 (Fig. 3, details in Supplementary Text S10).

The enhanced atmospheric oxidation capacity is mainly attributable to the impact of biogenic terpenoid emissions on HOx generation. Rising isoprene emissions drive increases in HCHO (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. S37), and the increased photolysis may lead to elevated HOx generation (average of 5.0 ± 16.1%), given the standard deviation is larger than the mean. In summer 2022, the average HOx produced from HCHO photolysis increased by 10.0 ± 18.1% compared to the 2020–2021 mean. This increase is amplified in the YRD and CY regions, with increases of close to 30.0 ± 18.2% relative to the 2020–2021 mean, reaching 40.0 ± 13.5% during extreme high-temperature events (in the BASE case). With a further rise in isoprene emissions (ADD5 case), the contribution of HCHO photolysis to HOx in the YRB increased by 100.0% compared to the 2020–2021 mean. In addition, rising biogenic terpenoid emissions increase the concentration of RO2 radicals through reactions with the OH radical [42]. A more than 50.0 ± 17.3% increase in RO2 is simulated in the YRB region, of which 70.0 ± 10.4% is attributed to the enhanced biogenic terpenoid emissions in summer 2022 compared to the 2020–2021 mean (Supplementary Fig. S38–S39).

Both hydroperoxyl radicals (HO2) and RO2 can oxidize NO to NO2 without consuming O3. Subsequently, photolysis of NO2 promotes net O3 formation [43]. These reactions, enhanced by increased atmospheric oxidation capacity from biogenic terpenoids and higher soil-emitted NO during heatwaves, contribute to net O3 production in the transition or NOx-limited regime (Fig. 4). This impact is more remarkable in the ADD5 case where a higher atmospheric oxidation capacity level is simulated compared to the BASE case, producing up to 7.0 ppb higher O3 (Fig. 3). Although a previous study reported that extremely high temperatures suppressed O3 formation [44], mainly occurring at leaf temperatures exceed 40.0 °C due to the decreased role of nitrogen oxide sequestration by peroxyacetyl nitrates and the reduction in isoprene emission [45]. Current findings suggest that rising temperatures remain generally associated with elevated emissions of biogenic terpenoids and soil NO, potentially leading to sustained O3 increases under future climate scenarios.

Rising soil temperature enhances emissions of biogenic volatile organic compounds (BVOCs) and the release of soil NO. Increased BVOC oxidation elevates HOx and RO2 radical levels, promoting conversion of NO to NO2. This pathway suppresses the direct consumption of O3 by NO (indicated by the red cross), thereby accelerating O3 accumulation and simultaneously promoting SOA formation. Biogenic terpenoid emissions drive SOA formation

The increase in biogenic terpenoids leads to higher SOA concentrations in the YRB, although PM2.5 is slightly underpredicted (Supplementary Figs. S16, S40, and S41). The average increase in SOA concentration in the YRB region is 2.0 ± 1.1 μg m−3, and this rises to over 4.0 μg m−3 in the CY and YRD. Similar to O3, there is a notable positive relationship between SOA concentration and temperature (Fig. 3c). A notable increase in SOA (on average 50.0%) occurs when the temperature exceeds 30.0 °C, while the most significant increase in O3 occurs at temperatures greater than 35.0 °C (up to a 70.0% increase). In addition, the enhanced atmospheric oxidation capacity from biogenic terpenoids led to a widespread increase in SOA concentrations across large areas in the NCP, Central China, and the YRB. This study includes major SOA precursors [19], but excluding volatile chemical products may underestimate increases in SOA during heatwaves (Supplementary Text S11 [46]). In contrast, secondary inorganic aerosol (SNA; sulfate, nitrate, and ammonium) is lower (up to −6.0 μg m−3) and regarded as the primary reason for the decrease in PM2.5, mainly due to reduced anthropogenic emissions (Supplementary Fig. S42) [47]. In summer, the increased biogenic terpenoids can compete with SNA precursors (e.g., SO2 and NOx) to form SOA rather than SNA.

The SOA concentration increases with biogenic terpenoid emissions, as shown in the ADD5 case, and this increase is mainly attributed to increased biogenic SOA (BSOA) (Supplementary Fig. S43–S44). On average, the SOA in the ADD5 case is 164.0 ± 8.2% and 194.0 ± 55.0% of the BASE case and the 2020–2021 mean, respectively. In addition, the contribution of BSOA to total SOA in the ADD5 case increases to an average of 63.0 ± 6.2% in the NCP (Supplementary Fig. S45), a region that has less vegetation cover. In the YRB, the maximum SOA concentration exceeds 12.0 μg m−3 in the ADD5 case, leading to an increase in PM2.5 (up to 4.0 μg m−3) compared to the BASE case (Supplementary Fig. S46). Simulations showed that enhanced biogenic terpenoid emissions substantially increased both PM2.5 and O3 in the CY and YRD regions, highlighting potential challenges for air-quality improvement under future warming.

Discussion and conclusion

Our findings provide insight into how air quality responds to extreme high-temperature events. The elevated biogenic terpenoids enhance atmospheric oxidation capacity by increasing the production of HO2 and RO2 radicals. These radicals convert soil NO to NO2 without consuming O3, driving severe O3 accumulation within NOx-limited regimes. With the ongoing abatement of anthropogenic NOx emissions, the areal extent of NOx-limited regimes is enlarging [48]. Furthermore, rising biogenic terpenoid emissions from greening activities accelerate this transition [49]. Consequently, the interactive effects between biogenic and soil emissions are expected to increasingly govern air quality under a warming climate.

The quantification of this biogenic-soil synergy remains subject to uncertainties in emission modeling. Specifically, the MEGAN model likely underestimates soil NO emissions, implying that the actual chemical intensity at high temperatures may be stronger than simulated. For this reason, our results represent a conservative estimation of the coupled natural feedback. Future research using updated emission factors and high-resolution surface fluxes will be critical for further constraining these non-linear interactions.

From a policy standpoint, these results caution that anthropogenic emission controls alone may yield lower mitigation efficiency during high temperatures as biogenic source enhancements become a significant driver of secondary pollutants. Under continued warming, concurrent increases in biogenic terpenoids and soil NO could offset the air-quality gains expected from further NOx reductions. Accordingly, anthropogenic control strategies would benefit from accounting for shifting biogenic baselines. More concretely, forest management strategies should integrate atmospheric chemical feedbacks into ecological planning. Such integration ensures that large-scale afforestation remains a viable tool for climate mitigation without inadvertently compromising regional air quality targets.
