RSV-related hospital admissions in a subset of hospitals in England and Scotland: A real-world study (2018–2023)
Authors: Rachel M. Reeves, Shammi Luhar, Triantafyllos Pliakas, Emily Phipps, Danielle E. Robinson, Alycia Perkins, Jamie Wallis, Lewis Carpenter, Elisa Turriani, Alen Marijam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRespiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is a major cause of acute respiratory infection (ARI), though its healthcare burden and costs are insufficiently described among adults in the United Kingdom. With the recent availability of vaccines, understanding RSV-related secondary care burden is essential. Routinely collected adult ARI hospitalization data between 2018 and 2023 from four English National Health Service (NHS) trusts and a Scottish Health Board were linked to RSV, influenza, and coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) microbiology tests and results. ARI incidence risk and ARI-related hospitalizations overall and per pathogen were described (including mortality, hospitalization length of stay [LoS], intensive care unit [ICU] admissions/LoS, and costs). RSV-positive microbiology tests and International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-10 RSV diagnoses were compared. In both settings, incidence risk of first ARI hospitalizations increased from 2018/19 to 2022/23. Of RSV-tested hospitalizations, 7.0% (England) and 4.1% (Scotland) had an RSV-positive test. Of these, 7.4% and 8.0% died in hospital, respectively; similar proportions were observed for influenza (5.5% and 5.0%). In England and Scotland, RSV-positive hospitalization LoS was 5 and 6 d, comparable to COVID-19 (8 and 10) and influenza (4 and 3). ICU admittance and direct costs per hospitalization were generally comparable across pathogens. In England and Scotland, 63.5% and 33.7% of RSV-positive hospitalizations had an RSV ICD-10 code, respectively. Among English and Scottish adults, RSV-positive hospitalizations had substantial healthcare resource utilization, comparable to COVID-19- and influenza-positive hospitalizations. ICD-10 codes are limited proxies for identifying RSV hospitalizations. Broadening RSV adult vaccination recommendations may reduce the RSV secondary care burden.

Introduction

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is a seasonal respiratory virus associated with a high risk of hospitalization and mortality, particularly among older adults and those with chronic conditions.1–3 Alongside influenza and coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), RSV is a major contributor to acute respiratory infection (ARI), incurring a significant burden to healthcare systems, predominantly from hospitalizations.4–7 In the UK, RSV hospitalization rates are estimated at 321 per 100,000 person years in individuals ≥60 y of age (YOA).8

To protect against lower respiratory tract disease (LRTD) caused by RSV, the European Medicines Agency (EMA) approved adjuvanted RSVPreF3 for adults ≥60 YOA and at-risk adults 50–59 YOA (2023), RSVPreF for adults ≥18 YOA (2023), and mRNA-1345 for adults ≥60 YOA and at-risk adults 18–49 YOA (2024). The UK Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA) approved these vaccines in accordance with official EMA recommendations, and a free vaccination program is available for adults 75–79 YOA in the UK.9–12

Quantifying the RSV health and economic burden among adults is essential to inform policymakers on the importance of RSV preventative measures in adults ≥18 YOA. Current UK literature on RSV burden among adults is limited and outdated.13 One study indicated that between 2001 and 2009, annual direct medical costs to manage ARIs in adults 19–49 YOA was approximately £175 million.6 Between 1995 and 2009 in the UK, an average of 17,799 hospitalizations and 8,482 deaths per RSV season were attributed to RSV among adults ≥18 YOA.14 Furthermore, this burden may be underestimated due to under-testing and under-reporting, caused by ambiguous disease classification, lack of routine testing, and poor test sensitivity.15–18

This study explored the incidence risk of ARI, healthcare resource use (HCRU), and direct costs of RSV management in English and Scottish secondary care hospital settings between 2018 and 2023 among adults ≥18 YOA. The burden of RSV-positive hospitalizations was evaluated against overall ARI-related hospitalizations and hospitalizations for COVID-19 and/or influenza. Additionally, the concordance of RSV International Classification of Diseases 10th Revision (ICD-10) coded diagnoses versus positive microbiology tests was described.

Materials and methods

Data collection

This database study was an observational, retrospective, and multi-center cohort study which analyzed the routine electronic health record (EHR) data of adults from secondary care settings in England and Scotland. Results are reported over five consecutive annual cycles, from 1st April to 31st March for 2018/19, 2019/20, 2020/21, 2021/22, and 2022/23. EHR data from the Arcturis Real-World Data Network database of four England National Health Service (NHS) trusts (Oxford University Hospital NHS Foundation Trust, Milton Keynes University Hospital NHS Foundation Trust, Hampshire Hospital NHS Foundation Trust, and Chelsea and Westminster Hospital NHS Foundation Trust) and a health board in Scotland (NHS Greater Glasgow and Clyde Health Board [NHSGGC]) were analyzed separately.

Index date was defined as the hospital entry date of an ARI ICD-10 coded hospitalization that fell within the observational period, using the ICD-10 codes in Table S1. Eligible individuals were ≥18 YOA at index and hospitalized with an ARI diagnosis within the observational period. Anonymized data on demographics, microbiology tests, diagnoses, procedures (OPCS Classification of Interventions and Procedures v4), ward movements (intensive care unit [ICU] admissions), recorded episodes, and spells were extracted.

Hospitalizations were attributed to the annual cycle in which the index date fell. Tests dated ≤7 d after a hospitalization were associated with that stay, accounting for recording delays and in line with the UK Health Security Agency’s target RSV test turnaround time; tests without dates, or dates falling outside the 7-d window following a hospitalization, were excluded.19 Test dates were attributed to the date of the test result, and tests linked to multiple hospitalizations were attributed to the closest prior stay.

Data analysis

Sub-cohort analysis

Among those hospitalized with an ARI diagnosis, a sub-cohort of individuals who underwent microbiological testing for RSV, COVID-19, or influenza during hospitalization were identified (pathogen-tested sub-cohort). Individuals who tested positive were further sub-grouped by pathogen type (Table S2). Hospitalizations could be included in multiple sub-groups if the patient tested positive for multiple relevant pathogens.

Characteristics of ARI-hospitalized adults

ARI hospitalizations over the observation period were stratified by sex, age, ARI diagnosis position and type, patient risk-group, comorbidities (either primary or secondary diagnoses and recorded ≤12 months prior to index; ICD-10 codes for comorbidities are listed in Table S3), and annual cycle. In line with a previously published RSV clinical trial, individuals were considered high-risk if they had asthma, cardiac disorder, chronic kidney disease (CKD), chronic liver disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) or other chronic restrictive pulmonary diseases, or diabetes recorded in the year prior to index; individuals without these comorbidities were considered non-high-risk.20

Incidence risk of first ARI hospitalization per annual cycle

Incidence risk of first ARI hospitalization, defined as first ARI hospitalization per annual cycle for each eligible patient, was estimated. The number of first ARI hospitalizations was divided by the adult population at risk of being hospitalized for ARI, as defined by the average of the 2018 to 2020 adult catchment populations of the included hospitals (Appendix A).21,22 Incidence risk is reported per 1,000 individuals alongside 95% confidence intervals (CI), estimated using the normal approximation method for proportions. Due to the descriptive nature of these analyses, no formal hypothesis testing was undertaken.

In-hospital mortality

An in-hospital death was defined as an individual who died during hospitalization, or had their death recorded within 7 d of discharge, accounting for potential reporting delays. Percentage of in-hospital deaths among ARI-related individuals are reported with associated 95% CI.

HCRU of ARI-associated hospitalizations

Median length of stay (LoS) and interquartile range (IQR) of hospitalization and ICU admissions were calculated as the difference between the respective start and end dates. Proportions of ARI hospitalizations with an associated ICU admission were also reported.

If only year (or year and month) of death was provided and a patient’s last recorded date in other medical records was in the same timeframe, that recorded date was adopted as the individual’s date of death. If there was no medical record in the reported year of death, the midpoint of the year (1st July) was assumed as the individual’s date of death. If only death year and death month were provided, the individual was assumed to have died at the approximate midpoint (15th) of the recorded month. Where only the year of death was provided prior to other recorded years in the medical records, the data were omitted from the analysis as implausible.

Direct medical cost per hospitalization

Unit costs for diagnoses and procedures were derived from NHS secondary care tariffs for healthcare resource groups (HRG), with costs based on HRG4+ 2022/23 National Costs Grouper.23 For HRGs with no nationally agreed tariff, patient-level cost data for the relevant HRG codes were averaged and applied as cost per patient hospitalization. Mean cost and standard deviation (SD) per hospitalization are reported in Great British Pounds (£, GBP).

RSV microbiology testing versus ICD-10 coding

To describe the concordance of RSV ICD-10 coded diagnoses and microbiology test results, RSV hospitalizations with an RSV microbiology test and/or an ICD-10 diagnosis (Table S4) were compared.

Ethical considerations

This study used anonymized EHR data from the Arcturis Real-World Data Network database, which has received research database ethical approval from the NHS Health Research Authority Yorkshire & The Humber – Leeds East Research Ethics Committee (research ethics committee [REC] reference: 24/YH/0164). An anonymized dataset was provided by the West of Scotland Safe Haven research database service at NHSGGC under REC approval 22/WS/0163. To protect patient privacy, some results are censored. Categories with fewer than five patients are reported as “<5” with no percentages, and additional categories may be masked using a range of five (inclusive) to prevent back-calculation. No results are presented for any annual cycle or sub-cohort with fewer than ten patients. Additionally, categories of categorical data were collapsed, where required, for example using 10-y age bands rather than 5 y. For the hospitalization cost analysis, summary statistics were reported only when more than 20 observations contributed.

Incidence risk of first ARI hospitalization per annual cycle

Over the observation period, 133,790 and 113,529 first ARI hospitalizations per annual cycle were identified in English trusts and NHSGCC, respectively. In the 2018/19 annual cycle, incidence risk of first ARI hospitalization was estimated at 14.56 (95% CI: 14.37–14.74) per 1,000 individuals in English trusts and 24.07 (23.76–24.37) per 1,000 individuals in NHSGGC (Table 2). An upward trend was observed between 2020/21 and 2022/23, corresponding to the transition to the post-COVID-19 pandemic period. Incidence risk increased from 14.90 (14.71–15.08) to 19.26 (19.05–19.47) per 1,000 individuals in English trusts between 2020/21 and 2022/23. In NHSGGC, the increase was 20.93 (20.65–21.22) to 25.50 (25.18–25.81) per 1,000 individuals.

Mortality during ARI hospitalization

Of ARI-related hospitalizations, 14.4% (95% CI: 14.2–14.6; 16,540 deaths) and 15.2% (14.9–15.4, 13,727) of individuals had an in-hospital death in English trusts and NHSGGC, respectively (Table 3; Table S6). Among pathogen-tested hospitalizations, comparable rates were observed at 15.2% (15.0–15.5) in English trusts and 16.8% (16.5–17.1) in NHSGGC. Regarding the three pathogen-specific cohorts, COVID-19-positive hospitalizations had the highest percentage of in-hospital deaths at 13.9% (13.5–14.4) for English trusts and 18.6% (18.0–19.2) for NHSGCC, followed by RSV-positive hospitalizations at 7.4% (5.9–9.4) and 8.0% (6.1–9.9) and influenza-positive hospitalizations at 5.5% (4.8–6.4) and 5.0% (4.3–5.7), respectively. HCRU of ARI hospitalizations

ARI hospitalization LoS

In both English trusts and NHSGGC, ARI hospitalization median LoS was 4 d (IQR: 1–12 d, 1–11 d, respectively [Table 3]). Among the pathogen-tested hospitalizations, median LoS was 6 d in both English trusts (2–14 d) and NHSGGC (2–15 d). In English trusts, RSV-positive hospitalizations lasted for 5 d (2–12 d), COVID-19-positive hospitalizations for 8 d (3–18 d) and influenza-positive hospitalizations for 4 d (1–10 d). Similarly, in NHSGGC, RSV-positive individuals were hospitalized for 6 d (2–12 d), COVID-19-positive for 10 d (3–27 d) and influenza-positive for 3 d (1–8 d). In English trusts and NHSGGC, ARI hospitalization LoS increased with age (Table S7).

ICU admittance and LoS

ICU admittance during ARI hospitalization was 4.0% in English trusts (median LoS: 5 d [IQR: 2–12]) and 1.4% in NHSGGC (6 d [2–12]); with 5.2% and 2.1% of patients in ICU having a pathogen test, respectively (Table 3). Of the hospitalizations where the patient was admitted to ICU and tested for RSV, COVID-19, and/or influenza, 5.6% (LoS: 3 d [2–8]) and 3.1% (11 d [5–16]) had an RSV-positive test, 5.6% (7 d [3–16]) and 3.2% (10 d [4–18]) had a COVID-19-positive test, and 3.7% (6 d [2–13]) and 2.4% (6 d [2–13]) had an influenza-positive test in English trusts and NHSGGC, respectively. ICU admittance and LoS decreased with age (Tables S8 and S9).

Mean direct medical costs of ARI hospitalization

Over the observational period, mean direct medical cost per ARI ICD-10-diagnosed hospitalization was £3,991.78 (SD: £4,393.73) and £3,596.56 (SD: £6,068.43) in English trusts and NHSGGC, respectively (Table 3); no notable differences were observed across annual cycles (Table S10). In English trusts, RSV-positive hospitalizations cost (£3,843.51 [£3,500.24]) per admission were comparable to the COVID-19-positive (£3,842.30 [£4,699.84]) and influenza-positive (£3,473.90 [£3,640.44]) cohorts. In NHSGGC, costs per RSV-positive (£3,565.80 [£5,593.54]) and influenza-positive (£3,123.54 [£5,068.66]) hospitalization were comparable. COVID-19-positive hospitalizations incurred higher and more variable direct medical costs, at £4,758.00 (£10,512.57) per hospitalization.

Direct medical cost per hospitalization, overall and by sub-cohorts, increased with age. In English trusts, costs among the 18−49 versus ≥90 y groups were £2,980.62 (SD: £4.407.96) versus £5,006.21 (£3,918.17) for RSV-positive hospitalizations; £2,606.01 (5,203.11) versus £4,635.67 (£3,692.50) for COVID-19-positive hospitalizations; and £1,754.91 (£2,445.66) versus £4,619.44 (£3,140.53) for influenza-positive hospitalizations. Similar trends were observed for NHSGGC. Costs were highest among hospitalizations with both lower and upper respiratory tract disease (L/URTD) in English trusts, whilst costs were highest in hospitalizations with LRTD alone in NHSGGC (Table S10).

Microbiology-confirmed RSV versus ICD-10 coding for diagnosis

Between 2018/19 and 2022/23, only 63.5% and 33.7% of microbiology-confirmed RSV-positive hospitalizations had an accompanying RSV-specific ICD-10 diagnosis code in English trusts and NHSGGC, respectively. In English trusts, this proportion was evenly distributed across age and risk groups, whilst in NHSGGC, proportions increased with age (Table S11). Of the hospitalizations with an RSV ICD-10 diagnosis code, 68.6% and 89.1% had an accompanying microbiology-confirmed RSV-positive test in English trusts and NHSGGC, respectively, with proportions increasing slightly with age.

Discussion

This study investigated the population characteristics, incidence risk, and economic burden of ARI-hospitalized adults ≥18 YOA from 2018/19 to 2022/23 in English and Scottish secondary care settings. Findings highlight the comparable burden of RSV-positive hospitalizations to COVID-19- and influenza-positive hospitalizations across both regions, underscoring the importance of effective vaccination strategies to reduce RSV’s burden.6,14 Importantly, these findings provide a pre-vaccine baseline against which the impact of the UK RSV adult vaccination rollout in 2024/25 for those aged 75–79 y can be interpreted. Early post-rollout evaluations reported approximately 30% reductions in hospitalizations, which is consistent with our observation that RSV hospitalization burden in adults, particularly older adults and those with comorbidities, is considerable and, therefore, amenable to meaningful reduction through vaccination.24,25

Incidence risk of first ARI-related hospitalization increased with age in both English trusts and NSHGGC. This aligns with Symes et al., who assessed RSV-ARI hospitalization among those aged ≥65 y in England and reported incidence rising from 30.9 per 100,000 individuals among those aged 65–74 y to 107.9 per 100,000 individuals among those aged ≥80 y.26 In the present analysis, although incidence was highest in the oldest age group, rates increased across successive age bands, indicating a progressive rise in ARI risk throughout older age rather than a concentration only among the oldest. This suggests that limiting RSV vaccination to those aged ≥75 y may overlook a substantial preventable burden in lower age bands.

Annual incidence risk of first ARI-related hospitalization increased from 2020/21 to 2022/23 in English trusts and NHSGGC. This pattern likely reflects a combination of true increases in respiratory virus transmission after relaxation of COVID-19 public health measures (with renewed circulation of multiple pathogens) and ascertainment effects related to changes in care pathways and diagnostics. In particular, the 2020/21 season coincided with periods of reduced circulation of non-COVID-19 respiratory viruses, whereas subsequent seasons were likely characterized by more typical respiratory virus activity following easing of restrictions.27 In parallel, there was likely heightened clinical awareness of respiratory infections and greater use of viral testing and multiplex polymerase chain reaction (PCR) panels among patients presenting with ARI symptoms compared with earlier years, which could increase the likelihood that an admission is coded and captured as ARI-related, independent of any underlying change in severity. A similar spike to that seen in the present analysis was observed in UK surveillance data between 2021 and 2023 following relaxation of COVID-19 public health measures, and increased pathogen testing among patients with ARI symptoms after the COVID-19 pandemic.28,29 Of the three pathogens in this study, RSV was the least frequently tested, accounting for only 13.3% and 26.2% of pathogen-tested ARI-related hospitalizations in English trusts and NHSGGC, respectively.

RSV-positive ARI-related hospitalizations had higher proportions of older adults and adults with comorbidities versus the pathogen-tested and COVID-19- and influenza-positive sub-cohorts, supporting evidence that these populations may have a higher risk of hospitalization following RSV infection.26,30 Of the older adult risk groups examined, there was a higher proportion of RSV-positive ARI-related hospitalizations among high-risk individuals versus non-high-risk individuals in both settings. While these observations could be attributed to different testing practices for each pathogen, literature has similarly reported that older age and comorbidities are significant factors for severe RSV infections, translating to secondary care presentation.31 ICU admissions decreased with age, whilst mortality increased. In line with our study, a UK analysis on ICU intensivist admission decisions reported that older or frail adults may generally be less likely to be admitted to ICU.32 A 2025 report from the European Respiratory Virus Surveillance Summary (ERVISS) similarly revealed that although RSV-related ICU admissions among those ≥60 YOA were low, mortality rates were high.33 Median RSV ICU LoS was shorter in English trusts than in NHSGGC (3 and 11 d, respectively), although IQR’s overlapped. This difference could be attributed to between-setting differences in clinical practice and recording, rather than a true difference in disease course. Limited data on RSV-associated ARI-hospitalized ICU LoS in England and Scotland among older adults are currently available for comparisons to be made.

There is limited-to-no literature exploring the healthcare and economic burden of RSV in the UK. In this study, the economic burden per RSV-positive hospitalization was comparable to influenza- and COVID-19-positive hospitalizations in English trusts, and to influenza-positive hospitalization in NHSGGC. Due to lower RSV testing compared with the other pathogens, and the extent of under-reporting RSV, total economic burden may be underestimated. However, as pathogen testing may be prioritized for patients presenting with significant symptoms, and, therefore, more severe disease, the potential for per-case cost overestimation must also be acknowledged. Direct NHS costs of ARI-related hospitalization generally increased with age. This was expected owing to established higher rates of multimorbidity among older adults, with the UK Health Security Agency reporting increasing hospital and community health service costs with age.34,35

Two-thirds of RSV-positive hospitalizations in NHSGGC did not have an accompanying RSV-specific ICD-10 code, compared with one-third of hospitalizations in English trusts, suggesting potential limitations of using ICD-10 codes to estimate RSV burden. Under-reporting can occur when RSV-positive spells are linked to generic ICD-10 codes and variations in RSV-test sensitivity.18,36 In practice, RSV may be missed in routine discharge coding if the summary focuses on a nonspecific ARI diagnosis, such as ARI itself or pneumonia, or if the RSV test result is not clearly documented or not available at the time of coding. These issues, alongside imperfect test sensitivity, are consistent with evidence that RSV under-ascertainment can be substantial and may be reduced by optimizing sampling/testing approaches. Indeed, studies exploring under-ascertainment of RSV suggested that RSV detection increased when using additional specimen versus nasopharyngeal swabs alone, requiring a ≥2-fold correction factor. A similar level of RSV under-reporting to that observed in our study was also reported in Denmark between 2015 and 2018, where only 58% of adults hospitalized with a positive RSV test received an ICD-10 RSV-specific diagnosis.37 A similar study in the Netherlands reported that the number of microbiology-confirmed RSV-positive hospitalizations was 5.6 times higher than the number of hospitalizations with an RSV-specific ICD-10 code.38

A recently published study by Public Health Scotland highlighted the crucial role of RSV vaccination in broader seasonal immunization programs.39 By improving access to RSV vaccines, RSV burden may be significantly reduced. Additionally, our study contributes to the growing body of evidence that RSV hospitalizations are notably higher among those ≥50 YOA and with comorbidities, supporting broadening current UK RSV vaccination recommendations to include younger at-risk populations. Expanding recommendations should be paired with continuous surveillance of RSV to track health outcomes and vaccine uptake, such as expanded RSV testing protocols or routine linkage of microbiology data and administrative records, aligning with the call of the UK Joint Committee on Vaccination and Immunization (JCVI) for improved surveillance to monitor the impact of RSV vaccination programs.4

This study has several limitations. By using EHR data, this study is subject to the typical limitations associated with real-world data. Consequently, the healthcare and economic burden may be underestimated for two reasons. First, ARI- and comorbidity-related diagnoses were solely identified using ICD-10 codes, meaning some cases may not have been captured and, second, NHS national tariffs and HRG codes do not capture the complete cost of treating a patient (excluded costs for high-cost drugs, rehabilitation centers, laboratory tests, outpatient, or emergency room attendances). In addition, as costs were based on HRG4+ 2022/23 tariffs, inflation and changes in treatment costs during the study period were not accounted for. Further research could also account for patient-level cost drivers, such as specific complications or long-term sequelae, to improve the precision of burden estimates beyond HRG tariffs. The four English trusts and NHSGGC catchment populations represented 3.5% and 21.7% of the adult populations of England and Scotland, respectively, and, therefore, may not be fully representative of the entire national adult population, potentially limiting the generalizability of the absolute incidence risks to the entire UK population. Nevertheless, age, ethnicity, sex, and socioeconomic status distributions closely resembled the national distribution, supporting the use of these data as a reasonable basis for estimating RSV burden in the absence of a national linked database.21,22,40–42 Due to small subgroup numbers, it was not possible to investigate multi-pathogen infections without needing to censor a large number of results. It may be of interest to investigate whether outcomes differ for single- and multi-pathogen infections in future work. Microbiology confirmation is subject to clinician testing behavior. Whilst the majority of tests were multiplex PCR panels and hence tested for all ARIs, some testing may have been clinician-driven and based on clinical presentation, severity, age, comorbidity profile, and infection control considerations. Therefore, the tested population may be enriched for patients with more severe illness or higher clinical risk compared with the full population of RSV-associated admissions. Additionally, the imputation of in-hospital mortality dates may impact the mortality rate. In particular, if only year of death was provided and a patient died during the last month of a calendar year, with an ARI hospitalization being the last recorded date earlier in the calendar year, the mortality rate may be overestimated. However, RSV and influenza in-hospital mortality rates in the present study were lower than 30-d mortality rates published in the literature.26 Finally, the RSV cohort was unvaccinated whilst the COVID-19 and influenza cohorts would have included an undetermined proportion of vaccinated individuals, due to many vaccines for respiratory illnesses being delivered within the community or in primary care. This potentially introduces heterogeneity and confounds comparisons as vaccination has the potential to reduce disease severity and thus, HCRU.

Conclusion

In conclusion, a comprehensive analysis of routine data for RSV burden in English and Scottish secondary care settings between 2018 and 2023 is provided, highlighting that RSV hospitalization imparts a burden comparable to influenza and COVID-19 hospitalization per case. Furthermore, RSV burden is likely underestimated in the UK, due to inconsistent RSV testing and diagnosis. These findings support the need for improved surveillance and for broadened vaccination recommendations in order to manage, monitor, and reduce the substantial healthcare burden of RSV among adults.
