Amplified friction via a cooperative entanglement domains and steric hindrance for damping hydrogels
Authors: Zimo Pang, Sijia Li, Muqing Si, Yujie Chen, Ping He, Chuan Wei Zhang, Chengzhen Du, Xiujun Han, Mingzhi Wei, Ximin He, Hezhou Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDamping hydrogels play a crucial role in dissipating vibrational and impact energy, thereby ensuring material durability. However, the increasingly diverse and complex application scenarios impose different requirements on damping hydrogels, and it remains a challenge to develop hydrogels that exhibit excellent multidimensional damping performance along with robust mechanical properties. Here, we propose a friction-amplification strategy based on synergistic entanglement domains and steric hindrance, which simultaneously increases the frictional resistance to chain sliding, expands the frictional interfaces, and enhances chains interactions. In this design, the entanglement domains act as amplifiers that significantly elevate the energy barriers for chain sliding imposed by steric hindrance. This approach differs from conventional methods that rely solely on increasing the density of binary entanglement points, which offer limited energy dissipation performance. This strategy achieves an order-of-magnitude enhancement in damping capacity (610-fold increase) of hydrogels while maintaining high damping efficiency (96.3%) and considerable loss factor. We further demonstrate that it substantially improves the mechanical properties of hydrogels, exhibits broad universality, and shows promise for impact protection and vibration suppression.Subject terms: Polymers, Gels and hydrogels, Mechanical properties

Introduction

Gel materials hold promise for applications in soft robotics1, electronic skins2, and biomedical engineering3 due to their tunable modulus and high elongation. The damping performance of gels, which plays a critical role in absorbing vibrational and impact energy while ensuring durability, has garnered increasing attention from researchers. Leveraging ionic interactions and solvent-polymer network interactions, numerous ionogels and organogels with excellent energy dissipation have been developed4–7. However, the practical application of ionic liquids and organic solvents is hampered by their significant environmental and biological toxicity8,9. Furthermore, the high cost of ionic liquids impedes their large-scale adoption10. In contrast, hydrogels offer advantages in terms of environmental friendliness and lower cost. Nevertheless, the inherent lubricating effect of water molecules within the polymer network weakens intermolecular interactions, making the development of hydrogels with high damping performance a persistent challenge11.

Currently, hydrogels with damping performance have been developed by leveraging the viscoelastic behavior of polymer chains, which exhibit a high loss factor12. However, a high loss factor does not necessarily indicate strong energy dissipation capability, as it is also constrained by the mechanical properties of the polymer. The inferior mechanical strength of some of them, for example, gelatin-chitosan13, prevents effective energy dissipation under extreme loading conditions (e.g., high-velocity impact). Therefore, excellent mechanical properties, together with a diversified damping evaluation framework—encompassing damping capacity (the absolute dissipated energy), damping efficiency (the ratio of dissipated to input energy), and loss factor (the ratio of viscous to elastic response)—are critical for assessing the overall energy dissipation performance of hydrogels. To enhance the damping performance of gels, researchers have developed a range of strategies, including double-network structures4, electrostatic interactions5, and hydrogen bonding14, phase separation15–17, nanofiller incorporation18,19, slide-ring architectures20, solvent trapping21,22, etc. However, energy dissipation through sacrificial covalent bond cleavage often causes irreversible network failure, while broken non-covalent bonds that fail to reform with their original partners reduce damping efficiency23,24. Certain strategies even enhance damping efficiency at the expense of capacity25. Developing hydrogels that simultaneously offer high damping and robust mechanical performance remains challenging.

Friction represents a key energy dissipation pathway in macroscopic systems, where mechanical energy is converted to thermal energy26. This fundamental principle has now been adapted to the molecular level25. Among various friction-enhancement strategies, chain entanglement has emerged as a promising approach27. Unlike filler incorporation, it avoids introducing heterogeneous components that compromise material homogeneity, and unlike phase-separation-induced friction, which may lead to progressive sacrifice of phase structure, gradual efficiency deterioration28. As demonstrated by Suo et al.29, when subjected to external forces, the relaxed chain will extend, causing polymer chains to slide against one another at the entanglement points, thereby dissipating the input energy through frictional interactions. Moreover, entanglement points facilitate the long-range transmission of tensile forces across multiple chains, simultaneously enhancing toughness in the polymer. However, current research on entanglements remains largely confined to increasing binary entanglement points between pairs of chains. Polymers designed through this approach typically exhibit small hysteresis loops with limited damping capacity and efficiency29–33.

A smooth shoelace with a single knot becomes easily untied while a rough shoelace secured with multiple knots requires more force to release (Fig. 1a). This phenomenon finds its explanation in Euler’s rope theory: the friction between ropes is enhanced by a higher coefficient of friction, an increased contact area, and a greater normal pressure induced by their topological configuration34. Inspired by this principle, we propose a chain friction amplification strategy. This strategy leverages the synergistic interplay between steric hindrance and dense entanglement domains to achieve substantial improvements in frictional dissipation. “Rough chains” are defined as chains whose mobility is impeded by significant energy barriers. The entanglement domains proposed in this strategy are localized regions characterized by high chain density and topological complexity surpassing conventional entanglement points. These domains provide an extensive contact interface, while the enhanced normal interaction originates from their geometric interlocking. The introduction of chains with impaired movement is functionally analogous to increasing the friction coefficient. When the polymer deforms under stress, friction occurs between interacting chains and generates heat (Fig. 1b, c). The obstructive interfaces ensure the initiation of friction, while the entanglement domains geometrically amplify this frictional effect (Fig. 1d). This is because, if each idealized domain contains n interacting chains, the number of frictional interfaces scales as n(n−1)2∝n2. Consequently, friction increases geometrically with domain compactness. This enhanced frictional force empowers the hydrogels with greater energy dissipation performance, enabling the simultaneous improvement of both damping capacity and efficiency.

In this study, methacrylatoethyl trimethyl ammonium chloride (MOTAC) serves as the fundamental building block for constructing the friction-enhanced hydrogels. The intrinsic α-methyl group of MOTAC introduces additional steric hindrance during chain sliding, thereby functioning as a molecular-scale “roughening agent”. Poly-(MOTAC) without dense entanglement domains (PMWODE) was synthesized to isolate the contribution of dense entanglement domains to the friction reinforcement behavior. The factor of steric hindrance was further probed by synthesizing a hydrogel using the less-hindered monomer (2-(acryloyloxy)ethyl)trimethylammonium chloride (AETC) as a comparison. Unlike simply increasing the entanglement points, our findings reveal a synergistic amplification of friction arising from the combination of dense entanglement domains and steric hindrance. The optimized PMOTAC sample achieved considerable performance: a high damping efficiency (96.3%), an orders-of-magnitude enhancement in damping capacity (~610 times greater), and significantly improved mechanical properties. Furthermore, it demonstrated outstanding potential for impact protection and vibration suppression. Finally, we have also verified the universality of our strategy.

Results

We demonstrate our design using two polyelectrolyte monomers (Supplementary Fig. 1). The fabrication of the friction-reinforced hydrogels begins with MOTAC, a monomer featuring a charged hydrophilic head and a hydrophobic alkyl tail. During the early stages of polymerization in a concentrated solution, MOTAC molecules aggregate, forming precursors for the subsequent dense entanglement domains (Fig. 2a). Upon completion of polymerization, these larger dense domains become interconnected by smaller domains and polymer chains, forming the friction-reinforced network. To deconvolute the specific contribution of these dense domains, a control sample, PMWODE, was ingeniously designed (Fig. 2b). This was achieved by polymerizing a MOTAC solution at a lower initial concentration (50 wt%), resulting in a more chain-like conformation rather than clustered domains. Furthermore, the PMOTAC-50 wt% gel was annealed to match the solid content of PMOTAC-30 wt% (detailed procedures are provided in the “Methods” section; unless otherwise stated, the water content of all hydrogels discussed in this study is 30 wt%). As the solvent evaporated, enhanced chain interactions increased the number of ordinary binary entanglements without forming dense domains35. To probe the synergy between domain-like entanglements and steric hindrance, a smooth monomer, AETC, was polymerized at a specific solid content. After undergoing a similar domain-formation process, the PAETC-30 wt% hydrogel with smooth entanglement domains was obtained (Fig. 2c). Due to the water-retention capability of the ionic functional groups, all three hydrogels maintained their water equilibrium at room temperature (Supplementary Fig. 2).

The AFM images obtained under freeze-dried conditions captured the chain conformation behavior of the three polyelectrolytes (Fig. 3a). PMOTAC features rigid, densely packed entanglement domains built from smaller entangled clusters. These rigid domains are interconnected by smaller clusters. In contrast, PMWODE shows a point-entanglement conformation without domains. PAETC, which lacks the steric hindrance units, shows smaller-scale domains interconnected by chains just like pearl-necklace, primarily due to the absence of the hydrophobic α-methyl group. Cryo‑scanning electron microscopy (Cryo‑SEM) further confirms the distinct structural conformation of the three polyelectrolytes (Supplementary Fig. 3): PMOTAC exhibited large domains, PAETC displayed smaller domains, while PMWODE showed almost no domain structures. This result aligns with the observations from small-angle X-ray diffraction (SAXS) (Supplementary Fig. 4). To verify the role of entanglement domains in PMOTAC, we conducted rheological characterizations (Supplementary Fig. 5). The results show that as frequency decreases, the storage modulus (G′) of both PMOTAC and PAETC exhibits a plateau in the range of 1–0.1 rad/s, indicating that their mechanical response is dominated by entanglement domain interactions without dissociation36,37. In contrast, the G′ of PMWODE rapidly decays with decreasing frequency, suggesting weak entanglement effects that fail to maintain the structure. Spin-spin relaxation time (T2) distributions from low-field 1H NMR spectra were used to probe the mobility of hydrogen-containing components within the gels (Supplementary Fig. 6). A higher T2 value indicates greater mobility of the corresponding species38,39. PMOTAC shows the highest proportion of rigid components, whereas PMWODE, lacking dense domains, exhibits the highest polymer chain mobility. PAETC falls between the two, constrained by its smoother domains. This explains PMOTAC’s high modulus, enabling it to resist deformation under gravity (Fig. 3b). The reinforced hydrogel also exhibits a stronger water-restricted capability, resulting in a higher proportion of bound water in PMOTAC. FTIR spectroscopy shows a bending vibration peak (H-O-H) for liquid water at ~1636 cm−1, with PMWODE having the most free water (Supplementary Fig. 7). The two hydrogels with steric hindrance show a redshifted C=O stretching vibration peak, reflecting the role of the α-methyl group in stabilizing hydrogen bonds14. Furthermore, the entanglement domains effectively resist erosion by external water, whereas a hydrogel with only ordinary entanglements cannot prevent gel disintegration (Supplementary Fig. 8).

A central question is how these distinct structures affect frictional dissipation, which can be presented by the hysteresis loops in cyclic tensile tests (Fig. 3c). The PMOTAC sample exhibited spindle-shaped hysteresis loops with a plump area, indicating greater energy dissipation. While PMWODE also showed a spindle shape, its stress level was significantly lower. In stark contrast, the hysteresis loop for PAETC was severely pinched, indicating minimal energy loss. The damping efficiency was quantified as the ratio of the dissipated energy to the stored energy, and damping capacity was calculated as the integrated area of the hysteresis loop. The damping efficiency for PMOTAC reached 96.3 ± 1.9% in the first cycle, with a low decay rate of only 0.9% per cycle over five cycles. In comparison, the initial damping efficiency for PMWODE and PAETC were 93.5 ± 0.6% and 13.2 ± 0.7%, with higher decay rates of 1.3% and 5.4%, respectively. Most strikingly, the damping capacity of PMOTAC reached 603.6 ± 8.2 kJ m−3 in the first cycle, which is 24 times and 610 times greater than that of PMWODE and PAETC, respectively. The Carr-Purcell-Meiboom-Gill (CPMG) sequence of T2 relaxation curves provided deeper insights into the frictional at the physical cross-links (Supplementary Fig. 9)40. PMOTAC-30 wt% exhibited the fastest decay rate, indicating that the motion of polymer chains faced more restriction and resistance. The performance comparison between PMOTAC and PAETC highlights the crucial role of steric hindrance in governing frictional interactions. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations further corroborate this, as evidenced by the radial distribution function (RDF) derived from the equilibrated trajectory (Supplementary Figs. 10 and 11). The shorter interatomic distances and higher atomic density observed for PMOTAC indicate the presence of steric hindrance effects. Meanwhile, the contrast between PMOTAC and PMWODE demonstrates the significant amplification effect provided by entanglement domains. Based on these results, we conclude that: (1) the frictional barrier caused by steric hindrance is the fundamental requirement for high damping efficiency, not the entanglement domains; (2) the dense entanglement domains act as amplifiers for friction; (3) the synergy between dense domains and steric hindrance enables simultaneous high damping efficiency and capacity.

In addition to dramatically improving the energy dissipation of PMOTAC, the friction-reinforcement strategy also led to a significant enhancement in its overall mechanical properties (Fig. 3d), including maximum stress, toughness, puncture resistance, puncture energy, fracture energy, Young’s modulus, and compression stress (Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Figs. 12 and 13). The presented friction-amplification approach outperforms existing strategies such as supramolecular networks41, phase separation15, and double-network4, sliding ring-chain friction20, solvent clogging21 and ion-dipole strategy5 in achieving concurrent enhanced effect in damping efficiency and capacity (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Table 2).

The toughening behavior of PMOTAC primarily arises from the following effects (Fig. 4a): under applied stress, the chains progressively extend. When the relaxed chains become fully taut, they begin to slide past one another within the domains, leading to the release of initially stored chain segments. This gives PMOTAC a high elongation at stress. The presence of steric hindrance generates significant frictional dissipation during this relative sliding. Within the domains, the collective enhancement of the contact interface, friction barrier, and interaction results in a multiplicative increase in frictional energy dissipation. This mechanism was directly confirmed by in situ tensile SAXS (Fig. 4b). The azimuthally integrated 1D SAXS profile revealed a broad peak (q-range: 0.28–0.52 A−1), corresponding to an inter-domains distance of 1.21–2.24 nm42. As the strain increased, the peak intensity decreased and the d-spacing reduced from 1.45 nm to 1.41 nm, signifying network extension and the force-triggered release of chains from the domains and clusters. When chemical cross‑linking is introduced, chain release in PMOTAC becomes hindered by the presence of cross‑linkers, resulting in lower elongation (Supplementary Fig. 14).

The formation of the reinforced architecture is governed by a combination of intermolecular forces. According to the Derjaguin-Landau-Verwey-Overbeek (DLVO) theory, which describes the behavior of charged colloids in solution, the system is influenced by both van der Waals attractions and electrostatic repulsions43. In the case of PMOTAC, the hydrophobic alkyl chains introduce an additional hydrophobic effect42. These three interactions create a superimposed force field (Supplementary Fig. 15): van der Waals forces promote the densification of interacting particles, electrostatic repulsion extends the domain-cluster-domain structure, and hydrophobic interactions drive the aggregation of polyelectrolytes. The solution concentration also influences the balance between the hydrophobic effect and electrostatic repulsion. In concentrated solutions, hydrophobic dominance promotes aggregation, whereas dilution enhances electrostatic dominance, leading to different PMOTAC conformations (Supplementary Fig. 16). Theoretical simulations further corroborate this mechanism. Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) determined the primary molecular weight of PMOTAC to be ~3400 Da, corresponding to a 16-mer (Supplementary Fig. 17). MD simulations revealed that a single PMOTAC 16-mer adopts a collapsed, cluster-like conformation in a 20 wt% solvent environment (Supplementary Fig. 18). As the solvent content increases, the chain gradually extends. This observation directly explains the absence of large-scale dense entanglement domains in the PMWODE sample, which was synthesized from a more dilute solution.

The network architecture, controlled by concentration, governs the friction reinforcement and mechanical properties. A higher precursor concentration generally leads to larger aggregates, which intensify frictional constraints on chain mobility (Supplementary Fig. 19). As the concentration decreases, PMOTAC gels undergo a distinct transition from a glassy to rubbery to viscous flow state (Supplementary Fig. 20). This transition is accompanied by a gradual decrease in Young’s modulus and a non-monotonic change in toughness, which first increases and then decreases (Supplementary Fig. 21). Lower concentrations also promote the emergence of multiple components with distinct rigidities and enhanced mobility (Supplementary Fig. 22). This heterogeneity arises from non-uniform hydration: dense entanglement domains are poorly hydrated, while smaller clusters and individual chains become better hydrated. This conclusion is supported by Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations (Supplementary Fig. 23) and is corroborated by FTIR spectroscopy, where the liquid water peak and the alkyl chain vibrations resolve into multiple features, indicating varied aqueous environments (Supplementary Figs. 24 and 25). An optimal density of entanglement domains enhances friction while still permitting chain slippage and energy dissipation, as opposed to fracture caused by chains being permanently locked within oversized clusters. Therefore, PMOTAC, with its balanced structure, achieves an exceptional toughness of 23.78 MJ m−3, sufficient to support the weight of an adult (Fig. 4c). This combination of extreme stretchability and toughness makes PMOTAC greater than most reported gels (Fig. 4d)29,30,33,38,39,42,44–49.

PMOTAC exhibits exceptional crack resistance, underpinned by a dual mechanism. On one hand, the dense entanglement domains act as reinforcing particles that impede crack propagation. On the other hand, the surrounding domains, upon sensing tensile stress, relax by releasing stored chain segments. The stress is distributed over multiple domains and chains via long-range transfer, with energy being dissipated through chain slippage and friction, thereby alleviating stress concentration (Fig. 4e). Under quasi-static tensile testing (10 mm min−1), a notched PMOTAC sample achieved a strain elongation of nearly 1500% (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Fig. 26). Crucially, upon reaching the critical stress, crack propagation is not instantaneous and catastrophic; this delaying effect becomes more pronounced at higher strain rates (Supplementary Fig. 27). The material’s toughness is further demonstrated by its puncture resistance. At a rate of 100 mm min−1, PMOTAC achieved an impressive balance of a 40.5 ± 4.1 N puncture force and a 33.5 ± 3.0 mm puncture distance, resulting in a puncture energy of 848.9 ± 76.9 mJ (Fig. 4g). The puncture force and puncture energy outperform those of various elastomers (Supplementary Fig. 28). This performance was significantly enhanced at faster puncture rates, highlighting its capacity to withstand high-speed impacts. The key to this robust mechanical behavior lies in the force-triggered unraveling of the dense entanglement domains, which releases chains and generates extensive friction.

The tolerance of PMOTAC to high-speed penetration suggests that it likely exhibits impact-enhanced properties. Its rate-dependent hardening and strengthening behavior, observed in tensile tests at different strain rates (Supplementary Fig. 29), can be attributed to the dynamics of friction during the force-triggered release of chains from the entanglement domains. The relationship between yield stress and the natural logarithm of strain rate follows a nearly linear trend (Supplementary Fig. 30), consistent with the Eyring model for force-induced dissociation of non-covalent bonds50,51.

To probe the damping capabilities of PMOTAC for impact protection applications, dynamic impact forces were recorded using a high-resolution force sensor (Fig. 5d). Upon impact from a 20 g steel ball dropped from 30 cm, the PMOTAC exhibited significantly greater performance compared to the control hydrogels, characterized by rapid stress wave attenuation, a reduced peak force, and effective vibration damping (Fig. 5e, f).

To demonstrate impact protection capability, an egg wrapped with a 2 mm thick PMOTAC hydrogel was dropped from a height of 60 cm (Supplementary Movie 1). High-speed camera footage revealed that the egg remained intact with only minimal rebound upon impact (Fig. 5g). In contrast, eggs without hydrogel protection or those protected by PAETC shattered instantly upon impact (Supplementary Fig. 31).

To further illustrate its potential for damping applications in shock absorption scenarios, we monitored the time-resolved acceleration oscillations induced by a free-falling object (20 g) (Fig. 5h and Supplementary Movie 2). The object was tethered to a 2 mm diameter, 10 cm long PMOTAC hydrogel fiber (detailed procedures for the synthesis of hydrogel fibers are provided in the supporting information), which effectively suppressed vibrations immediately upon impact (~0.15 s). Under the same conditions, an object tethered to PAETC exhibited harmonic-like oscillations (~9.82 s), as visually confirmed in optical images (Fig. 5i). This phenomenon is attributed to the fact that, when the network is stretched, the PMOTAC fibers dissipate the kinetic energy of the weight through frictional interactions within the sterically hindered molecular network. In contrast, the PAETC fibers, lacking such steric hindrance, can only temporarily store the excess energy as elastic potential energy. Dynamic mechanical analysis (DMA) provides evidence for the damping behavior of the hydrogel from the perspective of its macroscopic viscoelastic properties. Across frequency ranges most relevant to practical applications (0.1–100 Hz), the hydrogel demonstrates significant damping capacity (tan δ > 0.8) (Supplementary Fig. 32). At moderate water content, the PMOTAC hydrogel achieves a loss factor of up to 1.13, highlighting its potential for energy dissipation.

The generalizability of our friction-reinforcement strategy was confirmed by synthesizing hydrogels from various monomers, including methacrylamido propyl trimethyl ammonium chloride (MPTAC), (3-acrylamidopropyl)trimethylammonium chloride (ATLC), sulfopropylmethacrylate potassium (SMAP), and 3-sulfopropylacrylate potassium (SAP) (Fig. 6a). The rationale behind monomer selection involved choosing electrolyte monomers that possess both hydrophobic alkyl segments and hydrophilic electrostatic functional groups. To distinguish the effect of steric hindrance, the inclusion of an α-methyl group was necessary for comparison. The cyclic tensile curves of the resulting hydrogels exhibited similar shapes, confirming the general feasibility of this mechanism across different polyelectrolytes (Fig. 6b). Governed by the synergistic effect of dense entanglement domains and steric hindrance, these friction-reinforced gels also achieved a significantly elevated modulus, a multiplicative increase in frictional dissipation, and a concurrent enhancement in both damping efficiency and capacity (Fig. 6c, d).

Discussion

In summary, we have developed a friction-reinforcement strategy that enables a geometric amplification of interchain friction, leading to a dramatic improvement in the damping performance of hydrogels. This strategy relies on the synergistic cooperation between densely entangled domains and steric hindrance, and we highlight the indispensable role of both elements in achieving the coveted combination of high damping efficiency and damping capacity. Unlike the previously proposed binary entanglement junction hydrogels, entanglement domains significantly enhance chain contact interactions within confined spatial regions. While solely introducing steric hindrance increases the frictional barrier, the lack of dense entanglement domains results in scarce and weak contact points, leading to low overall energy dissipation. Conversely, dense domains without steric hindrance offer poor sliding resistance and thus low dissipation efficiency. This demonstrates that the two factors operate through a synergistic “A×B” relationship, rather than a simple additive effect. And the resultant enhancement surpasses the sum of the individual contributions from each strategy alone. Through this synergy, frictional dissipation can yield an order-of-magnitude increase in damping capacity. Furthermore, the domain-based architecture plays a critical role in reinforcement and in releasing stored chains to mitigate stress concentration, endowing the hydrogel with exceptional toughness, a high modulus, crack tolerance, and puncture resistance. These properties make it ideally suited for load-bearing structural components. Impact and vibration tests across multiple scenarios demonstrate its efficiency to significantly attenuate impact forces, protect fragile objects, and rapidly suppress vibrations. The generality of this strategy has been validated. Further systematic studies to explore composite systems by integrating this architecture with other hydrogels and investigating their resultant mechanical properties will be the scope of future work. This combination of attributes holds promise for developing ultra-strong damping hydrogels.

Methods

Materials

Methacryloxyethyltrimethyl ammonium chloride (MOTAC), (2-(acryloyloxy)ethyl)trimethyl ammonium chloride (AETC), and (3-acrylamidopropyl)trimethyl ammonium chloride (ATLC) were purchased from Aladdin. Methacrylamido propyl trimethyl ammonium chloride (MPTAC) was purchased from Macklin. Sulfopropylmethacrylate potassium (SMAP) and 3-sulfopropylacrylate potassium (SAP) were purchased from Adamas. 2-hydroxy-2-methylpropiophenone (HMPP) was purchased from Bidepharm as Irgacure.

Preparation of PMOTAC

The mixture (MOTAC-30 wt% solution) of 20 mmol MOTAC (4154 mg), 1780 mg deionized water, and 10 μl HMPP solution was stirred for 3 h to obtain a uniformly dispersed solution. In particular, the HMPP solution is a 40 μl ml−1 pre-prepared ethanol solution. To fabricate the PMOTAC-30 wt% hydrogels, MOTAC-30 wt% solution was degassed and transferred into a mold, and then exposed to UV light (320–400 nm UV lamp, 36 W, 10 cm distance) for 10 min at room temperature (RT) of 25 ± 3 °C and relative humidity (RH) of 30 ± 10%.

Preparation of PMWODE

The mixture (MOTAC-50 wt% solution) of 20 mmol MOTAC (4154 mg), 4154 mg deionized water, and 10 μl HMPP solution was stirred for 3 h to obtain a uniformly dispersed solution. To fabricate the PMOTAC-50 wt% hydrogels, MOTAC-50 wt% solution was degassed and transferred into a mold, and then exposed to UV light for 10 min. The PMOTAC-50 wt% was then subjected to annealing at 50 °C and weighed in real time until the solid content of the gel reached 70 wt%. The PMWODE-30 wt% hydrogel was obtained.

Preparation of PAETC, PMPTAC, PATLC, PSMAP, and PSAP

These hydrogels were synthesized following a procedure similar to that of PMOTAC. Briefly, the mixture of 20 mmol monomer, specific volume of deionized water (30 wt%), and 10 mL of HMPP solution was stirred for 3 h to obtain a uniformly dispersed solution. The resulting mixture was then polymerized via photopolymerization.

Preparation of PMPWODE and PSWODE

These hydrogels were synthesized following a procedure similar to that of PMWODE. Briefly, the mixture of 20 mmol monomer, specific volume of deionized water (50 wt%), and 10 mL of HMPP solution was stirred for 3 h to obtain a uniformly dispersed solution. The resulting mixture was then polymerized via photopolymerization to synthesize PMPTAC-50 wt% or PSMAP-50 wt%, and then subjected to annealing at 50 °C and weighed in real time until the solid content of the gel reached 70 wt%. The PMPWODE or PSWODE was obtained.

Preparation of PMOTAC with cross-links

The synthesis procedure for these hydrogels was similar to that of PMOTAC, except that 0.01 mmol of crosslinker (PEGDA, M.W. ~ 400) was additionally added to the MOTAC-30 wt% precursor solution.

Atomic force microscope (AFM)

Mica sheets were attached to the glass slides with double-sided adhesive tape, and then the top layer of mica was removed with frosted adhesive to ensure a clean and flat surface. Then, using a pipette to take 10 μl of the gel solution (1 mg ml−1) and drop it on the mica sheet. The mica sheet was rapidly transferred into liquid nitrogen to fix its shape, followed by freeze-drying once the temperature had stabilized. Once the water had completely sublimated, an AFM image was taken on Bruker Dimension XR with scanasyst air probes (AC240TS, resonant frequency 70 kHz, spring constant 1.7 N/m).

Cryo‑scanning electron microscopy (cryo‑SEM)

To preserve the structure of the sample, it was rapidly immersed in supercooled liquid nitrogen for cryo-fixation. After the temperature of the specimen stabilized, it was transferred to the cryo-transfer system chamber (Quorum PP3010, −140 °C, vacuum ≤5 × 10−6 mbar) for cryo-fracturing: a pre-cooled blade was used to perform brittle fracture on the specimen, exposing its internal structure. Subsequently, sublimation was carried out at −85 °C for 6 min. The treated specimen was then transferred to the cryo-SEM observation chamber (ZEISS Gemini300, −140 °C) for high-resolution imaging under accelerating voltages of 2 kV, in combination with a secondary electron detector.

Rheology test

The linear dynamic viscoelasticity of the samples was characterized using a rotational rheometer (DHR-20, TA) equipped with a 20-mm-diameter stainless steel parallel plate. For testing, the hydrogel was prepared as a disc with a diameter of 20 mm and a thickness of 1 mm. Frequency sweeps were performed from 100 to 0.1 rad/s at 25 °C under a constant linear strain of 0.1%. Prior to all measurements, a strain amplitude sweep was conducted to determine the linear viscoelastic region, ensure no sample slip occurred, and confirm the deformation remained within the linear regime. We employed a Dynamic Mechanical Analyzer (DMA850, TA) to investigate the dynamic behavior of PMOTAC at varying water contents. The measurements were conducted in tensile mode by sweeping the frequency from 100 Hz to 0.1 Hz to characterize the loss behavior at 25 °C.

Small-angle x-ray scattering (SAXS)

SAXS experiments were performed at the Xeuss 2.0, and a Pilatus 3 R 300 K detector was used. The X-ray wavelength was 1.54 Å. Samples were measured perpendicular to the beam with the sample-detector distance of 539 mm to cover the scattering vector q range from 0.162 to 5.6 nm-1 (q is the scattering vector;q=4πsinθλ; 2θ is the scattering angle). The scattering patterns were obtained with a short exposure time of 300 s. SAXS patterns were radially averaged to obtain the intensity profiles.

Attenuated total reflection-Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (ATR-FTIR)

ATR-FTIR spectra were measured with Nicolet 6700 FTIR (Thermo Fisher) from 500 to 4000 cm−1. The thickness of the hydrogels was 1 mm. The probe of the infrared spectrometer was pressed onto the surface of the hydrogels, and the FTIR spectrum was measured.

Density functional theory (DFT)

In this study, the optimization of geometry calculations was performed using DFT within the Gaussian 16 package, employing the B3LYP (dispersion correction: GD3BJ) functional with a 6-311 G* basis set. Vibrational modes with imaginary frequencies were absent, confirmed for all stable stationary points. To elucidate the thermodynamic characteristics of heterogeneous hydration in polycationic systems, we conducted a computational investigation of the internal energy profiles for two PMOTAC dimers under asymmetric water distribution conditions. The simulations were performed at a well-defined monomer-to-water molar ratio of 2:10, which was selected to closely mirror the experimentally determined hydration state of our PMOTAC-30 wt% hydrogel system.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation

The initial simulation box was constructed by immersing a 16-mer PMOTAC polymer chain consisting of 485 atoms into a solvent containing water molecules. The structure of 16-mer PMOTAC was fully relaxed using the Gaussian 16 software package with DFT, employing the B3LYP functional and the 6-31 G(d) basis set, along with the GD3BJ empirical dispersion correction. MD simulations were performed using Gromacs (version 2020.6) with the OPLS-AA force field and CM5 atomic charges, while water molecules were described using the simple point charge (SPC) model. Following energy minimization of the system, a 0.5-nanosecond isothermal-isochoric (NVT) simulation was conducted. During the simulation, a time step of 0.5 femtoseconds was employed, and Newton’s equations of motion were integrated using the leapfrog algorithm. Temperature was maintained at room temperature using the stochastic velocity rescaling algorithm, with a coupling time of 0.1 picoseconds. Electrostatic interactions were treated using the smooth particle mesh Ewald method, with a direct space cutoff of 1.2 nm. The cutoff for van der Waals interactions was also set to 1.2 nm. RDF is calculated using the Forcite module in Materials Studio, ver. 2023.

Tensile tests

All mechanical property tests were conducted on an electronic tensile machine LD23 503 (LISHI instrument) at RT of 25 ± 3 °C and RH of 30 ± 10%. To prevent the shoulders from being stretched, the ends of the dumbbell-shaped specimens were completely clamped. Furthermore, sandpaper was utilized to prevent slippage between the samples and the clamps. Unless otherwise stated, all tensile tests were conducted at a loading rate of 100 mm min−1. Cyclic tensile stress-strain curves with an initial strain amplitude of 100%, which was subsequently increased by 20% in each cycle, were also constructed at loading and unloading rates of 100 mm min−1. The elastic moduli were calculated from the initial slope of the stress–strain curves. Each mechanical test was repeated with at least three individual samples. The true stress was calculated by nominal stress × (strain + 100%) based on the assumption that the volume of the material remains constant throughout the deformation process.

To quantitatively assess the impact energy absorption capacity of the synthesized hydrogels, a standardized drop ball test was performed.
