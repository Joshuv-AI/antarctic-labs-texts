Therapeutic Modulation of Inflammatory Signaling via Physalis Peruviana‐Derived Exosomes: A Targeted Effect on IKK/NFκB/STAT1
Authors: Cristian Martínez Fajardo, Lucía Morote, Susana López‐López, Alberto José López Jiménez, Oussama Ahrazem, Lourdes Gómez‐Gómez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT Physalis peruviana is a carotenoid‐rich fruit widely recognized for its nutritional and medicinal value, yet the molecular mechanisms underlying its bioactivity remain incompletely understood. Plant‐derived extracellular vesicles have recently emerged as functional nanocarriers of bioactive metabolites; however, these vesicles from P. peruviana have not been systematically investigated. We isolated and characterized exosomes from P. peruviana fruits and evaluated their anti‐inflammatory and antioxidant activities in vitro. Vesicles were isolated and characterized using transmission electron microscopy, nanoparticle tracking analysis, protein and RNA profiling, thin‐layer chromatography, and untargeted metabolomics. The vesicles exhibited characteristic exosomal morphology with a mean diameter of ∼142 nm and contained proteins, miRNAs, lipids, and β‐carotene as a predominant metabolite. Functional assays revealed that, at non‐cytotoxic concentrations (25 µg/mL), they significantly suppressed IL‐6, TNF‐α, IFN‐β, iNOS, and STAT1 expression in LPS‐stimulated RAW 264.7 macrophages, accompanied by reduced IKK phosphorylation and inhibition of NF‐κB and STAT1 transcriptional activity. In H2O2‐challenged NIH3T3 fibroblasts, exosome treatment enhanced cell viability and upregulated endogenous antioxidant and mitochondrial defense markers, including CAT, SOD2, GPx4, and cytochrome c oxidase. These findings identify P. peruviana–derived exosomes as carotenoid‐rich nanocarriers with anti‐inflammatory and antioxidant properties, providing new mechanistic insight into the molecular actions of carotenoid‐containing foods.

Introduction

Physalis peruviana L., commonly known as goldenberry or Cape gooseberry, is a fruit‐bearing plant belonging to the Solanaceae family, native to the Andean region of South America. Over the last decades, it has gained increasing attention as a functional food with unique organoleptic properties, and as a valuable source of bioactive phytochemicals with potential therapeutic applications [1, 2]. Its fruit is small, orange‐colored, encased in a distinctive calyx resembling a paper lantern, and is appreciated for its sweet and sour flavor and nutritional richness. Traditionally used in folk medicine in Colombia, Ecuador, and Peru, P. peruviana has been credited with antipyretic, anti‐inflammatory, diuretic, and detoxifying effects, although its scientific validation is still under development [3, 4].

In recent years, a growing body of literature has focused on the phytochemical composition of goldenberry fruits, highlighting their high content of essential vitamins (such as A, C, and E), minerals (notably potassium and iron), dietary fiber, and a diverse set of secondary metabolites, including alkaloids, flavonoids, phenolic acids, carotenoids, and terpenoids [1, 5, 6]. Carotenoids are lipophilic pigments responsible for the yellow, orange, and red coloration of many fruits and vegetables. Among these compounds, carotenoids and their oxidative derivatives, apocarotenoids, have attracted increasing research interest due to their multiple biological functions and therapeutic properties, particularly in relation to oxidative stress and inflammation‐related diseases [7, 8]. In P. peruviana, the predominant carotenoids identified include β‐carotene, lutein, and zeaxanthin, which function not only as colorants but also as modulators of immune function and oxidative balance [9, 10, 11, 12]. Studies have reported total carotenoid concentrations in goldenberry juice reaching up to 4134 µg/100 g dry weight [12]. Beyond their nutritional value, carotenoids have been shown to exhibit antioxidant, anti‐inflammatory, anticarcinogenic, and immunomodulatory effects. For instance, β‐carotene and lycopene have been found to suppress the expression of pro‐inflammatory mediators such as iNOS and COX‐2 by inhibiting the NF‐κB signaling pathway, a central regulator of inflammation [13, 14]. Furthermore, carotenoids can stimulate T‐cell proliferation and modulate the release of cytokines such as IL‐1β and IL‐6, indicating their potential for immune system regulation [15]. Additionally, they mitigate oxidative stress by neutralizing reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby protecting cell membranes from lipid peroxidation and reducing apoptotic signaling in stressed cells [16]. Despite this growing interest, carotenoids are structurally unstable molecules that are highly susceptible to oxidative degradation, isomerization, and loss of bioactivity during gastrointestinal transit. These limitations pose significant challenges for their effective use in nutritional and clinical applications. Consequently, the development of strategies to improve their solubility, stability, protection, and targeted delivery has become a major focus in nutraceutical and pharmacological research [17]. In this context, nanotechnology‐based strategies have emerged as promising approaches to enhance the bioavailability and functionality of plant‐derived bioactives. Among these, plant‐derived exosomes, a subset of extracellular nanovesicles ranging from 30 to 150 nm, have recently garnered interest as natural, biocompatible, and bioactive delivery vehicles [18, 19]. Traditionally studied in the context of mammalian cell communication, exosomes are now recognized as ubiquitous mediators in plant systems, capable of transporting lipids, proteins, RNAs, and small metabolites, including flavonoids, polyphenols, and carotenoids [20, 21].

Growing evidence indicates that plant‐derived exosomes function as carriers of bioactive molecules capable of modulating mammalian cellular processes. These vesicles can be internalized by mammalian cells while retaining their biological activity. For instance, turmeric‐derived exosomes have shown anti‐inflammatory effects in murine models of colitis by reducing TNF‐α, IL‐6, and IL‐1β levels [22]. Likewise, exosomes from tea leaves decreased the secretion of pro‐inflammatory cytokines in activated macrophages while increasing the anti‐inflammatory cytokine IL‐1 [23]. Apple‐derived exosomes were also reported to inhibit NF‐κB signaling and IL‐1β mRNA expression in early inflammatory responses [24]. Beyond their role in regulating inflammatory pathways, plant‐derived exosomes have also demonstrated considerable potential in mitigating oxidative stress. For example, blueberry‐derived exosomes have been shown to reduce intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, enhance mitochondrial membrane potential, and protect cultured cells from apoptosis. These findings highlight the capacity of plant‐derived extracellular vesicles to modulate key cellular pathways associated with inflammation and oxidative damage. [25]. Moreover, a complex mixture of fruit‐derived exosomes administered orally to mice significantly decreased ROS levels in models of hydrogen peroxide‐induced oxidative stress [26]. Exosomes from Phellinus linteus, a medicinal fungus, have been shown to confer cytoprotection against UV‐induced photodamage in skin cells by attenuating the expression of matrix metalloproteinases and upregulating the activity of key antioxidant enzymes, thereby reinforcing their potential role in anti‐aging and oxidative stress mitigation [27].

Despite the increasing interest in plant‐derived exosomes as therapeutic carriers [28], there is a significant knowledge gap regarding the specific composition, bioactivity, and mechanisms of action of exosomes derived from P. peruviana. Preliminary work has suggested anti‐inflammatory potential of exosomes isolated from this species [29, 30, 31], but detailed studies are lacking, particularly in relation to carotenoid content, in vitro efficacy, and immunomodulatory pathways.

The primary objective of this study was to isolate and comprehensively characterize exosomes derived from P. peruviana fruits, with particular emphasis on their molecular cargo. We aimed to elucidate the role of these exosomes as bioactive carriers of carotenoids and other phytochemicals, and to assess their potential in modulating oxidative stress and inflammatory responses using an in vitro model of murine macrophages stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS).

Materials and Methods

Isolation of Extracellular Vesicles From the Goldenberry (P. peruviana)

Ripe fruits of P. peruviana were used for exosome isolation. The fruits were purchased from a local commercial supplier. The fruits were disinfected and thoroughly rinsed with sterile deionized water. Subsequently, the fruits were weighed and manually cut into smaller pieces, the same amount of saline solution (1xPBS) was added and processed with a potato masher until a homogeneous mixture was obtained. The mixture was then stirred for 20 min at room temperature to encourage the release of exosomes into the aqueous phase. The mixture was then passed through gauze to obtain the clarified juice. To remove residual debris, the juice was centrifuged at 2000 g for 20 min at 4°C, and the supernatant obtained was centrifuged at 5000 g for 40 min at 4°C, and the same procedure was repeated at 10 000 g for 60 min at 4°C. The supernatant resulting from these centrifugations was collected. The final clarification step consisted of ultracentrifugation at 180 000 g for 90 min at 4°C. The pellet obtained after ultracentrifugation was washed with 1x PBS and then resuspended in 1xPBS. Total protein content was quantified using the PierceTM BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermofisher scientific, Madrid, Spain) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The exosomes obtained were aliquoted and stored at ‐80°C until use.

Characterization of P. peruviana‐Derived Exosomes

Morphological Characterization of P. peruviana‐Derived Exosomes by Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

The morphology of the isolated exosomes was examined by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging. A 10 µL of isolated exosome suspension was deposited onto the surface of a carbon‐coated copper grid, then negatively stained with 1% (w/v) sodium phosphotungstate at pH 7.4 for 1 min. residual stain was removed using Whatman paper from the samples at 20°C. The stained samples were visualized using an FEI Tecnai G2 Spirit Biotwin 120 kW transmission electron microscope. Imaging was conducted at the electron microscopy facility of the Centro de Investigación Príncipe Felipe (CIPF, Valencia, Spain).

Quantification and Size Distribution Analysis of P. peruviana‐Derived Exosomes by Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA)

To evaluate both the particle concentration and size distribution of the isolated exosomes, nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) was performed NTA‐LM10 Nanosight (Malvern, UK), operated with NTA software version 3.4 Build 3.4.4. Prior to measurement, each exosome preparation was diluted 1:100 in 1xPBS. For each sample, five independent video captures of 1 min each were recorded under constant camera settings, with a detection threshold set at 3 to minimize background noise while preserving sensitivity.

Extraction and Profiling of Total Lipids of P. peruviana‐Derived Exosomes

Total lipid content from the isolated exosomes were extracted using the Folch method. A chloroform/methanol (2:1) solution was added to exosomes followed by vigorous vortexing. To enhance lipid recovery, the mixture was subjected to ultrasonic bath sonication for 20 min at room temperature. After sonication, the samples were centrifuged at 10000 g for 20 min at 4°C. The resulting organic phase was collected, dried, and resuspended in chloroform. For lipid class separation and preliminary profiling, the extracted lipids were applied onto Silica gel thin‐layer chromatography (TLC) plate (EMD Millipore) and resolved using a mobile phase composed of chloroform/methanol/acetic acid (190:9:1) and visualized with CuSO4 phosphoric acid reagent (10% CuSO4 in 8% phosphoric acid).

Isolation of RNA From P. peruviana‐Derived Exosomes

Total RNA was extracted from the P. peruviana‐derived exosomes using the TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Madrid, Spain), following the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, the exosome suspension was mixed with TRIzol reagent in a 1:3 (v/v) ratio and vigorously vortexed to ensure complete lysis of vesicular membranes and release of RNA contents. Following incubation at room temperature for 5 min, chloroform was added (200 µL per 1 mL of TRIzol), and the mixture was shaken vigorously for 15 s. After a 10‐min rest on ice, the sample was centrifuged at 12000 g for 15 min at 4°C, resulting in phase separation. The aqueous phase, containing total RNA, was carefully recovered, and precipitated by adding an equal volume of isopropanol. The RNA pellet was obtained by centrifugation (12000 g, 10 min, 4°C), washed with 75% ethanol, and briefly air‐dried before resuspension in nuclease‐free water. All steps were performed under RNase‐free conditions to preserve RNA integrity. To evaluate RNA quality and integrity, agarose gel electrophoresis was performed using a 1% agarose gel in 1×TAE buffer.

Protein Extraction and Analyses From P. peruviana‐Derived Exosomes

Proteins were extracted from P. peruviana‐derived exosomes utilizing RIPA cell lysis buffer (0.2 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X‐100, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 0.3 M NaCl, 0.1% SDS, 20 mM β‐glycerophosphate, 0.5% deoxycholic acid, and 25 mM HEPES pH 7.5), subjected to polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, and visualized through Coomassie blue staining.

Cell Culture Model

Raw 264.7 cells were obtained from ATCC (ATCC No.TIB‐71) and subculture at a density of 6–8 × 104 cells/cm2 in DMEM medium (Lonza) supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin‐streptomycin, and 4 mM L‐glutamine, and incubated overnight in complete DMEM with 5% FBS, and different treatments, 25 µg mL−1 of exosomes or dexamethasone 1 µM before activation with LPS or cytokines. Cell activation was assessed in each experiment by measuring nitrite production (Griess reaction) following proinflammatory stimuli. Macrophages were stimulated with 150 ng mL−1 LPS for 30 min, 8 h and 24 h for anti‐inflammatory assays.

NIH3T3 were acquired from ATCC (ATCC No. CRL‐1658.2) were subculture at a density of 3–5 × 103 cells/cm2 in DMEM medium (Lonza) supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin‐streptomycin, and 4 mM L‐glutamine. Fibroblasts were stimulated with 100 µM of H2O2 for antioxidant assays.

Assessment of Cell Viability

To examine possible toxic effects, Raw 264.7 and NIH3T3cells were treated with different concentrations of P. peruviana exosomes (0, 25, 50, 75, 100 µg/mL for RAW264.7 and 0, 10, 20, 30, 40, 50 µg/mL for NIH3T3) for 24 h. Similarly, to induce oxidative stress, NIH3T3 cells were treated with H2O2 at a different concentration for 2 h. Cells were pre‐treated with P. peruviana exosomes from 10 to 50 µg/mL for 24 h before the addition of H2O2, and cells viability was determined by MTT assays.

Cells were seeded onto 24 well plates (1.5 × 105 cells/well for Raw 264.7 cells and 6 × 104 cells/well for NIH3T3). The medium was removed. 200 µL of the MTT [3‐(4,5‐dimethylthiazol‐2‐yl)‐2,5‐diphenyl tetrazolium bromide] (0.5 mg/mL) was added to each well. After 30 min of incubation at 37°C when a purple precipitate was observed, the solution was removed and 500 µL of DMSO was added in each well. After 10 min, the color was read at 555 nm in a plate reader. Means and standard deviations generated from three independent experiments were reported as the percentage of viable cells vs. control (untreated) cells. The percentage of viability was calculated as follows: Viability=AverageO.D.oftreatedcells−AverageO.D.ofblankAverageO.D.ofcontrolcells−AverageO.D.ofblank×100O.D.=Opticaldensity.

Transient Transfection and Luciferase Assay

To assess whether exosomes influence the transcriptional activity, transient transfection assays were performed. Raw 264.7 cells were seeded 2.5 × 105 cells/well in triplicate in 24‐well plates. The next day they were transfected with Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, Madrid, Spain) using OPTI‐MEM medium (Thermo Fisher, Madrid, Spain) without supplements and 1.25 µg/well of EndoFree total plasmid DNA. The transcriptional activities of NF‐κB and STAT1 were studied using the reporter plasmids pNF‐κB‐luc and pSTAT1‐luc to detect the transcriptional activities of these genes. The Renilla expression vector pRLTK (Promega, Madrid, Spain) was used as an internal control for transfection efficiency. After the different transfections the cells were stimulated for 24 h with 150 ng/mL LPS or interferon gamma. Luciferase and Renilla enzyme activities were measured with the Dual Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, Madrid, Spain) in a Sirius luminometer (Berthold, Bad Wildbad, Germany), following the manufacturer's recommendations.

Protein Extracts and Western Blot Analysis

Prior protein extraction, cells were washed with low‐temperature PBS and scrape. Following resuspension in RIPA lysis buffer (25 mM Hepes, pH 7.5; 1.5 mM MgCl2; 0.2 mM EDTA; 1% Triton X‐100; 0.3 M NaCl; 20 mM β‐Glycerophosphate; 0.1% SDS; 0.5% deoxycholic acid), protease inhibitor cocktail and phosphatase inhibitor I and II (Sigma) were added, the extract was homogenized for 30 min at 4°C and centrifuged at 13000 g for 20 min. Protein concentrations were determined by PierceTM BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fischer Scientific, Madrid, Spain) according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Protein extracts (30‐40 µg) were separated by denaturing SDS‐PAGE on 10% polyacrylamide gels; after electrophoresis, proteins were transferred to Polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Sigma–Aldrich, Madrid, Spain) and processed according to the recommendations of the antibody suppliers. We used ECL (GE Healthcare, Madrid, Spain) for protein detection, and ERK2 expression as a loading control for total protein extracts. ImageQuant 800 Western blot imaging systems was used to visualize membranes. The following antibodies were used for this study, Anti‐pIKKα(Ser180)/β(Ser181), anti‐IRF1(D5E4), anti‐Phospho‐Stat1 (Tyr701) (58D6) and anti‐Stat1 (D1K9Y) were purchased from Cell Signaling. Anti‐iNOS from BD. Anti‐ERK2 acquired from Santa Cruz.

Metabolomics

To analyze carotenoids, anthocyanins, and flavonoids present in P. peruviana exosomes samples, an extraction procedure was carried out using a chloroform: methanol mixture (2:1), incorporating α‐tocopherol (50 mg/L) as an internal standard. The mixture was homogenized for 10 min at 20 Hz using a Mixer Mill. Subsequently, a 50 mM Tris‐HCl buffer (pH 7.5) containing 1 M NaCl was added. After thorough mixing, the samples were centrifuged at 12000 g for 10 min using a microcentrifuge.

Following centrifugation, the chloroform (organic) layers were collected and dried using a SpeedVac concentrator. The resulting residues were reconstituted in 100 µL of HPLC‐grade ethyl acetate, followed by a centrifugation step to remove any remaining particulates. From this, 50 µL of the clarified supernatant was transferred into HPLC vials for subsequent analysis.

Each sample was processed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility. Detection and quantification of flavonoids, anthocyanins, carotenoids, and other hydrophobic metabolites were performed using HPLC‐HRMS. Metabolite identification was tentatively assigned based on their accurate mass‐to‐charge ratios (m/z), referencing monoisotopic masses listed in the PubChem database or using the Adduct Calculator provided by the Metabolomics Fiehn Lab.

Statistical Analysis

Each experiment was carried out at least three times. The results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.0.2. Statistical comparisons were carried out using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA).

Results

Isolation and Characterization of Exosomes From P. peruviana Fruits

Exosome isolation from P. peruviana fruits was achieved through a sequential differential centrifugation protocol. Initial low‐speed centrifugations were used to eliminate cellular debris and larger vesicular structures, while final enrichment was accomplished via ultracentrifugation, which yielded a concentrated exosome suspension (Figure 1A). From 400 g of fruit, approximately 6 mL of exosome suspension were obtained with a protein concentration of 5.05 ± 0.24 mg/mL, corresponding to an estimated yield of 7.58 mg of exosomes per 100 g of fresh fruit.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis of the isolated vesicles (Figure 1B) confirmed the presence of spherical micro vesicles with an average diameter of ∼100 nm, consistent with the expected morphology of plant‐derived exosomes. Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) further validated the size distribution and concentration of the particles (Figure 1C), revealing a dominant population with a mean size of 142 nm and a particle density of approximately 2.5 × 106 vesicles/mL.

Protein composition was assessed by SDS‐PAGE (Figure 1D), revealing distinct bands, notably two, close to 17 kDa and around 28 kDa, and another between 28 and 36 kDa. Additionally, total RNA was extracted from the vesicle fraction and analyzed on a 1% agarose gel (Figure 1E), showing the presence of small RNA species (<100 bp), likely corresponding to microRNAs.

Thin‐layer chromatography (TLC) confirmed the presence of lipid components within the exosome structure (Figure 1F), which are presumed to play a role in stabilizing and encapsulating various bioactive cargoes, including RNA, proteins, and secondary metabolites.

Metabolomic Characterization of P. peruviana‐Derived Exosomes

To assess the secondary metabolite content of the exosomes isolated from P. peruviana fruits, we conducted untargeted metabolomic profiling. P. peruviana fruits are known to be rich in carotenoids, which contribute to their antioxidant activity and pharmacological potential [10]. However, the extent to which these metabolites are associated with fruit‐derived exosomes has not been previously characterized. The metabolites in the exosomes were extracted and analyzed, revealing the presence of two main metabolites (Table 2), β‐carotene and Ubiquinone 9 and 10, suggesting that these vesicles may serve as carriers for the intercellular or interspecies transport of these bioactive metabolites.

Effect of P. peruviana‐Derived Exosomes on RAW264.7 and NIH3T3 Cells Viability

To determine the optimal concentration of exosomes for cells treatments, an MTT assay was performed on two cell lines. As shown in Figure 2A, RAW 264.7 cells exhibited a significant reduction in viability beginning at 50 µg/mL, indicating a cytotoxic threshold at that concentration. Similarly, Figure 2B shows that NIH3T3 cells experienced a marked decrease in viability at 50 µg/mL. In both cases, a dose‐dependent inverse correlation was observed between exosome concentration and cell viability. Based on these results, 25 µg/mL was identified as the highest non‐cytotoxic concentration common to both cell types. This concentration was therefore selected for all subsequent anti‐inflammatory and antioxidant assays.

Anti‐Inflammatory Effect of P. peruviana Exosomes on LPS‐Stimulated RAW264.7 Cells

Activated macrophages are the most representative cells of the inflammatory process, especially in chronic inflammatory diseases. Repolarization of M1 to M2 macrophages has proven to be a very effective option against the progression of this type of disease. We investigated the anti‐inflammatory effect of P. peruviana exosomes (25 µg/mL) on LPS‐treated macrophages. Treatment with P. peruviana exosomes resulted in a significant downregulation of mRNA expression for key mediators of the inflammatory response (Figure 3). Notably, transcript levels of the pro‐inflammatory cytokines IL‐6, IL‐12, and IFN‐β were markedly reduced following exosome exposure (Figure 3A–C). In parallel, mRNA expression of the TNF‐α cytokine and the transcription factor STAT1 also decreased significantly (Figure 3D–E). Importantly, iNOS, a central effector of nitric oxide‐mediated inflammation, was also downregulated at the transcript level (Figure 3F). These data were corroborated at the protein level by western blot analysis. Exosome treatment reduced total STAT1 protein expression (Figure 4A), as well as its phosphorylated form (p‐STAT1), indicating impairment of STAT1 activation. Additionally, expression of IRF1, a downstream effector of the STAT1 pathway (Figure S1A–C), was also diminished, suggesting broader suppression of this inflammatory signaling.

Given the observed downregulation of TNF‐α, we further assessed activation of the NF‐κB pathway. Western blot data revealed a notable decrease in phosphorylated IKK, a key activator of NF‐κB signaling (Figure 5A), alongside a marked reduction in iNOS protein levels (Figure S2A‐B), supporting the inhibition of nitric oxide‐mediated inflammatory responses.

To evaluate whether exosomes interfere with transcriptional activity, we performed luciferase reporter assays targeting STAT1 and NF‐κB. As shown in Figures 4B and 5B, STAT1 transcriptional activity was significantly reduced (p < 0.05), while NF‐κB activity also declined markedly (p < 0.01) in exosome‐treated cells.

Taken together, these data show that P. peruviana exosomes exert anti‐inflammatory effects by suppressing both STAT1 and NF‐κB pathways, implicating them as potent modulators of inflammatory gene expression at both transcriptional and post‐translational levels.

Antioxidant Effect of P. peruviana Exosomes on NIH3T3 Cells Treated With H2O2

To study the antioxidant potential of P. peruviana exosomes, NIH3T3 murine embryonic fibroblasts were used. Initially, cell viability assays were conducted to assess the protective effect of exosomes under oxidative stress. Cells were treated with increasing concentrations of hydrogen peroxide (0, 65, 125, 250, 500, and 1000 µM) to establish a cytotoxicity baseline (Figure 6A). A significant reduction in viability was observed starting at 125 µM H2O2, confirming effective induction of oxidative damage.

To gain mechanistic insights, we analyzed the transcriptional response of key antioxidant enzymes using quantitative real‐time PCR (qRT‐PCR). Gene expression levels of catalase (CAT), superoxide dismutases (SOD1 and SOD2), cytochrome c oxidase (CytCox), and glutathione peroxidase (GPx4) were measured in three conditions: exosome‐treated cells, H2O2‐treated cells, and exosome‐pretreated cells followed by oxidative stress (Figure 7). In contrast, cells treated with 100 µM H2O2 alone showed a partial compensatory response, increased CAT and CytCox, but downregulation of SOD1, SOD2, and GPx4, consistent with oxidative stress‐mediated transcriptional suppression. Notably, pre‐treatment with 25 µg/mL exosomes followed by H2O2 exposure restored or enhanced the expression of CAT, SOD2, CytCox, and GPx4, indicating that P. peruviana exosomes modulate the endogenous antioxidant defense system and mitigate H2O2‐induced oxidative gene repression.

Discussion

The present study provides insights into the isolation, molecular composition, and biological functionality of exosomes derived from P. peruviana fruits, using differential ultracentrifugation, the standard method for exosome purification based on size and density gradients [32]. This approach yielded an average of 7.58 mg of exosomal protein per 100 g of fresh fruit, a value notably higher than many previously reported plant systems. Exosomes isolated from ginger rhizomes yielded 5 mg/100 g [33], while strawberry‐derived vesicles presented significantly lower yields (3.6 µg/100 g) [34]. In contrast, certain fungal species such as Biyang floral mushroom have reported higher yields (up to 16 mg/100 g) [35], suggesting that biomass composition and extraction parameters strongly influence vesicle recovery.

Characterization of P. peruviana exosomes confirmed the presence of canonical exosomal cargo, including proteins, lipids, miRNA, and a wide array of secondary metabolites, validating their integrity and biological relevance [36]. The lipid bilayer ensures the structural stability of the vesicles and serves as a protective barrier for labile compounds, such as carotenoids and RNAs, protecting them from enzymatic degradation or oxidation [37]. This protective effect has been linked to improved bioavailability, prolonged circulation time, and enhanced delivery to target cells, characteristics that are highly desirable in drug delivery systems [32].

Electron microscopy and nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) revealed the typical size distribution (∼100‐140 nm) and morphology of plant‐derived exosomes. These dimensions are critical for cellular uptake via endocytosis or membrane fusion, enabling efficient intracellular delivery of vesicle content [38].

A key novelty of this study lies in the demonstration that P. peruviana exosomes contain bioactive compounds as carotenoids, particularly β‐carotene, with well‐documented antioxidant, anti‐inflammatory, and immunomodulatory properties [39, 40], and ubiquinone 9 and 10. Ubiquinone‐10 (UQ10) has gained considerable attention for its therapeutic potential in cardiovascular diseases, cancer, reproductive health, and immune‐related conditions, primarily due to its antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory properties. By modulating redox signaling and energy metabolism, UQ10 not only reduces oxidative stress but also helps regulate inflammation, contributing to its effectiveness in conditions like hypertension, AIDS, chronic periodontitis, and male infertility [41, 42].

Similar findings have been reported in other plant systems. For instance, exosomes from Arbutus unedo (strawberry tree) and Crocus sativus (saffron) have been shown to contain not only β‐carotene but also zeaxanthin, crocin, flavonoids, and phenolic acids, contributing to a wide range of bioactivities [43, 44].

In oxidative stress assays using NIH3T3 cells, pre‐treatment with exosomes (10–25 µg/mL) significantly mitigated H2O2 induced cytotoxicity, suggesting a cytoprotective role linked to the exosomal antioxidant content. At the molecular level, exosome‐treated cells exhibited upregulated expression of endogenous antioxidant enzymes, including CAT, SOD1, SOD2, CytCox, and GPx4. These enzymes are central to the cellular defense against ROS, and their upregulation implies activation of the antioxidant response element (ARE)‐Nrf2 signaling pathway, previously associated with dietary carotenoids [45, 46, 47].

Our findings are consistent with recent reports indicating that carotenoid‐rich exosomes can enhance redox homeostasis. For example, crocin‐enriched exosomes from transgenic tomato (Tomafran) have shown neuroprotective effects in SH‐SY5Y neuroblastoma cells subjected to oxidative injury [48]. Similarly, tea leaf‐derived exosomes have demonstrated the ability to influence lipid metabolism and ROS detoxification, further supporting the therapeutic potential of plant‐derived vesicles in oxidative stress management [49].

Beyond their antioxidant effects, P. peruviana exosomes demonstrated potent anti‐inflammatory activity in the RAW 264.7 macrophage model. The observed downregulation of IL‐6, IL‐12, TNF‐α, IFN‐β, and iNOS, both at mRNA and protein levels, confirms modulation of inflammatory mediators. Furthermore, inhibition of phosphorylated STAT1 and IKK, along with reduced transcriptional activity of STAT1 and NF‐κB as shown in luciferase assays, points to dual suppression of the NF‐κB and JAK/STAT pathways which are essential in the inflammatory cascade (Figure 8).

Previous literature supports these mechanisms, β‐carotene and other carotenoids have been shown to attenuate NF‐κB activation and downstream cytokine expression (e.g., IL‐6, TNF‐α), while also interfering with STAT1 phosphorylation, reducing the expression of pro‐inflammatory genes like iNOS [13, 50]. Moreover, studies on Crocosmia crocosmiflora and Arbutus unedo exosomes have demonstrated similar effects, inhibiting nitrite accumulation via iNOS suppression [43, 51].

Plant exosomes are currently being studied in depth, as they have a very promising therapeutic potential [52]. Not only because of the number of bioactive compounds they contain, but also because they are a vehicle that increases the half‐life of different compounds or can help in their delivery. For example, lycopene is known to have a very short half‐life, as it is a metabolite that is easily degraded when exposed to light or oxidizing agents [53]. Another example could be relatively more stable compounds with low solubility, such as beta‐carotene, which has a lipid‐soluble nature; this characteristic is very important as it can hinder good bioaccessibility of certain metabolites [54]. This leads to the implication that plant‐derived exosomes represent a perfect group of nanocarriers for nutraceuticals, small RNAs, and other therapeutic agents. Their biocompatibility, stability, natural origin, and ability to cross biological barriers position them as top candidates for next‐generation delivery platforms in both clinical and functional food applications.

It has also been shown that a variety of plant‐derived exosomes have the capacity to influence physiological responses, as they are able to regulate the immune response, which indicates their potential utility in treating multifactorial diseases, including neurodegenerative disorders, autoimmune conditions, and chronic inflammatory syndromes [55]. The integration of natural exosomes into personalized medicine, nanoformulation design, and dietary therapeutics remains an exciting frontier, meriting continued exploration.

Conclusions

Exosomes derived from P. peruviana fruits exhibit significant anti‐inflammatory and antioxidant properties. In LPS‐stimulated RAW 264.7 macrophages, these nanovesicles effectively suppressed key inflammatory mediators by downregulating the NF‐κB and JAK/STAT1 signaling pathways. Furthermore, they inhibited the expression of pro‐inflammatory cytokines and effectors, including IL‐6, IL‐12, TNF‐α, IFN‐β, iNOS, STAT1, and IRF1, as well as reduced the levels of phosphorylated STAT1 and IKK, highlighting their impact at both transcriptional and post‐translational levels. Additionally, P. peruviana exosomes conferred cytoprotective effects under oxidative stress, enhancing cell viability in H2O2‐treated fibroblasts and upregulating crucial antioxidant enzymes such as CAT, SOD1, SOD2, cytochrome c oxidase, and GPx4. These findings support the role of these plant‐derived vesicles not only as bioactive compound carriers, but also as direct modulators of cellular homeostasis. Overall, our findings provide proof‐of‐concept in vitro evidence supporting the bioactivity of P. peruviana‐derived exosomes and highlight their potential as candidates for future biotherapeutic or nutraceutical applications. However, further studies addressing pharmacokinetics, biodistribution, stability, and in vivo efficacy are required to validate their therapeutic potential and to explore their possible integration into functional food, pharmaceutical, or nanomedical strategies.
