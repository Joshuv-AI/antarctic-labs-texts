Endogenous Circadian System Attenuates Nighttime Vascular Endothelial Function in People With Untreated Obstructive Sleep Apnea
Authors: Saurabh S. Thosar, Nicole P. Bowles, Matthew P. Butler, Andrew W. McHill, Sean P.M. Rice, Jonathan S. Emens, Steven A. Shea
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn people with obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), adverse cardiovascular events, such as myocardial infarction, occur more commonly in the middle of the night than at other times of the day. These events could occur as a result of the nighttime physiological changes in OSA, but the endogenous circadian system has been implicated in increased cardiovascular risk in OSA. Vascular endothelial function is a marker of prospective cardiovascular risk and is impaired in OSA. We hypothesized that the nighttime risk in OSA could be due to a circadian system–driven nighttime impairment in vascular endothelial function.MethodsTwelve adults (healthy except for untreated moderate to severe OSA, 8 men and 4 women, 51± SD 7 years; apnea/hypopnea index, 29±19 hours of sleep) completed a 5‐day circadian protocol consisting of 10 recurring 5‐hour 20‐minute periods (2 hours 40 minutes each of wake periods and sleep opportunities) to separate the circadian system’s effects from those of behaviors such as exercise, meal intake, and sleep. We measured flow‐mediated dilation of the brachial artery during each wake period in each participant.ResultsWe discovered a significant circadian rhythm in flow‐mediated dilation (P=0.019), and flow‐mediated dilation normalized to shear rate (P=0.037) with a trough at the circadian phase corresponding to ≈3:00 am with a peak‐to‐trough change of 2.5 percentage points, or 82%. There was no significant circadian rhythm in the baseline diameter (P=0.39).ConclusionsThese data suggest that the circadian system impairs vascular endothelial function during the biological night, potentially increasing vascular vulnerability in people with untreated OSA.RegistrationURL: https://clinicaltrials.gov/; Unique Identifier: NCT02202811.

There is overwhelming evidence in the general population that major adverse cardiovascular events, including myocardial infarction and sudden cardiac death, occur more frequently in the morning than at other times of the day. 1 , 2 , 3 The circadian system has been implicated in this increased risk, mainly by extrapolating findings from healthy individuals, including a circadian system–driven morning increase in plasminogen activator inhibitor‐1 and increased catecholamine reactivity to exercise in the morning. 4

Vascular endothelial function is a comprehensive cross‐sectional and longitudinal marker of cardiovascular disease risk. 5 Noninvasively measured vascular endothelial function at the bedside, brachial artery flow‐mediated dilation (FMD), is attenuated in the morning compared with other times of the day. 6 , 7 In healthy people, such attenuation is not due to the effects of overnight sleep or the inactivity that generally accompanies sleep but instead due to the effects of the endogenous circadian system independent of behaviors such as sleep, exercise, and meal intake. 8 , 9 The effects of the endogenous circadian system on vascular endothelial function in people with cardiovascular risk factors are not known.

To fill this knowledge gap, we focused on obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), a common condition affecting about 20% of the global population that significantly increases the risk for major adverse cardiovascular events and impairs vascular endothelial function. 10 , 11 , 12 Intriguingly, compared with the general population, people with OSA experience adverse cardiovascular events more frequently across the night rather than in the morning. 1 , 13 The increased frequency of these adverse nocturnal events could be related to numerous factors, including hypoxia‐associated chemoreceptor activation. 14 However, the circadian system may also be involved if the nocturnal biological rest phase is perturbed, as has been shown by perturbed clock gene expressions in peripheral blood mononuclear cells and a shifted circadian rhythm in resting blood pressure. 15 , 16 , 17 In this investigation, we tested the hypothesis that the endogenous circadian system, independent of behaviors such as exercise, sleep, and eating, attenuates FMD at night in individuals with OSA. We also explored whether sleep characteristics or the apnea–hypopnea index (AHI) influenced our results.

Methods

Data will be available upon reasonable request from the corresponding authors.

Subjects

This study was approved by the Oregon Health and Science University Institutional Review Board for Human Subjects Protection (No. 10101) and registered in ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier NCT02202811. Data were collected between November 2015 and January 2020. All participants signed a written informed consent before participation. Circadian variation in FMD in healthy controls has already been published. 8 , 18 Twelve people (8 women and 4 men) with untreated OSA (AHI range, 15.0–74.1/h) completed the study. We used intensive screening methods tailored to study circadian biology in midlife adults. 8 Participants underwent a stepwise screening process that included a phone screen, a first in‐person screening for laboratory measurements, and a second in‐person screening that included a physical examination. Barring the presence of moderate to severe sleep apnea detected during screening (WatchPat; Zoll, Itamar, Caesarea, Israel), all participants were healthy on the basis of self‐report, laboratory measurements (e.g., complete blood panel, ECG, blood pressure measurements in triplicate), and physical and psychological assessments by a physician (J.S.E.). No participants were taking medications, and all were free of drug use as assessed by urinalysis. Participants were excluded for pregnancy, a history of cardiovascular disease, or a smoking history. We ensured the stability of participants’ circadian systems by excluding participants with reported travel histories across >3 time zones within 3 months or shift work within 6 months of study. To ensure regular sleep–wake cycles before entry into the in‐laboratory protocol, participants maintained a stable self‐selected 8‐hour sleep schedule in darkness each day for 1 week. This schedule was verified by actigraphy (wGT3X‐BT; Actigraph Corporation, Pensacola, FL), phone calls to a time‐stamped mailbox at bedtime and on awakening, and a written sleep diary. During the at‐home monitoring, we asked participants to refrain from any over‐the‐counter medications, caffeinated food and beverages, alcohol, and high‐intensity physical activity >45 min/d. Adherence was verified using self‐report and urinalysis.

Circadian Protocol

Circadian protocol: participants stayed in an inpatient suite for 5 days.

After an 8‐hour baseline night and a baseline day, participants completed 10 alternating 2‐hour 40‐minute sleep and wake intervals, such that all behaviors were spaced evenly across the 24‐hour circadian cycle. DLMO indicates dim‐light melatonin onset.

We used a gold‐standard forced desynchrony protocol (Figure 1) that desynchronizes the sleep–wake schedule from the circadian pacemaker by delaying or advancing the daily sleep–wake schedule beyond the range of entrainment of the human circadian pacemaker. 19 , 20 , 21 We used a validated 5‐hour 20‐minute schedule that we have previously used in healthy midlife adults and in people with sleep apnea and epilepsy. 8 , 22 , 23 To examine underlying endogenous circadian effects on FMD independent from any effects on FMD induced by daily behavioral and environmental changes, participants completed a 5‐day forced desynchrony protocol that desynchronized the sleep–wake cycle from the endogenous circadian cycle, that is, a “forced desynchrony” protocol. 8 Specifically, after a baseline day and night, participants lived on a recurring 5‐hour 20‐minute schedule (2 hours 40 minutes of sleep opportunity in darkness <0.1 lux and 2 hours 40 minutes of scheduled wakefulness in dim light <3 lux), thereby allowing the human circadian pacemaker to run at its intrinsic rate of ≈24.1 hours. 20 Participants had no access to external cues such as sunlight or clocks. Staff were trained not to reveal the time of day when interacting with the participants. Equally spaced behaviors (e.g., sleep opportunities and identical isocaloric meals) and 10 equally spaced measurements of FMD (details below) allowed for uncovering any endogenous circadian variation in FMD.

Sleep assessment during each sleep episode in the protocol: Throughout the forced desynchrony, participants had 10 sleep opportunities, each 160 minutes in duration. We employed polysomnography to collect sleep, cardiac, and respiratory variables, including ECG, electroencephalogram, electrooculogram, and submental electromyograms according to the American Academy of Sleep Medicine guidelines. 24 Specifically, sleep measurements (Cadwell Easy III; Cadwell Industries, Kennewick, WA) were conducted on all participants using 2 frontal and central leads with reference electrodes placed at contralateral mastoid processes. We measured the electrooculogram using 2 offset electrodes placed lateral to the eyes, and chin electromyogram using 2 electrodes placed on the chin. Respiratory effort was monitored by thoracic and abdominal piezoelectric belts, whereas airflow was monitored using a nasal cannula. Sleep staging and sleep‐disordered breathing (eg, apneas and hypopneas) events were scored according to American Academy of Sleep Medicine criteria by a registered polysomnographic technician at the conclusion of each study. 24 , 25 Specific variables used in the analyses here were each sleep opportunity’s sleep duration, sleep efficiency (calculated as sleep duration divided by time in bed), and AHI.

Circadian Phase Assessment

We collected saliva samples using Salivette cotton swabs (Starstedt, Inc., Newton, NC) and immediately spun and froze the samples for later. After thawing all samples in a batch, we used radioimmunoassay (Kennaway G280 antimelatonin antibody; Bühlmann Laboratories, Schönenbuch, Switzerland) to measure melatonin levels. The dim‐light melatonin onset (DLMO) was used as the circadian phase marker to align all data. DLMO was determined as the linearly interpolated time point when melatonin exceeded 3 pg/mL from each 24‐hour day’s time series of salivary melatonin concentrations. 8 In participants whose salivary melatonin never crossed 3 pg/mL (low or high), we used a threshold equal to the mean and 2 SDs of the first 3 low daytime values (n=2). 26 Each participant’s FMD data were assigned a circadian phase, in degrees, relative to their own DLMO, set at 0 degrees, with each participant’s free‐running period in the protocol set to 360 degrees.

Flow‐Mediated Dilation

Baseline brachial artery diameter and FMD were measured in the dominant arm 10 times in each participant throughout the protocol using standard guidelines published by Thijjsen and colleagues in 2019 and, as we have published, using an identical protocol in healthy controls. 8 , 27 We obtained images of the brachial artery with a 2‐dimensional high‐resolution ultrasound system (Terason t3000, Teratec h Corp., Burlington, MA) using a 5‐ to 12‐MHz multifrequency linear‐array transducer. Once an optimal image was found, the area was marked on the participant’s skin to ensure the same transducer placement for all measurements. Doppler ultrasound was used to measure blood velocity concurrently. We corrected the Doppler flow signals at an insonation angle of 60 degrees and placed the sample volume in the middle of the artery. All analyses were blinded and performed offline using an automated edge detection software (Brachial Analyzer; Medical Imaging Applications LLC, Coralville, IA) as previously described. 8 , 27 To eliminate the impact of any variation in the stimulus needed for dilation, we also normalized FMD to the hyperemic shear area under the curve until peak dilation. 28

Statistical Analysis

We first calculated summary statistics for participant characteristics. To test for systematic rhythmicity, we performed cosinor analyses, a regression method that can capture periodic sinusoidal patterns. 29 , 30 Data were fit to the model: Yt=M+Acos2πt360−φ+et where M is mean, A is the amplitude, φ is the acrophase, e is the residual error, and t is circadian time in degrees from DLMO. This can be linearized through the cosine transformation to: Yt=M+Acosφcos2πt360+Asinφsin2πt360+et In addition to the fixed factor of the circadian phase (t), time into protocol (beginning of the baseline period for each measurement in hours) was included as a fixed factor, and participant was included as a random factor to account for repeated measures. We first tested whether the baseline brachial artery diameter showed a circadian variation. We then repeated this process for FMD, and FMD normalized to hyperemic shear. Furthermore, we estimated 95% bootstrapped CIs using 5000 bootstrapped samples (with resampling at the participant level). If the interval does not contain 0, it is considered statistically significant (akin to P<0.05). Additionally, we computed Cohen’s f2 effect sizes (small=0.02; moderate=0.15; large=0.35 31 ) to infer the substantiveness of the effect outside of statistical significance. 32 In separate cosinor models, we tested whether the previous sleep period’s sleep duration, sleep efficiency, or AHI affected the results. Each FMD data point was accompanied by 1 data point each for sleep duration, efficiency, and AHI. Each variable was separately added to the original model as a fixed factor. We also explored if AHI showed a circadian variation and if there were any relationships between FMD and AHI. Significance was set at P<0.05.

Results

Participant characteristics are described in the Table 1. Our midlife participants’ (age range, 40–64 years; AHI, 29±19 hours) average DLMO was 20:37±01:04 [hours:minutes], and the circadian period was 24.1±0.3 hours. These values are similar to previously published data in healthy midlife individuals from our laboratory 8 (mean DLMO, 20:51; mean period, 23.96 hours) and normative data in other healthy participants. 20 We did not find a circadian rhythm in the baseline diameter (cosine coefficient=0.03 [95% CI, −0.03 to +0.09]; P=0.40; f2≤0.002; sine coefficient=0.02 [95% CI, −0.07 to +0.1]; P=0.68; f2=0.01). However, we discovered a significant circadian rhythm in FMD (sine coefficient=−1.28 [95% CI, −2.18 to −0.39]; P=0.019; f‐squared=0.06) and in FMD normalized to hyperemic shear (sine coefficient=−0.00019 [95% CI, −0.00034 to −0.00004]; P=0.037, f2=0.04) with troughs at the circadian phase corresponding to ≈3:00 am (Figure 2). The peak‐to‐trough change in FMD was 82%. Furthermore, the minimum FMD for 7 of 12 individuals occurred at circadian phases corresponding to the usual sleep period in this group within a 6‐hour window (circadian phases 51 degrees to 140 degrees, ie, ≈12:00 am to 6 am) (Figure 3A). In separate models, our results were not affected by any of the 3 parameters: (1) sleep duration (sine coefficient=−1.32 [95% CI, −2.42 to −0.22]; P=0.019; cosine coefficient=−0.22 [95% CI, −1.26 to +0.83]; P=0.68), (2) sleep efficiency (sine coefficient=−1.32 [95% CI, −2.42 to −0.23]; P=0.018; cosine coefficient=−0.22 95% CI, −1.24 to +0.81]; P=0.67), or (3) AHI (sine coefficient=−1.32 [95% CI, −2.41 to −0.22]; P=0.019; cosine coefficient=−0.16 [95% CI, −1.13 to +0.80]; P=0.73). We did not find a circadian rhythm in AHI (sine coefficient=2.79 [95% CI, −0.71 to +6.30]; P=0.11; cosine coefficient=−1.49 [95% CI, −4.60 to +1.61]; P=0.34). There was no correlation between FMD and apnea–hypopnea in the full sample (r=0.15, P>0.1). Even at an individual level, correlation was equivocal. The correlations ranged from −0.8232 to +0.7178, with a median of 0.0284. Individual cosinor models are also plotted for visualization (Figure 3B).

Discussion

Using an intensive 5‐day in‐laboratory circadian protocol, we systematically isolated the effects of the endogenous circadian system on vascular endothelial function measured as FMD in people with untreated OSA. We discovered that in these people, the endogenous circadian system impairs FMD in the middle of the biological night, corresponding to the vulnerable period for adverse cardiovascular events in people with OSA. The circadian difference between high and low FMD (2.55 percentage points or 82%) is clinically meaningful because it is bigger than the improvement in FMD due to 3 months of continuous positive airway pressure. 33 These data are in contrast with data in healthy controls. In healthy people, circadian system–driven FMD is the lowest before the night and increases across the night before decreasing again in the morning. 8 , 9 This is the first evidence of a circadian system–driven cardiovascular function impairment that corresponds to the timing of cardiovascular vulnerability in people with OSA.

People with OSA have impaired vascular endothelial function, 34 particularly endothelial‐dependent vasodilation, which is rescued after treatment with continuous positive airway pressure. 35 In addition, there have been reports of a disrupted diurnal variation as compared with healthy controls, 36 with a relative impairment in the morning. Previous studies have not measured FMD during the biological night and, therefore, cannot detect an improvement or deterioration at that time. Our study overcame these limitations and uncovered an FMD impairment during the biological night.

Potential mechanisms: FMD is a barometer for nitric oxide bioavailability. 27 We also confirmed the circadian rhythm in FMD by normalizing FMD to the shear stimulus, suggesting that, independent of the hyperemic stimulus, the circadian system attenuates nitric oxide bioavailability in the peripheral circulation at night in people with untreated OSA. The endogenous circadian system helps the body prepare for the anticipated changes in behaviors and the environment. Additionally, the interaction between behaviors and circadian rhythms in cardiovascular physiology may increase risk in vulnerable people. 37 For example, in healthy people, we have recently shown that the circadian system promotes heightened coronary vascular function in the morning, presumably in anticipation of increased myocardial demand based on typical morning behaviors. 38 Similarly, in age‐matched healthy midlife adults using an identical circadian protocol, we have shown that FMD is the lowest before the biological night but increases across the night before decreasing again in the morning. 8 , 9 In people with OSA, a circadian system–driven attenuated nitric oxide bioavailability during the night will be ineffective in compensating for the apnea–hypercapnia‐induced surges in sympathetic activity and thus could increase the risk of nighttime adverse cardiovascular events. 39 Interestingly, we found no circadian rhythms in AHI or correlations between FMD and AHI, although this should be verified in a larger sample. It is also possible that there are other non–nitric oxide mechanisms involved. For instance, in ostensibly healthy people with OSA, endothelial nitric oxide synthase is upregulated in the microvasculature, and treatment with continuous positive airway pressure downregulates endothelial NO synthase transcription. 40

These results can be potentially usefully applied to the practice of circadian medicine in people with OSA. 41 For instance, there is a diurnal rhythm in cholesterol biosynthesis with a peak during the biological night, 42 and subsequently, the total cholesterol‐lowering effect of statins is most effective when short‐acting statins are taken before bedtime. 43

Strengths and Limitations

We selected midlife people with untreated OSA without concomitant chronic diseases and used a rigorous circadian protocol. We also enrolled men and women in a ratio equivalent to the epidemiology of sleep apnea. 12 Our population selection and the study design allowed us to study the isolated effects of the circadian system on vascular endothelial function in untreated OSA. We also adjusted for factors such as the previous sleep period’s sleep duration and efficiency, and AHI. Nevertheless, it is possible that hypoxia‐induced chemoreceptor activation during the prior sleep episode could influence FMD. We used a gold‐standard circadian protocol with repeated measurements within each individual that greatly strengthens our methodology, yet our study has some limitations, namely: (1) we did not calculate a priori sample size for a circadian rhythm in FMD, (2) the effect sizes of our findings are small to moderate, and (3) we did not conduct multiple testing across circadian bins. Even though FMD is an index of nitric oxide bioavailability, we do not precisely know the mechanisms (e.g., depletion of cofactor tetrahydrobiopterin 44 ) underlying the circadian variation.

In conclusion, our data suggest that in people with untreated OSA, the circadian system–imposed reduction in vascular endothelial function during the biological night may increase cardiovascular vulnerability. Such an impairment can increase the risk of adverse cardiovascular events in people with OSA. Testing whether the circadian rhythm in FMD is similar to that of controls in people treated with continuous positive airway pressure for OSA would further support the argument that the circadian system plays a role in the timing of adverse cardiovascular events.
