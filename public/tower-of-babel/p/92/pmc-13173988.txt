The impact of symbiosis between arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi and beans on potassium absorption and transporters in response to salt stress
Authors: Zahra Alizadeh, Parviz Heidari, Hamid Reza Asghari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPotassium (K) is one of the most important elements that has different functions in plants. Although K+ ions are found in large quantities in soil, their availability is limited due to strong adsorption to soil minerals. In this study, the effect of symbiosis of arbuscular mycorrhiza fungi (AMF), Rhizophagus irregularis and Funneliformis mosseae with bean roots on the absorption of K+, phosphorus and sodium as well as the activity of potassium transporters was investigated.ResultsThe results revealed that the symbiosis with both AMFs increased the absorption of K+ and phosphorus in the roots and transfer to the shoots. Also, under salinity stress, AMFs have been able to increase the K+/Na+ ratio and reduce the harmful effects of salinity. Besides, morphological traits such as plant height, stem diameter, number of side branches, and length of fruit increased under AM symbiosis conditions. In the comparison between AMFs, R. irregularis showed a greater ability in K+ availability than F. mosseae. Nineteen members of the KUP family (PvKUP) were identified and they were different from each other in terms of gene structure and physicochemical properties. Based on the phylogenetic tree, the members of PvKUP family were placed in five groups and the several duplications was identified between them. Gene expression pattern analysis showed that PvKUPs have both tissue-specific expression and general expression. PvKUP16 was expressed only in root cells inoculated with R. irregularis, while PvKUP18 showed increased expression in both root tissues and aerial organs.ConclusionsOverall, the results of this research determined that AMFs are effective in increasing potassium absorption in beans, although more studies are needed to better understand the mechanisms related to this interaction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08584-w.Keywords: Plant-microbe interaction, KUP genes, K transporters

Introduction

Potassium (K+) is the second most important mineral in plants, which has important functions in various plant life processes and it is responsible for 2 to 10% of the dry weight of the plant [1]. K+ not only to maintain the biochemical processes of plants such as protein synthesis, carbohydrate metabolism and activation of enzymes and also plays a role in several physiological processes such as stomatal movement and photosynthesis, in addition in response to biotic (such as diseases and pests) and abiotic (such as salinity, drought and cold) stress [2–4]. K+ exists in the soil in several different forms and its availability for plants is usually very low due to the strong absorption of K+ by silicate materials. In plants, K+ is first absorbed by the roots and then transferred to the aerial parts and distributed to different parts within the cells [5]. All of these processes require transport systems for potassium uptake, known as channels and transporters.

The maintenance of cytoplasmic K+ concentrations at approximately 100 mM is crucial for plant cells [6], although the levels in the rhizosphere are highly unpredictable, rarely surpassing 0.01 to 1 mm [7]. Plants require specialized transport systems for the uptake and redistribution of K+ internally. K+ transporter proteins and ion channel proteins work together to transport K+ within plants [8]. Within transporter protein families, the HAK (HAK/AK/KUP) family holds significance as the largest and essential for mediating intracellular K+ accumulation to sustain plant development and growth [9–11]. HAK proteins aid in the absorption of potassium in plants by utilizing a co-location mechanism of potassium and hydrogen ions, usually containing 10–14 transmembrane domains with an extended loop in the second to third membrane portion [12]. The HAK proteins have their C- and N-terminal ends positioned inside the cell that the position of terminal points plays a crucial role in recognizing and binding ions, as well as regulating the speed of potassium ion transportation and maintaining ion balance both within and outside the cell [8, 13]. Maintaining K+ balance relies on the ability to detect changes in both internal and external concentrations of potassium ions [14]. Within the K+ starvation signaling pathway, reactive oxygen species (ROS), calcium ions, and phytohormones serve as key regulatory signals [8, 15, 16]. Enhanced ROS production in Arabidopsis due to ethylene signaling results in elevated AtHAK5 expression and increased K+ levels under low-potassium conditions, leading to a higher K+/Na+ ratio [17, 18].

Although K+ ions are found in large quantities within soil, their availability is restricted by the strong adsorption to minerals. As a result, plants must devise effective methods to enhance the uptake of K+ from the soil [11, 19], including the implementation of high-affinity transportation systems or forming symbiotic relationships with microorganisms. Terrestrial plants benefit from improved hydro-mineral nutrition through mycorrhizal associations formed between their roots and underground fungi hyphae [20]. The majority of land plants engage in the important symbiotic interaction known as arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) symbiosis with AM fungi from Glomeromycotina [21]. The establishment of an AM symbiosis results in enhanced growth status for the plant partner, especially when facing nutrient deficiencies [20, 22–24]. Our understanding of how this mutualistic symbiosis benefits plant K+ nutrition is limited and inadequately investigated thus far. The study indicated that introducing the AM fungus Rhizophagus irregularis into tomato plants led to improved growth and enhanced K+ acquisition, underscoring the importance of avoiding K+ deficiency for optimal root growth and AM colonization [22]. It was also discovered a specialized tomato K+ transporter (known as SlHAK10) that was specifically activated in arbuscule-containing cells during mycorrhiza development [22]. The investigation using particle-induced X-ray emission (PIXE) demonstrated a higher level of K+ concentration in the root-sections of Aster tripolium colonized with Rhizophagus irregularis than in those without inoculation, hinting at a potential boost in K+ uptake facilitated by AM colonization [25]. Observing a similar K+ enrichment in plants that formed mycorrhizal relationships with different AMF was also documented in shoot tissues of Pelargonium peltatum [26], roots of Zea mays [27], and leaves of Lactuca sativa [28]. During a recent examination through whole-genome RNA sequencing, it was discovered that in Medicago truncatula mycorrhizal roots lacking K+, there was an increase in the activity of various genes responsible for encoding potential transporters, such as a suspected K+/H+ exchanger, in plants affected by K+ deprivation [20].

However, significant knowledge gaps remain. It is still unclear to what extent AM fungi modulate the expression and function of plant K+ transporter families, such as HAK/KUP/KT, ​​in different host species, especially under combined nutrient and abiotic stresses. Furthermore, the specific mechanisms and complete set of K+ transporters involved in the AM-mediated K+ uptake pathway in legumes have not been defined. To address these gaps, this study investigated the AMF-mediated K+ uptake system in common bean (Phaseolus vulgaris), a nutritionally critical legume. We hypothesized that symbiosis with Funneliformis mosseae or Rhizophagus irregularis would enhance K+ and P uptake and modulate the activity of KUP transporters under saline conditions. To test this hypothesis and elucidate the molecular basis of this interaction, we pursued the following specific objectives: (1) quantify the effect of AMF inoculation on K+, Na+, and P uptake in different bean tissues under salinity; (2) identify and characterize the KUP gene family in the common bean genome; and (3) analyze the expression profiles of key bean KUP genes in response to AM symbiosis and salinity stress. By integrating physiological nutrient analyses with genome-wide identification and expression profiling of KUP genes, this study provides new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying K+ nutrition by AMF in legumes. These findings may help to develop strategies to improve nutrient use efficiency and crop resilience in nutrient-poor or stressed soils.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and treatments

In this experiment, Kermanshah variety of common bean prepared from Karaj Seedling and Seed Research Center, was used. To disinfect them, the seeds were soaked in a 3% sodium hypochlorite solution for 5 min and then washed twice with sterile water. In the current work, two factors (3 inoculation treatments x 2 salinity levels) were investigated based on a completely randomized factorial experiment. The first factor included symbiosis with AMF (including Funneliformis mosseae and Rhizophagus irregularis) and without symbiosis (using mock inoculum), while the secondary factor consisted of varying levels of soil salinity (45 ± 3 ppm sodium for standard soil and 120 ± 8 ppm sodium for saline soil). The mycorrhizal inoculum (Turan Biotech-Co, Shahrood, Iran) was dried pot culture of Trifolium alexandrinum (as a trap culture) consisting of soil plus colonized root fragments, external hyphae and spores. The pH and sodium concentration was measured by EC meter and by flame emission method (Atomic Emission Spectrometry), respectively. The used soil had a pH of 6.8, and 45 ± 3 ppm sodium and silty was detected as the soil texture. Prior to commencing the experiment, the soil samples underwent autoclave sterilization and were subsequently dispersed into individual pots. The experimental protocol called for the addition of 20 g of mycorrhizal fungi inoculum to specific pots followed by the planting of three bean seeds in each individual pot. Three seedlings in each pot were considered as one experimental unit. Four pots were used for each treatment and 24 pots were used in this research. The used pots had a height of 25 cm and a diameter of 20 cm and chemical fertilizers were not applied for feeding the bean seedlings. Once the seeds were sown, the pots received their first watering and were placed in a controlled environment with a temperature range of 25 ± 3 °C and exposed to 14 h of light. Different plants parts were harvested from 6-week-old seedlings and immediately placed in liquid nitrogen and transferred to -80 °C freezer for other analysis. This experiment was performed in three biological replicates.

Confirmation of mycorrhizal colonization of roots

First, the seedlings were gently separated from the soil, so that the seedlings were least damaged. The sampled roots were washed well with water, and all soil and plant residues were removed from the roots. After sampling the washed roots, the roots were transferred into 50 ml glass containers. Then 10% KOH solution was added to the roots and placed at room temperature for 2 h or for 10 to 15 min in a bain-marie with a temperature of 90 °C (the duration of keeping in the KOH solution depends on the diameter of the roots). In order to neutralize the alkaline environment of the bleached roots, the roots were washed with distilled water and placed in 0.1 Normal HCl solution for 1–2 min. For root staining, the modified method of Phillips and Hayman (1970) [29] was used. In this method, bleached roots for 6 to 12 h in the solution trypan blue staining 0.01% were kept at room temperature. After removing the roots from the dye solution and washing with distilled water, the roots were ready to consider the colonization. We use a solution of alcohol, glycerol and water to keep the roots until the colonization rate is measured.

Measurement of potassium and sodium from plant tissues

The measurement of K+ and Na+ was done by flame emission method (Atomic Emission Spectrometry) or (AES) as follows. First, the root and leaf tissue of the desired plant was removed and the samples were completely dried in the oven for 24 h at a temperature of 42 °C. Then, the samples were crushed and weighed to the amount of 2 g of each tissue and kept in the oven at a temperature of 550 °C for 5 h. Next, 41.4 ml of 37% hydrochloric acid was added with distilled water to a volume of 250 mL, and 20 mL of the desired solution was added to each plant tissue. Then, we placed the samples in a Bain-Marie for 20 min at a temperature of 80 °C. The solution obtained is transferred into a balloon and made up to 100 ml with distilled water. The series of standard solutions (for potassium and sodium), the extract of the control sample and the treatments were diluted in a ratio of 1 + 9 with sodium chloride solution. Then we measure the standard series along with the existing samples with a film photometer with a wavelength of 766.5 nm and using propane gas with air.

Measurement of phosphorus from plant tissue

Phosphorus was measured by Colorimetric method (molybdate vanadate yellow color). In short, 2 g of the dry weight of the plant sample (including roots, leaves, and stems) were placed in an oven with a temperature of 550 °C and dried. The organic matter of the plant is destroyed by controlled heat and all nitrogenous compounds are released as gas. 10 ml of 2 M hydrochloric acid was added to the samples and then they were placed in a Bain-Marie at 80 °C for 20 min. Then, the contents were transferred through a fine filter paper into a 100 ml volumetric flask, and then the solution was made up to volume with distilled water. In the next step, 5 ml of the extract solution was transferred to a 25 ml flask and 5 ml of ammonium molybdate-vanadate and 15 ml of sterile water were added to it. Then, the standard series along with the existing samples were read by a spectrophotometer with a wavelength of 470 nm.

Identification and characterization of PvKUP genes

In this study to identify gene family members KUP in bean (PvKUP), in the first, the known protein sequences of KUP gene family in Arabidopsis were applied against bean genome (version: PhaVulg1_0) using BLASTP tool (with the e-value set to 1 × 10− 5) of the Ensembl Plants database [30]. Then in the next step, the results were analyzed and the short sequences (< 150 aa) were removed. In the following, in order to confirm the conserved domains, sequences were considered by the Conserved Domain site Database (CDD) and Pfam. Also, other orthologs of PvKUP in rice, green peas as in the previous stage of identification and were extracted. The prediction of physicochemical properties such as molecular weight (MW), isoelectric point (pI), instability index and the grand average of hydropathy (GRAVY) value of PvKUP proteins was investigated using the ProtParam tool [31]. Besides, in order to estimate the phosphorylation modifications, PvKUP protein sequences were introduced to the NetPhos3.1 server site [32] and based on the presence of serine, threonine and tyrosine amino acids, the regions with a confidence percentage of more than 90% were identified and collected. 3D structure of PvKUPs were investigated by PHYRE2 site [33]. The models that were most similar to the PvKUP proteins were selected and analyzed. Also, their active ligand binding regions were identified and analyzed.

Evolution and phylogenetic tree of PvKUPs

To construct the phylogenetic tree, first, the sequences of PvKUP proteins with their homologs in Arabidopsis, rice and green peas were subjected to multiple alignment by Clustal Omega [34]. Multiple alignment results were introduced to IQTREE website [35] and Maximum Likelihood method with 1000 Bootstrap was used. Finally, the phylogenetic tree was drawn by the iTOL site [36]. One of the important topics in the study of genomic evolution is the investigation of duplication events. In this study, in order to identify duplicated genes, the CDS sequences of the members of PvKUP gene family were subjected to multiple alignment analysis using the ClustalW method of the Multiple GenomeNet site. Then, the results of the investigation and direction of the genes that were more than 70% similar were introduced as duplicated genes. In addition, Ka and Ks indices were calculated by TBtools software [37]. Finally, the separation time of gene doubling events with the formula MYB= (Ks/(2 × λ))×10− 6، λ = 6.5 × 10− 9 was calculated [38].

Promoter analysis of PvKUP genes

A region of 1500 nucleotides upstream of the start code of the PvKUP genes was isolated and analyzed as the promoter region. First, the promoter sequence of PvKUP genes was extracted from the Ensembl Plants database, and then the sequences were analyzed by the PlantCare database [39].

RNA extraction and qPCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from root and shoot tissues using Hot phenol protocol. The extracted RNA was used as a template to make cDNA with the help of the reverse transcriptase enzyme by reverse transcriptase kit (Yekta Tajhiz Azma Co., Tehran, Iran), based on the manufacturer’s instructions. Specific primers were designed using the Primer3 software (Supplementary Table 1). In the present study, five PvKUP genes were selected based on phylogenetic tree to analyze their expression levels. Real-time PCR analysis was used to quantitatively check gene expression and compare their expression in different treatments by ABI Step One using Ampliqon 2X qPCR Master Mix green-High Rox (Ampliqon, Stenhuggervej, Denmark), based on the manufacturer’s protocol. The conditions without treatment were be considered as a control to better examine the results. Relative expression levels of each gene were calculated using the delta-delta ct method [40]. All experiments were performed in three biological replicates.

Data analysis

Data was tested for Normality by the Anderson-Darling method. The average morphological properties and concentration of potassium, phosphorus and sodium in different organs (root and shoot) and gene expression was compared based on Tukey’s test (P-value < 0.05) by a two-way ANOVA. Minitab v.17 was used for performing the statistical tests.

Results

Morphological modifications in response to symbiosis with AMFs

The staining results of bean roots confirmed that symbiosis (approximately 80%) occurred and Rhizophagus irregularis and Funneliformis mosseae were cloned inside the roots (Supplementary Fig. 1). Mycorrhizal colonization rate was around 80% for Rhizophagus irregularis and 74% for Funneliformis mosseae. Examining the morphological characteristics of bean seedlings under symbiotic conditions revealed that F. mosseae and R. irregularis improved the growth under symbiotic conditions and salt stress (Fig. 1). The plant height in the AM seedlings showed an increase in both saline and normal stress conditions compared to non-AM conditions (Fig. 1). Also, stem diameter and number of lateral branches were increased under symbiosis with F. mosseae and R. irregularis. In addition, the AM fungi had an effect on the functional traits and caused an increase in the pod length of the bean fruit.

Phosphorus concentration

In this study, P changes in root and shoot tissues were investigated in response to salinity and AM symbiosis conditions. The results revealed that under normal conditions, symbiosis with F. mosseae and R. irregularis increases P transfer from root to shoots of bean plants (Fig. 2). Although the symbiosis with R. irregularis gave the beans more ability to absorb phosphorus. Besides this, the concentration of P in the bean roots also increased to some extent. Under soil salinity conditions, symbiosis with F. mosseae and R. irregularis increased the amount of P in both root and shoot tissues. The comparison between the concentration of P in the root between stress and normal conditions showed that AM plants under stress conditions has more obvious effects on phosphorus absorption.

Changes in K+ and Na+ concentration in plant tissues

The examination of K+ concentration showed that under the AM symbiosis conditions, the K+ absorption was increased. The highest concentration of K+ was observed in root tissues of seedlings with R. irregularis and the lowest under non-AM conditions in shoot tissues of seedlings grown in saline soil (Fig. 3a). In comparison between AMFs, R. irregularis showed a greater ability in availability of K+ than F. mosseae. The ratio of potassium to sodium concentration in root and shoot tissues was investigated. The results showed that this ratio is higher under normal soil conditions than saline conditions, and the highest ratio of potassium to sodium was recorded in the tissues (root and shoot) of R.irregularis symbiotic seedlings (Fig. 3b). In general, these results determined that mycorrhizal fungi not only increase K+ absorption but also decreases sodium absorption. Moreover, under the condition of soil salinity, AMF symbiosis has reduced sodium absorption.

Identification and physiochemical properties of PvKUP

In the bean genome, 19 KUP family members (PvKUPs) were identified and characterized. These genes differed from each other in terms of sequence length, gene structure and physicochemical properties (Table 1). The length of these proteins varied from 387 (PvKUP16) to 846 amino acids (PvKUP5) and the number of exons was between 8 and 10. Based on the GRAVY index, all PvKUPs were identified as hydrophobic proteins and about 74% of the members of this family were predicted as stable proteins. The pI value varied from 5.64 to 9.40, although most of the PvKUP proteins had a pI greater than 7.

Phylogenetic analysis of KUP family

Examining the evolutionary relationships of PvKUP genes revealed that these genes had an evolutionary process close to their orthologs in green peas (genome version: v1a) (Fig. 4). Based on the phylogenetic tree, PvKUPs were separated into five groups, only PvKUP3 was in the group 1 and the largest number (10 members) was observed in group 5. According to the phylogenetic results, rice KUPs had a greater distance than dicot orthologs.

Duplication events in PvKUP family

The location of the genes showed that 19 PvKUP family members are non-uniformly distributed on seven bean chromosomes (Fig. 5). Most of the PvKUP genes were observed on chromosome 2 (seven genes) and chromosome 9 (5 genes). The results of the investigation of duplication events showed that duplications occurred between some members of this family during evolution (Fig. 5; Table 2). Eight doubling events were predicted, 6 of which were segmental and two were tandem duplication. Considering that the ka/ks ratio is less than one, as a result, all doublings have had a negative selection effect. Based on sequence changes, the first duplication between members of this family was estimated to be about 53 million years ago.

Promoter site analysis

Examining the promoter regions of PvKUP genes showed that these genes differ from each other based on the distribution of cis-regulatory elements (Fig. 6a). Among them, the highest frequency was related to cis-element Box 4 and MYB transcription factors. Also, it is known to be richer in having cis-elements among the PvKUP15, PvKUP18, PvKUP3 and PvKUP9. The grouping of cis-regulatory elements based on their function revealed that in the promoter of PvKUP genes there are more light and growth response elements (46%) and stress response elements (36%) (Fig. 6b). Also, among the hormone response cis-elements, ABA, MeJA and SA response elements are the most abundant in the upstream region of PvKUP genes around 28%, 25%, and 23%, respectively (Fig. 6c).

Pocket site and phosphorylation region analysis

The three-dimensional structure prediction of PvKUP proteins revealed that these proteins have differences in their structure and binding regions (pocket site) (Supplementary Fig. 2). For example, proteins PvKUP6, PvKUP8, PvKUP12 and PvKUP19 had similar structures and proteins PvKUP3, PvKUP4 and PvKUP14 were similar. These structural differences can affect their performance. The results of the analysis of the binding regions of PvKUP proteins showed that the amino acids leucine (LEU), isoleucine (ILE), alanine (ALA), valine (VAL), serine (SER), and glycine (GLY) are the most abundant in these regions (Fig. 7a), and as a result, they play a prominent role in the function of PvKUP proteins.

Frequency of each amino acid presented in pocket site of PvKUP proteins (a), and frequency of potential phosphorylation site of PvKUP proteins (b)

Phosphorylation potential regions in PvKUP proteins were analyzed and it was found that PvKUP family members differ from each other based on post-translational phosphorylation modifications (Fig. 7b). Proteins belonging to the same phylogenetic group were not similar in the number of phosphorylation sites. The highest phosphorylation potential was estimated in PvKUP1 and the lowest in PvKUP6 (Fig. 7b).

Expression profile of PvKUP genes

The study of gene expression under AM symbiosis (F. mosseae and R. irregularis) highlighted the tissue-specificity of PvKUP genes (Fig. 8). In the shoot tissue of seedlings engaged in a symbiotic relationship with the AMFs, there was an elevation in transcript levels of PvKUP3 more than 9-fold, contrasted with an increase in its expression specifically within root tissues associated with R. irregularis. Furthermore, the levels of PvKUP5 expression increased in shoot tissues under AMF symbiosis, but decreased in roots. The presence of R. irregularis in a symbiotic state led to an increase in the expression of PvKUP11 in shoot tissues, while no changes were observed when F. mosseae was involved. Conversely, the transcript levels of PvKUP11 decreased in roots. There was no discernible difference in the expression levels of the PvKUP16 gene in the shoots, showing only a minor decrease in expression. In the presence of symbiosis with R. irregularis, PvKUP16 gene exhibited elevated expression levels within the root tissues. The transcription level of PvKUP18 gene increased in the shoots of seedlings symbiotic with R. irregularis and in the roots of seedlings symbiotic with F. mosseae.

Discussion

Potassium is one of the macronutrient elements that significantly affects the growth and development of plants. K+ ions are present in high levels in the soil, but due to strong mineral uptake, they are too low for the roots [19, 41]. Consequently, there is a need to develop efficient strategies to improve K+ uptake from soil for plants. A variety of transporter and carrier systems have been developed in plants to absorb and transport ions [42]. Besides, formation of root symbiosis with arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi is one of the most efficient methods [20]. In this study, F. mosseae and R. irregularis, showed symbiosis with bean roots. The results of this symbiosis have increased the absorption of phosphorus and potassium in the roots and transfer to the shoots. Besides, morphological traits including plant height, stem diameter, pod length and number of side branches were increased in response to AMF symbiosis. It has been reported that AMF increases the absorption of mineral and nutrient elements, thus increasing plant biomass in Apple, Maize, and White Clover [43–45]. AMFs affect the absorption of nitrogen and some other elements such as calcium, and according to the role of nitrogen, plant growth increases [46, 47]. It has also been found that the concentration of K+ in corn plants symbiotically with AMF increased as a result of increased plant growth [41]. Also, the increase in K+ has an effect on the metabolism of aromatic amino acids, which increases the plant’s resistance to stress [41]. In this study, the concentration of phosphorus under soil salinity stress in root and shoot tissues was significantly increased compared to the control. Indeed, in addition to influencing phosphorus solubility, AMF appears to have affected phosphorus transport systems, leading to an increase in its concentration within aerial organs. Most studies have mainly focused on the role of phosphate (Pi) nutrition in AM symbiosis, and it has been found that the mycorrhizal-dependent pathway is key for Pi uptake in roots [48, 49].

Limited studies have investigated the effects of AMF on potassium absorption. Previous studies have shown that K+ plays an important role in enzyme activation, membrane transport, osmotic regulation as well as protein synthesis, including starch, cellulose and vitamins in plants [50]. In addition, K+ also participates in the opening of stomata and helps the plant adapt to water stress [51]. In this study, the concentration of K+ in the root and stem tissues of beans under the symbiosis with both AMF increased, and the ratio of potassium to sodium was also increased by AMF. Among the mechanisms involved in salinity tolerance in plants inoculated with AMF, the reduction of sodium ion absorption [52] and ionic homeostasis have been mentioned [52, 53]. In another study, plant root colonization with AMF reduced Na+ concentration and increased the activity of various enzymes related to reducing salinity stress [54]. Under salt stress conditions, AMF inoculation significantly can reduce oxidative damage in wheat plants [55]. It has also been reported that symbiosis with F. mosseae and R. irregularis increases potassium concentration in plant tissues and as a result drought damage is reduced [56]. We observed that the symbiosis with R. irregularis has a greater effect on the absorption of phosphorus and potassium elements and the reduction of sodium absorption in beans than the symbiosis with F. mosseae. Probably, the type of plant, variety and soil conditions are the factors that affect the efficiency of AMF symbiosis.

In plants, there are specialized transport systems for absorbing K + and for its internal distribution. Among the potassium transporters, KUP plays an important role in intracellular K+ accumulation and maintaining the growth and development of plant species [10, 57]. In the present study, 19 KUP family members (PvKUPs) were identified and characterized in the genome of common bean. Compared to other crops such as wheat (with 56 KUP members), corn (with 27 KUP members) and rice (with 27 KUP members) [12], fewer members were identified in beans. The number of gene family members in plants is more dependent on the ploidy level and doubling events [58]. Eight doubling events were predicted between PvKUP family members, suggesting that evolution pressures have caused the expansion of this plant gene family. Besides, mutations occurring in the regulatory regions upstream of genes, as well as within coding regions, can impact how newly introduced members are expressed and function [59, 60]. Based on physicochemical characteristics and genetic structure, differences between PvKUPs were observed. Evolutionary pressures and changes in the sequence can be the origin of this diversity. Besides this, the results of the phylogenetic tree suggested that the diversity in the members of this gene family occurred more after the derivation of dicots and monocots. The promoter analysis of PvKUP genes determined that there are differences based on the frequency of cis-regulatory elements between the members and in the upstream regions of these genes regulatory elements of response to light and growth are more distributed. Also, the regulatory elements of response to ABA, MeJA and SA are mostly identified in the upstream regions of PvKUP genes, which indicates the involvement of these genes in cellular responses related to abiotic and biotic stresses. Gene expression pattern analysis showed that PvKUPs have both tissue-specific expression and some have general expressions. For example, PvKUP16 was expressed only in root cells inoculated with R.irregularis, while PvKUP18 showed increased expression in both root tissues and aerial organs. It has been reported that HAK10, as a KT/KUP/HAK, only in root cells inoculated with R.irregularis [22]. Also, in barley plant, the difference in the expression pattern between KUPs in different tissues has been reported [61]. This difference in the expression pattern related to the regulatory systems in the promoter regions of the genes can be related, as a result, the upstream factors have different effects on the expression of the members of this family.

Conclusion

In this study, we investigate the symbiotic relationship between arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi and common beans, focusing on their effect on potassium absorption and transferring. Our results disclosed that AMFs improve the availability and absorbing K+ and P and increase the seedlings performance. Of the two types of AMF, the inoculation with R. irregularis was the most effective in the salinity tolerance and nutrient absorption of the beans. In addition, we found that 19 members of PvKUP family in beans with diverse structure. The results demonstrated that expression levels of PvKUP are affected by AMFs and tissue-specific expression was observed for them. In general, the symbiosis of AMFs caused an increase in the accumulation of potassium ions in the bean tissues, which resulted in an effect on ion availability and transporter activity.
