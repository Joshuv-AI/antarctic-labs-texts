RETRACTED ARTICLE: An intelligent learning system based on electronic health records for unbiased stroke prediction
Authors: Muhammad Asim Saleem, Ashir Javeed, Wasan Akarathanawat, Aurauma Chutinet, Nijasri Charnnarong Suwanwela, Pasu Kaewplung, Surachai Chaitusaney, Sunchai Deelertpaiboon, Wattanasak Srisiri, Watit Benjapolakul
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Stroke has a negative impact on people’s lives and is one of the leading causes of death and disability worldwide. Early detection of symptoms can significantly help predict stroke and promote a healthy lifestyle. Researchers have developed several methods to predict strokes using machine learning (ML) techniques. However, the proposed systems have suffered from the following two main problems. The first problem is that the machine learning models are biased due to the uneven distribution of classes in the dataset. Recent research has not adequately addressed this problem, and no preventive measures have been taken. Synthetic Minority Oversampling (SMOTE) has been used to remove bias and balance the training of the proposed ML model. The second problem is to solve the problem of lower classification accuracy of machine learning models. We proposed a learning system that combines an autoencoder with a linear discriminant analysis (LDA) model to increase the accuracy of the proposed ML model for stroke prediction. Relevant features are extracted from the feature space using the autoencoder, and the extracted subset is then fed into the LDA model for stroke classification. The hyperparameters of the LDA model are found using a grid search strategy. However, the conventional accuracy metric does not truly reflect the performance of ML models. Therefore, we employed several evaluation metrics to validate the efficiency of the proposed model. Consequently, we evaluated the proposed model’s accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, area under the curve (AUC), and receiver operator characteristic (ROC). The experimental results show that the proposed model achieves a sensitivity and specificity of 98.51% and 97.56%, respectively, with an accuracy of 99.24% and a balanced accuracy of 98.00%.

Introduction

Background

A stroke occurs when the blood supply to various parts of the brain is interrupted or reduced, depriving the brain cells of the nutrients and oxygen they need to survive. A cerebrovascular event, commonly referred to as a stroke, is a critical medical condition that requires immediate medical attention. Timely recognition and administration of appropriate medical interventions can prevent damage to the affected brain region and mitigate the associated systemic complications. According to the World Health Organization (WHO)1, there are an estimated 15 million strokes worldwide each year, with one death occurring every four to five minutes. According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), stroke is the sixth leading cause of death in the United States2. Stroke, which is classified as a non-communicable disease, is responsible for about 11% of all deaths in the United States. According to a WHO study, stroke leads to permanent disability in about 795,000 people in the United States3. In India, it is the fourth leading cause of death. An estimated 70 million people have suffered a stroke, resulting in a loss of about 200 million years of life4. Ischemic or non-hemorrhagic strokes (NHS) and hemorrhagic strokes (HS) are the two main types of stroke5 as shown in Fig. 1. In an ischemic stroke, clots form and block the outflow, while in a hemorrhagic stroke, a weak blood vessel bursts and bleeds into the brain.Fig. 1Main types of Strokes.

Main types of Strokes.

Adopting a healthy lifestyle can reduce the risk of a stroke. This includes avoiding smoking and excessive alcohol consumption, maintaining a healthy BMI and blood sugar level, and taking care of your heart and kidneys.

Motivation

Predicting a stroke is crucial. Timely treatment is necessary to prevent permanent disability or death. With advances in medical technology, it is possible to predict the time of a stroke using machine learning methods. Machine learning algorithms are useful because they enable reliable predictions through research. The prediction of cardiac events has been the focus of most stroke studies to date. Creating annotated medical records has allowed us to recognize patterns in the dataset using data mining techniques. These analyses have helped medical professionals make more accurate diagnoses for various diseases. This has led to better health outcomes and lower treatment costs. The implementation of data mining strategies in healthcare databases can have a significant impact on healthcare and biomedicine6,7. This allows doctors to recognize their patients’ first signs of disease earlier. The topic of stroke is particularly interesting to us, and one of our goals is to identify the most critical risk factors. Early detection of people at risk of stroke can help to prevent or minimize the consequences of a stroke8.

Machine learning (ML) algorithms for classification have shown promise in predicting stroke risk by analyzing medical data. Studies have shown that the number of strokes depends on age, socioeconomic status, access to healthcare, and medical practice. Although ML reduces the likelihood of bias in analyses because fewer people are involved, selection bias can still occur because large academic institutions usually conduct ML-based research with experts in biostatistics and informatics. It has been said that the quality and generalizability of machine learning algorithms are highly dependent on the quality of the dataset and how relevant it is to the population for which these clinical tools are to be used9. However, no research attempts to analyze the patient’s condition and identify the key features required for stroke prediction. In this work, the various patient data are systematically investigated to improve stroke prediction

Aim of study

Stroke occurrence can be predicted using models developed with machine learning10–12. We analyze a public dataset consisting of 5110 rows and 12 columns of patient data to determine the factors for stroke prediction. This study addresses two main issues, the resulting development of biased algorithm learning models and the reduced effectiveness of stroke prediction. To achieve this goal, logistic regression (LR), Gaussian Naive Bayes (GNB), K Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA), and Decision Tree (DT) models were developed and trained for the study. This study aims to discuss the problem of bias in the ML models and to find out how to deal with an unbalanced class distribution in the data using SMOTE. This study also proposes a new hybrid learning model consisting of an autoencoder and improved LDA models to solve the problem of low accuracy in stroke prediction. The study attempts to answer the following research questions to achieve these objectives.What are the best measures to deal with unbalanced data sets to reduce bias in stroke prediction models?What is the impact of SMOTE on the performance and accuracy of machine and deep learning datasets in stroke prediction?Can the hybrid model of autoencoders and LDAs improve the accuracy of stroke prediction?.

Related work

The scientific community has expressed a strong interest in developing tools and techniques for monitoring and predicting diseases that significantly affect human health. In today’s world of digitalization, healthcare has become a fundamental field within the medical discipline. In order to gain valuable insights and facilitate disease prediction, healthcare systems need to analyze large amounts of patient data13. This section provides a comprehensive overview of several studies that have shown promising results in applying machine learning classification algorithms to predict stroke risk.

Using ML methods14, a model is developed that can predict future events. The authors have developed a prototype for stroke classification using text mining tools and machine learning algorithms15. The automatic detection and categorization of strokes using this approach could support the early diagnosis and treatment of strokes.

A machine learning (ML) model to select patients at high risk of developing atrial fibrillation after acute ischemic stroke was created and compared16. To determine who should receive advanced cardiac monitoring after an AIS event, they compared ML models with the standard logistic regression model and traditional risk scores such as the CHADS-2 score, CHA2DS-VASc score, AS5F score, and HAVOC score. Using machine learning, the authors of17 automated and improved SICH prediction after thrombolysis by analyzing CT images of acute strokes. Clinical data from 116 individuals and CT scans of the brain were used to diagnose 16 cases of SICH. Predictive tools such as SEDAN and HAT were compared with the Support Vector Machine, where predictive performance was calculated using the area under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve.

In18, three different machine learning models were used, the support vector machine, the random forest, and the artificial neural network. They also evaluated the performance of a hybrid artificial neural network by 10-fold cross-validation and repetition. The SVM algorithm achieved the best performance for the ischemic stroke dataset with an f1 score of 87.8. The algorithm used preclinical and in-hospital data as feature inputs. Singh and Choudhary (2017) used multiple techniques and performed a comparative analysis with the decision tree method to predict the occurrence of stroke using the Cardiovascular Health Study (CHS)19. The investigated method has an accuracy of 97.7%, outperforming other established stroke prediction methods.

In addition, the authors investigated20 the use of predictive analytics techniques for stroke prediction using deep learning models applied to heart disease datasets. In21, the authors discussed the application of deep neural networks (DNNs) to a large-scale electronic health database for stroke risk estimation. The aim is to evaluate and validate the performance of DNNs in predicting ischemic stroke within 3 years of study inclusion. For this purpose, two test datasets with 11 million records from 840 thousand patients are used. The authors in22 used the Cardiovascular Health Study dataset to evaluate two stroke prediction methods: the Cox proportional hazards model and a machine learning technique (CHS). In conjunction with support vector machines, our proposed heuristic conservative means for automatic feature selection are used in an automatic feature selection algorithm (SVMs). Deep learning techniques such as deep neural networks (DNNs) are used in23 to predict the occurrence of strokes in a population-based database of electronic medical records (EMCs). The results show that DNNs and gradient-boosting decision trees (GBDTs) can make more accurate predictions than logistic regression and support vector machines on smaller patient data.

The different techniques show that many different variables can influence the results of a study. The results of a study can be influenced by several factors, such as the approach used to collect the data, the characteristics selected, the technique used to clean the data, the method used to fill in missing values, the degree of randomness and standardization of the data, and the degree of randomness in the data. Researchers need to understand the dependencies between the input variables in an electronic patient record and their impact on the accuracy of the final stroke prediction.

The authors of24 physiological parameters as risk factors for predicting the occurrence of a stroke. They found that the linear kernel of the Support Vector Machine (SVM) gave an accuracy of 90%.

Furthermore, in25, Classification algorithms were used to predict stroke disease, and principal component analysis was used to reduce the dimensions to determine the attributes that play a role in predicting the presence of stroke disease. Accuracy is measured by sensitivity and specificity, but no numerical values are given. In26, the authors presented a prediction model for living environments. They used machine learning and ensemble machine learning methods to suggest the most suitable living environment for individuals based on characteristic properties and features. The proposed model uses data collected through a questionnaire that considers people’s desires and economic opportunities and then calculates people’s quality of life indices. The SVM_LMT achieved its highest accuracy values through the 80.0% split method. According to the study conducted by27, the stack classification approach proved to be more effective than other methods, with an AUC of 98.9% and an F-measure, precision, and recall rate of 97.4%. The accuracy rate was 98.0%. The authors also suggested a strategy for evaluating social media for indicators of stroke symptoms and precautions that could help in the early detection of risk factors.

In addition,28 deals with applying various machine learning methods for predicting brain strokes based on physiological parameters. Of all the algorithms tested, the Naive Bayes classification proved to be the most accurate, with a hit rate of 82%.

Finally, in29, a contribution to existing research was made by proposing a stroke prediction algorithm (SPN) that uses an improvised random forest technique to analyze risk factors associated with different types of strokes. It also improves the prediction accuracy by up to 96.97% compared to other models. This facilitates stroke diagnosis and treatment planning.

All of the above studies used machine learning models and an electronic dataset to predict stroke. Some studies achieved excellent accuracy in stroke prediction, such as24,27–29. However, accuracy is not the only standard measure for validating machine learning models’ effectiveness, especially in automated diagnosis systems. Stroke is a rare event, and the majority of the sample in the dataset consists of healthy individuals; therefore, the participation of healthy individuals in the dataset will be larger than that of individuals suffering from stroke. This leads to a bias problem in the developed machine learning model. As a result, the actual behavior of the machine learning model cannot be determined based on accuracy alone. All the research mentioned above papers do not address this problem. To solve this problem, we used several evaluation metrics for the statistical analysis of our proposed model, such as equilibrium accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and Matthew correlation coefficient. To avoid the problem of machine learning model bias, we also used the synthetic minority oversampling (SMOTE) method to equalize the dataset classes in the training dataset while the constructed model was tested on the original test data.

Proposed work

This study used data from electronic health records (EHR) to develop an intelligent learning system for stroke prediction. Three autoencoder algorithms were used to evaluate the effectiveness of the machine-learning model. The learning system was improved by integrating the synthetic minority oversampling technique (SMOTE) method and linear discriminant analysis (LDA) into the model. An autoencoder extracts only the relevant features to minimize the risk of the proposed model picking up irrelevant or noisy data from the feature space. This could lead to overfitting of the machine learning model. The LDA model is a predictive model for determining individual stroke risk. In addition, SMOTE is used to account for unbalanced classes in the dataset. There are two different components in an autoencoder - the encoder and the decoder. The encoder extracts features from the condensed dataset while the decoder reconstructs the original data. The simplest form of autoencoder is a feedforward neural network consisting of a single-layer perceptron and a multilayer perceptron function (MLP). The MLP is characterized by having one or more hidden layers between the input and output layers of the neural network. The number of neurons in the input and output layers remains the same in order to minimize the loss of information from the original dataset. Autoencoders use unsupervised learning techniques to achieve their goals30. Information loss refers to the minimization of autoencoder reconstruction errors in autoencoders. The input and output layers have an equal number of neurons to minimize the loss of information from the original dataset. These models use unsupervised learning techniques to achieve their goals. In this process, the error is propagated backward, similar to other feedforward neural networks, where Z represents the average of the training set.

After feature extraction, the data is partitioned so that the proposed prediction models can be trained and tested. SMOTE was used to avoid bias in the learning model presented for objective stroke prediction. The SMOTE technique generates synthetic instances of the underrepresented class, resulting in a balanced distribution of classes in the dataset. The considered learning system is designed to be trained on balanced data to reduce the potential for bias in the ML model that may result from an unbalanced class distribution in the training dataset.

After data partitioning, SMOTE is used to balance the classes in the training dataset. However, it is important to know that using samples from the test dataset before data partitioning can lead to a bias in the ML model. There are different oversampling methods, but SMOTE works in feature space, not data space. It generates new samples from a starting point in the minority class31,32. These new samples are created by following the line connecting the k nearest neighbors of the minority class. Through this process, the minority class samples are synthesized (i.e., oversampled). The Fig. 2 shows the distribution of minority and majority classes in the dataset before and after using SMOTE. To apply the SMOTE approach in this study, we use the Python module “imbalanced learn”.

Conclusions

In this study, we have highlighted the problems of lower accuracy and bias of machine learning models for stroke prediction caused by unbalanced classes in the dataset. Experiments show that machine learning models trained on imbalanced data are biased towards the class with the best performing data. Consequently, we discovered high specificity but low sensitivity for the stroke detection problem because the stroke patient class was in the minority. In contrast, the class of healthy subjects was in the majority. We developed a state-of-the-art stroke diagnosis method to solve the problem of bias. The proposed model uses the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) to solve the problem of unbalanced class distribution in the dataset. The proposed model comprises three hybridized components that function as a learning system. First, the features of the dataset are extracted to reduce the dimensionality of the data. This improves the accuracy of stroke prediction while reducing the computational cost of the proposed approach. In the second part of the proposed model, we used an LDA classifier to perform a classifier function. The grid search technique determines the hyperparameters of the LDA model. Compared to a conventional LDA model, the proposed system improved the performance by 2%. Although the proposed model uses fewer features compared to the conventional LDA model, it is less complex in time than the LDA model.
