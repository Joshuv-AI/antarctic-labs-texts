Kawasaki Disease Accompanied by Acute Generalized Exanthematous Pustulosis
Authors: Wen Li, Qi Jia, Shu Teng, Huaping Wang, Zhenghong Qi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAcute generalized exanthematous pustulosis (AGEP) is a rare but serious adverse skin reaction usually caused by drugs. We reported a 4‐year‐old boy diagnosed with Kawasaki disease (KD) accompanied by AGEP who presented with typical symptoms of KD, including fever, scarlet‐like rash, swollen extremities, cervical lymph node swelling, conjunctival congestion, strawberry tongue, and cracked red lips on admission. On Day 5 of illness, a pustular eruption occurred and the patient was subsequently diagnosed with AGEP. After a double dose of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) and treatment with methylprednisolone and aspirin, the patient recovered and had no cardiovascular complications. KD and AGEP can coexist, and pustular rash is not always a cutaneous manifestation of Kawasaki disease.

Introduction

Kawasaki disease (KD), also known as acute febrile mucocutaneous lymph node syndrome, is an acute systemic vasculitis disease that predominantly occurs in children aged < 5 years [1]. Up to 25% of untreated children develop coronary artery aneurysms, emphasizing the importance of timely diagnosis and treatment [1]. Exanthema in patients with KD most commonly manifests as a diffuse and nonspecific erythematous maculopapular eruption described as morbilliform, scarlatiniform, or urticarial in appearance [2].

Acute generalized exanthematous pustulosis (AGEP) is a rare and serious cutaneous drug reaction with an incidence of 1–5 per million per year [3, 4]. It is a self‐limited disease generally attributed to medications, especially antibiotics [3, 4]. AGEP is characterized by the rapid onset of numerous non‐follicular sterile pustules against a background of systemic edematous erythema, typically accompanied by fever and leukocytosis [4].

Here, we report a rare case of KD presenting initially with a scarlatiniform rash followed by AGEP, to emphasize that KD may coexist with AGEP and that pustules may not be a cutaneous manifestation of KD.

Case History/Examination

A 4‐year‐old previously healthy boy was admitted to our hospital with a 3‐day history of fever and rashes. A sandpaper‐like rash had appeared on his abdomen and spread throughout his body. After 2 h of fever, cefixime was administered orally once. He was then diagnosed with scarlet fever and treated with amoxicillin‐clavulanate potassium for 3 days (1 day of oral and 2 days of intravenous treatment) without improvement. He had also developed edema of the hands and feet the previous day, followed by redness of his eyes on the day of admission. The patient's family history was unremarkable. The patient has previously taken Amoxicillin Clavulanate Potassium orally for an illness without experiencing any allergic reactions.

Physical examination revealed a temperature of 38.2°C, blood pressure of 106/51 mmHg, heart rate of 140 beats per minute, and respiratory rate of 28 breaths per minute. Diffuse blanching erythema with punctate papules was observed, mainly on the trunk and limbs, and several enlarged cervical lymph nodes were palpated (1.5 × 0.7 cm). Additionally, he had a strawberry tongue, circumoral pallor, cracked red lips, bilateral bulbar nonsuppurative conjunctival injection, and edema of the hands and feet. No additional abnormalities were found.

His laboratory results revealed a white blood cell (WBC) count of 16.7 × 109/L (normal, 4.4–11.9) (neutrophils 84.7%, eosinophils 6.0%), a hemoglobin concentration of 138 g/L (normal, 112–149), a platelet count of 228 × 109/L (normal, 188–472), and a C‐reactive protein level of 58.78 mg/L (normal, 0–10). On the 7th day of the disease, the eosinophil count reached 18.8%, and the eosinophil count was 2.47 × 109/L (normal, 0–0.68). The alanine aminotransferase and aspartate aminotransferase levels were 265 U/L (normal, 7–30) and 87 U/L (normal, 14–44), respectively. Immunoglobulin E level was 1105 IU/mL (normal, 0–60). Cytokines revealed elevated levels of interleukin (IL)‐5 (76.14 pg/mL, normal, 0–3.1), IL‐6 (130.58 pg/mL, normal, 0–5.4), IL‐8 (15.13 pg/mL, normal, 0–20.6), IL‐10 (24.37 pg/mL, normal, 0–12.9) and IL‐17 (72.17 pg/mL, normal, 0–21.4). The patient exhibited a urinary WBC count of 40–50 cells/high‐power field. Blood, urine, throat swab, and skin pustule cultures were negative. No specific pathogens responsible for the infection were identified by targeted next‐generation sequencing of the blood and throat swabs, which are responsible for infections. On Days 2 and 9 of hospitalization, echocardiography revealed no evidence of coronary artery dilatation.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

A clinical diagnosis of KD was made, and amoxicillin‐clavulanate potassium was discontinued. The patient was subsequently treated with aspirin and intravenous immunoglobulins (IVIG). Within 1 day of receiving IVIG, his symptoms, including conjunctival congestion and edema of the hands and feet, resolved. However, the patient's fever did not improve. Meanwhile, the morphology of the rash underwent a transformation, manifesting as the rapid emergence of white pustules on the abdomen, groin, and extremities. On Day 4 of hospitalization, during the peak period of the pustular rash (Figure 1a), there was also a notable occurrence of hand and foot desquamation (Figure 1b,c), in parallel with a significant increase in eosinophils, reaching a maximum of 2.47 × 109/L (normal,0–0.68). AGEP was diagnosed based on the validated score set by the EuroSCAR group [4]. The patient was simultaneously diagnosed with IVIG‐refractory KD and AGEP, and then received a second dose of IVIG combined with intravenous methylprednisolone (1.36 mg/kg, q12h).

(a) Pustular rash with erythematous background on the arm on the 7th day of onset. (b) Hand peeling that occurred on the same day. (c) Foot peeling that occurred on the same day.

After the second dose of IVIG and intravenous methylprednisolone treatment, the fever resolved within a day, and the pustular eruption improved, followed by desquamation of the affected skin, mainly in the groin and limbs. The patient was discharged on Day 10 of hospitalization. Oral methylprednisolone tablets were administered after discharge and discontinued on Day 15 of outpatient follow‐up. Repeat echocardiograms during the outpatient visits at 4 and 6 weeks remained normal, and aspirin was discontinued.

Conclusion and Results

Herein, we report the case of a 4‐year‐old boy who presented with fever and rash for three days and was finally diagnosed with KD combined with AGEP. Although AGEP has a favorable prognosis, there is a possibility of recurrence with future exposure to the same drug. Therefore, the possibility of AGEP should also be considered when pustular herpes occurs in KD, and if possible, a patch test can be performed to identify the drugs causing AGEP to avoid disease recurrence. It is regrettable that a skin biopsy was not performed.

Discussion

The exanthema observed in KD rarely manifests as erythema multiforme, pustular, or psoriasis‐like. There have been two previous reports of pustular eruptions occurring simultaneously with other symptoms of KD [5, 6]. Therefore, pustular eruption can be considered a skin manifestation of KD in the form of a rash. Bilateral bulbar non‐exudative conjunctivitis in KD usually occurs shortly after fever onset [1], which is consistent with the fact that conjunctival congestion in our patient also occurred shortly after fever onset, accompanied by a strawberry tongue and cracked red lips. Mucosal involvement only occurs in 20% of AGEP cases [4]. The patient's mucosal involvement occurred three days before the onset of pustules; therefore, it is believed that this patient's mucosal involvement was unrelated to AGEP. Both KD and AGEP involve desquamation, but the desquamation of KD starts from the extremities and occurs during the recovery phase, while AGEP starts from the lesion. The simultaneous occurrence of extremity desquamation and generalized pustular eruptions in this patient cannot be explained solely by KD.

AGEP is a rare but serious skin reaction typically caused by medications. Of note, certain medications have a higher epidemiological risk than others, including pristinamycin, ampicillin, amoxicillin, quinolones, hydroxychloroquine, sulfonamides, terbinafine, and diltiazem [7]. Cefixime‐induced AGEP is rare, with only a few cases having a latency period of 24–48 h [8]. The patient in our case did not undergo patch testing or lymphocyte stimulation testing to determine which drug caused the AGEP. However, based on the drug risk and onset time of the rash, we speculate that AGEP was associated with the use of amoxicillin and potassium clavulanate.

AGEP is a drug‐induced, delayed‐type hypersensitivity reaction mediated by T lymphocytes. Up to 30% of patients with AGEP have elevated eosinophils [4]. During the course of the disease, the eosinophil count peaked at 2.47 × 109/L. Such a marked elevation in eosinophils is uncommon in patients with KD. IL‐5 is a potent eosinophil stimulator and its levels in this patient were significantly elevated and were accompanied by a significant increase in the number of eosinophils, indicating that IL‐5‐producing Th2 cells were involved in the pathogenic process. The increase in IL‐8 in AGEP plays an important role in its pathogenesis, but our patient in this case did not have high levels of IL‐8. We hypothesized that this may be due to the decline in CD8+ counts in patients with KD and the early timing of the test.
