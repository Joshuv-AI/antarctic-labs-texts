Global, regional, and national temporal trend in burden of chronic respiratory diseases from 1990 to 2021: Findings from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021
Authors: Zifeng Ma, Bing Li, Yuheng Qian, Shuyi Mu, Yu Wang, Jie Cui, Lei Qiu, Jinxin Zheng, Yan Li, Fuqiang Wen, Zhenhui Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Chronic respiratory diseases (CRDs), including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), asthma, pneumoconiosis, interstitial lung disease (ILD), and pulmonary sarcoidosis, continue to pose a significant global health challenge. This study aims to assess the global, regional, and national burden of CRDs from 1990 to 2021, using data from the Global Burden of Disease Study (GBD) 2021 to identify key trends and advise future public health strategies.Methods:Data from the GBD 2021 were used to estimate the incidence, prevalence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) of CRDs and their subtypes across 21 global regions and 204 countries and regions from 1990 to 2021. The temporal trend was analyzed and the relationship between CRD burden and socio-demographic index (SDI) was examined using a smoothing spline model. A Bayesian age-period-cohort model was used to project the burden of CRDs to 2035.Results:The global age-standardized rate (ASR) of incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALY for CRDs declined by 24%, 27%, 37%, and 38%, respectively, from 1990 to 2021. However, the absolute burden of CRDs continues to grow due to population aging and demographic changes. Asthma accounts for most of the CRD-related incidence and prevalence, particularly in high-SDI regions, while COPD accounts for most CRD-related deaths and DALYs, especially in low-middle SDI regions. The ASR of incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs for ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis increased by 21%, 9%, 50%, and 28%, respectively, from 1990 to 2021. Notably, the increase was more pronounced in high SDI regions. Although air pollution decreased globally and in high-SDI, it slightly worsened in low-SDI regions. Since 2009, tobacco use has surpassed air pollution as the leading risk factor for CRD-related deaths and DALYs in high-middle SDI regions, although both are declining. In contrast, the increase in DALYs is because high body mass index (BMI) signals a change in risk factors for CRDs. By 2035, the incidence, prevalence, and mortality rates of CRD are anticipated to decline.Conclusions:The incidence of CRDs continues to represent a significant public health challenge, with variable trends in different regions influenced by a complex interaction of risk factors. Although progress has been made in reducing mortality and DALYs, particularly in higher SDI regions, the continued high incidence in low- and middle-SDI regions underscores the need for sustained and targeted public health efforts. Therefore, it is essential to address the underlying determinants, including environmental, lifestyle, and occupational risks to mitigate the global impact of CRDs in the coming decades.

Introduction

Chronic respiratory diseases (CRDs), including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), pneumoconiosis, asthma, interstitial lung disease (ILD), and pulmonary sarcoidosis, represent a significant public health challenge due to their long-term impact on airway and lung function.[1–3] These conditions not only reduce patients’ quality of life but also impose significant burdens on global health systems and economic development. Despite advances in the prevention and treatment strategies, CRDs remain the main cause of mortality worldwide, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).[4–7]

CRDs ranked third as the leading cause of death globally in 2017 and 2019, highlighting the significant burden these diseases place on the healthcare systems.[1,2,8,9] An aging population, air pollution, and persistent smoking have exacerbated the burden of CRDs over the past decade.[10,11] Our understanding of disease burden as global health research advances has moved from estimating prevalence and incidence to a more comprehensive multidimensional analysis.[12] CRDs are a significant global health challenge that requires in-depth studies of their epidemiological trends, burden, and influencing factors. Such research is valuable for the development of effective CRD prevention and control strategies, as well as informing policies for tackling health disparities.

This study presents a comprehensive assessment of key indicators of CRDs including incidence, prevalence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), at global, regional, and national levels from 1990 to 2021 based on the data from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021 (GBD 2021). The analyses examined temporal trends in disease burden and identified the main influencing factors of the disease burden. The comparison of disease burden across different time periods and geographical regions could highlight the global progress and the ongoing challenges in addressing CRDs, consequently guiding global and local policies toward better control of these conditions.

Methods

This study used the Disease Modeling-Bayesian meta-regression tool (DisModMR, version 2.1, Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation, University of Washington, Seattle, USA), integrating Bayesian priors with regularization and trimming (MR-BRT) modeling.[13] Mortality data were obtained mainly from national vital registration systems, maternal and child health surveillance networks, and census records. Incidence and prevalence data were obtained from disease surveillance systems, national health surveys, and published studies, while the burden of disability was estimated using case notifications, hospital discharge databases, household surveys, and cohort studies.[3,11] Comprehensive details regarding study design, data collection, and estimation methods can be found in the GBD 2021 documentation.

Data source

The GBD 2021 study represents a comprehensive international effort to estimate the incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs for 371 diseases and injuries, as well as 88 risk factors in 204 countries and territories from 1990 to 2021, stratified by age and sex. GBD 2021 was built on previous iterations of the GBD study and has updated its data sources, estimation models, and statistical analysis methods. This study adheres to the Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Health Estimates Reporting (GATHER), offering an improved interpretation of increased data availability compared to GBD 2019. Detailed information on the design, data collection, and estimation methods for CRDs and their subtypes in GBD 2021 is available in previously published articles.[3,9,11]

The Global Health Data Exchange tool (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results) was used to download data on the incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALY of CRDs and their subtypes (COPD, asthma, pneumoconiosis, ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis, and other CRDs) from 1990 to 2021. The data were categorized by year, age, sex, region, country, and territory and accessed through the Global Health Data Exchange website. The socio-demographic index (SDI) represents the level of social and economic development related to health in each region, which is a composite measure derived from the total fertility rate among women under the age of 25 years, the average educational attainment of individuals aged 15 years and over, and per capita income. The 2021 Global Development Index classifies countries and territories into five levels of development: low (<0.46), low-middle (0.46–0.60), middle (0.61–0.69), high-middle (0.70–0.81), and high (>0.81).[3,9,11]

The GBD 2021 study estimated mortality and DALYs due to various risk factors and their combinations in different geographic units, providing a standardized and comprehensive assessment of risk exposure and attributable burden. The data on age-standardized mortality rates (ASMRs) and age-standardized DALY rates due to risk factors are analyzed based on level 2, and include air pollution, alcohol use, child and maternal malnutrition, childhood sexual abuse and bullying, dietary risks, drug use, high body mass index (BMI), high fasting plasma glucose, high low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, high systolic blood pressure, intimate partner violence, kidney dysfunction, low bone mineral density, low physical activity, non-optimal temperature, occupational risks, other environmental risks, tobacco use, unsafe sex, and unsafe water, sanitation, and handwashing.[3,9,11,14]

Case definition

The classification of CRD follows the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems (ICD). CRDs served as a collection of the following causes: COPD, pneumoconiosis (silicosis, asbestosis, coal worker’s pneumoconiosis, other pneumoconiosis), asthma, ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis, and other CRDs.[9] COPD is defined in the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease classification by a measurement of <0.7 FEV1/FVC (one second of forceful exhalation/total forced expiration) on spirometry after bronchodilation. Asthma is a chronic lung disease characterized by spasms in the bronchi usually due to an allergic or hypersensitivity reaction causing difficulty in breathing, as diagnosed by the doctor and wheezing in the past year. Pneumoconiosis is a chronic lung disease characterized by lung scarring and other interstitial damage caused by exposure to dust and other contaminants, usually due to occupational exposure. It is characterized by lung fibrosis and other interstitial damage, as well as symptoms such as cough, shortness of breath and phlegm production. ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis are one of the CRDs that impair lung function and oxygen uptake due to scarring and/or inflammation, as defined by the American Thoracic Society as the gold standard definition.[2,3,9,11] The list of other CRD classifications, as well as the relevant codes of ICD-10 and ICD-9, are present in Supplementary Table 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C485.

Statistical analysis

The burden of CRDs and their subtypes (COPD, asthma, pneumoconiosis, ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis, as well as other CRDs) was quantified through age-standardized rates (ASR) of incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs, along with the corresponding counts of incidence, prevalence, deaths, and DALYs. ASRs, specific rates for different age groups, and their corresponding numbers were extracted from the GBD 2021 database and expressed as estimates with 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs). The formula used to calculate the ASR was the following: ∑i=1NαiWi∑i=1NWi in which αi is the age-specific rate in the ith age group, Wi is the count of individuals in the same age group as per the GBD 2021 standard population and N is the total number of age groups. The 95% UI was determined by the “age-adjust.direct” function of the “epitools” package in the R software (version 4.4.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria, https://cran.r-project.org).[8,15,16]

The changes in the percentage in the number of incident cases, prevalent cases, death, and DALYs, along with their corresponding rates from 1990 to 2021 were calculated using the following equation: Percentage changes = Value2021−Value1990Value1990×100%. The GBD database uses UIs instead of precise statistical values. Therefore, it was not possible to directly calculate statistical significance when comparing two values (such as numbers, rates, or percentages); only UIs were provided. If the UIs overlapped, it indicated no significant difference (P >0.05). However, if the UIs did not overlap, there was a statistical difference (P <0.05).[14,17,18]

The relationship between the ASRs, including age-standardized incidence rates (ASIR), prevalence rates (ASPR), mortality rates (ASMR), and DALY rates of CRD and their subtypes and the SDI across 21 geographic regions and 204 countries and territories were evaluated by smooth spline models. Locally weighted scatterplot smoothing was applied to fit the spline curves, with the degree, number, and position of the knots automatically determined according to the data and span parameters. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was calculated to assess the correlation between ASR and SDI, and its statistical significance was evaluated by computed P values. A value of P <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The average annual percentage change (AAPC) was calculated for global ASIR, ASPR, ASMR, and age-standardized DALY rates from 1990 to 2021 to assess trends in CRDs and their subtypes over pre-defined time intervals. A regression model was established to calculate the annual percentage change (APC), the AAPC, and the corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs), which reflected the trends in each segment of the observation period using the following formulas[19–21]:

APCi={exp(βi)−1}×100%

AAPCi={exp(∑Wiβi∑Wi)−1}×100%

In which i represents the number of segments, βi corresponds to the regression coefficient for each linear segment of the data, and Wi is the weight of the regression coefficient determined by the length of the corresponding segment. In this model, the number and position of the join-points were determined using the grid search method (GSM).[22] Model selection was optimized using the Monte Carlo permutation test and a modified Bayesian information criterion, choosing the model that minimized the mean squared error.[23,24] Both APC and AAPC were unitless indicators used to describe the direction and magnitude of trend changes. If the AAPC or APC was less than 0 and its 95% CI did not include 0, it indicated a yearly decrease; if the AAPC or APC was greater than 0 and its 95% CI did not include 0, the ASR was considered to have increased annually during the specified period. Otherwise, the ASR was considered as stable over time.[17,25]

A Bayesian age-period-cohort (BAPC) model with default parameters was used to examine the multiplicative effects of age, period, and cohort.[26,27] The model was defined by the equation: nij = log10(λij)= μ + αi + βj + γk, in which λij represents the number of cases, µ is the intercept, and αi, βj, and γk are age, period, and cohort effects, respectively. The BAPC model was performed using the integrated nested Laplace approximation (INLA).[14,28–30] Statistical analysis was performed using R software.

Results

CRDs burden and temporal trends

The global ASIR of CRDs in 2021 was 719.35 per 100,000 population, reflecting a 24% decrease since 1990 (95% UI: −0.25, −0.22). The ASPR for CRDs was 5785.36 per 100,000, representing a 27% reduction (95% UI: −0.29 to −0.25). The ASMR was 53.56 per 100,000, showing a 37% decline (95% UI: −0.43 to −0.27), and the age-standardized DALY rate was 1294.60 per 100,000, marking a 38% decrease (95% UI: −0.42 to −0.30) since 1990. The ASIRs for COPD, asthma, and pneumoconiosis decreased by 2%, 30%, and 29%, respectively, while the ASPRs for these conditions decreased by 1%, 40%, and 15%. The ASMRs for COPD, asthma, pneumoconiosis, and other CRDs decreased by 37%, 46%, 53%, and 37%, respectively. COPD is responsible for most of the mortality and DALY burden, with an age-standardized DALY rate of 940.66 per 100,000, reflecting a 37% decline, while asthma showed a 44% reduction to 264.62 per 100,000. Pneumoconiosis experienced a 54% decrease, with a rate of 5.22 per 100,000. The age-standardized DALY rate for other CRDs was 36.48 per 100,000, representing a 37% decrease compared to 1990. However, the ASIR for ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis increased by 21%, the ASPR by 9%, the ASMR by 50%, and the age-standardized DALY rate by 28% compared to 1990 [Table 1], with AAPCs of 0.025 (95% CI: 0.024 to 0.026), 0.124 (95% CI: 0.116 to 0.131), 0.026 (95% CI: 0.024 to 0.027), and 0.350 (95% CI: 0.335 to 0.366), respectively [Supplementary Table 2, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C485].

Despite the decrease in the burden of most CRDs, the number of CRD incident cases, prevalent cases, deaths, and DALYs continued to increase due to population aging and demographic changes, with a 48% increase in deaths compared to 1990. As the two most prevalent CRDs, asthma accounts for 68.58% of the total CRD incident cases and 55.63% of the total CRD prevalent cases, while COPD contributed to 84.27% of CRD-related deaths and 73.53% of CRD-related DALYs. Notably, the number of incident cases, prevalent cases, deaths, and DALYs of ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis increased by 1.48, 1.28, 2.42, and 1.69 times, respectively, since 1990 [Supplementary Tables 3–6, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C485].

Globally, males had higher ASIR, ASMR, and DALY rates for CRDs and their subtypes, including COPD, pneumoconiosis, ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis, compared to females in 2021, with a more pronounced decline observed in males since 1990, except for asthma, where females showed higher ASR of prevalence and DALY. Both sexes experienced similar increases in ASIR for ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis, and the ASMR and DALY rates for these conditions increased more significantly in females [Table 1]. Although no significant difference in sex in the specific rates of incidence and prevalence of CRDs, COPD, and asthma was found across all age groups in 2021, males had significantly higher rates of incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs for pneumoconiosis, ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis, and other CRDs in specific age groups, with the differences being most pronounced in elderly populations [Supplementary Figures 1–6, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C485].

The ASR of incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALY for CRDs, asthma, and pneumoconiosis between 1990 and 2021 generally declined across all SDI regions, with evident variations, including a continuing decline in pneumoconiosis, a U-shaped trend of ASIR in high SDI regions for CRDs and asthma, and increasing trends for ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis, particularly in high SDI regions, while low and low-middle SDI regions experienced increasing trends of ASPR for COPD [Figure 1, Supplementary Figures 7–11, and Supplementary Table 7, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C485]. No significant changes in CRDs were observed during the COVID-19 pandemic from 2019 to 2021; however, the potential long-term effects still need to be studied [Figure 1, Supplementary Table 2, and Supplementary Figures 7–11, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C485]. High-SDI regions showed the highest ASIR and ASPR for CRDs, asthma, ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis in 2021, while low-middle SDI regions recorded the highest rates for COPD [Table 2 and Supplementary Table 7, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C485].

The highest ASIR for CRDs and asthma were reported in Haiti in 2021, with Oman, Morocco, and Kiribati showing the most significant increase since 1990 for CRDs and asthma, COPD, and pneumoconiosis, respectively. The United States has the highest in ASPR for CRD and COPD, with Haiti, China, and Peru leading for asthma, pneumoconiosis, and ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis, respectively, while Saudi Arabia, Oman, and Kiribati experienced the most evident ASPR increase. In terms of ASMR, Papua New Guinea had the highest rates of CRD, COPD, asthma, and other CRDs, while the lowest rates were observed in Kuwait, Monaco, and Estonia, respectively, with Norway and Belarus showing the most significant ASMR changes. Papua New Guinea also recorded the highest age-standardized DALY rates for CRD, COPD, and asthma, while the lowest rates were in Estonia, Singapore, and Armenia, respectively, with a significant reduction in Singapore and an increase in Lesotho and Saint Vincent and the Grenadines for various respiratory diseases.

Association between ASRs and SDI

From 1990 to 2021, as the SDI increased, the ASPR for CRD significantly increased, and both the ASR of mortality and DALY significantly decreased, these trends varied by region. Different trend patterns were observed among asthma, COPD, pneumoconiosis, and ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis, reflecting different responses to the increase in SDI in various GBD regions [Figure 2, Supplementary Figures 12–16, and Supplementary Table 9, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C485].

Risk factors for ASMRs and age-standardized DALY rates

The primary contributors to global ASR of mortality and DALY for CRDs between 1990 and 2021 were air pollution, tobacco use, occupational risks, suboptimal temperature, and high BMI. The contribution of air pollution to the global ASMR for CRDs decreased from 53.4% in 1990 to 40.2% in 2021, while its contribution to the global ASR of DALY declined from 44.8% in 1990 to 34.2% in 2021. Air pollution showed a general decline worldwide, particularly in high-middle, high, and middle SDI regions, while it had a slight upward trend in low-SDI regions. Projecting disease burden

The ASIR, ASPR, and ASDR of CRD, including COPD, asthma, pneumoconiosis, and other CRDs are expected to decline between 2022 and 2035 [Figure 4 and Supplementary Figures 23–26, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C485]. In contrast, the incidence and prevalence of ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis are expected to decrease, with mortality initially rising [Supplementary Figure 27, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C485]. The projected ASIR for CRD is expected to decline to 571.06 (95% CI: 509.65–632.46) per 100,000 population by 2035, with an ASPR of 4760.10 (95% CI: 4367.82–5152.37) per 100,000 population. The ASMR is projected to be 39.21 (95% CI: 36.23–42.18) per 100,000 population [Supplementary Table 10, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C485]. The ASR of CRDs and their subtypes demonstrated a remarkable declining trend, while ASMR of ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis showed an upward trend [Supplementary Table 11, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C485].

Discussion

This study described the comprehensive analysis of the global burden and temporal trends of CRDs from 1990 to 2021. The results revealed a declined ASR of incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALY of CRDs in both sexes, whereas the overall number of all these indices increased due to population aging and demographic changes.[2,31] Although substantial progress has been made in reducing the burden of COPD, asthma, and pneumoconiosis globally, these trends vary among geographic regions, sexes, and age groups. Notably, ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis showed a significant increase burden, especially in high-SDI regions. The primary contributors to global ASRs of mortality and DALYs for CRDs included air pollution, tobacco use, occupational risks, suboptimal temperature, and high BMI. Although air pollution and tobacco use declined globally, particularly in regions with high SDI, the DALY rates attributable to high BMI continued to increase in all SDI regions.

COPD remains the leading contributor to CRD-related mortality and DALYs globally. Despite the anti-smoking campaign and improvements in air quality, COPD continues to pose a major public health challenge, particularly in LMICs,[32–34] where tobacco control policies and management of household air pollution remain insufficient.[35,36] The influence of an aging population also exacerbates the COPD burden, as older adults are more susceptible to the cumulative effects of smoking and other risk factors.[35,37]

Asthma has a relatively lower impact on mortality and DALYs compared to COPD despite its significant contribution to CRD incidence and prevalence. This difference can be due to the effectiveness of asthma management strategies, particularly in high-income regions.[38] However, the high prevalence of asthma, particularly among children and adolescents, raises concerns about tobacco exposure, environmental factor, and high BMI, which are associated with dietary habits, lifestyle, food intake, and sugar-sweetened beverage consumption.[39–41] These findings suggest the need for age-specific public health interventions that address environmental and lifestyle determinants of asthma.

Pneumoconiosis, primarily associated with occupational exposures, showed a significant decline in incidence, prevalence, and mortality, reflecting the impact of improved workplace safety standards. However, the disease remains a concern in regions where occupational health regulations are weak or poorly applied.[42–46] The continuing burden of pneumoconiosis in these regions underscores the importance of strengthening occupational health policies and ensuring their rigorous implementation.

In contrast to the declining trends observed in other CRDs, ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis showed a significant increase in incidence, prevalence, and DALY rates, particularly in high-SDI regions. This trend is probably due to improved diagnostic approaches and an aging population, which is more susceptible to these conditions.[47,48] The increasing burden of ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis highlights the need of research focused on these conditions, including the development of early detection and management strategies.

Males exhibit higher ASRs for incidenc, mortality, and DALY for most CRDs, largely due to historical patterns of smoking and occupational exposures.[11,33] The higher burden of COPD and pneumoconiosis in men reflects these historical exposures. However, the slightly higher ASPR for asthma in females suggested a gender-specific factors such as indoor air pollution, hormonal influences, and different patterns of healthcare use.[49–51] The increasing burden of ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis indicated a shift in environmental exposures, healthcare access, or diagnostic practices, warranting specific research and interventions.[52–55]

CRD burden increases markedly with age, particularly among individuals aged 60 years and older.[56,57] This is most evident for COPD, where the cumulative effects of long-term exposure to risk factors manifest more prominently in older adults.[2,11,58–59] Similarly, pneumoconiosis remains common in middle-aged and older individuals due to past occupational exposures.[60] Asthma, while often beginning in childhood, also affects older adults, where it may be complicates by declining lung function and comorbidities.[61,62] The rising burden of ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis in older adults is particularly concerning, which may be linked to the aging population, improved diagnostic techniques, and potentially changed environmental exposures.[63,64]

There are pronounced disparities in CRD burden across SDI regions.[2,31] High-SDI regions, such as North America, report higher ASRs of incidence and prevalence, likely due to better diagnostic capabilities, but lower mortality and DALY rates, reflecting advanced healthcare systems. In contrast, low- and middle-SDI regions, including Sub-Saharan Africa and parts of Asia, experience higher ASMR and DALY rates, driven by limited healthcare access, weak public health infrastructure. LMICs showed higher concentrations of particulate matter pollution due to a lack of legislation and/or adherence to air quality guidelines, higher prevalence of coal power stations, and without meeting vehicle emission standards.[2,65–67]

Our projections indicated that ongoing public health initiatives will probably continue to reduce the overall burden of CRDs. Although progress has been made in mitigating traditional risk factors such as tobacco use and air pollution, the increasing burden associated with high BMI represents a significant new challenge to global health.[68] The global disparities in CRD burden highlighted the urgency of international collaborations and the need of strengthening the healthcare system in low- and middle-income countries to alleviate the global impact of CRDs.[69]

This study has several limitations: (1) The analysis was based on data from the GBD 2021 study, which might be subject to inaccuracies in reporting, uneven regional coverage, and incomplete data collection, particularly in low-income and resource-limited regions.[1,2,58,70] (2) The GBD 2021 database uses model-based estimations rather than real-world data, which might lead to either overestimation or underestimation of the actual disease burden.[3,11] (3) The study focused primarily on disease burden and socioeconomic factors, with limited consideration of the influence of genetic predispositions, behavioral patterns, cultural practices, and environmental factors on the prevalence of CRDs. (4) Trend analyses were based on historical data, which might not account for future changes such as socioeconomic shifts, changes in health policies, or advancements in medical technologies. (5) Although cases of acute respiratory distress and outcomes specifically attributed to COVID-19 were excluded from CRD classifications to prevent misattribution, the COVID-19 pandemic might have led to increased mortality rates due to respiratory complications, which could confuse the results for CRDs.[48,71] (6) Changes in healthcare access and resource allocation during the pandemic might have affected the diagnosis and management of CRDs, further complicating the interpretation of trends. (7) The reliance of the study on aggregated data might obscure important regional variations and the specific needs of different populations.

In conclusion, this study highlights significant progress in reducing the global burden of CRDs from 1990 to 2021, with marked declines in ASR of incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs for most CRDs. COPD remains the leading cause of CRD-related mortality and DALYs, while asthma contributes most to incidence and prevalence. In contrast, the burden of ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis shows a remarkable increase, particularly in high-SDI regions. Regional and socioeconomic disparities still persist, with low- and middle-SDI regions facing slower declines in CRD burden compared to high-SDI regions, where asthma and ILD trends show concerning patterns. The main risk factors for CRDs include air pollution, tobacco use, and occupational hazards, with a reduction observed globally for air pollution and tobacco exposure. In contrast, high BMI is an emerging risk factor, particularly for asthma, and its burden continues to increase. Projections through 2035 suggest continued reductions in the overall CRD burden, but ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis will remain challenges. Addressing modifiable risk factors and regional disparities is critical to further mitigate the global impact of CRDs.
