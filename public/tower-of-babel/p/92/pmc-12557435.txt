Bronchiectasis and sinonasal diseases: a narrative review
Authors: Edoardo Simonetta, Alessandro De Angelis, Margherita S. Silani, Veronica Polelli, Mattia Nigro, Anna Stainer, Francesco Amati, Andrea Gramegna, Francesca Pirola, Giuseppe Mercante, Francesco Blasi, Luca Malvezzi, Stefano Aliberti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Bronchiectasis and chronic rhinosinusitis are chronic diseases of the upper and lower respiratory tract characterised by both infective and inflammatory pathways. The two share many clinical, radiological, microbiological and pathophysiological aspects. Bronchiectasis and chronic rhinosinusitis may be characterised by different endotypes, with inflammation driven by either neutrophils and/or eosinophils. Although the two conditions may coexist, the prevalence of their association remains uncertain. To date, few studies have investigated the pathogenetic relationship between these disorders, with ambiguous results obtained in heterogeneous populations. Some findings suggest that patients with both chronic rhinosinusitis and bronchiectasis may have a heavier disease burden consisting of more exacerbations, more debilitating symptoms, higher radiological severity and worse quality of life. In light of this, identification of treatable traits is crucial and patients are likely to benefit from a multidisciplinary approach involving, among others, pulmonologists, ear-nose-throat physicians, respiratory physiotherapists and allergists/immunologists.

Introduction

Bronchiectasis is a chronic respiratory disease characterised by a permanent dilatation of the bronchi associated with daily symptoms, such as cough and sputum production, and a history of recurrent pulmonary exacerbations [1–4].

Although bronchiectasis is a heterogeneous disease from an aetiological point of view, the pathogenesis of this condition is sustained by a vicious vortex characterised by airway inflammation, impaired mucus clearance, recurrent infections and lung tissue destruction [5–7]. Airway inflammation is frequently driven by neutrophils, but a significant proportion of bronchiectasis patients might express eosinophilic inflammation, which is associated with high disease severity, decreased pulmonary function, poor quality of life and a high number of exacerbations [8–11].

One of the main explanations for the complexity and heterogeneity of bronchiectasis is its coexistence with other respiratory diseases affecting different portions of the airways, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), asthma and chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS) [12]. Often, upper and lower airway diseases coexist and may share similar clinical presentations and pathogenic mechanisms, especially in terms of local inflammation [13, 14]. This coexistence not only significantly complicates the overall clinical picture but may also have an impact on the patient's overall health and treatment approaches. In particular, asthma and COPD are common among patients with bronchiectasis, but few large studies have explored the clinical implications of this coexistence. A recent data analysis from the EMBARC registry revealed that 31% of bronchiectasis patients have a diagnosis of asthma, while 25% have COPD [12, 13]. Notably, this study found that patients with overlapping conditions experienced worse symptoms and more frequent exacerbations than patients with bronchiectasis alone [12, 13].

CRS is an inflammatory condition affecting the nose and paranasal sinuses. It is defined by the presence of at least two symptoms for ≥12 weeks, with at least one being nasal blockage, obstruction, congestion or nasal discharge (either anterior or posterior). Other possible symptoms include facial pain or pressure and a reduced or lost sense of smell [14]. It is usually combined with endoscopic signs of inflamed mucosa (nasal polyps/oedema/discharge) and/or a computed tomography scan showing mucosal changes within the osteomeatal complex and/or sinuses [15]. According to the presence of nasal polyps during the endoscopic examination, two main CRS phenotypes have been identified: CRS with nasal polyps (CRSwNP) and CRS without nasal polyps (CRSsNP) [15]. CRSwNP is dominated by a significant increase in tissue eosinophilia and higher expression of type-2 (T2) cytokines; however, in some cases, a greater proportion of neutrophilic infiltration can be present [16, 17].

Because upper and lower airways are on a continuum, an inflamed sinonasal complex will likely influence the normal functionality of the lower respiratory tract, potentially causing exacerbations of coexisting underlying diseases (e.g. asthma, COPD, bronchiectasis), and vice versa (figure 1).

Changes in both relative and absolute abundance of different microorganisms in upper and lower airways (dysbiosis) is a risk factor or a potential disease modifier in both bronchiectasis and CRS [18, 19]. International guidelines and quality standard consensus suggest performing sputum culture in bronchiectasis patients to identify certain aetiologies (e.g. fungi or nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM)), detect chronic infections and recognise pathogens responsible for exacerbations [2, 3, 20]. International registries and previous studies demonstrate that the bacteria most commonly causing chronic infections in Europe are Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Haemophilus influenzae, while other frequently isolated bacteria include Moraxella catarrhalis and Staphylococcus aureus [21, 22]. Finally, up to 13% of bronchiectasis patients might have infection from NTM, while 8–9% might have NTM-pulmonary disease [23, 24]. In CRS, Staphylococcus, Corynebacterium and Propionibacterium are the commonly detected genera during steady state disease, whereas S. aureus and P. aeruginosa are the predominant species identified during acute exacerbations [25, 26].

This review focuses primarily on non-cystic fibrosis (CF) bronchiectasis, from now on referred to as bronchiectasis, because studies with CF-related bronchiectasis were excluded. We also excluded another genetic cause of bronchiectasis and sinusitis, primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD), owing to its distinct rhinosinusitis pathogenesis and early onset, which set it apart from other non-CF aetiologies. Given that some bronchiectasis populations may include patients with PCD, we have reported the prevalence of PCD within bronchiectasis study populations where available or noted instances where PCD patients were explicitly excluded beforehand.

Although, bronchiectasis and CRS share many common features, the relationship between the two diseases remains unclear. To date, there are few data assessing whether the coexistence of CRS and bronchiectasis leads to worse outcomes and a more difficult-to-treat overall condition. This review follows the current growing interest in the connection between upper and lower respiratory tract diseases, with the aim of collecting and analysing the newest scientific evidence.

Material and methods

This narrative review was informed by a literature search conducted across PubMed, Embase and Scopus from January 1980 to October 2024. The following keywords and their combinations were used: “bronchiectasis”, “chronic rhinosinusitis”, “endotypes”, “treatable traits”, “united airways” and “biological therapy”. The search was restricted to articles published in English. Approximately 1000 articles were identified through this process, and the most relevant studies were reviewed in detail to ensure a comprehensive and balanced discussion of the topic.

Inflammatory pathways in bronchiectasis and chronic rhinosinusitis

Inflammation is one of the essential components promoting and perpetuating bronchiectasis [5]. Because there is evidence that neutrophils outnumber other inflammatory cells in the sputum of most patients with bronchiectasis, chronic neutrophilic inflammation is considered to be the main driver of disease [27]. Although neutrophils are present in the airways of bronchiectasis patients both in stable state and during exacerbation, they are often functionally impaired [28–30]. The neutrophilic inflammatory response is characterised by the release of serine proteases, including neutrophil elastase (NE), proteinase-3 and cathepsin G, as well as the generation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) [30]. Keir et al. [31] used sputum proteomics to demonstrate that increasing NET concentrations correlate with disease severity, history of severe exacerbations and worse quality of life. By contrast, a consistent proportion of bronchiectasis patients (up to 25–30%) might present with eosinophilic inflammation [8, 9]. This T2-high endotype has been associated with distinct microbiome profiles, and blood eosinophils >300 cells·µL−1 are associated with shortened time to first exacerbation [10, 19, 25, 32, 33].

Regarding CRS, in Europe and North America CRSwNP is mostly driven by T2 inflammatory pathways, whereas CRSsNP is less well characterised, despite representing 80% of all patients with CRS [34]. Although the latter was thought to be caused by T-helper (Th)-1 inflammation based on an elevation in interferon-γ, recent studies have clarified that inflammatory endotypes in this condition are highly heterogeneous and there are significant geographical variations, with all three endotypes (Th-1, T2 and Th-3) represented [35]. A recent investigation by Stevens et al. [36] aimed to describe the association between CRS endotypes and specific clinical presentations in both CRSwNP and CRSsNP. They analysed data from 255 patients: among those with CRSsNP, the overall frequencies of Th-1, T2 and Th-3 inflammation were 21%, 55% and 27%, respectively; in patients with CRSwNP, the overall frequencies of Th1, T2 and Th3 inflammation were 17%, 87% and 18%, respectively. In both populations, 26% of patients had mixed endotypes. Differences in clinical presentation were remarkable between the two groups: asthma and atopy were significantly more common in patients with CRSwNP; by contrast, symptoms such as nasal discharge, sinus pressure, headache, fatigue, fever and cough were more often reported by patients with CRSsNP. The association between biological mechanisms and clinical features of CRS and lower respiratory tract symptoms other than cough was not investigated.

The diagnosis and follow-up of bronchiectasis and CRS are essential and typically rely on a combination of biomarkers, including clinical, radiological, microbiological, endoscopic and laboratory data. Additionally, some inflammation-related biomarkers, which suggest the type of inflammation, are already integrated in patient monitoring and decision-making, such as sputum colour, eosinophil counts, IgE levels and exhaled nitric oxide fraction [37, 38]. Emerging markers like metalloproteases, NE in sputum and inflammatory interleukins (ILs) show promise for future integration into clinical practice [37].

The relationship between bronchiectasis and chronic rhinosinusitis

In 2007, Krouse et al. [39] outlined the association between asthma and upper airway disorders and proposed the concept of “unified airways” pathophysiology. The respiratory tract is indeed a continuum of motile ciliated epithelium that extends from the nasal cavity, paranasal sinuses and middle ear to the trachea and bronchi. As such, mucociliary clearance impairment is fundamental in the development of several conditions, such as CF, PCD and Young's syndrome, affecting both upper and lower airways [40]. Moreover, immunodeficiency syndromes, either congenital or acquired, are known to potentially predispose individuals to frequent infections leading to both ear, nose and throat (ENT) disease and bronchiectasis [41].

CRS is often mentioned among common extrapulmonary comorbidities in bronchiectasis, which supports the adoption of the unified airways model. However, epidemiological data and clinical correlations between CRS and bronchiectasis vary significantly among studies (table 1).

Chronic rhinosinusitis in patients with bronchiectasis

A systematic review from Handley et al. [53] estimated a pooled CRS prevalence of 62% in patients with bronchiectasis (with nasal polyps present in 29% of the population), although CRS prevalence varied widely among studies (32–80%) due to diagnostic heterogeneity. Patients with both conditions showed greater bronchiectasis extension, poorer quality of life and reduced sense of smell.

A single observational Chinese study evaluated the impact of CRS on bronchiectasis exacerbation frequency, showing a higher exacerbation risk in patients presenting both conditions [45]. In this study, two patients (2.4%) presented with PCD as an aetiology of bronchiectasis.

Another retrospective study by Kim et al. [52] emphasised CRS as a critical comorbidity increasing healthcare utilisation in patients with asthma and bronchiectasis, leading to higher rates of emergency room visits and hospitalisations, and increased use of antibiotics and oral corticosteroids.

In a cross-sectional analysis of the Korean bronchiectasis registry, contrary to previous studies, only 7.1% of patients had CRS, showing no association between CRS and bronchiectasis severity or clinical outcomes [44]. Less than 2% presented with PCD or other primary upper airway diseases as a suspected aetiology of bronchiectasis.

Upper airway involvement in bronchiectasis was also assessed by Shteinberg et al. [46], who reported that 35% of patients in their cohort experienced upper airway symptoms. This subpopulation presented with lung symptoms starting at a younger age, along with greater eosinophilia, elevated IgE and more pulmonary exacerbations, despite similar hospitalisation rates (CF and PCD patients were excluded). No significant differences in lung function and radiological severity of bronchiectasis were observed.

The most recent study by the same group compared bronchiectasis patients with and without CRS, excluding individuals with asthma, PCD and COPD [33]. Sputum eosinophil percentages were significantly higher in patients with both conditions. Blood eosinophil counts were also predictive of sputum eosinophilia, and total IgE levels were elevated in individuals with higher sputum eosinophils. The authors suggested that the eosinophilic inflammation in patients with concomitant bronchiectasis and CRS may be distinct from the classical inflammation pattern found in asthma.

In an Australian cohort of bronchiectasis patients with CRS, worsening upper respiratory symptoms were associated with worse Leicester Cough Questionnaire score and reduced physical activity and social function [47]. However, CRS symptoms showed little correlation with bronchiectasis severity (Bronchiectasis Severity Index (BSI) and high-resolution computed tomography scoring) [47]. These findings should be interpreted cautiously, because 12% of participants had PCD.

In a large American monocentric retrospective study, Somani et al. [48] reported that 45% of their cohort had concomitant CRS (CF and PCD patients were excluded). Asthma, allergic rhinitis, gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD), antibody deficiency and peripheral eosinophilia were independently associated with CRS after adjustment for age, sex and ethnicity. Additionally, peripheral eosinophilia was significantly higher in patients with bronchiectasis and CRS compared to those with bronchiectasis alone after adjustment for confounding factors.

In another comprehensive case–control analysis, Schwartz et al. [49] explored the association between CRS and bronchiectasis, excluding persons with CF and PCD spectrum of disease. They reported a higher prevalence of CRS and a greater need for ENT procedures among individuals with bronchiectasis compared to those without, and revealed that CRS precedes the diagnosis of bronchiectasis by an average of over 6 years, indicating a potentially causal relationship.

Finally, a retrospective chart review study compared CRS radiological severity (Lund–Mackay score) in bronchiectasis and in allergic rhinitis patients, showing significantly higher severity in bronchiectasis [50]. Furthermore, microbiological correlation of upper and lower airway bacterial colonisation was demonstrated, with P. aeruginosa the most commonly isolated microorganism in both upper and lower airway cultures [50].

Bronchiectasis in patients with chronic rhinosinusitis

To date, few studies have investigated the role of bronchiectasis in CRS patients (table 1). The most relevant study was conducted by Peters et al. [51], who performed a retrospective analysis in a large cohort of 1109 patients with CRS, excluding those diagnosed with CF or PCD, to determine the prevalence of bronchiectasis and the radiological and clinical relationship between the two conditions. Among those with CRS and available chest computed tomography, 24.3% had the word “bronchiectasis” in their report. Patients with CRS had a significantly higher prevalence of bronchiectasis than asthma patients, which were taken as a control group. Lung function values were lower in CRS patients with bronchiectasis, and those with CRSwNP and bronchiectasis had greater radiological severity of sinus disease and were more likely to undergo sinus surgery.

Another recent Chinese prospective study explored the association between CRS and bronchiectasis in COPD patients [42]. The prevalence of bronchiectasis was 57.6% among COPD patients with CRS and 37.1% among those without CRS. As for the clinical implication, COPD–bronchiectasis patients with CRS showed significantly higher radiological severity of bronchiectasis, greater eosinophilia and elevated levels of inflammatory mediators in sputum. In this study, all patients were smokers or formers smokers and patients with a primary diagnosis of bronchiectasis, asthma or any other significant respiratory diseases were excluded.

Hypothesising that the unified airways concept applies to bronchiectasis associated with asthma and CRS, Sheng et al. [43] consecutively enrolled outpatients diagnosed with asthma and CRS, finding radiologically significant bronchiectasis in 41% of included patients. Asthma–CRS patients with bronchiectasis had a higher incidence of nasal polyps and asthma exacerbations in comparison to those without bronchiectasis. Bronchiectasis in asthma–CRS patients was associated with elevated T2 markers (blood eosinophils count and IgE levels), suggesting an eosinophilic rather than neutrophilic endotype.

In conclusion, the coexistence of bronchiectasis and CRS varies widely across studies, with prevalence rates ranging from 7% to 62%, influenced by methodological heterogeneity and geographical differences. Patients with both conditions seem to exhibit more pronounced eosinophilic inflammation and elevated IgE levels, suggesting a key role for the T2-high phenotype irrespective of the presence of nasal polyps. Moreover, even without considering the inflammatory footprint, individuals with both bronchiectasis and CRS tend to have worse clinical outcomes, because each condition exacerbates the severity of the other. This underscores the importance of further research to clarify the pathophysiological interactions and to evaluate the impact of neutrophilic and T2-high inflammation treatments across both and overlapping conditions to optimise management strategies.

Treatable traits, disease endotypes and target therapies in bronchiectasis and chronic rhinosinusitis

A treatable traits approach in chronic respiratory diseases was first suggested in COPD and asthma [54]. Treatable traits are represented by different disease phenotypes or underlying biological mechanisms explaining pathological processes, named endotypes, which could become targets for specific interventions [55]. Both bronchiectasis and CRS are complex diseases that share some clinical features, with possibly similar underlying biological mechanisms, yet with different radiological and functional alterations [15, 19]. The management of patients with both conditions, particularly if other comorbidities coexist, portends significant complexity. Because no single therapeutic approach can address all cases, an integrated and complementary strategy is essential. This approach should focus on managing treatable traits, which may be shared and aggravate both diseases (table 2). Asthma, characterised by T2 pathways, and COPD, marked by neutrophil-dominant inflammation, can exacerbate the inflammatory burden when coexisting with bronchiectasis or CRS, often leading to overlapping clinical features, confounding inflammatory biomarkers and higher disease severity [12, 13, 56–58]. Asthma and CRSwNP-associated eosinophilic inflammation may obscure the neutrophilic endotype of bronchiectasis. Conversely, COPD, active smoking, certain medications and other neutrophilic conditions can alter the expression of the T2 endotype. Optimising the management of these comorbidities can facilitate better care by reducing inflammatory complexity and improving treatment targeting in patients with overlapping diseases.

Inflammation as a treatable trait in bronchiectasis

In bronchiectasis, characterising patients through clinical phenotypes did not lead to significant results because such categories can include patients with very different conditions. This underlines the need to better stratify, classify and endotype patients through a deeper understanding of disease pathogenesis [59–61]. The concept of treatable traits was therefore adopted in bronchiectasis and a lot of effort has been put into the definition of a multimodal approach targeting pulmonary, aetiological, extrapulmonary and environmental aspects [62].

As for neutrophilic inflammation, a stronger relationship between NE, NETs and clinical outcomes in bronchiectasis is further supported by the current attention to dipeptidyl peptidase 1 (DPP-1) inhibitors [63]. NE is present at higher concentrations in the sputum of bronchiectasis patients during the stable state with or without chronic bacterial infection [64]. It increases with bacterial load [27, 28] and decreases with antibiotic treatment [65]. High NE levels are associated with lung function decline, risk of future exacerbation and all-cause mortality [31]. Therefore, NE represents an interesting target for treatment, and DPP-1 inhibitors are under intense study (figure 1). The phase 3 ASPEN trial [66] has now confirmed the efficacy of the novel DPP-1 inhibitor brensocatib, demonstrating a significant reduction in exacerbation frequency among patients with bronchiectasis, consistent with earlier phase 2 findings [63]. Additionally, the phase 2 AIRLEAF trial evaluating BI 1291583, another cathepsin C inhibitor, demonstrated a dose-dependent reduction in the risk of exacerbations in adults with bronchiectasis, further supporting the potential of DPP-1 inhibition as a therapeutic strategy [67].

A consistent proportion of bronchiectasis patients might show an eosinophilic footprint, leading to the definition of a T2-high endotype, regardless of the presence of asthma [8, 9]. In three case series of patients with severe asthma and concomitant bronchiectasis, biological drugs showed some benefits after treatment [68]. However, in non-asthma T2-high bronchiectasis patients, the effects of drugs directed against T2 cytokines (IL-4, IL-5 and IL-13) are unknown. Therefore, although there are no approved drugs for the T2-high endotype in bronchiectasis, the IL-5 pathway could represent a key factor in the pathogenetic mechanism and become a treatment target [69].

Inflammation as a treatable trait in chronic rhinosinusitis

A treatable traits approach has also been proposed for CRS to address the high complexity of the disease, facilitate the management of its comorbid conditions and promote a deeper understanding of its phenotypes and endotypes [70]. Disease-specific traits, as well as extra-nasal, environmental and behavioural features, are associated with different expression of the disease, therefore need specific treatment proposals [71].

Following the phenotypical classification based on the presence or absence of nasal polyps at nasal endoscopy, which is too limited for a thorough evaluation of the patient, additional phenotypes have been identified, such as CRS with aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease, allergic fungal rhinosinusitis and CRS secondary to CF [72]. Despite phenotypical classification correlating with the underlying biological mechanisms, it does not completely explain disease pathophysiology nor predict treatment response [36, 72]. Historically, indeed, a too simplistic definition described nasal phenotypes according to two different immune pathways: CRSwNP related to a T2 response and CRSsNP to a Th1 response. This dichotomy was proven wrong, confirming that a purely phenotypic approach is inadequate, and that all phenotypes exist with a combination of Th-1-associated, T2-associated and Th-3-associated signatures [34, 36].

According to studies on inflammatory endotypes in CRS, a multifaceted landscape emerges here as well, in which the same disease phenotype can have multiple underlying inflammatory backgrounds. This paves the way to personalised medicine, with the ability to predict treatment resistance and expected treatment outcomes based upon endotypes rather than phenotypes [73]. Although different endotypes have been suggested across different studies, the most consistent one is characterised by T2 inflammation, which is associated with more severe disease progression (i.e. worse sinus disease and symptoms, presence of asthma and higher rates of recurrence of disease after surgery) [74].

In view of the above-mentioned unified airways concept, biological drugs have been developed to treat asthma and comorbidities with the same underlying pathological pathways [14, 39, 75]. In the last years, three of these drugs (dupilumab, omalizumab and mepolizumab) have been approved for uncontrolled or severe CRSwNP after failure of intranasal corticosteroids or surgery, and eventually even as an alternative to surgery [76].

Therefore, correctly identifying patients through disease endotyping is a high priority in uncontrolled CRS management because it can allow for the selection of candidates for biological therapy. A useful marker of T2 inflammation to guide treatment initiation is a value of >10 eosinophils per high-powered field in biopsy specimens, as well as eosinophil count and total IgE as indicated in the guidelines [14, 73], although there is lack of consensus about the thresholds for the latter two [77]. Likewise, an endotype dominated by neutrophilic inflammation is assumed and, as for bronchiectasis, potential therapeutical targets are under investigation [78, 79].

Other treatable traits in bronchiectasis and chronic rhinosinusitis

Bacterial and fungal colonisation, particularly with P. aeruginosa, S. aureus and Aspergillus spp., contribute to persistent inflammation, exacerbations and tissue damage. Notably, S. aureus and Aspergillus spp. can also drive T2 inflammation. In bronchiectasis, P. aeruginosa occurrence in respiratory samples is associated with a higher risk of exacerbations and severe exacerbations requiring hospitalisation [80, 81]. In those experiencing frequent exacerbations or with persistent airway infection caused by P. aeruginosa, moderate-quality evidence supports the recommendation for inhaled antibiotics, for a duration of 3 months or longer [82]. Unlike in bronchiectasis, where long-term macrolides have proven benefit in reducing exacerbations, evidence for their use in CRS is lacking [83, 84]. Oral antibiotics may be appropriate for treating infectious CRS exacerbations, particularly in the presence of purulent drainage; however, strong evidence supporting their efficacy in CRS remains limited in the literature [85]. S. aureus enterotoxin-specific IgE contributes to nasal polyposis, enhanced eosinophilic inflammation and increased disease severity in asthma and CRS [86, 87]. S. aureus can trigger the release of alarmins like thymic stromal lymphopoietin and IL-33, which induce innate lymphoid cells to release IL-4, IL-5 and IL-13, forming the backbone of T2 inflammation [86, 87]. A Chinese study on bronchiectasis found that patients with S. aureus but without P. aeruginosa exhibited the highest S. aureus enterotoxin sensitisation rates and elevated IgE, suggesting an allergic predisposition linked to S. aureus colonisation [88]. Moreover, Aspergillus sensitisation, manifesting as allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis or allergic fungal rhinosinusitis, represents a common precipitating factor and treatable trait in patients with bronchiectasis and CRS [89, 90]. Routine sputum and nasal swab cultures, along with testing for antibiotic/antimycotic susceptibility, T2 inflammation markers and fungal-specific IgE, can guide treatment decisions, including eradication protocols, inhaled antibiotics, antifungal therapy and sinus surgery, when fungal colonisation is detected.

Impaired mucociliary clearance is central to the pathogenesis of both bronchiectasis and CRS, leading to mucus stasis and increased infection risk. Assessment methods, such as clinical history, nasal endoscopy and imaging to detect mucus plugs or thick secretions, can direct management strategies, including airway clearance programmes, nasal lavage and mucolytic agents [91, 92].

GORD is commonly associated with bronchiectasis and CRS, potentially triggering inflammation through gastric acid reflux and microaspiration. GORD can worsen bronchiectasis symptoms and quality of life and increase exacerbation frequency [93]. Clinical suspicion for GORD should remain high, and we advocate for systematic screening across all levels of disease severity to identify patients who may benefit from gastroenterology evaluation and specific diagnostic tests. These can guide targeted treatments, including proton pump inhibitors, lifestyle modifications and dietary adjustments, although clinical trial evidence supporting GORD-targeted treatments in improving outcomes in bronchiectasis is currently lacking [93, 94].

Discussion

Bronchiectasis and CRS are two chronic airway diseases that might overlap in terms of clinical presentation and pathological background.

A comprehensive overview about the pooled prevalence (62%) and characteristics of CRS in bronchiectasis patients has been published by Handley et al. [53]. A pitfall of this systematic review is the inclusion of very heterogeneous study populations with insufficient differentiation between patient subgroups. First, bronchiectasis aetiologies were characterised in only a few analysed studies, and for what we have described up to this point, some aetiologies, such as PCD, are often associated with upper airway disease due to the continuity of the respiratory epithelium. Therefore, this aspect should be taken into consideration when studying the association between these conditions. Second, there was also high heterogeneity in the study population because in some studies bronchiectasis was defined only as a radiological feature regardless of clinical respiratory symptoms. Moreover, to our knowledge, only one study analysed the impact of the coexistence of CRS during bronchiectasis exacerbations, showing significantly greater risks of such events compared to patients not affected by rhinitis during the 1-year follow-up period [45]. For the reasons mentioned above, it is difficult to draw definitive conclusions regarding the prevalence and outcomes of concurrent bronchiectasis and CRS. Bronchiectasis exacerbations have a major impact on quality of life and mortality, and any aspect that predicts or even promotes the tendency for exacerbation and could be addressed as a treatable trait could lead to increased survival in bronchiectasis patients [95].

There are few data concerning bronchiectasis in patients affected by CRS, mostly deriving from a single retrospective study with a prevalence of 24% of radiologically significant bronchiectasis [51]. This could be due to underdiagnosis, because symptoms of bronchiectasis may be mistaken for upper airway issues, and most CRS patients do not routinely receive chest imaging. The underestimation of bronchiectasis prevalence in CRS may have clinical implications because patients with both diseases in the above-mentioned study had greater radiological severity of sinus disease and more often required sinus surgery [51]. The implementation of screening of pulmonary conditions at least in selected cases could help in predicting treatment response and in patient management.

People with CRS and bronchiectasis reportedly have higher peripheral eosinophil counts than patients with bronchiectasis alone. It remains unclear whether the elevated peripheral eosinophil counts are simply a manifestation of CRS, a traditionally eosinophilic disorder, or if bronchiectasis patients with CRS have a different pathogenic mechanism that leads to a distinct eosinophil-driven endotype. Considering the hypothesis that eosinophilic inflammation might be a driver of pathogenesis in such patients, targeting this pathway could be beneficial for the clinical manifestations of both diseases (figure 1). In patients with bronchiectasis and CRS we could possibly identify a role for blood and/or sputum eosinophils, fraction of exhaled nitric oxide and other markers of T2 inflammation in suggesting the use of inhaled of intranasal corticosteroids or even, in the future, biological drugs [69]. On the other hand, the detection of eosinophilia should guide pulmonologists not only to exclude well-known lung T2 inflammation conditions such as allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis and asthma, but also CRS. Because biological drugs are already used in the treatment of uncontrolled or severe CRSwNP after failure of conventional treatment, it would be interesting to study the clinical outcome of this treatment in patients with overlap between CRS and bronchiectasis as a proof of principle of treatment of T2 endotype in bronchiectasis. Besides the T2-high endotype, other endotypes will become more and more relevant in CRS as new biologicals targeting other molecular pathways become available. For selected patients with CRS and underlying neutrophilic inflammation, hopefully Th-1 inflammation inhibitors will become available. Furthermore, the possible coexistence of two different inflammatory patterns also represents a challenge in the management of upper and lower airways diseases and provides a further stimulus to carry out research on how these two patterns interact with each other. The complexity of both CRS and bronchiectasis requires patient management by different healthcare professionals, offering their expertise in different aspects of patient care.

One of the most interesting aspects of the study by Schwartz et al. [49] is its robust demonstration of a temporal correlation between CRS and the subsequent development of bronchiectasis. This temporal evidence supports a potentially causal relationship, suggesting that early intervention in CRS could significantly influence the prevention and management of bronchiectasis. Future research should further explore this temporal dynamic, aiming to develop targeted strategies that could delay or prevent the onset of bronchiectasis in patients with CRS.

Our review highlights the pressing need for robust prospective studies to elucidate the long-term interplay between CRS and bronchiectasis. Current literature lacks sufficient longitudinal data to fully capture the temporal dynamics and progression of these interrelated conditions (table 3). Understanding how CRS evolves in patients with bronchiectasis, and vice versa, is pivotal for identifying shared pathogenic mechanisms and optimising management strategies. Long-term studies could shed light on critical outcomes, such as the impact of interventions on disease trajectory, quality of life and exacerbation rates, as well as the potential bidirectional influence of these conditions on disease severity. Addressing this gap is essential for improving prognostic insights and tailoring therapies that address the complex, multifaceted relationship between CRS and bronchiectasis.

Points for clinical practice

In bronchiectasis, multidisciplinary management by a group comprising respiratory physicians and physiotherapists, microbiologists, thoracic surgeons and general practitioners is essential to improve patient outcomes. Other professional figures may become relevant depending on specific underlying aetiologies or comorbidities, including ENT physicians [96]. Concerning CRS, a multidisciplinary team comprising allergists/immunologists and otolaryngologists is recommended especially in patients with recurrent or refractory disease [97], with the addition of a respiratory physician in case of severe asthma [98].

The unified airways model, the interplay between bronchiectasis and CRS and their possible common underlying pathological pathways suggest that a greater interaction is needed between otolaryngologists and respiratory physicians during the management of these clinical entities. Rhinosinusitis has already been defined as a treatable trait in bronchiectasis that needs to be addressed by a specialist. But, to our knowledge, bronchiectasis diagnosis and management is not included in CRS patient work-up. Bronchiectasis should always be considered in patients with frequent respiratory tract infections and persistent productive cough, especially with isolation of uncommon pathogens [99]. Good collaboration with other healthcare professionals helps to create a shared diagnostic and treatment plan for patients with symptoms of both diseases, which can lead to better recognition of the coexistence of the two conditions and improved patient management.

Conclusion

The coexistence of bronchiectasis and CRS represents a complex clinical scenario, highlighting the need for a comprehensive understanding of shared pathophysiological pathways, particularly through the lens of treatable traits. This approach highlights the importance of tailoring treatment to individual patients, targeting specific inflammatory endotypes, microbial colonisation and comorbid conditions. The integration of biomarkers and disease endotyping into clinical practice offers a promising approach to improve patient outcomes. Multidisciplinary collaboration remains vital, bringing together lung and ENT specialists, immunoallergologists and physiotherapists to ensure a cohesive and thorough management strategy.
