Formative research for a One Health, animal-inclusive approach to Community-Led Total Sanitation in rural Mali
Authors: Elizabeth D Thomas, Michel Dione, Ahmadou Sow, Mahfuza Islam, Natalie De La Cruz, Lily Li, Hazara Ouedraogo, Kirk Dearden, Laura H Kwong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite its focus on preventing human fecal matter from entering the environment, Community-Led Total Sanitation (CLTS) places little emphasis on animal feces management. Integrating safe management of animal waste into an existing community-level program to safely manage human feces may substantially reduce overall exposure to feces and improve both human and animal health.MethodsWe conducted formative research to inform the development of an animal-inclusive CLTS intervention in rural Mali. Across five villages eligible for the national CLTS program, we conducted focus group discussions and individual interviews with livestock owners, child caregivers, and other community stakeholders, unstructured observations of households with young children, a household-level survey, and community workshops.ResultsOur results highlight the close interactions between men, women, boys, and girls with a range of domestic animals, animal products, and animal waste, including fecal waste. We documented animal husbandry practices beyond feces management that may impact human, animal, and/or environmental health, including unhygienic milking and slaughtering practices, and the management of sick animals, along with generally low awareness and knowledge about zoonotic diseases.ConclusionsIntegrated interventions, like an animal-inclusive approach to CLTS, should be informed by both WASH and animal health sectors, and would benefit from a One Health perspective, where human, animal, and environmental health are accounted for. An animal-inclusive intervention should consider the types of animals present; gender- and age-specific roles that household members play in animal husbandry; potential interventions at critical control points; and context-specific practices, perceptions, and priorities that will influence intervention content and delivery.Keywords: One Health; Community-Led Total Sanitation; Animal waste; Zoonotic disease; WASH; water, sanitation, and hygiene

Introduction

Poorly managed human and animal feces in the domestic environment contribute to the burden of enteric infections for young children [1], [2], [3]. In rural Sub-Saharan Africa, 23% of people still practice open defecation [4], which facilitates the transmission of diarrheal disease and trachoma [5]. Open defecation can also have negative impacts on a person's quality of life and safety [6], [7], especially for women [8]. As in most rural settings, households in rural sub-Saharan Africa keep livestock and poultry for their eggs, meat, milk, manure, and skins, and as a source of cash, insurance against unforeseen events, dowry payments, and cultural ceremonies [9]. While livestock are important sources of food and resources that can support good health, their presence can also pose health risks if husbandry practices are not well implemented [10]: domestic animals generate 85% of the world's fecal waste [11], [12], and their waste contains pathogens that contribute a substantial disease burden to humans [1].

In resource-constrained settings, where water and sanitation infrastructure is often limited and hygiene conditions are poor, there are multiple opportunities for humans to come into contact with both human and animal feces [12]. Poor water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) can also create environments that are hazardous to the health of household livestock [13]. There have been multiple calls for integrated interventions to address uncontained feces that pose health threats to humans, animals, and their shared environments [14], [15]. These calls emphasize the need for interdisciplinary One Health-WASH collaborations to simultaneously improve animal and human health [16], [17].

Community-Led Total Sanitation (CLTS), one of the most widely used models to improve sanitation at the local level, aims to help communities assess their practice of open defecation in a way that is provocative and participatory [18]. While the outcomes of CLTS on human health are mixed [19], [20], [21], [22], a cluster-randomized controlled trial in Mali [23], where CLTS is a key component of the national public health strategy, found that it increased access to private latrines and reduced stunting, although there was no reduction in the prevalence of children who had diarrhea or were underweight. Intervention households were also less likely to have human and animal feces in their courtyards compared to controls.

CLTS has the stated aim of “total elimination” of uncontained feces and the safe disposal of human, animal, and domestic waste [18]; however, animal feces management is not a formal part of the intervention. In settings like rural Mali, where animals and humans live in close proximity to one another [24] and poorly managed feces are prevalent [23], adapting CLTS to be animal-inclusive could yield health benefits for both humans and animals. As part of formative research to inform an animal inclusive, One Health approach to CLTS, we conducted a mixed methods study in rural Mali to document animal husbandry and animal waste management practices that are particularly risky for human and animal health and may be amenable to change.

Methods

Study setting

We conducted this study in the rural south-central Ségou region of Mali. Ségou's residents are largely agropastoral; livestock are an important part of the local agrarian system [24]. Communities live in close proximity to their animals, including poultry, sheep, goats, and cattle, for extended periods, though some move their herds between the central Niger delta and southern areas on a seasonal basis to search for grazing pastures [25]. In November and December 2020, the Direction Régionale de l'Assainissement du Contrôle des Pollutions et des Nuisances (DRACPN) of Segou, a government entity responsible for CLTS implementation throughout Mali, and Union des Sociétés Coopératives des Eleveurs de Tamani (USCET), a farmers' organization and the implementing partner for this study, surveyed villages from five communes in Baroueli cercle for eligibility in the national CLTS program and involvement in study activities. See the Supplemental Information for details on the study setting and interdisciplinary research team.

Data collection

Exploratory qualitative research

In each of the five villages selected for exploratory qualitative research, we conducted focus group discussions (FGDs), individual interviews, and 5-h household-level observations to explore animal husbandry, human-animal interactions, candidate animal-focused interventions, child health and hygiene, and child-animal interactions. See the Supplemental Information for details.

Workshops

In July 2021, we held a series of workshops in qualitative research villages to inform the integration of animal health and feces management into Mali's established CLTS curriculum. Each workshop was attended by key stakeholders in partner villages, including livestock owners and mothers of young children. In two workshops, facilitators led participants in a discussion of their perceptions of human and animal feces, including comparing qualities (e.g., odor) and quantities of different types of feces. In one workshop, facilitators also asked participants to indicate if they would eat a meal if a fly were to first land on animal feces and then land on the food. This question was asked about different types of animal feces, and participants were asked to indicate if they themselves would eat the food and then estimate how many out of 100 people in their village would eat the food. The purpose of this activity was to explore how individuals might respond to animal feces being used in one of the activities in the CLTS “triggering” process, where the fecal-oral transmission route is explained by placing feces and food in proximity and observing flies moving between the two items [18]. Additional description of these workshops is available in a companion manuscript (Dione et al. [46]).

Household survey

In December 2021, USCET enumerators conducted a household-level survey among a convenience sample of 343 livestock-keeping households with children under five across 25 villages that were either eligible for CLTS or that had participated in CLTS, including four of the villages where qualitative activities had taken place. The purpose of the household survey was to determine the prevalence of various animal and animal feces management practices prior to implementing an intervention. See the Supplemental Information for details.

Data analysis

Interview and FGDs transcripts, field notes, observations, and workshop notes were collated, reviewed, and summarized. Descriptive statistics of the household survey data were calculated using Stata 13 (College Station, Texas). See the Supplemental Information for details.

Ethics

This study was approved by the ILRI Institutional Research Ethics Committee (ILRI-IREC2020–54/1) and the Comité National d'Ethique pour la Santé et les Sciences de la Vie of Mali (2022 09/MSMD-CNESS). We conducted all data collection activities in accordance with COVID-19 safety guidelines, including wearing masks, applying hydro-alcoholic gel, physical distancing, and conducting FGDs and interviews outdoors.

We present results from the exploratory qualitative research and household survey together by topic area. Most of the 340 household survey respondents were female (n = 255; 75%). On average, household size was 7.1 persons and there were 6.5 households per compound (Table 1). Individual demographic data were not collected for qualitative research participants beyond the eligibility criteria described in the methods section and the Supplemental Information.

Purpose and importance of livestock production

Most households surveyed owned chickens (89.1%) and goats (87.1%) and almost three-quarters owned sheep (71.5%) and cattle (71.5%), while some also owned donkeys (30.7%), cats (7.1%), and dogs (6.8%). The participants of animal-focused FGDs and interviews owned the same variety of animals. During FGDs, participants said that cattle were raised primarily to labor in the field, and for their production of milk, meat, and manure. Sheep and goats were also raised for their products, to sell and pay for small household expenses (e.g., school fees, drug prescriptions), and to sacrifice for religious or social events. The primary purpose of keeping donkeys was as a mode of transportation for people, goods, and water. Poultry were raised for their meat and eggs and because they could be sold quickly to cover household expenses (e.g., purchase of soap and clothing). Some participants mentioned keeping dogs to guard the house and livestock and keeping cats to chase away vermin.

In FGDs, when participants were asked to rank common livestock species by importance, most men ranked cattle first, followed by sheep, goats, donkeys, poultry, and then dogs and cats. Most women ranked sheep and goats first, followed by donkeys, poultry, cattle, and then dogs and cats. This ranking mirrored livestock management roles: men reported being primarily responsible for cattle, and women for small ruminants and poultry, though most reported sharing in animal care roles. One participant called cattle the “backbone of the family,” and many commented on their high milk output and monetary return. Women noted that sheep and goats could be raised without the support of men, and that small ruminant manure supported women's gardens. A few participants referred to poultry as a “secondary species,” or lower priority than others, due to their comparatively low revenue when sold.

Livestock location, movement, and housing

According to most FGD and interview participants, the location of livestock during the day and night varied by species and season (Fig. 1). During the rainy season, most cattle, sheep, and goats spent the day and sometimes night in pastures “in the bush” outside of the village. In the dry season, some participants said that cattle moved to other grazing locations (i.e., transhumance). Participants said that animals not moved to other grazing locations in the dry season would spend the day grazing in the bush nearby and return to the household yard or nearby wells for water when needed. Regardless of the season, participants said that some animals, especially those that were sick, young, lactating, or being fattened, stayed by the house. At night, most participants said that their cattle were kept outside the compound or village in enclosures made from local materials like wood, dead brush, and thorns under supervision of adults and young boys. Small ruminants might be in separate enclosures made from mud bricks, wood, and dead hedges outside the village or within the compound, or tethered inside the compound in an unfenced space. A few participants said that small ruminants might spend the night loose around the village. In one FGD, participants noted that separation of certain animals at night was important for both practical and traditional reasons:

“The animals that spend the night inside the living area (compound) cannot be grouped in one batch, as sheep and goats are not put together in the same pen, as tradition forbids it. Those that spend the night outside cannot be grouped in one batch either, to avoid accidents and aggressions, or else, in the morning, we will not be able to have milk, as the young will suckle their mother all night long.” – FGD, Village 2, Female livestock keeper.

Layout of a typical household compound in rural Segou, Mali, with day and nighttime confinement locations of domestic animals.

Donkeys were reported to either wander during the day, be tied to a stake at home, or work in the field. Nighttime locations for donkeys were similar. Poultry scavenged at the house or within the village during the day and spent the night in the compound either in trees or in coops made from mud bricks or wood covered in mud. Dogs and cats were reported to be at home during the day and wander to scavenge at night.

The household survey corroborated the qualitative reports while providing additional insight on animal confinement (Table 2). During the day, almost half (48.9%) of households reported not confining healthy goats/sheep, or cattle, though one-third to one-half (36.7%–56.8%) confined them outside the compound. Almost all households (96.7%) did not confine poultry during the day. At night, healthy adult chickens were primarily kept in coops (74.3%) and the courtyard (12.5%). Laying hens, chicks, and sick adult chickens were more likely to be kept in a storeroom (Table 2). At night, healthy goats and sheep were typically tied to stakes inside the compound (48.6%) or fenced inside the compound (31.2%). Healthy cattle were tied to stakes (42.8%), fenced inside the compound (34.6%), or confined outside the compound (22.6%). Animal health management

Survey respondents reported that their poultry experienced several signs of poor health in the last three months prior to the survey, including having white stool (20%), rough feathers (17%), and paralysis (9%). Small ruminant keepers reported diarrhea (35%), runny nose (21%), and difficulty walking (26%) as the most common recent health problems. During FGDs and interviews, participants mentioned specific diseases among animals that caused economic impacts, including foot-and-mouth disease among cattle and small ruminants; sheep pox and parasites (e.g., tapeworm) among small ruminants; Newcastle disease and fowl pox among poultry; and rabies for dogs and cats.

To prevent disease, FGD and interview participants said that some livestock keepers participate in vaccination campaigns organized by the government or collectively request a veterinarian to come vaccinate animals in the community. Vaccinations reported included those for Newcastle disease for poultry and pasteurellosis for cattle and peste des petits ruminants for small ruminants. Most participants said that veterinarians administered most vaccinations, though some said men in the household would vaccinate small ruminants and poultry themselves. Participants also noted that male heads of household made the decisions about animal vaccination. Aside from vaccination, participants said they provided antibiotics (e.g., oxytetracycline for cattle) and deworming medications to animals, and that the owner of the animal or head of household was responsible for any associated costs. Households surveyed said men in the household treated sick poultry (14.8%), goats/sheep (16.8%), and cattle (23.1%) (Table S1, Table S2). Additional veterinary services included treatment and general advice (e.g., breeding, fattening). A couple of FGD and interview participants mentioned that para-veterinarians conducted checks of meat from animals slaughtered when sick. (Para-veterinarians are village-based private animal health workers who have basic skills in animal health and management, including administration of oral deworming medication. They assist private or government veterinarians and conduct epidemiological surveillance of animal diseases and vaccination coverage.)

When asked about the management of sick animals at home, all FGD and interview participants said that they kept sick animals at home rather than sending them to graze, out of fear of them dying or making them sicker. Several also said that they separated sick animals at home, feeding and watering them separately from healthy animals to avoid contamination. The household survey data showed that households confined sick goats, sheep, and cattle like they confined healthy animals. Most FGD and interview participants, including para-veterinarians, said that sick animals likely to die were slaughtered for eating; however, participants also said that if the animals' innards, such as the intestines, were “destroyed” or “affected”, then the animal was not eaten. Participants also noted differences between the slaughter and meat of healthy versus sick animals, such as the amount of blood and the texture of the meat.

“If an animal is sick and likely to die, we slaughter it. If the intestines aren't affected, we eat it. I don't know the difference between the two because I don't do the slaughtering…Meat from a sick animal can take much longer to cook than meat from a healthy animal. Meat from a healthy animal is more delicious than meat from a sick animal.” – Interview, Village 2, Female livestock keeper.

Animal feces management

Animal feces management was extensively explored during qualitative activities and the household survey. In FGDs and interviews, participants mentioned that men, women and children were involved in animal feces management. This was also seen in the survey, where half of respondents reported that men cleaned the chicken confinement area while 40%, 21%, and 7% of respondents also reported that chicken confinement areas were cleaned by boys, women, and girls, respectively (Table 3); several qualitative reports corroborated children's involvement in cleaning poultry housing. Responsibility for cleaning goat/sheep confinement areas was similar, though with a greater involvement of men (72%). Cleaning cow confinement areas was primarily the responsibility of men (93%) and boys (36%) and rarely women (3%) and girls (2%). However, respondents reported that cleaning up animal feces outside of confinement areas (i.e., in other household spaces) was primarily the responsibility of women (approximately 90%) and girls (15–30%); men and boys were rarely involved (<10%). During discussions and interviews, participants reported that animal feces were removed from animal confinement locations within the compound and other household spaces using brooms, shovels, rakes and calabashes; cans, cups and pieces of tin were also used for cleaning poultry housing. In the survey, sweeping was the most common method reported for animal feces removal (Table S4).

Most discussion and interview participants said that any animal feces within the compound were disposed of on the household garbage heap (Fig. 2). This was corroborated by observation, where observers noted animal feces disposed in garbage heaps in 14 of 15 households. Alternatively, cattle manure collected in cattle enclosures outside of the village would remain there until just prior to the rainy season, when it would be transported to crop fields. Small ruminant manure was applied to smaller gardens. Some discussion and interview participants said that animal feces were also kept in pits, with added water, ahead of use in gardens or crop fields. 100% of household survey respondents said their household composted cattle manure, with full and equal involvement of men, women, boys, and girls. Survey respondents reported that 85 to 99% of men, 14 to 25% of women, 17 to 30% of boys, and 0 to 1% of girls used manure as fertilizer, depending on the type of animal. A couple participants mentioned that cow dung was used “to beautify homes.” In contrast, all survey respondents said they did not compost chicken feces (99%) or goat/sheep droppings (97%). In a few FGDs, we also explored timing of animal feces manure application to crops (as fertilizer) and found variation in responses: all year round or cycles of every 15 to –30 days, with waiting periods of 20 to 30 days before harvest, or no waiting period between application and harvest. Participants also said that they used chemical fertilizers.

Some participants said that feces removal practices did not vary by season, while others said that animal confinement places were not cleaned during the rainy season due to other obligations. Several participants said that feces from cattle enclosures were not removed during the rainy season; rather, cattle were moved to other enclosures, and then the feces were collected ahead of the following rainy season. Otherwise, the frequency of removing animal feces from confinement locations varied: monthly, every three months, or a couple of times a year for cattle; daily, weekly, or every two weeks for small ruminants; and every two weeks to every couple of months for poultry; or, for some, “as needed” depending on the volume and number of animals. Most survey respondents said that animal feces inside the house was removed multiple times a day, while feces in the courtyard was removed less frequently for both poultry and small ruminants (Table S3).

Human contact with animals and animal products

In addition to questions about animal feces management, we explored other contacts with animals and animal products through the qualitative activities and household survey.

Milking

According to FGD and interview participants, milking of cattle was primarily done by men, with some support from children. Milking of small ruminants was done by women and children. When FGD and interview participants described how they milked their animals, there was very little mention of hygiene practices such as washing animals' udders or milkers' hands. However, the majority of survey respondents reported washing hands (96%), the collection bucket (97%), and the animal's teat (74%) before milking, though fewer reported washing the animal's teat after (26%) milking (Table S7).

Slaughter

Almost all qualitative participants said that the slaughter of animals was done by men, and that women prepared and cooked the meat, while children washed stomachs and intestines and burned the limbs and heads of animals after slaughter. A single participant said that children were not involved in the slaughter of animals. Individual-level involvement in slaughter was not asked about in the survey.

FGD and interview participants reported several different approaches to disposal of slaughter waste: disposing of the waste onto household garbage heaps or burying waste in holes for later use as fertilizer (if it was not first consumed by dogs); burning of waste (out of concern that other animals would smell the waste and become upset); and disposing of waste outside the village, including in old wells. In the survey, the most common disposal location for slaughter waste was the garbage heap (88%). Survey respondents noted that one hygiene practice when slaughtering animals was killing the animal in a clean location away from a drinking water well or toilet (Table S5).

Birthing

According to FGD and interview participants, only adults were involved in calving or difficult animal births. Some participants reported that placentas from animal birthing were hung on tree branches to dry and to be kept out of reach of dogs. Birthing was only explored during qualitative activities.

Carcass disposal

In cases where animals died of disease before being slaughtered, FGD and interview participants said the carcasses were disposed of far from the village to prevent community members from smelling them. Poultry and small ruminants that died of disease were typically thrown in the bush near the village or into a pit or unused well. Before the carcasses decomposed, village dogs and raptors were said to feed on them. Cattle carcasses were typically burned, as were those of some small ruminants. The vast majority of survey respondents said that animal carcasses were thrown in the bush. According to the household survey, carcass disposal was primarily the responsibility of men and boys (Table S6).)

Other contacts between adults and animals

In addition to the activities described above, other contacts between adults and animals described during FGDs and interviews included contact during general care like feeding or washing (sheep only), or when animals approached them while they were preparing or cooking food. Additional contacts were reported during agricultural activities, when leading animals from one point to another, when animals fought, or to pet the animal.

Other contacts between children and animals

During each of the 15 household observations conducted, children were observed either caring for or playing with animals, and animals were observed eating and/or drinking out of kitchen utensils. FGD and interview participants also said that children also came into contact with animals when giving them general care, and that they were often responsible for tying and untying animals before moving them out to pasture. Children also reportedly played with animals during activities like milking, but also independently; for example, by using them as mounts, trying to catch chickens, or just playing with dogs and cats.

Adults identified risks and benefits to children's interactions with animals, as one male participant describes here:

“Children help us take care of our animals. Children give food and bran to goats. They collect fodder from the bush, water the animals, tie and untie animals that are under the shed. During the rainy season, the children lead the small ruminants to pasture. When the children tie up or release the animals, they take the animals by their necks or by their legs. This can build the bond between children and animals. Animals can kick or gore children, children can be injured by animals. Children can stain themselves with pet hair.” – FGD, Village 4, Mother of a young child.

In six of the household observations, children were seen playing with animal feces. In one FGD, mothers also mentioned that children played with animal feces, which was disliked by adults:

“Children, especially girls, play with the excrement of small ruminants. They pretend to cook this excrement; this game has no specific name. When we see the children playing with the feces, it makes us uncomfortable, so we ask the children to throw away the excrement.” – FGD, Village 4, mother of a young child.

During household observations, children were also observed eating (5/15) and drinking (5/15) from containers shared by animals, playing in animal enclosures (8/15), and playing in garbage heaps (2/15). All of the children observed spent the majority of the observation period playing or otherwise on the ground, and animal excrement was present in the yards of all observed households.

Recognition of health risks and zoonotic disease transmission

In most FGDs, participants said that they thought that animal feces could make people sick, frequently mentioning malaria as a concern, along with colds, stomachaches, headaches, and other ailments. In these discussions, participants cited flies and mosquitoes as ways animal feces could make people sick. A couple of participants mentioned the smell of feces or urine as a source of disease. Several participants said that small ruminant feces were the main issue, as those animals were kept in closer proximity to household dwellings. In one discussion, poultry feces were noted as a concern for the same reason:

“The excrement of small ruminants can make us sick because their enclosure is next to our rooms. There are also poultry droppings, because they defecate in our living spaces like the kitchen and our bedrooms.” – FGD, Village 1, Female livestock keeper.

When asked about zoonoses (“Have you heard of any diseases/health issues spreading from animals to humans?”), in about half of the FGDs, participants said they were aware of the possibility, but many also said they did not know how it could happen.

“We are used to hearing that animals can transmit diseases to humans, but this is testimony [by word-of-mouth]… We have no concept of communicable diseases spreading from animals to humans, let alone how they are transmitted.” – FGD, Village 2, Female livestock keeper.

In one group discussion, participants mentioned malaria as a disease that could be transmitted from animals (mosquitoes) to humans, and in another, participants said they had heard that chickens could transmit disease to humans.

In IDIs, most participants said they did not think that animal feces could cause disease, or that animals could transmit disease to humans.

“In my humble opinion, I do not agree and do not support the hypothesis of a disease going from animals to humans, because we are not of the same creature.” – Interview, Village 3, Male livestock keeper.

However, several interview participants said that animal feces could cause health problems for people, and that animals could transmit disease to people. Proximity of animal feces, especially wet feces, to household living spaces was again mentioned when describing how people could get sick from animal feces.

“Yes, animals can make people sick if they are in the yard, but my animals are kept outside my yard. I really ignore [don't worry about] (animal) excrement…because my animals are kept outside.” – Interview, Village 5, Male livestock keeper.

Malaria, stomach aches, headaches, coughing, and eye issues were mentioned as health problems people could get from animals or animal feces, but, similar to group discussions, some interview participants said that they did not know how people could get sick, or what the diseases were.

“I have heard of diseases/health issues spreading from animals to humans. I don't really know which animal may be concerned. I don't know the name of the disease. I learned that anyone can be affected by this disease.” – Interview, Village 5, Female livestock keeper.

Perceptions of animal and human waste

In the qualitative activities and the two community workshops where animal feces and human feces were discussed and compared, participants noted that animal feces smelled less than human feces and attributed this to differences in diet (humans as omnivores and livestock as herbivores). Participants in one workshop said that more flies swarmed on human feces. In both workshops, human feces were considered more disgusting or bothersome compared to animal feces. Human feces were said to have the most offensive odor, followed by poultry feces, dog and cat feces, small ruminant and donkey feces, and lastly cattle feces. A noted difference between animal and human feces was that animal feces were considered a valuable resource because they were used as fertilizer in fields and gardens, whereas human feces were not.

In the workshop where participants were asked about consuming food touched by flies that had recent contact with animal feces, five of eight said that they would eat the food if the flies had recently landed on cattle or small ruminant feces because they had affection for these species. However, none said they would eat the food if the flies had recently landed on donkey feces, because donkey products are not consumed. Likewise, none said they would consume the food if the flies had recently landed on poultry, dog, or cat feces, due to disgust associated with those droppings. They predicted that their community members would have similar reactions.

Discussion

While most prior studies on human exposure to animal feces have examined risk factors related to the source (e.g., animal ownership) and contaminant concentration (e.g., presence of animal feces, animal-origin pathogens), instead of human behavior [26], we explored exposure to zoonotic transmission more broadly to inform an animal-inclusive version of CLTS that advances human, animal, and environmental health. Our interdisciplinary research team, which included public health, WASH, and veterinary experts, synergistically combined their knowledge of content and methodology to explore a wide range of risk factors for zoonotic disease transmission beyond fecal-oral exposure, including animal milking, birthing, slaughtering, carcass disposal, and other animal handling practices like feeding, watering, and providing medical care. While these behaviors are frequently documented in livestock and zoonotic disease research, they are rarely considered in WASH programs or research efforts [26], [27], [28]. Examining this range of behaviors allowed us to capture child-animal interactions that facilitate exposure to animals and animal feces, including children playing with livestock and in livestock confinement locations and engaging in livestock care, including milking, slaughter, and carcass disposal. There are few studies from similar settings that describe the wide range of behaviors and practices that contribute to human exposure to animal feces in this detail [29], [30], [31], [32], [33], [34], [35]. Ballard et al. developed and validated a survey assessment of children's fecal-oral exposure to zoonotic enteropathogens (FECEZ Enteropathogens Index) in rural Ecuador [36]. Their index considers a wide range of possible exposures to animal feces from the environment and children's behavior, including caregivers' and children's contacts with animals during animal care, children's geophagy behaviors, and animal entry and defecation inside the house. Our findings, like those from Ecuador [36], underscore the importance of looking beyond animal ownership and the presence/absence of animal feces as indicators of animal feces exposure.

Our study also highlights the importance of considering gender and age dynamics in the type and degree of exposures to animals and animal feces experienced by various household members. Similar to our study findings, an assessment of zoonotic pathogen exposure in South Africa found that women were responsible for caring for poultry and small ruminants (and sometimes cattle) and slaughtering poultry, while men were responsible for caring for cattle, sometimes assisting in difficult cattle births, and slaughtering goats and cattle [37]. However, in that setting, there were no reports of children's interactions with animals beyond taking goats to graze [37]. Our study adds to the emerging evidence that men, women, boys, and girls may have different degrees of exposure to different zoonoses [38] and, relatedly, different opportunities for intervention [37]. As such, W(animals)SH and One Health programs should consider exposure differences by age and sex when determining an intervention's target population and implementation strategy [39]. For example, an animal-inclusive WASH intervention aiming to reduce chicken infections through vaccination and improve chicken feces management may need to involve both women and men since women are often in charge of day-to-day poultry management while men are often responsible for administering medicine or vaccines, and building poultry housing [40], [41]; both men and women would need to be involved in risk assessment studies and intervention program delivery.

One Health-informed W(animals)SH interventions work synergistically to reduce human exposure to zoonoses while supporting the health of animals [27], which provide critical contributions to household incomes, food security, and female empowerment [39]. Our study also suggests that soil health can also be supported by including training on composting to reduce the use of uncomposted manure and chemical fertilizer on gardens and crop fields, which can overburden the soil with nutrients and pathogens and cause water pollution [42], [43]. Child nutrition can also be supported if healthier or more abundant animals produce more animal products for children to consume [44], [45].

To advance the development of One Health-informed W(animals)SH interventions, we explored how animal feces might be incorporated into CLTS “triggering” activities, where uncontained (typically human) feces serve as visual aids and the foci during demonstrations intended to motivate community members into action to reduce open defecation [18]. In our setting, we found that context-specific values ascribed to certain livestock carried over to risk perceptions about their feces. This suggests that some animals' feces may not elicit the same disgust reaction that the CLTS triggering activities rely on when done with human feces. We also shared the findings from this formative research with participating communities and programmatic and government key stakeholders to help inform how to best integrate animals into CLTS with a One Health approach. The complete methods and results for intervention development and implementation are discussed in a companion article [46].

There are limitations to this work. First, for some topics explored during group discussions and interviews, we focused on breadth rather than depth, such as the roles of household members in animal husbandry. However, this meant that some topics were not sufficiently explored, including the age ranges of children engaged in animal husbandry activities. As a result, we did not design the household survey to capture the activities that young children versus adolescents might engage in. These differences could have important implications for exposures and targeted interventions. Additionally, we primarily explored feces management for production animals and did not explore how dog and cat feces were managed; this is an under-explored topic in the W(animals)SH literature. Second, the animal husbandry practices documented in this research were self-reported, which may mean exposures might not be fully captured due to social desirability bias. Third, the qualitative data were collected only once, at the end of the dry season. While this allowed both men and women to participate, we may have captured more detailed information about seasonal differences had we conducted the focus groups and interviews across two seasons. Lastly, the majority of our survey respondents were female, so we may not have captured accurate or comprehensive information on cattle and animal health management as these are usually managed by men in our study setting.

Conclusion

Our study suggests that to develop effective One Health-informed W(animals)SH interventions, intervention development teams should include experts from both WASH and animal health sectors, evaluate and consider the types of animals present, gender- and age-specific roles in animal husbandry and critical control points; and assess context-specific practices, perceptions, and priorities that will influence intervention content and delivery.
