Revisiting Globalized CAD Risk Assessment in the Era of Artificial Intelligence
Authors: Junjie Yang, Ran Xin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Coronary artery disease (CAD) remains the leading cause of mortality worldwide.1 Studies have consistently documented a disproportionately increased burden of obstructive CAD among specific Asian ethnic subgroups, particularly those of South Asian ancestry.2 The pretest probability (PTP) assessment for CAD serves as a foundation for clinical decision-making, directly influencing diagnostic pathways, resource allocation, and ultimately patient outcomes.

However, accumulating evidence reveals significant limitations in current PTP models when applied to diverse ethnic and geographic populations. PTP models derived from Western populations (eg, European Society of Cardiology-PTP, American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology-PTP) have demonstrated significant calibration bias when applied to Asian cohorts, compromising their clinical utility in these populations.3 This discrepancy is particularly problematic considering the unique epidemiologic characteristics of CAD in Asian populations, where atypical symptom presentation is more common and traditional risk factors may have different predictive values compared with Western populations. Although region-specific models such as the Chinese registry in early detection and risk stratification of coronary plaques score have demonstrated promising performance, their generalizability to other ethnic groups remains uncertain.4 Collectively, these findings underscore the urgent need for more globally applicable PTP assessment tools that adequately account for the substantial heterogeneity in CAD presentation and risk factors across different populations.

In this issue of JACC: Asia, Baskaran et al5 proposed The Global Pretest Probability Study of Coronary Artery Disease (GPS-CAD) and aim to address these limitations by integrating multicenter data from 6 continents to develop a universally applicable PTP model through the use of advanced artificial intelligence (AI) technologies. This initiative is poised to establish a new benchmark for equitable risk estimation in the cardiovascular health domain.

Key Innovations and Advantages of the GPS-CAD Study

The GPS-CAD study uses a novel global multicenter design, with a target enrollment of 100,000 participants and more than 60,000 subjects already integrated from 17 countries across 6 continents. This extensive and representative dataset provides an essential foundation for developing robust, generalizable PTP models that account for global ethnic and geographic variations. The geographic distribution of the study sites achieves comprehensive global coverage, with enhanced representation from regions that have been historically under-represented.

To address the challenge of data heterogeneity in multicenter studies, the GPS-CAD study applies rigorous quality control through a central coordinating center and standardized parameter definitions. These measures are designed to harmonize data acquisition and processing across diverse sites. Specifically, the study defines consistent criteria for coronary artery calcium and computed tomography scanning protocols, thereby ensuring data consistency across all participating sites. This standardized approach is especially essential for imaging-derived parameters such as plaque composition and coronary artery calcium scores, which exhibit marked ethnic variability and necessitate careful calibration to enable valid cross-population comparisons.

Beyond the robust data foundation, the methodologic innovations of GPS-CAD represent another key advantage. It innovatively incorporates a “country + race/ethnicity” composite variable within multivariable, mixed-effects logistic regression models. This approach significantly enhances traditional PTP models by incorporating hierarchical data structures and population-level variations. The mixed-effects model integrates both fixed-effect variables (eg, age, sex, and traditional risk factors) and random-effect variables (eg, study site, geographic region, and study design), allowing a more nuanced analysis of the complex interplay between patient-level characteristics and population-specific factors. This improvement is particularly significant because it addresses the historical tendency of Western-developed models to overestimate CAD risk in Asian populations. The integration of machine learning algorithms, particularly eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), with traditional statistical methods constitutes a further innovative dimension of the GPS-CAD methodology.6 This hybrid approach maintains clinical interpretability while leveraging machine learning to identify complex nonlinear relationships and interactions among predictors.

The Main Challenges Faced by the Study

Despite its sophisticated methodology, the GPS-CAD study has to address the persistent challenge of advancing racial and ethnic classification beyond the current reliance on self-reported categories. The investigators should incorporate mechanisms for continuous refinement with new data, potentially through Bayesian updating or periodic recalibration. This is particularly important given the rapid epidemiologic changes underway in many Asian and other developing countries, where changes in lifestyle, diet, and environmental exposures are significantly altering CAD risk profiles. Moreover, the implementation of GPS-CAD in real-world clinical practice will require the development of decision-support tools that are integrated into electronic health record systems. These tools should provide not only risk stratification but also clinical decision guidance based on local resources and practice patterns, along with the establishment of effective performance feedback mechanisms. Additionally, to ensure the quality and robustness of AI-based prediction modeling central to this initiative, several fundamental criteria for development and validation must be borne in mind, including complete reporting, carefully defined intended use of the model, rigorous validation, sufficiently large sample size, and openness of code and software.7

Future Prospects of the GPS-CAD Study

Continuous technological iterations can effectively integrate genetic biomarkers and socio-cultural factors, thereby establishing more refined population classifications with richer biological information.8 Furthermore, the integration of polygenic risk scores offers a promising direction to enhance model precision across diverse populations.9 However, this approach must be carefully implemented to ensure that genetic data adequately represent global diversity and avoid the bias toward European populations in genome-wide association studies. Recent initiatives aimed at diversifying genetic databases offer a promising avenue to overcome this limitation in future iterations of the model. Beyond enhancing the intrinsic accuracy of the model, an equally important future direction lies in its real-world implementation. The development of a stratified implementation framework is explicitly designed to accommodate the substantial heterogeneity in healthcare resources across different global settings. Future iterations of such models could be tailored for distinct clinical environments, acknowledging the marked disparities in diagnostic capabilities between advanced tertiary care centers and primary care facilities. For instance, in well-resourced tertiary hospitals with comprehensive cardiac imaging capabilities, a full multivariable model could be deployed to maximize predictive accuracy by incorporating advanced parameters. Conversely, for primary care settings and regions with limited diagnostic resources, a streamlined version with a reduced set of core clinical variables should be developed to maintain robust predictive performance while ensuring practical applicability and ease of use. This stratified approach recognizes that an ideal risk assessment tool must balance predictive accuracy with practical accessibility.

The era of globalization in healthcare has already begun and its momentum is irreversible. Currently, it resembles a network being woven, unevenly tight in different areas. In some sectors, such as research and development, the network is already closely knit, but there remain gaps in areas such as standardized algorithms based on the global medical data. The key going forward will be how to strike a balance between the efficiency gains from accelerated innovation, and the sovereignty of national health systems. This requires international organizations, governments, medical institutions, and technology companies to work together to build more effective cooperation and regulatory frameworks. On the whole, we are in the early, crucial shaping period of the wave of healthcare globalization. It is hoped that the GPS-CAD study will significantly contribute to global CAD risk assessment in the era of AI.
