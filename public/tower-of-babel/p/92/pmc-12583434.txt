Application of ultra-wide-field optical coherence tomography angiography in detecting peripheral lattice degeneration
Authors: Chunyang Cai, Peiyu Liu, Qingquan Wei, Jiawei Huang, Lan Yi, Fang Wei, Qinghua Qiu, Chao Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aims to characterize retinal and vitreous morphological changes in peripheral lattice degeneration using ultra-wide-field optical coherence tomography angiography (UWF-OCTA) guided by scanning laser ophthalmoscope (SLO) and to provide illustrative clinical applications.MethodsA total of 15 eyes from 15 patients with peripheral retinal lattice degeneration were enrolled between January 2024 and March 2024. Peripheral lattice lesions were localized by SLO, followed by optical coherence tomography (OCT) and UWF-OCTA to evaluate retinal and vitreous changes.ResultsUWF-OCTA identified diverse retinal and vitreous changes in LD, including vitreous traction, retinal rupture, retinal thinning, vitreous detachment, and subretinal fluid collection. Clinical cases demonstrated that UWF-OCTA may detect lesion details missed by OCT, especially tiny retinal rupture holes.ConclusionUWF-OCTA enables high-resolution imaging of peripheral retina and comprehensive visualization of retinal and vitreous changes across lesion areas. SLO-guided UWF-OCTA shows potential as a supplementary imaging tool for visualizing subtle retinal lesions in peripheral lattice degeneration, laying the groundwork for future studies on its role in guiding clinical decisions.

Introduction

Lattice degeneration (LD) is the most common type of peripheral retinal degeneration, affecting approximately 6–8% of the population [1]. It typically occurs in the equatorial or more peripheral retina, presenting as elongated, round, or oval-shaped lesions [2]. Histologically, LD is characterized by inner retinal thinning, adjacent vitreous liquefaction, and vitreous condensation at the edges of degenerative areas [3]. Retinal thinning at the lesion site and vitreoretinal adhesion at the edges may result in retinal ruptures or even rhegmatogenous retinal detachments, ultimately threatening vision and ocular health [4]. Therefore, early identification of vitreous traction and retinal ruptures in LD areas is crucial for assessing the necessity for preventive treatments such as laser photocoagulation.

Currently, LD diagnosis relies primarily on indirect ophthalmoscope, fundus photography, and optical coherence tomography (OCT) [5]. The Optos Daytona P200T scanning laser ophthalmoscope (SLO), with an ultra-wide-angle fundus imaging system, captures 200° (82%) of the retina in a single scan without pupil dilation, facilitating the screening or monitoring of peripheral LD [6]. However, SLO cannot confirm vitreous traction, localized neuroepithelial detachment, or retinal holes within degenerative areas [5, 7]. OCT, another key diagnostic tool in LD, detects vitreous-retinal interface, subclinical retinal detachments, and retinal holes around lesions, and has been proposed as an adjunct for assessing the necessity of preventive laser therapy for LD [4, 8]. However, OCT may miss small holes and subtle lesion details [5, 9].

Optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA) is primarily used for central retinal pathology, and its utility in peripheral retinal imaging was previously considered limited [10]. Consequently, few studies have explored the application of OCTA in LD diagnosis. However, the ultra-wide-field OCTA (UWF-OCTA) from TowardPi Medical Technology offers a maximum imaging range of 24*20 mm, corresponding to 120° intraocular angles. Combined with patient eye rotation, it can scan peripheral and far-peripheral retina. Its fine scanning lines enhance capture of peripheral retinal pathology details.

In this study, SLO was used to localize and define the extent of retinal LD. Guided by SLO, UWF-OCTA (in structural imaging mode) characterized the morphological changes in the retina and vitreous within LD areas. We present SLO, OCT, and UWF-OCTA images from a series of patients with LD to explore the clinical utility of SLO-guided UWF-OCTA.

Methods

The study involving human subjects adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki, and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Tongren Hospital. All participants provided informed consent for the use of ophthalmic examination images.

From January 2024 to March 2024, patients underwent comprehensive ophthalmic examinations at Shanghai Tongren Hospital, including best-corrected visual acuity, intraocular pressure, slit-lamp examination, SLO (P200T, Daytona, Optos, Dunfermline, Scotland, UK), OCT and UWF-OCTA (BM-400 K BMizar, TowardPi Medical Technology, Beijing, China). The SLO generated 200° retinal images with 14 μm resolution in less than 0.5 s (https://www.optos.com/products/daytona/). The UWF-OCTA had a 24 × 20 mm imaging range, 3.8 μm axial resolution, 10 μm lateral resolution, and 1536 × 1280 blood flow imaging resolution. Patients cooperated with examiners for eye rotation during OCT/UWF-OCTA scans.

All images were independently reviewed by two clinical experts (C.S. and Q.Q.) with over 10 years of vitreoretinal experience. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus, with an overall inter-rater agreement of 97.33%. The scanning parameters of UWF-OCTA can be dynamically adjusted according to the characteristics of the lesion. For the size of the lesion, the minimum scanning range that can cover the lesion (3 mm*3 mm, 6 mm*6 mm, 12 mm*12 mm, 15 mm*15 mm, etc.) can be selected, thereby optimizing the resolution for specific lesion sizes.

Qualitative assessments of SLO, OCT, and UWF-OCTA images focused on anatomical changes in the retina and vitreous, including: (1) (U-shaped) vitreous traction; (2) retinal rupture (including atrophic holes and retinal detachments); (3) focal retinal thinning; (4) vitreous detachment; (5) subretinal fluid accumulation [8].

Discussion

This study explores UWF-OCTA as an adjunct tool for peripheral LD detection. Consistent with structural OCT, we used UWF-OCTA’s conventional scanning mode (not blood flow detection) to visualize retinal structures. SLO, OCT and UWF-OCTA examinations were conducted on 15 patients with retinal LD, with both OCT and UWF-OCTA detecting vitreous traction, retinal ruptures, retinal thinning, vitreous detachment, and subretinal fluid. These characteristic changes, especially retinal ruptures, were challenging to detect using SLO alone.[11] For example, in cases 4 and 5, OCT and UWF-OCTA revealed anatomical details of the retina and vitreous that were not visible in the SLO, including significant vitreous traction, retinal thinning, extensive retinal ruptures, and subretinal fluid accumulation. In case 3, OCT and UWF-OCTA revealed anothor lesion area in temporal inferior retina that was unsuspected in SLO, along with evident U-shaped vitreous traction, retinal thinning, retinal rupture, and subretinal fluid accumulation.

UWF-OCTA can provide a more comprehensive display of the lesion details and capture tiny retinal ruptures. Large retinal ruptures (e.g., Case 4) are easily detected by both modalities and even SLO. However, for small retinal ruptures, OCT scan may skim over the tiny area, whereas the UWF-OCTA can increase the detection possibility of small lesions. In case 1, the OCT missed a tiny retinal hole, whereas UWF-OCTA clearly captured multiple images of the hole. This could potentially alter subsequent treatment or follow-up plans for the patient with LD. Moreover, UWF-OCTA provides more consistent lesion visualization. In case 2, only one OCT image showed subretinal fluid, whereas UWF-OCTA consistently presented the subretinal fluid and retinal morphology across multiple images, aiding assessment of extent and 3D structure for LD area.

Currently, there is controversy about the necessity of preventive treatment for LD [12]. Some advocate laser photocoagulation only for LD with retinal ruptures, previous history of retinal detachment, retinal detachment in the other eye, or a family history of retinal detachment [13–15]. Conversely, others recommend laser photocoagulation for overlying vitreous traction even without ruptures to prevent subsequent retinal pathology [16]. Regardless of the decision to pursue preventive treatment for LD, it is crucial for clinicians to possess efficient, accurate visualization of retinal/vitreous changes in LD region by examinations[17].

OCT provides high-resolution, non-invasive images of retina, choroid, and vitreous, aiding diagnosis of various fundus diseases [16, 18]. In LD, OCT identifies vitreous traction, retinoschisis, retinal ruptures and detachments [11], supporting its use as an adjunct for treatment decisions [4, 8]. However, conventional OCT equipment exhibits relatively large intervals between scan lines, potentially resulting in missing small holes and certain lesion details [5, 9]. In contrast, UWF-OCTA offers denser scan lines, enabling the capture of more comprehensive lesion details and compensating for this limitation. Previous OCTA devices had limited scanning ranges, with a maximum range of 12*12 mm corresponding to a resolution of 1024*1024 and an intraocular angle of 75 degrees, which was considered insufficient in assessing peripheral retinal lesions [19, 20]. However, the UWF-OCTA used in this study has a maximum scanning range of 24*20 mm, corresponding to a resolution of 1536*1280 and an intraocular angle of 120 degrees. Therefore, this study innovatively utilized UWF-OCTA to examine peripheral LD. Beyond OCT capabilities, UWF-OCTA detects micro retinal holes and provides seamless lesion visualization, supporting its potential as an adjunct for determining prophylactic laser need.

It should be noted that this study was based on exploration. After consulting with the patients, OCTA examinations were also conducted on those patients who had positive results from OCT. In clinical practice, UWF-OCTA is mainly performed when the OCT results are unclear or the technology is insufficient. This process not only conforms to the clinical protocol but also minimizes the economic burden on the patients to the greatest extent.

Limitations and future directions

This study is exploratory, with a small sample size and a lack of long-term outcome data, which reflects its focus on clinical observation rather than formal diagnostic validation. The selection of cases was based on clinical needs, introducing selection bias that might overestimate its utility. Therefore, it is currently not possible to conclude that UWF-OCTA can guide the decision-making for preventive laser treatment. It mainly proposes a hypothesis that UWF-OCTA may help increase the detection rate of high-risk lesions.

Notably, UWF-OCTA should be positioned as a supplement to, not replacement for, OCT. Its value lies in capturing tiny lesions within comparable scan ranges, and this advantage becomes useful only when OCT results are inconclusive. The clinical selection of cases leads to an inherent imbalance in the dataset, making direct quantitative comparisons of detection efficacy between modalities methodologically inappropriate. The lack of a control group and quantitative validation (e.g., calculation of sensitivity, specificity, or inter-modality agreement rates) prevents us from systematically evaluating UWF-OCTA’s diagnostic performance relative to OCT. Future prospective studies with consecutive enrollment and a predefined control group are needed to fill this gap. Besides, standardized lesion grading systems would enhance objectivity, but current challenges include dynamic scan range adjustments and random detection of sub-millimeter holes (dependent on scan line spacing). Future studies with larger cohorts should develop multi-modal grading criteria.

UWF-OCTA images may be affected by several artifacts that could lead to false-positive interpretations. For instance, posterior vitreous detachment (PVD) can create linear or punctate shadows mimicking retinal holes. During our study, 2 cases initially presented such PVD-induced artifacts, which were later confirmed as non-pathological through multi-modal correlation (combining SLO fundus morphology and OCT structural sections). Additionally, eye movement during scanning may cause motion artifacts, manifesting as discontinuities in retinal layers that could be misinterpreted as retinal ruptures. To mitigate such misjudgments, we strictly applied the following criteria: (1) All suspicious lesions identified by UWF-OCTA were cross-validated with at least one other modality (SLO or OCT); (2) For ambiguous cases, repeat UWF-OCTA scanning was performed to confirm lesion stability.

While this study focused on UWF-OCTA’s structural imaging mode, we also explored the potential of flow data. Preliminary observations showed that flow angiograms could visualize perilesional vascular changes (e.g., reduced capillary perfusion around retinal holes), which might indicate lesion activity. However, we found that flow data did not improve the detection of tiny retinal holes, and flow mode provided no additional diagnostic value for identifying hole boundaries or distinguishing true holes from artifacts. Given this limited utility and the exploratory design of the study, we did not include flow data in the analysis of our manuscript, but this finding highlights a direction for future research to evaluate whether flow parameters can predict lesion progression.

Conclusion

UWF-OCTA enables comprehensive visualization of anatomical structures in peripheral lattice degeneration and captures subtle details (e.g., tiny retinal holes) that may be missed by SLO or OCT. As a supplementary tool to OCT, it improves the visualization of peripheral lesions but does not replace conventional modalities. Due to the exploratory design, small sample size, and lack of quantitative validation, the current results are preliminary and intended to propose a research direction rather than draw definitive conclusions. Future prospective studies with control groups and outcome data are needed to further validate UWF-OCTA’s clinical utility in lattice degeneration management.
