Addressing the Recurrent and Protracted Cholera Outbreaks in Africa: Challenges and the Way Forward
Authors: Nebiyu Dereje, Merawi Aragaw, Kyeng Mercy, Hiwot Moges, Tamrat Shaweno, Landry Dongmo Tsague, Collins Tanui, Sofonias Kifle Tessema, Tajudeen Raji, Yap Boum, Mosoka Papa Fallah, Ngashi Ngongo, Jean Kaseya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The recurrent and protracted outbreaks of cholera in Africa demand concerted multi-sectoral interventions. It is critical to increase investment in water, sanitation and hygiene, enhance multisectoral response coordination, strengthen vaccination, and ensure political commitment to address the cholera outbreak.Subject terms: Viral infection, Preventive medicine, Developing world, Public health

Background

Cholera has been a recurrent and protracted public health emergency that has been claiming lives, affecting the socio-economic well-being of countries, and testing the resilience of public health systems in Africa1. Over the decades, several African countries have been affected by recurrent outbreaks of cholera, with 535,321 cases and 4007 deaths in 2023. Globally, 1.3–4.0 million cases and 21,000–143,000 deaths are attributed to cholera every year1. This burden, exacerbated by underinvestment in water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH), climate change, and insecurities, has tested the resilience of public health systems in Africa. The Global Task Force on Cholera Control (GTFCC) sets targets and strategies to end cholera (reduce deaths by 90% and eliminate cholera from 20 high-burden countries) by 2030, including early detection and containment of outbreaks through rapid multisectoral response, a focus on cholera priority areas for multisectoral interventions, effective coordination mechanism for technical support, advocacy, resource mobilization, and partnership at local and global levels2.

The World Health Organization (WHO) Regional Office for Africa conducted self-readiness assessments in 2022 and 2024 to evaluate countries’ capacities for detecting and controlling cholera outbreaks. Based on the 2022 assessment results and preliminary analysis of the 2024 assessment, no country in the WHO African Region demonstrated good readiness3. Specifically, the assessments revealed that 21 countries possessed moderate capacity, while 11 countries exhibited limited capacity to effectively respond to cholera outbreaks3. To operationalize the global strategy, the WHO African Region released its regional framework in August 2018 with clear objectives and targets for Africa. However, cholera outbreaks remain widespread and protracted with higher mortality rates in Africa, underscoring the urgent need to change the approach with stronger political commitment, enhanced multisectoral and coordinated action, and collaborations from every stakeholder4.

As of June 01, 2025, twenty African Union Member States have reported 133,181 cases and 2687 deaths attributed to cholera (Case fatality rate (CFR) = 2.0), with 11 countries above the World Health Organization’s targeted CFR threshold of less than 1.0%2. Major drivers of cholera outbreaks and the way forward

Cholera remains a disease of inequity in Africa, disproportionately affecting the impoverished and vulnerable communities. Its resurgence is driven by weak inter-ministerial coordination, inadequate WASH (water, sanitation, and hygiene) infrastructure, and limited political prioritization5,6. Many African countries lack access to safe drinking water, with open defecation still prevalent in countries like South Sudan and the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC)6,7. Rapid urbanization has led to the growth of informal settlements lacking basic services, creating ideal conditions for cholera transmission. Climate change further exacerbates outbreaks by increasing flooding, droughts, and water contamination. Conflict and displacement—such as in Sudan, Nigeria, Ethiopia, and the DRC—disrupt health services and worsen vulnerability7,8. Countries must invest in integrated urban planning, climate-resilient health systems, and culturally tailored sanitation solutions to address this outbreak. Urgent, equity-driven investment in WASH infrastructure is essential to interrupt transmission, protect vulnerable populations, and advance the broader goal of cholera elimination in Africa. Peacebuilding and cross-sectoral collaboration are essential to ensure equitable access to health and prevent future outbreaks.

Africa’s health systems remain fragile, with weak surveillance, limited diagnostics, and poor access to medical countermeasures, such as oral cholera vaccines (OCVs). Access to essential medical countermeasures—diagnostics, treatments, and vaccines—is vital for timely outbreak response. It is also critical that regional and global efforts are made to strengthen supply chains and improve last-mile delivery. Integrating OCVs into national cholera control strategies will help countries respond more effectively to both endemic and epidemic situations. Risk communication and community engagement (RCCE) strategies must be tailored to local contexts to reduce vaccine hesitancy and promote behavior change.

OCVs, including Shanchol™ and Euvichol-Plus™, are vital for prevention, especially in endemic and humanitarian settings9. Thus, countries must reinforce preventive vaccination programs, particularly in communities affected by climate impacts, conflicts, or living in humanitarian settings. However, global supply shortages of OCVs have severely limited access. In 2023, African countries requested 70.7 million doses but received only 26.1 million. In 2024, Africa received just 26.3 million of the 43.2 million requested. This shortage is not solely due to rising cholera cases but also stems from limited commercial interest among manufacturers, especially in high-income countries, as the vaccine is low-cost and requires bulk sales to be profitable. Drawing lessons from recent outbreaks in Africa, it is critical to establish public–private partnerships and early investment to accelerate vaccine development and distribution in Africa by strengthening leadership and donor engagement towards accelerated mechanisms for cholera vaccine manufacturing in Africa10. Africa must invest in vaccine manufacturing and vaccine research and development. Moreover, supportive cholera treatments such as oral rehydration salts (ORS), IV fluids, and antibiotics need to be available and accessible. However, these treatments often face stock-outs during large outbreaks due to weak supply chains, underscoring the need to ensure prior preparedness to outbreak response.

Surveillance is largely passive, underfunded, and fragmented, with minimal community-based or cross-border coordination11. Despite progress in genomic sequencing—expanding from 7 countries in 2019 to 43 in 2025—basic diagnostic tools like rapid tests remain scarce in rural areas due to infrastructure gaps and supply chain issues. Decentralizing laboratory systems and training personnel are critical to improving outbreak detection and response9.

In response to the rising number of cholera cases and deaths, and the need for a concerted and well-coordinated response, the Heads of State of cholera-affected countries proposed a multi-sectoral cholera response plan under the leadership and coordination of the Africa CDC and WHO12. This recommendation builds upon the experiences from the continental incident management support team (IMST), such as the One-team, One-plan, One-budget, and One M&E coordination of the response, strengthened surveillance, early warning systems, and cross-border collaboration, laboratory, and increased access to vaccines and efficient last-mile delivery of medical countermeasures13,14. The need for increased domestic health financing, accelerating the African vaccine manufacturing agenda, improving case management efforts, proactively working towards peace and stability, and comprehensively expanding WASH infrastructure and community engagement to address the overwhelming cholera outbreak in multiple countries in Africa has been underscored (Table 2)15.

Proposed cholera outbreak response pillars, objectives, and key actions

Countries must establish or strengthen a National Presidential Task Force on cholera to coordinate multisectoral action. These high-level bodies will serve as the core for multisectoral coordination, bringing together ministries of health, water and sanitation, education, infrastructure, finance, and humanitarian affairs. Their role is to ensure that cholera control is not siloed but integrated across sectors, with clear accountability, emergency resource allocation, and cross-border collaboration to detect and respond to outbreaks swiftly.

Water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) infrastructure remains a cornerstone of cholera prevention. There is an urgent need to scale up equitable investments in safe water supply and sanitation, particularly in high-risk and underserved communities. Informal settlements and rural areas often lack even the most basic WASH services, making them hotspots for recurrent outbreaks. Accelerated investment in inclusive urban planning and rural WASH infrastructure is essential to break the cycle of transmission.

In conflict-affected and hard-to-reach areas, cholera response is often disrupted by insecurity. Africa CDC proposes coordination with the AU Humanitarian Agency and the Department of Health, Humanitarian Affairs and Social Development (HHS) to establish secure humanitarian corridors. These corridors are essential for the uninterrupted delivery of medical supplies, clean water, and sanitation services.

Conclusions

The current surge of cholera in several countries in Africa, fueled by under-investment in WASH infrastructure, impacts of armed conflicts, climate change, and behavioral challenges of the populations, requires concerted efforts of international and national multi-sectoral communities. The response efforts must be well-coordinated, targeted, and systematically monitored. The lessons and best practices from other outbreak responses, including coordination and leadership, surveillance, strengthening the laboratory systems, investment in WASH, vaccination, research, RCCE, and logistics and supply, must be leveraged in a multi-sectoral approach to effectively control the current outbreak and expedite the progress towards ending cholera by 2030. Ending cholera by 2030 will also require transformation of primary healthcare (PHC) systems into community-rooted, epidemic-ready, and climate-resilient platforms.
