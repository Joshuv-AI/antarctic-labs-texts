The 30-Day Risk of Sudden Death in Patients With Epilepsy: A 10-Year Population-Based Cohort Study
Authors: Cheng-Yi Fan, Chih-Wei Sung, Eric H Chou, Yun-Ting Chih, Edward Pei-Chuan Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and PurposeEpilepsy is associated with increased risk of sudden death (SD). The risk factors of developing SD in patients with epilepsy are not fully addressed. This study aimed to develop and validate a short-term prediction model for SD in patients with epilepsy using nationwide data from Taiwan.MethodsA retrospective cohort study used data from the National Health Insurance Research Database from 2011 to 2020. It focused on epilepsy patients over 18 years old who were prescribed anti-seizure medication (ASM). The study evaluated patient comorbidities, medication adherence, and recent hospital admissions. It aimed to assess the association between these factors and the occurrence of SD within 30 days. The analysis used a multiple logistic regression model and decision-tree classifier and assessed predictive accuracy using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve.ResultsOut of 161,773 treatment events, 3,454 SD events were identified (2.1%). Factors associated with increased risk of SD included older age, intensive care unit admission, chronic kidney disease, psychotic disorder, poor ASM adherence (medication possession rate <0.5), and recent intravenous ASM use. The logistic model's area under curve was 0.752 in the 2020 testing dataset, and the testing dataset's sensitivity, specificity, positive likelihood ratio, negative likelihood ratio, and diagnostic odds ratio were 0.569, 0.832, 3.391, 0.518, and 6.543, respectively. A calibration curve showed reasonable alignment between predicted and observed probabilities of SD.ConclusionsPatients with epilepsy recently admitted to the hospital, showing poor ASM compliance and using intravenous ASMs, face an increased risk of SD within 30 days. Improving ASM adherence and treatment optimization may reduce this risk.Keywords: epilepsy, seizure, anticonvulsants, medication adherence

INTRODUCTION

Epilepsy is a neurological disorder that affects individuals of all ages, with a prevalence ranging from 2.6 to 14.9 per 1,000 people.1 Patients with epilepsy may have comorbidities, including cardiovascular disease, metabolic disorders, or central nervous system neoplasms.2 Both epilepsy and its comorbidities are associated with an increased risk of sudden death (SD). The annual incidence of SD among patients with epilepsy is approximately 1.2 per 1,000, markedly higher than the 36.3 to 55.4 per 100,000 observed in the general population.3,4

The causes of SD in patients with epilepsy are multifactorial. For example, compared to those without epilepsy, patients with epilepsy have three times the rate of SD.5 Additionally, cardiovascular disease-related deaths and traumatic SDs from drowning or vehicle accidents are also common in patients with epilepsy.6,7 Some cases of SD in patients with epilepsy are not linked to any identifiable cause, a phenomenon known as sudden unexplained death in epilepsy. Extensive research into the risk factors for SD in epilepsy has not yet resulted in a generalizable model for addressing this complex condition.

Identified risk factors, mostly from case-control studies, include epilepsy profiles, anti-seizure medication (ASM) usage, and patient comorbidities.8 Other studies linked increased seizure frequency and recurrent generalized tonic-clonic seizures (GTCS) with SD.9,10 Epileptic disorders often require long-term and regular ASM treatment, with a higher risk of SD observed in patients undergoing polytherapy with multiple ASMs and those with frequently changing treatment regimens.11 Poor treatment adherence increases seizure frequency; however, its association with SD risk in epilepsy remains unclear. Opeskin et al.12 found no difference in adherence between patients who experienced SD and those who died from other causes and Sveinsson et al.13 discovered that patients with documented nonadherence had a 2–3 times higher risk of SD compared to those who adhered to their recommended treatment regimen. These conflicting findings highlight the need for additional, well-powered investigations.

We require prediction models created using larger sample sizes to validate potential risk factors for SD. This study aims to develop and validate a short-term SD prediction model for patients with epilepsy using a nationwide health database in Taiwan.

METHODS

Study design and setting

The retrospective longitudinal cohort study based on population data applied a repeated-measurement analysis. From January 1, 2011, the enrollment day was defined as the date patients had their first outpatient clinic visit with ASMs prescribed. To allow sufficient time for baseline variable assessment, the first observation day was defined as 90 days after the enrollment date. Subsequent observations were conducted every 30 days until the end of the study period. At each 30-day interval, we independently evaluated the predictor variables and outcome status. A prediction model was generated by pooling data from all repeated measurements.

For example, if a patient was enrolled on January 1, 2011, their first observation day would be March 31, 2011 (90 days later). The outcome would then be assessed within the period from March 31 to April 29, 2011. The next observation would begin on April 30, 2011, with outcome assessed from April 30 to May 29, and so on (Supplementary Fig. 1A in the online-only Data Supplement).

The patients' data were collected from the National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD), managed by Taiwan's Health and Welfare Data Science Center. The database was based on the National Health Insurance, which reached a 99% coverage of Taiwan's citizens in 2004. This database provides de-identified medical records encompassing patients' demographics, diagnoses, medication, and health expenditures. The study has been approved by the National Taiwan University Hospital Hsin-Chu Branch (approval number: 110-152-E) and the National Health Research Institutes (approval number: EC1101217-E). Informed consent from the patients was waived.

Patients with epilepsy: inclusion and exclusion criteria

In the NHI system, patients with epilepsy were identified from January 1, 2011, to December 24, 2020, using diagnostic codes (International Classification of Diseases [ICD]-9th Edition code 345 and ICD-10 code G40) and prescriptions for ASMs. The inclusion criteria were: 1) patients over 18 years, 2) diagnosed with epilepsy using specific diagnostic codes, and 3) prescribed at least one ASMs.

To ensure relevance to routine epilepsy management, we included only patients who were initially prescribed ASMs commonly used as first-line treatment. Based on national data, we selected ASMs that were prescribed as the first ASM in more than 1% of patients with epilepsy in Taiwan.14 A complete list of included ASMs can be found in Supplementary Table 1 (in the online-only Data Supplement).

We excluded the following patients 1) those who began treatment after December 24, 2020; 2) those who died before the first observation day; 3) those who died within 7 days after the first observation day; and 4) those who were not prescribed with any qualifying first-line ASM. A 7-day buffer was applied to accommodate the death documentation process.

Given that some patients may not require ASMs if their condition is well-controlled, we discontinued observation for any period during which a patient went more than 180 days without ASM use. If a patient started retaking ASMs after this period, we began a new observation period. This approach has been used in other epilepsy studies (Supplementary Fig. 1B in the online-only Data Supplement).15

Potential predictors and definitions

Pre-existing comorbidities—including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, cardiac disease, chronic kidney disease, cerebrovascular disease, asthma, hyperlipidemia, peripheral arterial occlusive disease, and psychotic disorders—were identified using the NHIRD's “Specific Treatment Items,” a set of fee-for-service codes that physicians submit when managing chronic conditions. For each comorbidity, a patient was classified as having the condition if they had ≥2 outpatient claims for the corresponding code. Each treatment-item code is mapped to one or more ICD-10 diagnoses; the full list of codes and their mappings is provided in Supplementary Table 2 (in the online-only Data Supplement). Intensive care unit (ICU) or ward admission was defined as any admission within the previous 365 days.

The medication possession rate (MPR), ranging from 0 to 1, is a clinical metric used to assess medication adherence. MPR is calculated based on the prescription records by dividing the number of days a patient has taken their prescribed medication by the total number of days in a specified observation period. All fills with supply dates falling anywhere within the window were included, even if no claim occurred exactly on the interval boundary. Higher MPR values indicate better adherence. Polytherapy was used to designate the use of two or more ASMs. Patients with intravenous ASM use are those who have used intravenous ASMs within the previous 365 days. Regimen changes are defined as having more than three changes in the medication regimen over the past year. Cold months were defined as the six months with lowest monthly average temperature based on reports from Taiwan's Central Weather Administration, which is from November to April.

Outcome

The main outcome assessed was the occurrence of SD events within 30 days following each observation day. That is, for every 30-day observation interval, we determined whether an SD event occurred in the subsequent 30 days. SD events were identified using specific ICD-9 codes (427.4, 427.5, 798, and 798.1) and ICD-10 codes (I46, I46.2, I46.8, I46.9, I49.0, I49.01, and R99) in combination with a triage level 1 status assigned upon arrival to the emergency department. Given the nature of the NHIRD, which lacks access to autopsy findings or documented cardiac rhythms, we could not determine the precise cause of death (e.g., cardiac vs. neurological). Therefore, this operational definition of SD was designed to broadly capture unexpected death events potentially related to epilepsy, encompassing both cardiac and non-cardiac etiologies.16

We chose a 30-day window to assess the outcome to align with Taiwan's National Health Insurance policy, where patients with a “chronic illness refill prescription” typically receive 30 days of medication per fill.17 This period reflects real-world medication-taking behavior and allows us to evaluate the short-term relationship between MPR and the risk of SD.

Statistical analysis

Two analysts managed the dataset and conducted statistical analyses using the SAS software (version 9.4, SAS Institute Inc.) and the R software (version 4.4, R Foundation for Statistical Computing). The unit of analysis for model development was individual observation period, rather than unique patients or treatment events. Data from 2011 to 2020 were used for model development and evaluation. Specifically, data from 2011 to 2019 were used for model development, and 80% of the data were allocated as the training set for model training. The remaining 20% were used as the internal validation set used in the assessments of performance and reproducibility. Treatment events from the year 2020 were held out as an external testing cohort. The potential predictors are chose based on previous studies on SD risk in patients with epilepsy first, then we run a univariate logistic regression to identify those with p value below 0.1. The final predictors selected by these two criteria will enter the multiple logistic regression model.

The Student's t-test and chi-square test were used to compare continuous and categorical variables, respectively. Multiple logistic regression analysis was conducted to evaluate the association between predictors and SD events, with results presented as adjusted odds ratio (aOR) and 95% confidence interval (CI). A nomogram was constructed to graphically represent each predictor's impact on the probability of SD. In the nomogram, age corresponds to points based on the line segment provided. The density plot for the total score and age displays their distribution. For other categorical variables, their distribution was represented by the size of the box in the box plot. The points are determined by the middle point of the box in the plot. The scores of all variables were summed to obtain the final total score, which was then used to estimate the probability of SD occurrence.

In addition to logistic regression, we trained a decision-tree classifier to explore potential non-linear interactions among predictors. Modelling was performed in SAS with PROC HPSPLIT, using the same training cohort (2011–2019; 80%) as the logistic model. To prevent over-fitting, we restricted tree complexity to a maximum depth of 20 and no more than 25 terminal leaves. After training, the tree was applied without modification to the internal-validation (2011–2019; 20%) and external-testing (2020) datasets, and class-membership probabilities were exported. The optimal probability cutoff was chosen at the maximum Youden Index derived from the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve.

For both the logistic and decision-tree models, discrimination was assessed using the area under curve (AUC) in training and testing data. A calibration curve assessed the agreement between predictions and observations across different percentiles of the predicted values. A confusion matrix was used to calculate sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), positive likelihood ratio (PLR), negative likelihood ratio (NLR), and diagnostic odds ratio (DOR) for direct performance comparison.

RESULTS

Patient selection process

Fig. 1 illustrates the stepwise selection process for epilepsy patients from 2011 to 2020. Initially, 181,266 patients diagnosed with epilepsy were identified. We excluded patients younger than 18 (n=20,861) and with unknown (i.e., undocumented) sex (n=1,281). Patients observed outside the study period (n=2,276) and those who died before the first observation day (n=5,167) or within seven days of an observation day (n=2,757) were also excluded. Lastly, we excluded patients who were not prescribed any of the selected first-line ASMs (n=12,430) and those who were not prescribed any ASM at all (n=2,619). In the end, 161,773 treatment events were included. The flowchart eventually identifies 3,454 SD events occurring within 30 days following the observation day, representing 2.1% of the eligible treatment events.

Table 1 showed the characteristics of the population on their first observation day. Patients who experienced SD were distinctly older than survivors (63.3±17.5 years vs. 54.4±19.2 years, p<0.001) and had more utilization of ICU (32.3% vs. 23.3%, p<0.001) and general ward (23.0% vs. 16.7%, p<0.001). Pre-existing diseases—including chronic kidney disease, cerebrovascular disease, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and psychotic disorder—were all significantly more prevalent in the SD group. Treatment patterns also differed: intravenous ASM use (44.2% vs. 33.8%, p<0.001) and polytherapy (31.2% vs. 29.1%, p<0.001) were more common among SD cases, whereas poor adherence was slightly less common (47.4% vs. 52.0%).

The model development cohort included 149,670 treatment events, while the testing cohort included 12,103 (Supplementary Table 3 in the online-only Data Supplement). The training cohort was older and had higher rates of ICU and ward admission and more pre-existing comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, cardiac diseases, chronic kidney disease, asthma, MPR, and intravenous ASMs. However, there were fewer SD events during cold months.

Each variable in the model was assigned a weighted point score proportional to its regression coefficient. Predictive performance of the 30-day risk of SD in the 2020 testing dataset

Fig. 3 depicts the ROC curve, indicating how well the model can identify the 30-day risk of SD in the testing dataset for 2020. The curve compares the true and false positive rates at different thresholds. It represents the model's ability to differentiate between patients who experienced SD within 30 days and those who did not. The diagonal line represents the point at which the model performs no better than random chance (AUC=0.5). The AUC was calculated to be 0.752 with a 95% CI ranging from 0.751 to 0.753. An AUC of 0.752 indicates a moderate level of discrimination, suggesting that the model can reasonably classify patients at risk of SD correctly.

ROC curve for SD prediction. The ROC curve of 30-day SD in the testing set was displayed, along with the AUC and the corresponding 95% CIs. AUC, area under the curve; CI, confidence interval; ROC, receiver operating characteristic; SD, sudden death.

Table 3 summarizes the predictive model's performance for 30-day SD in both validation and testing datasets. In the validation dataset, the model had a sensitivity of 0.655, specificity of 0.791, and accuracy of 0.791. The PPV was 0.001, and the NPV was 0.999. The PLR was 3.132, and the NLR was 0.436. The DOR was 7.180. In the testing dataset, the sensitivity was 0.569, the specificity was 0.832, and the accuracy was 0.832. The PPV was 0.002, and the NPV remained at 0.999. The PLR was 3.391, and the NLR was 0.518. The DOR in the testing dataset was 6.543.

DISCUSSION

Study summary and strengths

We developed a short-term SD prediction model for use with patients with epilepsy. This model showed a moderate level of discrimination. Older patients and those with recent ward or ICU admission, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, and psychotic disorders had a higher 30-day SD risk. Poor ASM compliance and recent intravenous ASM appear to increase SD risk in our model. A significant study strength was our use of a nationwide longitudinal database. The model provides physicians with a precise estimated short-term SD risk. We also evaluated the MPR drug compliance using direct calculations from the database to ensure accuracy. By excluding patients with untreated episodes, we avoided underestimating the influence of adhering to a prescribed drug regimen.

Model performance may be constrained by the inherent unpredictability of SD

Despite several modelling efforts, the AUC of our logistic regression remained stable at 0.752, indicating a ceiling to discriminative improvement. First, a substantial fraction of SD cases—over one-third in prior chart-review studies—lack a clear etiology and therefore evade prediction by any risk-factor model.18 Second, predictors used in the model focus on epilepsy management and comorbidities; deaths unrelated to seizure might be difficult to predict. In the decision-tree classifier, the extreme class imbalance (approximately 2% events) and the rarity of SD impose constraints on the algorithm's sensitivity. Even non-linear decision trees underperformed due to this imbalance, enforced leaf sparsity, and predominantly additive predictor–outcome relationship—conditions under which the parsimonious logistic model is inherently optimal.

Trauma and psychotic disorders can cause SD in patients with epilepsy

We examined all SD etiologies, including those resulting from trauma. Unlike other conditions, epilepsy increases the risk of trauma.19 For example, patients newly diagnosed with epilepsy and those with comorbidities had a markedly increased risk of accidental injuries and psychotic disorders (potentially increasing suicide risk).20,21 In our analysis, we found that patients with epilepsy's comorbid with psychotic disorder were associated with higher rate of SD (aOR 1.238; 95% CI: 1.102–1.390). Therefore, considering all potential SD etiologies may inform the development of future prediction models.

Epilepsy treatment and disease status are associated with SD

In our models, general ward (aOR 2.143; 95% CI: 1.841–2.494) and ICU admission (aOR 1.713; 95% CI: 1.457–2.014) last year had the highest odds ratio. Intravenous form of ASM is usually used in hospital for patients with breakthrough seizure or refractory epilepsy.22 These three factors may indicate poor epilepsy control. These findings aligned with studies from DeGiorgio et al.9 and Serrand et al.,23 which found that frequent seizure or GTCS were associated with increased risk of SD.

Multiple, co-administered ASM treatments may indicate drug-resistant epilepsy.24 Polytherapy also increases the risk of SD compared to monotherapy.9,11,25 However, a meta-analysis found that adjunctive ASM may reduce the incidence of SD in patients with previously uncontrolled seizures.26 Sveinsson et al.13 found that, after adjusting for GTCS frequency, polytherapy (>3 ASMs) predicted increased SD risk. They also found that poor treatment compliance and prolonged time since the last dispensed ASM were major risk factors for SD. In our study, polytherapy is a potential protective factor against SD after adjusting to patients' compliance (aOR 0.869; 95% CI: 0.765–0.987). The current study applied the MPR to quantify the ASM compliance on a per-patient basis. Previously, a post-hoc analysis from the RANSOM study found that an MPR<0.8 was associated with a 3-fold higher risk of mortality.15 In our study, we chose a lower threshold for poor compliance (MPR <0.5), and it was associated with higher risk of SD (aOR 1.149; 95% CI: 1.013–1.304). The association between ASM compliance and SD might be due to the increased risk of seizure attacks in patients with poor ASM compliance; these attacks may increase the SD risk.13

Study limitations

The study had several limitations. First, we excluded patients not treated for more than 180 days. While this could cause a selection bias, this method avoids misclassifying patients whose SD risk is well controlled as having poor drug compliance (although these cases might also demonstrate true nonadherence). Second, we failed to account for several important SD risk factors in patients with epilepsy (e.g., age of epilepsy onset, intellectual disability, and nocturnal seizure).9,23 Third, we were unable to distinguish between epilepsy-related and non-epilepsy-related admissions, potentially overestimating epilepsy's predictive value. Finally, we failed to estimate the overall incidence of SD as only SDs resuscitated in the hospital were included in our analysis.

In conclusion, epilepsy patients recently hospitalized, on intravenous ASMs, and with poor adherence face higher 30-day SD risk. A health policy to improve compliance and treatment effects is essential for enhancing epilepsy treatment quality.

A: Illustrates a continuous treatment episode, in which the first observation day occurs 90 days after ASM initiation (enrollment), followed by repeated 30-day observation intervals throughout the study period (2011/01/01 to 2020/12/31). B: Depicts an interrupted treatment episode with re-initiation: after the initial enrollment and 90-day lead-in period, a gap of ≥180 days without ASM marks treatment discontinuation. Resumption of ASM thereafter constitutes a new enrollment, with its own 90-day lead-in and subsequent 30-day observation windows. ASM, anti-seizure medication.

Supplementary Fig. 2

Calibration plots for predicting 30-day SD. The dotted line represents perfect calibration, where predicted probabilities align with observed outcomes. The 10 dots, accompanied by red confidence intervals, represent deciles of the population, divided according to predicted probabilities. SD, sudden death.
