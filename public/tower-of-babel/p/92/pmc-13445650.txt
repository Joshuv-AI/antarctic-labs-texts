Contrasting perspectives and applications of the Good Samaritan Overdose Prevention Law: insights from people who use and distribute drugs in Rhode Island
Authors: Joseph Silcox, David Pletta, Jaclyn M.W Hughto, Sabrina Rapisarda, Sofia Zaragoza, Patrick J.A. Kelly, Stephanie Vento, Josiah Rich, Traci Green
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe overdose crisis in the U.S is complex, with opioid overdoses compounded more recently with rising stimulant-involvement. Individuals who experience or witness overdoses often avoid calling 911 due to fear of criminal-legal system consequences. Many U.S. states have “Good Samaritan Laws” (GSLs) protecting those seeking help for overdoses, but research is lacking on the understanding and role of GSL in overdose response among people who use stimulants (PWUS) and criminal-legal populations. Our mixed-methods analysis focused on PWUS from a community-based sample in a state with GSL protections, Rhode Island, and participants incarcerated at the Rhode Island Department of Corrections (RIDOC) for charges related to drug distribution. We analyzed survey and interview data to discern differences in knowledge and attitudes regarding Rhode Island’s GSL in the two samples.ResultsOverall awareness of the GSL was widespread among the community sample (61.1%) and the RIDOC sample (63.3%); however, more RIDOC participants (70%) than PWUS in the community (42.9%) could accurately describe the law. Participants in both samples reported deeply rooted fears associated with the consequences of help-seeking and skepticism of protections provided by the GSL, particularly around criminal-legal consequences. While the Rhode Island GSL provides protections for probation or parole violations, many RIDOC participants were fearful that these protections would not apply to them, despite the law being amended to provide these protections. Additionally, participants from both samples reflected on differences in help-seeking behaviors among unexpected opioid and stimulant overdoses, discussing how their interpretation of overdose symptoms influenced decisions to call for help (e.g., call 911) or not.ConclusionsVarying degrees of understanding of the Rhode Island GSL, along with fears of police involvement in overdose situations, underscore the need for targeted interventions, policy change, and re-education efforts about overdose prevention and the GSL. Findings can inform policy and professional practices associated with GSLs.

Introduction

The current opioid crisis, exacerbated by a toxic and unregulated drug supply, has underscored the need for effective overdose response measures. Most problematic within the volatile drug supply is fentanyl, which, when found unexpectedly in the stimulant supply (e.g., crack and powdered cocaine), can increase overdose fatalities (Hughto et al., 2022; Spencer et al., 2022). People who primarily use stimulants may lack opioid tolerance and may be less familiar with opioid overdose prevention (Hughto et al., 2022; Kelly et al., 2023). Relatedly, fatalities involving stimulants only, excluding fentanyl, are also on the rise, highlighting the need to better understand and respond to stimulant-involved overdose (CDC, 2025; Hoots et al., 2020; Hughto et al., 2025). Moreover, fear of legal repercussions can deter individuals from seeking medical attention during overdoses (Latimore & Bergstein, 2017; Wagner et al., 2021), contributing to approximately 107,941 overdose deaths in the U.S. in 2022 (Spencer et al., 2022).

In response to overdose surges, ‘Good Samaritan Laws’ (GSLs) for overdose intervention of fatal overdose prevention were introduced to provide selective legal immunity to individuals seeking medical help for themselves or others during drug-related emergencies (Green & Nace, 2024; Latimore & Bergstein, 2017; Moallef et al., 2021; Reader et al., 2022). First enacted in New Mexico in 2007, GSLs now exist in the majority of U.S. states (Lieberman & Davis, 2023). GSLs have evolved over time to be responsive to concerns among people who are at risk of overdose, incorporating provisions aimed at enhancing their effectiveness (Reader et al., 2022) and limiting the possible repercussions of criminal-legal system exposure (Popp, 2022). While some studies have found that GSLs increase emergency service calls (Jakubowski et al., 2018), others have identified persistent barriers to help-seeking and inconsistent application by criminal-legal system actors (Latimore & Bergstein, 2017; McLean, 2018; Pollini et al., 2006; Wagner et al., 2021). GSLs typically include caveats that allow police to arrest those calling for help or experiencing overdose if there are outstanding warrants, probation or parole violations, weapons possession, or if they are e suspected of drug distribution (Koester et al., 2017; Latimore & Bergstein, 2017; Wagner et al., 2019). Conversely, research has shown that GSLs providing specific protections against arrest are more effective than those only protecting from prosecution or simple possession charges (Hamilton et al., 2021; Reader et al., 2022; van der Meulen & Chu, 2022).

Research exploring perspectives of people who use drugs (PWUD) regarding GSLs offers valuable insights into the impact and efficacy of these laws, and have primarily focused on the impact of GSLs on reducing opioid overdose mortality (Hamilton et al., 2021; McLean, 2018). Several studies highlight varying degrees of understanding of GSLs and the protections they provide (Jakubowski et al., 2018; Schneider et al., 2020); however, less is known about the impact of GSLs on 911 help-seeking among people who use stimulants (PWUS). Most studies focused on GSLs awareness among PWUS suggest this population may not possess adequate knowledge of GSLs and may be less likely to call 911 following an overdose (Ambrose et al., 2016), relative to people who use opioids who may receive training on GSLs through their exposure to harm reduction programming (Evans et al., 2016). Such research is particularly relevant given the rise in stimulant overdose fatalities not involving opioids (Kariisa et al., 2021; Spencer et al., 2022). Similar to opioid overdoses, avoiding calling 911 may be motivated by fear of criminal-legal system consequences driven by disproportionate enforcement on racial and ethnic minorities (Kariisa et al., 2021; Rouhani et al., 2021). However, stimulant overdoses have a different clinical presentation and may advance to life-threatening conditions that necessitate medical attention. Therefore, there is a need to examine best practices related to help-seeking behaviors when these events occur (Hughto et al., 2025).

Additionally, research is needed to explore the views of people who distribute drugs (PWDD), including those incarcerated for charges related to drug distribution or manufacturing, as this population may be likely to encounter someone experiencing overdose and are positioned to intervene. Moreover, PWDD may also partake in drug use themselves, creating further challenges regarding help-seeking due to fear of criminal-legal involvement. PWDD and those incarcerated for drug-related crimes may also have a better understanding the limits of GSL protections due to proximity to the criminal-legal system. Arresting PWDD could result in drug market disruptions that further perpetuate overdose risk (del Pozo et al., 2024; Ray et al., 2023). Some states, like Rhode Island, have drug-induced homicide laws (Peterson et al., 2019) that can be enforced when a drug is supplied to a person who has overdosed, which may dissuade PWDD from calling 911 due to fear of arrest or incarceration (Beletsky, 2019; Carroll et al., 2023). Understanding these perspectives is crucial for tailoring interventions and policies that effectively address the complex dynamics of drug-related harm and overdose prevention (Bowles and Lankenau, 2019).

Rhode Island is a New England state heavily affected by the opioid crisis, an early adopter of the Good Samaritan Law (GSL), and has a long-standing program of overdose education and widespread naloxone provision, even in correctional settings (Martin et al., 2025; The Good Samaritan Overdose Prevention Act of 2016 R.I. Gen. Laws § 21-28.9-4, 2016, Green et al., 2015). Our study examined and contrasted the perspectives of PWUS and incarcerated PWDD in Rhode Island to explore how knowledge and awareness of the GSL differs between these groups. In comparing these groups, we focus on how the GSL is understood and interpreted to discern meaningful differences in how the GSL is conceptualized in the state. Further, because this study focused on both opioid and stimulant overdose events, our analysis explores whether there were meaningful differences in overdose responses from community members who use drugs versus those incarcerated for drug distribution at the Rhode Island Department of Corrections (RIDOC). Findings from this study can strengthen messaging about GSLs for both PWUS and PWDD, two critical stakeholder groups that may hold different concerns and perspectives towards the law.

Methods

This analysis draws from a larger mixed methods study of people who use or distribute drugs in two New England states, Massachusetts and Rhode Island, to identify risk and protective factors for stimulant and opioid-involved overdose (Hughto et al., 2024). All participants were surveyed, of which a purposive sub-sample completed an in-depth interview. We recruited (1) PWUS —cocaine (powdered or crack), methamphetamine, or street-obtained prescription stimulants—and (2) PWDD sentenced and incarcerated at RIDOC for charges related to drug distribution and/or manufacturing. Comparisons between these groups should be interpreted as reflecting differences in the current context (community vs. incarceration), rather than differences in past exposure to the criminal-legal system. Participants provided verbal consent to participate and were compensated $20 for completing a survey, and an additional $20 for completing an interview. The present analysis includes only participants from Greater Providence Rhode Island and RIDOC to compare community and incarcerated samples regarding knowledge and experience with Rhode Island’s GSL. Our mixed-methods approach used qualitative findings as the primary lens through which we interpreted and contextualized our quantitative results.

Community-based sample

Sampling procedures in the parent study involved both formative research with PWUD (Hughto et al., 2022) and review of surveillance data from Massachusetts and Rhode Island (Carroll et al., 2017; Shrestha et al., 2021). These processes informed both the locations where data were collected and overall study eligibility criteria. Participants of the parent study were then sampled through a modified respondent-driven sampling approach (see Hughto et al., 2024 for additional details). The Greater Providence Rhode Island community-based sample includes 56 participants recruited between March and April 2022 (Hughto et al., 2024). Eligible participants were 18 years or older; spoke English or Spanish; used an illicit stimulant in the past 30-days (e.g., powdered and crack cocaine, methamphetamine, or non-prescribed stimulants); and lived in or spent the majority of their time in the Greater Providence region.

Incarcerated sample

From April to July 2023, PWDD were recruited at RIDOC with the primary goal of learning how and why fentanyl enters into the stimulant supply and to further inquire on other adjacent topics (e.g., overdose response). Prior to data collection, a select group of 61 inmates were sent a letter that described the research and provided study contact information. Seven bushiness days after letters were sent, research staff screened and enrolled potential participants. All eligible participants in this sample were sentenced and incarcerated in RIDOC for manufacturing and/or distributing illicit drugs; incarcerated for ≤ three years; 18-years-old or older; and spoke and understood English or Spanish. Following screening processes, 30 participants were enrolled. All participants were first surveyed and then invited to be interviewed by research staff over the course of one hour. Interviews occurred in a private space where correctional staff were not present. Following site visits, staff documented post-visit ethnographic field notes further reflecting on emergent themes. For further details, see Kelly et al., 2025.

Survey and variables

The survey collected socio-demographics, drugs use, overdose history, knowledge of naloxone and overdose prevention policies, attitudes towards treatment, contamination of the drug supply, packaging and drug distribution, and use of protective tools like naloxone, fentanyl test strips, and drug checking. Participant survey responses were coded (yes/no) for analysis: awareness of the GSL; able to describe the GSL correctly in their own words, prior experience with an opioid overdose, and whether this occurred after consumption of opioids or after use of stimulants. Experiences with last witnessed opioid overdose and last witnessed stimulant overdose included: whether the participant personally called 911; personally administered naloxone; had any police interaction, and whether this interaction was positive/neutral vs. negative; had any interaction with Emergency Medical Services (EMS), and whether this interaction was positive/neutral vs. negative; familiarity with naloxone, including awareness, knowing where to get naloxone, and whether they carry or carried naloxone. The survey was programmed in Qualtrics for ease of administration.

Interviews

Community survey respondents were invited to participate in a qualitative interview if their survey responses warranted qualitative exploration (e.g., participant had unique or extensive use patterns of stimulants and opioids, overdose experiences, etc.) (n = 22) (Hughto et al., 2024). All 30 RIDOC participants completed an in-depth interview. Interviews assessed similar domains as the survey in greater depth, although there were some notable differences with respect to the topics covered in each interview guide (available upon request). Both community and RIDOC participants were asked to reflect on opioid and stimulant overdose responses, naloxone administration and interactions with first responders. Community participants were asked specific questions about the GSL, whereas RIDOC participants organically and unprompted reflected on GSL in discussions about overdose response.

Analysis

Analysis of quantitative data employed chi-square tests of independence using SAS OnDemand for Academics to explore associations between sample type (community or RIDOC) and the following survey variables: awareness of the GSL and ability to correctly describe it; characteristics of personally experienced opioid overdoses; characteristics of last witnessed opioid overdose and characteristics of last witnessed stimulant overdose. Transcribed qualitative interviews were assigned a randomly generated pseudonym, coded and analyzed in Dedoose version 9.0.17 and thematically analyzed by the research team (Braun and Clarke, 2006). While coding procedures are detailed elsewhere (see Hughto et al., 2024), briefly, interviews with PWUS were coded using a codebook developed by three trained coders and the third author. We used deductive codes consistent with the interview guide and inductive codes that emerged during an open coding process. This codebook was adapted to code interviews with PWDD following a similar deductive and inductive process. Following training and a reliability assessment, transcripts were independently coded, reviewed, and discussed at weekly coding meetings to resolve uncertainties and ensure consistent code application.

The current qualitative analysis was limited to interview content specific to GSL. Community sample excerpts tagged with a GSL code were exported into a separate document for analysis. Because RIDOC interviews included no specific GSL questions, our analysis is limited to questions and codes about overdose experience, many of which contain emergent perspectives on the GSL. To improve the rigor of our approach, we conducted a keyword search across all RIDOC transcripts for “GSL”, “Good Sam” and “Good Samaritan Law” to pull data not reflected in existing codes; the excerpts were also exported for analysis. All qualitative data were reviewed and thematically analyzed using Braun & Clark’s (2006) six-phase framework, employing an inductive approach to allow for themes to emerge from the data. The first author reviewed and applied secondary codes to the data, drafted reflexive analytic memos about emergent GSL-related themes. These themes were then refined and discussed collaboratively by the first and senior author.

The current study’s research protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Brown University as well as the RIDOC Medical Research Advisory Group.

Interview data converged with the survey data such that RIDOC participants tended to articulate a greater awareness of the GSL and a better overall understanding of its application during interviews relative to PWUS in the community. Participants from RIDOC spoke about their knowledge of GSL, reflecting on their lack of trust in the law, and also demonstrating a nuanced understanding of criminal consequences. ‘Luke’, an incarcerated participant who has a history of prior substance use, noted that, while GSL may afford some protections in an overdose situation, police may leverage information from the overdose scene for future action. He shared:It [the Good Samaritan Law] -- applies in my situation because I was out cold and I didn’t know somebody was calling. So, all they could do when they came to me in a car, was take my drugs. But if they [Police] come to your house and they see everything [drugs] -- they going back two days later, building a report and raiding your house two days later. They didn’t arrest you that day, but -- now they know what you are and what you’re doing…I mean, for me in a car, all they could do was take my drugs. So, that’s fine, but coming to your house, that’s a different story.

Conversely, our quantitative results helped explain that PWUS in the community were less likely to correctly describe the GSL. Even when community participants were familiar with GSL, they described a general lack of understanding about GSL among their peers and discussed the burden of needing to educate others. In an interview, ‘Curtis’, a community participant, noted “It’s very frustrating to me how few people seem to know about the Good Samaritan Law. I feel like I’m constantly telling people about that”. Similarly, another community participant, ‘Justin’, commented on the lack of consistent knowledge of GSL among community members, stating:It’s very important, because some people… they don’t even know [about the GSL], and you have one person that knows, like myself, and I tell you call the ambulance. But even if they don’t, I’m going to…because I know [the GSL]. So, they’re not going to be scared to get charged now. You know what I’m saying?

While there was recognition of insufficient and inconsistent knowledge of GSL, some community participants acknowledged the importance of the law in providing what they believed were necessary protections. For instance, ‘Thomas’, a community participant, shared his perceptions of GSL:It’s good [the GSL] because I really don’t care what’s going on, if I got warrants at the time, I’m too worried about the person getting help at the time, so it doesn’t really matter.

Here, Thomas describes how GSL aligns with his altruistic stance to responding to an overdose yet his remark conveys a level of misunderstanding about the law; there is no language in the Rhode Island statute that provides protections for outstanding warrants. Conversely, RIDOC participants, like ‘Cedric’, more consistently communicated how they would respond:“I’m not playing with somebody else’s life. Can’t have that on my conscience. I’m calling [the police], but I might leave…I might leave, make sure somebody’s there with them, but I’m not sticking around for the cavalry to show”.

Our survey results showed that RIDOC participants had an overall better understanding of GSL than community participants. However, there were meaningful qualitative differences as community members often conveyed that the law was not well understood broadly and shared a sense of responsibility to educate others regarding its protections. That said, both samples shared a similar distrust toward and fear of engaging with the police due to how the police might leverage these situations.

Criminal-legal system involvement as a barrier to calling 911

Most PWUS in the community had some level of involvement with the criminal-legal system within their lifetime: 91.1% had been arrested and 78.6% had been incarcerated. Discussion

This mixed methods study explored and compared the perspectives of PWUS in the community and incarcerated PWDD in Rhode Island, uncovering differences in their understanding and application of the GSL. We found that PWUS in Rhode Island were less likely to accurately describe the GSL than the incarcerated RIDOC sample, suggesting a need to further investigate the dissemination and application of the law in Rhode Island. Prior research indicates that bystanders with accurate GSL knowledge are more likely to call 911 compared to those with incorrect or incomplete understandings (Jakubowski et al., 2018). Consistent with the literature, our research found that PWUS in the community-based sample who were familiar with the GSL felt more compelled to intervene and call for help during overdose situations and felt a stronger sense of responsibility to educate others about the law. However, while greater knowledge of the law is beneficial, deep fears of, mistrust in, and contact with the criminal-legal system remain prominent and evidently drive reticence to seek help; therefore, increasing knowledge of the GSL alone may not always translate into traditional help-seeking behaviors like calling 911 (Jakubowski et al., 2018; Latimore & Bergstein, 2017; van der Meulen & Chu, 2022).

The PWDD we interviewed were savvy to GSL limitations likely applicable to their circumstances (i.e., drugs packaged for distribution on scene) and therefore largely refrained from help-seeking. This is likely because PWDD from the RIDOC sample, were more acutely aware of the consequences of criminal-legal involvement, whereas in some instances participants from the community may have not fully understood the protections of the GSL. Given that our community sample was comprised of people who use stimulants, it may be that people who use drugs other than opioids may benefit from more education on GSL application and protections. Alternatively, PWUS may utilize informal practices to mitigate stimulant overdose symptoms without formal intervention (i.e., calling 911) and may not be as well connected with harm reduction organizations that provide overdose response education and training on GSL typically to people who use primarily opioids (Evans et al., 2016; Kelly et al., 2023).

There were also discrepancies between the law on the books and the perception of GSL protections. Some participants suggested that police misinterpret circumstances where the GSL should protect PWUD or leverage information during overdose response calls for subsequent criminal pursuits. Despite the intended goals of GSLs, practical application by police officers allows for discretion through their institutionalized lens (Beletsky et al., 2005). Studies have shown that despite having GSLs, police do not always consistently apply these laws and cannot always accurately define to whom or when they apply (Banta-Green et al., 2013; Carroll et al., 2020; Koester et al., 2017; Xavier et al., 2021), though police trainings have been shown to improve GSL knowledge (Saucier et al., 2016). Increasing transparency on GSL protections may encourage help-seeking behavior in PWUD and PWDD (Jakubowski et al. 2024), yet persistent fears of criminal-legal system contact may hinder their doing so. Future research should explore the need for targeted police interventions, along with further investigating police discretion in GSL application across U.S jurisdictions.

Similar to other New England states (e.g., Massachusetts, Vermont), the Rhode Island GSL contains protections extending to probation and parole aimed to mitigate concerns of calling 911 during an overdose (The Good Samaritan Overdose Prevention Act of 2016 R.I. Gen. Laws § 21-28.9-4, 2016), which were included because of high community supervision rates and the need for extended immunities (Bender, 2016). Despite this language, RIDOC participants communicated fears that calling for help during an overdose would violate conditions of their probation or parole. In some instances, participants described calling for help and subsequently being punished or incarcerated, suggesting greater oversight of GSL application is needed. Targeted interventions should focus on educating currently and formerly incarcerated people on all aspects of the GSL to mitigate fears of violating probation or parole when seeking help during an overdose. While violation fears were particularly salient within our RIDOC sample likely due to the fact that they were currently incarcerated at the time of the interview; these views were less commonly expressed by our community-based sample. Additionally, we found that Rhode Island’s drug-induced homicide law impacted decision-making related to calling 911. Prior research found that these laws dissuaded people for calling for help (Peterson et al., 2019), our research extended these findings highlighting that PWDD were aware of these laws and fearful of being given harsher sentences if they were to be charged with a homicide following an overdose. In turn, decision-making in these instances reflected dealers pulling products off the market or intervening by calling 911. Further research on the possible synergy between GSLs and drug induced homicide law effects is warranted.

Conversely, participants differed in their perceptions on when bystanders should call 911 based on the type of overdose events. We distinguished between unexpected opioid overdoses (i.e., overdoses caused by fentanyl unexpectedly present in a stimulant) and stimulant overdoses (i.e., overdoses caused solely by the effects of using stimulants). Our findings found no significant difference between the community and incarcerated samples in calling for help during opioid overdoses. However, we found that RIDOC participants were significantly less likely to call 911 during stimulant overdose events due to fears of stimulant overdose effects and potentially harmful police interactions with someone overdosing on stimulants (i.e., overamping) (Mansoor et al., 2022). Further, research on PWUS by Ambrose et al. (2016) found that reported stimulant use by the people who experienced an overdose was associated with lower odds of calling 911, suggesting a need for behavioral interventions focused on educating PWUS on best practices around overdose response. In Rhode Island, the GSL uses language attributed to “drug or alcohol overdose” and does not use expansive language to describe what “drug” means (The Good Samaritan Overdose Prevention Act of 2016 R.I. Gen. Laws § 21-28.9-4, 2016.). Our findings suggest the need for comprehensive overdose education curriculum to specifically address the GSL in application to opioid and stimulant overdoses, including signs, symptoms, and best practices for responding to both types of overdoses (Hughto et al., 2024).

Finally, the majority of participants in both samples communicated mistrust and fear of engaging with police following an overdose. This commonality suggests that fear surrounding help-seeking may be largely based on the extensive criminal-legal system involvement histories that many participants shared (McLean, 2018). Our findings point to opportunities for educating police on proper application of the GSL, and providing alternative pathways for help-seeking where individuals can call for help during an overdose without police involvement (e.g., emergency medical professional or peer trained first responders) may mitigate this fear (Formica et al., 2022; Wagner et al., 2019). De-implementing policies of universal police co-response in suspected overdose is another strategy, since such calls are rarely “combative” and minimize criminal-legal involvement on scene (Becker, 2021; Macmadu et al., 2022).

Limitations

There are several important limitations to our study. We only examined GSL in one state, yet states vary in the legal protections afforded to help-seekers and those experiencing a suspected overdose. Some GSLs provide protection from arrest, probation or parole violations, prosecution for drug charges, or other protections like sentence mitigation (Hamilton et al., 2021). Thus, our findings may not be transferable to states with different GSLs. Additionally, our research focused primarily on PWUS and did not include perspectives from people who only use opioids or other drugs. Given the illegality of most drugs in the U.S., we believe the help-seeking behaviors and perspectives of PWUS we describe are relevant if not directly generalizable to people using other drugs. Interview questions about GSL were limited to community participants due to time constraints, despite that RIDOC participants commonly discussed GSL in relation to prior overdose experiences and responses; this might have limited our ability to directly compare and contrast perspectives. Finally, the incarceration setting and experience may have influenced reporting of traumatic events like an overdose, potentially contributing to under-reporting and limited accounts among the RIDOC sample. While we took measures to support study participants throughout survey and interview administration, we recognize the potential trauma associated with an overdose experienced, witnessed, or responded to and the need for support and resources in the community and institutions like RIDOC (Shearer et al., 2019; Song et al., 2023).

Conclusion

Our research highlighted varying degrees of understanding of the Rhode Island GSL between PWUS in the community and those incarcerated in RIDOC for drug distribution. Examining both these samples is meaningful as it provided context into how PWDD and PWUS, understand the GSL. Findings indicate the need to clarify definitions and expand education related to the GSL and responding to stimulant overdoses, with a focus on PWUS and criminal-legal system actors to increase help-seeking and reduce overdose deaths. Future studies should consider policy interventions and educational and behavioral interventions to address these gaps.
