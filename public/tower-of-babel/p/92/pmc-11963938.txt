Randomized Trials of Renal Denervation for Uncontrolled Hypertension: An Updated Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Syed Hamza Mufarrih, Nada Qaisar Qureshi, Mohammed Saud Khan, Mohammed Kazimuddin, Eric Secemsky, Michael J Bloch, Jay Giri, Debbie Cohen, Rajesh V Swaminathan, Dmitriy N Feldman, Khaldoon Alaswad, Ajay Kirtane, David Kandzari, Herbert D Aronow
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite optimal medical therapy, a significant proportion of patients' blood pressure remains uncontrolled. Catheter‐based renal denervation (RDN) has been proposed as a potential intervention for uncontrolled hypertension. We conducted an updated meta‐analysis to assess the efficacy and safety of RDN in patients with uncontrolled hypertension, with emphasis on the differential effect of RDN in patients on and off antihypertensive medications.Methods and ResultsOnline databases were searched to identify randomized clinical trials comparing efficacy and safety of RDN versus control in patients with uncontrolled hypertension. Subgroup analyses were conducted for sham‐controlled trials and studies that used RDN devices that have gained or are currently seeking US Food and Drug Administration approval. Fifteen trials with 2581 patients (RDN, 1723; sham, 858) were included. In patients off antihypertensive medications undergoing RDN, a significant reduction in 24‐hour ambulatory (−3.70 [95% CI, −5.41 to −2.00] mm Hg), office (−4.76 [95% CI, −7.57 to −1.94] mm Hg), and home (−3.28 [95% CI, −5.96 to −0.61] mm Hg) systolic blood pressures was noted. In patients on antihypertensive medications, a significant reduction was observed in 24‐hour ambulatory (−2.23 [95% CI, −3.56 to −0.90] mm Hg), office (−6.39 [95% CI, −11.49 to −1.30]), home (−6.08 [95% CI, −11.54 to −0.61] mm Hg), daytime (−2.62 [95% CI, −4.14 to −1.11]), and nighttime (−2.70 [95% CI, −5.13 to −0.27]) systolic blood pressures, as well as 24‐hour ambulatory (−1.16 [95% CI, −1.96 to −0.35]), office (−3.17 [95% CI, −5.54 to −0.80]), and daytime (−1.47 [95% CI, −2.50 to −0.27]) diastolic blood pressures.ConclusionsRDN significantly lowers blood pressure in patients with uncontrolled hypertension, in patients off and on antihypertensive medications, with a favorable safety profile. The efficacy of RDN was consistent in sham‐controlled trials and contemporary trials using US Food and Drug Administration–approved devices.Keywords: denervation, hypertension, renal, renal denervation, uncontrolledSubject Categories: Hypertension, Treatment, High Blood Pressure, Meta Analysis, Quality and Outcomes

Hypertension affects >1 billion individuals globally and remains a leading and preventable cause of fatal and nonfatal cardiovascular events. 1 Despite the widespread availability of pharmacologic therapies and targeted lifestyle interventions, nearly half of patients with hypertension remain uncontrolled. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 Approximately 10% of these patients have resistant hypertension and remain at even higher risk of cardiovascular events. 6 , 7

Catheter‐based renal denervation (RDN) has been studied as a potential intervention for uncontrolled hypertension. In initial proof‐of‐concept studies, including SYMPLICITY HTN‐1 and HTN‐2, significant reductions in blood pressure were observed with RDN. 8 , 9 However, after the incorporation of a sham control arm into the trial design, these results were not replicated in SYMPLICITY HTN‐3. 10 It was theorized that patient selection, device design, procedural technique, postprocedure changes in medical therapy, and medication adherence may have impacted trial outcomes. 11 , 12 Subsequent trials, conducted separately in patients who were on and off antihypertensive medications, addressed many of these earlier limitations, yielding more favorable results for RDN. 13 , 14 , 15

Available meta‐analyses have combined RDN trial results from patients who were on or off medications, which may have obscured key insights into RDN efficacy and safety in different settings. Additionally, these meta‐analyses have included trials using devices for which US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval is no longer being sought. Finally, newer trials have not yet been incorporated into updated meta‐analyses. 16 , 17 For the above reasons, we conducted an updated meta‐analysis of available randomized trials, including the most recently published TARGET BP OFF (Treatment in Patients with Uncontrolled Hypertension on No Antihypertensive Medication), RADIANCE II (Reinforcement of Arterial Dilation using an Inflatable balloon for Neovascular Conditions in the Endovascular treatment of renal denervation), and SPYRAL HTN (Sympathetic Renal Denervation in Patients with Uncontrolled Hypertension), ON‐Med Expansion trials, investigating the blood pressure–lowering effects of RDN devices in patients on and off medication to explore the differential effect of antihypertensive medications. We have also conducted subgroup analyses with trials using devices that are FDA approved or pending approval to explore the contemporary application of RDN.

Methods

The study was conducted following Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses guidelines and was registered with the International Database of Prospectively Registered Systematic Reviews (identifier: Center for Reviews and Dissemination 42023488533). 18 Under the American Heart Association Journals' Transparency and Openness Promotion Guidelines, the data used in this study are available online, and the methods are detailed in Data S1.

Databases and Search Strategy

PubMed, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library were searched for randomized clinical trials comparing renal denervation with control in patients with hypertension. Two authors (S.H.M., N.Q.Q.) created a search filter using relevant keywords with corresponding medical subject heading terms ([“renal denervation” OR “renal sympathetic denervation” OR “RSD” OR “RDN”] AND [“sham” OR “control” OR “sham procedure”] AND [“hypertension” OR “HTN” OR “blood pressure” OR “BP”]). The search was not limited by publication date, including all studies since the publication of SIMPLICITY‐I in 2009. Search engine results were filtered on the basis of the eligibility criteria described below.

Eligibility Criteria

Randomized clinical trials that compared outcomes following RDN versus control in patients with uncontrolled hypertension were included. Observational studies, review articles, prospective trials without randomization of subjects, and studies published in any language other than English were excluded. Additionally, trials investigating the impact of RDN on other parameters (eg, heart rate 19 ) or for other indications (eg, atrial fibrillation 20 ) were excluded. Studies with <20 participants 21 , 22 and those involving patients who also underwent another procedure 23 , 24 , 25 were excluded.

To avoid duplication of data and overestimation of effects, the SPYRAL HTN ON and OFF Med pilot trials and their subsequent follow‐up studies were excluded. Instead, data derived from the frequentist model of SPYRAL HTN ON Med Expansion and SPYRAL HTN OFF Med Pivotal trials were included in this meta‐analysis. Additional details on randomized clinical trials (RCTs) excluded after full‐text review are summarized in Table S1.

Study Selection Process

The search results were exported to EndNote x9 (Clarivate Analytics, London, UK), and duplicates were removed. The titles and abstracts of remaining studies were screened to exclude those that did not meet the eligibility criteria. Full‐text versions of the remaining studies were retrieved and reviewed to assess eligibility for inclusion. The study selection process was completed independently by 2 authors (S.H.M., N.Q.Q.), and the results were compared at each step. Conflicts were resolved by 2 experts (M.S.A., M.K.). A final list of studies to be included in the meta‐analysis was reviewed and approved by each coauthor.

Data Collection Process, Data Items, and Outcome Definition

Two authors (S.H.M., N.Q.Q.) extracted data into Excel 2013 (Microsoft Corp, Redmond, WA). Data were extracted for (1) study characteristics (year of publication, number of participants, follow‐up duration, medical management [on versus off medications], denervation method and device, country and number of centers involved, and escape criteria), (2) baseline patient characteristics (demographics, comorbidities, and baseline 24‐hour ambulatory and office systolic and diastolic blood pressures), (3) efficacy outcomes, and (4) safety outcomes (death, acute kidney injury, embolic events, renal vascular complications, access site complications, hypotension, hypertensive crisis, stroke, and acute coronary syndrome). The primary efficacy outcomes were change in 24‐hour ambulatory systolic and diastolic blood pressure from baseline to study‐defined primary outcome ascertainment time point. Secondary efficacy outcomes included (1) changes in office, home, daytime, and nighttime systolic and diastolic blood pressures from baseline to study‐defined primary outcome ascertainment time point and (2) changes in medication burden (Data S1.4) at 6‐month follow‐up. Exploratory outcomes assessed RDN's efficacy at 6 months, the proportion of patients with a ≥5–mm Hg blood pressure reduction, those achieving a blood pressure of <135/85 mm Hg, and the proportion meeting escape criteria in the RDN group versus control (Data S1.5). Primary and secondary efficacy outcomes (ambulatory, office, home, daytime, and nighttime systolic and diastolic blood pressures, as well as the mean number of medications, change in mean number of medications, defined daily doses, and medication load index) were assessed separately in patients on antihypertensive medication (ON‐Med) versus off antihypertensive medication (OFF‐Med). Data for safety outcomes were pooled only when reported consistently across studies. Exploratory outcomes were not pooled separately for ON‐Med and OFF‐Med trials. Follow‐up data beyond 6 months were not included as these were unavailable at the time of this meta‐analysis. Any conflicts on extraction and pooling of variables reported heterogeneously among the 3 studies were resolved by 3 experts (M.S.A., M.K., and H.D.A.). Subgroup analyses were conducted for (1) all sham‐controlled RCTs and (2) trials using RDN devices that have received or are currently seeking FDA approval, with stratification into ON‐Med and OFF‐Med groups.

Quality Assessment

The quality of evidence synthesized for each outcome was appraised using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation reporting criteria. 26 Risk of bias was assessed using the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies‐2 tool.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Review Manager, version 5.4.1 (Cochrane, London, UK), and XLSTAT, version 2013.1.5 (Lumivero, Denver, CO). Blood pressure measurements used for primary and secondary efficacy outcomes were recorded as continuous variables across all trials. Mean±SD was used for calculation of the mean difference between baseline and study follow‐up. Studies reporting medians (interquartile ranges) were converted to means (SDs) using the Wan formula for pooled statistics. Studies reporting mean with SE were converted to mean (SD) using the following formula: SD=SE×√n (where √n is the square root of the sample size). Similarly, studies reporting mean (95% CI) were converted to mean (SD) using the following formula: SD = (upper limit of CI−lower limit of CI)/(2×1.96), where CI is the 95% CI. Treatment estimates for primary and secondary efficacy outcomes were reported as weighted mean differences (WMDs) in mm Hg with 95% CIs. The random effects model was used for trials comparing RDN with control because of the significant variability in control groups, population characteristics, and other trial‐specific factors that introduced heterogeneity across the studies. The fixed effects model was used for sham‐controlled trials and sham‐controlled trials with FDA‐approved devices as these subgroups were more uniform by design and, thus, exhibited less heterogeneity. Treatment estimates for additional outcomes were reported as risk ratios (RRs) with 95% CIs. The threshold for statistical significance was established at a 2‐tailed α level of <0.05. Trial sequential analysis was performed to ensure the reliability of our findings by adjusting for repeated testing and cumulative sample size using the trial sequential analysis tool, version 0.9.5.10 Beta (The Copenhagen Trial Unit, Centre for Clinical Intervention Research, The Capital Region, Copenhagen University Hospital–Rigshospitalet, 2021). Publication bias was not assessed given the small number of included studies (<10). Heterogeneity was reported using I2 statistics.

Sensitivity Analysis

Sensitivity analysis was conducted using the fixed effect model for trials comparing RDN with control for the ON‐Med trials. In the OFF‐Med group, a sensitivity analysis was not necessary as all trials in the OFF‐Med group were sham controlled and a fixed effects analysis was conducted as part of the subgroup analysis. Sensitivity analyses were performed without stratification into ON‐/OFF‐Med groups for primary and secondary outcomes in all 3 subgroups, including all RCTs, sham‐controlled trials, and trials using RDN devices that have been granted or are seeking FDA approval. Additional sensitivity analyses were conducted on subgroups with trials seeking FDA approval after excluding the TARGET BP OFF‐MED trial from OFF‐Med trials as it was the only trial using alcohol RDN systems. A second sensitivity analysis was conducted on the same subgroup after excluding REQUIRE (Renal Denervation in Patients with Resistant Hypertension) trial data from the ON‐Med trials given that it differed significantly in design (eg, lack of standardized medical therapy, unblinded).

Results

Study Selection

Study selection is outlined in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses flow diagram (Figure 1). The initial database search yielded 346 studies (PubMed [90], Cochrane Library [62], and Web of Science [194]). A total of 269 failed to meet the inclusion criteria. After the removal of duplicates (n=65), abstracts of 77 studies were reviewed by 2 authors (S.H.M., N.Q.Q.). Thirty‐one studies were excluded after review. Full texts of the remaining 46 studies were retrieved and reviewed for eligibility. Fifteen trials, including 2581 patients (RDN, 1498; sham, 1083), satisfied this study's inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Study and Patient Characteristics

Of the 15 included studies, RDN was compared with a sham procedure in 12 studies. The remaining 3 studies compared RDN with optimal medical therapy available at the time. A total of 10 trials of 1723 patients (RDN, 1024; control, 699) permitted continuation of stable optimal medical therapy in both groups (ON‐Med), whereas 5 trials of 858 patients (RDN, 474; control, 384) recruited patients who were off antihypertensive medication for at least 4 weeks (OFF‐Med). Nine trials used radiofrequency‐based RDN, 5 trials used ultrasound‐based RDN technology, and 1 trial used alcohol‐mediated RDN. Primary outcome ascertainment was at 6 months for 8 trials, 3 months for 2 trials, and 2 months for 5 trials. Baseline study characteristics are detailed in Table 1. The mean±SD age was 56.3±10.2 years, and the mean±SD BMI was 30.5±5.1 kg/m2. Of the cohort, 853 were women. Over half of the patients were White (1310 of 2180 patients; from 12 studies) and 1 in 5 were Black (370 of 1760 patients; from 7 studies). Of the included trials, 11 reported the number of antihypertensive medications that patients were taking. Ninety‐nine patients (7.4%; from 7 studies) were started on antihypertensive medication for study‐defined escape criteria (RDN, 5.6%; versus sham, 9.7%). Primary Efficacy Outcomes

24‐Hour Ambulatory Systolic and Diastolic Blood Pressures

OFF‐Med Trials

In patients who had been off medication undergoing RDN, a significant reduction in baseline‐adjusted 24‐hour ambulatory systolic blood pressures (WMD, −3.70 [95% CI, −5.41 −2.00] mm Hg; P<0.001) was noted at the time of primary outcome ascertainment compared with the control group (Figure 2). Consistent results were observed in subgroup analyses with sham‐controlled trials and sham‐controlled trials using devices pending/approved by the FDA (Figures S1 and S2). A similar reduction was not observed in 24‐hour ambulatory diastolic blood pressures (WMD, −1.36 [95% CI, −4.11 to 1.40] mm Hg; P=0.33) at primary outcome ascertainment compared with control (Figure 3). However, in subgroup analyses with sham‐controlled trials and sham‐controlled trials using devices approved by the FDA, a significant reduction in diastolic blood pressure was observed (Figures S3 and S4).

ON‐Med Trials

In patients on antihypertensive medication undergoing RDN, the reduction in 24‐hour ambulatory systolic blood pressures at the time of primary outcome ascertainment was significant (WMD, −2.23 [95% CI, −3.56 to −0.90] mm Hg; P=0.001) compared with control (Figure 4). Consistent results were observed in subgroup analyses with sham‐controlled trials and with sham‐controlled trials using devices pending approval/approved by the FDA (Figures S5 and S6). A significant reduction was also noted for diastolic blood pressures with RDN (WMD, −1.16 [95% CI, −1.96 to −0.35] mm Hg; P=0.005) compared with the control group (Figure 5). Consistent results were observed in subgroup analyses with sham‐controlled trials and with sham‐controlled trials using devices pending approval/approved by the FDA (Figures S7 and S8).

Secondary Efficacy Outcomes

Changes in Blood Pressure at Primary Outcome Ascertainment Time Point

OFF‐Med Trials

Patients off medication undergoing RDN demonstrated a significant reduction in office (WMD, −4.76 [95% CI, −7.57 to −1.94] mm Hg; P<0.001) and home (WMD, −3.28 [95% CI, −5.96 to −0.61] mm Hg; P=0.02) systolic blood pressures compared with the control group (Figure 2). Results were consistent in subgroup analyses with sham‐controlled trials and with sham‐controlled trials using devices pending approval/approved by the FDA (Figures S1 and S2). There was no difference in daytime (WMD, −2.95 [95% CI, −8.79 to 2.89] mm Hg; P=0.32) or nighttime (WMD, −2.16 [95% CI, −5.64 to 1.32] mm Hg; P=0.22) ambulatory systolic blood pressure in trials comparing RDN with control (Figure 2) and in the subgroup analyses including sham‐controlled trials only (Figure S1). However, a significant reduction was noted in subgroup analyses of sham‐controlled trials using devices pending approval/approved by the FDA (Figure S2).

For diastolic blood pressure, no difference was observed in office (WMD, −2.14 [95% CI, −4.59 to 0.30] mm Hg; P=0.09), home (WMD, −2.09 [95% CI, −4.73 to 0.56] mm Hg; P=0.12), daytime (WMD, −1.51 [95% CI, −5.74 to 2.72] mm Hg; P=0.48), or nighttime (WMD, −0.56 [95% CI, −2.24 to 1.12] mm Hg; P=0.51) diastolic blood pressures (Figure 3). However, subgroup analyses for sham‐controlled trials and sham‐controlled trials using devices pending approval/approved by the FDA demonstrated a significant reduction in office, home, and daytime diastolic blood pressure (Figures S3 and S4).

ON‐Med Trials

Compared with the control group, patients on antihypertensive medication undergoing RDN demonstrated a significant reduction in office (WMD, −6.39 [95% CI, −11.49 to −1.30] mm Hg; P=0.01), home (WMD, −6.08 [95% CI, −11.54 to −0.61] mm Hg; P=0.03), daytime (WMD, −2.62 [95% CI, −4.14 to −1.11] mm Hg; P<0.001), and nighttime (WMD, −2.70 [95% CI, −5.13 to −0.27] mm Hg; P=0.03) systolic blood pressures (Figure 4). Results were consistent in subgroup analyses with sham‐controlled trials and with sham‐controlled trials using devices pending approval/approved by the FDA (Figures S5 and S6).

For diastolic blood pressures, a significant reduction was observed in the office (WMD, −3.17 [95% CI, −5.54 to −0.80] mm Hg; P=0.009) and daytime (WMD, −1.47 [95% CI, −2.50 to −0.45] mm Hg; P=0.005) blood pressures (Figure 5). This was consistent in subgroup analyses with sham‐controlled trials and with sham‐controlled trials using devices pending approval/approved by the FDA (Figures S7 and S8). There was no significant difference noted for home (WMD, −3.16 [95% CI, −6.51 to 0.19] mm Hg; P=0.06) and nighttime (WMD, −1.06 [95% CI, −2.46 to 0.34] mm Hg; P=0.14) diastolic blood pressures, consistent in subgroup analyses (Figure 5, Figures S7 and S8).

Changes in Medication Burden at 6‐Month Follow‐Up

OFF‐Med Trials

Compared with the control group, those undergoing RDN until the primary outcome ascertainment time point had a lower defined daily dose (WMD, −0.58 [95% CI, −0.87 to −0.29]; P<0.001) and a smaller antihypertensive medication load index (WMD, −0.16 [95% CI, −0.27 to −0.05]; P=0.003) at 6‐month follow‐up (Figure 6). There was no significant change in the mean number of antihypertensive medications that patients were taking from baseline to primary outcome ascertainment (WMD, −0.13 [95% CI, −0.46 to 0.21]; P=0.46) (Figure 7).

Safety Outcomes

No major procedural complications were reported in any of the included trials (Table S2). Access site complications occurred in 8 patients (2.3%) at a mean follow‐up of 5 months, and hypertensive crisis occurred in 45 patients (1.9%) at a mean follow‐up of 5 months. Mortality occurred in 5 patients, 3 in the RDN group and 2 in the sham group, at a mean follow‐up of 4 months. Five cases of renal artery stenosis (0.4%) were observed in the RDN group at a mean follow‐up of 4.7 months, and 3 patients in the RDN group required renal artery stenting for the progression of preexisting renal artery stenosis. Stroke was reported in 16 patients (RDN, 9; sham, 7) at a mean follow‐up of 6 months. The pooled safety outcomes appear in Table 5.

Exploratory Outcomes

(1) Of the 12 trials reporting 6‐month outcomes, 7 13 , 16 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 had a primary outcome ascertainment time point before 6 months, where medications were added/adjusted asymmetrically in both groups to optimize blood pressure control. Patients undergoing RDN (with medications added or adjusted) had significantly lower office and home systolic blood pressures, but there was no significant difference between changes in 24‐hour, daytime, or nighttime ambulatory systolic blood pressures. A significant reduction was noted in 24‐hour ambulatory and home diastolic blood pressures, but there was no significant difference in 24‐hour office, daytime, and nighttime diastolic blood pressures in the RDN group with medications versus control (which included medications alone or sham with medications) at 6‐month follow‐up (Figures S9 and S10). (2) The proportion of patients achieving a >5–mm Hg decrease in ambulatory systolic blood pressure at the primary outcome ascertainment time point was significantly greater in the RDN group compared with control patients (RR, 0.65 [95% CI, 0.49–0.85]; P=0.002) (Figure S11). (3) The proportion of patients achieving a blood pressure target of <135/85 mm Hg at the primary outcome ascertainment time point was also significantly greater in the RDN than control group (RR, 0.82 [95% CI, 0.76–0.89]; P<0.001) (Figure S11). (4) The proportion of patients meeting escape criteria at primary outcome ascertainment was greater in the control than the RDN group (RR, 0.60 [95% CI, 0.41–0.90]; P=0.01) (Figure S12).

Quality Assessment

The primary outcome of 24‐hour systolic and diastolic blood pressures synthesized moderate‐quality evidence, as per the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation criteria. The quality of evidence for secondary outcomes ranged from high quality to very low quality; these findings have been summarized in Table 4. Most studies included in the meta‐analysis were assessed as high quality by the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies‐2 tool, suggesting a low risk of bias (Figures S13 and S14). Trial sequential analysis demonstrated a significant blood pressure–lowering effect of RDN (pooled effect, −2.50 [95% CI, −3.98 to −1.02]; P<0.001) while accounting for heterogeneity (I2=64%) and diversity (D2=71%) in the combined ON‐Med and OFF‐Med trials (Figures S15–S17).

Sensitivity Analyses

Sensitivity analysis was conducted using a fixed‐effects analysis for trials comparing RDN with control for the ON‐Med trials. Results for 24‐hour, daytime, nighttime, ambulatory, office, and home systolic blood pressures and 24‐hour, daytime, nighttime, and office diastolic blood pressures were consistent with the random‐effects model (Figures S18 and S19). The home diastolic blood pressure showed a significant reduction with the fixed‐effects analysis contrary to the insignificant results yielded by the random‐effects analysis.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted without stratification into ON‐Med and OFF‐Med groups. A significant reduction in 24‐hour ambulatory, office, and home systolic and diastolic blood pressures was observed in all RCTs (Figures S20 and S21). A significant reduction in 24‐hour ambulatory, office, and home systolic, as well as daytime diastolic, blood pressure was noted in sham‐controlled RCTs (Figures S22 and S23). In trials using RDN devices with FDA approval/pending approval, a significant reduction in 24‐hour ambulatory, office, home, and daytime systolic and diastolic blood pressure was observed (Figures S24 and S25).

Exclusion of the TARGET BP OFF‐Med trial from the OFF‐Med trials, using RDN devices with/pending FDA approval, showed a significant reduction in systolic (24‐hour ambulatory, office, daytime, and nighttime) and diastolic (24‐hour ambulatory, office, and daytime) blood pressures, consistent with the primary analysis (Figures S26 and S27).

Exclusion of the REQUIRE trial from the ON‐Med trials, using RDN devices with/pending FDA approval, showed a significant reduction in systolic (24‐hour ambulatory, office, and nighttime) and diastolic (office, daytime, and nighttime) blood pressures. However, a significant reduction was not seen in daytime systolic and 24‐hour ambulatory diastolic blood pressures. The results for systolic (24‐hour ambulatory and daytime) and diastolic (24‐hour ambulatory and nighttime) blood pressures were not consistent with the primary analysis (Figures S28 and S29).

Discussion

In this meta‐analysis of 15 RCTs, RDN resulted in a significant reduction in 24‐hour ambulatory systolic blood pressure in both ON‐Med and OFF‐Med trials. The results were consistent in sham‐controlled trials and sham‐controlled trials using RDN devices already approved or pending approval by the FDA (Figure 8). Adverse events were rare and occurred at a similar frequency in RDN‐ and sham‐treated patients. Although effect sizes for 24‐hour diastolic, daytime, nighttime, ambulatory, office, and home systolic and diastolic blood pressures showed mixed directional preferences between the ON‐Med and OFF‐Med groups, blood pressure reduction was more consistently significant in ON‐Med trials.

Although initial proof‐of‐concept studies varied in study design, patient selection criteria, concomitant medical therapies, and RDN techniques, 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 32 more contemporary studies that overcame many earlier trial limitations demonstrated positive results for RDN. 13 , 14 , 16 , 17 , 27 , 29 , 30 , 33 , 34 A 2019 meta‐analysis showed improved efficacy of so‐called “second‐generation” (newer) over “first‐generation” (earlier) trials in reducing daytime ambulatory systolic blood pressure. 35 However, more recent meta‐analyses have yielded conflicting findings. Yang et al reported significant blood pressure reductions with RDN, 36 whereas Fernandes et al found no between‐group differences. 37 Additionally, currently available meta‐analyses have not investigated the impact of background antihypertensive therapy on RDN results. 35 , 36 , 37 Our study is the most comprehensive meta‐analysis to date exploring the differential effect of RDN in patients on and off antihypertensive medications.

Newer RDN studies, conducted in the off medication setting, were designed to assess the efficacy of RDN without the potential confounding effect of variable medication adherence reported in previous trials. 10 The demonstrated reduction in 24‐hour ambulatory, office, and home systolic blood pressures supports the efficacy of RDN in a diverse patient population and is reassuring. The lack of significant reduction in 24‐hour ambulatory diastolic blood pressures in the OFF‐Med studies may be attributed to the markedly negative results reported by the REDUCE HTN: REINFORCE trial, which contrasted with other studies. However, the reduction in diastolic blood pressure was significant in subgroup analyses of trials using RDN devices seeking FDA approval. These markedly negative findings from the REDUCE HTN: REINFORCE trial could be attributable to a type II error from insufficient power (sample size=51) and the delayed treatment effect noted in this study. It is also important to consider that variable compliance with background medications in both the RDN and control groups might have impacted the results.

Although our study only investigated short‐term blood pressure outcomes following RDN, available longer‐term data demonstrate sustained efficacy in blood pressure reduction. The 36‐month follow‐up data from the SPYRAL HTN ON‐Med trial demonstrate reductions of −10.0 and 5.9 mm Hg in 24‐hour ambulatory systolic and diastolic blood pressures, respectively, when compared with sham. 33 The RADIANCE HTN SOLO trial demonstrated that RDN reduced office systolic and diastolic blood pressure at 36‐month follow‐up by 8.4 and 4.4 mm Hg, respectively, when compared with baseline screening, despite no increase in medication use. 38 At 24 months, RADIANCE TRIO demonstrated that RDN reduced in‐office systolic and diastolic blood pressures by 14.6 and 8.4 mm Hg, respectively, when compared with screening, despite reductions in the mean number of antihypertensive medications from 3.9 to 3.4 during the same interval. 39 Interestingly, even the first‐generation SYMPLICITY HTN‐3 trial, which was negative on its primary end point at 6 months, demonstrated reduced blood pressure following RDN at 3 years. 33 , 38 , 40 Similar observations have been published from the real‐world Global SYMPLICITY Registry, reporting a 16.5–mm Hg reduction in office systolic blood pressure at 3 years. 41

The observed reductions in blood pressure were modest, including mean differences of −2.23 (95% CI, −3.56 to −0.90) mm Hg and −1.16 (95% CI, −1.96 to −0.35) mm Hg in 24‐hour ambulatory systolic and diastolic blood pressures, respectively, in ON‐Med trials and −3.70 (95% CI, −5.41 to −2.00) mm Hg and −1.36 (95% CI, −4.11 to 1.40) mm Hg in OFF‐Med trials. Nevertheless, large observational studies have shown that reductions in blood pressure of as little as 2 mm Hg are associated with a 10% lower stroke rate and a 7% ischemic heart disease and other vascular mortality rate. 42 , 43 The SPRINT (Systolic Blood Pressure Intervention Trial) RCT demonstrated prospectively that when compared with a more standard approach, an intensive blood pressure reduction strategy decreased the incidence of cardiovascular events and death. 44 Trials relating RDN to hard clinical outcomes, impact on cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, and long‐term safety are lacking, but data suggest that those who realize greater blood pressure reductions following RDN have more favorable clinical outcomes. 45 Importantly, our results also demonstrate that a greater proportion of patients undergoing RDN than sham can achieve target blood pressures of <135/85 mm Hg. 10 Whether this translates into long‐term cost‐effectiveness remains to be seen. 17 , 29 , 34 , 46 , 47 , 48 , 49 , 50 , 51 The true impact of RDN on cardiovascular health will be driven by its applicability in the real‐world setting, where patient preferences will heavily influence its use.

There are noteworthy limitations of our trial‐level meta‐analysis. Data were pooled from multiple trials with varying study protocols and patient characteristics, a limitation inherent to meta‐analyses. We conducted subgroup analysis with narrower eligibility criteria to increase homogeneity, such as trials with only sham controls and trials using only approved devices or those pending FDA approval. Among the 15 included trials, 3 different technologies were used to perform the RDN procedure, but we believe an analysis stratified by technology would not have been sufficiently powered and was therefore not conducted. Future real‐world registries may shed light on whether device‐specific differences in blood pressure reductions exist. The follow‐up period during which efficacy and safety outcomes were assessed was limited to short‐term follow‐up at the time of primary outcome ascertainment assessment in each study (ranging between 2 and 6 months) and may have introduced a degree of heterogeneity in our blood pressure outcomes, mitigated partially by the use of a random‐effects model. Additionally, the characterization of patient ethnicity excludes geographies where such reporting is not permissible (eg, Europe). Despite these limitations, our meta‐analysis has synthesized available evidence from high‐quality randomized trials shedding light on the efficacy and safety of RDN in the ON‐Med and OFF‐Med settings.

Conclusions

RDN for uncontrolled hypertension significantly reduces blood pressure in patients off and on antihypertensive medications. Larger RCTs and postmarket registries with extended follow‐up are needed to establish the long‐term safety and efficacy of RDN.
