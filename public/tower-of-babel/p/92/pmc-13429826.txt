Trends in Dairy and Sugar-Sweetened Beverage Consumption among Korean Young Adults Aged 19–29 Years: Analysis of Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 2010–2021
Authors: Jiwon Bang, Mi-Jung Choi, Eun-Hee Cho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBeverage consumption patterns in Korea have shifted in response to rapid lifestyle changes, including the growth of cafe culture and increased access to convenience foods. This study examined trends in major beverage consumption among Korean young adults.MethodsBeverage consumption trends were analyzed using data from 7,681 participants aged 19–29 years from the Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (2010–2021). Dietary assessment methods differed by survey cycle: a simple food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) in 2010–2011, a semi-quantitative FFQ in 2012–2016, and beverage-specific questions in 2019–2021. Beverages included milk, yogurt, soda, coffee, and fruit juice. Weekly consumption frequency, consumption volume, and estimated sugar intake were analyzed by sex.ResultsBeverage consumption patterns showed distinct temporal changes. The frequency of dairy product intake declined significantly (5.2 to 3.2 times/week; P<0.0001), mainly because of reduced milk consumption. Yogurt intake initially increased and then declined, particularly among females. In contrast, the overall frequency of sugar-sweetened beverage (SSB) intake remained stable, although coffee intake frequency decreased and soda and fruit juice intake increased slightly. Total SSB volume increased from 1.9 to 2.8 L per week (P<0.0001). The absolute increase was slightly greater in males (899.5 mL/week vs. 761.6 mL/week), whereas the relative increase in soda intake was greater in females (93% vs. 61%). Correspondingly, weekly sugar intake from SSBs rose sharply (122.7 to 177.6 g/week, P<0.0001), whereas sugar intake from dairy products did not change significantly.ConclusionAmong Korean young adults, dairy consumption has declined, whereas SSB intake and related sugar exposure have increased markedly, with notable sex differences in beverage consumption patterns.

INTRODUCTION

In Korea, rapid economic development has been accompanied by the westernization of dietary habits and expansion of the beverage market, leading to changes in eating behaviors and food consumption patterns. Beverage consumption patterns have shifted in line with broader societal trends.1-4 In particular, the widespread growth of cafe culture and increased availability of convenience foods have substantially influenced beverage choices and contributed to changes in consumption patterns. Several additional societal factors have likely shaped beverage consumption in Korea. The rapid growth of food delivery platforms and easy access to convenience stores have increased both the availability and affordability of sugar-sweetened beverages (SSBs), particularly among younger populations.3,5-7 Beverage intake is an important determinant of individual health, and SSBs contribute substantially to the risk of metabolic and chronic diseases, including obesity, diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, and mortality.8-17 In the United States, although SSB consumption has declined over the past several decades, intake remains high among both youth and adults.18,19 Recent Centers for Disease Control and Prevention data also indicate that a substantial proportion of young children continue to consume SSBs.20 In some parts of the United States, SSBs reportedly account for half of all purchases at convenience stores and small food shops.21 Among adults, the prevalence of SSB intake remains high, and sociodemographic characteristics are strongly associated with higher consumption.22,23 In China, a survey of individuals aged 6 to 17 years found that 9.6% consumed SSBs more than seven times per week, while the proportion of SSB consumers increased from 46.0% to 66.6%.24 Conversely, although the proportion of SSB consumers has decreased in Australia, average daily intake has shown an increasing trend.25

The current study examined trends in SSB consumption in South Korea between 2010 and 2021, addressing a gap in the literature since the previous analysis of Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES) data from 1998 to 2009.1 We aimed to clarify changes in consumption patterns of dairy products and SSBs over time, stratified by sex and survey cycle.

METHODS

Ethics statement

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Kangwon National University (IRB No. KWNUIRB-2024-10-014). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the IRB.

Study participants

The KNHANES is a nationwide survey conducted by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency to assess the health and nutritional status of the Korean population. It uses a complex, stratified, multistage probability cluster sampling design and is conducted using a standardized questionnaire. The data are publicly available on the KNHANES website.26 Beverage consumption data were obtained from KNHANES V (2010–2012), VI (2013–2015), VII (2016), and VIII (2019–2021). During this period, 79,181 individuals participated in the survey. After excluding participants outside the target age range and those with incomplete dietary survey data, the final sample included 7,681 individuals. For the analysis, we selected beverage categories that were consistently surveyed during the study period: milk, yogurt, soda, coffee, and fruit juice. Dairy products included milk and yogurt, whereas SSBs included soda, coffee, and fruit juice. All participant data were de-identified to prevent individual identification, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants during the survey process.27

Food frequency questionnaire in KNHANES

The dietary assessment methods and beverage items surveyed varied considerably across KNHANES cycles. In 2010 to 2011, a simple food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) was administered to participants aged ≥12 years. This FFQ collected only consumption frequency and did not include consumption volume. Beverage items included milk, yogurt, soda, and coffee, but fruit juice was not surveyed as a separate item during this period. From 2012 to 2016, a semi-quantitative FFQ was administered to adults aged 19 to 64 years. Unlike the simple FFQ, this version assessed both frequency and average consumption volume, enabling estimation of weekly intake. Beverage items included milk, liquid yogurt, thick yogurt, soda, coffee, and fruit juice. Because liquid and solid yogurt were recorded separately, they were combined to calculate total yogurt consumption frequency and volume. In 2019 to 2021, instead of a full FFQ, beverage-specific questions were included in the dietary behavior section of the survey. These questions targeted participants aged 6 to 29 years and assessed both consumption frequency and volume for milk, yogurt, soda, coffee, and fruit juice. During this period, milk, coffee, and fruit juice were each divided into sweetened and unsweetened categories, whereas yogurt was surveyed as a single item. For the analysis, sweetened and unsweetened items were combined, and total consumption frequency and volume were used, consistent with the approach applied to liquid and solid yogurt in 2012 to 2016.

Definition of study variables

Participants’ beverage consumption frequency was assessed using the FFQ. Given differences in the specific beverage items surveyed across KNHANES cycles, this study focused on milk, yogurt, soda, coffee, and fruit juice, which were included across the relevant survey periods. For analytical purposes, milk and yogurt were grouped as dairy products, whereas soda, coffee, and fruit juice were classified as SSBs.

Because the response categories varied across survey periods, data were classified according to years with consistent response criteria. From 2010 to 2011, the frequency options included (1) 1, 2, or 3 times per day; (2) 1, 2–3, or 4–6 times per week; (3) 1 or 2–3 times per month; (4) 6–11 times per year; and (5) rarely consumed. From 2012 to 2016, the options were (1) 1, 2, or 3 times per day; (2) 1, 2–4, or 5–6 times per week; (3) 1 or 2–3 times per month; and (4) rarely consumed. From 2019 to 2021, the response categories were (1) 1, 2, or 3 times per day; (2) 1, 2–4, or 5–6 times per week; and (3) less than once per week. Based on these criteria, the average weekly frequency of beverage consumption was calculated as 21, 14, 7, 5 (or 5.5), 3 (or 2.5), 1, 0.625, or 0.25 times per week; ‘rarely consumed’ and ‘less than once per week’ were assigned a value of 0 times per week.

To estimate sugar intake from beverages, we multiplied the average weekly beverage consumption volume (mL/week) by the sugar content (g/100 mL) for each beverage category. Sugar content values were obtained from the Food Nutrition Database provided by the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety of Korea: milk, 4.8 g/100 mL; yogurt, 12.5 g/100 mL; soda, 8.2 g/100 mL; coffee, 24.0 g/100 mL; and fruit juice, 13.0 g/100 mL. To estimate sugar intake from coffee, we classified coffee into unsweetened and sugar-added types and conservatively applied a 40:60 ratio based on previous Korean studies.28-30

Statistical analysis

All data analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute), and a P value<0.05 was considered statistically significant. To compare the average weekly frequency, volume, and sugar intake from beverage consumption among individuals aged 19 to 29 years, data from KNHANES V and VIII were analyzed. Weekly consumption frequency was compared across the three dietary assessment periods (2010–2011, 2012–2016, and 2019–2021), whereas weekly volume and sugar intake were compared between the semi-quantitative FFQ period (2012–2016) and the beverage-specific question period (2019–2021). A generalized linear model was used for average weekly frequency, whereas t-tests were used for average weekly volume and sugar intake. All analyses accounted for primary sampling units, stratification, and sample weights by applying the survey procedures in SAS version 9.4, thereby providing nationally representative estimates and appropriate variance estimates based on KNHANES data. Continuous and categorical variables are presented as mean±standard deviation and frequency (%), respectively.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

Table 1 summarizes the baseline characteristics of the study participants according to the type of FFQ administered. The final sample included 7,681 young adults aged 19–29 years: 1,601 from the simple FFQ period, 3,704 from the semi-quantitative FFQ period, and 2,376 from the beverage-specific question period. Some demographic shifts were observed across these periods. Female participants constituted the majority in the first two periods (58.5% and 56.2%, respectively), whereas the sex distribution was more balanced in the final period (50.4% female). In addition, there was a modest increase in the proportion of participants in the highest income quartile (from 33.0% to 39.3%), whereas the proportion residing in urban areas remained stable (88.8% to 87.9%).

Trends in average weekly frequency of beverage consumption among adults aged 19 to 29 years

Table 2 presents the weekly consumption frequency of various beverages among young adults aged 19 to 29 years across the three FFQ periods. The overall frequency of dairy product consumption showed a notable downward trend (5.2 to 3.2 times/week, P for trend <0.0001). This decline was primarily driven by decreased milk consumption, which was observed in both males (3.8 to 2.3 times/week, P<0.0001) and females (3.4 to 1.8 times/week, P<0.0001), with a slightly greater reduction in females. For yogurt, the overall trend showed an initial increase followed by a decrease (1.7 to 1.1 times/week, P<0.0001). A significant downward trend was observed in females (1.8 to 1.2 times/week, P<0.0001), while a more modest decline was observed in males (1.4 to 1.1 times/week, P=0.0052).

Conversely, the overall frequency of SSB consumption showed no significant trend (overall: 7.9 to 7.5 times/week, P=0.1449; males: 8.6 to 8.1 times/week, P=0.2964; and females: 7.5 to 7.0 times/week, P=0.1348). Among individual SSBs, coffee consumption frequency decreased markedly in both sexes (overall: 6.1 to 4.3 times/week, P<0.0001; males: 6.3 to 4.5 times/week, P<0.0001; and females: 5.9 to 4.2 times/week, P<0.0001), although males consistently maintained a higher frequency of consumption. Soda consumption frequency remained stable overall (1.9 to 2.0 times/week, P=0.0506), as well as among males (2.3 to 2.4 times/week, P=0.5271) and females (1.6 to 1.7 times/week, P=0.2587). However, males consumed soda more than 1.5 times as frequently as females throughout the study period (approximately 2.4 times/week vs. 1.6 times/week). The overall trend for fruit juice was not significant (1.1 to 1.2 times/week, P=0.0873); however, a significant increase was observed among females (1.0 to 1.1 times/week, P=0.0235), whereas no change was observed among males (1.2 to 1.2 times/week, P=0.9454) (Fig. 1).

Trends in average weekly volume and sugar intake from beverage consumption among adults aged 19 to 29 years

When consumption volumes were compared between the semi-quantitative FFQ period (2012–2016) and the beverage-specific question period (2019–2021), the overall volume of dairy product intake did not change significantly (973.0 to 1,018.6 mL/week, P=0.1622) (Table 3). However, a modest but significant increase was observed among males (1,040.2 to 1,156.5 mL/week, P=0.0241). In contrast, overall SSB intake increased substantially (1,927.4 to 2,770.6 mL/week, P<0.0001), a trend observed in both males (2,084.4 to 2,983.9 mL/week, P<0.0001) and females (1,811.8 to 2,573.4 mL/week, P<0.0001). Although the absolute increase in soda volume was slightly greater in males, the relative increase was more pronounced in females (93% vs. 61%, both P<0.0001).

The absolute increase in coffee volume was slightly greater in males (1,429.7 to 2,173.0 mL/week, P<0.0001) than in females (1,534.8 to 2,252.1 mL/week, P<0.0001), although the overall patterns of increase were similar across sexes. Fruit juice volume increased substantially in both sexes (males: 420.1 to 843.3 mL/week, P<0.0001 and females: 350.7 to 730.1 mL/week, P<0.0001) (Fig. 2).

Reflecting these volume changes, average weekly sugar intake from beverages showed divergent trends. Total sugar intake from dairy products did not change significantly (64.1 to 67.5 g/week, P=0.1167); a significant increase was observed among males (65.9 to 73.5 g/week, P=0.0258) but not among females (62.8 to 62.1 g/week, P=0.8102). Among individual dairy beverages, sugar intake increased for both yogurt (35.6 to 51.8 g/week, P<0.0001) and milk (39.6 to 45.2 g/week, P=0.0004). Because the dairy subtotal also includes participants who consumed only one of the two beverages, the change in the subtotal was attenuated relative to that of the individual items.

In contrast, sugar intake from SSBs increased markedly (122.7 to 177.6 g/week, P<0.0001), with significant increases in both males (138.6 to 196.1 g/week, P<0.0001) and females (111.0 to 160.5 g/week, P<0.0001). This increase was driven by all subcategories, including soda (males: 56.9 to 91.5 g/week and females: 35.3 to 68.0 g/week, both P<0.0001), coffee (males: 60.6 to 92.1 g/week and females: 65.1 to 95.5 g/week, both P<0.0001), and fruit juice (males: 54.6 to 109.6 g/week and females: 45.6 to 94.9 g/week, both P<0.0001) (Fig. 3).

DISCUSSION

Among Korean young adults aged 19 to 29 years, beverage consumption patterns showed distinct temporal shifts across the three dietary assessment periods. The frequency of dairy product consumption declined significantly, mainly because of decreased milk intake in both sexes. In contrast, the overall frequency of SSB intake remained stable, although coffee consumption frequency decreased and soda and fruit juice consumption remained steady or increased slightly, particularly among females. In terms of volume, total SSB intake increased markedly from 1.9 to 2.8 L per week (P<0.0001), whereas dairy intake volume changed little. Soda and fruit juice volumes nearly doubled, with a relatively greater increase among females. Reflecting these volume changes, weekly sugar intake from dairy products did not change significantly, whereas sugar intake from SSBs increased sharply across all beverage types and in both sexes.

In contrast, SSB consumption among adolescents and adults in the United States has generally declined in recent years. Analyses using National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey data indicate that SSB intake decreased between 2003 and 2016, although intake levels remain high and substantial sociodemographic disparities persist.31 Similar declines have been reported in SSB intake and added sugar consumption, with reductions largely attributable to decreased soda intake.18,32 Collectively, these findings suggest that public health initiatives such as school-based nutrition education, labeling policies, and local soda taxes have contributed to declining SSB intake in the United States. Nevertheless, overall consumption remains a concern and provides an important comparison point for Korea.

This positive trend in the United States is likely related to the effectiveness of public health initiatives, including nationwide campaigns and school-based nutrition education, which have increased awareness of the health risks associated with excessive sugar intake, including obesity and type 2 diabetes mellitus. Policy changes, including restrictions on SSB sales in schools and promotion of healthier beverage options, have also played important roles.33 In addition, restricting the purchase of SSBs through the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program has been suggested as a potential policy tool to reduce SSB intake among children and low-income populations.34 Enhanced consumer awareness, supported by improved labeling regulations and local soda taxes, has encouraged a shift toward water and low-calorie beverages and prompted the beverage industry to expand its range of zero-sugar and functional drinks.35-38

In contrast, SSB consumption has been increasing among Korean adolescents and young adults. To address this trend, Korea may benefit from adapting successful strategies used in other countries, including school-based education, parental involvement, and community campaigns. Policy measures such as sugar taxes, marketing restrictions, and youth-focused digital media campaigns may further reduce intake. Commercial settings can also promote healthier choices by offering smaller default sizes and unsweetened options. Coordinated implementation of these strategies may help curb excessive sugar consumption in Korea.

SSB consumption is a global public health challenge that has prompted diverse policy responses. Countries such as Mexico, the United Kingdom, and Chile have implemented sugar taxes, front-of-package labeling, and advertising restrictions targeting children and adolescents.39-41 In South Korea, national sugar reduction guidelines have been established.42 However, strategies must be adapted to rapidly changing consumption patterns, especially among youth and young adults.

Reducing SSB consumption requires a comprehensive, multisectoral approach. Educational campaigns should raise awareness of the health risks associated with SSBs and promote healthier alternatives. School-based interventions, such as removing SSBs from cafeterias and vending machines, are effective in modifying beverage preferences.43,44 Policy measures, including sugar taxes, mandatory labeling, and marketing restrictions, can further decrease consumption at the population level. In addition, behavioral economics strategies, such as defaulting to smaller portion sizes and reducing SSB visibility in retail settings, can encourage healthier choices. Coordinated efforts among government, schools, families, and the food industry are essential to reduce SSB-related health burdens and promote long-term public health.

This study has several limitations. First, the type and content of the FFQs differed across survey periods. Adults aged ≥30 years were included up to 2016, whereas from 2019 to 2021, only individuals aged 6 to 29 years were surveyed. In addition, the FFQ formats and beverage items assessed varied by period: a simple FFQ in 2010–2011, a semi-quantitative FFQ in 2012–2016, and beverage-specific questions in 2019–2021. In the semi-quantitative FFQ, liquid and solid yogurt were recorded separately but combined for analysis, whereas in the beverage-specific questions, sweetened and unsweetened milk, coffee, and fruit juice were aggregated. Despite these harmonization efforts, differences in FFQ format, item coverage, and target populations mean that the data are not fully comparable. Accordingly, the observed temporal changes should be interpreted with caution, as they may partly reflect methodological differences rather than true longitudinal shifts in beverage consumption.

Second, differences in dietary assessment tools limit comparability with previous studies. Earlier research on SSB consumption in Korea from 1998 to 2009 used 24-hour recall data,1 whereas the present study relied on FFQs and beverage-specific questionnaires, which better capture long-term, habitual intake. Unlike a single-day recall, FFQs assess usual dietary patterns over extended periods and, since 2012, have included portion-size information to improve the validity of estimates for both frequency and volume. However, FFQ survey cycles and target age groups varied, and some years lacked FFQ data, reducing cross-method comparability. Therefore, comparisons with earlier 24-hour recall-based studies are limited, and those data are cited only for historical context.

Third, reliance on self-reported FFQ data introduces the potential for recall and reporting bias. Despite these limitations, a major strength of this study is its use of a large, nationally representative Korean dataset, which enhances the generalizability of the findings. Unlike regional or hospital-based studies, these results offer valuable evidence for shaping national health policies and public health strategies. In addition, sugar intake from beverages was estimated using standard sugar content values rather than direct measurements of the actual products consumed. Although these values were derived from the national Food Nutrition Database, they may not fully capture variations in sugar content across brands or individual consumption patterns. Notably, coffee-related sugar intake was estimated using an assumed 40:60 ratio of unsweetened to sugar-added coffee, which was based on previous Korean studies but may not accurately reflect all participants’ choices. As a result, calculated sugar intake may have been underestimated or overestimated, particularly for beverages with diverse formulations.

In conclusion, beverage consumption patterns among Korean young adults have shifted over the past decade, with a decrease in the frequency of dairy consumption and a marked increase in the volume and sugar intake from SSBs. Coffee showed the largest absolute increase in volume, whereas the largest relative increase in sugar intake was observed for fruit juice. These trends varied by sex, with a greater decline in dairy frequency among females and a slightly greater absolute increase in SSB volume and sugar intake among males. To address the health risks associated with rising SSB consumption, coordinated action by government, schools, families, and the food industry is needed to support long-term public health improvements.
