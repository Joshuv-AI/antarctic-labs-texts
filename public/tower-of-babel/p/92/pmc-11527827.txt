Association of biomarkers and Barthel Index with occurrence of age‐related adverse health outcomes in individuals with diabetes
Authors: Kotaro Umamoto, Ryotaro Bouchi, Kotaro Soeda, Shosuke Satake, Tohru Hosoyama, Mitsuru Ohsugi, Kohjiro Ueki, Hiroshi Kajio
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAims/IntroductionThe clinical significance of age‐related biomarkers in patients with diabetes has not been fully elucidated. In this study, we aimed to establish models to predict the progression of aging in patients with diabetes using biomarkers.Materials and MethodsThis single‐center, retrospective cohort study included 115 Japanese patients with diabetes aged ≥60 years. Age‐related adverse health outcomes were defined as emergency hospitalization, any increase in the level of nursing care certification, admission to a nursing home or death. The associations of age‐related biomarker levels (adiponectin, growth differentiation factor 15 [GDF15], C‐X‐C motif chemokine ligand 9 and apelin) and clinical indicators with age‐related adverse health outcomes were evaluated. Factors that predominantly influenced the occurrence of age‐related adverse health outcomes were explored using the Cox proportional hazards model.ResultsThe mean age of the 115 participants was 73 years, 50.6% were men, the mean body mass index and hemoglobin A1c level were 25.3 kg/m2 and 9.79%, respectively. There were 26 age‐related adverse health outcomes during the study period (median 1.93, range 0–4.65 years). In a model combining clinical indicators and biomarkers, including the Barthel Index, GDF15 and adiponectin, the occurrence of age‐related adverse health outcomes was found to be significantly associated with GDF15 and Barthel Index. The group with both GDF15 and adiponectin levels higher than the median proved to be significantly higher than the group with both lower.ConclusionsThe measurement of GDF15 and adiponectin levels and the Barthel Index might be useful for predicting age‐related adverse health outcomes in patients with diabetes.Keywords: Aging, Biomarker, Diabetes

INTRODUCTION

Advances in various treatments have extended the life expectancies of patients with diabetes. In Japan, the average age of patients with diabetes during the 10‐year period from 2001 to 2010 was reported to be 71.4 years for men and 75.1 years for women, extending to the same level as those without diabetes. More than half of the patients with diabetes were aged >60 years 1 . Therefore, achieving a long and healthy life expectancy in patients with diabetes is the ultimate treatment goal. Given that patients with diabetes are more likely to develop geriatric syndromes, such as sarcopenia and frailty, than those without diabetes 2 , the identification of patients with diabetes who are at a high risk of developing age‐related adverse health outcomes is important to achieve individualized diabetes care.

Aging progression is associated with a decline in activities of daily living (ADL), and the Barthel Index is a simple indicator for assessing ADL. The Barthel Index can be easily calculated by evaluating the degree of independence of 10 items including eating, toileting and bathing 3 . A previous study reported an association between a marked decline in the Barthel Index or hyperglycemia and short‐term in‐hospital mortality among hospitalized elderly patients with diabetes 4 . However, no studies have used the Barthel Index to examine the progression of aging in elderly patients with diabetes and mildly impaired ADLs. In addition, no studies have examined the association between the Barthel Index and age‐related adverse health outcomes other than death.

Several biomarkers have been associated with aging: growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF15) is a member of the transforming growth factor‐β cytokine family, and is secreted by macrophages, cardiomyocytes, vascular smooth muscle cells and endothelial cells stimulated by stress signals, such as hypoxia, oxidative stress, inflammation and tissue injury 5 . Regarding glucose metabolism, GDF15 suppresses gluconeogenesis, inhibits liver fibrosis, reduces food intake, suppresses bodyweight and improves insulin resistance 6 , 7 , 8 . In relation to aging, GDF15 is a biomarker of mitochondrial dysfunction due to aging, although a mechanism by which GDF15 directly contributes to aging has not been identified.

Adiponectin is an adipokine derived from adipocytes that binds to its receptors (AdipoR1/2) widely expressed in the skeletal muscle and liver, and is known to have various effects on glucose metabolism, such as preventing insulin resistance, obesity, diabetes, and atherosclerosis 9 . In relation to aging, the administration of “AdipoRon,” which activates AdipoR in ob/ob mice with a knockout of AdipoR1/2, extends the shortened lifespan caused by a high‐fat diet 10 . Therefore, adiponectin is speculated to be a predictive biomarker of fat‐induced aging in diabetes that co‐occurs with obesity.

Apelin is an endogenous apelin receptor agonist that is widely expressed and has many physiological functions, including vasodilation, enhancement of myocardial contractility, fluid balance, metabolic regulation and skeletal muscle regeneration 11 . In relation to the cardiovascular system, apelin receptor activation prevents arrhythmia, inhibits thrombosis, has anti‐inflammatory and anti‐fibrotic effects, and promotes water diuresis 12 . Recently, it was reported that apelin‐deficient mice show an accelerated onset of sarcopenia and aging, whereas supplementation of aged mice with apelin enhances muscle strength and physical activity 13 , 14 , 15 .

C‐X‐C motif chemokine ligand 9 (CXCL9) is a member of the CXC chemokine family that is induced by interferon‐γ during inflammation. It binds to its receptor CXCR‐3 and promotes lymphocyte mobilization at sites of inflammation 16 . Several clinical studies have shown the usefulness of CXCL9 as a biomarker of disease activity in rheumatoid arthritis and its association with the formation of atherosclerosis 17 , 18 . Furthermore, a correlation has been observed between circulating CXCL9 levels and the presence of frailty 19 .

Although many studies have investigated biomarkers related to aging, only a few studies have explored the biomarkers related to the progression of aging, specifically in patients with diabetes. Furthermore, there have been no clinical investigations into whether a combination of age‐related biomarkers is useful in predicting the progression of aging. In the present study, we aimed to establish models to predict the progression of aging in patients with diabetes using the aforementioned biomarkers.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Participant recruitment

The present single‐center, retrospective cohort study included 1,183 patients admitted to the Department of Diabetes, Endocrinology and Metabolism, Center Hospital, National Center for Global Health and Medicine (NCGM; Tokyo, Japan) who agreed to participate in a registry study between February 2016 and June 2023 (MISSION‐DM‐ENDO). MISSION‐DM‐ENDO collects blood and fecal samples at the time of admission and discharge, and clinical information, mainly from patients admitted for the treatment of diabetes. Abundant clinical information, not only demographic information, but also the duration of diabetes, diabetic complications and treatment of diabetes, is registered with a high degree of complete coverage. Of the 1,183 patients, we excluded patients without diabetes (n = 77), patients without serum samples (n = 594), patients without adequate evaluation of sarcopenia/frailty (n = 231), patients aged >60 years (n = 95), patients undergoing treatment for malignancy, patients treated for malignancy within 5 years and patients with comorbid inflammatory diseases (n = 71). A total of 115 participants were included and analyzed (Figure 1). People who were evaluated as having age‐related indicators were predominantly older and had a longer duration of diabetes than those who were not; however, there were no significant differences in body mass index (BMI), hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), sex or prevalence of microvascular complications of diabetes.

Patient flow diagram of this study.

Information obtained and specimen analysis

Age, sex, body height, bodyweight, duration of diabetes, diabetic complications (peripheral neuropathy, autonomic neuropathy, retinopathy and nephropathy), HbA1c, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), albuminuria‐to‐creatinine ratio, body composition including skeletal muscle index (which was calculated using the bioelectrical impedance analysis method), Barthel Index (which was calculated from electronic medical records), “Kaigo‐do” (level of nursing care certification; Table S1), the presence of sarcopenia (which was determined by the criteria for Asian people proposed by the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia 2019) and the presence of frailty (which was determined by the revised Japanese version of the Cardiovascular Health Study criteria) were collected 20 , 21 . Blood samples were collected early in the morning before breakfast on the second day of hospitalization after a 10‐h fast. Blood was collected in 6‐mL test tubes containing ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid‐added blood collection tubes and blood collection tubes for biochemistry tests. Each was centrifuged to collect plasma and serum (Allegra X‐15R Centrifuge; Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA; adaptor 339179, 2,091 g, 10 min, 4°C), and 1.2 mL each was aliquoted into a 2.0‐mL screw cap tube and stored in a deep freezer at −80°C. For the measurement of age‐related biomarkers, stored serum specimens were thawed and aliquoted, cooled again to −80°C, and sent to the National Center for Geriatrics and Gerontology for analysis.

Serum CXCL9, GDF15 and adiponectin levels were measured by quantitative sandwich enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay using a commercial kit, according to the manufacturer's protocol (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA). Serum apelin peptide concentrations were also measured according to the manufacturer's protocol using a kit based on the principle of competitive enzyme immunoassay method (Phoenix Pharmaceuticals, Burlingame, CA, USA). Measurements were carried out on undiluted samples of CXCL9, fourfold diluted samples of GDF15, 100‐fold diluted samples of adiponectin and twofold diluted samples of apelin.

Age‐related adverse health outcomes were defined as emergency hospitalization, any increase in the level of nursing care certification (“Kaigo‐do”), admission to a nursing home or death, and its follow up was carried out from the electronic medical record 22 .

Statistical analysis

Spearman's correlation coefficients were evaluated to determine the association between the four age‐related biomarkers and various clinical indicators. Second, Kaplan–Meier curves were created for the cumulative incidence of age‐related adverse health outcomes for each patient group divided by biomarkers, and differences were evaluated using the log‐rank test. For each biomarker, the occurrence of age‐related adverse health outcomes was evaluated in two groups: one above and one below the median. Third, we carried out Cox proportional hazards regression analyses for several models combining clinical indices and/or biomarkers: a model in which only the clinical indices (age, sex, BMI, HbA1c, duration of diabetes and eGFR) obtained in usual care were input (model A), and a model in which model A plus one of the four biomarkers (apelin, adiponectin, CXCL9 and GDF15) or the Barthel Index were analyzed. Based on this analysis, multiple factors affecting the occurrence of age‐related adverse health outcomes were added to model A and analyzed using the Cox proportional hazards model. Receiver operating characteristic curves were constructed for several indices obtained in this analysis. Models including age, sex, BMI, HbA1c, duration of diabetes and eGFR alone (model 1); model 1 plus the Barthel Index (model 2); model 1 plus adiponectin (model 3); model 1 plus GDF15 (model 4); model 1 plus adiponectin and GDF15 (model 5); and model 1 plus adiponectin, GDF15 and Barthel Indices (model 6) were compared. Net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) were calculated to compare the predictive ability of each model for age‐related adverse health outcomes. All statistical analyses were carried out using EZR (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan), which is a graphical user interface for R (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). For each analysis, statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Ethical matters

The collection of clinical information and blood samples during hospitalization was carried out as a registry study approved by the Ethical Committee of the NCGM (approval number NCGM‐S‐000240‐05), and conformed to the provisions of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written consent was obtained from all participants. Subsequent tracking of age‐related adverse health outcomes was carried out as a clinical study approved by the Ethics Committee of the NCGM (approval number NCGM‐S‐004450‐04). The opt‐out method was used to obtain consent for this study.

There were a total of 26 age‐related events, including nine changes in the level of nursing care certification and 17 emergency hospitalizations (6 infections, 4 falls/fractures, 3 strokes, 1 hyperglycemia, 1 hyponatremia and 2 others); however, neither admission to a nursing home nor deaths were observed as age‐related adverse health outcomes during the observation period. The median duration from the timing of biomarker measurements to the occurrence of age‐related adverse health outcomes was 615.2 days (interquartile range 122.5–983.5 days).

Next, we examined the occurrence of age‐related adverse health outcomes using measured biomarkers (Figure 2). For each biomarker, the occurrence of age‐related adverse health outcomes was evaluated in two groups: one above and one below the median. Among the four biomarkers, the group with higher adiponectin levels had significantly more age‐related adverse health outcomes than the group with lower adiponectin levels (P = 0.041). The high GDF15 group tended to have more age‐related adverse health outcomes, but the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.144). There was no significant difference in the cumulative incidence of age‐related adverse health outcomes between the groups above and below the median apelin or CXCL9 levels.

We also carried out Cox proportional hazards regression analysis for several models that combined clinical indices and biomarkers. In the model in which only clinical indicators obtained in usual care (age, sex, BMI, HbA1c, duration of diabetes and eGFR) were included (model A), no statistically significant factors were found. Next, the model combining the factors included in model A with one of the four biomarkers or the Barthel Index was analyzed. Apelin (hazard ratio [HR] 0.895, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.983–1.019) and CXCL9 (HR 1.000, 95% CI 0.999–1.002) were not significant predictors. Adiponectin was not statistically significant, but had an impact on the occurrence of age‐related adverse health outcomes (HR 1.064, 95% CI 0.992–1.141). GDF15 (HR 1.313, 95% CI 1.038–1.659) and Barthel Index (HR 0.927, 95% CI 0.876–0.982) were found to be factors significantly affecting the occurrence of age‐related adverse health outcomes. Based on these findings, we created and analyzed a model in which adiponectin, GDF15 and Barthel Index were added to model A. We found that adiponectin, GDF15 and Barthel Index had a significant impact on age‐related adverse health outcomes (Table 3). When patients were divided into groups with adiponectin and GDF15 higher or lower than the median, the risk for age‐related adverse health outcomes was significantly higher in the group with high levels of both GDF15 and adiponectin compared with the group with low levels of both GDF15 and adiponectin (HR 7.084, 95% CI 1.259–39.86; Figure 3). Previous studies have reported higher GDF15 levels in patients receiving metformin 23 , 24 . In the present study, GDF15 levels were also higher in the metformin group (3.19 ± 1.49, 2.52 ± 1.34, P = 0.02). Similarly, GDF15 levels were also higher in the glinide group (3.52 ± 1.90, 2.65 ± 1.31, P = 0.03). Subsequently, Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was carried out using a model based on age, sex, BMI, HbA1c, duration of diabetes, eGFR, presence of metformin and glinide, Barthel Index, GDF15, and adiponectin, but neither metformin nor glinide was a significant predictor (Table S2). In the present study, adiponectin was significantly correlated with low BMI, suggesting that some cases with high adiponectin levels might be complicated by thinness, a high‐risk pathology for age‐related adverse health outcomes. Therefore, we examined the association between adiponectin and age‐related events by dividing patients into two groups with a cutoff of BMI 25 kg/m2, which is close to the median value in this study (25.4 kg/m2), but no significant association was found in either group.

Finally, we examined the predictive ability of each model in which age‐related biomarkers and clinical indices were combined to predict the occurrence of age‐related adverse health outcomes (Figure 4). The model including only age, sex, BMI, HbA1c, duration of diabetes and eGFR (model 1) had an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.632 (95% CI 0.506–0.758). The model with model 1 plus the Barthel Index (model 2) had slightly better predictive power (AUC 0.675, 95% CI 0.558–0.791), but the difference was not statistically significant compared with model 1 (NRI 0.096, 95% CI −0.160–0.352, IDI 0.029, 95% CI −0.024–0.083). Model 3, in which adiponectin was added to model 1, also showed a slight improvement in predictive ability (AUC 0.634, 95% CI 0.508–0.760) compared with model 1, but the difference was not significant (NRI 0.074, 95% CI −0.122–0.270, IDI 0.004, 95% CI −0.009–0.017). In model 1 with GDF15 (model 4), the predictive ability improved (AUC 0.732, 95% CI 0.623–0.842) and the difference was statistically significant (NRI 0.292, 95% CI −0.006–0.590, IDI 0.096, 95% CI 0.016–0.176). Model 4 plus adiponectin showed further improvement in predictive power compared with model 4 (AUC 0.739, 95% CI 0.631–0.848), but the difference was not statistically significant (NRI 0.002, 95% CI −0.119–0.123, IDI 0.004, 95% CI −0.007–0.014). Adding the Barthel Index to model 5 improved the predictive power to an AUC of 0.757 (95% CI 0.651–0.864), but this difference was not statistically significant (NRS 0.117, 95% CI −0.081–0.315, IDI 0.026, 95% CI −0.025–0.076).

DISCUSSION

In the present study, we showed that among the four biomarkers, GDF15 and adiponectin levels were useful predictors of age‐related adverse health outcomes in patients with diabetes. In addition to the clinical indicators easily obtained in routine medical care, such as the duration of diabetes, HbA1c, eGFR and BMI, the Barthel Index, GDF15 and adiponectin were found to improve the prediction of the occurrence of age‐related adverse health outcomes.

A study that measured GDF15 levels in 1801 community‐dwelling elderly individuals in Japan showed that older adults with higher GDF15 levels were at a higher risk of mortality. This study shows that declining renal function might have contributed to elevated GDF15 levels, and a similar mechanism might be at play in the present study 25 . The elevation of GDF15 level in aging is thought to be derived directly from damaged mitochondria or to be compensatory to restore damage to the liver and other organs due to mitochondrial dysfunction 26 . Adiponectin is expected to exert a positive effect on lifespan extension in basic studies, especially in obese diabetes models 10 . In contrast, many previous clinical studies have shown that adiponectin levels are positively correlated with frailty and decline in physical function in community‐dwelling and nursing‐home‐admitting elderly people 27 , 28 , 29 . This paradox has been attributed to various causes, including adiponectin resistance, compensatory responses to organ damage due to aging, renal dysfunction and reduced adiponectin clearance from the liver 30 .

In contrast, CXCL9 and apelin levels did not correlate with the progression of aging or with clinical indices related to diabetes in this cohort. CXCL9 is assumed to share a pathology with the development and progression of diabetic complications, such as atherosclerosis and angiogenesis 17 , 18 . Furthermore, apelin has been suggested to be associated with the progression of aging in mice 13 , 14 , 15 . However, previous clinical studies measuring apelin levels in community‐dwelling elderly people have reported no correlation between apelin levels and frailty or physical function 31 . Further studies are required to clarify the usefulness of CXCL9 and apelin in predicting age‐related adverse health outcomes in patients with diabetes.

There are several useful indices for assessing ADL, including the Barthel Index. The Barthel Index, which was used in the present study, is very easy to assess, because it adopts the degree of independence in 10 items, including eating, toileting and bathing, by interviewing patients 3 . Previous studies have used the Barthel Index to predict the occurrence of age‐related adverse health outcomes. For example, elderly people with highly impaired ADLs whose Barthel Index is <60 points, among those with acute myocardial infarction, have been reported to have a significantly increased risk of 1‐year mortality 32 . In patients with diabetes, a severe decline in the Barthel Index has also been reported to correlate with mortality 4 . However, the usefulness of the Barthel Index in elderly patients with mildly impaired ADL has not been adequately studied. In addition, few studies have examined the relationship between the Barthel Index and the occurrence of age‐related adverse health outcomes other than death. The present study included patients with diabetes aged 60 years. Of the 115 patients analyzed, 103 had a Barthel Index of 100 points, 10 had a Barthel Index of ≥80 points and the remaining two had a Barthel Index of <80 points, showing that few patients had severely impaired ADLs. In other words, the present study indicates that evaluation of the Barthel Index in patients with diabetes with relatively preserved ADL is useful for predicting the onset of age‐related adverse health outcomes. Therefore, the Barthel Index must be evaluated to provide personalized care for elderly patients with diabetes and realize healthy longevity. This study shows that aging‐related biomarkers are significant predictors of aging‐related events independent of the Barthel Index in patients with diabetes with relatively preserved ADLs. If aging‐related biomarkers can actually be evaluated in clinical practice, it might be possible to identify high‐risk groups for early aging progression.

Despite the abundance of baseline clinical data and the detailed assessment of geriatric syndromes, including the presence of sarcopenia and frailty, the present study had limitations. First, the sample size and the number of age‐related adverse health outcomes were relatively small. Second, those who were evaluated for age‐related indicators were significantly older and had a longer duration of diabetes than those who were not, suggesting that a selection bias might exist. Third, among the age‐related adverse health outcomes, emergency hospitalization and changes in the level of nursing care certification depend, to some extent, on artificial factors, such as decisions made by the physician or local welfare personnel. Fourth, we were unable to analyze the genetic background of this study. The genetic causes of several age‐related diseases, including Alzheimer's disease, have been studied thus far 33 . Genetic background might also influence aging in patients with diabetes. Considering that the cohort in this study consisted only of Japanese individuals, it might be difficult to apply these findings to other ethnic groups. Fifth, the participants in this study were patients with diabetes, it is difficult to elaborate the result to patients without diabetes. Sixth, this study was carried out on hospitalized patients, presumably leading to some selection bias. Finally, although this study used baseline clinical data and biomarkers, we could not assess these changes over time.

In conclusion, the measurement of GDF15, adiponectin and the Barthel Index is useful in predicting age‐related adverse health outcomes in older adults with diabetes, in addition to the usual clinical indices.
