Impact of nine dietary minerals on the risk of chronic kidney disease: a multi-model analysis in US adults using NHANES data
Authors: Zhuoga Cairen, Xinyun Chen, Shenju Gou, Ping Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic kidney disease (CKD), affecting 8–16% of the global population, presents substantial public health burdens. While constituting only 5% of daily dietary intake, minerals critically influence physiological processes. This study investigates associations between dietary mineral intake and CKD risk; utilizing publicly available data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), we employed multivariate logistic regression, restricted cubic spline (RCS), weighted quantile sum (WQS), quantile-based g-computation (QGcomp), and Bayesian kernel machine regression (BKMR) models to assess CKD-mineral relationships; multivariate logistic regression revealed significant inverse associations between CKD risk and calcium (OR = 0.72), phosphorus (OR = 0.69), magnesium (OR = 0.65), copper (OR = 0.81), potassium (OR = 0.85), and zinc (OR = 0.88). RCS analysis demonstrated linear protective trends for calcium, phosphorus, zinc, potassium, and selenium, while magnesium, iron, and copper exhibited U-shaped dose-response patterns. WQS regression identified a protective mineral mixture (weighted index OR = 0.83), with magnesium (35.2%) and calcium (28.1%) as primary contributors. QGcomp analysis highlighted magnesium’s protective effect (−0.21 risk score) contrasting with iron’s adverse association (+0.18). BKMR modeling confirmed magnesium’s significant protection (PIP = 0.92) and sodium’s risk elevation (PIP = 0.87); magnesium and calcium emerge as robust protective factors against CKD, while sodium intake increases disease risk. Phosphorus, potassium, zinc, and copper demonstrate potential benefits, whereas iron shows context-dependent effects. These findings emphasize mineral-specific dietary strategies for CKD prevention.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is becoming increasingly prevalent worldwide, affecting 8–16% of the global population and presenting significant public health challenges.(1,2) CKD patients often experience multiple comorbidities, including hypertension, cardiovascular disease (CVD), and CKD-mineral and bone disorder (CKD-MBD), which contribute to substantial healthcare costs.(3,4) As the kidneys are central to maintaining mineral homeostasis, disturbances in mineral metabolism are closely associated with the pathogenesis and progression of CKD.(5) In particular, mineral imbalances, such as hypercalcemia and hyperphosphatemia, can exacerbate kidney dysfunction, leading to additional complications that further deteriorate both renal function and overall health. Although minerals account for only about 5% of the human diet, they are essential for a wide range of physiological processes, including growth, tissue maintenance, and overall bodily function.(6) For example, copper plays a critical role in oxidation-reduction reactions and is a key component of enzymes like ceruloplasmin and cytochrome c oxidase.(7,8) Given that mineral metabolism is strongly influenced by dietary intake, it is essential to understand and manage the balance of minerals in the diet, not only to support general health but also to help slow the progression of CKD.

In this context, the relationship between dietary minerals and CKD has garnered considerable attention. Both excessive intake of certain minerals and deficiencies in others can have detrimental effects on kidney function. For instance, high sodium intake has been consistently linked to an increased risk of developing CKD.(9) The KDIGO (Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes) guidelines emphasize the importance of carefully managing mineral intake in CKD patients. Specifically, they recommend restricting minerals such as calcium, phosphorus, and sodium to prevent further progression of kidney damage and associated complications. Conversely, iron supplementation is commonly recommended to address anemia, a prevalent comorbidity in CKD patients.(10,11) Moreover, research by Liang et al. has demonstrated that elevated serum levels of toxic metals, such as cadmium, lead, and mercury, are positively associated with CKD, while higher levels of beneficial minerals like manganese and selenium show a negative correlation with CKD progression.(12) These findings highlight the critical need for individualized dietary strategies aimed at optimizing mineral intake in CKD patients.

However, most existing studies primarily focus on the individual dietary effects of specific minerals on CKD, often neglecting the complex interactions between various dietary minerals and their collective impact on CKD. To address this gap, this study seeks to identify dietary minerals associated with CKD using U.S. nationwide data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), utilizing a range of statistical models to assess the combined effects of nine dietary minerals—calcium, phosphorus, magnesium, iron, zinc, copper, sodium, potassium, and selenium—on CKD. These models include logistic regression, weighted quantile sum (WQS), quantile-based g-computation (QGcomp), and Bayesian kernel machine regression (BKMR). Ultimately, our goal is to provide evidence-based dietary recommendations for the prevention and management of CKD.

Materials and Methods

Study population

This study analyzes data from the 2011–2012 NHANES survey cycle. NHANES is a comprehensive, cross-sectional survey designed to assess the health and nutritional status of non-institutionalized civilian populations in the United States. Conducted annually by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), NHANES collects demographic, socioeconomic, and nutritional health data through standardized questionnaires and personal interviews. The NHANES dataset is freely accessible to the public and does not require specific permission for research use. Laboratory data are obtained via physical examinations conducted at mobile examination centers (MECs). Detailed information about NHANES can be accessed on the NHANES website (https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/). Our study does not involve a clinical trial, Clinical Trial Number: not applicable.

Initially, the study included 9,756 participants. However, 1,353 participants were excluded due to missing data on dietary minerals, and 3,358 were excluded due to missing information on age, race, serum creatinine (Scr), and urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (uACR). The analysis was further restricted to adults aged 20 and older, excluding pregnant individuals and those with unreliable energy intake (i.e., males with less than 800 ‍kcal/day or more than 4,200 ‍kcal/day; females with less than 500 ‍kcal/day or more than 3,500 ‍kcal/day). In total, 3,875 participants were included in the final analysis (Supplemental Fig. 1*).

Dietary intakes

Mineral intake values were estimated using the USDA’s Food and Nutrient Database for Dietary Studies (FNDDS) applied to the 24-h recall data. Two consecutive 24-h dietary recalls were conducted by trained interviewers to evaluate total dietary intake. The initial recall was performed face-to-face during the MEC visit, while the subsequent recall was gathered via telephone 3–10 days later. The average of the two recalls was used to calculate dietary intake, or a single recall if only one was available. The study focused on nine dietary minerals—calcium, phosphorus, magnesium, iron, zinc, copper, sodium, potassium, and selenium—sourced from the NHANES dietary questionnaire. Both dietary and supplement intakes were accounted for in the total dietary intake calculations.

Outcome variable

CKD is characterized by persistent abnormalities in kidney structure or function. Kidney function was evaluated using the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and the albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR). Urinary albumin levels were measured via a solid-phase fluorescent immunoassay, while serum and urine creatinine levels were determined using the Jaffe rate method (kinetic alkaline picrate), and other relevant biochemical parameters were determined according to standardized protocols described in the official NHANES Laboratory Procedure Manuals. These manuals provide detailed information on specimen collection, processing, quality control, and assay methodology. The eGFR was calculated using the CKD-EPI equation based on Scr levels. CKD was defined as eGFR below 60 ‍ml/min/1.73 ‍m2 or ACR ≥30 ‍mg/g.

Definition of covariates

Covariate data were collected through surveys, physical assessments, and laboratory analyses. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared, with height and weight measured directly. Race was categorized as Mexican American, other Hispanic, non-Hispanic white, non-Hispanic black, and other races. Diabetes mellitus (DM) diagnosis was based on doctor’s diagnosis, use of diabetes medication or insulin, fasting plasma glucose (FPG) test ≥200 ‍mg/dl, 2-h plasma glucose (2-h PG) ≥200 ‍mg/dl during OGTT, or HbA1c ≥6.5%. Hypertension was defined by an average systolic blood pressure over 130 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure over 90 mmHg, physician diagnosis, or use of hypertension medications. CVD included a history of congestive heart failure, coronary artery disease, angina, or myocardial infarction. Alcohol consumption was categorized into never (fewer than 12 drinks in a lifetime), low-to-moderate (up to one drink per day for women and two for men in the past year), and high (more than one drink per day for women and two for men in the past year). Smoking status was categorized as never (fewer than 100 cigarettes in a lifetime), former (over 100 cigarettes in a lifetime but not currently smoking), and current smokers (over 100 cigarettes in a lifetime and currently smoking). Physical activity levels were determined by weekly metabolic equivalent minutes. Average daily caloric intake was assessed over two days, with extreme values excluded.

Statistical analyses

The baseline characteristics were categorized according to the presence or absence of CKD. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages, while continuous variables are reported as means (SDs) or medians (IQRs), depending on their distribution. Differences between groups for continuous variables were evaluated using Student’s t test or Mann–Whitney U test, while the Chi-square test was applied to categorical variables. Dietary minerals were logarithmically transformed to minimize the influence of extreme values. Spearman’s correlation analysis was performed to assess the correlation coefficient matrix. A significance level of 0.05 was used for all analyses. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (ver. 4.3.3). Four statistical models were used to assess the effects of dietary minerals on CKD.

Statistical model 1: multivariate logistic regression models and restricted cubic splines (RCS)

Multivariate logistic regression models were used to examine the relationship between mineral levels and CKD risk, with results presented as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Model 1 was an unadjusted crude model. Model 2 adjusted for demographic factors, including age, sex, and race. Model 3 further adjusted for BMI, smoking status, alcohol consumption, calorie intake, CVD, hypertension, and diabetes. Additionally, RCS were employed to explore potential nonlinear associations between dietary minerals and CKD.

Statistical model 2: WQS regression model

WQS regression was employed to evaluate the potential combined effects of multiple dietary minerals on CKD. This method offers considerable flexibility in assessing the overall impact of minerals, accounting for collinearity, and determining the individual contributions of each mineral to the observed associations. Additionally, WQS regression uses the Bootstrap method to assign weights to each mineral, facilitating the identification of the most significant components within the mixtures. The weights, which range from 0 to 1, sum to 1. This approach effectively addresses mixed factors commonly encountered in real-life scenarios and demonstrates greater sensitivity than univariate analysis in identifying key predictors. Statistical model 3: QGcomp

Additionally, QGcomp, a novel statistical approach, was utilized to investigate the overall impact of dietary minerals on CKD risk and to determine the corresponding weights for each mineral. This method merges the inferential simplicity of WQS with the adaptability of g-computation. It relaxes the directional homogeneity assumption, enabling individual components within the mixtures to contribute either a positive or negative weight to the mixture index.(14)

Results

Population characteristics

The baseline characteristics of participants, stratified by CKD status, demonstrate significant differences across various parameters (Supplemental Table 1*). Participants with CKD are, on average, older (62.57 years) compared to those without CKD (46.19 years). CKD participants are also more likely to have lower educational attainment and report lower income levels. Health conditions such as CVD, hypertension, diabetes, and cancer are more prevalent among CKD participants, who also tend to have a higher BMI. Lifestyle factors further differentiate the groups, with CKD participants exhibiting higher smoking rates and lower alcohol consumption. Additionally, CKD participants have lower median intakes of calcium, phosphorus, magnesium, iron, copper, sodium, potassium, and selenium compared to their non-CKD counterparts (Supplemental Table 2*).

Correlation between nine dietary minerals

Given the complexity of dietary mineral intake, it is not feasible to consume only one type of mineral in isolation. Therefore, further exploration of the relationships between the nine minerals is warranted. The correlation matrix presented in Supplemental Fig. 2* illustrates the interrelationships among the dietary intakes of nine minerals. The correlations range from 0.38 to 0.80, indicating varying degrees of positive associations. Notably, phosphorus shows strong correlations with calcium (0.75), magnesium (0.71), zinc (0.77), and selenium (0.76). Magnesium is significantly correlated with sodium (0.75) and potassium (0.69), while sodium and potassium also exhibit a strong correlation (0.69). Zinc has substantial correlations with both phosphorus (0.77) and selenium (0.76). Consistent with previous nutritional studies,(15) dietary mineral intakes are often highly correlated due to shared food sources (for example, dairy providing both calcium and phosphorus). Such inherent multicollinearity highlights the importance of applying mixture-based analytical approaches, such as WQS and BKMR, to evaluate joint and potentially nonlinear effects.

Multivariate logistic regression models for assessing the association between dietary minerals and CKD

Multivariate logistic regression reveals significant associations between the intake of various minerals and the risk of CKD (Table 1). Calcium, phosphorus, magnesium, copper, potassium, and zinc intakes are inversely related to CKD risk, with ORs of 0.70, 0.62, 0.47, 0.64, 0.52 and 0.78 in Model 3, respectively (all p<0.05). Continuous intake of selenium, sodium and iron are not significant in Model 3. Consistent with the previous results, quartile analysis of calcium, phosphorus, magnesium, zinc, copper, and potassium shows a consistent trend of decreasing risk with higher intake (all p for trend <0.05), while iron, sodium and selenium is not significant in Model 3.

RCS regression analysis of the association between dietary minerals and CKD

The RCS analysis (Fig. 1) demonstrates that calcium, phosphorus, zinc, potassium, and selenium exhibit significant overall effects with predominantly linear associations (p for non-linearity = 0.2260, 0.3389, 0.0953, 0.6384, and 0.2279, respectively). In contrast, magnesium, iron, and copper display highly significant overall effects with pronounced non-linearity (p for non-linearity = 0.0057, 0.0002, and 0.0097, respectively), suggesting more complex dose-response relationships. Sodium, however, shows no significant overall effect (p for overall= 0.6237), aligning with the findings from the logistic regression analysis.

WQS regression model for assessing the association between dietary minerals and CKD

The WQS regression analysis was conducted to examine the relationship between dietary mineral intake and the prevalence of CKD. The WQS index of dietary minerals was inversely associated with CKD prevalence (OR = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.85–0.94, p<0.001), indicating a protective effect. Furthermore, the analysis identified the weighted contributions of individual minerals to the WQS index. As illustrated in Fig. 2A, magnesium had the highest contribution, with a weight of 0.405, followed by calcium (weight = 0.318), both of which exhibited strong associations with CKD. Zinc, selenium, and phosphorus contributed moderately, with respective weights of 0.118, 0.046, and 0.043. In contrast, copper (0.037), potassium (0.026), sodium (0.005), and iron (0.002) had lower weights, suggesting minimal associations with CKD.

QGcomp for assessing the association between dietary minerals and CKD

The QGcomp provides greater flexibility in modeling non-linear relationships and interactions between multiple exposures compared to WQS regression. As shown in Fig. 2B, QGcomp analysis revealed a significant association between dietary mineral intake and CKD prevalence (OR = 0.80, 95% CI: 0.78–0.81, p<0.001). The QGcomp regression analysis yielded mixed findings, with some minerals demonstrating positive associations (higher risk) and others exhibiting negative associations (protective effects). According to the QGcomp model results, iron had the highest positive weight, indicating a strong association with an increased risk of CKD. Sodium, potassium, and zinc displayed moderate to lower positive weights. In contrast, magnesium had the highest negative weight, suggesting a strong protective effect against CKD. Additionally, copper, calcium, phosphorus, and selenium also had negative weights, indicating potential protective associations and a reduced risk of CKD.

BKMR model for assessing the association between dietary minerals and CKD

The BKMR model, a non-parametric Bayesian variable selection framework, was employed to evaluate the joint effects of dietary minerals on CKD. To avoid arbitrary categorization, we treated the nine minerals as individual components without hierarchical grouping. Supplemental Table 3* summarizes the PIPs for each mineral in the BKMR model.

Among the nine dietary minerals, magnesium and sodium had the highest PIPs, with values of 0.92 and 0.88, respectively, indicating greater posterior support for their inclusion in the model relative to the other minerals. Calcium showed a moderate PIP of 0.374, suggesting comparatively weaker but non-negligible evidence of association within the mixture framework. In contrast, the PIPs for phosphorus, potassium, iron, zinc, copper, and selenium were relatively lower (all PIPs <0.5), suggesting a weaker effect on CKD risk in the context of the mixture.

Figure 3 illustrates the exposure–response relationship between each mineral and CKD risk while holding all other minerals at their median levels. Consistent with other models, the exposure–response curve suggested a positive trend between sodium intake and CKD risk, suggesting that higher sodium consumption is linked to an increased probability of CKD. In contrast, magnesium intake demonstrated a robust negative slope, indicating an inverse association against CKD. Calcium also exhibited a protective trend, whereas phosphorus showed a weak positive association. The dose-response curves for iron, zinc, copper, selenium, and potassium were relatively flat, suggesting limited evidence of association on CKD risk in this joint model.

As shown in Fig. 4A, the overall mixture effect was evaluated by simultaneously increasing all nine minerals to specific percentiles of their joint distribution. When all minerals were set above their median levels, the estimated probability of CKD tended to decrease relative to the reference level. Conversely, when all minerals were simultaneously set below approximately 40th percentile, the estimated risk appeared to increase. These results suggest that the joint exposure profile of dietary minerals may be associated with CKD risk.

Additionally, the single-exposure effects at varying percentiles (25th, 50th, and 75th) of other minerals are shown in Fig. 4B. A change in magnesium concentration from the 25th to the 75th percentile was consistently associated with a decreased risk of CKD, regardless of the levels of other minerals. Finally, Fig. 5 visualizes potential bivariate interactions. The interaction plots display parallel surfaces for most mineral pairs, indicating that there is no clear evidence of interaction among the nine dietary minerals between the 25th and 75th percentiles.

Discussion

This study comprehensively investigates the associations between the intake of nine dietary minerals and the risk of CKD using four statistical models: logistic regression, WQS, QGcomp, and BKMR. By employing multiple statistical models, the study provides a robust and nuanced understanding of the relationships between minerals intake and CKD. The multi-model approaches enhance the reliability and validity of the findings. The results offer valuable insights into the complex relationships between mineral intake and CKD.

Multivariate logistic regression indicated that higher intakes of calcium, phosphorus, magnesium, zinc, copper, and potassium are associated with a reduced risk of CKD. However, logistic regression assumes a linear relationship between the log-odds of CKD and mineral intake, which may not capture non-linear relationships or interactions. Therefore, RCS was employed in our study. RCS allows for flexible modeling of non-linear relationships, capturing complex dose-response curves that logistic regression might miss. Calcium, phosphorus, zinc, potassium, and selenium show significant overall effects with predominantly linear relationships. In contrast, magnesium, iron, and copper exhibit significant non-linear relationships. Interestingly, sodium’s negligible relationship with CKD in the RCS analysis contrasts with its significant positive association in the BKMR model. This discrepancy may be due to BKMR’s superior ability to model interactions between sodium and other variables. If sodium interacts with other minerals or dietary factors in a way that affects CKD risk, BKMR can reveal these interactions, leading to a significant association. In contrast, RCS might not capture these interactions as effectively, potentially resulting in a negligible relationship if sodium’s effect is primarily through interactions. Other studies tend to suggest that excessive sodium intake poses a higher risk for CKD, which aligns more closely with our BKMR results. Patients with high dietary sodium intake and urinary protein excretion will have a high risk for CKD progression.(16–18) Additionally, urinary sodium, which is positively correlated with sodium intake, has been shown to be a potent correlation of proteinuria in patients with CKD in the Chronic Renal Insufficiency Cohort study.(19)

The WQS and QGcomp models further emphasize the protective effects of magnesium and calcium, highlighting their substantial contributions to reducing CKD risk. WQS is designed to handle multicollinearity among predictors, which can lead to different interpretations compared to methods that do not account for this. However, by summarizing the combined effect of minerals, WQS may miss individual non-linear effects and interactions that other models, such as QGcomp and BKMR, can capture. QGcomp can model non-linear relationships and interactions, providing a more nuanced understanding that differs from simpler models. The BKMR model corroborates these findings by identifying magnesium as the most significant mineral impacting CKD risk. In the BKMR model, calcium also shows a protective effect against CKD, but its influence is less significant compared to magnesium. BKMR further validated that, potassium, iron, zinc, copper, and selenium have minimal impact on CKD risk, as indicated by relatively flat trends in their dose-response relationships. BKMR’s strength lies in its non-assumption of a specific functional form, allowing it to capture complex, non-linear relationships and interactions that other models might miss. Therefore, using several models together can complement each other and provide a comprehensive exploration of the role of minerals in CKD.

Magnesium, in particular, emerged as a key mineral with the highest weight in different models, underscoring its potential role in CKD prevention. Previous studies have shown that low serum magnesium levels are associated with poorer renal outcomes in CKD patients.(20–23) Specifically, in stages 3 and 4 of CKD, hypomagnesemia is linked to higher all-cause and non-cardiovascular, non-malignancy mortality.(24) Maintaining high serum magnesium levels may reduce the cardiovascular mortality risk associated with elevated phosphate levels in hemodialysis patients.(25) These findings highlight the adverse effects of low blood magnesium levels, which may be due to low dietary magnesium intake. This is consistent with our research results indicating that higher magnesium intake is associated with a reduced risk of CKD. Additionally, other literature has reported the benefits of dietary magnesium intake; for instance, magnesium intake was inversely associated with arterial calcification.(26)

The relationship between calcium intake and CKD is intricate due to the kidneys’ crucial role in calcium balance. As CKD progresses, serum calcium levels tend to decrease.(27) While dietary calcium intake can influence serum calcium concentrations to some extent, several other factors also play significant roles. These include kidney function, mineral-regulating hormones, bone turnover, the use of vitamin D analogs. Balance studies in CKD patients, which measure stool and urine calcium content while subjects consume a diet with a known calcium level, have shown that gut calcium absorption is lower in CKD patients compared to those without CKD.(28) Both non-dialysis and dialysis patients with CKD increased risks of mortality and disease progression associated with either higher or lower serum calcium levels.(29–32)

Overall, our study’s findings emphasize the importance of adequate intake of specific minerals in mitigating CKD risk. These results support the development of dietary guidelines that prioritize the consumption of magnesium, calcium, and other key minerals to prevent CKD. Future research should continue to explore the mechanisms underlying these associations and investigate the potential benefits of mineral supplementation in CKD prevention and management.

Utilizing data from the NHANES survey, which includes a wide demographic range, enhances the generalizability of the results. This ensures that the findings are applicable to various population subgroups, including different ages, races, and socioeconomic backgrounds. However, there are also limitations to consider. The study’s cross-sectional nature limits the ability to infer causality. Longitudinal studies are needed to establish a cause-and-effect relationship between mineral intake and CKD risk. Besides, the reliance on self-reported dietary intake data from 24-h recalls can introduce recall bias and measurement errors. Although efforts were made to average intakes over two days, more precise measurement methods, such as biomarkers, could improve accuracy. Furthermore, the study focuses on U.S. adults from the NHANES dataset. While this provides valuable insights for this population, the findings may not be directly applicable to populations with different dietary habits and environmental exposures. Replication of the study in diverse global populations would enhance the generalizability.

Conclusion

The various models, including multivariate logistic regression, RCS regression, WQS regression, QGcomp, and BKMR, consistently highlight the significant protective effects of magnesium and calcium against CKD, while indicating that higher sodium intake increases CKD risk. The results demonstrate that magnesium and sodium are the most impactful minerals, with magnesium showing a strong protective effect and sodium presenting a substantial risk. The other minerals, including phosphorus, potassium, iron, zinc, copper, and selenium, exhibit negligible independent effects on CKD risk.

This study underscores the significant role of dietary minerals in CKD prevention and management. The findings provide a strong foundation for developing targeted dietary guidelines and public health strategies to reduce CKD risk. Future research should address the identified limitations, particularly through longitudinal studies and improved dietary assessment methods, to build on these important insights.
