Label‐Free Detection of Nuclear Envelope Nucleoporation using 2D Morphological Embeddings and Machine Learning
Authors: Keivan Rahmani, Hamed Naghsh‐Nilchi, Leah Sadr, Einollah Sarikhani, Chichi Onwuasoanya, Yu Ching Wong, Weijia Wen, Zeinab Jahed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHigh‐aspect‐ratio nanostructures enable nuclear delivery through transient nuclear envelope (NE) disruption, but sporadic nucleoporation limits efficiency. This study presents a label‐free, machine‐learning approach detecting nucleoporation from morphological changes. U2OS cells cultured on silicon nanopillars undergo NE disruption detected via Ku‐80 mislocalization. A custom longest‐line algorithm quantifies Ku‐80 versus DAPI intensity profiles outside the nucleus, establishing ground truth for 714 cells (451 intact, 263 porated). An orientation‐invariant variational autoencoder, pre‐trained on publicly available mouse embryonic fibroblast images, generates 32‐dimensional embeddings for cell and nuclear shapes from binary masks. These embeddings, combined with four morphological descriptors (cell area, nucleus‐to‐cell area ratio, centroid distance, axis alignment) to reconstruct actual cell‐nucleus geometry, train a support vector machine (SVM). Training comprised 462 cells from five experimental chips; testing used 258 cells from two separate chips. The model achieves 87.0% area under receiver operating characteristic curve and 82.9% test accuracy. SHAP analysis reveals nucleus‐to‐cell area ratio as the strongest predictor, with specific nuclear features (surface smoothness, localized protrusions, radial bulging) and cell features (boundary complexity, elongation patterns, polarization) critically influencing nucleoporation probability. This demonstrates a simple AI workflow for investigating whether cell/nuclear shapes correlate with or predict phenotypes/events, enabling high‐throughput, non‐invasive monitoring with relatively small datasets.Keywords: Cell Engineering, Cell Therapy, Gene Delivery, Gene Therapy, Machine Learning, Nanopillar, Nano‐Injection, Nuclear delivery, Nuclear envelope rupture, nucleoporation

Introduction

Significance of Overcoming Cellular Barriers to Delivery

Efficient delivery of cargo into the nucleus remains a major challenge in manufacturing gene‐edited cell therapies due to the formidable barrier posed by the nuclear envelope (NE). While viral vectors can bypass these barriers, their application is limited by high production costs, immunogenicity, and off‐target effects.[ 1 , 2 ] Non‐viral delivery systems, especially physical methods, offer cost‐effectiveness and versatility. Specifically, high‐aspect‐ratio nanostructures, such as nanopillar, nano‐needles,[ 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 ] and nano‐tubes,[ 8 , 9 , 10 ] have shown promise for non‐viral delivery by mechanically disrupting cellular membranes to enable cargo transport. Nano‐injection with these nanostructures enables cytoplasmic delivery through enhanced endocytosis or localized membrane poration while maintaining high cell viability.[ 4 , 9 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 ] Despite significant advancements in optimizing cytoplasmic delivery nuclear delivery remains inefficient due to the sporadic and poorly understood nature of nuclear membrane disruption on nanostructures.[ 4 , 15 , 16 ] Thus, there is a critical need for scalable, high‐throughput techniques to detect and quantify nuclear envelope nucleoporation (NE nucleoporation) events.

Current Methods for Visualization and Quantification of Nuclear Membrane Nucleoporation

Various techniques have been used to detect NE nucleoporation, each with distinct strengths and limitations. Electron microscopy methods, such as TEM and SEM,[ 17 ] have provided high‐resolution, static images of the cell/material interface that can reveal the nuclear membrane morphology and nucleoporation sites.[ 18 ] While these techniques provide valuable structural insights, they are labor‐intensive, require fixed samples, and have very low throughput, allowing only a limited number of cells to be analyzed and making them unsuitable for high‐throughput or dynamic studies. Live‐cell fluorescence microscopy is a widely used alternative for dynamic studies. Techniques such as confocal or immunofluorescence microscopy use fluorescently labeled nuclear localized proteins such as nuclear localization signal (NLS)‐GFP[ 19 ] or DNA repair factor Ku‐80‐GFP,[ 20 , 21 ] which disperse into the cytoplasm upon nuclear membrane disruption, or cyclic GMP‐AMP synthase (cGAS), which detects cytosolic DNA at NE disruption sites.[ 22 , 23 , 24 ] While these methods enable real‐time visualization, they rely on fluorescent labels or genetic modification of cells that can interfere with native cellular processes and require manual, time‐consuming processing, limiting their scalability.

Emergence of Machine Learning Techniques for Label‐Free Detection of Cellular Events

Recent advancements in machine learning algorithms have enabled the accurate prediction of various cellular processes, including apoptosis,[ 25 ] mitosis,[ 26 ] and disease progression,[ 27 ] based solely on the morphological properties of cells, thereby eliminating the need for fluorescent labeling. Building on these successes, we sought to determine whether events such as NE nucleoporation could be detected from cellular morphology without any labeling. To investigate this, we first performed a comparative analysis of the morphological differences between cells that experienced NE nucleoporation and those that did not. Our preliminary findings revealed distinct morphological signatures associated with NE nucleoporation, indicating that cell and nuclear morphology contain predictive information about these events. Building on these observations, we developed a machine‐learning‐driven, label‐free approach for detecting NE nucleoporation using morphological embeddings of cell and nuclear shapes. We applied a state‐of‐the‐art, orientation‐invariant autoencoder,[ 28 ] which we initially trained on publicly available binary images of mouse embryonic fibroblasts,[ 29 ] to segmented binary images of cells cultured on nanopillar substrates. This approach enabled the accurate prediction of nucleoporation events based solely on morphological changes. Our results identified key predictive features, including nucleus‐to‐cell area ratio as the strongest predictor, along with specific nuclear morphological changes (surface smoothness, localized protrusions, and radial bulging) and cellular features (boundary complexity, elongation patterns, and polarization), that strongly correlate with NE nucleoporation. These findings underscore the potential of machine learning techniques for high‐throughput, label‐free detection of rare cellular events, providing a foundation for the systematic study and optimization of nanostructure‐mediated delivery strategies.

Results and Discussion

Collection of Datasets of Nuclear Envelope Nucleoporation on Nanopillars

To develop a model for the label‐free detection of nanopillar‐induced NE poration, we first generated an experimental dataset consisting of hundreds of U2OS cells cultured on Silicon nanopillar substrates to induce NE nucleoporation (Figure 1a,b). The nanopillars tested in this study were fabricated by projection lithography followed by deep reactive ion etching, yielding average radius of 0.37 ± 0.05, pitch of 3.51 ± 0.04, and height values of 1.34 ± 0.1, respectively (detailed in Experimental Section and Figures S1 and S2, Supporting Information). To identify NE poration events, we utilized Ku‐80, a DNA repair factor that becomes mislocalized to the cytoplasm upon NE poration[ 21 ] (Figure 1c). In a recent study, we demonstrated that nanopillars induce transient disruptions in the nuclear membrane without penetrating the cell, thereby enabling controlled, temporary NE poration that can be detected by the cytoplasmic mislocalization of Ku‐80.[ 15 ] Prior to poration analysis, we confirmed that nanopillar substrate did not impact cell viability over the 8 h experimentation. The Live/Dead staining shows comparable viability on nanopillars (98.92% ± 0.55%) and flat control (99.56% ± 0.52%) (Figure S3, Supporting Information), validating that Ku‐80 mislocations are not due to cytotoxicity rather due to mechanical disruptions. Since EthD‐1 is a membrane‐impermeable marker and was not detected on cells seeded on nanopillars, this further confirms that no plasma membrane disruption occurred under these conditions. To quantify NE poration, we detected Ku‐80 mislocalization to the cytoplasm. Specifically, we used an automated segmentation method (Biodock software[ 30 ]) to delineate cell and nuclear boundaries in polygonal format (using actin for the cell edge and DAPI for the nucleus) (Figure 1c). We then developed a custom “longest‐line” algorithm (detailed in Experimental Section in Supporting Information) that intersects both boundaries (Figure 1d). Along each line, we measured the normalized and smoothed intensities of Ku‐80 and DAPI outside the nuclear polygon. The mean‐squared error (MSE) between these two signals indicates Ku‐80 leakage into the cytoplasm, serving as a quantitative NE nucleoporation readout. To quantitatively separate intact versus porated nuclei, we applied a Gaussian mixture model (GMM) to the distribution of Ku‐80/DAPI mean‐squared error (MSE) values (Figure S4, Supporting Information). This yielded data‐driven thresholds of MSE < 0.0035 for NE‐intact, MSE > 0.0091 for NE‐porated, and a narrow buffer zone between 0.0035 and 0.0091 to capture uncertain cases (posterior probability <0.9) (Figure 2a). Using these criteria, 451 nuclei were classified as intact, 263 as NE porated, and 6 fell within the buffer zone (N = 720 total cells). In total, 720 cells were analyzed across seven independent experiments, with an average NE poration rate of 0.37. Building on this dataset, we next sought to develop a label‐free predictor of NE nucleoporation based solely on morphology. For this purpose, images of the cell and nucleus were converted into binary masks, one representing the cell and the other the nucleus. These masks were passed to an orientation‐invariant autoencoder[ 28 ] to extract compact feature embeddings. The resulting embeddings for the cell and nucleus were combined with four classical morphological descriptors: cell area, relative area (nucleus‐to‐cell ratio), centroid distance (distance between the nuclear and cellular centers), and axis alignment (angle between the major axes of the nucleus and the cell). These features were then used to train a support vector machine (SVM) classifier to predict NE nucleoporation events based on morphology (Figure 1e). Finally, this framework was applied to quantify how morphological variations modulate the probability of NE nucleoporation (Figure 1f).

Morphological Changes in Cells and Nuclei Following Nuclear Envelope Nucleoporation

To further investigate the morphological correlates of nuclear envelope nucleoporation, we quantified a comprehensive set of single‐cell and nuclear shape descriptors (Figure 2; definitions in Experimental Section, experiment‐level distributions in Figure S5, Supporting Information). The Ku‐80/DAPI MSE difference along the nuclear boundary (Figure 2a) provided the quantitative discriminator of NE status using GMM‐derived thresholds with a defined buffer zone. At the level of combined nucleus‐to‐cell parameters, nucleoporated NE samples exhibited significantly higher nucleus‐to‐cell area ratios (Figure 2b), lower nucleus‐to‐cell circularity ratios (Figure 2c), higher nucleus‐to‐cell longest axis ratios (Figure 2d), and greater extent (Figure 2e), while no difference was observed for the nucleus–cell centroid distance (Figure 2f). At the cellular level, nucleoporated NE cells displayed significantly larger area (Figure 2g), higher circularity (Figure 2i), shorter perimeter (Figure 2h), and significantly altered longest axis (Figure 2j), eccentricity (Figure 2l), and axis ratio (Figure 2m), whereas no significant differences were observed for the shortest axis (Figure 2k). At the nuclear level, nucleoporated NE samples had significantly larger area (Figure 2n), higher perimeter (Figure 2o), reduced circularity (Figure 2p), and significantly increased longest and shortest axes (Figure 2q,r), while eccentricity (Figure 2s) and axis ratio (Figure 2t) did not differ. Together, these results indicate that NE nucleoporation is associated with larger and more circular cell boundaries, as well as nuclei that are expanded and less circular, consistent with a more irregular nuclear morphology.

AutoEncoder Training and Its Performance

To compress the cell and nucleus images into a low‐dimensional latent representation, we employed a variational autoencoder (VAE) architecture adapted from Burgess et al. (2024),[ 28 ] chosen for its ability to provide a rotationally invariant embedding space (Figure 3a). We first trained this model on publicly available binary images of mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs),[ 29 ] which were cultured on circular or triangular fibronectin micropatterns (as well as unpatterned controls) to enforce diverse cell shapes. The trained VAE was applied to our collected cell and nucleus images, generating a 32‐dimensional embedding for each cell and a separate 32‐dimensional embedding for its nucleus, totaling 64 morphological features per cell‐nucleus pair. An example of the VAE's reconstruction performance on our dataset is shown in Figure 3b. Subsequently, we use these 32 embedding values as morphological descriptors for each cell and its corresponding nucleus. To evaluate reconstruction quality, we computed the MSE and binary cross‐entropy (BCE) across the collected data set (Figure 3c). The average MSE for cells was 0.020 ± 0.010 and for nuclei 0.014 ± 0.007, while the average BCE for cells was 0.089 ± 0.089 and for nuclei 0.058 ± 0.043 (mean ± standard deviation). These metrics confirm that the autoencoder effectively captures the key morphological features of both cells and nuclei.

In contrast, higher NE nucleoporation is observed in nuclei when they spread out and develop irregular shapes.

NE Nucleoporation Predictor Model Performance

As presented above, our comparison of nucleoporated NE and intact cells revealed that features such as cell circularity, area, longest axis, and the combined 32‐dimensional embeddings of cells and nuclei could be predictive of NE nucleoporation. To explore this, we developed a predictive model using a total of 68 features. This feature set consisted of the 32‐dimensional embedding of the cell, the 32‐dimensional embedding of the nucleus, and four additional morphological descriptors: cell area, relative area (nucleus area relative to cell area), centroid distance (the distance between the centers of the cell and nucleus), and axis alignment (the angle between the longest axes of the cell and nucleus). Together, these features capture both the absolute and relative morphological characteristics of the cell–nucleus pair (Figure 3e). The ground truth labels for NE porated (1) and intact NE (0) states were generated using our custom “longest‐line” algorithm applied to immunostained images as described in the methods section.

To evaluate the model's generalizability, we divided the dataset into training and test sets. The training set included experiments N1–N5 (n = 462), while experiments N6 and N7 (n = 258) were reserved for testing. An SVM classifier trained on these 68 features achieved a robust area under the ROC curve (AUC‐ROC) of 0.87, together with a precision–recall AUC of 0.83, indicating strong predictive performance (Figure 3f). The distribution of predicted probabilities for intact and nucleoporated nuclei is shown in Figure 3g, demonstrating clear separation between the two classes. Confusion matrices summarizing the model's performance on both the training and test sets are provided in Figure 3h. For the training set, the classifier reached an accuracy of 0.873, precision of 0.865, true positive rate (TPR) of 0.871, and false positive rate (FPR) of 0.259. On the independent test set, it achieved an accuracy of 0.829, precision of 0.855, TPR of 0.855, and FPR of 0.245. These results highlight the model's robustness and sensitivity, with high precision limiting false positives while strong sensitivity ensures accurate detection of true positives. The balanced trade‐off between precision and recall, as reflected in the precision–recall curve, confirms the classifier's effectiveness in distinguishing nucleoporated NE from intact nuclei.

Model Interpretation

To understand which features are most important in predicting NE nucleoporation, we utilized SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) summary plot (Figure 4a). The SHAP results indicate that the nucleus‐to‐cell area ratio (i.e., relative area) contributes the most positively to the model's NE nucleoporation probability scores. This observation aligns with our earlier findings (Figure 2), where NE porated samples exhibited both statistically significant larger nuclei and higher nucleus‐to‐cell area ratios compared to intact samples. Moreover, the positive correlation between cell size and SHAP values reinforces the notion that larger cells, and thus potentially more nanopillars supporting a single nucleus, are more prone to mechanical stress and subsequent NE poration.

To investigate how nucleus and cell morphology influence predicted NE nucleoporation probability, we synthesized new samples from the test set by varying their most important morphological features (identified via SHAP) from extreme negative to extreme positive values. For each modified feature set, we used the decoder portion of the trained VAE to reconstruct the corresponding cell or nucleus image, while all other parameters were held constant. We then fed these reconstructed samples into our SVM classifier to evaluate how predicted NE poration probabilities shift across the morphological spectrum. This approach provides a controlled, parametric exploration of morphological effects on NE nucleoporation, revealing how specific shape changes translate into higher or lower NE nucleoporation probability.

Nucleus Shape Impact on NE Nucleoporation Probability

Among the 32 nuclear morphological features analyzed, three distinct features emerged as having the greatest impact on NE nucleoporation probability (Figure 4a). While all three are linked to nuclear shape, each captures a different aspect of nuclear morphology, providing complementary insights into how shape influences membrane permeability. For clarity, we describe these features based on the specific morphological changes they represent: 1) surface smoothness (embed_nuc_11, Figure 4b), 2) localized curvature and protrusions (embed_nuc_25, Figure 4c), and 3) radial bulging and contraction (embed_nuc_12,Figure 4d).

The first feature (surface smoothness, Figure 4b) spans a gradient from lumpy to smooth nuclei. At negative values, nuclei exhibit irregular lobes and sharp angles, likely local stress sites associated with higher NE nucleoporation probability. As smoothness increases, nuclei become rounder and more stable, reducing NE nucleoporation probability. This feature reflects overall membrane uniformity, where smoother shapes correspond to more stable membranes that resist disruption.

The second feature (localized curvature/protrusions, Figure 4c) captures discrete deformations—bulges, flared edges, and ear‐like protrusions—that create high‐curvature regions and increase NE nucleoporation probability due to concentrated mechanical stress. Unlike the first feature's global smoothness, this one highlights asymmetric distortions and protrusions that emerge in discrete regions. As these protrusions recede and the nucleus becomes more isotropic (uniform), NE nucleoporation probability declines, illustrating how local shape irregularities affect membrane integrity differently from global features.

The third feature (radial bulging and contraction, Figure 4d) modulates the nucleus between isotropic, compact profiles and irregular, outward‐bulging shapes. At extreme positive values, nuclei appear circular and smooth, corresponding to low NE nucleoporation probability and enhanced stability. Toward the negative end, asymmetric bulges and swellings disrupt radial uniformity, elevating NE nucleoporation probability by creating localized sites of high curvature and stress. Unlike embed_nuc_11 (global smoothness) or embed_nuc_25 (localized protrusions), this feature reflects intermediate‐scale distortions in which the nucleus expands or contracts unevenly across its surface. Such radial bulges likely amplify local membrane stress, making the envelope more vulnerable to rupture.

Together, these three features show a consistent trend: nuclei with irregular lobes, localized protrusions, or uneven radial bulges are associated with a higher probability of NE nucleoporation. In contrast, nuclei that are smoother, rounder, and more isotropic distribute mechanical stress more evenly across the envelope and therefore exhibit greater membrane stability with reduced rupture likelihood.

Cell Shape Impact on NE Nucleoporation Probability

Similarly, we analyzed the top three cell morphological features that influence NE nucleoporation probability (Figure 4a). Each feature captures a unique aspect of cell shape which offers distinct insights into how variations in morphology affect nuclear membrane permeability. For simplicity, we describe these features based on the specific shape changes they represent: 1) boundary complexity and protrusions (embed_cell_3, Figure 4e), 2) cell elongation and symmetry (embed_cell_28, Figure 4f), and 3) polarized vs spread morphology (embed_cell_6, Figure 4g).

The first feature (boundary complexity/protrusions, Figure 4e) tracks how cells transition from smooth, rounded forms (low NE nucleoporation probability) to irregular shapes with elongated tails or protrusions, adopting teardrop‐like shapes (high NE nucleoporation probability). This trend suggests that these protrusions may generate localized mechanical stress on the nucleus, making it more susceptible to NE disruption.

The second feature (cell elongation/symmetry, Figure 4f), captures a distinct morphological progression. At the negative extreme, cells appear narrow and necked, with elongation concentrated in a single region. As the feature value increases, cells transition through teardrop‐ or triangular‐like shapes and ultimately develop into highly protrusive, star‐shaped forms at the positive end. Interestingly, NE nucleoporation probability decreases along this continuum, despite the more geometrically complex appearance of the star‐shaped cells. A plausible explanation is that narrow, elongated shapes focus cytoskeletal tension on the nucleus, increasing the probability of NE nucleoporation. In contrast, multiple, less pronounced protrusions in star‐shaped cells may distribute mechanical forces more evenly, reducing stress on the nucleus and thus lowering NE nucleoporation probability. This feature highlights how the pattern and distribution of protrusions, rather than just their presence, can critically influence nuclear membrane stability.

The third cell feature (polarized vs spread morphology, Figure 4g) captures how cells transition from strongly elongated to more evenly extended shapes. At the negative end, cells adopt teardrop‐ or comet‐like forms, characterized by a single dominant protrusion. Such polarized morphologies concentrate contractile and traction forces along one axis, transmitting uneven mechanical stress to the nucleus and resulting in a higher probability of NE nucleoporation. Toward the positive end, cells become rounder or develop multiple smaller protrusions, distributing forces more uniformly across the cortex. In contrast to embed_cell_3 (which reflects boundary complexity) and embed_cell_28 (which captures elongation), embed_cell_6 highlights whether cellular forces are focused in one direction or spread across several, a distinction that appears critical in determining nuclear envelope stability.

Overall, these three cell features illustrate how boundary protrusions, elongation patterns, and global curvature differentially influence NE nucleoporation. A consistent trend emerges in which regions with pronounced negative curvature or sharp geometric transitions, particularly when located on broader areas of the cell body rather than at the tail, are strongly associated with increased NE nucleoporation probability.

Direct NE nucleoporation can enhance delivery efficiency in both non‐dividing and dividing cells. In non‐dividing cells, it provides a pathway for cargos that cannot rely on mitotic nuclear envelope breakdown. In dividing cells, it enables earlier nuclear access,[ 24 ] potentially increasing expression before the next cell cycle, and increases the fraction of successfully transfected cells regardless of their position in the cell cycle. Furthermore, direct poration can facilitate the import of large or transport‐restricted cargos that do not efficiently pass through nuclear pore complexes. These advantages highlight the potential of integrating our detection platform with diverse cytoplasmic delivery methods to synchronize nuclear access with cargo availability.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed a ML‐driven label‐free platform to detect NE nucleoporation in cells on engineered nanopillar substrates. By combining automated segmentation, an orientation‐invariant autoencoder, and a robust classifier, we accurately predicted nucleoporation events using morphological features alone. This obviates the need for electron microscopy or fluorescent labeling, which are low‐throughput, invasive, and resource‐intensive. Our analysis identified nuclear curvature, surface protrusions, and increased nuclear area as key predictors, validated by Ku‐80 mislocalization as a marker. These findings demonstrate that morphology can enable non‐invasive, high‐throughput monitoring of NE disruption.

Our label‐free approach is scalable for cell engineering, drug development, and mechanobiology, where understanding NE dynamics matters. Avoiding fixation, fluorescent labeling, and genetic modifications minimizes perturbations to native processes and allows real‐time analyses across large datasets. Moreover, because our method does not require large training sets, it can be applied effectively to smaller datasets, where conventional methods would struggle. This is particularly useful for the systematic optimization of nanostructure‐mediated gene delivery, often hampered by sporadic nuclear membrane disruption.

Combining this predictive framework with functional assays and immunofluorescence could further reveal the biological consequences of transient NE disruptions, with implications for therapeutic gene editing and precision medicine. Ultimately, our tool provides a scalable and adaptable strategy for refining nanostructure‐mediated gene delivery while advancing mechanistic insights into nuclear envelope integrity and precision nanomedicine applications.

Limitations

The model was trained on a specific cell line and experimental configuration, including the nanopillar shape used here. Applying it to other cell types or different experimental conditions will require retraining and threshold calibration. Results should be treated as context‐limited.

Experimental Section

Here, the data‐processing pipeline was summarized for converting annotated cell and nucleus boundaries into binary (black‐and‐white) images and extracting morphological features, as well as the key parameters used throughout the analysis.

Polygon Detection on Microscopic Images

To obtain polygons for both cell and nucleus boundaries from microscopic images, Biodock was employed.[ 30 ] The exported polygons formed the foundation for the subsequent morphology‐based assessments and NE nucleoporation detection workflows.

Polygon Parsing and Preprocessing

Each image was associated with a JSON file containing polygon annotations for the nucleus and the cell boundary. These JSON files were parsed to extract the coordinates of each annotated polygon. Coordinates were then converted into shapely Polygon objects. Each nucleus polygon was matched to its corresponding cell polygon by checking for If an annotated polygon does not explicitly repeat its first coordinate at the end of the list (i.e., was not closed), the initial coordinate was appended to ensure well‐formed geometry.

Generating Binary Images

To visualize or feed polygon shapes into machine‐learning pipelines, each shapely Polygon was converted into a binary (black‐and‐white) mask in the following steps. 1) A blank 2D canvas (PIL or numpy array) of the desired resolution (128 × 128) was initialized (default color = black). 2nd, The polygon's coordinates were mapped onto the canvas, and all pixels lying within the polygon were set to white (intensity = 255). The polygon's bounding box was centered and/or scaled so that the largest dimension was standardized (e.g., 62 pixels). The cell mask was generated by filling the entire cell polygon in white on a black background. The nucleus mask was generated similarly from the nucleus polygon. After computing the required scale factor based on each polygon's bounding‐box dimension, the polygon coordinates, then were rescaled and re‐rasterize onto a 128 × 128 canvas, keeping the polygon centered for consistency.

Intensity Profiling Through Cell and Nucleus for NE Nucleoporation Detection

To quantify nucleus NE nucleoporation via intensity profiles, candidate lines passing through the cell–nucleus interface were generated, and then selected an optimal “best” line for further analysis. For each cell polygon, first its major axis was calculated by identifying two boundary points on the convex hull that were maximally distant. The angle of this axis with respect to the horizontal (x‐axis) served as the reference angle (θref). Around θref, angles θi were drawn from a Gaussian distribution with a small standard deviation (e.g., ±10∘). Each angle θi ensures a direction approximately aligned with, but not identical to, the cell's longest axis was sampled. Then all the generated lines were computed within the cell polygon, discarding lines whose intersection was empty with the nucleus. The line producing the maximum Πi was selected as the “best” line, which by definition, satisfied the line which is long, and has the longest intercept with Nucleus. This approach ensures a robust sampling path that spans both the nucleus and cytoplasm as fully as possible. Threshold Determination

To classify nuclei as intact or nucleoporated, the distribution of MSE values in log scale was analyzed using a Gaussian mixture model (GMM). This revealed two well‐separated populations corresponding to intact and disrupted nuclear envelopes. The decision threshold was set at the intersection of the posterior probabilities of the two Gaussians, with an additional buffer zone defined by the local valley and slope‐change methods to account for overlap between populations. Final thresholds were: intact (MSE < 0.0035), buffer (0.0035 ≤ MSE ≤ 0.0091), and disrupted (MSE > 0.0091). A detailed description and supporting plots were provided in the Supporting Information.

Autoencoder

The orientation‐invariant autoencoder used in this study was described in detail by.[ 28 ] This autoencoder was trained on the mouse embryonic fibroblast (MEFs) dataset was generated and described in the paper by Jude M. Phillip et al.,[ 29 ] which underwent the same preparation steps as the nucleus and cell samples. Training was carried out for 100 epochs on 32,000 samples using an embedding dimension of 32 and a learning rate of 0.001. Next, the trained encoder was applied to embed both the nucleus and cell images into 32‐dimensional representations.

Feature Selection

It was aimed to include features that fully capture the morphological properties of both the cell and nucleus, individually and in relation to each other, for each sample. By applying the trained encoder to cell images and their corresponding nucleus images, 32 embedding features each for cells and nuclei, resulting in 64 total embedding features were obtained that describe shape in a size‐ and orientation‐independent manner. In addition, cell size, the relative size (i.e., the nucleus to cell size ratio), the distance between the centers of the nucleus and the cell, and the angular difference between their longest axes were incorporated. Altogether, these features enable us to reconstruct the full cell and nucleus geometry along with their relative properties.

Support Vector Machine (SVM) Hyper Parameters

Optuna, a Bayesian optimization library, was employed for hyperparameter tuning, together with 5‐fold cross‐validation. In each iteration, one unique experimental setup from the training data was used for validation. The best hyperparameters identified through this process are as follows: a polynomial kernel ('poly') with a penalty parameter C of ≈421.67, a gamma value ≈0.0002113, a polynomial degree of 2, and a coefficient of roughly 0.65696, with no class weighting applied.

Nanopillar Fabrication

The fabrication of the silicon nanopillars was carried out using projection lithography and deep reactive ion etching (DRIE). Silicon wafers were first cleaned using Piranha solution, then spin‐coated with photo‐resist AZ‐7908. The nanopillar pattern was then exposed using an ASML stepper, and the wafer was developed using FHD‐5 developer. The photoresist was used as the mask during DRIE etching. The etching depth across the silicon wafer was 2µm ± 0.2µm, with multiple measurements taken by JEOL‐7100F SEM. The schematic can be found in Supporting Information.

Substrate Preparation

To coat the Silicon nanopillar chips for cell adhesion, the chips were sterilized by rinsing with autoclaved deionized (DI) water, followed by pure ethanol (Thermo Scientific, USA), and allowed to dry at room temperature inside a chemical fume hood. Dry nanopillar chips were UVO‐treated for 10 min and incubated with 100 µg mL−1 poly‐L‐lysine (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA) solution[ 31 ] followed by 0.5% glutaraldehyde solution (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA) for 30 min each at room temperature. Lastly, a pre‐warmed 0.1% gelatin solution (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA) was applied to the chip and incubated at 37 °C for an additional 30 min. Each solution was applied by inverting the chips onto an 80 µL droplet on parafilm, with the nanopillar side contacting the droplet. The chips were rinsed 3 times with PBS pH 7.4 (1×) (Gibco, USA) between each solution application.

Cell Culture and Seeding

U2OS cells were obtained from ATCC; U2OS cells were cultured in McCoy's 5A medium (ATCC) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Invitrogen, USA) and 1% penicillin‐streptomycin (Gibco, USA) and cultured under humidified conditions at 37 °C and 5% CO2.

To seed cells onto nanopillar chips, the cells were detached from culture using TrypLE Express Enzyme (1×) (Gibco, USA), and a 100 µL suspension of McCoy's 5A media containing 2.5 × 104 cells was prepared. The nanopillar chips were placed in a 24‐well plate and rinsed with pre‐warmed PBS prior to seeding.[ 31 ] The cell suspensions were mixed thoroughly to ensure uniform distribution of cells and then applied onto the nanopillar chips. After 10 min of initial adhesion, 1 mL of growth media was added, and the cells were incubated for 8 h at 37 °C before fixation for further analysis.

Fluorescent Microscopy

Cells were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences, USA) for 10 min and washed with PBS to remove excess fixative. Cells were then permeabilized with a 1% solution of Triton X‐100 (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA) for 10 min and blocked using a 2% w/v solution of Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA) for 1 h. The cell samples were rinsed with PBS between each step.[ 32 ] Immunostaining protocols were carried out as described in.[ 21 ] The primary antibody used for immunofluorescence was Rabbit anti‐human Ku‐80 (2180S, Cell Signaling Technology, USA; dilution 1:200) with the secondary antibody conjugated to Alexa Fluor 488 (4412S, Cell Signaling Technology, USA; dilution 1:1000). Samples were washed in PBS and stained with 4′,6‐ diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (DAPI) (Thermo Scientific, USA; dilution 300 nm) for 5 min and Alexa 594‐phalloidin (Invitrogen, USA; dilution 1:400) for 20 min in the dark to stain the nucleus and F‐actin respectively. Samples were again washed thoroughly with PBS to prepare for fluorescence imaging. Fluorescence images were collected with an Echo Revolution microscope equipped with a 40× magnification PLAN Fluorite LWD CC Phase Ph2 type objective lens, with a numerical aperture (NA) of 0.60.

Cell Viability Assessment

U2OS cells were cultured at 37 °C in 5% CO2 in McCoy's 5A medium (ATCC) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Invitrogen, USA) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, USA). At 80–90% confluency, cells were detached with TrypLE Express (Gibco, USA) for 5 min, centrifuged at 300 g for 3 min, resuspended to 1 × 10⁶ cells mL−1, and seeded onto silicon nanopillars in 24‐well plates at 2.5 × 10⁴ cells cm− 2. After 8 h incubation, cell viability on flat and nanopillar regions was assessed using the LIVE/DEAD cytotoxicity kit (Thermo Scientific, USA), which labels live cells with calcein AM (green) and dead cells with ethidium homodimer‐1 (red). For nuclear staining, Hoechst 33342 (blue) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was added together with the LIVE/DEAD assay. Cells were incubated with the fluorescent probes for 30 min at 37 °C incubator and imaged on an Echo Revolution microscope with a 20× objective (NA 0.40).

SEM Imaging

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) was performed using a FEI Apreo Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscope (FESEM). Imaging was conducted under high vacuum and operated at 25 pA and 3 kV voltage. To enhance the visibility of the nanopillars’ height, images were captured at a 45° tilt. Samples were prepared for SEM imaging by adapting methods previously described by.[ 33 ] The fixed cell samples were rinsed 3 times in autoclaved DI water and dehydration was carried out by replacing the water with increasing concentrations of ethanol (Thermo Scientific, USA). Ethanol solutions diluted to 30, 50, 70, 90, and 100% were added to the fixed samples and each concentration was incubated on the chips for 10 min. The ethanol was then displaced from the sample by covering the chip with a 1:1 v/v of pure ethanol to Hexamethyldisilazane (HMDS) (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA) for 15 min, followed by incubation in 100% HMDS for an additional 15 min. The sample was then allowed to dry overnight in a fume hood to ensure complete evaporation of HMDS.
