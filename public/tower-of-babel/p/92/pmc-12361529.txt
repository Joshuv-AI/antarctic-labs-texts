Polymer-free β-cyclodextrins and mefenamic acid inclusion complex nanofibers for enhanced drug solubility and biomedical applications
Authors: Sonaimuthu Mohandoss, Kuppu Sakthi Velu, Naushad Ahmad, Ramachandran Srinivasan, Prasanta Roy, Prathap Somu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In this study, cyclodextrin (CDs) derivatives were used to form mefenamic acid (MFA) supramolecular inclusion complexes (ICs) via a polymer-free electrospinning technique, aiming to improve drug delivery and therapeutic applications. MFA, a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID), has low water solubility, but its solubility increases when complexed with CDs. Three β-CDs derivatives such as hydroxypropyl β-cyclodextrins (HCN), methyl β-cyclodextrins (MCN), and sulfobutylether β-cyclodextrins (SCN) were used for MFA ICs. Phase solubility studies showed increased MFA solubility with rising amounts of CDs, with stability constants of 337.1, 684.2, and 931.5 M− 1 for β-CDs: MFA (HCN: MFA, MCN: MFA, and SCN: MFA). SEM analysis of electrospun β-CDs: MFA nanofibers revealed bead-free structures with average diameters of 675 ± 128, 343 ± 92, and 248 ± 42 nm for β-CDs: MFA. FTIR, XRD, and TGA confirmed the characteristics of the nanofibers, and molecular docking studies suggested their stable 3D structures. Drug release studies showed rapid dissolution, with MFA concentrations of 51 ± 2.1 µg/mg, 63 ± 1.3 µg/mg, and 89 ± 1.7 µg/mg released within 60 s, and maximum releases of 82 ± 0.02, 90 ± 0.18, and 98 ± 0.12 µg/mg after 150 s, indicating sustained release. Antibacterial testing against E. coli and S. aureus showed that SCN: MFA NFs had the highest antimicrobial efficiency (99.3 ± 2.89% inhibition against E. coli and 98.3 ± 1.69% inhibition against S. aureus). In vitro cytotoxicity assays on HCT-116 colon cancer cells revealed significant anticancer effects, with inhibition (%) increased to 96.3 ± 2.3%, 91.4 ± 1.7%, and 99.1 ± 1.6% at 125 µg/mL for HCN: MFA, MCN: MFA, and SCN: MFA NFs, respectively. Additionally, fluorescence microscopy (DAPI and PI staining) confirmed enhanced cellular uptake and apoptosis induction in cancer cells after 24 h of incubation with the NFs. These findings highlight their potential as fast-dissolving drug delivery systems, effective antimicrobial agents, and promising candidates for targeted cancer therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-15651-z.

Introduction

In recent years, the pharmaceutical industry has focused on fast-dissolving drug delivery systems as a novel way to manufacture medications1. These systems allow medications to dissolve in the mouth upon contact with saliva2, eliminating the need to drink plenty of water or adjust chewing techniques3. Fast-dissolving drugs are especially useful for medications that do not dissolve well in water, making them more effective4. Multiple ways have been employed to create these fast-dissolving choices, such as powders, tablets, capsules, strips, and films. These include spraying, casting, tableting, freeze-drying, phase separation, sol-gel, interfacial polymerization, and electrospinning5–12. Among these, electrospinning is a versatile and successful approach for producing drug-loaded films, such as fast-dissolving nanofibers. This technique can be carried out in one of two ways: using a solution or by melting13. The electrospinning method is popular because it is simple and can be easily changed. Furthermore, it works at room temperature, making it ideal for combining medicine with nanofibers14. This procedure is easy and inexpensive, allowing the synthesis of extremely fine fibers from various natural and man-made components15.

Electrospinning relies heavily on polymers, which are typically large-molecular-weight and thick fluids16. To create smooth, bead-free nanofibers, the polymer chains must become tangled and overlap17. According to previous studies, parameters such as elasticity and relaxation time are more relevant than molecular entanglement in determining the electrospinnability of solutions18. Because of their nanoscale dimensions, the resulting NFs have a large surface area, lightweight structure, and many porous characteristics. These unique features make NFs suitable for a range of applications, including biotechnology19, textiles20, membranes21, catalysis22, batteries23, supercapacitors24, sensors25. Furthermore, the flexibility in designing NFs can effectively encapsulate active pharmacological compounds26. However, one key difficulty is that most electrospinning polymers are synthetic and require environmentally toxic organic solvents, restricting their application in industries such as medicine, pharmaceuticals and food packaging27. Furthermore, electrospinning is challenging for low-molecular-weight molecules28. Despite this, limited research has been conducted on the electrospinning of non-polymeric systems such as cyclodextrins (CDs)29, phospholipids30, chitosan31, and gelatin32. Starch-based oligosaccharides, such as CDs, are water-soluble and bio-renewable, making them an alternative to synthetic polymers for electrospinning supramolecular drug-loaded ICs NFs. This makes them intriguing candidates for the development of novel and improved functional NFs; however, further research is needed in this area33.

Cyclic oligosaccharides, commonly known as cyclodextrins (CDs), are naturally found, water-soluble ring-shaped sugar molecules formed by the enzyme-mediated degradation of starch34. Cyclodextrins (CDs) differ from linear dextrin in that they have a hydrophobic gap formed by the carbon chains of glucopyranose units. This distinct structure strengthens CDs against non-enzymatic degradation and improves their stability in neutral and basic environments35. This allows them to easily form stable connections with a variety of hydrophobic substances. CD-based electrospun nanofibers are gaining popularity because of their capacity to interact with pharmaceuticals via non-covalent mechanisms such as van der Waals forces, electrostatic attractions, and hydrogen bonds36. This characteristic improves drug stability and solubility. Additionally, CDs are non-toxic, making them attractive choices for the food and drug industries. They can help produce varied release patterns in medicinal formulations, hide unpleasant tastes, and reduce tissue irritation37. Chemically modified CDs, including hydroxypropyl, methyl, and sulfobutylether cyclodextrins38–40, have improved solubility and safety compared to the original forms (α-CD, β-CD, and γ-CD)41. Various drug molecules, such as ibuprofen37, chloramphenicol42, naproxen43, ferulic acid44, sulfisoxazole45, piroxicam11, tetracycline46, formononetin47, acyclovir48, paracetamol49, and hydrocortisone50, have been effectively integrated into polymer-free CD-based ICs to form NFs, as shown in studies by other groups.

Mefenamic acid (MFA) is an aminobenzoic acid derivative, specifically an anthranilic acid, in which a 2,3-dimethyl phenyl group replaces the hydrogen attached to the nitrogen atom51. In this study, MFA was selected as an anticancer drug because it belongs to the class of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs)52,53. Additionally, MFA is commonly prescribed for treating menstrual cramps and various other conditions54. However, MFA has several limitations, including poor solubility and stability, and the need for high doses (250–500 mg taken twice daily). It is also associated with adverse effects, particularly gastrointestinal issues such as bleeding and ulceration55. To address these challenges, previous studies by Sid et al.56 and Siva et al.57 have shown that forming supramolecular ICs between the MFA and cyclodextrins (CDs) can improve the physicochemical stability and solubility of the drug. While native CDs are water-soluble, their solubility is somewhat limited due to intramolecular hydrogen bonding, which interferes with hydrogen bond formation with water molecules. Despite the advantages of MFA-loaded NFs, there is a lack of research on MFA NFs in the literature. Therefore, our study aims to demonstrate the formation of supramolecular MFA-CD NFs to enhance antibacterial, anticancer, and solubility properties.

In this work, we used three types of β-CDs; HCN, MCN, and SCN to form inclusion complexes with MFA and fabricate NFs via electrospinning without the use of a polymeric matrix (Fig. 1). MFA exhibited improved solubility when complexed with HCN, MCN, and SCN showing stability constants of 337.1, 684.2, and 931.5 M− 1, respectively. SEM analysis of electrospun nanofibers demonstrated bead-free structures with average diameters of 675 ± 128 nm (HCN: MFA), 343 ± 92 nm (MCN: MFA), and 248 ± 42 nm (SCN: MFA). The FTIR, XRD, and TGA analyses confirmed the successful formation of ICs. The molecular docking studies indicated stable and energetically favorable 3D structures of the β-CDs: MFA ICs. In addition, the release of MFA from SCN: MFA nanofibers were significantly higher at 25 °C than that from HCN: MFA and MCN: MFA, which is attributed to the higher retention of MFA in the SCN: MFA complex. Moreover, the antibacterial tests revealed enhanced activity against S. aureus and E. coli, suggesting its potential for use in antimicrobial applications. Additionally, cytotoxicity studies on HCT 116 cancer cell lines showed that MFA-loaded nanofibers reduced cell viability, indicating their promising anticancer potential.

Experimental details

Materials

Hydroxypropyl β-cyclodextrins (HCN), Methyl β-cyclodextrins (MCN), and Sulfobutylether β-cyclodextrins (SCN) were purchased commercially from Sigma-Aldrich chemicals (South Korea). Mefenamic acid (MFA) was obtained from TCI chemicals (South Korea). All other chemicals and solvents were used without further purification. The Milli-Q ultrapure water system provided the distilled deionized water used throughout the study. The human colorectal cancer cell lines (HCT-116) were purchased from Korean Cell Line Bank (Seoul, South Korea).

Fabrication of nanofibers

The HCN, MCN and SCN (100% w/v) were accurately weighed and dissolved in deionized water to prepare the β-CDs: MFA (HCN: MFA, MCN: MFA, and SCN: MFA) ICs solutions. The MFA drug molecule was then added in such a manner that the molar ratio of MFA to each type of β-CDs was maintained at 1:1. This molar ratio was determined using the molecular weights of every component to ensure stoichiometric binding. The mixtures were stirred constantly overnight at room temperature (RT) in the dark to avoid photodegradation, and all of the samples had a cloudy appearance, suggesting the formation of ICs. To prepare for electrospinning, 10 mL of each β-CDs: MFA solution was carefully put into 20 mL plastic syringes using metallic needles (0.5 mm diameter). These syringes were attached to a syringe pump and fed at a steady rate of 1.0 mL/hour. The electrospinning method was carried out for 10 h. A high-voltage power source (15 kV) was provided to the needle during electrospinning to establish an electric field and facilitate the synthesis of β-CDs: MFA nanofibers. The β-CDs: MFA nanofibers were collected on a grounded collector, an aluminum foil sheet placed 15 cm away from the syringe. The process was conducted at RT and 60% relative humidity to optimize the electrospinning conditions. In addition, a comparative study was carried out the NFs of the pristine β-CDs were fabricated under the same electrospinning conditions. The schematic representation of the preparation process of electrospinning solutions and the fabrication of the nanofibers is shown in Fig. 1.

Phase solubility analysis

For the phase solubility analysis was employed from the method described by Higuchi and Connors58. A volume of 50 mL of distilled water was mixed with a 50 mg of MFA with β-CDs (0 to 12 mM). The resulting mixtures were stirred in the dark at a speed of 200 rpm and a temperature of 25 °C for 24 h to ensure complete equilibration. After the equilibration period, the solutions were filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane filter paper to remove any undissolved MFA particles. Each experiment was performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility. The final filtrates were then analyzed using UV-Vis spectrophotometry at a wavelength of 283 nm. The solubility diagram was constructed by plotting the concentration of β-CDs against the concentration of MFA, based on the absorbance values obtained at 283 nm.

In vitro releasing study

In vitro release behavior of the fabricated NFs (HCN: MFA, MCN: MFA, and SCN: MFA) was assessed for time-dependent release. Approximately 50 mg of each nanofiber sample was placed in 50 mL of distilled water at RT. Each nanofiber sample was added to individual glass beakers containing 50 mL of distilled water. The solutions were stirred continuously at 200 rpm using a magnetic stirrer to maintain uniform distribution. At predetermined time intervals (0–5 min), 1.0 mL of the test solution was withdrawn from each beaker. After extracting a sample, we replenished the container with additional distilled water to maintain a total volume of 50 mL. UV-Vis spectroscopy at 283 nm to determine MFA was produced by the NFs of HCN: MFA, MCN: MFA, and SCN: MFA. Following that, plotted the release data to see how MFA was released over time from the NFs we created.

Antibacterial study

The antibacterial properties of MFA and its combinations with β-CDs NFs such as HCN: MFA, MCN: MFA, and SCN: MFA against Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli using colony counting. The cultured bacteria in a nutrient-rich broth at 37 °C for a full day, shaking it regularly. Following the 24-hour incubation, adjusted the bacterial content to a known level and performed serial dilutions by mixing 1.0 mL of the culture with 9.0 mL of phosphate buffer. In addition, the dispersed 100 µL of each bacterial mix evenly on agar using a sterilized glass spreader. After 30 min, the β-CDs: MFA NFs transferred to sterilized paper discs or wells. Colonies of bacteria were counted after another 24 h of incubation at 37 °C.

Cytotoxicity

The cytotoxic effects of MFA, HCN, MCN and SCN, and their ICs (HCN: MFA, MCN: MFA, and SCN: MFA NFs) were tested on the human colorectal cancer cell line HCT-116. In an initial step, HCT-116 cells were grown in DMEM media with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C with 5% CO2. For 24 h, the cells were adhered in 24-well plates with a density of 104 cells per well. HCT-116 cells were seeded into 96-well microtiter plates at a density of 4000–5000 cells per well in order to assess their cytotoxicity. As a final step, the cells were treated with fresh media containing different concentrations of ICs by adding (25–125 µg/mL). Incubation at 37 °C was continued for another 4 hours after adding WST solution following a brief centrifugation. A proliferation assessment was then performed by measuring 450 nm optical density. Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted in triplicate and are shown as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Additionally, where applicable, ANOVA variance analysis was performed on the data, and the p-value was calculated. A p-value of ≤ 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Morphology of β-CDs: MFA

An aqueous solutions of HCN, MCN and SCN as well as their 1:1 molar ratio ICs with MFA (HCN: MFA, MCN: MFA, and SCN: MFA) at a 100% (w/v) concentration. These formulations were used in the electrospinning technique to create nanofibers with MFA and β-CDs ICs. This observation suggests that MFA had fully complexed with the modified CDs and became water-soluble. The addition of MFA to each β-CDs solution did not disrupt the electrospinning process. As a result, all solutions produced self-standing, flexible nanofibers that could be easily folded without structural damage, demonstrating their potential suitability for rapid oral delivery systems61. Figure 3a–f shows the SEM images of the fabricated NFs. These images correspond to pristine β-CDs and their respective MFA complexes (HCN: MFA, MCN: MFA, and SCN: MFA). The SEM images clearly show that the fabricated NFs were bead-free, smooth, and uniformly distributed. The average fiber diameter (AFD) of the nanofibers was calculated using the ImageJ software package, based on the size of 50 nanofibers. The bar diagram representing these values is shown in Fig. 4a–f. The measured AFD values are 802 ± 276, 752 ± 211, 715 ± 187, 675 ± 128, 343 ± 92, and 248 ± 42 nm (p < 0.05, statistically significant) for HCN, MCN and SCN, HCN: MFA, MCN: MFA, and SCN: MFA NFs, respectively. Interestingly, the SCN: MFA NFs exhibited a smaller average diameter, which may be attributed to the lower viscosity and higher conductivity of this particular system62. Based on these results suggest that the ICs formation between MFA and the modified β-CDs led to the successful creation of smooth, bead-free, nanosized, film-like materials. This process underscores the potential for these nanofibers to be used in advanced drug delivery applications, particularly in the development of oral delivery systems that are both efficient and capable of dissolving rapidly in the body.

Functional group analysis

The FT-IR spectra of HCN, MCN and SCN, MFA, and their respective ICs (HCN: MFA, MCN: MFA, and SCN: MFA) are illustrated in Fig. 5a–g. In the FT-IR spectrum of HCN, a broad stretching band corresponding to hydroxyl groups is evident on both the primary and secondary faces of the molecular cavity. Additionally, C-C/C-O stretching vibrations and the asymmetric stretching of the C-O-C glycosidic bridge are observed in the range of 1020–1150 cm− 1 (Fig. 5a). A peak at 2296 cm− 1 corresponds to O-H stretching vibrations63, while peaks at 1649 cm− 1 and 2929 cm− 1 represent C-H stretching and H-O-H bending vibrations, respectively64. For MCN, a broad absorption band at 3368 cm− 1 corresponds to O-H stretching vibrations associated with methylated group residues, while a strong peak at 1030 cm− 1 is linked to –OCH3 stretching vibrations (Fig. 5b)65. In the FT-IR spectrum of SCN, a peak at 667 cm− 1 indicates the presence of an S-O bond, whereas asymmetric and symmetric stretching vibrations of the SO3 group appear between 1151 and 1000 cm− 1 (Fig. 5c)66. The MFA spectrum features sharp, intense absorption bands characteristic of its functional groups, including methyl, carboxyl, amine, and aromatic groups. Notably, bands at 3310 cm− 1 and 2915 cm− 1 correspond to N-H and O-H stretching vibrations, respectively (Fig. 5d). Furthermore, C = O and C-N stretching vibrations are observed at 1649 cm− 1 and 1257 cm− 1, respectively67. Peaks at 1184, 1157, 1047, and 1031 cm− 1 correspond to the in-plane and out-of-plane bending vibrations of the aromatic rings C-H bonds.Moreover, the FT-IR spectra of the ICs (HCN: MFA, MCN: MFA, and SCN: MFA), it is evident that MFA characteristic peaks are present but show decreased intensity compared to the pure MFA spectrum. This reduction in intensity is likely due to the interaction between MFA and the β-CDs in Fig. 5e–g. The O-H stretching band of β-CDs moves when complexed with MFA, indicating the formation of hydrogen bonds between MFA and modified β-CDs. Additionally, the phenyl ring frequencies of MFA shift in β-CDs: MFA ICs. The spectrum alterations suggest that MFA phenyl ring contributes to ICs in the β-CDs cavity. The spectrum changes indicate that MFA and modified β-CDs have formed a complex68. Based on these data, it may be predicted that the most polar groups of MFA are positioned along the broader rim of the β-CD cavity or exposed to the outside, while the hydrophobic section of the MFA including the benzene ring is likely enclosed within the β-CDs cavity. In addition, the MFA phenolic moiety fits well with the β-CDs cavities enabling this arrangement.

Crystallinity analysis

Figure 6a–g display the XRD analysis of HCN, MCN and SCN, MFA, HCN: MFA, MCN: MFA, and SCN: MFA NFs. The pure HCN shown in Fig. 6a reveals a broad halo pattern, which is characteristic of its amorphous structure69. Likewise, the XRD pattern of MCN in Fig. 6b also confirms its amorphous nature, showing similar features to HCN. The XRD pattern of SCN in Fig. 6c displays a broad peak at a diffraction angle of 24.4°, further confirming the amorphous structure of SCN, as previously reported70. The amorphous nature of these β-CDs is indicated by the broad peaks, suggesting a lack of crystalline order in the materials. In contrast, the XRD pattern of pure MFA shown in Fig. 6d, exhibits characteristic strong peaks at diffraction angles of 13.1°, 14.5°, 15.7°, 20.6°, 21.3°, 26.7°, 27.9°, and 31.4°, which are indicative of high crystallinity71. These sharp, well-defined peaks confirm that MFA is a crystalline compound. The XRD patterns of HCN: MFA, MCN: MFA, and SCN: MFA display a broad halo pattern between 20° and 25° in Fig. 6e–g, which is similar to the XRD patterns of the pure β-CDs. This broad halo is indicative of an amorphous structure, suggesting that MFA has been fully incorporated into the β-CDs cavities, resulting in the complete amorphization of MFA. The disappearance of the sharp crystalline peaks of MFA in the XRD patterns confirms the successful IC formation between MFA and each β-CDs. The incorporation of MFA molecules into β-CDs cavities results in loss of crystallinity, indicating complete complexation. The XRD patterns show no crystalline structure of the MFA, indicating the production of stable ICs72.

Thermal analysis

To assess the thermal stability of the HCN: MFA, MCN: MFA, and SCN: MFA NFs was performed by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and the results are presented in Fig. 7 (a). The TGA profiles of HCN and MCN reveal two basic heating processes: dehydration and degradation. HCN weight loss ranges from 79 °C to 300 °C, while MCN degradation occurs between 74 °C and 315 °C. The SCN sample has weight loss at 71 °C, 228 °C, and 270 °C due to dehydration and heat degradation. The initial weight loss between 50 °C and 100 °C in all β-CDs is attributed to dehydration, as the samples lose water molecules. For pristine MFA, the TGA spectrum indicates a significant weight loss starting at approximately 229 °C, likely due to the breakdown of MFA. This is consistent with the finding that MFA does not exist in a polymorphic form, as previously reported by Sultan Alshehri et al.73. The TGA profiles of the HCN: MFA, MCN: MFA, and SCN: MFA NFs show weight loss at temperatures below 100 °C, which is primarily attributed to water evaporation. However, the major weight loss events for the ICs occur at higher temperatures at 275 °C for HCN: MFA, 285 °C for MCN: MFA, and 270 °C for SCN: MFA. These peaks are associated with the primary thermal degradation of the β-CDs, indicating the point at which the β-CDs molecules begin to degrade due to heat. The TGA results show that MFA complexed with β-CDs degrades at a slightly higher temperature than pure MFA. The ICs may improve the stability of MFA in β-CD cavities under heat exposure. In HCN: MFA, MCN: MFA, and SCN: MFA, the major temperature at which MFA breaks down rises to between 250 and 280 °C and 380–390 °C up from the original values of 240 °C and 365 °C, respectively. This indicates the formation of ICs between MFA and modified β-CDs (HCN, MCN, and SCN). The DTG curves indicate that the thermal breakdown range of β-CDs in these ICs is comparable to that of pure β-CDs74. Moreover, ICs-NFs lose less weight than original β-CDs, indicating that the combination of MFA and β-CDs contributes to weight loss reduction Fig. 7b Additionally, the remarkable stability of NFs helps extend shelf life by encasing and protecting the active MFA molecule from degradation and loss of effectiveness. The formation of MFA into the CDs cavity plays a role in prolonging shelf life, making it more appropriate for long-term storage and application. Throughout our research, we found that maintaining a moderate relative humidity (below 60%) is essential for ensuring the product’s stability and protecting it from degradation due to thermal stress.

Absorption properties

UV-Vis spectroscopy is a useful method for studying the interaction of MFA with HCN, MCN and SCN. Figure 8 shows UV spectra for NFs containing HCN: MFA, MCN: MFA, and SCN: MFA. The spectra of the β-CD derivatives reveal no major UV absorption peaks, aligning with data reported by N. Vimalasruthi et al.44. The spectrum of pure MFA reveals two prominent absorbance peaks at 353 nm and 283 nm. These peaks are indicative of transitions involving molecules with carbonyl groups (C = O) and nitriles (C ≡ N), as well as other unsaturated functional groups. The peak at 283 nm also corresponds to the π-π* transition of the phenolic group in MFA. The UV spectra of the ICs HCN: MFA, MCN: MFA, and SCN: MFA closely resemble that of pure MFA. However, a noticeable change is observed in the aromatic peak at 283 nm, which experiences a bathochromic shift (a shift to longer wavelengths) in the ICs. Specifically, the peak shifts to 291 nm in HCN: MFA, 293 nm in MCN: MFA, and 294 nm in SCN: MFA. These shifts suggest that the MFA interacts with the hydrophobic cavity of the modified β-CDs, leading to a change in the electronic environment of MFA. The bathochromic shift implies that the phenolic group of MFA is likely involved in the ICs formation with the β-CDs. This supports the idea that MFA can form stable ICs with the modified β-CDs, where MFA is encapsulated within the β-CD cavity, altering its UV absorption properties.

Molecular docking

A molecular docking study was carried out to get a better understanding of molecular interactions inside stable supramolecular host-guest ICs. This study aims to provide mechanistic insights into the pristine materials, including MFA, HCN, MCN and SCN as well as the ICs of HCN: MFA, MCN: MFA, and SCN: MFA as shown in Fig. 9. Docking simulations were carried out utilizing the PatchDock and FireDock servers75. Figure 9 depicts the docking results, which indicated high-quality molecular structures obtained using a geometry-based docking technique. The HCN: MFA combination contains the (-COOH) carboxylic acid group of MFA inside the cavity and the benzene ring outside as shown in Fig. 9. This arrangement matches the desired interaction configurations. For MCN: MFA and SCN: MFA, the docking analysis indicated that both the -COOH and N-H groups of MFA are located at the center of the β-CD cavities, with the benzene ring still positioned outside. The best-fitting 1:1 ICs models were identified based on the highest geometric complementarity scores (3132 for HCN: MFA, 3242 for MCN: MFA, and 3298 for SCN: MFA), with corresponding interface area sizes (332.60, 400.00, and 360.60 Å2) and atomic contact energies (-281.76, -341.39, and − 313.78 kcal/mol), which were calculated using the PatchDock program (Table S1).

Molecular docking studies of 3D structure of MFA, HCN, MCN and SCN, and its ICs of HCN: MFA, MCN: MFA, and SCN: MFA.

Additionally, global binding energies, van der Waals interactions, and atomic contact energies were calculated using the FireDock program. These values are as follows: global binding energy (-43.93, -48.68, and − 55.04 kcal/mol), attractive van der Waals energy (-16.87, -16.68, and − 20.00 kcal/mol), repulsive van der Waals energy (1.97, 2.82, and 2.84 kcal/mol), and atomic contact energy (-13.69, -16.39, and − 17.31 kcal/mol) for HCN: MFA, MCN: MFA, and SCN: MFA, respectively (Table S1). The binding energy of SCN: MFA (-55.04 kcal/mol) is notably lower than those of HCN: MFA (-43.93 kcal/mol) and MCN: MFA (-48.68 kcal/mol), suggesting that the sulfobutylether group substitution in SCN enhances its ability to form ICs with MFA. The negative binding energies indicate that these ICs are thermodynamically favorable, confirming the successful formation of the ICs76. The docking study results thus suggest that MFA, with its benzene ring and functional groups (-COOH and N-H), is effectively encapsulated within the β-CD cavities, further supporting the formation of the ICs between MFA and the modified β-CDs.

In vitro releasing study

Solubility plays a crucial role in drug delivery systems, particularly for poorly water-soluble drugs like MFA, which exhibits limited solubility in ethanol, chloroform, ether, and water (40 µg/mg at 25 °C, pH 7.1, and 80 µg/mg at 37 °C, pH 7.1). However, numerous studies have shown that converting such drugs into nanofibers (NFs) via electrospinning significantly enhances their physicochemical properties and solubility77–79. The release profiles of MFA from ICs-based NFs HCN: MFA, MCN: MFA, and SCN: MFA demonstrated a notable improvement in dissolution and drug release compared to pristine MFA, with maximum concentrations of 51 ± 2.1 µg/mg, 63 ± 1.3 µg/mg, and 89 ± 1.7 µg/mg (p < 0.05, statistically significant), respectively, reached within 60 s. A plateau phase was observed around 150 s, with final concentrations of 82 ± 0.02, 90 ± 0.18, and 98 ± 0.12 µg/mg (p < 0.05, statistically significant) for the respective NFs (Fig. 10). The amorphous structure of NFs helps maintain the release profile because it requires less energy to dissolve compared to a crystalline form. Moreover, the improved water solubility of the ICs aids in rapid disintegration and drug release. Furthermore, a smaller diameter with increased porosity enhances drug permeability, facilitating easier fluid penetration and promoting quicker drug diffusion from the fiber. These structural characteristics improve interaction with the dissolving medium and provide efficient penetration pathways, as also reported in studies on CD-ICs-based NFs of caffeic acid, where nearly complete drug release was achieved within 30 s. The combined effects of molecular encapsulation and nanofiber formation significantly enhance dissolution and release rates, suggesting that HCN: MFA, MCN: MFA, and SCN: MFA NFs could serve as an efficient, fast-dissolving oral drug delivery system for MFA. Furthermore, we determine the kinetic mechanism of the release studies using these data’s (cumulative release and time). Initially, we observed the various kinetic models such as Zero-order, First-order, and the Higuchi model. We calculate the R2 value for each model using our release data. The R2 value serves as a coefficient to identify the most suitable release mechanism, with the highest R2 indicating the best-fit kinetic model. By adhering to these procedures, we found that the Higuchi model (Square root of time versus cumulative releasing (%)) was the most suitable, with R2 values of 0.09971, 0.9907, 0.9971, and 0.9958 for MFA, HCN: MFA, MCN: MFA, and SCN: MFA, respectively, as demonstrated by the linear slope (Figure S1). This model implies that the sustained release of MFA from the NFs might be governed by Fickian diffusion. The information was incorporated into the revised manuscript, highlighted in yellow.

Antibacterial activity

Several studies have highlighted the antibacterial properties of MFA, with research demonstrating its effectiveness against pathogens such as E. coli and S. aureus80,81. There is growing interest in utilizing natural sterilization methods rather than chemical preservatives to mitigate health risks. Studies indicate that the antibacterial efficacy of drugs improves when incorporated into NFs-based ICs82,83. Based on these, the antibacterial activity of HCN: MFA, MCN: MFA, and SCN: MFA NFs was assessed using the colony cell counting method, revealing significantly enhanced antimicrobial properties (Fig. 11). The inhibition rates against S. aureus were 65.7 ± 2.14%, 88.2 ± 0.97%, and 98.3 ± 1.69% (p < 0.05, statistically significant), while against E. coli, they were 72.4 ± 1.57%, 89.1 ± 3.12%, and 99.3 ± 2.89% (p < 0.05, statistically significant), respectively (Fig. 11). From a mechanistic perspective, electrostatic interactions take place between the positively charged functional groups on the NFs and the negatively charged bacterial cell membrane. This interaction results in membrane damage and pore formation, which ultimately leads to the death of bacterial cells. Additionally, the ability to generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) offers an extra advantage by causing oxidative stress that damages bacterial proteins and DNA. Moreover, the solubility, stability, and release profile of MFA play a crucial role in enhancing their antibacterial activity. In the solubility perspective, due to its low solubility, MFA tends to aggregate together or precipitate, hindering its ability to penetrate bacterial cells. Enhancing its solubility allows MFA to dissolve more easily in bodily fluids, which aids in its approach to the bacterial cell wall and facilitates interaction with its target DNA, proteins, or enzymes. Additionally, we explore the potential of sustained-release MFA to deliver the drug directly to infection sites, thereby increasing its concentration at the target area and improving its antibacterial efficacy. Furthermore, among these NFs, SCN: MFA NFs exhibited the highest antibacterial activity, correlating with their superior solubility and binding efficiency compared to HCN: MFA and MCN: MFA NFs. Additionally, sulfation can further alter the surface properties of NFs, affecting their interaction with the bacterial cell membrane. This is consistent with previous findings that showed significantly higher antibacterial efficacy of MFA when complexed in ICs due to increased water solubility84. The results demonstrate that the produced NFs have significant antibacterial capabilities, which are strengthened by the production of IC-based NFs, making them potential candidates for antimicrobial applications.

Cytotoxicity study and cellular activity

The WST assay was employed to assess cell viability and examine the in vitro cytotoxicity of MFA, HCN, MCN, SCN, and their respective NFs such as HCN: MFA, MCN: MFA, and SCN: MFA against the HCT-116 colon cancer cell line with various concentrations (25–125 µg/mL) were applied to the cancer cells. MFA and its derivatives have been described as anticancer agents against several cancer cell lines, including mouse CT26 and human HT-29, SW480, and HCT-11685. However, pristine MFA exhibits moderate anticancer activity due to its poor solubility and stability, which limits its efficacy. The β-CD derivatives (HCN, MCN, and SCN) alone showed limited activity due to the absence of functional groups such as -COOH, -NH, or a phenyl ring that enhance anticancer interactions. As illustrated in Fig. 12a–c, the incorporation of MFA into NFs significantly improved inhibition (%) and increasing anticancer activity, with values reaching 96.3 ± 2.3%, 91.4 ± 1.7%, and 99.1 ± 1.6% (p < 0.05, statistically significant) for HCN: MFA, MCN: MFA, and SCN: MFA NFs, respectively, at the highest concentration (125 µg/mL). In addition to calculating the IC50 values, GraphPad Prism software is employed because it aids in fitting dose-response curves and precisely determining the 50% inhibition (IC50). The graph was presented in Figure S2. The IC50 values obtained are 48.23 ± 3.1, 8.65 ± 2.01, 12.45 ± 1.75, and 7.511 ± 2.33 µg/mL (p < 0.05, statistically significant) for MFA, HCN: MFA, MCN: MFA, and SCN: MFA, respectively. A lower IC50 value signifies a greater ability of a substance to inhibit a biological process. Among all the compounds, SCN: MFA exhibited the lowest IC50, which might be attributed to the sulfur group’s influence on cell interaction or the superior binding mode of SCN: MFA, contributing to its reduced IC50 value. These activities is attributed to the sustained release properties of the ICs and the controlled release of MFA. Among the three, SCN: MFA NFs exhibited the highest activity, which can be attributed to the presence of the sulfur functional group, higher complexation efficiency, smaller nanofiber diameter, and improved solubility, as confirmed by SEM analysis and phase solubility studies.

To further investigate the cellular uptake and interaction of these NFs with cancer cells, fluorescence imaging was performed using Bright-field, DAPI, and PI-staining methods. After 24 h of incubation with 125 µg/mL of HCN: MFA, MCN: MFA, and SCN: MFA NFs, images were captured to assess cell morphology, nuclear integrity, and apoptotic activity (Fig. 13). DAPI staining, which selectively binds to DNA, highlighted the intact nuclei of viable cells, whereas PI staining, which penetrates only compromised cell membranes, revealed apoptotic or necrotic cells. The merged images showed an increased proportion of PI-positive (red-stained) cells in the SCN: MFA-treated group, indicating higher levels of cell death compared to the other formulations. This suggests that SCN: MFA NFs were more efficiently internalized by HCT-116 cells, likely due to their specific affinity for the cancer cell surface, enhanced solubility, and increased binding interactions. These findings strongly support the potential of the fabricated NFs as effective anticancer drug carriers. The synergistic effect of molecular encapsulation within β-CD cavities and the nanofibrous structure significantly improved the bioavailability, cellular uptake, and therapeutic efficacy of MFA. Given their enhanced physicochemical and biological properties, HCN: MFA, MCN: MFA, and particularly SCN: MFA NFs could serve as promising candidates for targeted drug delivery in human colon cancer treatment.

Conclusions

In this study, electrospun supramolecular nanofibers (NFs) of HCN: MFA, MCN: MFA, and SCN: MFA were successfully fabricated without the use of polymers. Phase solubility studies confirmed the improved solubility of these NFs, revealing that all β-CDs significantly increased the stability constant, with values of 337.1, 684.2, and 931.5 M− 1 for HCN: MFA, MCN: MFA, and SCN: MFA systems, respectively. SEM analysis revealed that the NFs with smaller diameters were free of beads, with measured diameters of 675 ± 128, 343 ± 92, and 248 ± 42 nm for HCN: MFA, MCN: MFA, and SCN: MFA ICs NFs, respectively. The amorphous nature, functional groups, and enhanced thermal stability were confirmed by FTIR, XRD, and TGA analyses. The solubility of pristine MFA in water (40 µg/mg at 25 °C, pH 7.1) was significantly enhanced upon encapsulation in β-CDs, with maximum dissolution values of 82 ± 0.02 µg/mg, 90 ± 0.18 µg/mg, and 98 ± 0.12 µg/mg for HCN: MFA, MCN: MFA, and SCN: MFA NFs, respectively. Moreover, antibacterial studies using the colony counting method showed that the NFs exhibited high antimicrobial activity, with inhibition rates of 65.7 ± 2.14%, 88.2 ± 0.97%, and 98.3 ± 1.69% against S. aureus and 72.4 ± 1.57%, 89.1 ± 3.12%, and 99.3 ± 2.89% against E. coli for HCN: MFA, MCN: MFA, and SCN: MFA NFs, respectively. The highest antibacterial efficacy was observed in SCN: MFA NFs, which was attributed to their enhanced solubility and binding efficiency. Additionally, cytotoxicity studies using the WST assay on HCT-116 colon cancer cells demonstrated that cell viability significantly decreased with increasing NFs concentrations. At 125 µg/mL, the inhibition (%) was 96.3 ± 2.3%, 91.4 ± 1.7%, and 99.1 ± 1.6% for HCN: MFA, MCN: MFA, and SCN: MFA NFs, respectively. At the same time, the IC50 was reduced. In conclusion, our findings indicate that enhanced solubility, stability, release characteristics, thermal properties, and antibacterial efficacy are beneficial for in vivo studies, bioavailability evaluations, and clinical trials related to oral drug delivery systems.
