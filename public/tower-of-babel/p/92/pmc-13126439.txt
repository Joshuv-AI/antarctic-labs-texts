Procedural‐Based Contemporary Management of Bell's Palsy Long‐Term Sequelae: A Narrative Review

Bell's palsy (BP) is an acute unilateral facial palsy of unknown etiology that affects men, women, and less commonly children. 1 The leading etiologic theory involves a herpes simplex virus reactivation within the geniculate ganglion causing inflammation and edema of the facial nerve and subsequent compression injury. 2 The ensuing facial palsy peaks within 24 to 72 hours and can be complete or incomplete. Acute management involves high‐dose steroid administration within 72 hours of onset; while concurrent antiviral therapy is an option, it remains controversial. 3 Facial nerve decompression (FND) is not recommended due to cost, lack of accessibility to acute electroneurography (eNOG) testing, and the procedure's inherent risks. 3 BP is a diagnosis of exclusion that does not require imaging unless being used to rule out other potential etiologies, including but not limited to: cerebrovascular insult, infectious pathology (Ramsay Hunt Syndrome, Lyme Disease, etc.), systemic diseases, and neoplasms (specifically parotid tumors, peri‐parotid cutaneous malignancies, brain tumors, and facial nerve tumors). 4 , 5 It has been proposed that patients with depressive disorder (DD) are at an increased risk of developing BP due to the chronic immunologic and inflammatory changes. 6

The typical clinical course for BP demonstrates at least some degree of recovery within 3 months, with full recovery in up to 70% to 85% of patients during that time span. 7 , 8 Recovery rates are much lower in untreated cases and those with higher denervation percentages on ENoG. 8 If there is no evidence of facial palsy improvement after 3 months, one must reevaluate their diagnosis and refer the patient to a facial nerve specialist in accordance with the American Academy of Otolaryngology‐Head & Neck Surgery clinical practice guidelines. 3 Additionally, if a patient develops any long term sequelae, such as synkinesis, facial asymmetry, or ocular complications (exposure keratitis, lagophthalmos, etc.) they should also be referred to a facial nerve specialist. These long‐term sequelae are often debilitating and significantly reduce patients' quality of life, and although anecdotal, it is the senior author's experience that many patients live with these sequelae for months to years without knowledge of available treatment options. Miller et al published a review of treatment options for sequela of BP in 2020, which covers many of the same techniques discussed herein. 9 However, there have been continued additions to the scientific literature on this topic in intervening years. There does remain, however, a need for more comparative studies in the literature, with few prospective studies evaluating outcomes between groups receiving varied treatment. We have framed our review primarily for the general otolaryngologist (rather than the facial nerve specialist), as a reference and review of treatable sequelae from BP, in order to facilitate timely and appropriate discussion and referral for patients seen in this setting.

Methods

A literature search was performed on December 18, 2024, and subsequently August 8, 2025 (to address revisions) via PubMed using keywords such as “synkinesis,” “selective myectomy,” “selective neurectomy,” “botulinum toxin,” “chemodenervation,” “pathophysiology,” “depressor anguli oris,” “depressor labii inferioris,” “orbicularis oculi,” “mentalis,” “platysma,” “facelift,” “rhytidectomy,” “physiotherapy,” “quality of life,” “treatment,” “intervention” all in the context of “Bell's palsy.” Over 185 articles were reviewed with 84 selected based on preference given to articles published within the last 5 years, although inclusion of select older articles that provided necessary context for the current topic were also included. Articles included in the review were written in English or had available English translations. Preference was given to meta‐analysis and systematic review articles, but randomized control prospective clinical trials and retrospective chart reviews were included as well. The selected articles were then reviewed and agreed upon by all authors for inclusion. See Table 1 for additional search strategy details.

Discussion

Clinical Presentation

Initially, BP presents with varying degrees of flaccid paralysis, with ptotic tissue and lack of tone; however, this can ultimately develop into a non‐flaccid facial paralysis (NFFP) with up to 15% of patients experiencing some degree of synkinesis. 10 Synkinesis typically begins 3 to 6 months following the initial facial nerve insult and may continue to evolve for up to 2 years, taking on different forms and patterns depending upon the severity and muscles involved. Synkinesis is further subdivided and named by the voluntary facial region attempting movement followed by the synkinetic facial region affected, for example: oral‐ocular synkinesis, occurring in 85% to 96.5% of postfacial palsy patients with synkinesis, 11 , 12 describes unintentional orbicularis oculi contraction during volitional perioral musculature activation, narrowing the palpebral fissure. Other patterns include ocular‐oral, ocular‐nasal, ocular‐chin, ocular‐stapedial, chin‐ocular, and chin‐oral. 13 Oculostapedial synkinesis occurs rarely in BP where patients experience episodic tinnitus and/or hearing loss with facial movement and can be treated via a stapedial tendon transection if severe. 13 Acute ocular symptoms include dryness, irritation, tearing, bothersome appearance, and vision change; whereas long‐term ocular symptoms include tightness and asymmetry as the result of synkinesis.

Psychosocially, facial expression and smiling are vital for individuals' quality of life (QOL) and their assimilation into social networks. 14 Studies have even shown those with facial paralysis are deemed less attractive and thought to have a negative affect at baseline. 15 , 16 Due to the pain, lack of emotive control, functional deficits (oral incompetence or decreased palpebral fissure), and facial asymmetry that occur, synkinesis has shown to reduce patients' QOL significantly and increase their risk of developing depression or anxiety compared to facial paralysis alone. 17 These patients may present with impaired communication, worse QOL scores, and depression with more severe paralysis associated with higher chances of depression. 18 , 19

Evaluation

In addition to a formal ophthalmologic evaluation, the severity of a patient's facial paralysis and/or synkinesis should be objectively graded to track progression and treatment response. 20 Tedeschi et al's systematic review identified 17 separate tools reported to grade postfacial palsy structure and function. 21 Historically, the House Brackman scale was most commonly used but there has been a shift towards systems such as the Sunnybrook Facial Grading Scale (SFGS) which factor in the patients' level of synkinesis in addition to their resting symmetry and symmetry with voluntary movement. 22 , 23 The Facial Disability Index (FDI), 24 Facial Clinimetric Evaluation (FaCE), 25 and the Synkinesis Assessment Questionnaire (SAQ), 26 allow patients to grade their own perceived degree of synkinesis and associated impairment. The literature supports utilizing both the physician and patient‐based grading systems as patient‐based tools have been shown to provide better insight into patients' QOL. 25 , 27

The patterns and severity of synkinesis will vary from patient to patient, and across facial regions for any one patient. It is important to evaluate each patient on an individual basis in order to determine the best personalized treatment plan for them based on their unique presentation rather than attempting to standardize a treatment plan for all facial palsy patients. 28

Long Term Sequelae: Synkinesis

Synkinesis is the involuntary contraction of one facial muscle that occurs simultaneously during voluntary contraction of a separate muscle. This can develop following facial nerve injury of any kind, with a varied presentation and degree of severity amongst individuals. Although largely described as aberrant or misguided axon regeneration following facial nerve injury, 29 other physiologic alterations contribute to the clinical findings such as facial nucleus hyperexcitability and disrupted somatotopy, altered ephaptic nerve transmission, and higher cortical center reorganization. 30 , 31 A recent review by Rail et al reviews these concepts in detail. 32 Proposed terminology including NFFP or facial nerve aberrant reinnervation syndrome (ARS), 33 are characterized by varying levels of synkinesis, hyperkinesis, and hypomobility in different regions of the affected hemiface as a result of either proper or improper axonal regeneration. 34

Long Term Sequelae: Facial asymmetry

Asymmetric aging occurs in chronic BP as the affected hemiface has hyperkinetic regions amidst paralyzed regions. The hyperkinesis deepens the nasolabial folds while also slowing midface descent and decreasing neck jowling compared to the contralateral side. 35 , 36 The affected forehead may appear smoother compared to the naturally occurring rhytids in the unaffected forehead.

Long Term Sequelae: Ocular complications

The eye must be managed at each stage of BP. Acutely, complications such as corneal injury from paralytic lagophthalmos and exposure keratopathy may occur if the eye is not being heavily lubricated and taped. 37 A phenomenon known as Bogorad syndrome, gustatory lacrimation, or crocodile tears syndrome is epiphora that occurs during eating and is thought to occur due to salivary fibers aberrantly innervating the lacrimal gland. This complication occurs in approximately 3.3% of BP patients. 38 BP‐induced blepharospasm has also been suggested to occur from a combination of aberrant nerve regeneration and an abnormal sensitization of the blink reflex following altered corneal sensory input from excessive irritation. 39

Treatment: Physiotherapy

Although current guidelines do not make any formal recommendation for physiotherapy, 3 there is literature describing improved SFGS scores following intensive physiotherapy intervention that may include: education, neuromuscular retraining, massage, meditation, and mime therapy. 40 It is well established that earlier physiotherapy may improve facial functioning in the long‐term, but its impact on reducing or preventing synkinesis is controversial. 41 , 42 The goal of physiotherapy is to help patients cope with their new face, relearn their new smile, and learn massage and relaxation techniques for any associated pain.

Treatment: Chemodenervation

Regaining facial symmetry with botulinum toxin‐mediated chemodenervation involves targeting 5 main facial regions: periocular, perioral, chin, platysma, and select contralateral musculature (brow/forehead and depressor labii inferioris). Figure 1 highlights these potential facial areas for injection. Hetzler et al and Shinn et al have published informative guides for starting doses based on facial location, 43 , 44 but it is important to keep in mind dosages may increase with long term treatment and there is literature on the potential for developing antibodies and resistance to botulinum toxin over time with repeated doses. 45 , 46 Treatment resistance may be overcome by with higher doses initially, but may require switching botulinum toxin formulations. Periocular chemodenervation attempts to weaken the orbicularis oculi to increase palpebral fissure height, 47 , 48 while avoiding worsening any co‐existing lagophthalmos. 36 For perioral chemodenervation, one should focus primarily on the depressor anguli oris (DAO) and buccinator, as weakening the orbicularis oris, zygomaticus major and minor, and risorius has the potential to impact speech and oral competence and are typically better treated via massage. 36 , 49 The DAO is a smile antagonist and lowers the corner of the mouth during smiling if synkinetic. It is important to aim for the upper half of the DAO to avoid injecting the nearby and overlapping depressor labii inferioris (DLI). 50 The buccinator muscle pulls the commissure posteriorly, antagonistic to proper excursion while smiling. Injecting the buccinator may also prevent accidental biting of the inside buccal mucosa. 51 The mentalis elevates the chin and lower lip and is another important smile antagonist target as it can lead to lower lip elevation and chin dimpling if synkinetic. The mentalis is antagonistic to the DLI muscle, which is important in a full denture smile pattern. Yi et al published an injection guide based on external landmarks (5 mm lateral to the pogonion) to avoid injecting the DLI, which overlaps the superolateral mentalis. 52 Platysma synkinesis may be painful and lead to an undesirable tight neck band, snarling, and asymmetric open‐mouth smiling. 36 , 53 For proper chemodenervation, the platysma requires a broad field of injections along its entire course. 53 The contralateral frontalis and DLI can also be chemodenervated in hopes of regaining forehead and lower lip symmetry. 54 As the chief lip depressor, an intact DLI opposing a weakened DLI leads to an asymmetric smile and lower lip position. 36 Choi et al used 3D imaging and cadavers to reliably map out injection points for both the DLI and DAO. 55

Treatment: Selective Myectomy

There are surgical options for managing synkinesis, including myectomy (namely DAO and platysma), selective neurolysis/neurectomy (SN), and facial nerve and free muscle transfers. DAO myectomy has been shown to increase oral commissure median excursion, smile angle, dental show (Figure 2) with improvements in FaCE and eFACE scores and increased perceived happiness with smile. 56 , 57 The utility of DAO myectomy can be predicted with a local anesthetic muscle block trial although some studies report the myectomy itself outperforms the block. 58 , 59 DAO resection is a safe and relatively simple procedure that can be performed in the office under local anesthesia via an intra‐oral incision by isolating the DAO, demonstrated in Figure 3A. Buccinator myectomy has been performed in conjunction with the DAO myectomy with no significant clinical benefit compared to DAO alone. 60 Platysmyectomy has been shown to improve FaCE and QOL outcomes as well by resecting a full‐thickness segment of the platysma, which can be done through a limited incision in the neck overlying the platysma or via a facelift incision (Figure 3B). 61 Crucial to this maneuver is avoidance/identification of the nerve branch to the depressor labii inferioris muscle (“marginal mandibular” branch) which must be preserved for full dentate smile function. Orbicularis oculi myectomy has also been described via modified upper and lower blepharoplasty incisions. 62

Treatment: SN

SN involves dissecting out distal facial nerve branches and selectively ligating branches contributing to synkinetic facial regions. Branches to sacrifice include those supplying the platysma, DAO, mentalis, and buccinator, while preserving branches to the DLI and orbicularis oris. Branches to the zygomaticus complex, which must be preserved, are typically the superior limit of the nerve dissection shown in Figure 4. Two systematic reviews, one with 11 studies with 363 total patients, and the other with 7 studies and 250 total patients reported that SN is highly effective at improving both clinician and patient‐reported outcomes such as level of synkinesis, facial symmetry and function, patient satisfaction, and QOL. A patient who has undergone SN is shown in Figure 5. 63 , 64 The SN can be tailored to specific facial regions as well with one study demonstrating improvements on the Sunnybrook Facial Grading System scale in periocular synkinesis following selective orbicularis neuromyectomy. 65 The various surgical options can be synergistic when combined with rhytidectomy, lid surgery, static reanimation, and so forth. 66 A platysma myectomy in conjunction with DAO resection and selective neurectomy was found to significantly improve smile excursion and decrease postoperative platysma botulinum toxin administration. 67 DAO resection with SN that targeted mentalis specific branches improved lower lip symmetry (particularly the lower lip elevation seen on the affected side) compared to those without specific mentalis targeting. 68 Consideration must be given to oral competency when targeting branches to the mentalis, and older patients may be less able to compensate for some weakening of the mentalis muscle.

Treatment: Static and Dynamic Facial Reanimation and Facial Rejuvenation

Facial nerve transfers are also part of the surgical armamentarium however they are less successful for the long‐term BP patient phenotype of NFFP. 69 , 70 In contrast, static reanimation procedures may be considered, though these are more commonly utilized in flaccid facial paralysis. 71 Synkinetic facial regions may display signs of faster aging 36 compared to their unaffected contralateral counterpart, and thus, facial rejuvenation procedures are also considered for restoring facial symmetry. This ranges from filler to add volume to a deepened nasolabial fold, 55 to unilateral brow lifts, 36 to combining rhytidectomy with SN to improve upon isolated SN results. 72

Treatment: Ocular Specific

Treating the eye effectively in patients with BP requires finding balance between treating the periocular synkinesis and lagophthalmos. As synkinesis treatment has been previously discussed, this section will expand on lagophthalmos treatment. There are 4 ways to obtain palpebral fissure closure: lower the upper eyelid, raise the lower eyelid, tighten medially, and tighten laterally. Lid weighting is a very common upper lid treatment as it is easy to perform, low risk, and easy to remove if needed. 73 Platinum weights' higher density and slimmer profile are preferred over gold for improved cosmesis, less allergy risk, less extrusion, and less capsule formation rates despite being more expensive. 74 , 75 A palpebral sling may also be explored to correct upper lid retraction. 76 Lower lid laxity may be addressed via lower lid sling, tarsal strip, or lateral tarsorrhaphy—with the tarsorrhaphy reserved as a last resort due to its poor cosmesis and reducing the patient's visual field. 76

Treatment: Pitfalls and Limitations

While there are risks to all of the above procedures, it is important to note that risk severity and permanence is much higher for invasive surgical procedures, primarily the selective neurectomy. Aside from common procedural risks with facial surgery (eg, hematoma), the selective neurectomy procedure requires correct identification of the distal facial nerve branches, both for sacrifice and for preservation. Azzizadeh et al noted the most common complications are temporary oral incompetence (11%) and revision surgery (17%). 77 Oral incompetence may be better compensated for in younger patients, though this is anecdotal based on this author's experience. Additionally, there are concerns about the durability of both myectomy and neurectomy procedures with some evidence of recurrent dysfunction and re‐initiation of botulinum toxin therapy. 78 However, long‐term data and large comparative studies are still lacking for these procedures.

Treatment: Summary

Synkinesis treatment has evolved rapidly over the last decade with refinements in surgical techniques, all with the goal of restoring facial symmetry and function. First‐line treatment involves chemodenervation with botulinum toxin to affected facial regions, as well as physiotherapy. Surgical options exist in the form of selective myectomy, selective neurectomy, and facial rejuvenation and reanimation procedures. Treatment options are summarized in Table 2. There are no formal guidelines recommending selection of one technique over another, and this decision often relies upon the varied clinical presentation and desires of the patient. Combining approaches may offer synergistic benefits as an adjunctive to the baseline chemodenervation. 64 There have been a number of studies within the past 5 years that have sought to compare techniques' outcomes. Sanchez et al performed DAO and platysma myectomy along with selective neurectomies in one group and compared smile excursion and postoperative botulinum toxin administration to a DAO myectomy only group and retrospectively found slightly improved excursion and significantly decreased botulinum toxin injection in the selective neurectomy cohort. 67 When comparing chemodenervation and facial rehabilitation to chemodenervation, facial rehabilitation, and surgery, Wagoner et al demonstrated improved facial symmetry on Emotrics software in the trimodal therapy group. The greatest palpebral fissure symmetry and improvement in smile angle was in patients who underwent selective neurectomy, and selective neurectomy combined with facelift, respectively, when compared to no surgery. 72 Barbot et al compared outcomes among 4 different groups with varied myectomy and neurectomy targets. Ultimately, the best outcomes in repose, volitional movements, and synkinesis reduction as measured by SFGS, FaCE, FDI, and SAQ were in the group who underwent platysma myoneurectomy along with DAO and buccinator selective neurectomies. 80

Prognosis

Although most patients with BP fully recover, roughly 30% will continue to have some degree of mimetic dysfunction. 7 , 8 The treatment options discussed herein can help improve synkinesis, restore facial symmetry, and improve quality of life as described above. Management of the long‐term sequelae of BP does require active and ongoing treatment and should be tailored to each individual patient's goals and symptoms. Long‐term follow‐up is pertinent as patients' mental health needs to be under constant surveillance, and their synkinesis may evolve for up to 2 years following the initial insult. 81 Edalati et al demonstrated that 8.27% of BP patients will develop a new onset mental health disorder (MHD). 82 Treating physicians should be aware of the associated risk factors for developing new onset MHD like headache and facial pain. 82 Providers should also be aware of the increased risk of stroke in patients with BP, with Chiu et al reporting a 4% incidence compared to the general population's 1.6%, 83 perhaps associated with the increased risk of stroke in those with HSV‐1 and HZV. 84

Limitations

Our review is not without limitations. Given the lack of high‐quality studies on the topic, we performed a literature search of relevant keywords from a single database to create this narrative review, PRISMA guidelines were not followed as this is not intended to be an exhaustive systematic review of the topic. Although each author approved the included articles, their interpretation was still subject to biased interpretation given the nature of the review type.

Implications for Practice

This review may increase awareness among providers, helping to facilitate appropriate and early referral for patients suffering from long‐term BP sequelae to a facial nerve specialist. We summarized the various long‐term sequelae of BP present, signs and symptoms to monitor for, and described the current procedural treatment options that are available for each sequela. General otolaryngologists and other general providers will be more adept at identifying which patients among their broader Bell's palsy patient population may warrant referral. Providers will also be more familiar with the available treatment options which will allow them to offer more informed and current counseling to their patients. By facilitating referral to a facial nerve specialist, providers allow these patients to benefit from the significant recent scientific and clinical advances in management of the long‐term sequela of BP.

Future research should focus on further delineating the pathophysiology of BP itself as its exact mechanism, and the degree of contribution of each proposed mechanism, is still debated. Additionally, clinical research is being done to track patient outcomes in respect to rhytidectomy timing and whether it's advantageous to perform at the time of selective neurectomy or perform as separate procedures, as well as development of longer‐lasting botulinum toxin formulations, and perfecting additional selective myectomies techniques.
