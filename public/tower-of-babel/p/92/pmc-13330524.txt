High-throughput, multiplex immunofluorescence for the computer-automated immunophenotyping of Mycosis Fungoides
Authors: Patrick McMullan, Marc R Benoit, Nathan Gasek, Sydney Riddick, Joseph Masison, Katalin Ferenczi, David Rowe, Gillian Weston
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe diagnosis of mycosis fungoides is difficult and often delayed, exacerbated by the limitation of conventional immunohistochemistry to analyze only 1 antigen per tissue section, often necessitating repeat biopsies and extensive workups. We sought to validate a high-throughput multiplex immunofluorescence method, coupled with computer-automated image analysis, to generate comprehensive immunophenotyping data from a single formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded biopsy. We applied an 11-biomarker multiplex immunofluorescence panel across 18 archived skin specimens (9 mycosis fungoides/TCR clonality positive and 9 control/TCR clonality negative). Initial validation confirmed that multiplex immunofluorescence antigen expression and spatial localization were concordant with those of sequential immunohistochemistry-stained sections. Whole-slide image stacks were analyzed using both computer-assisted and computer-automated pipelines. Both methods successfully delineated immunophenotypic differences. Mycosis fungoides specimens showed a significant expansion of hematopoietic cells, atypical T-lymphocytes, and proliferative T-lymphocytes when compared with controls. Our results validate the potential of multiplex immunofluorescence to obtain comprehensive, high-dimensional diagnostic information from a single tissue section. Integration with computer-automated analysis offers a scalable, high-throughput platform that can significantly aid in the timely and accurate diagnosis of cutaneous lymphomas.Keywords: Immunohistochemistry, Immunophenotyping, Multiplex immunofluorescence, Mycosis fungoides

Introduction

Mycosis fungoides (MF) is a spectrum of non-Hodgkin lymphomas characterized by the expansion and accumulation of monoclonal malignant T-cells (Vermeer et al, 2022; Willemze et al, 2019). Diagnosis of MF can be particularly challenging within the early stages of the disease because of its equivocal clinical and sometimes histopathologic presentation, with an estimated mean delay in diagnosis being 2–4 years and can require an estimated mean of 2.89 biopsies per patient (Amorim et al, 2018; Skov and Gniadecki, 2015). Histopathologic diagnosis of MF often relies on immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining to identify the surface antigen expression profile or “immunophenotype” of malignant monoclonal T-cells (Horna et al, 2015; Willemze et al, 2019). Although IHC is a primary and widely adopted modality for MF diagnosis, it is typically limited in its ability to visualize usually 1, but rarely 2, antigens per single tissue section. This limitation can result in practical challenges, especially within equivocal cutaneous lesions, which can not only lead to exhaustion of the available tissue and the need for repeat biopsies but also often requires further diagnostic workup in the form of TCR molecular studies and flow cytometry (Gibbs et al, 2020; Lefebvre et al, 2023; Marks et al, 2021). In addition, these methods limit the potential to simultaneously visualize the comprehensive immunophenotype within the tumor microenvironment and readily distinguish between malignant and benign T-cells in situ.

In an effort to obviate these shortcomings of conventional immunophenotyping with IHC, multiplex immunofluorescence (MIF) has emerged as a promising technology for the identification and visualization of multiple cellular populations within a single tissue section (Gerdes et al, 2013; Lin et al, 2018; Phillips et al, 2021). MIF modalities can be broadly divided into simultaneous staining methodologies (ie, antigens of interest are all labeled at once) and cyclical staining protocols (Harms et al, 2023). Although simultaneous staining approaches benefit from their relative simplicity, their throughput is limited by spectral overlap between staining fluorophores. To overcome this, cyclical methods utilize repeated cycles of staining and imaging tissue specimens with a panel of primary fluorescently labeled antibodies that can be visualized by fluorescence microscopy (Figure 1), followed by signal quenching (Gerdes et al, 2013; Lin et al, 2018; Phillips et al, 2021). After each image acquisition, a photo-inactivation step can be performed to the stained tissue section to clear any remaining fluorescence signal from previous cycles, enabling multiple subsequent staining cycles on the same section and allowing for the detection of >30 antigens (Lin et al, 2018; Lin et al, 2015). Concatenated digital whole-slide image stacks of each specimen are generated to visualize the expression and spatial distribution of multiple antibodies and can be further processed computationally to generate comprehensive immunophenotyping data of similar quality to that of flow cytometry. In addition, with the generation of new digital whole-slide image immunophenotyping pipelines, these microscopy data can be analyzed at the single-cell resolution to distinguish the presence and relative abundance of distinct cellular populations between individual patient specimens in malignant versus benign disease states. Datasets can also be leveraged to explore spatial relationships among diverse cell populations that are unobtainable through traditional IHC or flow cytometry.

MIF is an emerging dermatopathological technique, particularly in the field of cutaneous oncology (Harms et al, 2023; Sun et al, 2024; Tan et al, 2020; Yaseen et al, 2022). Reports examining its utility in malignant melanoma and Merkel cell carcinoma demonstrated its ability to precisely characterize T-cell populations (CD3, CD8, FOXP3) and myeloid cells (CD68, CD163) alongside tumor markers (such as SOX2 for Merkel cell carcinoma or SOX10 for melanoma) and immune checkpoint molecules (PD-L1). This spatial analysis is crucial for predicting the response to checkpoint inhibitor immunotherapy in Merkel cell carcinoma and melanoma, given that the proximity of cells like PD-1+ and PD-L1+ or cytotoxic CD8+ T-cells to tumor cells can be highly predictive of efficacy, a finding that surpasses single PD-L1 expression (Harms et al, 2023; Sun et al, 2024; Tan et al, 2020; Yaseen et al, 2022). MIF is also employed in hereditary epidermolysis bullosa within the context of immunofluorescence antigen mapping, recognized as a standard diagnostic modality that functions as a multiplex assay utilizing multiple structural protein antibodies on a single slide (Berk et al, 2013; Rao and Shetty, 2021).

The primary goal of this study was to examine the utility of an antibody-based MIF assay to identify, distinguish, and quantify aberrant immune cell populations within MF skin tissue sections in comparison to control benign inflammatory dermatoses (Oh et al, 2021; Phillips et al, 2021; Stoll et al, 2021; Willemze et al, 2019). Herein, we first validated the relative expression and spatial localization of MIF-stained tissue sections in comparison to sequential sections stained with conventional IHC and H&E. We subsequently demonstrated the potential to generate immunophenotyping data from MIF digital whole-slide image stacks using 2 distinct image processing pipelines that collectively distinguished significant variations in atypical T-cell populations between MF and control specimens (Table 1) with high sensitivity and specificity.

Results

Validation of MIF workflow

We first examined H&E and IHC archived slides of 5 TCR gene rearrangement-confirmed MF cases and 2 inflammatory dermatosis cases with negative TCR gene rearrangement to validate MIF as an effective immunophenotyping modality. As demonstrated in Figure 2, we first compared the relative tissue-antigen expression and spatial localization of MIF-stained sections to both the H&E and IHC-stained sections examined by board-certified dermatopathologists to establish each case’s clinical diagnosis (Figure 2a). MIF provided concordant antigen expressions and spatial localization for a multitude of antigens, including CD4, CD5, CD7, and CD8, when compared with IHC (Figure 2b). Additional quantitative image analysis revealed a direct correlation in the number of normalized CD3, CD4, CD5, and CD7 positive cells between sequential IHC and MIF-stained slides (Supplementary Figure S1). Given these correlations, we next examined whether quantification of the CD4:CD8 ratio of MIF whole-slide image stacks could directly correlate with the diagnosed CD4:CD8 ratio obtained by visual inspection by board-certified dermatopathologists (Figure 2c). MIF whole-slide image stacks directly correlated in 72% (5/7) of examined cases with the original pathology report CD4:CD8 ratio in addition to digital quantified whole-slide images of IHC slides (Figure 2c). MIF overestimated the CD4:CD8 ratio, as documented on the initial pathology report, in 28% (2/7) of cases, which we hypothesize was secondary to lower detection in the absolute number of CD8+ T-cells within MF cases (Supplementary Figure S1).

Given this initial validation, an 11-biomarker panel frequently utilized in the diagnostic workup of MF (Table 2) was adapted to allow for 4 cycles of MIF on a single patient-derived tissue specimen (Figure 3a). This panel was used to examine 18 patient specimens with clinical and histopathologic suspicion for MF, consisting of 9 TCR clonality-negative (“control”) cases as well as 9 TCR clonality positive (“MF”) cases. Processed whole-slide digital image stacks demonstrated atypical monoclonal T-lymphocytes within the MF intraepidermal tumor microenvironment, with their immunophenotype exhibiting strong CD4 and CD45, marginal CD3 and CD5, and an effective absence of CD7, CD8, and CD30 antigen expression (Figure 3b) (Willemze et al, 2019). Collectively, these data validate MIF and represent its potential to generate a highly comprehensive immunophenotyping dataset.

Comprehensive immunophenotyping by MIF reveals global distinctions in cellular populations between MF and control samples

MIF whole slide image stacks were subsequently quantified using the open-source image cytometry software QuPath (Bankhead et al, 2017). Following cell detection and segmentation, automated thresholding was used for each individual antibody within the MIF panel to discern variations in global immunophenotyping between control and MF specimens (Figures 4a–d). MF specimens exhibited a significant expansion in CD45+ hematopoietic cell populations, CD4+ T-lymphocytes, Ki67+ proliferative cells, and atypical CD4+, CD7− T-lymphocytes when compared with control specimens (Figure 4c and 4d). These expanded populations consequently resulted in a significant reduction in the relative global percentage of pan-cytokeratin-positive epithelial cells within MF specimens compared with controls (Figure 4c and d). We did not identify any significant variations in the absolute number of total cells or pan-cytokeratin+ epithelial cells per unit of tissue area between control and MF specimens; however, MF specimens did exhibit a significant increase in the absolute number of CD4+ T-lymphocytes and CD4+, CD7− atypical T-lymphocytes per unit of tissue area (Supplementary Figure S2).

In addition to demonstrating global immunophenotypic variations, we quantified critical parameters relied upon for MF diagnostic workup, including: (1) CD4:CD8 T-lymphocyte ratio; (2) atypical malignant CD4 T-lymphocyte percentage defined according to CD45+, CD4+, CD7− surface expression; and (3) CD4+ proliferative index, demonstrating the percentage of Ki67+, CD4+ cell types (Figure 4e–g). Although we did not identify significant variations within the calculated CD4:CD8 ratio, MF specimens globally displayed a significant increase in the number of atypical CD45+, CD4+, CD7− T-lymphocytes and proliferative CD4+ T-lymphocytes (Figure 4e–g). Secondary analyses performed on intraepidermal immune populations did not reveal significant variations in CD4:CD8 ratio or atypical T-lymphocyte percentage; however, MF specimens similarly revealed an increase in the percentage of intraepithelial Ki67+ cells (Supplementary Figure S3). Collectively, these data provide direct evidence demonstrating the potential of MIF image stacks to delineate distinct immunophenotypic profiles among clinically and histopathologically equivocal MF and control specimens.

In order to further examine the diagnostic potential of MIF, we next calculated areas under the curve (AUC) of the receiver operating characteristic for 4 parameters: (1) CD4:CD8 ratio; (2) atypical CD4+, CD7− T-lymphocyte percentage; (3) proliferative T-lymphocyte index; and (4) CD45+ hematopoietic cell percentage (Figure 5). Similarly to the initially observed aggregated data, MF specimens did not exhibit a statistically significant variation in CD4:CD8 ratio AUC (P =.627); however, MF specimens exhibited increased AUCs in proliferative T-lymphocyte index (P = .0023), atypical CD4+, CD7− T-lymphocyte percentage (P = .0379), and CD45+ hematopoietic cell percentages (P = .00172) (Figure 5a). Sensitivity and specificity analyses revealed that MF samples could be readily distinguished from control samples under the following parameters: (1) proliferative CD4+ T-lymphocyte index >20% led to a sensitivity and specificity of 89%; (2) atypical CD4+, CD7− T-lymphocyte percentage >65% with 77.8% sensitivity and 89% specificity; (3) CD45+ hematopoietic cell percentage >50% with 89% sensitivity and specificity (Figure 5a).

Given the heterogeneity of histologic features and tumor immunophenotypes observed within mycosis fungoides, we subsequently developed an MIF composite scoring system to evaluate if the simultaneous analysis of multiple biomarkers could improve its diagnostic potential.(Garola and Singh, 2019; Uguen et al, 2015) Figure 5b demonstrates an MIF composite scoring system on the basis of CD4:CD8 ratio, proliferative CD4+ T-lymphocyte index, atypical CD45+, CD4+, CD7− T-lymphocytes and CD45+ hematopoietic cell percentages. This scoring system identified significant variations between control and MF specimens (Figure 5c), and a cutoff score of >7 revealed 89% sensitivity and specificity and the greatest AUC among individual IHC analyses (Figure 5d). Despite the limited sample size, these diagnostic data reveal sensitivity and specificities directly in alignment with those of TCR gene rearrangement PCR and TCR next-generation sequencing (Shinohara et al, 2024).

Processing of MIF stacks using a computer-automated image cytology pipeline readily identified immunophenotype distinctions between control and MF specimens

Encouraged by our initial immunophenotyping data, we next explored the utility of an end-to-end computer-automated multiplex image processing and analysis pipeline referred to as the Steinbock toolkit (Windhager et al, 2023) to identify and quantify MIF image stacks. Using both control and MF image stacks as the primary input, the Steinbock toolkit performed image segmentation and single-cell data generation through a fully computer-automated fashion, with subsequent bioinformatic data analyses completed using preexisting open-source software packages in R. This pipeline immunophenotyped 12 distinct cellular populations according to antibody expression by using its cell segmentation and hierarchical clustering algorithms (Figure 6a–c, and Supplementary Figures S4 and S5). Global cellular immunophenotyping revealed statistically significant variations between MF and control samples, with concordant trends to those previously observed using QuPath (Figure 6d). In particular, MF samples exhibited globally increased percentages of CD45+ hematopoietic cells, CD4+ T-lymphocytes, and atypical CD4+, CD7− T-lymphocytes when compared with controls (Figure 6d), in addition to a global percentage of epithelial cells without a reduction in absolute keratinocyte number (Supplementary Figure S2). In addition, although we did not identify statistical variations in the CD4:CD8 ratio between MF and control specimens (Figure 6e), MF specimens similarly revealed significant increases in atypical CD45+, CD4+, CD7− T-lymphocyte and proliferative CD4+ T-lymphocyte percentages (Figure 6e). Of note, although statistical trends remained consistent, the absolute quantified values and percentages varied between QuPath and the Steinbock toolkit (Supplementary Figure S2), which we hypothesize can be attributed to variations in image segmentation and thresholding between human and computer-automated workflows.

To further evaluate the diagnostic concordance between MIF image stacks analyzed using the Steinbock toolkit, we also calculated the AUC of receiver operating characteristic curves for 4 parameters: (1) CD4:CD8 ratio; (2) atypical CD4+, CD7− T-lymphocyte percentage; (3) proliferative T-lymphocyte index; and (4) CD45+ hematopoietic cell percentage (Figure 6f). These parameters in isolation provided direct correlations in sensitivity and specificity to the previous data. In addition, MF samples similarly exhibited a statistically significant MIF composite score, which using the previous diagnostic cutoff score of 7, revealed a sensitivity and specificity of 77.8% (Figure 6g and h). These computer-automated data not only directly correlate with the previous QuPath data, but also demonstrate, for the first time to our knowledge, the successful use of a computer-automated MIF image analysis pipeline to aid in the diagnosis of cutaneous lymphoma in a potentially high-throughput manner.

Discussion

Cutaneous T-cell lymphomas encompass a rare spectrum of skin cancers that can be difficult to diagnose because of the need for extensive cellular and molecular diagnostic tests. Here, we demonstrate the potential of MIF to obtain high dimensionality immunophenotyping information from a single skin tissue section in a condition that often requires multiple skin tissue sections and/or biopsies. We specifically examined equivocal cutaneous lesions with both clinical and histologic features concerning for MF that required additional diagnostic workup in the form of both IHC and TCR gene rearrangement. Herein, we developed an 11-antibody panel that could distinguish distinct immunophenotypic signatures between control and MF specimens. We successfully identified a population of atypical lymphocytes within TCR gene rearrangement-confirmed MF specimens defined by a CD45+, CD4+, CD7− immunophenotype that are significantly enriched compared with control tissues (Murphy et al, 2002). Given that immunophenotyping via MIF-based assays may help predict response to therapies such as immune checkpoint inhibitors, (Lu et al, 2019) this work may serve as a foundation for future efforts in precision medicine. In addition, MIF holds significant potential for immunophenotyping for research purposes within the field of cutaneous T-cell lymphoma.

Although these data support the use of MIF in the diagnosis of cutaneous lymphomas, we greatly appreciate the limitations of this current antibody panel given the breadth of lymphoproliferative disorders and associated antigenic variation. We acknowledge that MF cases exhibiting atypical CD4+, CD7− T-lymphocytes represent a fraction of underlying subtypes, and alternative immunophenotypes for CTCL can include monoclonal T-lymphocytes that are CD4+, CD7+, CD8+, or TCRγ/δ, which based on this current assay would not be appropriately captured. This can be addressed in future studies by implementing additional antigens within or panel including but not limited to CD56, βF1, TCRγ/δ, PD1, ALK, or EMA (Stoll et al, 2021) to characterize aberrant T-cell neoplasms.

We also acknowledge that there are several technical limitations to our MIF protocol in its current form that restrict widespread adoption into clinical pathology and laboratory medicine. One practical limitation can be determining an effective size and scaling of the assay based on the required number of antibodies and staining rounds. In this assay, we completed a total of 4 imaging rounds to allow for staining with 11 antibodies. When including the lead time of antigen retrieval, primary antibody staining, image acquisition, and photoinactivation steps, the total estimated time to complete this assay on 18 samples was 12 hours for staining, with a subsequent 4 hours for image analysis using QuPath and Steinbock workflows in parallel. This timeframe can vary greatly based on the required timeframe of primary antibody incubation, resolution and exposure time for fluorescent microscope image acquisition, specimen size, and image cytometry expertise.

Perhaps one of the greatest technical hurdles is the access to capital equipment, including a multi-channel fluorescent microscope and camera for image acquisition. In addition, workflow standardization with respect to tissue specimen antigen retrieval and primary antibody incubation is a significant technical hurdle to more universal adoption. Considering these hurdles, there is a growing effort not only to validate antibodies from research applications suitable for MIF, but also to detail standardized protocols and reagents that could be implemented into an automated slide staining device workflow (Lin et al, 2018; Lin et al, 2015; Neumann et al, 2022).

An additional limitation within MIF workflows involves photoinactivation treatment after staining rounds and the risk of nonspecific background autofluorescence carrying into subsequent imaging rounds. Within our study, each specimen was visually examined on each fluorescent channel after each photoinactivation step to ensure photobleaching. Although background autofluorescence was able to be manually thresholded out of digital image stacks when visualized through QuPath, we identified the presence of nonspecific and likely background signaling within image stacks analyzed through the Steinbock toolkit. This was particularly noticeable in low-density cellular clusters, including CD8+ T-cells, CD20+ B-cells, and CD30+ T-cells, which were sparse in our initial analyses completed using QuPath, where manual thresholding was performed.

One final limitation includes proficiency in image processing and analysis of digital whole-slide image stacks. Here, we have utilized 2 validated image analysis pipelines, including QuPath for computer-assisted analysis, in addition to Steinbock, which is an automated image segmentation and thresholding analysis workflow that requires technical expertise in bioinformatics and statistical software. There are ongoing efforts to utilize artificial intelligence and develop computer-automated image analysis software to efficiently process and analyze MIF images (Cajigas et al, 2024; Surace et al, 2019; Yaseen et al, 2022). In addition, further statistical models in the form of multidimensional scaling can be implemented to determine whether MIF can discern between distinct cohorts based on general immunophenotype.

In conclusion, our data can serve as an essential framework demonstrating the potential of multiplex imaging in improving the diagnosis of skin cancers and inflammatory skin conditions. This will provide a foundation for the development of, in addition to, disease-specific MIF panels and imaging technologies.

Materials and Methods

Human biospecimen selection

All studies were approved by the University of Connecticut Health Center’s Institutional Review Board (Protocol #23-137-1). Archived formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded biospecimens were identified by retrospective review of pathology reports that included the term “mycosis fungoides.” Biospecimens were selected based on the following criteria: (1) cases with a differential diagnosis of mycosis fungoides; (2) cases that had undergone IHC for diagnostic workup; and (3) cases sent for molecular studies in the form of TCR gene rearrangement. Eighteen biospecimens were selected and sectioned for downstream MIF analyses (Table 1), which included 9 spongiotic dermatitis cases with negative TCR gene clonality categorized as “control” biospecimens and 9 TCR gene clonality positive cases categorized as “MF” biospecimens.

Slide preparation and antigen retrieval

Five μm formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded sections were first deparaffinized and rehydrated with xylene and graded alcohol treatments and subsequently underwent heat-based sodium citrate (pH 6.0) antigen retrieval for 15 minutes at 95 °C. Sections were washed 3 times with phosphate-buffered saline and pre-treated with photobleaching solution (containing 4.5% hydrogen peroxide and 100 mM sodium hydroxide) for 1 hour while exposed to white light to reduce background autofluorescence. (Lin et al, 2018; Brewer et al, 2019; Neumann et al, 2022)

MIF

Slides were permeabilized in 0.1% Triton X for 10 minutes, blocked using 2% bovine serum albumin in PBS for 45 minutes, and stained with fluorescently conjugated primary antibody for 1 hour at room temperature (antibodies and dilutions are listed in Table 2). The slides were subsequently washed in PBS and mounted in a 50% glycerol/PBS solution containing DAPI (1:1000 dilution) and imaged using a Zeiss Axioscan Z1 high-speed automated image acquisition system (Cat#440640-9903-000) and a high-resolution camera (AxioCam HRm) with a 20× objective lens with 10× magnification (200×). Slide visualization was completed using filter cubes for 4 standard fluorescent channels (DAPI/Alexa350, FITC/Alexa488, Texas Red/Alexa594, and CY5/Alexa647). Following image acquisition, slides were submerged in PBS to allow for coverslip removal without compromising tissue integrity and were treated with photobleaching solution again for 60 minutes while being exposed to white light. Each slide was visually inspected in each fluorescent channel after photobleaching to ensure a lack of residual background autofluorescence from the previous staining cycle prior to proceeding. This cyclic process continued for a total of 4 rounds of MIF imaging.

Image stack assembly

Digital whole slide image stacks were uploaded, concatenated, and aligned using the nuclear (DAPI) channel as a common reference marker between subsequent imaging rounds by using the HyperStackReg plugin (Sharma, 2018) within the open-source software ImageJ.

Cell detection, segmentation, and quantification of single cell fluorescence intensity

Concatenated digital image stacks were first uploaded to the open source bioimage analysis software QuPath. (Bankhead et al, 2017) For images obtained by fluorescence microscopy, cell segmentation and detection were performed using the nuclear (DAPI) channel for fluorescence channels and hematoxylin for brightfield images. For all image analyses, all cells within the tissue section were included in the quantification. Quantification of cell number per unit tissue area (mm2) for each individual channel (marker) was identified and employed across all samples using the automated cell detection feature and was normalized based on the biopsy tissue area. Quantification of cells expressing multiple markers was completed using the composite classifier function and was normalized in a similar fashion based on the biopsy tissue area. Comprehensive immunophenotyping of whole-slide images was determined by examining the total number of cells expressing individual surface markers in comparison to the total number of cells within the entire image. Phenotyping of intraepidermal immune populations was generated using a pixel classification modifier by identifying the total number of cells contained within a boarder of cytokeratin-positive cells.

Computer-automated multiplex image processing and analysis

For computer-automated detection and immunophenotyping of all cells across the tissue section, a comprehensive end-to-end multiplex image analysis workflow referred to as the Steinbock toolkit (developed by Windhager et al.) (Windhager et al, 2023) was implemented. The primary input consisted of digital whole-slide concatenated image stacks under 1 image segmentation and pixel classification using a deep learning model incorporated into the workflow. The segmented cells and pixel classification data were further analyzed using both single-cell and spatial analysis toolkits to allow for hierarchical clustering and heat map generation. For computationally derived cellular clusters, manual inspection of immunophenotype data based on fluorescence intensity was completed to classify the underlying cell etiology.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, California, USA) with P-values < .05 considered statistically significant. For all analyses that compared data obtained from control and MF cohorts, an unpaired two-tailed t-test was utilized. All receiver operator characteristic curve analyses and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve were completed using the Wilson/Brown method. In addition, all analyses comparing cell type percentages from computer-automated image processing in Supplementary Figure S5 utilized a Wilcoxon rank sum test. Each n value refers to the number of biospecimens. All data points were included in the data analysis. No samples were excluded. P-values for each statistical comparison are indicated within the figure panels. All human data are included within the main and supplementary figures in this manuscript.

Supplementary Figure S1

Multiplex immunofluorescence displays concordant antigen expression compared with conventional immunohistochemistry. Bar graphs denote the number of normalized CD3, CD4, CD5, CD7, and CD8 cell expressions per unit tissue area between technical replicates of cases when stained in sequential slides by conventional IHC and MIF. Bar graphs denote the mean ± SD (n = 2 technical replicates per sample). IHC, immunohistochemistry; MIF, multiplex immunofluorescence.

Supplementary Figure S2. Normalized MIF-stained myocosis fungoides biopsies exhibited no significant variations in total cells or epithelial cells but did exhibit an increased number of CD4+ T-lymphocytes and atypical CD4+, CD7−T-lymphocytes when compared with controls. Bar graphs of quantified cell numbers per unit tissue area are shown between MIF digital image stack analyses completed by both QuPath and computer-automated Steinbock toolkits. Bar graphs depict comparisons in absolute total cell number, epithelial cells, CD4+ T-lymphocytes, and atypical CD4+, CD7− T-lymphocytes within control (n = 9) and MF (n = 9) image stacks. Each bar graph represents the mean ± SD. MF, mycosis fungoides; MIF, multiplex immunofluorescence.

Supplementary Figure S3. MIF-stained mycosis fungoides biopsies displayed an enhanced intraepidermal proliferative index did not reveal significant variations in intraepidermal CD4:CD8 ratio, CD4+ or atypical CD4+, CD7− T lymphocytes or Ki67+ CD4+ T-lymphocytes when compared with benign controls. Bar graphs of the quantified CD4:CD8 ratio, percentage of CD45+, CD4+, CD5+, percentage of atypical lymphocytes (CD45+, CD4+, CD7−), and proliferative (Ki67+) CD4+ T-lymphocytes within control (n = 9) and MF (n = 9) patient image stacks are shown. Each bar graph represents the mean ± SD. MF, mycosis fungoides; MIF, multiplex immunofluorescence.

Supplementary Figure S4. UMAP of all single segmented cells from combined control and MF MIF image stacks depicting relative spatial localization of antigen expression. MF, mycosis fungoides; MIF, multiplex immunofluorescence.

Supplementary Figure S5. Bar graphs of comprehensive immunophenotyping data obtained from control and MF image stacks using the Steinbock toolkit demonstrating relative percentage of each cell type across the entire number of analyzed cells. Clusters with significant variations between control and MF specimens are identified with P-value. Each bar graph represents the mean ± SD. MF, mycosis fungoides.
