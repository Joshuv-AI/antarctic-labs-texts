Impact of plasma pTau181 levels on clinician diagnostic confidence and management in memory and cognition clinics: A multi‐site before‐and‐after study
Authors: Johannes C Michaelian, Azadeh Feizpour, James C Vickers, Jane E Alty, Jessica M Collins, Vincent Doré, Catriona Ireland, Anna E King, Ralph N Martins, Michael Woodward, Sharon L Naismith, Christopher C Rowe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONRecent advances allow blood tests to detect key proteins linked to Alzheimer's disease (AD).METHODSIn this before‐and‐after study across three Australian memory and cognition clinics, we evaluated the impact on clinicians' diagnostic confidence and management following disclosure of routine patients' AD probability, using predefined plasma phosphorylated tau 181 (pTau181) thresholds set at 90% sensitivity and 90% specificity for amyloid‐beta (Aβ) positron emission tomography (PET) positivity.RESULTSOne hundred thirteen participants (mean age: 71.2 ± 8.4; mean Mini‐Mental State Examination score: 27.7 ± 2.5) with dementia (n = 17, 15.0%), mild cognitive impairment (n = 48, 42.5%), and subjective cognitive decline (n = 48, 42.5%) were enrolled. Blood test results were “probably negative,” n = 81, 71.7%; “indeterminate”, n = 24, 21.2%; and “probably positive,” n = 8, 7.1%. In 12 cases (10.6%), pTau181 changed clinician diagnosis and increased mean diagnostic confidence from low‐to‐moderate (61%) to moderate‐to‐high (80%). Aβ PET in 40 participants showed plasma pTau181 improved diagnostic accuracy by 5%.DISCUSSIONThis study demonstrates the benefits of plasma pTau181 in real‐world clinical practice particularly when diagnostic confidence is only low‐to‐moderate.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, blood‐based biomarkers, dementia, diagnostic change, management change

BACKGROUND

The accurate and timely diagnosis of Alzheimer's disease (AD), and particularly the early prodromal stage referred to as mild cognitive impairment (MCI), remains a significant challenge. 1 While multidisciplinary specialist assessment services for dementia and cognitive decline, often referred to as memory and cognition clinics, are best placed to provide comprehensive assessment, access and availability to these services are limited. 2 , 3 , 4 In Australia, there is considerable heterogeneity in diagnostic services and processes across memory and cognition clinics, with limited harmonization of assessment pathways, restricted access to gold‐standard biomarkers such as positron emission tomography (PET) imaging and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) assays due to cost and availability, and variable capacity for timely diagnosis and post‐diagnostic support. 5 Adding to this, approximately 50% of dementia cases go undetected in primary care, 6 , 7 with average delays of more than 3 years between initial symptom presentation and formal diagnosis. 6 , 7 Moreover, while cognitive and clinical assessments are the foundation for diagnosis, they lack sensitivity in determining the underlying mechanisms of disease, particularly in prodromal stages. 8

In this regard, while the recent advancements in biomarker‐based approaches, including PET imaging and CSF analysis, have facilitated the investigation and quantification of the neuropathological hallmarks of disease before death, namely amyloid‐beta (Aβ) and phosphorylated tau (pTau), these investigations are invasive, costly, and not easily accessible in routine clinical practice. 9 , 10 It is here that recent advancements in blood‐based biomarkers have emerged as a promising tool for detecting underlying AD neuropathology. 11 Indeed, plasma biomarkers targeting pTau have demonstrated strong concordance with in vivo gold‐standard Aβ PET and CSF assays. 12 Plasma pTau181 has been shown to accurately predict Aβ PET positivity with high sensitivity and specificity in a population of older adults undergoing routine assessment, 13 , 14 supporting it as a potential biomarker to improve the diagnostic process. However, despite these advancements, there remain limited real‐world studies evaluating the impact of disclosing routine patients’ AD probability, determined by plasma pTau181 levels, on clinicians' diagnostic confidence and management.

Therefore, in this before‐and‐after study across three Australian memory and cognition clinics, we aimed to evaluate the impact of disclosing a routine patient's AD probability, determined by plasma pTau181 levels, on clinician (1) diagnosis and diagnostic confidence, (2) management decisions and management confidence, and (3) perspectives on enablers and barriers in implementing a blood test for AD.

METHODS

Study design and eligibility criteria

Patients attending three memory and cognition clinics (Austin Health Cognitive Dementia and Memory Service [CDAMS] in Melbourne, Healthy Brain Ageing [HBA] Clinic in Sydney, and ISLAND Clinic in Hobart) for routine assessment were consecutively enrolled in this multi‐center, before‐and‐after study. To facilitate a “real‐world” study, participants were included if they were attending the memory and cognition clinic for an assessment for cognitive concerns or dementia, aged 50 years or older, presented with a Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) score of 23 or greater, and were able to identify a “support person” (e.g., family member, spouse, caregiver) to be involved in the study. Following ascertainment of eligibility and informed consent, participants provided a fasting blood sample and completed self‐report questionnaires (or consent was sought to utilize already collected data) to ascertain mental health well‐being (World Health Organization‐Five [WHO‐5] Well‐Being Index), 15 quality of life (EQ‐5D‐5L,), 16 depressive symptoms (15‐item Geriatric Depression Scale), 17 and disability (WHO Disability Assessment Schedule 2.0). 18

Participant diagnosis and management before and after blood test disclosure

During patient case conference at the memory and cognition clinic, and before disclosing blood test results, clinicians completed a structured questionnaire to document their diagnosis, management plan, and level of confidence. For both HBA and ISLAND clinics, as patient case conference was conducted on the day of assessment, blood test results were not available to be disclosed to study clinicians until 4 to 6 weeks afterward; however, as Austin Health CDAMS conducted case conferences approximately 12 weeks following patient assessment to allow for the collection and reporting of further investigations (i.e., MRI and fluorodeoxyglucose [FDG] PET imaging), clinician diagnosis and management, including levels of confidence, were captured immediately prior to disclosure of blood test results (Supplementary Data File 1). Where Aβ PET imaging results were available for participants enrolled in the study, the Aβ PET results were withheld until following blood test result disclosure and impact assessment. For all sites, following blood test disclosure (see Figure S1 for blood test result examples), the same study clinician completed a structured questionnaire (Supplementary Data File 2) to evaluate whether the blood test result had an impact on their patient's diagnosis and management plan, including their level of confidence.

Study clinician close‐out survey

At the conclusion of the study, all study clinicians were provided an opportunity to complete a close‐out survey to capture the enablers and barriers toward the clinical implementation of a blood test for AD (Supplementary Data File 3).

Study approval and registration

The study was approved by the Human Ethics Review Committee (RPAH Zone) of the Sydney Local Health District (approval/protocol number: X21‐0427). The study was prospectively registered on the Australian and New Zealand Clinical Trial Registry (ACTRN12622000515796, March 31, 2022). All participants provided written informed consent to take part in the study, and all data were obtained in compliance with the Helsinki Declaration.

Plasma pTau181 threshold development and analysis and Aβ PET imaging and brain amyloid quantification for subsample of participants

Detailed methods for plasma pTau181 threshold development and analysis, including assay procedures, validation cohort characteristics, and Aβ PET acquisition and quantification protocols, are provided in Supplementary Data File 4. Briefly, however, plasma pTau181 thresholds were derived against Aβ PET positivity (≥ 35 Centiloids [CL]), with concentrations <24 pg/mL classified as “probably negative,” >34 pg/mL as “probably positive,” and 24 to 34 pg/mL as “indeterminate”.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive analyses (i.e., frequencies, means, standard deviations, correlations) were performed in SPSS version 29.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) to provide an overview of the data. Scatter plots were generated in R Statistical Software (R Version 4.4.2; R Core Team 2024), including concordance analyses that were conducted using McNemar's test and proportion comparison.

RESULTS

Baseline participant and study site characteristics

As illustrated in Figure 1, participant recruitment across three study sites (Austin Health CDAMS, HBA Clinic and ISLAND Clinic) occurred between February and October 2024, with a total of 113 participants enrolled in the study. Moreover, as reported in Table S1, two study sites were considered “hybrid” (i.e., clinics embedded within universities, funded via grants and Medicare) receiving the majority (>90%) of patient referrals from general practitioners (GPs), while one was located in a metropolitan public hospital and received patient referrals from both GPs (45%) and medical specialists (55%). Among the eight clinicians who participated in the study, the majority were geriatricians (n = 7, 87.5%).

Participant recruitment and enrollment flowchart between February and October 2024.

As detailed in Table 1, participants across all study sites were on average 71 years old, with participants enrolled at the HBA Clinic significantly younger (mean age = 67.5 years) and more likely to be currently working (n = 20, 50.0%). Compared to Austin Health CDAMS, a significantly higher proportion of female participants were enrolled at HBA (n = 31, 77.5%) and ISLAND Clinics (n = 21, 63.6%). While clinical diagnoses and classifications varied across sites, the ISLAND Clinic had a significantly higher proportion of dementia cases (n = 9, 27.3%), whereas the HBA Clinic had significantly fewer participants with dementia (n = 2, 5.0%) and a higher proportion with subjective cognitive decline (SCD, n = 32, 80.0%). MMSE scores were consistent across sites (mean = 27.7), as were education levels (mean = 13.7 years) and the proportion of participants who spoke English at home (mean = 90.2%). Notably, nearly two‐thirds of participants (n = 73, 65.2%) were born in Australia. While estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) aligned with the average age of participants across study sites (mean = 76.7), 19 body mass index (BMI) values (mean = 28.4) would be considered overweight/pre‐obese according to current WHO general classification criteria. 20 Furthermore, according to the Index of Relative Socioeconomic Advantage and Disadvantage (IRSAD) calculated for all participants, 21 ISLAND Clinic participants had significantly lower socioeconomic conditions (mean = 4.3) compared to participants enrolled at HBA Clinic (mean = 9.0) and Austin Health CDAMS (mean = 7.6).

Plasma pTau181 levels across clinical classifications

Comparisons of initial clinical classifications/diagnoses across all study sites revealed significantly lower plasma pTau181 levels in participants classified with SCD (mean ± SD, 16.6 ± 15.6 pg/mL) compared to participants with MCI (22.7 ± 20.5 pg/mL, t(73.8) = −3.52, p < 0.001) and dementia (24.6 ± 23.5 pg/mL, t(20.9) = −3.39, p = 0.003). Plasma pTau181 levels did not significantly differ between participants classified with MCI (22.7 ± 20.5 pg/mL) and diagnosed with dementia (24.6 ± 23.5 pg/mL, t(63) = −0.67, p = 0.504).

Changes in diagnostic and management confidence before and after blood test disclosure

Of the 113 participants enrolled in the study, and based on the validation study, most blood test results received were “probably negative” (n = 81, 71.7%: SCD, n = 43 [53.1%]; MCI, n = 29 [35.8%]; dementia, n = 9 [11.1%]), followed by “indeterminate” (n = 24, 21.2%: SCD, n = 5 [20.8%]; MCI, n = 14 [58.4%]; dementia, n = 5 [20.8%]) and “probably positive” (n = 8, 7.1%: SCD, n = 0 [0%]; MCI, n = 5 [62.5%]; dementia, n = 3 [37.5%]). As shown in Table 3, mean diagnostic and management confidence following the initial clinical assessment was moderate‐to‐high across all sites, at 77.2% and 81.1%, respectively, and were strongly positively correlated (Spearman's ρ = 0.81, p < 0.001). In the sample where clinicians reported diagnostic change following disclosure of blood test results (n = 12, 10.6%: “probably negative”, n = 7; “probably positive”, n = 2; “indeterminate”, n = 3), diagnostic confidence increased in these patients from on average low‐to‐moderate (60.8%) to on average moderate‐to‐high (79.6%), a mean increase of 18.8%. These diagnostic changes were predominantly observed among patients assessed at Austin Health CDAMS (n = 10/12, 83.3%), the only tertiary referral memory and cognition clinic in this study, which featured a more clinically complex patient population. All diagnostic changes occurred within the MCI group and reflected refinement of the suspected underlying etiology, most commonly involving shifts toward or away from AD, rather than changes in overarching diagnostic classification. Similarly, in the sample where clinicians reported management change following disclosure of blood test results (n = 8), their management confidence increased in these patients from low‐to‐moderate (66.9%) to moderate‐to‐high (83.9%), a mean increase of 17.0%. In these cases, management changes primarily reflected refinement of clinical decision‐making, including the commencement or cessation of pharmacological interventions (e.g., antidepressants, cholinesterase inhibitors, and dietary supplements such as Souvenaid), referral for Aβ PET imaging, and earlier follow‐up review. In approximately a quarter of participants (n = 30, 26.5%), the blood test results did not change the clinician's diagnosis or management but increased overall mean confidence, with diagnostic confidence rising from 69.0% to 81.5% and management confidence from 72.6% to 80.7% (Table 4).

Aβ PET imaging subsample enrolled at Austin health CDAMS

All Austin Health CDAMS participants (n = 40) underwent Aβ PET imaging, which revealed a low prevalence of AD pathology, 32.5% (Figure 2). Overall, in this subsample, concordance between Aβ PET and a clinical diagnosis suggestive of an underlying AD process before blood test disclosure was assessed at 60% (n = 24), improving to 65% (n = 26) after blood test disclosure. DISCUSSSION

This real‐world study provides novel insights into the clinical impact of disclosing AD probability based on plasma pTau181 levels for routine patients attending a memory and cognition clinic. Our findings demonstrate that among patients who underwent a multidisciplinary assessment and initially recorded a diagnosis with low‐to‐moderate confidence, disclosure of AD probability based on plasma pTau181 levels led to a change in underlying diagnosis for 10% of cases, increasing diagnostic confidence in these cases from low‐to‐moderate to moderate‐to‐high. Similarly, in 7% of cases, disclosure of blood test results led to a change in management, increasing management confidence from low‐to‐moderate to moderate‐to‐high. Importantly, we report that two‐thirds of study clinicians would recommend to their colleagues the clinical use of a blood test for AD in clinical practice with further amendment.

Consistent with previous reports, in this study, plasma pTau181 levels significantly increased across the dementia continuum, from SCD to MCI, as well as between participants with SCD and dementia. 14 , 22 Although plasma pTau181 levels were, on average, higher in participants diagnosed with dementia compared to those with MCI, there was no significant difference likely due to the limited number of participants receiving a diagnosis of dementia enrolled across study sites (n = 17 dementia versus n = 48 MCI). Indeed, in a subsample of participants (n = 40) who underwent Aβ PET imaging, the prevalence of Aβ positivity (≥35 CL) was low at 32.5%. This finding is in contrast to a meta‐analysis of other cohort studies involving routine memory and cognition clinic patients, which reports an average Aβ PET positivity rate exceeding 50% 23 and was unexpectedly lower than the validation cohort, which exhibited an underlying Aβ PET positivity rate of 64%.

When taken together, the overarching diagnostic and management impact results of this study suggest a meaningful role for blood‐based biomarkers in addressing clinical uncertainty, particularly for patients in the prodromal stage of disease, who may be indicated for an amyloid‐targeting monoclonal antibody therapy for AD. 24 While our findings warrant further large‐scale investigation across various memory and cognition clinics, it may be suggested that the clinical implementation of a blood test for AD would enhance diagnostic accuracy in cases with diagnostic uncertainty (i.e., low‐to‐moderate confidence) and facilitate the triaging of eligible patients for a disease‐modifying therapy.

Moreover, the novelty of this study lies in its real‐world application of incorporating plasma pTau181 levels to determine AD probability for routine patients attending a memory and cognition clinic for assessment. This approach addresses a key gap in the literature 25 and has provided valuable insights into clinician perspectives on the enablers and barriers to implementing a blood test for dementia in clinical practice. Examination of these enablers and barriers demonstrated that the blood test report template (Figure S1) enabled the clear reporting and interpretation of results. However, clinician experiences varied – some found the report helpful, while others noted difficulties in interpretation or inconsistencies with patients’ clinical symptoms. Looking to the future, clinicians also raised concerns about the cost and accessibility of such a test in practice, as well as whether existing infrastructure (e.g., pathology collection services) could support its widespread implementation. Therefore, as the field moves ever closer to the clinical implementation of a diagnostic blood test for AD, it is essential to ensure that reporting templates are standardized and that clinicians have access to training and support to facilitate further diagnostic investigation with such blood tests.

This study was not without limitations. At the time of study design and commencement, investigators were constrained in their ability to measure plasma pTau217, which has been shown to demonstrate greater sensitivity, specificity, and overall diagnostic accuracy for AD over plasma pTau181. 26 Future studies should seek to replicate the current study's methodology using pTau217 to better evaluate the impact of disclosing a routine patient's AD probability on clinicians' diagnostic confidence and management. Moreover, while the strength of this study was the enrollment of routine patients across three Australian memory and cognition clinics, two out of three clinics were “hybrid university/research” clinics (i.e., clinics embedded within universities, funded via grants and Medicare). These clinics predominantly receive GP referrals from help‐seeking individuals for recent onset cognitive and/or mood symptoms, and as such participants enrolled across these sites may not be generalizable to routine patients at memory and cognition clinics that are typically referred directly from clinicians in primary or tertiary care, manifesting in this study as a cohort with a lower underlying prevalence of amyloid pathology and, consequently, a reduced proportion of participants classified as “probably positive.” In addition, it is acknowledged that the multi‐site design, while enhancing real‐world applicability, likely introduced some inter‐clinic heterogeneity in patient complexity, comorbidities, and referral pathways, contributing to biological and clinical variability that is inherent in real‐world clinic populations.

Furthermore, the clinics involved in this study employed a multidisciplinary team for assessment, including a consensus diagnosis meeting, which supports a high level of confidence in initial diagnostic and management decisions. Future studies should thus seek to elucidate the impact of such a blood test on clinician diagnostic confidence and management in settings with solo practitioners. Similarly, due to strict regulatory and ethical requirements from both the Australian Therapeutic Goods Administration and the overseeing Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC), the inability to disclose results to participants precluded evaluation of patient‐level outcomes, including the psychological impact of testing and perceptions of a biomarker‐supported diagnosis, which will be important to address in future studies conducted under routine clinical conditions where disclosure is permitted.

Lastly, among the subsample of Austin Health CDAMS participants who underwent Aβ PET imaging revealing a lower than expected Aβ‐positivity prevalence, this finding may suggest a bias in participant selection toward those without typical AD presentations, which is supported by the higher overall proportion of participants with SCD and MCI compared to dementia. Practically, lower disease prevalence improves the negative predictive value while reducing the positive predictive value of diagnostic tests with moderate‐to‐high accuracy such as pTau181. Indeed, this low prevalence effect is consistent with the findings from the subsample of participants undergoing Aβ PET shown in Figure 2. As such, in this study, it may be suggested that plasma pTau181 was more indicative as a diagnostic test to rule out rather than rule in participants with an underlying AD process, particularly as participants with clinical dementia returned an indeterminate result. It is also acknowledged that while a 35‐CL threshold intentionally chosen to align with pathologically meaningful amyloid burden, prioritizing clinical relevance and diagnostic specificity over the detection of early or subthreshold amyloid accumulation, this approach may have resulted in indications that this approach may classify some “early‑positive” individuals as “probably negative” for an underlying AD process according to their plasma pTau181 levels. Despite these limitations, however, management impact benefits were demonstrated. Nonetheless, greater clinical benefit is likely to be seen with plasma pTau217, given its superior sensitivity, specificity, and overall diagnostic accuracy for AD when compared to Aβ PET. 12

In conclusion, this study provides novel real‐world insights into the clinical implementation of a blood test for AD, demonstrating the impact on clinician decision‐making, a key gap that has remained in the literature. Overall, our findings highlight that disclosure of a patient's AD probability based on plasma pTau181 levels can increase clinician confidence in their diagnosis and management in cases with diagnostic uncertainty (i.e., low to‐moderate confidence). Moreover, while two‐thirds of participating clinicians supported the clinical implementation of a blood test for AD, further refinement is needed, particularly around the validation of more sensitive biomarkers such as plasma pTau217. Challenges also remain regarding clinical interpretation, potential costs to the patient, accessibility, and infrastructure readiness. While further large‐scale studies are needed to build on the methodological framework of this real‐world study, our findings mark significant progress toward the clinical implementation of a blood test for AD, paving the way for more accessible and scalable diagnostic approaches in routine care.
