Proteomic and Phosphoproteomic Characteristics of the Retina, Choroid, and Sclera in Guinea Pigs with Form-Deprivation Myopia
Authors: Yifan Song, Zhe Xu, Hong-Tao Li, Yunxiao Xie, Lianghui Zhao, Jiaojiao Feng, Anfeng Luo, Jiajing Dai, Jing Li, Xinran Guo, Jike Song, Hongsheng Bi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMyopia is a growing global public health concern. Recent studies have revealed that the regulation of eye growth occurs via a complex signaling cascade, which originates in the retina and across the choroid to the sclera. Identifying key proteins and specific biological processes (BPs) in the retina, choroid, and sclera is crucial for understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying myopia development. We conducted comprehensive proteomic and phosphoproteomic analyses of the retina, choroid, and sclera from form-deprivation myopia guinea pigs using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry. Differentially expressed proteins and phosphosites were identified, followed by functional annotation and signaling pathway enrichment analyses. The expression of key proteins was assessed using Western blotting and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. Distinct proteomic and phosphoproteomic profiles were observed across the three tissues, with 6470, 6708, and 3236 proteins and 9613, 9416, and 3685 phosphosites in the retina, choroid, and sclera, respectively. Proteomic analysis showed that neural signal transduction was enriched in the retina, with downregulation of NTRK2, suggesting impaired neurotrophic signaling. The upregulation of SYK and BTK, along with increased NF-κB, p65, and IL-1β levels in the choroid, indicated enhanced inflammatory responses. TNNT3, TPM2, and ACTN3 were upregulated in the sclera, reflecting cytoskeletal remodeling associated with scleral expansion. Phosphoproteomic analysis indicated key roles of phosphoproteins in BPs, particularly the spliceosome signaling pathway, which was broadly involved across all three tissues. Kinase network analysis revealed PRPF4B as a key kinase for SF3B1, suggesting the potential regulation roles of RNA splicing in myopia progression. The present study systematically elucidates the proteomic and phosphoproteomic characteristics of the retina, choroid, and sclera of form-deprivation myopia in guinea pigs, highlighting significant tissue-specific BPs to myopia. The findings provide a theoretical foundation for understanding that different tissues exhibit distinct biological reactions to myopia, each through specific signaling pathways and regulatory mechanisms.Keywords: proteomics and phosphoproteomics, form-deprivation myopia, retina, choroid, sclera

Myopia, characterized by excessive eye elongation, causes images of distant objects to focus in front of the retina, causing blurred distance vision (1). The relentless increase in the prevalence of myopia is precipitating a global public health challenge affecting 10 to 30% of adults in many countries and reaching 80 to 90% among young adults in certain regions of East and Southeast Asia (2, 3, 4, 5). However, the underlying pathogenesis and signaling mechanisms remain elusive (6, 7). Recent studies have revealed that visual stimuli control the rate of eye growth through a multilayered and complex signaling cascade. This cascade originates in the retina, continues through the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) and the choroid, and acts on the sclera, causing changes in the scleral extracellular matrix (ECM), which manifests as axial elongation (6, 8, 9). Despite extensive research, the specific molecular mechanisms and interactions among the retina, choroid, and sclera remain unknown and are a focus of current studies (10).

As the first station to sense and encode visual cues, the retina initiates the retina–choroid–sclera signaling cascade and is crucial in the etiology of myopia. The retina is a fine-layered structure with multiple cell types that process intricate visual signals via multiple molecular pathways (11). Researchers have been investigating the role of the retina in regulating myopia for decades, exploring numerous aspects, including neurotransmitters [such as dopamine (12), retinoic acid (13), vasoactive intestinal peptide (14), and melanopsin (15, 16)], noncoding RNAs (17, 18), and cellular functions (19). Notably, cones and rods seem to exert different functions on refractive development as well as on experimental myopia. Previous studies have shown that lack of cone function due to Gnat2 mutation increases susceptibility to form-deprivation myopia (FDM) in mice (20). In addition, Park et al. observed that Gnat1−/− mice, which lack functional rods, did not respond to form deprivation at any age (21). The choroid is a highly vascularized layer between the retina and the sclera. Studies have demonstrated that the choroid participates in emmetropisation by modulating its thickness to adjust the retina to the focal plane of the eye (choroidal accommodation) and by releasing growth factors that can regulate scleral ECM remodeling (22). Gene expression profiling performed in a myopia marmoset model has implicated several RPE/choroidal signaling pathways in refractive eye development, including those of toll-like receptor, p38 MAPK, and hypoxia (23). The sclera is a dense, viscoelastic connective tissue forming the outer protective layer of the eye and determining eye shape and size across species. Molecular studies have revealed that restructuring of the sclera during refractive eye development occurs primarily through alterations of the production of vital proteins, such as type I collagen, CAPS2, and crystallins. Additionally, this process involves proteins related to cell adhesion, cytoskeletal organization, transcriptional regulation, and ECM remodeling (24). ECM remodeling in the sclera occurs downstream in myopia, resulting in a decline in scleral strength, scleral thinning, and axial elongation (25). A study revealed hypoxia signaling as a critical factor in the remodeling of the sclera associated with myopia (26).

Therefore, multiple factors and signaling pathways related to the development of the retina, choroid, and sclera influence myopia. Most studies on the molecular mechanisms of myopia are limited to individual signaling pathways, failing to provide a global perspective of the changes in the retina, choroid, and sclera in FDM. The mechanisms via which signals originating from the retina traverse the vascular-rich choroid to affect the sclera remain unclear (6).

With advancements in systems biology, "omics" applications enable the revealing of the underlying mechanisms of the pathogenesis of myopia (27). In addition, proteomic analysis enables the assessment of simultaneous alterations in numerous proteins or post-translational modification (PTM), facilitating comprehensive molecular investigation during physiological or pathophysiological processes. PTM refers to the modification of protein properties and functions through the addition or removal of functional groups (28). The application of proteomics in myopia studies has been valuable in uncovering the molecular underpinnings of myopic progression and response to therapeutic interventions. Bian et al. conducted a comprehensive proteomic analysis of a lens-induced myopia model in guinea pigs and underscored the significance of lipid metabolism in early myopic changes (29). In addition, Moon et al. applied retinal region proteomic studies and suggested that oxidative stress may be the primary driver of myopia progression, with the far-peripheral retina being a crucial responder (30). Lin et al. used proteomics to demonstrate that enhanced scleral glycolysis facilitates myopia through histone lactylation (7).

Phosphorylation is a prevalent and crucial PTM in eukaryotes owing to its extensive involvement in signal transduction, providing organisms with a rapid and flexible means of responding to physiological or pathological changes. This process swiftly regulates protein structure and function by opposing the actions of protein kinases and phosphatases (31). Previous reports have suggested that 30 to 75% of proteins are phosphorylated primarily at the serine, threonine, and tyrosine residues (32, 33). Dysregulation of phosphorylation-mediated cell signaling has been observed in numerous diseases (34). Liu et al. revealed the proteomic and phosphoproteomic characteristics of the cortex, hippocampus, thalamus, lungs, and kidneys of female K18-hACE2 mice infected with COVID-19 (35). Onken et al. identified an oncogenic Gq/11-driven protein kinase signaling network in uveal melanoma through phosphorylated proteomics and bioinformatics analysis (36). However, to date, reports providing comprehensive proteomic and phosphoproteomic data from the myopic retina, choroid, or sclera are lacking. The aim of the present study was to combine proteomics and phosphoproteomics in the analysis of the retina, choroid, and sclera of FDM in guinea pigs.

Experimental Procedures

Experimental Design and Statistical Rationale

This study aimed to comprehensively characterize the proteomic and phosphoproteomic alterations in the retina, choroid, and sclera of guinea pigs following induction of FDM, compared to normal control (NC) animals. Thirty-six healthy male tricolored guinea pigs (2-week-old, 110–130 g) were randomly assigned to either the FDM group (n = 18) or the NC group (n = 18). For subsequent liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analyses, tissues from a subset of these animals were used, with three biological replicates per group for each tissue (retina, choroid, and sclera) subjected to proteomic and phosphoproteomic profiling. Sample processing for LC-MS/MS was conducted by randomizing the order of samples which were feasible to minimize potential batch effects. No external retention time standards or spiked protein/peptide standards were employed for calibration beyond the standard internal instrument calibrations. Data-independent acquisition (DIA) using the parallel accumulation serial fragmentation (PASEF) mode was employed, which inherently includes MS1 survey scans prior to MS/MS fragmentation. The m/z range for MS1 was 100 to 1700. For MS/MS, the DIA-PASEF method utilized an isolation window of 25 m/z across a precursor range of 400 to 1200 m/z, with 10 PASEF MS/MS scans acquired per full MS scan cycle. A spectral library was generated from the DIA data of all analyzed samples using the Spectronaut software's Pulsar search engine against the Cavia porcellus FASTA database, rather than using a pre-existing or data-dependent acquisition-generated library. Statistical significance for differential protein and phosphosite expression was determined using Student's t-tests on log2-transformed relative abundance values. Functional enrichment analyses utilized Fisher's exact test. Biometric and validation data, including Western blotting and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), were analyzed using analysis of variance. These statistical methods were chosen for their appropriateness in pairwise omics comparisons, categorical enrichment analysis, and multigroup experimental data, respectively.

Induction of FDM Model in Guinea Pigs

Male tricolored guinea pigs (C. porcellus, 110–130 g, 2-week-old) were obtained from Jinan Jinfeng Laboratory Animal Company. Guinea pigs were subjected to an alternating 12-h light/12-h dark cycle and had free access to food and water throughout the experiment. Guinea pigs with congenital myopia or cataracts were excluded from the study. In the NC group, both eyes of the guinea pigs were exposed, without interventions. In the FDM group, the right eyes of guinea pigs were covered by a translucent occluder.

All animal experiments in the present study were approved by the Animal Care and Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Eye Hospital of Shandong University of Traditional Chinese Medicine, Jinan, China. All procedures were performed according to the guidelines outlined by the Association for Research in Vision and the Ophthalmology Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research.

Measurement of Ocular Biometrics and Refractive Status

All animals in the present study were subjected to biometric measurements, specifically assessing axial length (AL) and refraction at 0 and 4 weeks. AL was measured using A-scan ultrasonography (Cinescan; Quantel Medical), and the parameters were adjusted for suitability for guinea pigs. The AL of each eye was measured 10 times, and the average values were calculated. For pupil dilation and cycloplegia, 1% ophthalmic grade tropicamide was administered topically to the conjunctival sac of guinea pigs at 5-min intervals, repeated thrice. An infrared photorefractor (Steinbeis Transfer Center) was used to measure the refractive state (37). The guinea pigs were positioned on a pedestal with their eyes facing the photorefractor, which was adjusted to obtain a clear focused image of the eye (38). The average of the three measurements was used as the final refraction. Only guinea pigs with anisometropia <2.00 D were included in further study.

Collection of Retina, Choroid, and Sclera

After 4 weeks of form deprivation, the guinea pig eyeballs were analyzed further. Excess tissue outside the sclera was removed carefully to ensure a smooth surface. Subsequently, the eyeball was cut along the corneoscleral edge, and the anterior segments comprising the cornea, iris, and lens were extracted. Subsequently, the vitreous was eliminated, and the posterior segment was rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (39).

To separate the retina, choroid, and sclera, the folded posterior ocular wall was subjected to successive extrusions using an intraocular scraper. All three samples were stored rapidly in liquid nitrogen until further use. However, the RPE and choroid cannot be separated for technical reasons and are usually combined in studies (39, 40, 41). Therefore, RPE-choroid complex samples were used as a whole, subsequently referred to as the choroid. This approach is common but represents a potential limitation in distinguishing RPE-specific changes from choroid-specific changes.

Sample Preparation and LC-MS/MS Analyses

Protein Extraction

The sample was sonicated on ice for 3 minutes using a high-intensity ultrasonic processor (Scientz) in 300 μl lysis buffer (8 M urea, 1% protease inhibitor cocktail III [Merck Millipore, Cat No. 539134], 1% protease inhibitor cocktail V [Merck Millipore, Cat No. 539137], and 1% phosphatase inhibitor cocktail [Selleck, Cat No. B15001]). The remaining debris was removed by centrifugation at 12,000 g at 4 °C for 10 min. Finally, the supernatant was collected, and the protein concentration was determined with BCA kit (Beyotime, Cat No. P0011) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Trypsin Digestion

An equal amount of protein (1 mg per sample) was taken for enzymatic digestion. Proteins were precipitated by the slow addition of trichloroacetic acid (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat No. T4885-2 KG) to a final concentration of 20% (m/v), vortexed, and incubated for 2 h at 4 °C. The precipitate was collected by centrifugation at 4500g for 5 min at 4 °C, washed three times with precooled acetone (Hangzhou Hannuo Chemical), and briefly dried. The protein pellet was redissolved in 200 mM triethylammonium bicarbonate buffer (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat No. T7408–500 ml) and ultrasonically dispersed. Trypsin (Promega, Cat No. V5117) was added at a 1:50 trypsin-to-protein mass ratio for the first digestion overnight. The sample was then reduced with 5 mM DL-dithiothreitol (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat No. D9163–25 G) for 60 min at 37 °C and alkylated with 11 mM iodoacetamide (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat No. V900335-5 G) for 45 min at room temperature in darkness. A second digestion was performed for 4 h at a trypsin:protein mass ratio of 1:100. Peptides were then acidified with 10% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA, Sigma-Aldrich, Cat No. 302031-1L) to pH 2 to 3 and centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min. The supernatant was desalted using a Strata X SPE column (Phenomenex, Cat No. 8B-S100-AAK), which was first activated with methanol (ThermoFisher Scientific, Cat No. A452–4) and equilibrated with 0.1% TFA. Peptides were eluted with 80% acetonitrile (ACN, ThermoFisher Scientific, Cat No. 204433) in 0.1% TFA. Peptide concentration was determined using a BCA kit.

Phosphopeptide Enrichment

For phosphoproteomic analysis, desalted peptides were dissolved in enrichment buffer (50% ACN/0.5% acetic acid [Sigma-Aldrich, Cat No. 695092–100 Ml]) and transferred to prewashed immobilized metal affinity chromatography microspheres (ThermoFisher Scientific, Cat No. A32992) for incubation with shaking. The immobilized metal affinity chromatography microspheres were then washed sequentially with 50% ACN/0.5% acetic acid and 30% ACN/0.1% TFA to remove nonspecifically adsorbed peptides. Phosphopeptides were eluted with 10% ammonia solution (NH4OH, Sigma-Aldrich, Cat No. 338818) with vibration. The eluted samples were collected, lyophilized, and desalted using C18 ZipTips (Millipore, Cat No. ZTC18M096) according to the manufacturer’s protocol and vacuum freeze-dried for LC-MS/MS analysis.

LC-MS/MS Analysis

The tryptic peptides were dissolved in solvent A, directly loaded onto a home-made reversed-phase analytical column (25 cm length, 100 μm i.d.). The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (0.1% formic acid [Fluka, Cat No. A117–50], 2% ACN/in water) and solvent B (0.1% formic acid, 90% ACN/in water). Peptides were separated on an Easy-nLC 1000 UHPLC system (Bruker) at a constant flow rate of 450 nl/min using the following gradients: For proteomic analysis: 0 to 8 min, 9%-24% B; 8 to 12 min, 24%-35% B; 12 to 16 min, 35%-90% B; and 16 to 20 min, 90% B and for phosphoproteomic analysis: 0 to 18 min, 2%-22% B; 18 to 22 min, 22%-35% B; 22 to 26 min, 35%-90% B; and 26 to 30 min, 90% B. The peptides were subjected to a capillary source followed by a timsTOF Pro mass spectrometer (Bruker). The electrospray voltage applied was 1.75 kV for proteomic analysis and 1.70 kV for phosphoproteomic analysis. Precursors and fragments were analyzed at the TOF detector. The timsTOF Pro was operated in DIA-PASEF mode. The full MS scan range was set to 100 to 1700 m/z, and 10 PASEF-MS/MS scans were acquired per cycle. The MS/MS scan range was 400 to 1200 m/z, and the isolation window was 25 m/z. The total cycle time for one full MS scan and 10 PASEF MS/MS scans was approximately 1.8 s.

Bioinformatic Analysis

DIA Data Processing and Library Generation

The DIA data derived from this study were processed using Spectronaut (v.17, Biognosys AG, Schlieren, Switzerland) software, which utilizes an embedded Pulsar search engine. A project-specific spectral library was generated directly from the DIA runs of all analyzed samples. Tandem mass spectra were searched against the UniProt C. porcellus database (identifier: Cavia_porcellus_10141_PR_20230529.fasta, containing 25,052 sequences) and concatenated with a reversed decoy database to estimate false discovery rates (FDR). For library generation, default Spectronaut parameters were used, including precursor and fragment mass tolerances optimized by the software based on the data characteristics. Peptides were identified with a PSM, peptide, and protein group FDR of <0.01. Ion mobility data from the timsTOF Pro was utilized by Spectronaut for alignment and scoring. Decoy entries were generated by Spectronaut using the pseudo-reverse method.

Peptide Identification and Quantification from DIA Data

For proteomic data (DA), Trypsin/P was specified as the cleavage enzyme, allowing up to two missed cleavages. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine was set as a fixed modification, while oxidation of methionine and acetylation of protein N termini were set as variable modifications. For phosphoproteomic data (DPST), in addition to the above, phosphorylation of serine, threonine, and tyrosine was included as a variable modification. Spectronaut's algorithms were used for matching experimental DIA data to the generated spectral library, employing dynamic retention time and ion mobility alignment with narrow tolerances. Peak selection for quantification was based on the software's default settings, typically using the top N most intense fragments. Phosphorylation site localization was determined by Spectronaut, which provides a localization probability score for each identified phosphosite; sites with a probability >0.75 were considered confidently localized. The FDR for peptide, protein, and PSM identification was adjusted to <0.01.

Quantification and Differential Expression Analysis

Protein quantification for the proteome was based on the sum of intensities of unique and razor peptides, as determined by Spectronaut. These intensities were transformed into relative quantitative values (R) after centralization (Rij = Iij/Mean(Ij), where i is the sample and j is the protein). For phosphosite quantification, the relative quantitative values of modified peptides were calculated similarly and then normalized by dividing by the relative quantitative value of the corresponding protein to account for changes in protein expression levels. Outlier rejection or minimum intensity thresholds were applied as per Spectronaut's default quality control settings. Protein isoforms within a group were handled by Spectronaut's protein grouping algorithm, typically reporting quantification based on the leading protein. Fold change (FC) was calculated as the ratio of the mean relative quantitative values for each protein (k) between the groups: FC = Mean (Rik, i ∈ FDM)/Mean (Rik, i ∈ NC).

To determine the significance of differences, the relative quantitative values of proteins or phosphosites across the two sample groups (FDM versus NC) were analyzed using a Student’s t test on log2-transformed values. Differential proteins and phosphosites were screened using a threshold of a FC > 1.3 for significant upregulation and FC < 1/1.3 (approximately 0.769) for significant downregulation, with a p-value <0.05.

Functional Enrichment and Network Analysis

Functional enrichment analysis for Gene Ontology (GO) terms (biological process [BP], molecular function [MF], and cellular component [CC]) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways was conducted using Fisher's exact test. The identified proteins or phosphosites in each dataset served as the background. Functional terms with a fold enrichment >1.5 and a p-value <0.05 were considered statistically significant. After removing redundancies, the top 20 enriched items in each tissue were chosen and visually represented as bubble maps. To identify interaction networks of differentially expressed proteins (DEPs), database numbers or protein sequences were matched with the STRING protein interaction network database (version 11.5 or later), extracting differential protein interaction relationships with a confidence score >0.7. The interaction network was visualized using the "visNetwork" R package. Hub proteins within these networks were identified using the CytoHubba plugin in Cytoscape (version 3.8 or later). Lastly, the GPS5.0 software was used to predict kinase-substrate regulations, where the regulatory state of a kinase reflects changes in phosphorylation levels at its substrate sites.

Western Blotting

Western blotting was used to compare the protein levels of NTRK2, GNGT2, Rho, pan phospho-threonine, and SF3B1 in the retina; SYK, BTK, NF-κB p65, pan phospho-threonine, and SF3B1 in the choroid; and ACTN3, PGK1, TPM2, TNNT3, pan phospho-threonine, and SF3B1 in the sclera between FDM and NC groups. The retina, choroid, and sclera of guinea pigs in each group were homogenized in RIPA buffer supplemented with PMSF (G2008, Servicebio). The tissues were then ground thoroughly at 4 °C and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatants were collected, and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit. Proteins were separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) and transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane. After incubating with the primary antibodies against NTRK2 (1:1000; ABclonal Technology), GNGT2 (1:1000; ABclonal Technology), Rho (1:1000; Affinity Biosciences), SYK (1:1000; ABclonal Technology), BTK (1:1000; ABclonal Technology), NF-κB p65 (1:1000; ABclonal Technology), ACTN3 (1:1000; ABclonal Technology), PGK1 (1:1000; ABclonal Technology), TPM2 (1:1000; ABclonal Technology), TNNT3 (1:1000; Affinity Biosciences), pan phospho-threonine (1:1000; ABclonal Technology), and SF3B1 (1:1000; SANTA) overnight at 4 °C, the polyvinylidene difluoride membranes were washed with TBST three times and then incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h. Images were captured using a chemiluminescence imaging system and quantified with AIWBwell (Servicebio).

ELISA Quantitation

IL-1β levels in the choroid were quantified using an ELISA. Tissues were ground in liquid nitrogen for 1 to 2 min, and enhanced RIPA lysis buffer (with PMSF) was added at a 1:10 (mg: μL) weight to volume ratio. The tissue solutions were centrifuged at 12,000 g min−1 for 8 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were collected, and the level of bioactive IL-1β was determined using a Rat IL-1β ELISA kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses for biometric data, Western blotting, and ELISA were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 29 (IBM Corp., GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software Inc.) was used for graph plotting. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Differences between groups were assessed using analysis of variance, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant, and p < 0.01 considered highly significant. For proteomic and phosphoproteomic data, statistical analyses are detailed in the Bioinformatic analysis section.

Results

FDM and Ocular Tissue Collection

In the present study, an FDM animal model was established (Fig. 1A). Before the form deprivation treatment, there were no significant differences in the AL or refraction between the groups (p > 0.05) (Fig. 1, B–C). After 4 weeks of form deprivation treatment, the AL in the FDM group (8.33 ± 0.08 mm) was significantly longer than that in the NC group (8.16 ± 0.12 mm, p < 0.01) (Fig. 1B). Additionally, the refraction in the FDM group (−3.00 ± 1.27 D) was significantly more myopic than that in the NC group (+0.55 ± 0.76 D, p < 0.01) (Fig. 1C).

Global Proteomic and Phosphoproteomic Characterization of the Retina, Choroid, and Sclera

In the present study, comprehensive proteomic and phosphoproteomic analyses were performed based on LC-MS/MS (Fig. 2A). Principal component analysis of quantifiable proteins (Fig. 2B) and phosphosites (Fig. 2C) confirmed that holistic molecular alterations were induced by FDM in the retina, choroid, and sclera.

The numbers of quantifiable proteins in the proteome of the retina, choroid, and sclera were 6470 (87 upregulated and 121 downregulated), 6708 (219 upregulated and 118 downregulated), and 3236 (138 upregulated and 181 downregulated), respectively (Fig. 2D); the numbers of quantifiable phosphosites and phosphoproteins in the phosphoproteome were 9613 (557 upregulated and 566 downregulated) sites in 2676 proteins, 9416 (428 upregulated and 652 downregulated) sites in 2618 proteins, and 3685 (208 upregulated and 177 downregulated) sites in 1014 proteins (Fig. 2E), respectively. The sclera had the lowest number of proteins identified in the proteomic analysis primarily because of the high abundance of certain proteins and the vast dynamic range of the expressed proteome.

Nine-quadrant analysis was performed to visualize the correlation between proteomic and phosphoproteomic changes within each tissue. This analysis revealed a generally limited direct correlation between changes in protein expression and alterations in phosphorylation levels for the same proteins across all three tissues. For instance, in the retina, a substantial number of proteins (1,277) exhibited increased phosphorylation without a corresponding change in their overall protein expression levels (unchanged protein—upregulated phosphorylation category), whereas only 18 proteins showed concurrent upregulation in both protein abundance and phosphorylation levels (upregulated protein—upregulated phosphorylation category) (Supplemental Fig. S1). Similar patterns, where phosphorylation changes occurred largely independently of protein abundance alterations, were observed in the choroid (1022 proteins in unchanged protein—upregulated phosphorylation versus 43 in upregulated Protein—upregulated phosphorylation) and the sclera (713 proteins in unchanged protein—upregulated phosphorylation versus 50 in upregulated protein—upregulated phosphorylation) (Supplemental Fig. S2 and S3). Conversely, a significant number of proteins demonstrated decreased phosphorylation without changes in overall protein levels (1295 proteins in unchanged protein—downregulated phosphorylation category in the retina) (Supplemental Fig. S1). These observations underscore that post-translational modification by phosphorylation constitutes a distinct regulatory layer that is often, though not always, decoupled from direct changes in protein expression in response to FDM.

Venn diagrams were used to illustrate the overlap and uniqueness of proteins and phosphosites in the retina, choroid, and sclera, revealing five proteins (CRYAA, ENPP6, KRT72, KRT75, and MYL1) and five phosphosites (EIF2S2_S2, TFRC_S34, SLC20A2_T347, Cisd1_S43, and CNP_S169) shared among the three ocular tissues. Furthermore, each tissue exhibited a distinct repertoire of proteins and phosphosites (Fig. 2, F–J), and there was a low correlation between the proteomic and phosphoproteomic profiles as further evidenced by the nine-quadrant plots. The results suggest that the biological responses induced by FDM differ among ocular tissues and that the changes in protein expression and phosphorylation may play distinct roles in mediating the response to form deprivation.

Functional Heterogeneity Observed in the Retina, Choroid, and Sclera Based on Proteomics

To explore regulation network changes, GO analysis of DEPs was performed, including BP, MF, and CC in the retina, choroid, and sclera of myopic guinea pigs. In the retina, the main altered BP were related to neuron activity, including changes in cell projection morphogenesis, neuron projection development, sensory perception of light stimulus, photoreceptor cell development, and visual perception. The findings indicate that form deprivation disrupted retinal photoreception and mediated an imbalance in retinal neural signaling (Fig. 3A). To obtain more detailed molecular insights, protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis was conducted. The NTRK2-centered protein cluster was identified, which regulates neuronal survival, proliferation, migration, differentiation, synapse formation, and plasticity. The decreased NTRK2 expression may be a key factor leading to neural transmission disorders (Fig. 3B). The major MF enriched were ion channels and receptor activities, including G protein-coupled glutamate receptor activity, ion-gated channel activity, gated channel activity, and passive transmembrane transporter activity, further supporting impaired neural signal transduction (Supplemental Fig. S4A). According to CC analysis results, DEPs were enriched mainly at the cytosolic ribosome, endocytic vesicle lumen, and internode region of the axon (Supplemental Fig. S4B).

E, biological process (sclera). F, PPI network (sclera). DEP, differentially expressed protein; GO, Gene Ontology; PPI, protein–protein interaction.

In the choroid, BP analysis in the FDM versus NC group showed that most of the DEPs were involved in inflammatory responses, including regulation of neutrophil activation, chronic inflammatory response, regulation of interleukin-10/4 production, B cell-mediated immunity, regulation of mononuclear cell/lymphocyte proliferation, and similar BPs (Fig. 3C). The PPI results revealed an immune-related protein cluster centered around SYK. We observed that SYK and BTK, which are nonreceptor cytoplasmic tyrosine kinases expressed primarily in hematopoietic lineage cells, are involved in multiple pathways associated with the inflammatory response. Both are key mediators that couple activated immunoreceptors to downstream signaling events, influencing a range of biological functions from cellular proliferation, differentiation, and adhesion to innate and adaptive immune responses. This suggests that they may play significant roles in responses to choroidal inflammation (Fig. 3D). In terms of MF, choroidal DEPs were involved in metabolism-related functions, such as arachidonic acid binding, acyl-CoA hydrolase activity, long-chain fatty acid binding, and CoA hydrolase activity (Supplemental Fig. S4C). CC analysis indicated that DEPs were localized mainly in the nucleus and intracellular anatomical structure (Supplemental Fig. S4D).

In the sclera, BP enrichment analysis showed that most DEPs were largely associated with cell mechanics and muscle contraction, such as muscle filament sliding, actin–myosin filament sliding, striated muscle contraction, and regulation of muscle system process. Additionally, several DEPs were enriched in gluconeogenesis processes (Fig. 3E). In addition, PPI analysis identified cytoskeleton-associated protein clusters centered on ACTN3, TNNT3, and TPM2, as well as glycolysis-related clusters centered on PGK1. PGK1 was involved in the striated muscle cell differentiation pathway, suggesting that glycolytic proteins may also be involved in muscle activity (Fig. 3F). The MF and CC analysis results were highly consistent with the BP results. MF enrichment analysis revealed that numerous DEPs were associated with structural molecule activity, actin binding, structural constituent of muscle, and troponin T binding (Fig. S4E). According to CC analysis, ACTN3, TNNT3, and TPM2 were enriched significantly in the sarcomere, contractile fiber, myofibril, and supramolecular fiber, with the highest number of proteins found in the cytoskeleton (Fig. S4F).

To further understand the impact of DEPs on signaling pathways in the retina, choroid, and sclera of FDM, KEGG enrichment analysis was performed (Fig. 4, A–F). In the retina, the neuroactive ligand–receptor interaction pathway exhibited the most significant changes (p < 0.02), with five DEPs (Fig. 4, A–B). In the choroid, multiple pathways related to inflammation and metabolic responses were enriched significantly, including the IL-17 signaling pathway, NF-kappa B signaling pathway, and arginine and proline metabolism (Fig. 4, C–D). In the sclera, pathways such as glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, cardiac muscle contraction, and calcium signaling pathway were enriched significantly (Fig. 4, E–F). The findings are similar to the results of the GO analysis, indicating that the altered protein expression in each tissue plays a distinct role in response to FDM.

Molecular Biology Findings in the Retina, Choroid, and Sclera

Western blotting and ELISA analyses were performed on characteristic proteins from the three tissues. In the retina, significant downregulation of NTRK2 was observed in the FDM group (p < 0.01), indicating impaired neurotrophic signaling. Additionally, we examined the expression of GNGT2 and Rho, which are expressed specifically in cone and rod cells. In the FDM group, GNGT2 was downregulated, and Rho was upregulated (p < 0.001, Fig. 5, A–B). In the choroid, SYK and BTK proteins were upregulated significantly in the FDM group (p < 0.05), indicating activation of immune signaling pathways in response to form deprivation. Moreover, NF-κB p65 expression was increased significantly (p < 0.001), suggesting activation of inflammatory pathways (Fig. 5, C–D). The level of the proinflammatory cytokine IL-1β was elevated significantly in the FDM group based on ELISA (p < 0.001), potentially amplifying local inflammatory responses in the choroid. In the sclera, proteomics-related hub proteins were also validated, with significant upregulation of cytoskeleton-related proteins TNNT3, TPM2, and ACTN3 in the FDM group (p < 0.01), indicating enhanced cytoskeletal remodeling, which may be related to scleral expansion and myopia development. Furthermore, the downregulation of PGK1 was consistent with the proteomic results, which may influence cellular metabolism and affect scleral tissue remodeling (p < 0.01, Fig. 5, E–F).

Biological Process Variability Revealed in the Retina, Choroid, and Sclera Based on Phosphoproteomics

Phosphoproteomic changes were analyzed to further explore insights at the phosphorylation level. Phosphate groups serve as energy signals, activating or inhibiting protein function. Here, phosphoproteins were categorized, and the biological processes in which upregulated or downregulated phosphorylation was involved were examined in three tissues of guinea pigs with FDM.

In the retina, upregulated phosphoproteins were associated primarily with protein localization, positive regulation of spindle assembly, and positive regulation of cell cycle. Among the proteins related to protein localization, the upregulated phosphorylation of RIC8A and TJP1 may alter synaptic signaling and intercellular connections in neural cells. Additionally, the key proteins involved in cell cycle regulation, such as DYNC1H1 and NUMA1, exhibited upregulated phosphorylation at multiple sites, particularly in processes like positive regulation of spindle assembly and positive regulation of cell cycle. The alterations in their phosphorylation may mediate cellular division and proliferation activities in the retina, potentially serving as an adaptive response to neural cell death induced by form deprivation (Fig. 6, A–B). Among downregulated phosphoproteins, CTR9 is associated with histone H3-K79 methylation, which implies epigenetic alterations in the retina during myopia progression. Moreover, MECP2, which is expressed exclusively in neurons, exhibited reduced phosphorylation in the negative regulation of gene silencing by miRNA, possibly suggesting diminished miRNA-mediated gene silencing, which could affect gene expression in retinal neuron development (Fig. 7, A–B). Notably, both upregulated and downregulated phosphoproteins were enriched significantly in the biological processes related to RNA splicing, mRNA processing, and mRNA metabolic process. The phosphorylation of several key RNA splicing proteins changed significantly, including the increased phosphorylation of SRRM2 and HNRNPA0 and decreased phosphorylation of SRSF7 and TRA2B (Fig. 6, A–B and Fig. 7, A–B), suggesting the roles of RNA splicing in myopia progression.

In the choroid, upregulated phosphoproteins mainly showed activation of metabolism-related pathways. Key phosphoproteins related to amino acid transport and carbohydrate metabolism, such as SLC1A4 (involved in L-alanine transport) and SLC38A1 (involved in glutamine transport), were upregulated in the choroid, suggesting increased energy metabolism and nutrient transport demands under form treatment, possibly as a compensatory response to myopic choroidal atrophy. Additionally, the phosphorylation of the glycolytic enzyme Eno1 at two sites (Eno1_S40 and Eno1_S79) was upregulated in the glucose catabolic process to pyruvate, further supporting metabolic remodeling in the choroid (Fig. 6, C–D). Downregulated phosphoproteins mainly involved in cytoskeletal function and repair, such as MAP1A and MAP1B, which showed decreased phosphorylation at multiple sites in the regulation of microtubule depolymerization, indicated reduced microtubule dynamics in the choroid and highlighted cell atrophy and death. Furthermore, downregulated phosphoproteins involved in the cellular response to interferon-gamma, such as CAMK2G, EPRS1, IFGGD1, and PML, indicated suppression of immune response activity within the choroid. Interferon-γ, an essential immunomodulatory factor, plays a critical role in antiviral and immune regulation, and its downregulation may impair the ability of choroid to effectively control local immune responses in inflammatory environments, exacerbating tissue damage (Fig. 7, C–D).

In the sclera, phosphoproteins were upregulated significantly, especially in the pathways related to cell cycle and cytoskeletal regulation. Key phosphoproteins, such as RGCC and NUMA1, were upregulated in positive regulation of mitotic nuclear division and cell cycle G1/S phase transition, indicating increased proliferation and division activity in scleral fibroblasts. These phosphorylation changes may reflect structural remodeling and expansion of the sclera, driving myopia progression. Additionally, multiple sites of phosphoproteins like FLNA were upregulated, indicating significant cytoskeletal remodeling in the sclera under FDM, particularly in processes such as cytoskeleton organization and spindle assembly (Fig. 6, E–F). Among downregulated phosphorylation pathways in the sclera, proteins involved in cytoskeletal dynamics regulation, such as MAP1A and MAP1B, showed decreased phosphorylation in negative regulation of cytoskeleton organization and negative regulation of protein depolymerization, suggesting suppressed negative regulation of cytoskeletal remodeling, further supporting extensive cytoskeletal reorganization in scleral tissue. Moreover, the phosphorylation of IRS1, a key protein involved in the regulation of fatty acid beta-oxidation, was downregulated, implying dysregulation of fatty acid β-oxidation in the sclera, which may further induce glycolysis under hypoxic conditions (Fig. 7, E–F).

Spliceosome Signaling Pathway Involved Extensively in the Retina, Choroid, and Sclera

To further investigate the role of phosphorylation in myopia, the commonly regulated KEGG signaling pathways were analyzed based on phosphoproteomic data in the three tissues. Multiple differential phosphoproteins were enriched significantly in the spliceosomal pathway, especially SF3B1, ACIN1, HNRNPA3, and TRA2A, in all three tissues (Fig. 8, A–B). Additionally, using CytoHubba, a PPI network of enriched spliceosome proteins was constructed, and hub proteins were analyzed. SF3B1 ranked the highest among the four proteins, suggesting that changes in SF3B1 phosphorylation may play an important role in myopia progression (Fig. 8C). To identify the upstream kinases of SF3B1, a protein kinase interaction network was constructed, revealing GSK3A/B and PRPF4B as the main kinases of SF3B1 (Fig. 8D). We analyzed the upstream kinases of SF3B1, and it was evident that the phosphorylation response was widespread across the three tissues. Notably, the protein levels of eight kinases showed minimal changes, while the phosphorylation of these kinases changed significantly at multiple sites. Specifically, PRPF4B demonstrated a tissue-specific phosphorylation response, with 30 phosphosites showing altered phosphorylation states in the three tissues, indicating its potential role in modulating tissue-specific responses during myopia progression. The diverse phosphorylation patterns of PRPF4B suggest it may play a key role in regulating splicing and transcriptional activities through differential phosphorylation across the retina, choroid, and sclera (Fig. 8E).

Further supporting the significance of SF3B1 phosphorylation, Western blotting analysis using a pan-phospho-threonine antibody demonstrated a significant increase in the overall threonine phosphorylation of SF3B1 in the retina, choroid, and sclera of FDM guinea pigs (Supplemental Fig. S5). This finding, while reflecting total threonine phosphorylation changes, aligns with the observed alterations in specific SF3B1 phosphosites and underscores the broad impact of FDM on SF3B1 phosphorylation status across ocular tissues. It is important to note that while the pan-antibody indicates an overall increase, the sclera exhibited different specific phosphosite modifications, which collectively may contribute to this observed trend of increased total threonine phosphorylation.

Discussion

The present study characterized the multitissue signature responses in the retina, choroid, and sclera observed during the early stages of myopia in guinea pigs, providing an integrated dataset encompassing proteomic and phosphoproteomic data. A key observation was the distinct nature of biological changes across these three tissues, with limited overlap in DEPs or phosphosites. Furthermore, within each tissue, the correlation between protein expression changes and phosphorylation modifications was also limited, as evidenced by nine-quadrant analyses. This suggests that protein expression and phosphorylation mediate responses to myopia through largely separate, yet potentially coordinated, biological processes. This finding aligns with the understanding that phosphorylation can offer a rapid and flexible regulatory mechanism, often independent of slower changes in protein abundance. The complexity of refractive eye development signaling, involving hundreds or thousands of genes and proteins compartmentalized within different ocular cell populations, underscores the necessity of identifying tissue- and cell-specific pathways for effective therapeutic interventions (8). Myopia pathogenesis is not a monolithic process but rather a composite of distinct molecular events in each ocular layer, with phosphorylation acting as a versatile and often primary regulator.

In the retina, form-deprivation induced a state of neural stress characterized by dysregulation of multiple biological processes crucial for normal retinal function, including neurotransmitter balance, G protein–coupled receptor activity, and photoreceptor cell development and differentiation. The observed alteration in G protein–coupled glutamate receptor activity may disrupt neuronal excitatory transmission, contributing to the imbalance between glutamate and GABA, a known factor in myopia (42). Our results provide novel insights into the altered G protein–coupled glutamate receptor activity that may be a crucial mechanism of the disruption of neuronal excitatory transmission. Another factor contributing to retinal neuronal dysfunction could be NTRK2 (nerve growth factor receptor), a key neuroregulatory protein that influences retinal structural development and signal transduction by regulating neuronal growth and survival (43). Although NTRK2 has not been linked directly to myopia previously, its dysregulation suggests a potential role in mediating retinal neuronal dysfunction during myopia. Over the years, morphological alterations of photoreceptors (elongation of rod outer segments), reduction in photoreceptor cell density, and changes in electroretinogram associated with experimentally induced myopia have all pointed toward the possible role of photoreceptors in the refractive development of the eye (44). Our results revealed that GNGT2, a cone photoreceptor-specific protein (45), was downregulated in the FDM retina, suggesting that cone dysfunction may be a factor in myopic eye growth. Liang et al. hypothesized that the extension of rod photoreceptors might contribute to the retina-choroid-sclera cascade and myopia development. Such extension, through outer segment elongation and retinomotor "push," exerts pressure on the RPE, potentially leading to choroidal vessel occlusion (11, 46, 47). Notably, we observed that Rho, a protein expressed specifically in the outer segments of rod photoreceptors (48), was upregulated, which indicates either elongation of the rod outer segments or an increase in rod cell numbers, supporting the idea that rod photoreceptor function is altered in myopic conditions. This is corroborated by the observation of Rho upregulation in FDM retinas by Hu et al., (49) which may represent a key mechanism of myopia development. Phosphoproteomic analysis revealed the upregulation of phosphoproteins associated with protein localization and cell cycle regulation, alongside the downregulation of proteins related to histone methylation. These changes indicate the presence of compensatory mechanisms aimed at repairing neuronal damage and maintaining cellular function. Furthermore, form deprivation modulates retinal RNA activity extensively by altering the phosphorylation status of various splicing factor proteins. Collectively, our findings suggest that impairment of neural signal transmission and photoreceptor dysfunction in the retina are key factors during myopic development. Future studies should investigate the roles of key retinal proteins (such as NTRK2) to facilitate the development of targeted interventions that protect retinal function and halt myopia initiation.

In the choroid, proteomic analysis showed significant inflammatory changes characterized by neutrophil aggregation and chronic inflammation, which may impair vascular function and tissue perfusion. Neutrophil aggregation in the choroid, through the release of reactive oxygen species and enzymes, can destroy local tissue and participate in tissue remodeling (50). This may lead to local vascular and matrix structural damage, compromising normal choroidal function and affecting nutrient and oxygen supply to the retina and sclera (51). Chronic low-grade inflammation was identified from our data. Such a prolonged inflammatory state may alter the local immune microenvironment, further impacting the functions of adjacent ocular tissues (52, 53). Our study also revealed the metabolic complexities of FDM. Upregulated phosphorylation levels influenced the transport processes of amino acids such as L-alanine and glutamine significantly. Amino acids are not only essential substrates for protein synthesis but also play a crucial role in maintaining cellular metabolism and mediating the stress response (54, 55). During early myopia, the metabolic demands of choroidal cells increase significantly, and enhanced amino acid transport may indicate that choroidal cells are maintaining their normal functions under inflammatory and metabolic stress by modulating metabolic pathways. This process is particularly vital for the vascular cells of the choroid, as their high metabolic activity requires a continuous supply of energy and enhanced amino acid metabolic pathways may help cells cope with inflammation-induced metabolic stress. The phosphorylation upregulation of glycolytic enzyme ENO1 supports the occurrence of metabolic remodeling. In summary, inflammatory responses and metabolic remodeling in the choroid may play critical roles in the acceleration of myopia. Future studies should focus on the regulatory mechanisms of choroidal inflammation and explore the potential exploitation of anti-inflammatory and metabolic regulators in myopia prevention and treatment.

In the sclera, proteomic analysis revealed significant enrichment of pathways related to muscle contraction, cytoskeletal remodeling, and fibroblast-to-myofibroblast transition (FMT). FMT is characterized by fibroblasts acquiring a contractile phenotype with increased ECM synthesis, which contributes to changes in tissue rigidity and the biomechanical properties of the sclera. Seo et al. observed that enhanced actin-mediated cellular contractility can induce mechanical activation of matrix cells, leading to the conversion of adipose matrix cells into myofibroblasts (56). Additionally, myosin can mediate cellular mechanosensitive responses that activate FMT through adhesion complexes (57). As choroidal inflammation increases, blood supply and oxygenation to the sclera diminish, prompting inflammatory cells to release various biochemical factors that trigger fibroblast phenotypic changes into myofibroblasts, resulting in significant alterations in the tissue microenvironment, such as marked reduction of type I collagen in the myopic sclera (58). Upregulation of proteins such as TNNT, MYL, and ACTN in our study supports FMT occurrence in the myopic sclera. This process is likely driven by increased mechanical stress and altered cytokine signaling, as shown in previous studies. Furthermore, we identified several core pathways related to energy metabolism, including glycolysis, in the myopic sclera. This may represent another factor affecting FMT. Lin et al. observed that elevated lactate levels from scleral glycolysis promote FMT and myopia (7). Phosphorylation changes promoting cell cycle progression and cytoskeletal remodeling suggest an active proliferative response in scleral fibroblasts, contributing to tissue expansion and increased AL. Notably, the downregulation of cytoskeletal depolymerization pathways implies stabilization of the myofibroblast phenotype, making the sclera more susceptible to mechanical stretching, thereby facilitating ocular elongation. Briefly, these findings indicate that scleral remodeling during myopia involves coordinated structural and metabolic changes, driven by FMT and cytoskeletal reorganization. Intervening in such pathways may provide an opportunity to modulate scleral biomechanics and slow myopic progression.

A striking finding from the phosphoproteomic analysis was the extensive involvement of the spliceosome signaling pathway across all three ocular tissues. Proteins central to RNA splicing, including SF3B1, ACIN1, HNRNPA3, and TRA2A, showed significant phosphorylation changes. SF3B1, a core component of the U2 small nuclear ribonucleoprotein essential for recognizing the pre-mRNA branch site during splicing, emerged as a key hub protein (59). Its overall threonine phosphorylation was found to be significantly increased in the retina, choroid, and sclera of FDM animals, as confirmed by pan-phospho-threonine Western blotting. This global increase, however, may mask site-specific changes, as suggested by the differential phosphorylation patterns observed in the sclera. Upstream kinase prediction identified PRPF4B and GSK3A/B as principal kinases regulating SF3B1. PRPF4B, a kinase itself associated with the spliceosome (also known as PRP4K in humans, which phosphorylates other spliceosomal components like PRP6 and PRP31 (60)), exhibited tissue-specific phosphorylation patterns, suggesting it may fine-tune SF3B1 activity and splicing outcomes differently in each tissue. Dysregulation of SF3B1 and other splicing factors is implicated in various diseases, including cancers like uveal melanoma [a choroidal malignancy where SF3B1 mutations are common and affect splicing (61)] and retinal degenerations like retinitis pigmentosa, where mutations in PRPFs (including PRPF4, an interactor of PRPF3 which is related to PRPF4B's family) lead to retina-specific splicing defects (62).The observed upregulation of SF3B1 phosphorylation in the retina might disrupt neuronal RNA processing, while in the choroid, it could modulate inflammatory or metabolic gene expression. In the sclera, altered SF3B1 phosphorylation, particularly the noted downregulation at T416, might contribute to the aberrant expression of cytoskeletal or ECM genes. This widespread impact on RNA splicing regulation suggests it is a fundamental molecular response to FDM, potentially contributing to the diverse pathological changes observed in myopia across different ocular compartments.

The integration of proteomic and phosphoproteomic data reveals a complex regulatory landscape in FDM. While direct overlap between DEPs and phosphoproteins was limited, the functional themes identified by each omics layer often converged on related biological processes within each tissue (63). For example, in the retina, proteomic changes pointed to altered "neuron activity," while phosphoproteomics highlighted dynamic regulation of "RNA splicing" and "cell cycle" processes in neurons; these are not mutually exclusive, as splicing changes can alter neuronal protein isoforms, and cell cycle dysregulation can be a neuronal stress response. In the choroid, proteomically identified inflammation (SYK, BTK, and NF-κB upregulation) was paralleled by phosphoproteomic signatures of metabolic shifts (Eno1 phosphorylation, altered amino acid transport) and modulated interferon-gamma responses. These processes are often interconnected, with inflammation known to drive metabolic reprogramming. Similarly, in the sclera, proteomic evidence for cytoskeletal remodeling/FMT (ACTN3, TNNT3, and TPM2 upregulation) and altered glycolysis (PGK1 downregulation) was complemented by phosphoproteomic data showing increased phosphorylation of cell cycle and cytoskeletal regulatory proteins (FLNA and NUMA1) and decreased phosphorylation of negative regulators of cytoskeletal dynamics (MAP1A/B). This convergence indicates that phosphorylation often provides immediate, fine-tuned modulation of pathways whose components may also be undergoing longer-term changes in abundance or whose overall activity state is being critically reshaped.

This study has several limitations that warrant discussion. Primarily, the findings are correlative, and direct functional validation of the identified key proteins and pathways through gene knockout, overexpression, or inhibitor studies was not performed. Such experiments are crucial for establishing causal relationships and understanding the precise mechanistic roles these molecules play in myopia pathogenesis. Secondly, the study focused on myopia established after 4 weeks of form deprivation. Investigating molecular changes at earlier time points, during the active development of myopia, could reveal different initiating mechanisms compared to those maintaining the myopic state. The signaling events at the onset of myopia can differ significantly from those in established conditions (64). Thirdly, the collection of the RPE and choroid as a combined RPE–choroid complex, due to technical difficulties in separation, limits the ability to definitively attribute observed changes solely to the choroid or the RPE. While this is a common approach, future studies employing techniques like laser capture microdissection could provide purer tissue samples and enhance the specificity of findings. Additionally, the use of a guinea pig model, while valuable, may not fully recapitulate all aspects of human myopia.

Future research should aim to functionally validate the key targets identified herein. Longitudinal multi-omics studies, incorporating transcriptomics and metabolomics, could provide a more holistic understanding of the dynamic interplay between different molecular layers and tissues during myopia development. Single-cell omics approaches would be invaluable for dissecting the cell-type–specific responses within each complex ocular tissue.

Despite these limitations, the comprehensive dataset generated in this study offers numerous potential therapeutic targets for myopia. In the retina, strategies could focus on neuroprotection via modulation of the NTRK2 pathway or by targeting photoreceptor health. In the choroid, anti-inflammatory approaches targeting SYK, BTK, or NF-κB signaling, or interventions aimed at improving choroidal metabolism and perfusion, hold promise (65). For the sclera, inhibiting FMT or ECM remodeling, for instance by modulating YAP signaling or scleral glycolysis and lactate pathways, could help maintain scleral integrity. The widespread dysregulation of the spliceosome pathway, particularly involving SF3B1 and its upstream kinase PRPF4B, presents a novel area for therapeutic exploration, potentially offering pan-tissue targets for myopia control (59).

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study provides comprehensive proteomic and phosphoproteomic profiles of the retina, choroid, and sclera in guinea pigs with FDM in vivo. Numerous signaling proteins and phosphosites linked to myopia development were identified and analyzed both qualitatively and quantitatively. The findings reveal distinct molecular signatures in each tissue: the retina exhibits impaired neurotrophic signaling and photoreceptor dysfunction; the choroid displays pronounced inflammatory responses and metabolic remodeling; and the sclera undergoes significant cytoskeletal and ECM alterations consistent with FMT, coupled with metabolic shifts including changes in glycolysis. Furthermore, dysregulation of RNA splicing, particularly involving SF3B1 phosphorylation, emerged as a common pathway affected across all three tissues. By revealing the links between tissue-specific alterations and changes in protein expression and phosphorylation, the present study facilitates the construction of a more complete signal transduction network crucial for understanding myopia regulation. The DEPs and predicted kinases identified herein could serve as targets for screening potential drugs, providing new insights and data to facilitate the clinical prevention and treatment of myopia. Future studies should focus on elucidating the precise mechanisms underlying these processes and developing strategies for preventing or slowing myopia progression.
