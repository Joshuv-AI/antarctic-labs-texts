Natural variation in the GhTTL promoter modulates cotton fiber elongation through the GhTALE–GhTTL–GhBIN2 module
Authors: Le Liu, Zhao Liu, Liqiang Fan, Mengli Yu, Ghulam Qanmber, Longfei Wang, Yujun Li, Fuguang Li, Shuangxia Jin, Zuoren Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn cotton, fiber length is a key determinant of industrial utility and is one of the most important agricultural traits shaped during domestication. However, the genetic determinants and molecular mechanisms underlying natural variation in fiber length remain poorly characterized. In this study, we identified GhTTL as a critical positive regulator of fiber elongation through genome-wide association study (GWAS). Natural polymorphisms in the cis-regulatory element of the GhTTL promoter region were found to significantly affect its binding affinity for the transcription factor GhTALE, thereby modulating GhTTL expression across diverse cotton populations. Mechanistic investigations revealed that GhTTL interacts with GhBIN2, a central negative regulator of the brassinosteroid (BR) signaling pathway, and anchors it to the cell membrane. This reduces cytoplasmic GhBIN2 levels, preventing it from associating with GhBES1, a master transcription factor of BR signaling. As a result, GhBES1 localizes to the nucleus, where it promotes fiber cell elongation. These findings elucidate the molecular basis of natural variation in cotton fiber length and provide deeper insights into the regulation of fiber elongation. In addition, this study identifies potential targets for genetic improvement to enhance cotton fiber quality and yield.Key words: cotton fiber, brassinosteroid, GWAS, GhTTL, GhBIN2

Introduction

Cotton (Gossypium spp.) is a globally cultivated commercial crop with a central role in agriculture and the textile industry (Zhang et al., 2014). The genus Gossypium comprises more than 50 species with diploid and tetraploid genomes and exhibits remarkable phenotypic diversity (Chen et al., 2007). Through long-term natural selection and artificial domestication, cotton has adapted to diverse climatic conditions and cultivation systems, resulting in rich genetic diversity (Dai et al., 2016; Viot and Wendel, 2023). This diversity provides a robust foundation for the sustainable genetic improvement of cotton (Fang et al., 2017a; Wang et al., 2017). Among cotton’s agronomic traits, fiber quality has been a primary focus of domestication and modern breeding, with particular emphasis on increasing fiber length and quantity while reducing photoperiod sensitivity. These improvements have expanded cotton’s adaptability and contributed to the development of high-performing cultivated varieties. As a core domestication trait, fiber length is intrinsically linked to textile quality and industrial utility, directly influencing cotton’s economic value (Krifa, 2006; Aslam et al., 2020; Yuan et al., 2021; Viot and Wendel, 2023). Cotton fiber development begins with the initiation of single-cell outgrowths from the ovule surface at anthesis, followed by a rapid elongation phase from 5 to 20 days post-anthesis (DPA), which largely determines the final fiber length (Jan et al., 2022). For example, in upland cotton (Gossypium hirsutum), fiber length naturally ranges from 22 to 33 mm, reflecting the complexity of its genetic regulation (Wakeham, 1955). However, the genetic mechanisms and molecular basis underlying this natural variation remain poorly understood, limiting the potential for breakthroughs in fiber quality improvement.

The tetratricopeptide thioredoxin-like (TTL) gene family encodes four plant-specific proteins (TTL1 to TTL4), characterized by six tetratricopeptide repeat (TPR) domains and a thioredoxin-like (Trx-like) domain at the C terminus (Rosado et al., 2006). The TPR domain, a conserved sequence of 34 amino acids, adopts a helix-turn-helix structure and is widely involved in mediating protein–protein interactions, often acting as a scaffold for assembling multiprotein complexes and anchoring proteins to specific cellular structures (Zeytuni and Zarivach, 2012; Perez-Riba and Itzhaki, 2019). The Trx-like domain, known for its enzymatic redox activity, can also function as a non-catalytic module that mediates protein interactions (Lu and Holmgren, 2014). The TTL gene family has been implicated in plant adaptation to abiotic stress. For instance, TTL1 is crucial for osmotic stress tolerance, whereas TTL1, TTL3, and TTL4 are broadly expressed under normal growth conditions and exhibit differential expression under osmotic and salt stress, suggesting both overlapping and distinct roles in abiotic stress responses. In contrast, TTL2 is specifically expressed in pollen, where it is essential for male gametophyte development and transmission (Lakhssassi et al., 2012). TTL3 is critical for regulating lateral root development under high osmotic stress by modulating cell wall biophysical properties (Xin et al., 2022). Mutants lacking TTL1, TTL3, and TTL4 exhibit vascular developmental defects and abnormalities in male sporogenesis, phenotypes commonly associated with brassinosteroid (BR)-deficient mutants (Amorim-Silva et al., 2019). These observations suggest that the TTL family plays pivotal roles in abiotic stress adaptation, root development, and reproductive growth, potentially through cellular signaling pathways. However, the roles of TTL genes in cotton remain unexplored.

Cotton fiber, as a highly specialized single-cell structure that undergoes extraordinary elongation, is an ideal model for studying plant cell growth and elongation (Yang et al., 2020). This remarkable elongation involves dynamic remodeling of cell morphology, precise biosynthesis of cell wall components, and efficient intracellular transport. BRs are crucial regulators of fiber cell elongation. Key components of the BR signaling pathway, such as BES1, PAS1, DET2, and EXO, have been shown to promote fiber elongation (Liu et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024). BIN2 (BRASSINOSTEROID-INSENSITIVE 2), a central negative regulator of BR signaling (Li et al., 2001), inhibits this pathway by phosphorylating downstream transcription factors such as BES1 and BZR1, thereby affecting their localization, stability, and transcriptional activity (He et al., 2002; Zhao et al., 2002). Beyond BR signaling, BIN2 participates in diverse physiological processes, including drought response, photomorphogenesis, and chloroplast development, coordinating plant growth dynamics and environmental adaptation (Jiang et al., 2019; Li et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2021; Dong et al., 2023). However, the role of BIN2 in fiber development and the upstream regulatory networks modulating its activity remain largely unexplored (Kondo et al., 2014; Song et al., 2021; Kim et al., 2023).

In this study, we performed a genome-wide association study (GWAS) and identified natural variations in the GhTTL promoter region that are significantly associated with cotton fiber length. Biochemical analysis revealed that these variations alter the binding affinity of the transcription factor GhTALE to cis-regulatory elements in the GhTTL promoter, thereby modulating GhTTL expression in natural cotton populations and ultimately affecting fiber elongation. Mechanistic studies uncovered a novel regulatory pathway in which GhTTL directly interacts with GhBIN2 and anchors it to the cell membrane. This reduces the cytoplasmic levels of GhBIN2, preventing its interaction with the downstream transcription factor GhBES1 and facilitating GhBES1’s nuclear localization. By inhibiting the formation of the GhBIN2–GhBES1 complex, this mechanism promotes fiber cell elongation. Our findings reveal a molecular mechanism in which the GhTALE–GhTTL module coordinates with the GhBIN2–GhBES1 signaling axis to regulate cotton fiber elongation. This study advances our understanding of the regulatory network underlying fiber development and offers new theoretical insights for improving cotton fiber quality and yield.

Results

GhTTL is associated with fiber quality

A genomic region significantly associated with fiber length was identified on chromosome D10 through a genome-wide association study (GWAS) of 419 G. hirsutum whole-genome resequencing datasets (Figure 1A and 1B; Supplemental Table 1). This region, located between 13.6 and 14.37 Mb (Figure 1C; Supplemental Table 2), contained 1485 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) (Supplemental Table 1). Within this region, 10 candidate genes were identified (Supplemental Table 3), seven of which harbored SNPs in either their promoter regions (2000 bp upstream) or coding sequences (Supplemental Table 4). Expression profiling of these 10 genes across various fiber developmental stages revealed that GhATHB16, Gh0994, GhTGA4, GhSULTR2, and GhTTL were highly expressed in cotton fiber (Supplemental Figure 1), which suggests their potential involvement in fiber development. Functional validation using virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) showed that fiber length in VIGS-GhTTL plants was significantly reduced compared to the vector control (Supplemental Figure 2). These findings suggest that GhTTL is a critical regulator of fiber development within this genomic region.

GhTTL shares high sequence similarity and a close evolutionary relationship with AtTTL3 (Supplemental Figure 3A). Structural analysis demonstrated that the encoded protein contains a canonical domain architecture with seven tandem tetratricopeptide repeat (TPR) motifs (Supplemental Figure 3B and 3C). Located within a linkage disequilibrium (LD) block on chromosome D10 (Figure 1C and 1D), six SNPs were identified in or near GhTTL: five in its promoter region and one in its coding region, forming two haplotypes (Hap1 and Hap2) (Figure 1E). These SNPs were significantly associated with fiber traits, including fiber length (FL), fiber strength (FS), micronaire value (M), elongation ratio (E), length uniformity (LU), spinning consistency index (SCI), lint percentage (LP), lint index (LI), and fiber weight per boll (FWPB) (Figure 1F). Phenotypic analysis of these nine traits revealed significant differences between the two GhTTL haplotypes in FL, FS, LP, SCI, LI, E, and LU (Figure 1G–1J; Supplemental Figure 4). Collectively, these results suggest that GhTTL is a potential regulator of fiber elongation and plays a key role in determining fiber quality traits in G. hirsutum.

The cell membrane protein GhTTL positively regulates fiber elongation

GhTTL expression increased markedly during the rapid elongation stage (5–15 DPA) of cotton fiber development (Supplemental Figure 3D). Subcellular localization analysis revealed that the GhTTL protein was localized to the cell membrane (Supplemental Figure 3E). To further elucidate its biological function and assess the potential effect of its coding SNP, two overexpression lines, 35S:GhTTL-C and 35S:GhTTL-G, were generated. Both lines exhibited significantly elevated GhTTL expression (Figure 2A) and stable production of the GhTTL-C/G–GFP fusion protein (Supplemental Figure 5). Phenotypic analysis showed enhanced fiber length in both overexpression lines at key developmental time points (10, 15, and 25 DPA) and at maturity (Supplemental Figure 6; Figure 2B–2D), with no significant differences between the two genotypes when expression levels were comparable (Figure 2E and 2F). GWAS results further indicated an association between GhTTL and fiber strength, and ultrastructural analysis revealed a slight thickening of the fiber cell wall in the overexpression lines (Supplemental Figure 7). Collectively, these findings suggest that GhTTL positively regulates fiber elongation independently of its coding SNP.

Effect of natural variation in the GhTTL promoter region on gene expression

A total of six SNPs were identified in the coding and promoter regions of GhTTL (Supplemental Figure 8). Among these, the SNP in the coding region had no significant effect on gene function. In contrast, the five SNPs in the promoter region were in linkage disequilibrium, forming two haplotypes (Hap1 and Hap2) (Figure 3A). Phenotypic analysis of different accessions revealed that Hap1 varieties exhibited significantly greater fiber length than Hap2 varieties (Figure 3B). Correspondingly, GhTTL expression was markedly higher in Hap1 than in Hap2 varieties (Figure 3C). These findings suggest that variations in the GhTTL promoter region influence fiber elongation by regulating gene expression.

To investigate how natural promoter variation affects GhTTL expression, luciferase (LUC) reporter constructs containing promoter fragments from the two genotypes (0800-Hap1 and 0800-Hap2) were generated. Transient expression assays in tobacco leaves showed significantly higher LUC activity driven by 0800-Hap1 than by 0800-Hap2 (Figure 3D and 3E). Further analysis using truncated promoter fragments identified the Hap1-P1 region as the primary determinant of differential expression (Figure 3F–3J). Four SNPs were detected in the Hap1-P1 region. To assess their functional significance, we performed LUC assays using promoter constructs with individual SNP substitutions, which identified SNP_14334971 as the critical locus influencing GhTTL promoter activity (Supplemental Figure 9A–9D). Furthermore, PlantTFDB-based transcription factor binding site analysis revealed that SNP_14334971 overlaps with the binding motifs of GhDof, GhAP2, and GhTALE, suggesting a potential role in modulating transcription factor binding and downstream gene regulation (Figure 3K). A yeast one-hybrid (Y1H) assay confirmed strong binding of GhTALE to the GhTTL-Hap1-P1 promoter, with weaker binding to GhTTL-Hap2-P1 (Figure 3L and 3M). This was further validated by LUC reporter assays, which showed that co-expression of GhTALE with GhTTL-Hap1-P1 increased LUC activity by approximately three-fold, whereas co-expression with GhTTL-Hap2-P1 resulted in only a 20% increase (Figure 3N–3Q). To precisely evaluate the effect of SNP_14334971 on GhTALE binding, we generated promoter constructs harboring the SNP_14334971 base mutation. Subsequent LUC, Y1H, and electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) analyses demonstrated that the C-to-T substitution at SNP_14334971 directly affected GhTALE binding affinity to the GhTTL promoter region (Figure 3R and 3S; Supplemental Figure 9E and 9F). These results indicate that natural variation in the GhTTL promoter alters GhTALE binding affinity, thereby modulating GhTTL expression and influencing its positive regulation of fiber elongation.

Loss-of-function mutation in GhTTL reduces fiber length

To further investigate the role of GhTTL in cotton fiber development, we designed a pair of sgRNAs targeting the first exon of GhTTL. Using the CRISPR/Cas9 gene-editing system, three ghttl mutant lines with different editing types were successfully generated in the cotton variety ZM49. Sequencing analysis revealed edits at the sgRNA1 target site in all three lines, resulting in frameshift mutations in GhTTL (Figure 4A). Phenotypic evaluation showed that the ghttl mutants exhibited significantly shorter fibers during the rapid elongation stage (10, 15, and 20 DPA) (Figure 4B–4E). Analysis of mature fibers confirmed a substantial reduction in fiber length in the mutant lines relative to wild-type plants (Figure 4C and 4E). Ultrastructural observations further revealed a slight decrease in fiber cell wall thickness in the ghttl mutants (Supplemental Figure 10). These results indicate that GhTTL is a key positive regulator of fiber elongation, with loss of function leading to shortened fibers and reduced cell wall thickness.

GhTTL directly interacts with GhBIN2, a negative regulator of fiber development

To investigate the molecular mechanism by which GhTTL regulates fiber development, a yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) library was constructed using cDNA from cotton fibers, and 14 candidate interacting proteins were identified. Subsequent pairwise Y2H assays confirmed that GhTTL specifically interacts with GhBIN2 (Figure 4F). This interaction was validated through multiple experimental approaches. A glutathione S-transferase (GST) pull-down assay demonstrated that GhBIN2-GST binds specifically to GhTTL-His (Figure 4G). Co-immunoprecipitation (coIP) and LUC assays further confirmed the GhTTL–GhBIN2 interaction in plant cells (Figure 4H and 4I). In addition, biolayer interferometry (BLI) revealed that GhTTL-His interacted directly with GhBIN2-GST, but not with the GST control (Figure 4J). Collectively, these results provide strong evidence that GhTTL forms a stable interaction with GhBIN2.

To assess the functional role of GhBIN2 in fiber development, GhBIN2-overexpressing cotton lines (35S:GhBIN2) were generated. qPCR analysis showed significant upregulation of GhBIN2 expression in 35S:GhBIN2 plants relative to the control (Figure 5A). Morphological analysis revealed that fiber length was significantly reduced in 35S:GhBIN2 lines during the critical fiber elongation stage (10, 15, and 20 DPA) (Figure 5B and 5C). Analysis of mature fibers revealed a significant reduction in fiber length (Figure 5D and 5E) and a decrease in secondary cell wall thickness (Figure 5F) in 35S:GhBIN2 lines relative to the control. These results demonstrate that GhBIN2 functions as a negative regulator of cotton fiber development.

GhBIN2 contains a conserved S_TKc domain and shares high sequence similarity with BIN2 homologs from Arabidopsis, soybean, and rapeseed (Supplemental Figure 11A–11C). Tissue-specific expression analysis revealed that GhBIN2 was highly expressed during the rapid elongation stage of cotton fibers (Supplemental Figure 11D). GhBIN2-GFP was predominantly localized to the cell membrane, with weak nuclear localization (Supplemental Figure 11E). BIN2, a core member of the GSK family, is known to negatively regulate the brassinosteroid (BR) signaling pathway (Li and Nam, 2002). To explore GhBIN2’s potential role in BR signaling, an in vitro ovule culture assay was used. Treatment of 35S:GhBIN2 ovules with 15 μM bikinin, a GSK family-specific inhibitor, partially rescued the fiber elongation defects observed in 35S:GhBIN2 lines (Supplemental Figure 12). In addition, treatment of ovule cultures with 1 μM brassinolide (BL) markedly promoted fiber elongation in wild-type plants, whereas 35S:GhBIN2 lines were insensitive to 1 μM BL, with no significant change in fiber length (Supplemental Figure 13). These findings suggest that GhBIN2 functions as a GSK family member that regulates BR signaling during cotton fiber elongation.

GhTTL anchors GhBIN2 to the cell membrane and inhibits the formation of GhBIN2–GhBES1 modules

Phenotypic analysis of mature fibers in hybrid progeny (35S:GhTTL × 35S:GhBIN2) revealed intermediate fiber lengths compared with the parental lines (Figure 5G and 5H). Together with the phenotypes of 35S:GhTTL/ghttl and 35S:GhBIN2 lines, this suggests that GhTTL and GhBIN2 act antagonistically to regulate fiber elongation. As a classical protein kinase, GhBIN2 primarily functions through phosphorylation. In vitro phosphorylation and ADP-Glo kinase activity assays showed that GhBIN2 does not directly phosphorylate GhTTL (Figure 6A and 6B).

Previous studies identified a functional GhBES1 (Liu et al., 2023), which we used as a downstream reporter to investigate the molecular mechanism underlying the antagonistic regulation of fiber elongation by GhTTL and GhBIN2. A split-luciferase interaction assay confirmed a direct interaction between GhBIN2 and GhBES1 (Figure 6C and 6D), with fluorescence signals significantly higher than those of the negative controls. However, GhTTL overexpression using 35S:GhTTL weakened the interaction between GhBIN2 and GhBES1 (Figure 6E and 6F). Yeast two-hybrid and three-hybrid assays further supported this finding, showing that GhTTL expression significantly reduced the binding affinity between GhBIN2 and GhBES1 (Figure 6G–6I; Supplemental Figure 14). Notably, an in vitro phosphorylation assay revealed that GhBIN2 efficiently phosphorylated GhBES1, and the addition of GhTTL-His did not significantly affect this phosphorylation (Figure 6J), suggesting that GhTTL does not directly block the binding or phosphorylation of GhBES1 by GhBIN2. These results imply that the regulatory effect of GhTTL on GhBIN2 and GhBES1 involves additional intracellular mechanisms beyond simple competitive binding or phosphorylation.

Scaffold proteins are known to regulate cellular signaling by anchoring interaction partners to the plasma membrane. Based on these findings, we hypothesized that GhTTL functions as a scaffold protein, anchoring GhBIN2 to the plasma membrane and modulating its interaction with, and phosphorylation of, GhBES1. A bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assay demonstrated a clear interaction between GhTTL and GhBIN2 at the plasma membrane (Supplemental Figure 15). Fluorescence localization experiments in protoplasts showed that GhBES1-GFP-His alone predominantly localized to the nucleus and cytoplasm. Co-expression with GhBIN2-GST shifted GhBES1-GFP-His fluorescence to the plasma membrane, which suggests GhBIN2-mediated relocalization. However, co-expression of GhTTL-His, GhBIN2-GST, and GhBES1-GFP-His resulted in fluorescence signals localized to the plasma membrane, cytoplasm, and nucleus, which suggests that GhTTL altered GhBIN2’s ability to regulate GhBES1 localization (Figure 6K). Western blot analysis of nuclear and membrane protein fractions confirmed these observations (Figure 6L). Collectively, these results demonstrate that GhTTL anchors GhBIN2 to the plasma membrane, thereby disrupting the GhBIN2–GhBES1 interaction and positively regulating fiber elongation.

Discussion

Cotton fibers are highly elongated single cells, and the BR signaling pathway is critical for their elongation. Fiber development depends on the precise modulation of signaling networks (Wang et al., 2012). Although TTL genes are associated with microtubule and endomembrane dynamics and are potentially involved in BR signaling (Amorim-Silva et al., 2019), their specific role in cotton fiber elongation has remained unclear. A GWAS revealed that GhTTL was significantly associated with fiber length. Further analysis showed that natural variation in the GhTTL promoter region influences the binding efficiency of GhTALE, thereby modulating GhTTL expression and affecting fiber elongation. Mechanistic studies demonstrated that GhTTL functions as a scaffold protein, anchoring GhBIN2—a core negative regulator of BR signaling—to the plasma membrane. This alters GhBIN2’s ability to regulate the subcellular distribution of GhBES1, a downstream transcription factor. The precise control of the GhBIN2–GhBES1 interaction and phosphorylation ultimately determines the degree of fiber cell elongation. These findings improve our understanding of the BR signaling network in cotton fiber elongation and offer new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying plant cell elongation.

Natural variation provides fundamental genetic resources for genetic improvement

Natural variation plays a pivotal role in the evolution of functional genes and the regulation of crop trait differentiation. Recent advances in genomics and molecular biology have established a strong foundation for dissecting functional gene variation. For example, two SNPs in the coding region of the NRT1.1B gene have been identified as key contributors to enhanced nitrogen use efficiency in indica rice, improving nitrate uptake and transport and simultaneously enhancing grain quality (Hu et al., 2015). Similarly, natural variation in the promoter region of the WD40 family member OsKRN2 significantly increases grain yield in maize (Chen et al., 2022). Furthermore, two insertions or deletions and three SNPs upstream of the ZmDREB2.7 gene enhance drought tolerance by regulating dehydration response signaling (Liu et al., 2013). Another notable example is the 2-bp polymorphism in the UPA2 gene, which modulates plant architecture by affecting the binding efficiency of UPA2 with DRL1 and downstream genes. This variation narrows the leaf angle, improving canopy light interception efficiency and grain yield under dense planting conditions (Tian et al., 2019). Collectively, these studies highlight how natural variation drives both evolutionary and domestication processes and provides invaluable genetic resources for crop improvement.

Cotton, a vital commercial crop and a key raw material for the global textile industry, has undergone complex evolution and domestication driven by both natural and artificial selection. These processes involve multiple layers of genetic variation, including genome rearrangements, whole-genome duplication, gene mutations, and regulatory changes in gene expression (Bao et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2022; Viot and Wendel, 2023). Natural evolution has enabled cotton species to adapt to diverse ecological environments, leading to significant differentiation in traits such as fiber length, seed morphology, and stress resistance (Wendel et al., 2009). Domestication has further refined these traits through long-term artificial selection, significantly improving fiber quality, enhancing boll dehiscence, reducing plant height, compacting architecture, increasing boll size, and reducing photoperiod sensitivity (Fang et al., 2017b; Li et al., 2021). Several genes have been identified as critical for cotton domestication and improvement. For instance, GhSI7 significantly enhances the seed index (Liu et al., 2022), GhROPGEF5 promotes fiber length and strength (Wang et al., 2025), and cis-regulatory element variation in GhRPRS1 contributes to petal color diversity (Hu et al., 2024). Using GWAS in upland cotton, our research identified GhTTL and revealed that natural variation in its promoter region is pivotal for regulating fiber elongation. These findings underscore the essential role of natural variation in the genetic improvement of cotton.

However, intense artificial selection during domestication has substantially reduced genetic diversity in cotton, including the loss of stress-resistance genes found in wild species. To address this, future research should use wild species such as Gossypium raimondii (a D-genome species) and Gossypium herbaceum (an A-genome species) as reservoirs of superior alleles for stress tolerance, pest and disease resistance, and fiber quality. In addition, genomic selection (GS), which integrates genomic and phenotypic data, offers a powerful approach to predicting the genetic potential of target traits. Combining GWAS with GS holds promise for accelerating cotton breeding.

GhTTL is a key regulator in the GhTALE–GhTTL–GhBIN2 module during cotton fiber elongation

The TTL gene family is unique to land plants and plays a significant role in plant development and environmental interactions (Amorim-Silva et al., 2019). In Arabidopsis, the TTL family comprises four members (AtTTL1–4), each exhibiting differential responses to abiotic stress (Lakhssassi et al., 2012). Mutations in AtTTL1, the first characterized member, reduce tolerance to NaCl and osmotic stress, resulting in impaired root elongation and disorganized root meristems (Rosado et al., 2006). Similarly, AtTTL2 mutations affect male gametophyte development and severely restrict primary root growth under osmotic stress (Lakhssassi et al., 2012), whereas AtTTL3 loss of function delays lateral root primordia development, reducing the number of lateral roots (Xin et al., 2022). Despite these insights, the role of TTL genes in cotton fiber development has remained unexplored. In this study, we identified GhTTL as the most promising candidate gene for regulating fiber elongation. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that GhTTL is highly similar and evolutionarily close to AtTTL1 and AtTTL3 (Supplemental Figure 3). Overexpression of GhTTL significantly increased fiber length and secondary cell wall thickness, whereas GhTTL knockdown produced shorter fibers with thinner secondary cell walls (Figure 2, Supplemental Figures 7 and 10). These results establish GhTTL as a positive regulator of fiber development in cotton.

GhTTL is the first characterized member of the TTL protein family in cotton and encodes a protein containing seven tandem tetratricopeptide repeat (TPR) domains (Supplemental Figure 3B and 3C). TPR domains function as protein–protein interaction modules composed of all-helical secondary structures and play critical roles in protein folding, mRNA stability, and plant hormone signal transduction (Blatch and Lässle, 1999; D'Andrea and Regan, 2003; Yang et al., 2005). Proteins with TPR domains are involved in signaling pathways associated with ethylene, cytokinin, gibberellins, auxin, and abscisic acid (Prodromou et al., 1999; Gounalaki et al., 2000; Fedoroff, 2002; Wang et al., 2004; Silverstone et al., 2007). In upland cotton, GhTTL acts as a scaffold protein and directly interacts with GhBIN2, a negative regulator of the BR signaling pathway. This interaction facilitates the formation of the GhBIN2–GhBES1 complex, a central regulatory node in BR signaling. Our findings highlight the unique role of GhTTL in BR-mediated processes and provide new insights into the involvement of TPR domains in plant hormone signaling networks.

This study demonstrated that natural variation in the GhTTL promoter region significantly influences the binding efficiency of the transcription factor GhTALE, thereby altering GhTTL expression and affecting fiber elongation. GhTALE proteins are highly conserved transcription factors characterized by a homeodomain (HD) DNA-binding domain, which includes a three-amino-acid loop extension crucial for DNA-binding specificity (Chen et al., 2003). These proteins play diverse roles in plant development, including maintaining meristematic activity, regulating leaf morphogenesis, influencing secondary growth, and modulating hormone signaling pathways (Yang et al., 2000; Chen et al., 2003; Hamant and Pautot, 2010; Liu et al., 2010; Arun et al., 2019). We showed that GhTALE binds to the GhTTL promoter to regulate its expression, thereby enhancing BR signaling and promoting cotton fiber elongation. Because secondary growth and xylem development restrict fiber elongation in the later stages of cotton fiber development, and maintaining meristematic activity extends the elongation period, future research could investigate whether GhTALE influences cotton fiber elongation by delaying secondary growth and xylem development while maintaining meristematic activity.

The spatial regulation of GhBIN2 establishes a new regulatory level in BR signaling

BIN2 (BRASSINOSTEROID-INSENSITIVE 2), a plant-specific serine/threonine protein kinase from the glycogen synthase kinase 3 (GSK3) family, serves as a central regulator of plant growth and development. It is critical for diverse biological processes, including cell differentiation, cell elongation, photomorphogenesis, and responses to various abiotic and biotic stresses (Jonak and Hirt, 2002; Li and Nam, 2002). The primary role of BIN2 in these processes is mediated through the phosphorylation of downstream targets such as BZR1 and BES1, effectively inhibiting BR signaling and regulating plant growth (He et al., 2002). However, emerging evidence indicates that BIN2’s function extends beyond the BR pathway, with intricate connections to other hormone signaling pathways, including those of auxin, gibberellin, and abscisic acid (Cho et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2018).

BIN2 regulation is orchestrated by a multilayered network of dynamic mechanisms. For instance, the phosphatase BSU1 deactivates BIN2 by dephosphorylating a conserved tyrosine residue (Tyr200) (Kim et al., 2009). In contrast, BIN2 degradation is mediated via the ubiquitination pathway; the F-box protein KIB1 facilitates proteasome-dependent degradation, reducing BIN2 protein abundance (Zhu et al., 2017). In addition, the COP1/SPA complex modulates BIN2 activity without affecting its expression by disrupting its interaction with PIF3 (Ling et al., 2017). Collectively, these mechanisms highlight the complex and dynamic regulation of BIN2 through its phosphorylation status, protein stability, and activity.

Building on these insights, our study reveals a novel regulatory mechanism for BIN2: GhTTL directly interacts with BIN2 and anchors it to the plasma membrane, thereby spatially separating it from its downstream target GhBES1. This subcellular localization identifies a new dimension of BIN2 regulation and provides a deeper understanding of its functional dynamics. Experimental evidence shows that the GhTTL–GhBIN2 interaction modulates cotton fiber elongation through this spatial mechanism. Furthermore, TTL expression is significantly altered under salt stress, which suggests that the dynamic regulation of TTL-mediated BIN2 membrane anchoring may contribute to abiotic stress response in plants. Given the existence of BIN2 homologs, such as AtSK22–24, in Arabidopsis and other plant species, future studies could investigate whether similar membrane-localization mechanisms operate for these homologs. It also remains to be determined whether environmental or developmental signals regulate the expression or activity of TTL or its homologs, enabling dynamic control over BIN2 membrane localization and downstream signaling pathways.

In summary, GhTTL was identified as a key gene regulating fiber elongation in the natural population of G. hirsutum. A SNP in the GhTTL promoter region modulates the binding affinity of the transcription factor GhTALE, leading to differential transcription of GhTTL, which in turn affects fiber elongation. Mechanistic analysis revealed a novel regulatory pathway for fiber elongation: GhTTL anchors GhBIN2 to the plasma membrane, thereby restricting its active free state. Elevated GhTTL expression enhances the membrane localization of GhBIN2, reducing its nuclear–cytoplasmic shuttling and phosphorylation of downstream targets, ultimately promoting fiber elongation. In contrast, reduced GhTTL expression increases the pool of free GhBIN2, reducing GhBES1 nuclear localization and enhancing phosphorylation of downstream substrates, thereby inhibiting fiber elongation (Figure 7). These findings offer a new perspective on the regulatory network governing cotton fiber elongation and highlight GhTTL as a promising target for genetic improvement of fiber quality.

A working model for GhTTL-mediated regulation of fiber elongation.

In a natural Gossypium hirsutum population, single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) within the GhTTL promoter influence the binding affinity of the transcription factor GhTALE, leading to differential transcriptional regulation of GhTTL. The GhTTL protein anchors the GhBIN2 kinase to the cell membrane, thereby restricting the pool of free GhBIN2. Elevated GhTTL expression enhances GhBIN2 membrane localization, reducing its interaction with GhBES1 and preventing excessive phosphorylation of downstream targets, ultimately promoting cotton fiber elongation. In contrast, reduced GhTTL expression increases the abundance of free GhBIN2, enhancing its interaction with GhBES1 and promoting phosphorylation of downstream targets, which inhibits fiber elongation. Methods

Plant material and growth conditions

The upland cotton cultivar ZM49 (G. hirsutum L.) was selected as the recipient material for genetic transformation. Plants were cultivated in a controlled growth chamber at 28°C with a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod to mimic optimal growth conditions. During the first month post-germination, seedlings were grown in vermiculite and commercial potting soil mixed at a 3:1 ratio (v/v) to ensure adequate aeration and water retention. Subsequently, plants were transplanted into a substrate containing yellow soil and potting soil at a 1:2 ratio (v/v) for further growth. Irrigation was applied every 72 h to maintain optimal soil moisture throughout the experimental period.

Genome-wide association study

GWAS was performed using SNP markers obtained from whole-genome re-sequencing of a diverse panel of cotton accessions. The raw resequencing data and SNP variants are available from NCBI under accession number PRJNA399050 (SRP115740). Fiber phenotypic data are provided in Supplemental Table 1. TASSEL 5.0 was used with a mixed linear model to account for population structure and kinship. LD blocks around key SNPs were examined, and LD heatmaps were generated using Haploview to visualize regional association. Manhattan and quantile–quantile (Q–Q) plots were produced using the R package "qqman" to illustrate the distribution and significance of association signals (Turner, 2014).

RNA extraction and real-time qPCR analysis

Cotton samples were collected at different time points during fiber development, namely 5, 10, 15, 20, and 25 days post-anthesis (DPA), from field-grown ZM49 plants. Additional samples included 10 DPA fiber from virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) plants, transgenic lines (35S:GhTTL-C/G, 35S:GhBIN2), and their wild-type controls grown under laboratory conditions. Samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and ground into fine powder. Total RNA was extracted using the TIANGEN Plant RNA Extraction Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quality and quantity were verified before cDNA synthesis. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed on a Roche LightCycler 480 system using the TaKaRa Premix Ex Taq II kit. The qPCR program included an initial denaturation at 95°C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 10 s and annealing/extension at 60°C for 30 s.

VIGS

Gene fragments for silencing of target genes were designed using the SGN VIGS Tool (http://vigs.solgenomics.net/), and primers were synthesized accordingly. Genomic DNA from ZM49 plants was extracted using the TIANGEN Plant DNA Kit and used as a template for PCR amplification. The resulting fragments were ligated into enzyme-digested pCLCrV vectors via homologous recombination. Recombinant constructs were validated by sequencing and transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 competent cells. At 10 days post-germination, plants with fully expanded cotyledons were injected with combinations of pCLCrVB, pCLCrV–target gene, pCLCrVA, and pCLCrV-Su constructs. Following injection, plants were incubated in the dark for one day and then transferred to the greenhouse to observe silencing effects.

Construction of overexpression and gene-editing vectors

The GhBIN2 and GhTTL-C/G genes were cloned into the WMV067 vector under the control of the 35S promoter to generate overexpression vectors. CRISPR target sites for the GhTTL gene were designed using the CRISPR 2.0 tool (http://crispr.hzau.edu.cn/CRISPR2/), and sgRNA sequences were inserted into the WMV016 vector containing the AT26 promoter to create gene-editing constructs. Recombinant plasmids were validated by double enzyme digestion and sequencing, then transformed into A. tumefaciens EHA105 cells. Positive clones were identified by colony PCR, and Agrobacterium cultures were expanded for use in the shoot apical meristem transformation system to generate transgenic plants (Ge et al., 2023).

Evolutionary and expression analysis of GhBIN2 and GhTTL

Amino acid sequences of AtBIN2 and AtTTLs from Arabidopsis thaliana were retrieved from the TAIR database (https://www.arabidopsis.org/), and full-length sequences of GhBIN2 and GhTTL family members were obtained from the CottonFGD database (https://www.elabcaas.cn/). Multiple sequence alignments were performed using MEGA 7.0, and phylogenetic trees were constructed with the neighbor-joining method using 1000 bootstrap replicates and 95% site coverage. Sequence alignments were visualized with Jalview (https://www.jalview.org/), and conserved domains were analyzed with the SMART tool.

Cotton fiber phenotypic analysis

Fiber lengths were measured using ImageJ software (https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/) at 10, 15, 20, and 25 DPA and at maturity. Six biological replicates were included for each time point to ensure statistical accuracy. Mature fibers were embedded in SPI-Chem low-viscosity resin, sectioned with an ultramicrotome, and analyzed for ultrastructural characteristics using high-resolution environmental scanning electron microscopy.

Western blotting

Fibers were collected at 10 DPA from 35S:GhTTL-C/G and wild-type plants. Total protein was extracted using a plant protein extraction kit (Yeasen Biotechnology), and concentrations were determined using the Bradford method. Proteins were separated on 10% SDS–PAGE gels and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in Tris Buffered Saline with Tween-20 (TBST) for 4 h, incubated with a primary GFP antibody (1:2000 dilution) for 2 h, and then with a secondary goat anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G antibody (1:2000 dilution) for 1 h. Signals were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) and visualized with a chemiluminescence imaging system.

Luciferase reporter assay

GhTTL promoter fragments were cloned into the 0800 vector, which contains a luciferase (LUC) cassette, whereas GhTALE was cloned into the 62SK vector. Recombinant plasmids were validated by sequencing and co-infiltrated into Nicotiana benthamiana leaves via A. tumefaciens-mediated transient expression. Luciferase activity was measured 72 h post-infiltration using the Tanon 5200 chemiluminescence imaging system and quantified with a Dual Luciferase Reporter Gene Assay Kit (Yeasen Biotechnology).

Y1H assay

The GhTTL-Hap1-P1, GhTTL-Hap2-P1, and GhTTL-Hap1-SNP promoter variants were cloned into the pLacZi vector, and the GhTALE, GhAP2, and GhDof coding sequences were cloned into the pB42AD vector. Recombinant constructs were co-transformed into the EGY48 yeast strain and selected on SD/−Trp/−Ura medium. Transformants were transferred to SD/−Trp/−Ura/−Gal/Raf/X-Gal medium to assess interactions. Positive controls included pLacZi-ProLOG and pB42AD-RGN1.

EMSA

The CDS of GhTALE was cloned into a prokaryotic expression vector containing a 6× His tag and transformed into Escherichia coli BL21(DE3). The GhTALE-His protein was obtained via Isopropyl β-D-thiogalactoside(IPTG) induction, ultrasonic cell disruption, and purification. A biotin-labeled probe and a competitor probe approximately 100 bp long were designed based on the binding motifs in the target gene promoter region. The GhTALE-His protein was incubated with the probe for 30 min and electrophoresed on a 6% (w/v) polyacrylamide gel. The electrophoresis products were transferred to a nylon membrane at 60 V for 1 h and detected using an EMSA kit (Beyotime Biotechnology).

Y2H assay

The coding sequences of GhTTL and GhBES1 were cloned into pGADT7, and that of GhBIN2 was cloned into pGBKT7. The vectors were co-transformed into the Y2HGold yeast strain and selected on SD/−Trp/−Ura medium. Transformants were transferred to SD/−Ade/−His/−Leu/−Trp medium for further interaction analysis under stringent selection conditions.

In vitro pull-down assay

The coding sequence of GhBIN2 was cloned into the pETX-4T vector to generate the recombinant vector GhBIN2-GST, and the coding sequence of GhTTL was cloned into the pET30a vector to generate the recombinant vector GhTTL-His. The recombinant proteins GhBIN2-GST and GhTTL-His were expressed in Escherichia coli BL21(DE3) and purified using affinity chromatography. Purified GhTTL-His was incubated with GhBIN2-GST at 4°C for 1 h, followed by incubation with pre-equilibrated Glutathione Sepharose 4 Fast Flow resin (Yeasen Biotechnology). Elution was carried out using a buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5) and 5 mM reduced glutathione. Eluates were resolved via SDS–PAGE, transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes, and detected using anti-His tag antibodies. Results were visualized using the Tanon 5200 Multi chemiluminescence imaging system.

CoIP assay

The coding sequence of GhBIN2 was inserted into the 1300-Flag vector to produce the recombinant plasmid GhBIN2-Flag, and the coding sequence of GhTTL was cloned into the 2300-GFP vector to produce GhTTL-GFP. After sequence verification, the plasmids were transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101. Using an Agrobacterium-mediated transient expression system, bacterial suspensions were co-infiltrated into Nicotiana benthamiana leaves. Total protein was extracted from infiltrated leaves using a plant protein extraction kit and incubated with Protein G beads for 2 h. The crude protein extract served as the input sample, which was then incubated with anti-Flag M2 agarose for 2 h; non-specific components were removed by washing. Eluates were analyzed by western blot, and the results were visualized using the Tanon 5200 Multi chemiluminescence imaging system.

Split-luciferase complementation assay

The coding sequence of GhBIN2 was cloned into the CLuc vector, and the coding sequences of GhTTL and GhBES1 were cloned into the NLuc vector. Constructs (GhBIN2-CLuc, GhTTL-NLuc, and GhBES1-NLuc) and empty vector controls were each transformed into A. tumefaciens GV3101. Using an Agrobacterium-mediated transient expression system, suspensions were co-infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves. After 24 h of dark incubation followed by 48 h of light exposure, luciferase activity was detected using the Tanon 5200 Multi chemiluminescence imaging system.

BLI assay

The binding kinetics of the interaction between GhBIN2-GST and GhTTL-His were analyzed using the Octet RED96 system (ForteBio). Biosensors were pre-wetted in PBS/Tween buffer for 10 min. GhTTL-His was immobilized on the biosensors, and unbound protein was removed. GhBIN2-GST was subsequently introduced to the immobilized GhTTL-His in dilution buffer, and the association phase was monitored for 30 min. Dissociation was observed by washing the biosensors in PBS/Tween buffer for 30 min. The binding and dissociation curves were analyzed using a 1:1 binding model to calculate the association rate constant.

In vitro phosphorylation assays

Two complementary approaches were used to assess phosphorylation. First, protein phosphorylation status was determined using the Phos-Assay Acrylamide reagent (Vazyme) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. SDS–PAGE gels were prepared with Phos-Assay reagent and Mn2+ ions, then a standard western blot protocol was performed. Second, kinase activity was measured with the ADP-Glo Kinase Assay Kit (Promega). The 25 μl reaction mixture contained 2.5 μl of 10× phosphorylation buffer (200 mM Tris-HCl [pH 7.5], 500 mM KCl, and 100 mM MgCl2), 1 μl of 10 mM ultra-pure ATP, 5 μl each of GhBIN2-GST and either GhTTL-His or GhBES1-His, and 11.5 μl of nuclease-free water. After incubation, luminescence was measured using the Tanon 5200 Multi chemiluminescence imaging system.

Yeast three-hybrid assay

The coding sequence of GhBES1 was inserted into the pGADT7 vector to generate pGADT7-GhBES1. GhBIN2 was cloned into the pBridge vector to create pBridge-GhBIN2, and GhTTL was subsequently inserted to construct pBridge-GhBIN2-GhTTL. These plasmids were co-transformed into the Y2HGold yeast strain. Transformants were first selected on SD/−Trp/−Ura medium and then transferred to SD/−Ade/−His/−Leu/−Trp and SD/−Ade/−His/−Leu/−Trp/−Met dropout media. The methionine-induced expression of GhTTL was evaluated for its regulatory effect on the GhBIN2–GhBES1 interaction.

Subcellular localization analysis

The coding sequences of GhBIN2 and GhTTL were cloned into the pCAMBIA2300-GFP vector, and that of GhBES1 was cloned into the same vector with a His tag. Protoplasts were isolated from 14-day-old Arabidopsis seedling leaves using a commercial kit (Coolaber). Recombinant plasmids (GhBIN2-GFP, GhTTL-GFP, and GhBES1-His-GFP) were introduced into the protoplasts, and subcellular localization patterns were examined using laser scanning confocal microscopy.

In vitro ovule culture system

Cotton bolls (1 DPA) from wild-type and 35S:GhBIN2 lines were surface-sterilized by sequential immersion in 75% ethanol for 5 min and 95% ethanol for 5 s, followed by rinsing with sterile distilled water. Ovules were aseptically excised and cultured in BT liquid medium supplemented with either 1 μM BL (dissolved in ethanol) or 15 μM bikinin (dissolved in DMSO). Cultures were incubated at 30°C in complete darkness for 8 days (bikinin treatment) or 10 days (BL treatment).

BiFC analysis

The full-length coding sequences of GhBIN2 and GhTTL were independently cloned into BiFC vectors containing the N- or C-terminal fragments of GFP. Constructs driven by the CaMV 35S promoter were transformed into A. tumefaciens GV3101. Protein interactions were evaluated using Agrobacterium-mediated transient expression in N. benthamiana leaves. Fluorescence was analyzed using confocal microscopy after 24 h of dark incubation followed by 48 h of light exposure.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM). One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD post hoc test was used for comparisons among multiple groups, and independent-sample t-tests were used for two-group comparisons. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 (∗) or p < 0.01 (∗∗). Experiments were performed in triplicate, and data are presented as mean ± SEM. Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism version 8.0.
