Association between neutrophil-to-HDL cholesterol ratio and the risk of gout or hyperuricemia: evidence from NHANES 2007–2018
Authors: Yan Ma, Junxiao Wang, Mingqi Zhang, Fengzhen Li, Zujie Qin, Jianlong Shu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeutrophil-to-HDL-C ratio (NHR) has recently emerged as a composite biomarker integrating lipid metabolism and inflammatory status. Nevertheless, its potential association with the risk of gout or hyperuricemia remains inadequately explored. The present study aimed to examine the possible link between NHR and both conditions.MethodsThis study included 31,117 eligible adults from the 2007–2018 NHANES database in the United States. Participants were categorized into NHR quartiles, and weighted multivariate logistic regression along with restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses was performed to assess its association with gout and hyperuricemia risk. Subgroup analyses were performed to assess the robustness and heterogeneity of the association across different subpopulations. All analyses were weighted to guarantee the generalizability of the findings to the national population.ResultsA positive correlation was observed between NHR and the risk of both gout and hyperuricemia. As NHR levels increased, the proportion of participants with gout or hyperuricemia rose significantly—specifically, the prevalence of gout was 2.46%, 3.69%, 4.24%, and 5.79% (p < 0.001), and for hyperuricemia, it was 13.63%, 17.38%, 20.65%, and 26.08% (p < 0.001), respectively. Multivariable logistic regression indicated that, in the unadjusted Model 1 analysis, each 1-unit increase in NHR was associated with a 5.1% higher risk of gout (OR = 1.051, 95% CI 1.036–1.069, p < 0.001) and a 5.8% higher risk of hyperuricemia (OR = 1.058, 95% CI 1.051–1.066, p < 0.001). The positive association remained stable and statistically significant after adjusting for potential confounding variables. Further RCS analysis revealed a nonlinear trend in the relationship between NHR and both conditions, with a potential risk threshold of approximately 16, beyond which the risk of disease increased substantially. Additionally, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis showed that the NHR had better discriminatory performance than either HDL-C or NEU alone in predicting hyperuricemia (AUC = 0.682) and gout (AUC = 0.81).ConclusionNHR showed a significant association with the risk of gout and hyperuricemia, demonstrating a nonlinear dose–response pattern. NHR may serve as a promising inflammation–metabolism marker for the early identification of individuals at risk for uric acid-related disorders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03106-0.Keywords: NHR, NHANES, Gout, Hyperuricemia, Neutrophil, HDL-C

Introduction

Gout and hyperuricemia are prevalent metabolic disorders with a steadily increasing incidence in recent years, posing a significant global public health challenge [1, 2]. Hyperuricemia not only underlies the development of gout, but is also strongly associated with cardiovascular diseases, renal disorders, and metabolic syndrome [3–5]. Although serum uric acid concentration serves as a key diagnostic indicator, its predictive value for gout risk remains limited [6, 7], highlighting the need to explore novel biomarkers for early detection and risk stratification.

Inflammation plays a pivotal role in the initiation and progression of gout. Neutrophils are among the first immune cells to be activated during acute gout flares. Through the release of inflammatory mediators and the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), they intensify local inflammation and tissue injury [8, 9]. In parallel, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) is recognized for its anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and immunoregulatory properties. Reduced HDL-C levels are often linked to chronic low-grade inflammation and multiple metabolic disturbances [10–12]. In recent years, the neutrophil-to-HDL-C ratio (NHR) has emerged as a novel composite marker of systemic inflammation and metabolic status. It has demonstrated promising predictive value in cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and metabolic syndrome [13–16]. As a comprehensive biomarker of inflammation and metabolic status, NHR may be associated with gout or hyperuricemia. However, systematic investigations into the association between NHR and hyperuricemia or gout remain limited. Particularly, large-scale epidemiological evidence in population-based settings is still lacking. Thus, further studies are urgently needed to elucidate this potential relationship.

Accordingly, this study aimed to systematically assess the relationship between the NHR and the risk of gout or hyperuricemia, using large-scale data from the 2007–2018 NHANES cycles. We hypothesized that elevated NHR levels would be associated with a higher risk of gout or hyperuricemia, and that this association would remain statistically significant after adjustment for potential confounders. By leveraging a large sample size, multivariable adjustments, and subgroup analyses, this study aimed to investigate the epidemiological utility of NHR as a potential inflammation–metabolism biomarker and to provide novel evidence for the early detection and risk stratification of uric acid-related diseases.

Materials and methods

Data and sample sources

This study utilized data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS). NHANES is a nationally representative cross-sectional survey aiming to assess the health and nutritional status of the U.S. civilian population, including data on demographics, socioeconomic status, dietary intake, and health conditions. To ensure diversity and representativeness, NHANES adopts a stratified, multistage probability sampling design to recruit participants across the United States. The study protocol received approval from the NCHS Ethics Review Board of the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. NHANES data are publicly accessible at https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/.

This analysis focused on adult health data from the 2007–2018 NHANES cycles. The initial sample consisted of 59,842 participants. First, participants under the age of 20 were initially excluded. Subsequently, individuals with missing data on serum uric acid (UA), HDL-C, or neutrophil counts were excluded. Additionally, those lacking gout diagnosis information were excluded. Ultimately, 31,117 individuals were included in the final analysis, including 1472 individuals with gout and 6404 with hyperuricemia. Outcome variables

In the NHANES program, serum uric acid concentrations were quantified using the timed endpoint method on the DxC800 analyzer. According to established criteria, hyperuricemia was defined as a serum uric acid concentration ≥ 7.0 mg/dL in males and ≥ 6.0 mg/dL in females. Gout diagnosis was determined based on participants’ self-reported responses—specifically, whether a physician had ever informed them of a gout diagnosis. Participants were coded as 1 (diagnosed) or 0 (not diagnosed) depending on whether they met the diagnostic criteria.

Covariates

Guided by existing literature and clinical relevance, this study incorporated multiple confounding variables, including age, sex, race/ethnicity, education level, family income, poverty–income ratio (PIR), marital status, BMI, systolic and diastolic blood pressure (SBP, DBP), HbA1c, fasting blood glucose (FBG), non-HDL-C, LDL-C, triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), smoking status, alcohol consumption, hypertension, and diabetes. Race/ethnicity was classified into six groups: Mexican American, Non-Hispanic Asian, Non-Hispanic Black, Non-Hispanic White, other Hispanic, and other or multiracial. Educational attainment was categorized into three levels: high school or less, some college, and college graduate or higher. Marital status was classified as never married, married or living with a partner, and widowed/divorced. PIR was divided into three categories: < 1.30, 1.30–3.49, and ≥ 3.50. Hypertension was identified based on either a physician diagnosis or the use of antihypertensive medications. Smoking status was defined as having smoked at least 100 cigarettes in one's lifetime; participants answering “yes” were classified as smokers. Diabetes was diagnosed if participants met at least one of the following criteria: (1) HbA1c ≥ 6.5%; (2) FPG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L; (3) a physician diagnosis of diabetes; or (4) current use of glucose-lowering medications or insulin. All covariates were extracted from the NHANES database and standardized before being included in the analytical models to minimize the potential confounding effects on the study outcomes.

Statistical methods

All analyses in this study accounted for NHANES’s complex survey design, applying weighted statistical methods to ensure the representativeness and robustness of the findings. NHANES achieves national representativeness through a complex multistage probability sampling design. In accordance with recommendations from the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), we applied sampling weights (WTMECPRP), pseudo-strata (SDMVSTRA), and pseudo-clusters (SDMVPSU) to account for the complex survey design.

Participants were initially categorized based on gout or hyperuricemia status and further stratified into NHR quartiles. For continuous variables with normal distribution, weighted Student’s t-tests were used for two-group comparisons, and weighted one-way ANOVA for comparisons across multiple groups. For non-normally distributed continuous variables, weighted Mann–Whitney U tests and Kruskal–Wallis tests were employed for two- and multi-group comparisons, respectively. Comparisons of categorical variables were conducted using weighted Chi-square tests. Descriptive statistics were reported as weighted means ± standard deviations for continuous variables and unweighted counts with weighted percentages for categorical variables.

To further explore the association between NHR and gout or hyperuricemia risk within population subgroups, subgroup analyses were performed across key categorical variables, including sex, race/ethnicity, educational level, PIR, marital status, smoking status, hypertension, and diabetes. Multivariable logistic regression models were used to assess differences in the predictive association of NHR with gout or hyperuricemia across subgroups.

To assess the risk association between NHR and gout or hyperuricemia, three multivariable logistic regression models were developed in this study. Prior to model development, variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis was performed to evaluate multicollinearity among covariates. Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 adjusted for sex, age, and race/ethnicity. Model 3 additionally adjusted for education level, family income, PIR, marital status, BMI, SBP, DBP, HbA1c, FBG, non-HDL-C, LDL-C, TG, TC, smoking status, alcohol consumption, hypertension, and diabetes. In the regression analysis, NHR was assessed both as a continuous variable and by quartile categorization. Results of each model were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

To examine potential nonlinear relationships between NHR and disease risk, restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was conducted. The “smart-filtered restricted cubic spline” and “threshold effect” methods were applied to determine the location of knots and threshold turning points. Finally, sensitivity analyses were performed to validate the robustness of the logistic regression and RCS models. These included adjusting various covariate combinations, excluding extreme values, and performing stepwise regression analysis to assess the consistency of model estimates under different conditions. A two-tailed p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant in all analyses. All statistical analyses were conducted using DecisionLinnc version 1.0 [17], and all results were repeatedly validated to ensure the accuracy and robustness of the analyses.

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants with and without gout or hyperuricemia

This study included 31,117 participants, with a mean age of 47.57 years. Among the overall sample, 48.29% were male (n = 15,105), and 51.71% were female (n = 16,012). Compared with individuals without gout, participants with gout had significantly elevated age, BMI, SBP, HbA1c, FPG, TG, UA, neutrophil count, and NHR levels (p < 0.001), whereas LDL-C, TC, and HDL-C levels were significantly lower (p < 0.001). Additionally, the proportions of sex, race/ethnicity, marital status, BMI, smoking status, alcohol use, hypertension, and diabetes were significantly different between the two groups (p < 0.05).

Compared with individuals without hyperuricemia, those with hyperuricemia had significantly higher levels of age, BMI, SBP, DBP, HbA1c, FPG, TG, UA, neutrophil count, and NHR (p < 0.001). Conversely, LDL-C and HDL-C levels were significantly lower (p < 0.001). Moreover, the distributions of race/ethnicity, educational level, PIR, marital status, BMI, smoking status, alcohol consumption, hypertension, and diabetes also significantly differed between the groups (p < 0.001). Relationship between NHR quartiles and baseline characteristic

To investigate the dose–response relationship between NHR and participants’ baseline characteristics, participants were categorized into four quartiles (Q1–Q4) according to NHR values. Results showed that, compared with participants in the lower NHR quartiles, those in the higher NHR quartiles showed significantly increased levels of BMI, SBP, DBP, HbA1c, FPG, non-HDL-C, TG, UA, and neutrophil count (p < 0.001), while LDL-C, TC, and HDL-C levels were significantly decreased (p < 0.001). Additionally, the distribution of sex, race/ethnicity, educational level, marital status, PIR, BMI, smoking status, diabetes, and hypertension differed significantly across NHR quartiles (p < 0.05). Of particular note, the prevalence of gout and hyperuricemia increased significantly with ascending NHR quartiles, with respective rates of 2.46%, 3.69%, 4.24%, and 5.79% for gout, and 13.63%, 17.38%, 20.65%, and 26.08% for hyperuricemia (p < 0.001). Association between NHR and gout or hyperuricemia risk

To further investigate the association between NHR and the risk of gout or hyperuricemia, multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed (see Table 3). In the unadjusted model (Model 1), NHR as a continuous variable showed a significant positive association with the risk of gout and hyperuricemia. Specifically, each one-unit increase in NHR was associated with a 5.1% higher risk of gout (OR = 1.051, 95% CI 1.036–1.069, p < 0.001) and a 5.8% higher risk of hyperuricemia (OR = 1.058, 95% CI 1.051–1.066, p < 0.001). When stratified by quartiles, participants in the highest quartile (Q4) exhibited a 2.43-fold increased risk of gout (OR = 2.434, 95% CI 1.862–3.182, p < 0.001) and a 2.24-fold increased risk of hyperuricemia (OR = 2.235, 95% CI 2.011–2.484, p < 0.001), compared with those in the lowest quartile (Q1).

Restricted cubic spline analysis

To investigate the nonlinear relationship between NHR and the risk of gout or hyperuricemia, a restricted cubic spline (RCS) model was used (see Fig. 4). Results indicated significant nonlinear associations between NHR and the risk of both gout and hyperuricemia (p < 0.001). This nonlinear association was consistently observed in the unadjusted model (Model 1), partially adjusted model (Model 2), and fully adjusted model (Model 3) (p < 0.001).

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses of the relationship between NHR and the risk of gout or hyperuricemia under different modeling conditions. A–C illustrate the association between NHR and hyperuricemia risk, and panels D–F depict the association between NHR and gout risk. In each panel, the models are as follows: unadjusted (A and D), partially adjusted (B and E), and fully adjusted (C and F)

In hyperuricemia, RCS analysis revealed that the three knot positions in each model were 3.94, 7.93, and 15.26. When NHR exceeded 15.26, the odds ratio (OR) surpassed 1, indicating a markedly increased risk of hyperuricemia. Further threshold effect analysis demonstrated that the NHR inflection points in Models 1–3 were 16.00, 15.79, and 17.67, respectively, suggesting that the impact of NHR on hyperuricemia risk progressively increased beyond these thresholds.

For gout, the three knots were located at 3.94, 7.93, and 15.26. Further analysis identified NHR inflection points of 15.71, 16.67, and 11.61 for Models 1 through 3, respectively, suggesting that NHR exhibited an increasing effect on gout risk once the threshold was exceeded, across different models. These findings highlight the complex dose–response relationship between NHR and the risk of gout and hyperuricemia, providing important theoretical evidence to support clinical risk assessment and the development of intervention strategies.

Sensitivity and robustness analyses

To assess the robustness of the models, sensitivity analyses were performed on both restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis and logistic regression models. For logistic regression models, key covariates such as BMI, LDL-C, TG, alcohol consumption, hypertension, and diabetes were progressively removed. In addition, extreme values and outliers from related variables were excluded. Despite these adjustments, the association between NHR and the risk of gout or hyperuricemia remained statistically significant (p < 0.05), confirming the robustness of the model estimates.

In the RCS analysis, regardless of whether BMI, HbA1c, FBG, non-HDL-C, LDL-C, TG, and TC were randomly added or removed, the nonlinear associations between NHR and the risk of gout or hyperuricemia remained statistically significant (p < 0.001). These findings further support the consistent association between NHR and the risk of gout or hyperuricemia, suggesting that this relationship remained robust under various analytic conditions.

Predictive value of the NHR for hyperuricemia and gout

To assess the discriminatory performance of the NHR in identifying hyperuricemia and gout, we generated receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and calculated the area under the curve (AUC), as shown in Fig. 5.

Predictive performance of HDL-C, NEU, and NHR for hyperuricemia and gout (ROC curves). A shows the predictive performance of each marker for hyperuricemia, and B for gout. All models were adjusted for the full set of covariates

For hyperuricemia prediction (Fig. 5A), the AUCs of HDL-C and NEU were 0.598 and 0.543, respectively, while the AUC of the NHR was 0.682, indicating superior discriminatory ability of the ratio over the individual markers. For gout prediction (Fig. 5B), the AUCs for HDL-C and NEU were 0.589 and 0.785, respectively, while the NHR achieved an AUC of 0.81, further demonstrating its enhanced predictive performance. DeLong’s test confirmed that the NHR outperformed either HDL-C or NEU alone in both hyperuricemia and gout prediction (p < 0.001); detailed results are presented in Table S1.

Overall, the NHR exhibited better discrimination than either HDL-C or NEU alone in identifying both hyperuricemia and gout. These findings suggest that the ratio may serve as a convenient and effective biomarker for risk prediction, with promising potential for future research and clinical application.

Discussion

This study utilized large-scale population-based data from the 2007–2018 NHANES cycles and systematically assessed the association between NHR and the risk of gout and hyperuricemia. The results indicated that elevated NHR levels were significantly associated with a higher risk of gout and hyperuricemia, and this association remained robust after adjusting for multiple covariates. Furthermore, restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis revealed significant nonlinear associations between NHR and the risks of gout and hyperuricemia, indicating a possible threshold effect where disease risk increased significantly beyond a certain NHR level. These findings not only deepen the current understanding of the relationship between NHR and hyperuricemia and gout but also provide novel evidence for further exploration of the role of inflammation–metabolic imbalance in abnormal uric acid metabolism.

The development of gout and hyperuricemia involves multiple mechanisms, including uric acid production, excretion, and inflammatory responses. Recent evidence suggests that inflammation plays a critical role in the progression from hyperuricemia to gout [18–20]. Neutrophils constitute a key component of the innate immune system and massively infiltrate the joint cavity during acute gout attacks, releasing proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6) and forming neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), which further amplify the local inflammatory response [21–24]. Moreover, neutrophil-derived reactive oxygen species (ROS) and proteases can promote joint tissue damage and perpetuate inflammatory amplification [25, 26]. In this study, elevated NHR was significantly associated with an increased risk of both gout and hyperuricemia, possibly reflecting a state of chronic low-grade systemic inflammation. Chronic inflammation has been recognized as an independent risk factor for both hyperuricemia and gout [27].

On the other hand, HDL-C is not only a crucial regulator of lipid metabolism, but also plays an essential role in anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and uric acid regulatory functions [28, 29]. In addition, studies have demonstrated that HDL-C can inhibit the production of proinflammatory cytokines, improve endothelial function, and maintain renal health, thereby playing a protective role in various physiological processes [30–32]. However, reduced HDL-C levels impair the body’s anti-inflammatory capacity, resulting in impaired uric acid excretion and increased accumulation and deposition of urate [33, 34]. Therefore, NHR, as a ratio of pro-inflammatory neutrophils to anti-inflammatory metabolic factors (HDL-C), may provide a more comprehensive reflection of the systemic inflammation-metabolism balance and may play a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of hyperuricemia and gout.

Our subgroup analyses revealed that the association between NHR and hyperuricemia was stronger in participants without hypertension or diabetes, with higher ORs observed compared to their counterparts. This finding may reflect the differential pathophysiological background between these groups. In individuals without established metabolic disorders, elevated NHR may serve as a more sensitive marker of subclinical inflammatory-metabolic imbalance that precedes overt disease. Conversely, in those with hypertension or diabetes, systemic inflammation and lipid abnormalities are often already present and influenced by disease progression or medical treatment, potentially attenuating the incremental predictive value of NHR. These results suggest that NHR may be particularly valuable for identifying early-stage or high-risk individuals in seemingly healthy populations, warranting further prospective validation.

Another key finding of this study was the nonlinear association between NHR and the risk of gout and hyperuricemia. Specifically, when NHR exceeds a threshold of approximately 16, the risk of disease increases markedly. This finding suggests that at lower NHR levels, the body’s inflammation-metabolism regulatory mechanisms may be sufficient to maintain uric acid homeostasis. However, once the NHR level crosses this threshold, the increased inflammatory burden may overwhelm the body’s compensatory capacity, thereby accelerating urate deposition and ultimately contributing to the onset of gout or hyperuricemia. The existence of this threshold effect indicates that NHR may serve as a potential biomarker for early disease screening and risk stratification, thereby offering valuable guidance for precision clinical intervention.

In comparison with previous studies, this is the first study to systematically examine the association between NHR and gout and hyperuricemia in a large-scale population. Prior research has shown that increased neutrophil counts (NEU) are associated with acute gout attacks, while lower HDL-C levels are associated with a higher risk of hyperuricemia [35, 36]. However, prior studies primarily focused on NEU or HDL-C in isolation, without fully evaluating their combined interaction. By incorporating NHR as a composite indicator, this study offered a more comprehensive perspective, further supporting the critical role of the inflammation–metabolism axis in the development of hyperuricemia and gout. In addition, existing research on NHR has predominantly focused on cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and metabolic syndrome, whereas the present findings extend the potential utility of NHR, indicating its possible predictive value in metabolism-related inflammatory conditions.

This study was conducted using a nationally representative large-scale dataset and is the first to systematically assess the association between NHR and the risk of gout and hyperuricemia. The findings indicate that NHR, as a composite indicator of inflammation and metabolism, may have potential clinical utility in early disease identification and risk stratification. Nevertheless, some limitations should be acknowledged. First, as a cross-sectional study, causal relationships between NHR and gout or hyperuricemia cannot be inferred. Therefore, future prospective cohort studies are warranted to further verify the predictive value of NHR in the onset and progression of these conditions. Second, the diagnosis of gout relied on self-reported information, which may introduce recall bias or misclassification, potentially affecting the accuracy of outcome ascertainment. Future studies are recommended to adopt standardized clinical diagnostic criteria and integrate laboratory and imaging data to enhance the accuracy of disease identification and the validity of study conclusions. Although this study adjusted for a range of potential confounders, important variables such as dietary patterns, family history, medication use, and inflammatory markers—which may influence NHR levels and disease risk—were not accounted for. Future studies may integrate lifestyle information, multi-omics data, and inflammation-associated biomarkers to comprehensively explore the potential mechanisms linking NHR with gout and hyperuricemia.

Conclusions

Based on nationally representative population data, this study found that elevated NHR levels were significantly associated with increased risk of gout and hyperuricemia. This finding suggests that NHR may act as a potential biomarker reflecting inflammation-metabolism status and could be used for early risk identification and stratified assessment of uric acid-related diseases.
