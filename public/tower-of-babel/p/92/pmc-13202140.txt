Decolonising Neglected Tropical Diseases research in Africa: insights, challenges and promising practices
Authors: Cristina Juan Jimenez, Arantzazu Quintana Jorge, Berta Mendiguren de la Vega, Ewerton Luiz Figueiredo Moura da Silva, Gloria Dada Chechet, Irene Cerdá Lentijo, Anna Giné-March
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractColonial legacies have significantly shaped global health, creating power imbalances between the Global North and the Global South, which in turn affect research, priorities and health interventions, particularly in the field of Neglected Tropical Diseases (NTDs). Decolonisation aims to promote equity, local leadership and inclusivity. Despite growing global attention to NTDs, the governance and research landscape has yet to be interrogated from a decolonial perspective. This paper offers a novel contribution by analysing historical and structural asymmetries through a literature review and insights from a panel discussion at the XII Iberian Congress of African Studies. Decolonising global health research requires equitable collaborations, recognition of local leadership and an understanding of how the logic of race impacts inequity. Key challenges include power imbalances in funding and authorship, limited leadership opportunities for researchers from the Global South, extractive practices that overlook local contexts and intersectionality and persistent barriers to participation in global forums. Progress can be made by promoting holistic approaches, supporting research exchanges, reintegrating trained professionals into local systems and advocating for community-driven, interdisciplinary research. Strategies such as fostering equitable partnerships, strengthening research infrastructure, engaging local stakeholders and prioritising social acceptability in intervention design are essential for African countries to address systemic inequities in knowledge production and utilisation. Grounded in an analysis of power imbalances in NTD research, this paper proposes pathways towards more inclusive, locally led and context-sensitive approaches that advance epistemic justice and sustainable health solutions.Keywords: Global Health, Africa

Introduction

Global health and research have historically been shaped by colonial structures that perpetuate unequal power dynamics between the Global North and Global South. These inequalities continue to influence knowledge production, research priorities and health interventions. Decolonisation in global health and research is a critical approach that challenges historical and contemporary power dynamics, inequalities and dependencies in the production of knowledge and the implementation of health policies. The decolonisation movement has gained momentum, driven by calls for inclusive research agendas. However, in the context of Neglected Tropical Diseases (NTDs), research and policy have been little examined from a decolonial perspective, despite ongoing evidence of Global North–South power imbalances shaping priorities, funding and knowledge production.1

NTDs are a diverse group of diseases caused by various pathogens, with major health, social and economic impacts.2,4 While concentrated in impoverished tropical regions, they affect over 1 billion people globally, with 1.6 billion needing intervention. The group includes more than 21 diseases, such as buruli ulcer, lymphatic filariasis, noma, leishmaniasis, leprosy and mycetoma.4 The concept of NTDs emerged in 1997, when the World Health Organization (WHO) grouped certain infections to highlight the health needs of impoverished populations. It later gained global prominence through support from the Rockefeller Foundation and European partners, who advocated for low-cost interventions.5 6 Between 2005 and 2006, the first peer-reviewed publications employing NTDs as a MeSH term appeared in PubMed, coinciding with the creation of a dedicated WHO Department of NTDs. In 2006, WHO launched the first global initiative to control and eliminate NTDs, consolidating the category.2

While this marked a milestone in advocacy and funding, existing literature remains largely confined to equity-based frameworks focused on correcting disparities. This article departs from that paradigm by advancing a decolonial critique of the historical, political and epistemic conditions that constitute NTDs. It critically examines NTD research, funding and interventions within enduring colonial legacies and structural power imbalances, framing these diseases as products of colonial domination, racialised hierarchies and global capitalism and thereby reconfiguring the field’s foundations.

This paper draws on two complementary sources: a literature review using the terms ‘decolonisation’ and ‘global health’ to identify relevant studies, reports and policy documents, alongside insights from a panel at the XII Iberian Congress of African Studies (Barcelona, January 2025). The panel, organised by the Group of Studies on Culture and Domination and the Group of Studies on African Societies (GECID-GESA), included specialists from diverse areas of NTD research, although representation from frontline practitioners and policymakers was limited. The discussion examined decolonisation initiatives, barriers to inclusive NTD research and future roles for institutions and funders. Discussions were recorded, thematically analysed and integrated with the literature review to blend empirical and documented perspectives.

Colonial legacies in global health and the emergence of decolonial approaches

Colonialism can be defined as the state-sponsored creation of inequality that benefits one group at the expense of another, with significant implications for global health, a field focused on improving health and equity worldwide.7,10 These legacy structures shape global health by privileging certain ways of knowing, while colonial structures continue to concentrate knowledge, resources and agenda-setting in historically dominant centres.

These colonial structures not only lead institutions in research but also dominate institutions in education, philanthropy, commerce and international governance.18 11,14 Beyond that, they influence the broader geopolitical dimensions often referred to as the Global North and the Global South. The Global North comprises high-income, industrialised nations historically empowered through colonial and economic expansion, while the Global South includes historically marginalised countries shaped by colonial legacies and structural inequities, often identified by socioeconomic indicators such as the Human Development Index.15 While analytically useful, this binary can also risk oversimplifying complex transnational inequalities and reproducing the hierarchies it seeks to critique; thus, it should be understood as a heuristic grounded in historical processes rather than a fixed geographical division.9 15

The colonial logic of domination was embedded in scientific practices, particularly in the emergence of tropical medicine—the precursor to the modern field of global health—established in the late 19th and early 20th centuries by colonial European officers to protect settlers, administrators and military personnel in African and Asian colonies.57 8 10 16,18 These officers sought prestige by studying diseases in indigenous groups while neglecting sanitation and overlooking health determinants and health equity, exemplifying early forms of epistemic violence that ignored local knowledge.78 10,13 16 19 The term ‘tropical’ itself is a misnomer, as these diseases are not confined to tropical regions but rather reflect conditions of poverty, marginalisation and structural inequities that colonial medicine systematically obscured.22

Colonial tropical medicine prioritised the health and economic interests of colonisers, reinforcing racial and class hierarchies.22 It became institutionalised through the creation of schools (such as those in Liverpool, London, Hamburg and Lisbon), specialised journals and professional societies that legitimised and disseminated colonial medical knowledge.23 24 This institutional framework structured medical practices and entrenched global asymmetries, systematically subordinating or excluding knowledge from colonised regions.

After independence, states inherited weak health systems and remained dependent on Western-centric policies, perpetuating colonial-era inequalities in governance and knowledge production.25 Despite such formal independence, the structural legacy of colonial health governance persisted, reappearing in new forms of institutional dominance by global organisations such as the WHO and the World Bank (WB). These organisations perpetuated colonial hierarchies not merely through policy but through epistemic control, deciding what counted as valid science and whose knowledge was recognised. Additionally, they shape research agendas, control funding, concentrate studies in Northern metropolitan centres, emphasise data-driven metrics and marginalise researchers and communities from the Global South.716 20 26,31

Although anticolonial movements across Asia, Africa and Latin America gained strength after World War II, the international health system continued to reinforce colonial hierarchies.10 16 20 27 32 During such a century and previously, organisations like the League of Nations and the WHO adopted technocratic disease control approaches that ignored structural inequalities, reinforced colonial perspectives and marginalised indigenous health systems. Furthermore, most international health initiatives have focused on vertical programmes targeting diseases such as malaria and polio, achieving some success but largely ignoring the broader context and social determinants of health.13 20 Since then, the concept of ‘international health’ promoted by cooperation agencies prompted cuts in public spending and the migration of young professionals seeking better opportunities abroad.10 These dynamics illustrate the persistence of neocolonial relations under the guise of ‘development assistance’, where conditionality and austerity reproduce dependency.

Building on neocolonial dynamics, global health policy sought reform through initiatives like the Alma-Ata Declaration (1978), which advocates ‘health for all’. However, structural adjustment programmes in the 1980s and 1990s, led by the WB and the International Monetary Fund (IMF), cut public health spending, leaving non-governmental organisations, private actors and donors to fill the resulting gaps. This fragmentation reflects the persistent coloniality of global health, where power, knowledge and resources remain unevenly distributed.

In response to these inequities, the Decolonising Global Health (DGH) movement critically challenged the concept of global health.2 4 9 12 20 23 25 26 Although this movement gained traction in the 21st century, its roots lie in earlier anticolonial struggles questioning whose knowledge counts and who benefits from health interventions. This movement, prioritised by Global North institutions such as the National Institute of Health Fogarty International Centre and the London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine (LSHTM), highlights a paradox: efforts to elevate Global South voices can inadvertently reinforce the perception that Global South practitioners lack meaningful leadership.18 26 33 In contrast, scholars and activists from the Global South have long advocated for epistemic autonomy, knowledge plurality and dismantling structural dependency, marking an enduring first step towards decolonisation.

Postcolonial frameworks and structural inequalities in the field of NTD research

Building on earlier efforts, the 2012 London Declaration—signed in a Global North capital—committed key actors to eliminate 10 NTDs by 2020, leading to significant drug donations and expanded mass drug administration programmes. While the Declaration marked a new level of global commitment, its centralised decision-making processes and philanthropic framing reflect the continued control of health agendas by the Global North. A subsequent milestone came in 2015, when the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) included NTDs under Goal 3, focusing on ensuring healthy lives and promoting well-being for all. Following the SDG analysis, the NTD community worked with the WHO to develop a 2021–2030 road map, introduced by the 73rd World Health Assembly in November 2020, outlining strategies to prevent, control, eliminate and eradicate NTDs.5 Building on this framework, the Kigali Declaration on NTDs, launched on 27 January 2022, by Uniting to Combat NTDs, was endorsed at the 23 June Kigali Summit, where global leaders reaffirmed their commitment to tackling these diseases.

Though these initiatives mark significant progress in NTDs, they continue to expose persistent structural hierarchies. Often launched in African or Asian capitals, these initiatives—funded and shaped by Northern institutions—reflect progress while reinforcing Global North dominance over health priorities and funding.34 All in all, this reflects a philanthropic-neocolonial logic where ‘partnership’ often translates into dependency, reinforcing asymmetrical flows of power, knowledge and credit.

The dominance of NTD research in the Global North, despite the heavier disease burden in the Global South, highlights this imbalance.9 28 35 This manifests as structural inequalities in collaboration, colonial patterns in knowledge and funding, extractive scientific practices and barriers to equitable participation and knowledge circulation, as detailed below.

Research partnerships often reproduce the language of capacity building, yet decision-making power and authorship remain externally anchored, as most private partnerships are frequently based in and led by actors from the Global North.9 12 13 36 Additionally, Global South countries often face significant challenges, including limited research capacity, inadequate infrastructure, weak networks and insufficient administrative support, which hinder project implementation and sustainability.13 17 36 Furthermore, limited research prioritisation, instability and weak political will in many Global South governments exacerbate these challenges.10 17 27 33 36 This epistemic blind spot reflects a hierarchy between biomedical and indigenous knowledge, where local systems are dismissed or instrumentalised and funding mechanisms push governments to follow externally defined priorities over community-driven health agendas.

In terms of human resources, researchers from the Global North often gain access to laboratory and field settings in the Global South, thereby benefiting from research experience and data collection opportunities.2026 29 35,37 This research model, often framed as ‘collaboration’, perpetuates epistemic extraction of knowledge generated in the Global South while credit stays in the Global North.36

Consequently, Global North researchers often gain recognition as ‘Global Health Experts’ after brief engagements, while Global South researchers rarely receive similar acknowledgement. This asymmetry exposes the coloniality of expertise, where legitimacy depends on institutional affiliation rather than local knowledge, often overlooking social, cultural and intersectional factors that shape vulnerability and access to care within colonial legacies.9 13 29 33 36 Moreover, the ongoing ‘brain drain’ of Global South professionals to Northern institutions—driven by better pay, resources and prestige—reflects systemic inequities that perpetuate dependency.9 12 17 28 29 35 36

In terms of publications, citations, lead authorship and editorial board members, scholars from the Global South remain under-represented.810 26 36 38,40 Authorship hierarchies remain one of the clearest indicators of epistemic inequality, where intellectual credit and narrative control are unequally distributed. Global North institutions prioritise the publication of findings in high-impact journals that are often not open-access, focusing primarily on their academic success.13 17 26 40 This reinforces a prestige economy of science that privileges visibility over reciprocity. Most documents are published in English, resulting in the exclusion of local or regional languages that are not English.12 36 41 Similarly, travel costs and conference fees at international forums are often prohibitively high, and the visa process in some countries can be complex.12 26 These mobility barriers not only restrict participation but also reproduce a geography of exclusion where knowledge from the Global South is consumed rather than co-produced. The lack of diversity in team composition, particularly with African representation, is also reflected in most grant proposals, whose agendas focus on short-term impact.12 Short-termism reflects donor-driven priorities incompatible with systemic, community-led approaches required to address social determinants of NTDs.

Pathways for equity in global health and NTD research

As highlighted in the preceding sections, health research is a powerful tool for generating knowledge, informing political decisions and advocating for improved population health; yet it is also shaped by power structures, inequalities and sociopolitical contexts. Recognising research as both a site of knowledge production and of power negotiation is essential for understanding how epistemic hierarchies persist under the guise of neutrality and scientific rigour. In this sense, exploring decolonisation means pushing back against global power structures and transferring control from the Global North to the Global South.16 33

Beyond geographic redistribution, decolonisation calls for epistemic pluralism—valuing diverse ways of knowing, including indigenous and community-based knowledge—and focuses on who designs methods, controls data and benefits.8 16 19 29 42 Over the past two decades, these theoretical discussions have increasingly informed practical initiatives aimed at transforming research governance and collaboration models. These efforts seek to promote stronger international collaborations, focusing on partnerships and prioritising projects led by Global South researchers, while addressing social, political, economic and cultural determinants.9 16 35 41 43

Achieving a decolonial transformation and equity in global health requires redistributing epistemic authority, promoting multilingual and community-led research and valuing diverse, locally grounded knowledge systems. Meaningful community participation through the research process, supported by intersectional and multidisciplinary approaches, ensures that studies reflect local realities. Additionally, active involvement of local authorities and stakeholders—such as religious leaders and self-help groups—fosters legitimacy, sustainability and trust.29 41 44

Promoting open science is thus essential both to support research on locally defined health challenges, particularly in African contexts, and to foster epistemic justice by making knowledge production more inclusive and responsive to historically marginalised communities.40 However, to make such initiatives truly transformative, it is equally important to address structural barriers within the Global South—limited opportunities for personal development, inadequate working conditions and weak health systems—that perpetuate dependency and brain drain.45 Complementary measures include valuing social relevance and local authorship in citation metrics, as well as recognising Global South researchers by waiving publication fees or requiring local authorship and promoting preprint publications to provide free, rapid access to research while avoiding high publishing costs.18 40 46 47

Sustainable decolonisation also depends on equitable capacity strengthening. This involves long-term collaborations, reflexive training for Global North researchers and mentorship that respects contextual differences while promoting shared decision-making and mutual learning.9 12 21 26 28 29 32 33 36 37 40 The WHO Special Programme for Research and Training in Tropical Diseases (TDR) and the Sub-Saharan African Network for TB/HIV Research Excellence (SANTHE) Consortium, funded by the African Academy of Science, offer models for capacity strengthening, directly empowering African-based research, although they may still reproduce dependencies or prioritise externally set agendas.6 36 48 Another recommendation involves investing in funding and salary support for Global South-led studies on NTDs, alongside developing infrastructure that reintegrates researchers trained abroad within Ministry of Health systems while preserving their autonomy over research agendas.26 32 37 41

Recent initiatives, such as the Leprosy Research Initiative (LRI) RESILIENTD grant, the Appel à projets de recherche sur les Maladies Tropicales Négligées de la peau and the European and Developing Countries Clinical Trials Partnership (EDCTP), highlight the increasing focus on international collaboration to advance research on skin-related NTDs in sub-Saharan Africa.49 The involvement of local civil society organisations is another encouraging step to ensure that the voices of marginalised populations affected by NTDs are not only heard but also prioritised in both research and intervention planning.50

Finally, international frameworks, including the Global Health Decolonisation Movement in Africa and the WHO’s equity strategies, promote fairer health governance.10 18 35 36 Beginning with the Paris Declaration (2005) and continuing through the Accra (2008) and Busan (2011) agreements, international efforts increasingly emphasised the alignment of external aid with national priorities.10 13 18 29 These forms of engagement should be grounded in mutual accountability and long-term relational ethics, avoiding extractive or performative collaborations that lack genuine commitment. Some ways to enact such engagement include showing support for country strategies, involving authorities in proposals and joining policy dialogues. Furthermore, thematic funding and open-access support offered by funding agencies are crucial to incentivise research that addresses African challenges.38

Despite these academic and policy advances, epistemic hierarchies remain deeply embedded within funding structures themselves, demonstrating that decolonisation must also address how excellence and impact are defined. Achieving truly equitable research, therefore, requires decolonising funding criteria to value social relevance, local authorship and epistemic diversity as key indicators of excellence. In addition to the structural and systemic elements discussed, advancing decolonisation in global health research also requires practical measures that can be implemented directly within calls for proposals and research projects. These measures involve designing studies and interventions that are inclusive, context-aware and responsive to the realities of affected populations. By integrating strategies such as community engagement, local leadership, intersectionality considerations, open-access dissemination and capacity-strengthening initiatives, research on NTDs can move beyond abstract principles and contribute to more equitable, participatory and locally grounded knowledge production.

Conclusion

The decolonisation of research in the context of NTDs remains an urgent and evolving challenge, revealing persistent structural and epistemic inequities in knowledge production, dissemination and application. Beyond geographic redistribution of research leadership from the Global North to the Global South, decolonisation requires epistemic pluralism, recognising indigenous, community-based and experiential knowledge as equally valid alongside biomedical paradigms.

Equitable global health research necessitates addressing power asymmetries in governance, authorship and the allocation of funding. Capacity strengthening through mentorship, context-sensitive training and long-term collaborations is essential to cultivate sustainable leadership within endemic regions. Research impact should be assessed not only through traditional metrics but also by policy influence, community engagement and the strengthening of local health systems.

Effective decolonisation further demands inclusive, locally led studies, community participation, intersectional approaches and engagement with civil society and local authorities to ensure research reflects the needs and priorities of affected populations. Funding mechanisms and international initiatives must align with these principles, prioritising local leadership, social relevance and epistemic diversity over performative indicators of excellence. Only by dismantling structural, epistemic and geographic hierarchies can global health advance towards genuine epistemic justice, with knowledge that is participatory, contextually grounded and socially accountable.
