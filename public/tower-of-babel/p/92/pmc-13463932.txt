vetInspECT: a platform for the multicentric evaluation of treatment response after electrochemotherapy in veterinary oncology - feline tumors
Authors: Nataša Tozon Mask, Urša Lampreht Tratar, Nina Milevoj, Francesca De Terlizzi, Tanya Birk, Gregor Sersa, Maja Cemazar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractElectrochemotherapy (ECT) is a local ablative treatment and in veterinary oncology, evidence supporting ECT efficacy is mainly derived from case reports and small case series, underscoring the need for standardized, multicentric data collection comparable to human oncology registries. The primary aim of this study was to establish VetInspECT, a veterinary clinical registry adapted from the human InspECT platform, to enable standardized reporting and multicentre data collection for ECT. The secondary aim was to evaluate antitumor effectiveness using data from the single centre that initiated the registry. VetInspECT was designed to systematically record patient characteristics, tumor features, treatment parameters, and follow-up outcomes in animals treated with ECT. Retrospective data spanning from 2012 to 2024 from feline patients treated at a Slovenian center were entered into the database. Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) was the most frequently treated tumor (66%), followed by mast cell tumors (MCT; 12%) and other carcinomas and sarcomas. Complete response rates were highest in MCT (95%), followed by non-SCC carcinomas (90%), SCC (74%), and sarcomas (33%). Tumor size, clinical stage, anatomical location, and mucosal involvement significantly affected treatment response and progression-free survival. In conclusion, VetInspECT represents an important step toward standardized ECT reporting in veterinary oncology and supports the use of shared registries to enable robust multicentric analyses and broader clinical integration of ECT.Keywords: Electrochemotherapy, Cats, Veterinary oncology, Clinical study

Introduction

Electrochemotherapy (ECT) is a local ablative therapy of different tumour types in different animal species [1]. It consists of intratumoral or intravenous application of specific cytostatic such as bleomycin or cisplatin and application of electric pulses to the tumor [2]. In ECT the main mechanism of action is electroporation as drug delivery system for poorly permeant drugs such as bleomycin or cisplatin. Consequently the tumor cell membrane becomes more permeable which leads to increase of drug influx into the cells and potentiates its antitumor effect [3].

In veterinary oncology several case series and case reports have been published evaluating the safety and tumour effectiveness in mostly cats and dogs. The prevalent tumor type treated with ECT in cats was squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), however only few of them reports the effectiveness in larger number of animals included [4–6]. Other published papers that used ECT in cats, reported the results of treated tumours such as basal cell carcinoma (BCC) [7], ganglioneuroblastoma [8], haemangiopericytoma [9], injection-site sarcoma [10], metastatic apocrine sweat gland adenocarcinoma [11], oral melanoma (OM) [12], rhabdomyosarcoma [13], soft tissue sarcomas (STS) [14] and thymoma [15]. However, all of the aforementioned papers where mainly case reports as these tumours are quite rare tumours and therefore larger studies are difficult to conduct. One of the major limitations of presenting the ECT in veterinary oncology as a standard treatment is lack of larger cohort’s studies. Because there is problem of enrolment of larger groups of animals, the multicentric clinical studies could present the solution of this problem. One of the approaches how to overcome this hurdle is establishment of clinical registry, as was done for data from clinical studies on ECT in human oncology, where the clinical data are gathered from multiple centres across Europe, thus providing different data from the patients treated with ECT. This so-called InspECT consortium published numerous papers on results from different tumors treated with ECT, highlighting also different aspects of the treatment, such as effectiveness in different tumour types, differences in using different cytostatics, different treatment modalities, such as in combination with systemic chemotherapy or immunotherapy. Therefore, the aim of this study was to establish a similar platform for the veterinary studies, where similarly as in InspECT group, veterinarians who are using IGEA™ generators of electric pulses, could gather the information and analyse the data from the different centres more effectively. The secondary aim of this study was to evaluate the results from antitumor effectiveness that such a database can provide. Here, we report the construction of VetInspECT database and the results from a single centre that initiated the database.

Patients and methods

Database

The vetInspECT database was designed on the basis of InspECT database [3]. Similarly, as in human clinic, here we also included pre-session visit information, ECT session information, follow up and off study information. In the pre-session visit the demographic data such as data of first visit, species, breed, birth date, sex, body weight, type and location of cancer, distinguish if cutaneous or mucosal, date of diagnosis, stage of the disease and information about previous treatments and the occurrence of metastatic disease and the definition if this is primary or secondary cancer. In the ECT session information about the dose, route of administration, type of anesthesia, type of cytostatic, size of tumors and the electrode used was recorded. In the follow up section information about the date of follow up, local disease progression, and concomitant treatments were elaborated. Additionally, information about the treated tumors was evaluated such as change in size and the response. Also, if there is a retreatment needed and the date of retreatment. Furthermore, the time to complete response (CR) was evaluated. In the off-study section, the patients that terminated the enrollment in the study was defined and the reason for that.

Clinical study

The inclusion criteria consisted of cytologically or histologically confirmed tumor, good general health status of the animal with the basic hematology and biochemistry profile within reference limits; normal renal and cardiovascular function; and an expected survival time of at least 3 months. The diagnosis was made based on examination of fine-needle aspiration biopsies taken from lesions and/or pathohistological analysis if owners agreed upon the treatment in general anesthesia. Staging protocol was adapted according to the tumor which was diagnosed by aforementioned cytology and/or histology. In case of advanced stage of the disease the CT scan was performed and in case of oral tumors and other such as mast cell tumors (MCT) the abdominal ultrasound examination with fine needle aspiration of the spleen, liver and regional lymph nodes was performed. In other tumors, e.g., STS the thoracic radiography or CT scan of chest was selected to exclude pulmonary metastatic lesions. The clinical stages were defined using clinical TNM staging, specific for each tumor type [16–18].

Treatment protocol

Written informed consent was obtained from each owner before beginning of the treatment. All selected patients passed the preanesthetic clinical examination, including laboratory hematological analysis and selected biochemical parameters according ASA classification and clinical features. Cats were premedicated as already published [6] with a combination of medetomidine (0.02 mg/kg Domitor; Pfizer Animal Health) and ketamine (2 mg/kg Bioketan; Vetoquinol). General anaesthesia was induced with propofol (1 mg/kg Diprivan; Zeneca) and maintained with isoflurane (Forane; Abbott Laboratories). During anesthesia, animals received Hartmann’s solution (B Braun Melsungen) at a rate of 10 ml/kg/h. The tumors were measured where the longest diameter was used for the evaluation of the response [19]. ECT treatment was performed as already published [20], the dosage of bleomycin was determined according to the body surface area (BSA), calculated from the bodyweight (BW) mass with the formula: BSA (m2) = (BW(g))2/3 × 10–3. ECT consisted of intravenous injection of BLM (3 mg (4500IU)/ml Blenoxane; Bristol-Myers) at a dosage of 15,000 IU/m2. Eight minutes after injection of BLM, electroporation of the tumor was performed with the electric pulse generator (Cliniporator; Igea) using plate (two parallel stainless-steel electrodes 8 mm apart and with a length of 30 mm), needle (two rows of needles with 4 mm gap and with a length 10 mm to 30 mm), finger (2 rows of 4 needles with 4 mm gap, length 10 to 20 mm) or hexagonal (7 electrodes in an hexagonal configuration and 1 eletrode in the centre, distance between electrodes 8 mm, length 10, 20–30 mm) electrodes. Eight electric pulses were delivered with 1300 V/cm (plate) or 1000 V/cm (needle, hexagonal, finger) amplitude to electrode distance ratio, a duration of 100 µs and a frequency of 5000 Hz.

Additionally, when needed (site of the tumor, tumor size) animals were provided with oral analgesia using the non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug meloxicam (Loxicom; Norbrook Laboratories) at a dose of 0.1 mg/kg/day for three consecutive days.

Treatment evaluation

Treatment response was evaluated as already reported [6, 20]. The follow-up visit consisted of one week after the treatment, 1 month, 2 month, 6 months and 12 months after the treatment. At each follow up visit, the lesion was carefully examined and measured if possible and photographed. CR was defined as complete eradication of the treated tumor with re-epithelialization of skin. Partial response (PR) was defined as a decrease of > 50% of the largest perpendicular diameters of measurable lesions. Stable disease (SD) was defined as a reduction of < 50% and an increase of < 25% of the above-mentioned measurements. Progressive disease (PD) was defined by an increase of > 25%. When comparing different tumor types, we compared four of the most represented tumor types: SCC, MCT, other carcinoma tumor types (including all carcinoma except SCC), sarcoma (all sarcoma type of tumors). Observation period was defined as the interval between the date of the first treatment and the date of the last examination of the patient and progression free survival (PFS) was calculated as the time from enrollment to the progression of the disease [19].

Possible side effects were evaluated according to Veterinary Cooperative Oncology Group Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (VCOG–CTCAE) toxicity scale at each follow up visit [21].

Descriptive analysis was conducted for continuous variables using the mean, standard deviation, median value and range, while categorical variables were reported by absolute number and percentages.

Comparisons among groups were conducted using Chi square-test for trend analysis with categorical variables. Local tumor control was expressed as PFS, which was the time from ECT up to the date of relapse or progression or last follow-up. Survival curves for PFS were calculated using the Kaplan–Meier model. Comparison among survival curves was performed using the long-rank test. Significance of tests was reported with p-value, where a value < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant. All the analysis has been conducted by means of NCSS® version 9.16 software.

Results

Characteristics of the patients and tumors in the study cohort

In this analysis we included 94 cats with 138 nodules that were treated between the October 2011 and October 2024, 51 males and 43 females. The median age was 10.5 years (range 8 months to 20.3. years) and median weight 5 kg (range 2–11.4 kg). Eleven patients (12%) had previous surgical excision. The representation of patients with specific tumor histology were: SCC (62; 65.7%), MCT (11; 11.7%), other carcinoma tumors: BCC (3; 3.2%), BISC (4; 4.3%), mammary carcinoma (1; 2.1.%) and cutaneous adenocarcinoma (1; 1.1.%) and mesenchymal tumors: STS (4; 4.3%), FSA (fibrosarcoma) (2; 2.1%), PNST (2; 2.1%) leiomyosarcoma (1; 1.1%) and myxosarcoma (1; 1.1%) and of one case of melanoma and myeloma (Table 1). The average size of the tumors was 12 mm (range 1.0–65 mm) (Tables 1 and 2). The median ECT sessions per animal was 1 (range 1–5), median number of treated tumors per session was 1 (range 1–8) and the average dosage of bleomycin was 2550 IU per animal (range 1500 IU – 4500 IU) and in majority of treatments (60%) plate electrodes were used (Tables 1 and 2).

Side effects

We also monitored the occurrence of possible side effects, although all clinical studies to date have shown that ECT is safe and that cats tolerate the treatment very well. We did not observe any side effects with intravenous administration of BLM. After ECT, we expect a mild to moderate inflammatory reaction in the first week, followed by necrosis of the tumor tissue (grade 1 VCOG–CTCAE), which does not cause serious problems for the treated cats. In rare cases, with larger tumors in the nasal area, the treatment site requires more extensive cleaning, primarily to ensure normal breathing and greater comfort for the animal. Scab formation is expected over approximately four weeks. Even in cases where a scab did not form, no cats suffered from a local or systemic infection, despite not receiving any antibacterial treatment. Altogether in our study, 49 animals experienced before mentioned side effect, majorly scabs (which were present in 41 animals) which were temporary, followed by 5 cases of inflammation, 1 case of erosion, 1 case of fistula and 1 case of erythema.

Response

Response to treatment has been evaluated on 82 cats and 110 nodules, the remaining 12 cats were left out from the analysis as follow up was not available. Out of 82 animals, 56 animals (67%) had CR, 20 (24%) PR, 6 animals had SD and in 3 animals tumors progressed. Time to reach CR was on average 2.9 months (median 1 month; range 1–15 months). Concerning the number of ECT session needed to achieve CR, in 44 animals (78%) CR was achieved after one session, in 7 animals after two sessions (13%), in 3 animals after three sessions (5%) and in 2 after four and five session (2%). In case of nodules, CR was achieved in 82 (74%), PR in 20 (18%), SD and PD in 8, four of each (4%) (Table 3). Additionally, 14 nodules (13%) progressed on average after 7.6 months (median 4.9 months, range 1–40.9 months), which were then retreated. ECT session was performed only once in 57 animals (61%), in 26 animals two times (28%), three times in 7 animals (7%), four times in 3 animals (3%) and five times in one animal (1%) (Table 3; Fig. 1).

In SCC tumors, the tumors with lower stage (T1) achieved higher CR (76%) than tumors with T2 stage (64%) (Table 5). Similar was observed in case of non-SCC carcinoma group where T1 stage all responded to the treatment and T3 only achieved SD (Table 5). Long-term response

Next, we evaluated the long-term response, which was available for the 82 animals. The average time for the follow up was 7.87 months (median 4.72 months with a range of 1 months to 45.6 months). The PFS was 81% at 12 months and 75% at 24 months (Fig. 1). Fourteen tumor nodules (13%) went into progression after on average 7.65 months (median 4.9 months, range 1–40.9 months, Fig. 2A). The number at risk for the PFS of all animals was 110 animals at 0 months, 47 animals at 6 months, 26 animals at 12 months, 12 animals at 18 months and 5 animals at 24 months.

PFS of all animals (1A), regarding the different tumor types (1B), regarding the different tissue type (1C) and regarding the tumor size (1D)

The PFS was the highest in the case of non-SCC carcinoma and MCT where it was 100% at 6 months, whereas in case of sarcoma it was 78% and SCC it was 88%. At 12 months, the highest was in case of MCT of 100%, followed by non-SCC carcinoma at 93%, SCC at 79% and sarcoma at 58%. The differences were statistically significant (p = 0.0289, Fig. 2B). Next, the evaluation was performed for the difference in PFS of tumor located in the cutaneous area or the mucosal. The analysis showed that the PFS at 6 months was 90% for cutaneous tumors and 88% for mucosal tumors. This percentage changed at 12 months, when for cutaneous tumors PFS was at 85% whereas the percentage for mucosal tumors dropped to 44%. The difference was statistically different (p = 0.0122, Fig. 2C). Finally, regarding the tumor size, the analysis showed that the survival rate at 6 months was at 88% for tumors bigger than 20 mm and 92% for tumors smaller than 20 mm at 6 months. At 12 months the survival rate for tumors bigger than 20 mm dropped to 76% whereas for tumors smaller than 20 mm stayed at 92%. Although there was a notable difference at 12 months, this was not statistically significant (p = 0.2049, Fig. 2D).

Discussion

The aim of this study was to establish a platform for the reporting of ECT treatment in spontaneous tumors in veterinary oncology and to evaluate the results of antitumor effectiveness of ECT treatment in cats. We adapted the human InspECT platform to summarize from 2012 to 2024 of electrochemotherapy (ECT) outcomes in cats treated in single centre in Slovenia and to lay the groundwork for a veterinary counterpart, vetInspECT [3], that would enable centers worldwide treating spontaneous tumors in cats, dogs, and other animals to pool their data. Such collaboration would accelerate the evaluation of ECT effectiveness, including for rare, deep-seated, or highly aggressive tumors. Our results show that collecting patient, treatment, and follow-up data was straightforward, allowing us to refine the patient form to help veterinarians systematically document essential information. Establishing this shared dataset is an important step toward connecting practitioners of this not yet widely used technique among veterinarians and supporting the integration of ECT as a standard treatment in veterinary oncology.

The results of this study showed that the most frequent tumor treated was SCC (66%), which is the most frequent malignant tumor in cats and MCT (12%) which is less frequent compared with dogs, but still one of the most frequent feline skin tumors [17]. Both tumor types are locally aggressive with low metastatic rates. Because of rapid grow of oral SCC with bone invasion, and in many cases diagnosis in advance stage of the disease, the prognosis is relatively poor with medium survival time of 1–3 months without treatment. Cutaneous SCC is strongly associated with sun exposure, therefore it occurs mainly on sun exposed areas that are poorly pigmented and hairless, such as the ears, the snout, but also the eyelids [16, 22]. Cutaneous SCC has better prognosis if it is diagnosed and treated in early stages using surgery or ECT. The advantage of ECT is the excellent functional and cosmetic effects with comparative outcome and survival time with surgery [4–6]. MCT can occur anywhere on the body, but often on the head, neck and limbs, which can be unfavorable for surgical treatment. Prognosis for cutaneous well differentiated MCT is generally good with complete surgical excision, and less favorable in poorly differentiated and pleomorphic type which is more aggressive. In visceral form, the prognosis is guarded to poor, but some cats can live months to years post splenectomy if systemic signs are under control [18].

Cats with other cutaneous and subcutaneous tumours were significantly less frequently included in the presented clinical study, mostly in cases with locally advanced disease, where extensive surgery would cause significant functional impairment, or where the owners did not agree to suggested surgical treatment. Post-vaccinal soft tissue sarcomas are a particular challenge and can often be found in places where surgery is not an option, such as between the shoulder blades, when the tumour encrusts the bones of the vertebrae, nerves and spinal cord [10].

The results of the antitumor effectiveness showed that the tumour type where the ECT showed to be most successful was MCT (95% CR), then non-SCC carcinoma (90% CR), then SCC (74% CR) and the last sarcoma (33% CR). These findings do support the results obtained from other studies where it was shown that in MCT where study of Holanda et al. showed that after ECT the results reached 100% CR in 4 cats for the duration of 7 months, when one of the patients developed a new lesion [23]. Next in the case of SCC, which is the most presented tumour type in cats treated by ECT [4–6], showed to be about 70–80% effective which was also shown in our study. Next in non-SCC carcinomas, the complete response was achieved in 90% of cases which again corresponded with the data from studies of BCC; where the complete response was achieved in 100% for the duration of 9 months to 1 year [7]. One of the studies from 2007 where they treated STS with ECT, showed that the tumour recurrence was seen in approximately half of the animals, which was higher than in our case where CR was achieved in third of the patients. The difference that could influence the outcome was that in our study we included apart from STS also the FSA, PTNS, leiomyosarcoma and myxosarcoma which have different prognosis [24].

Similar as in different tumours we also observed the difference in the different stages, where the prognosis was worse in higher clinical stages which is accordance with other treatments as well as already observed in the studies in cats [17]. The difference was also evident when comparing cutaneous and mucosal tumours where we observed the difference between the tumour responses, in favour of cutaneous tumours, which was even more evident in the case of evaluating of PFS, where the percentage of PFS was lower in case of mucosal tumours. This again is in accordance with other studies of standard treatment or with ECT treatments as is it known that mucosal tumours are more aggressive [25]. Together with this data we also evaluated the impact of tumour location to the tumour response which similar as in previous analysis we shown that the locations that are more connected to the mucosal tissue which are lip or nose have worse effectiveness and less favourable prognosis. Further on, as many of the ECT studies shown that the size of the tumour influences the antitumor effectiveness, we also conducted that the tumours larger than 20 mm in the largest diameter has worse tumour response than those that are smaller [5, 6, 26].

Last but not least, we analysed the PFS, and the influence of different parameters. We concluded that the PFS is the longest in MCT tumours, then in non-SCC carcinoma tumours, SCC and the last sarcoma, which is similar as observed in tumour local control. Additionally, what was already observed in tumour local response, the tumour size and tumour location also influenced on the prolongation of the PFS.

We conclude that this platform presents an important step in standardisation and reporting of ECT in veterinary oncology. Additionally, by using this platform we showed that these results support the findings published so far by several case reports and case series of using ECT in feline tumours. Moreover, the platform of this scope is an excellent tool to base the connection of different centres to gather the results and publish joint studies.

Limitations of the study

One of the main drawbacks of this study was that the animals were treated in only one centre, however in this stage we wanted to evaluate the platform for the reporting. Additionally, the majority of tumours included here were SCC with mainly clinical stage 1, which will improve by adding several other centres to this platform, where also results from underrepresented rare and/or advanced tumours could be evaluated. Moreover, inclusion of larger number of animals will could also provide additional statistical tests involving correlation between different tumour characteristics and treatment response.
