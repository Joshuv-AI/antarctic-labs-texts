New scoring system combining WHO ordinal scale and MASCC score in predicting mortality of COVID-19 patients with malignancy and neutropenia: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Pin-Chen Lin, Yan-Bo Huang, Feng-Che Kuan, Chip-Jin Ng, Shou-Yen Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCOVID-19 has become a common infectious disease globally and predicting prognosis of hospitalized COVID-19 patients is important, especially for patients with risk factors like febrile neutropenia. Our research aimed to analyze prognostic factors of COVID-19 patients with neutropenia and develop effective scoring system to predict the mortality in these patients.MethodsData of COVID-19 patients with neutropenia who presented to the emergency department were extracted from electronic health records across multiple hospitals between April 2021 and September 2022. The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality. Various scoring systems, including the Multinational Association for Supportive Care in Cancer risk index (MASCC) score, World Health Organization (WHO) ordinal scale, and the combination of these two scoring systems, were used and compared for prediction of prognosis.ResultsA total of 304 adult COVID-19 in-hospital patients with neutropenia were included into the study. Higher rates of intubation, oxygen use, and ICU admission were observed in the non-survival group. The MASCC score, the WHO ordinal scale and the combined scoring system showed predictive ability for in-hospital mortality, and the combined scoring system had the best predictive power.ConclusionAlthough MASCC score and WHO ordinal score were useful to predict the prognosis of COVID-19 patients with neutropenia, the new system combining MASCC score and WHO ordinal scale had the best performance in predicting the mortality. Intubation, body temperature, and creatine level were the most significant prognostic factors.

Introduction

The coronavirus 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, caused by the novel severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus-2 (SARS-CoV-2), emerged in late 2019 in Wuhan, China. It appeared that all ages of the population were susceptible to COVID-19 infection and COVID-19 infection had caused severe consequences in some patient groups, such as the elderly and patients with chronic pulmonary diseases, diabetes mellitus, chronic renal diseases, and malignancy [1–3].

Currently, there are limited studies on the prognosis of COVID-19 patients with neutropenia, who were considered more vulnerable to infectious diseases and secondary bacterial infection after contracting COVID-19 [4–6]. Some studies indicated that COVID-19 patients with neutropenia following chemotherapy have a higher mortality [7, 8]. Therefore, it is important to find or develop a scoring system for predicting the prognosis of COVID-19 patients with neutropenia and determining patient-based therapy to reduce the mortality.

Clinically, the Multinational Association for Supportive Care in Cancer risk index (MASCC) score and the World Health Organization (WHO) Ordinal Scale are widely used for assessing the prognosis of febrile neutropenia in cancer patients and the severity of COVID-19, respectively [9, 10]. While these scores offer predictive utility in their intended contexts, they may not capture the full prognostic landscape for COVID-19 patients with concurrent neutropenia. Specifically, MASCC was designed to predict bacterial infection-related outcomes rather than viral infections like COVID-19, and the WHO scale, although useful for respiratory assessment, lacks measures of immune function [10, 11]. This discrepancy suggests a need for a specialized scoring system that addresses the unique prognostic needs of patients facing both COVID-19 and neutropenia.

Our study aims to preliminarily validates the existing scoring system and develop a new scoring system as a tool for assessing mortality risk among COVID-19 patients with neutropenia, not only providing clinicians with a straightforward method to identify high-risk patients but also with a promising means to enhance treatment strategies by informing risk-based clinical decisions. Furthermore, this research was expected to lay the groundwork for future large-scale validation studies to confirm the clinical utility and applicability of this new scoring system across diverse patient populations.

Materials and methods

Study design and data source

This retrospective cohort study utilized electronic health records (EHR) extracted from the Chang Gung Research Database (CGRD). The Chang Gung Research Database (CGRD) is a de-identified EHR database from Chang Gung Memorial Hospital (CGMH), Taiwan’s largest healthcare system. CGMH in Taiwan consists of seven medical campuses, providing 10,070 beds and caring for over 280,000 patients annually. Since 2000, the CGRD has been collecting EHRs for all patients without requiring specific registration, encompassing extensive information such as demographics, laboratory results, inpatient and outpatient records, emergency data, nursing details, disease categories, and treatment data, including medications and procedures. The study was approved by the institutional review board of the Chang-Gung Memorial Hospital, Taiwan (IRB no. 202401139B0). (Clinical trial number: not applicable)

Study setting and population

We extracted the data of patients who visited the emergency department (ED) and were diagnosed COVID-19 infection between April 2021 and September 2022 from CGRD. From this data of COVID-19 patients, we included those who had severe neutropenia, which is defined as an absolute neutrophil count (ANC) of < 500 cells/mm3 [12], and excluded those who did not require hospitalization, ages under 18, and without cancer.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome is in-hospital mortality of patients with COVID-19 and neutropenia. We analyzed and compared factors between survival and non-survival groups. Different scoring system were used to evaluate the patients, including MASCC score, WHO Ordinal Scale and the combination of MASCC score and WHO Ordinal Scale.

Multinational Association for Supportive Care in Cancer risk index (MASCC) score is a tool to measure the risk for serious medical complication of febrile neutropenia, such as death, ICU admission, confusion, cardiac complications, respiratory failure, renal failure, hypotension, bleeding, and other severe medical issues. The maximum score is 26. As for the combined scoring system, we first classified the patients by the WHO ordinal scale, followed by the MASCC score categorizing each aspect into high-risk and low-risk groups. Since most patients were concentrated within WHO scores 3 to 5, we focused our analysis on this subset. As a result, we ultimately analyzed six groups for the outcomes discussion. The efficacy of the scoring systems in prognosticating in-hospital mortality of patients with COVID-19 and neutropenia was analyzed additionally.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (version 24.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages, and continuous variables are presented as either mean and standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range (IQR), as appropriate. The collected data of the included patients were compared using Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and Pearson’s chi-square test for categorical variables. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed for independent significant factors. Univariate logistic regression analysis was used to analyze different scoring systems and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 98,763 patients were diagnosed with COVID-19 from our database during the study period and 677 patients had neutropenia (Fig. 1). We included 304 patients after excluding those who did not require hospitalization, aged < 18 years, and without history of cancer. These patients were then divided into survival group (N = 251) and non-survival group (N = 53).

Discussion

The global all-age mortality of COVID-19 is 120.3 deaths per 100,000 of the population, while in other research, the mortality was reported to be 3.4% [15, 16]. In one case report and systemic review, the overall mortality of patients with COVID-19 combined with febrile neutropenia is 15.8%, which may be caused by coinfection of virus, bacteria, and fungus [4, 17]. Bacterial and fungal infections are common complications of viral pneumonia [6]. In a previous study, it was mentioned that there is an 11–35% chance of coinfection across all age groups [4]. Moreover, for patients with neutropenia, who have compromised immune systems, the probability of experiencing coinfection might be even higher. One research mentioned that neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio may be one of the risks of mortality, indicating that functional apoptosis of cytotoxic lymphocytes has been linked to the onset of viral infections. While the exact mechanism behind lymphopenia in COVID-19 remains uncertain, it is believed that COVID-19 infection causes an overproduction of pro-inflammatory cytokines, which in turn triggers extensive lymphocyte apoptosis [18, 19]. Additionally, some research also indicated that patients with cancer have poor prognosis because of being under chemotherapy or immunosuppressant treatment [20, 21]. Since COVID-19 patients with neutropenia have higher mortality and worse prognosis than all-age patients, it could be helpful to find out the prognosis factors or to have a useful scoring system to predict the mortality of these patients.

Intubation was proved an independent prognostic factor in our study. For COVID-19 patients, those who require intubation and ICU admission indicated severe hypoxia and respiratory failure and high mortality was not surprising [22]. Previous studies reported that the mortality of those who were intubated is much higher than non-intubation patients and mortality of those in ICU is above 30%, which is much higher than the average mortality [16, 23–26]. Another prognostic factor was body temperature which was found to be lower in the non-survival group. One previous research indicated that both high and low body temperature associate with poor prognosis [27]. Another study found that COVID-19 patients who had lower body temperatures upon admission to the ICU experienced more than twice the mortality rate compared to those with higher temperatures, although the mechanisms were not fully understood [28]. Regarding creatinine levels, the non-survival group had higher creatinine values compared to the survival group. Similar findings have been noted in other studies and revealed a significant association between elevated serum creatinine levels and heightened risk of developing acute kidney injury while hospitalized contributing to elevated mortality rate [29–31]. In addition, patients with pre-existing chronic kidney disease and abnormal baseline serum creatinine levels were at greater risk of severe outcomes [32]. However, low prevalence of comorbidities such as hypertension and diabetes and no obvious difference between survival and non-survival group were noted in our study, which may not accurately reflect the general COVID-19 population. This lack of difference in our study may be caused by small patient number and possible selection bias. Future studies should be done to enhance the generalizability and clinical relevance of the findings by including a broader range of patients with these comorbidities.

The WHO ordinal scale is a tool developed to categorize the severity of COVID-19 in patients based on their clinical respiratory status and required interventions [33]. This scale ranges from 0 to 8, mainly concerning the patients’ oxygen need when they first came to the hospital. With higher scores, it indicates greater severity of illness and need for more intensive medical support. Previous studies have shown that the WHO ordinal scale can effectively predict in-hospital mortality [10]. Patients categorized in higher severity levels have significantly higher mortality rates​. Therefore, WHO ordinal scale can be used to predict the COVID-19 prognosis. However, the accuracy of predicting mortality COVID-19 patients with neutropenia group is not proven before. Our study proved that WHO ordinal scale has good accuracy in predicting the prognosis of this group.

The MASCC score is widely used to guide treatment decisions and optimize care for cancer patients with febrile neutropenia. It provides a standardized way to measure clinical improvement or deterioration, facilitating comparison across different studies and patient populations. The total score can range from 0 to 26, with higher scores indicating a lower risk of complications. It can also help to identify low-risk patients who can safely receive treatment outside the hospital, thereby reducing healthcare costs and improving patient quality of life [34]. Previous study has shown that MASCC score can effectively predict the risk of neutropenia patients [9]. However, for these febrile patients with diagnosis of COVID-19, the application of MASCC score to assess the prognosis had not been proven yet. Our study revealed that MASCC score may be used to predict the mortality of COVID-19 patients with neutropenia, but the accuracy was not as good as WHO ordinal scale. One possible explanation was that the MASCC score was originally designed to predict prognosis related to febrile neutropenia, which is usually caused by gram positive bacteria, not respiratory-related diseases, or viral infections like COVID-19 [35]. For COVID-19 infection, the criteria used in the MASCC score were not as appropriate as those in the WHO ordinal score. However, MASCC score still had some predictability because it represented the immune status of the COVID-19 patients with neutropenia and may associate with the mortality of the COVID-19 infection. Besides, secondary or combined infection could happen for these febrile neutropenic patients with COVID − 19 infection and increase the mortality during admission [4–6, 34, 35]. Therefore, MASCC score still holds a certain level of effectiveness in evaluation.

For advantages of both WHO ordinal scale and MASCC score, we combined these two scoring systems due to the inspiration of WHO ordinal scale combined with inflammation risk category in previous study [10]. We replaced inflammatory risk category by MASCC score, which also categorized risk levels as high and low risk like inflammatory risk category. The combined scoring system showed best predicting accuracy for mortality of COVID-19 patients with neutropenia comparing to WHO ordinal scale or MASCC score. This combined scoring system may not only evaluate the clinical respiration status of COVID-19 infection but also the risk of secondary or simultaneous bacterial or fungal infection for these patients and had better predicting ability to evaluate the outcomes of COVID-19 patients with neutropenia. Previous studies have shown that some prediction models based on chest films or images achieved AUCs ranging from 0.61 to 0.825, depending on various readers [36, 37]. This variability may limit the generality of these models. On the other hand, prediction models using artificial intelligence (AI) were reported to have good performance on predicting prognosis of COVID-19 patients, with AUC ranging from 0.74 to 0.98 [38–40]. Although these AI models had good ability in prediction prognosis of COVID-19 patients, it would be difficult to apply these models globally because these models were mostly trained and adjusted by local database and not validated in different hospitals or countries. Further adjustment and validation would be necessary before using these AI models.

The combined scoring system developed in our study was easy to use and could be useful for clinical physicians to assess the risk of febrile neutropenic patients when they get COVID-19 infection and decide the treatment plan. For those countries or hospitals which were unable to apply this combined scoring system, WHO ordinal scale may be an alternative method which was simple to use and had good predicting ability. Further research may be necessary to validate the clinical practicality of this new combined scoring system.

Limitation

Our research has some limitations. First, our study subjects are primarily composed of single country, without cross-ethnic discussions. Therefore, the result may not be useful in other countries. Additionally, the patients’ data came from only one database, which may lead to selecting bias. Second, neither some factors like clinical status nor other scoring system concerning neutropenia, such as Clinical Index of Stable Febrile Neutropenia (CISNE), were discussed in our study. Also, our database did not record whether patients’ ANC (absolute neutrophil count) decline was caused by chemotherapy drugs, nor did we specifically exclude DNR (Do Not Resuscitate) patients. These factors could potentially affect the prognosis. Third, we did not analyze the severity of these patients’ imaging studies, such as chest X-rays (CXR) or CT scans, nor did we examine whether their PCR cycle threshold (CT) values were associated with their prognosis. Additionally, our database lacks records of radiation therapy. Therefore, we are unable to determine the relationship between radiation therapy and neutropenia in our study. To sum up, the factors mentioned above could potentially lead to our research being less comprehensive.

Conclusions

Intubation, body temperature, creatine level and were the most significant prognostic factors for COVID-19 patients with neutropenia. Although MASCC score and WHO ordinal scale were effective in predicting mortality of COVID-19 patients with neutropenia, the combination scoring system of MASCC score and WHO ordinal scale demonstrated best performance in predicting mortality among these patients. Further research should be done to validate the new combined scoring system and evaluate the effectiveness in clinical applications.
