Correlation of advanced lung cancer inflammation index with all-cause, cardiovascular, and cancer mortality in individuals with metabolic syndrome: Evidence from NHANES 2007–2018
Authors: Long Ma, De-Sheng LI, Li-Wei Zhang, Jing-Wen Li, Yi-Chen Hou, Qing-Chao Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Metabolic syndrome is associated with persistent low-grade inflammation. The advanced lung cancer inflammation index (ALI) is a comprehensive index that measures inflammation. The purpose of this study was to determine the relationship between ALI and all-cause, cardiovascular, and cancer mortality in a metabolic syndrome (MetS) population. We extracted cohort data from the 2007–2018 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey for analysis. Weighted Kaplan–Meier analyses and multivariate adjusted Cox analyses were employed to evaluate the association between ALI and mortality due to all causes, cardiovascular diseases, and cancer in individuals with MetS. Nonlinear relationships were assessed using restricted cubic spline analysis. Subgroup and interaction analyses were conducted to enhance result robustness. This study enrolled 3110 participants, with 366 deaths from all-cause, 92 deaths due to cardiovascular diseases, and 98 deaths related to cancer during the 81-month follow-up period. The population was stratified into 4 groups based on ALI quartiles. After adjusting for covariates, the analysis revealed a significantly reduced risk of all-cause mortality in (Q2, Q3), and Q4 group compared to the reference group (Q1). Similarly, a decreased risk of cardiovascular disease mortality was observed in association with ALI in Q2 and Q3 group compared to the reference group. Stratified analyses further show the robustness of these relationships. In addition, this research also reveals that in the MetS population ≥ 60 years of age, Q2 group is associated with a lower risk of cancer mortality than Q1 group. The restricted cubic spline analyses further revealed a nonlinear association between ALI and all-cause mortality in the MetS population, while demonstrating a linear association with cardiovascular mortality and cancer mortality. ALI is a reliable biomarker of systemic inflammation in the MetS population. In individuals with MetS, reduced levels of ALI are strongly associated with an increased risk of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, as well as increased cancer mortality in those aged > 60 years.

1. Introduction

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) is a cluster of metabolic disorders encompassing insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, central obesity, and hypertension. The latest data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) reveals that in 2020, the disease prevalence rates were recorded at 24% for males and 22% for females in the United States.[1] The presence of MetS is significantly associated with an elevated risk of cardiovascular disease[2,3] and various types of cancers.[4–8] Importantly, MetS is also linked to a 1.5-fold increase in all-cause mortality and a 2-fold increase in cardiovascular mortality.[9,10] as well as heightened mortality rates across multiple cancer types.[11–13] Therefore, it is critical to identify prognostic factors that may prevent or delay mortality in individuals with MetS.

MetS is basically a chronic low-grade inflammatory illness, according to earlier research.[14,15] Patients with higher levels of inflammation had a higher risk of hospitalization or death than other patients.[16,17] Inflammation may therefore be a major factor in the development of MetS and ultimately lower the prognosis of MetS patients.

The advanced lung cancer inflammation index (ALI) was initially developed to assess the inflammatory status and prognosticate outcomes in patients diagnosed with lung cancer.[18] ALI has also demonstrated favorable responsiveness to the inflammatory status and long-term prognosis of patients with gastrointestinal cancers,[19] hypertension,[20] and diabetes mellitus.[21] However, MetS represents complex chronic inflammatory disorder, and it remains uncertain whether ALI accurately corresponds to the inflammatory state of patients and their prognostic outcomes.

The objective of this study is to investigate the association between ALI and the risk of all-cause, cardiovascular, and cancer mortality in patients with MetS, aiming to provide valuable insights for the treatment and management of individuals with MetS.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study population

The NHANES database is a biennial cross-sectional survey conducted in the United States, encompassing participant health interview surveys and physical health assessments. All data collected from NHANES participants have obtained approval from the National Center for Health Statistics Ethics Review Board, with comprehensive information accessible on the web at https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/. In this study, we analyzed data from 6 consecutive cycles of NHANES from 2007–2018 containing a total of 59,842 individuals. We excluded individuals under the age of 18, those without diagnostic indicators for MetS, and acquired a total of 3163 participants with MetS. Finally, we excluded 3 individuals who had no follow-up or ALI information, resulting in a final sample size of 3110 participants for this study (Fig. 1).

Complete flow chart for participants recruitment.

2.2. Definition of MetS

We used the National Cholesterol Education Program Adult Treatment Panel-III (ATP-III) criteria to define MetS, which means that a participant was diagnosed with MetS if he or she met at least 3 of the following 5 criteria[22,23]: abdominal obesity: waist circumference ≥ 102 cm in men or ≥ 88 cm in women; hypertriglyceridemia: serum triglycerides ≥ 150 mg/dL (1.7 mmol/L) or medication for elevated triglycerides; low high-density cholesterols (low HDL-c): serum HDL-c < 40 mg/dL for men and < 50 mg/dL for women; hypertension: systolic blood pressure (SBP) equal to or >130 mm Hg, diastolic blood pressure (DBP) equal to or >85 mm Hg, receiving antihypertensive treatment, or being diagnosed by a physician. SBP and DBP were determined by calculating the average of multiple measurements (up to 4) for all participants. Hyperglycemia: Fasting blood glucose levels ≥ 100 mg/dL or receiving antihyperglycemic therapy, insulin treatment, or diagnosed with diabetes mellitus.

The information for waist circumference, SBP, and DBP were obtained from examination data, while laboratory data provided information on triglycerides, HDL-c, and fasting blood glucose levels. Questionnaire responses were used to gather personal medical history (including hypertension and diabetes mellitus) as well as medication history (including antihypertensive, hypoglycemic, and lipid-lowering medications).

2.3. Measurement of ALI

The ALI is determined by the formula of body mass index (kg/m2) multiplied by albumin level (g/dL), divided by neutrophil to lymphocyte ratio (NLR), which is calculated as the neutrophil count divided by the lymphocyte count. Body mass index, albumin, neutrophil count, and lymphocyte count can be obtained from examination and laboratory data. Participants were categorized into 4 groups based on their ALI quartiles and weights, specifically quartile 1 (Q1, ALI ≤ 49.9), quartile 2 (Q2, 49.9 < ALI ≤ 68.0), quartile 3 (Q3, 68.0 < ALI ≤ 89.2), and quartile 4 (Q4, ALI > 89.2).

2.4. Covariates

The covariates information was selected based on clinical experience and previous studies.[20,21] The demographic data collected includes age, gender, race/ethnicity, educational background, and poverty-to-income ratio (PIR). Age is divided into 2 groups based on the following criteria: individuals aged 60 or above and those below 60 years old. Race/Ethnicity was divided into 4 groups: Mexican American, Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, and Other Races/Ethnicities; Education was categorized into 2 groups: less than high school and high school or above. The PIR was stratified into 4 groups based on the following criteria: PIR ≤ 1, 1 < PIR ≤ 2, 2 < PIR ≤ 4, and PIR > 4.

The questionnaire data encompasses information on smoking status and alcohol consumption, with 3 distinct categories identified for individuals based on their engagement in smoking: “Never” (those who have never smoked or smoked <100 cigarettes), “Former” (those who have smoked more than 100 cigarettes in their lifetime but currently do not smoke at all), and “Current” (those who have smoked more than 100 cigarettes in their lifetime and currently smoke some days or every day). Moreover, individuals who consumed a minimum of 12 servings of any alcoholic beverage (equivalent to 12 oz beer, 5 oz wine, or 1.5 oz liquor) within a span of 1 year were classified as drinkers. In addition, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), glycohemoglobin (HbA1c), and creatinine (Cr) can be obtained from laboratory data.

2.5. Clinical outcome

The National Center for Health Statistics integrates data derived from population surveys with death certificate records in the National Death Index. The publicly accessible dataset encompasses 2 crucial components essential to our study: date of death and cause of death, encompassing all-cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, and cancer mortality. The follow-up period for participant survival status ended on December 31, 2019.

2.6. Statistical analysis

In this study, we employed the sampling weights provided by NHANES to ensure the representativeness of our findings. These weights account for the complex survey design, including stratification and clustering. Specifically, we used the final examination weight (WTMEC2YR) for the physical examination component and the fasting weight (WTSF2YR) for the laboratory component. Weighted statistics were calculated using the survey package in R. Categorical variables were expressed as counts (weighted percentages). Baseline differences in categorical variables were assessed using the chi-square test with Rao & Scott second-order correction. Differences at baseline for continuous variables were estimated using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for comparisons of complex survey samples and are presented as weighted medians (weighted 25th percentile, 75th percentile).

The Kaplan–Meier analysis was utilized to investigate the connection between ALI levels and all-cause, cardiovascular, and cancer mortality in MetS individuals. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were conducted to further investigate the impact of ALI levels on all-cause mortality, cardiovascular disease mortality, and cancer mortality in patients with MetS. The results were presented as hazard ratios (HR) along with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Three models were employed in this study: Model 1, which was unadjusted for confounders; Model 2, which accounted for age, sex, and race/ethnicity to control for demographic factors; and Model 3, which adjusted for age, sex, race/ethnicity, education level, PIR, smoking status, alcohol consumption, ALT, HbA1c, and Cr levels to account for demographic factors as well as healthy living behaviors and laboratory indicators. In addition, after dividing each participant’s ALI level by 10, we included it as a continuous variable in multivariate Cox regression analyses designed to assess the effect of each 10-unit change in ALI on all-cause, cardiovascular, and cancer mortality in patients with MetS.[21]

Subsequently, multivariable-adjusted Cox restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression models were employed to examine the dose-response association between ALI and all-cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, and cancer mortality. In case a nonlinear relationship was detected, we will use a recursive algorithm to determine its inflection point. A segmented multivariable-adjusted Cox regression model was utilized to estimate the threshold effect of ALI on mortality. The model was adjusted by incorporating age, gender, race/ethnicity, education level, PIR, smoking status, alcohol consumption, ALT, HbA1c, and Cr. Finally, we conducted stratified analyses to assess disparities in ALI levels across diverse populations.

The aforementioned analyses were conducted using NHANES-recommended weights to ensure data representativeness. Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, https://www.Rproject.org/, Vienna, Austria). A significance level of 2-sided P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

3. Results

3.1. Baseline characteristics of the participants

This study included a total of 3110 participants with MetS, 1792 aged <60 years (weighted percentage 65%), 1318 aged greater than or equal to 60 years (weighted percentage 35%), 1359 males (weighted percentage 46%), and 1751 females (weighted percentage 54%). Participants were divided into 4 groups based on weighted quartiles of ALI levels, with 25% of participants in each group. The median ALI values for Q1, Q2, Q3, and Q4 groups were 40, 59, 77, and 111, respectively.

Compared to the Q1 group, the Q2, Q3, and Q4 groups demonstrated the following characteristics: a higher proportion of individuals under 60 years old, a decreased percentage of Non-Hispanic White participants, and a lower prevalence of current smokers. The MetS component exhibited an increased prevalence of abdominal obesity and a reduced incidence of hypertension. Additionally, there were fewer occurrences of all-cause mortality and cardiovascular-related deaths. The physical examination revealed elevated body mass index) values. The laboratory examination revealed elevated levels of albumin, decreased neutrophil counts, elevated lymphocyte counts, and increased ALT levels (Table 1).

3.2. Kaplan–Meier analysis of the relationship between ALI and mortality in individuals with MetS

During a median follow-up of 81 months, a total of 366 deaths (weighted percentage 9%) from all causes, including 92 cardiovascular deaths (weighted percentage 2%) and 98 cancer deaths (weighted percentage 2%), were documented among the 3110 participants. Kaplan–Meier analyses were employed to preliminarily evaluate the association between ALI levels and mortality for all-cause, cardiovascular, and cancer outcomes in individuals with MetS. Lower ALI levels in the MetS population were found to be significantly associated with higher mortality for all-cause (P < .001), cardiovascular (P < .001), and cancer (P = .042) outcomes (Fig. 2).

3.3. Cox regression analysis of the relationship between ALI and mortality in individuals with MetS

The association between ALI and mortality, including all-cause, cardiovascular, and cancer-related deaths, was further investigated using univariate and multivariate Cox regression analysis (Table 2). In Model 3, after adjusting for all covariates, the analysis revealed a decreased risk of all-cause mortality in Q2 (HR: 0.52, 95% CI: 0.37–0.75), Q3 (HR: 0.39, 95% CI: 0.29–0.54), and Q4 (HR: 0.40, 95% CI: 0.25–0.65) group compared to the reference group (Q1) (P for trend: < 0.001). Similarly, when assessing the association between ALI and cardiovascular mortality using Model 3 with adjustment for all covariates, a reduced risk of cardiovascular mortality was observed in Q2 (HR: 0.38, 95% CI: 0.20–0.73) and Q3 (HR: 0.29, 95% CI: 0.12–0.70) group compared to the reference group (P for trend: 0.152). However, no significant associations were observed between ALI and cancer mortality in our study. Furthermore, we observed no discernible disparity in the prognostic impact of dynamic alterations in per 10 U ALI within the MetS population.

3.4. Dose–response relationship between ALI and mortality in individuals with MetS

The RCS was further employed to investigate the association between ALI and mortality outcomes (Fig. 3). After adjusting for all covariates, we observed an L-shaped nonlinear relationship between ALI levels and all-cause mortality (P for nonlinear < 0.001) in individuals with MetS, while a linear relationship was found between ALI and both cardiovascular (P for nonlinear = 0.148) and cancer (P for nonlinear = 0.107) mortality. By conducting a threshold analysis on the impact of ALI on all-cause mortality, we observed that for every 10 U increase in ALI below 67.9, there was a significant 25.45% reduction in the risk of all-cause mortality (HR: 0.7455, 95% CI: 0.6717–0.8275). Conversely, for every 10 U increase in ALI above 67.9, there was a marginal increment of only 0.06% in the risk of all-cause mortality (HR: 1.0006, 95% CI: 1.0001–1.0010) (Table 3).

3.5. Subgroup analyses

The impact of gender and age on the association between ALI and mortality was further explored through subgroup analyses (Fig. 4). It was observed that stratifying the study population by age (interaction P = .538) and sex (interaction P = .117) did not modify the relationship between ALI and all-cause mortality, indicating a consistent association across different demographic groups. Similarly, the association of ALI with cardiovascular mortality remained consistent across age and sex subgroups. Furthermore, significant differences in the association between ALI and cancer mortality were observed exclusively among individuals aged ≥ 60 years.

4. Discussion

This cohort study employed the ALI to evaluate the inflammatory status in a population with MetS. The findings of this study indicate that a lower ALI is associated with an increased risk of mortality of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in individuals with MetS, even after adjusting for various confounding factors. Furthermore, this association remains consistent across different age groups and genders. However, it was observed that the relationship between ALI and cancer-related mortality was only significant among individuals aged over 60 years. These results suggest that heightened levels of inflammation as indicated by ALI are linked to an elevated risk of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality within the MetS population.

The pathogenesis of the MetS may involve a more complex inflammatory response than the previously mentioned obesity, hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia. Fat tissue, especially visceral fat, acts as an endocrine organ, secreting pro-inflammatory cytokines (adipokines) such as leptin and chemerin, which exacerbate systemic inflammation and are linked to insulin resistance.[24] Moreover, visceral fat releases a large amount of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-α, interleukin-6, and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1.[25] These inflammatory mediators circulate throughout the body, impairing insulin signaling, causing endothelial dysfunction, and elevating blood pressure.[25] Additionally, inflammation activates the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, which is a key pathological mechanism underlying hypertension.[26] Numerous studies have shown that various components of the MetS are strongly associated with chronic inflammation, including obesity and insulin resistance,[27,28] hypertension,[29] as well as dyslipidemia.[30,31] A study involving 42,875 participants revealed a robust association between elevated levels of chronic inflammation and increased risk of cardiovascular and all-cause mortality.[32] Moreover, hypertensive patients with higher levels of inflammation had an increased risk of all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality.[9,10] In a separate study involving 6682 diabetic patients undergoing coronary angiography or percutaneous coronary intervention, it was observed that individuals with an elevated inflammatory state exhibited a significantly heightened risk of mortality.[33] Therefore, research and interventions should focus on the interactions between MetS and inflammation to achieve more effective disease management and prevention.

BMI reflects the balance between lipid overflow and sarcopenia, which are central to the metabolic disturbances seen in MetS.[34] Inflammation heightens capillary permeability, prompting serum albumin to escape, which in turn expands the interstitial space and boosts the distribution volume of albumin. Moreover, the half-life of albumin is shortened, which consequently diminishes the total albumin mass.[35] Previous studies have combined these 2 parameters with inflammatory markers to comprehensively assess the level of systemic inflammation in cancer patients.[20] Neutrophils play a crucial role in the inflammatory response, clearing pathogens and initiating tissue repair by releasing mediators such as reactive oxygen species and proteases. However, excessive neutrophil activation can lead to tissue damage and chronic inflammation. An elevated NLR typically indicates an increase in neutrophils and/or a decrease in lymphocytes, which may be due to immune dysregulation.[35] The NLR of individuals with MetS is higher than that of those without MetS.[36] By combining these parameters, ALI, calculated as BMI (kg/m2) × serum albumin level (g/dL)/NLR, provides a comprehensive assessment of systemic inflammation in MetS. The ALI reflects the body’s overall level of inflammation, with an increase in the ALI indicating a decrease in inflammation.[21] The latest researches has revealed that ALI is also indicative of the inflammatory status in patients suffering from gastrointestinal cancers,[19] hypertension,[20] and diabetes.[21] However, there is a dearth of studies investigating the association between ALI and inflammatory status in individuals with MetS. Therefore, a cohort study was conducted to elucidate this relationship. In this study, it was found that low ALI in persons with MetS was associated with high all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality. And there was a nonlinear L-shaped relationship between ALI levels and all-cause mortality in patients with MetS, whereas a linear relationship was observed between ALI and cardiovascular mortality. This implies that despite MetS involving a more intricate inflammatory response in the body compared to diabetes and hypertension, ALI still serves as an indicator of the systemic inflammatory state with long-term prognostic implications.

While numerous studies have consistently reported an association between ALI and cancer mortality, our study specifically identified a significant correlation between ALI and an increased risk of cancer mortality in individuals aged 60 years or older with MetS. This finding may be attributed to the role of inflammation as a potential link connecting various aging mechanisms and the development as well as progression of cancer.[37]

Our study identified ALI = 67.9 as the inflection point for all-cause mortality. This threshold value can be used to stratify patients with MetS into high-risk and low-risk groups for mortality. In clinical practice, ALI could serve as a practical indicator for risk stratification, guiding the frequency of follow-up and the intensity of interventions. Patients with an ALI below 67.9 may benefit from more frequent monitoring and targeted anti-inflammatory treatments. Future research should focus on determining the optimal frequency of ALI monitoring and identifying specific interventions that could improve outcomes in high-risk patients.

Our study has several limitations. Firstly, the diagnosis of MetS was partially based on self-reported information, potentially introducing recall bias. Secondly, employing a single test may not adequately capture the inflammatory status of patients throughout the entire follow-up period, which could vary over long-term observation. Lastly, this study included a limited sample size with a low incidence of cardiovascular and cancer-related deaths, thereby diminishing its statistical robustness. Consequently, it is imperative to conduct studies with larger sample sizes to validate our findings.

5. Conclusion

The ALI serves as a reliable biomarker for systemic inflammation in the MetS population. Reduced levels of ALI are significantly associated with an elevated risk of all-cause mortality and cardiovascular death among individuals with MetS, as well as an increased susceptibility to cancer-related mortality in those aged > 60 years.
