Association between vitamin D and COVID-19 infection and mortality in Taiwanese patients
Authors: Ching-Ching Sheng, Shuan-Yi Su, Ying Liang, Hui-Chuan Cheng, Hsin-Yi Huang, Hsiao-Hui Chiu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Vitamin D plays a role in immune regulation and may influence coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) outcomes. This study examined the association between serum vitamin D levels and COVID-19 infection and mortality in symptomatic patients in Taiwan.Methods:This retrospective cohort study included symptomatic patients who underwent COVID-19 real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) assay between 2020 and 2023. Serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25(OH)D) levels were categorized as deficient (<20 ng/mL), insufficient (20-30 ng/mL), or sufficient (≥30 ng/mL). Logistic regression models were used to evaluate the impact of vitamin D levels on COVID-19 infection and mortality, adjusting for potential confounders including age, sex, and comorbidities.Results:Among the 481 participants (mean age: 58.2 years; 66.7% female), 96 (19.96%) tested positive for COVID-19. Vitamin D level was not associated with COVID-19 infection (odds ratio [OR] = 1.00, 95% CI, 0.99-1.02; p = 0.687); however, vitamin D deficiency significantly increased the risk of COVID-19-related mortality (OR = 10.68; 95% CI, 1.18-96.45; p = 0.035). The mortality rate in patients aged 65 to 74 years was higher compared to those aged 19 to 44 years (OR = 12.91; 95% CI, 1.29-128.83; p = 0.029).Conclusion:Vitamin D levels were not associated with susceptibility to COVID-19 infection. However, vitamin D deficiency, particularly in older patients, was associated with an increased risk of mortality among those diagnosed with COVID-19. These findings support the role of vitamin D in reducing COVID-19 mortality and emphasize the importance of maintaining sufficient levels in high-risk populations.

1. INTRODUCTION

Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) is caused by severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), and it was first brought to the attention of the World Health Organization on December 31, 2019, following reports of viral pneumonia cases in Wuhan, China. Similar to other coronaviruses, SARS-CoV-2 can evade detection by the host innate immune system by inhibiting interferon (IFN) signaling, suppressing interferon-stimulated gene expression, and targeting post-transcriptional and translational steps to block host protein production.1,2 These disruptions contribute to cytokine overproduction, and the underlying viral mechanisms have been characterized. However, patients with severe COVID-19 have been reported to exhibit an attenuated type I IFN response with elevated levels of antiviral cytokines, leading to cytokine release syndrome, also referred to as a cytokine storm.3 Regarding treatment, the efficacy and safety of the oral antiviral drugs nirmatrelvir and ritonavir have been partially supported by evidence in patients with mild-to-moderate SARS-CoV-2 infection at high risk of severe disease in reducing the risk of the patient becoming critically ill and requiring hospitalization. COVID-19 can also be treated with a range of medications in addition to supportive care, including steroids, immunomodulators, monoclonal antibodies, and antivirals, while the symptoms are severe.4

Vitamin D (serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25(OH)D)) is a crucial factor in bone health and an important regulator of the immune system in response to microbial infection. Poor vitamin D status has been associated with increased susceptibility to viral infections, including respiratory infections.5 Accumulating evidence indicates that vitamin D is integral to immune function and essential for defense against bacterial and viral infections.6 Vitamin D not only exerts a direct antiviral effect by enhancing the immune response, but also plays an immunomodulatory role by modulating the production of inflammatory cytokines and inhibiting the proliferation of inflammatory cells.7 Vitamin D has also been shown to enhance signaling in pattern recognition receptors and IFN pathways and enhance the production of interferon-stimulated genes, thereby limiting viral replication and transmission.8

Some clinical reports have shown that vitamin D supplementation, especially in older individuals and patients with low serum 25(OH)D levels, can help protect against COVID-19 infection and severe disease progression.9,10 Conversely, vitamin D deficiency has been associated with susceptibility to COVID-19 infection and more severe disease. Many researchers have reported an association between vitamin D levels and COVID-19 severity, hospitalization, and mortality,9,11–13 and suggested that vitamin D exerts a protective effect to prevent or reduce the incidence of COVID-19 infection and the need for intensive care unit (ICU) admission.14 A meta-analysis demonstrated that individuals with a serum 25(OH)D level below 20 ng/mL were 2.42 times more likely to develop severe COVID-19 infection (OR = 2.42; 95% CI, 1.13-5.18; p = 0.022), and consequently recommended vitamin D supplementation to prevent COVID-19 and negative outcomes.13

Nevertheless, findings on the association between vitamin D deficiency and COVID-19 outcomes have been inconsistent. Although Athanassiou et al11 reported that vitamin D deficiency in patients with severe COVID-19 infection was related to disease outcomes, another study showed no significant association between vitamin D levels and in-hospital mortality as a single endpoint among hospitalized COVID-19 patients.9 In addition, a meta-analysis on the impact of vitamin D status and supplementation on COVID-19-related mortality and health outcomes found no conclusive evidence to support the current practice of using high-dose vitamin D in patients with COVID-19, although the results suggested that it may have a protective effect on COVID-19-related ICU admissions.15 Vitamin D supplementation at doses of 400 to 1000 IU per day was recommended for bone health maintenance during the COVID-19 pandemic, particularly during periods of home isolation. Moreover, several studies from the UK have suggested that vitamin D may play a preventive role against SARS-CoV-2 infection. However, standardized dosage recommendations and clinical reference criteria remain to be established.16 According to a literature review, serum 25(OH)D significantly decreases during the first 48 hours of an acute illness such as COVID-19, making it an unreliable measure of vitamin D status in the presence of systemic inflammation.17 The low serum 25(OH)D levels during COVID-19 infection may reflect an acute-phase response.

It has been suggested that widespread vitamin D supplementation could potentially reduce global infection rates in a cost-effective manner, with a low incidence of associated adverse effects. This study aimed to examine the association between vitamin D levels and the risk of COVID-19 infection and related mortality, and evaluate whether vitamin D may act as a protective factor against COVID-19.

2. METHODS

2.1. Data source and study population

We conducted a retrospective study to investigate the association between vitamin D levels and COVID-19 infection and mortality in patients aged over 18 years, using data from a medical center in northern Taiwan. Since the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, individuals presenting with symptoms indicative of COVID-19 infection, such as high fever, sore throat, and persistent fatigue, have been referred to our hospital for real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) assay. The date of the COVID-19 RT-PCR assay was defined as the index date. Patients who underwent RT-PCR testing for COVID-19 between 2020 and 2023 and had serum 25(OH)D levels measured within one year before the index date were included in the analysis. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Taipei Veterans General Hospital (IRB No.: 2023-08-004CC), which waived the requirement for informed consent.

2.2. Measurement of vitamin D

Serum 25(OH)D was measured in blood samples using an electrochemiluminescence immunoassay. According to the Endocrine Society Clinical Practice Guidelines,13 the patients were classified into three groups: vitamin D deficient (serum 25(OH)D <20 ng/mL), vitamin D insufficient (20 ng/mL ≤ serum 25(OH)D < 30 ng/mL), and vitamin D sufficient (serum 25(OH)D ≥30 ng/mL). Measurement of serum 25(OH)D was considered an out-of-pocket expense, and informed consent was obtained from all patients before testing.

2.3. Covariates

Patient demographics (sex and age) and comorbidities including diabetes mellitus, (International Classification of Diseases 10th Revision—Clinical Modification [ICD-10-CM] codes E10, E11-12), hypertension (ICD-10-CM code I15), hyperlipidemia (ICD-10-CM codes E78.0-5), ischemic heart disease (ICD-10-CM codes I20-I25), stroke (ICD-10-CM codes I60-69), and body mass index (BMI) were collected as covariates.

2.4. Outcomes

The primary outcome was laboratory-confirmed COVID-19 infection according to a positive COVID-19 RT-PCR result. The secondary outcome was COVID-19-related mortality, defined as any cause of death in the death registry data within 1 year from the index date, including ICD-10-CM code U071 or U072, which indicates COVID-19 infection.

2.5. Statistical analysis

Data were presented as mean ± SD for continuous variables, and as frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. To assess the association between vitamin D status and COVID-19 infection, univariate analysis was conducted first. Risk factors for mortality among the COVID-19 patients were evaluated using logistic regression models. Variables with a p value of <0.1 in the univariate analysis were subsequently included in the multivariable logistic regression to adjust for potential confounders. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs were estimated to examine the associations between demographic characteristics, comorbidities, vitamin D status, and mortality outcomes. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY), Stata 16.0 was used to create graphs; and a two-sided p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

3. RESULTS

A total of 481 symptomatic patients (160 [33.3%] male and 321 [66.7%] female) who underwent COVID-19 RT-PCR and had serum 25(OH)D levels measured within 12 months before the index date were included in the analysis. Of these patients, 385 (80.0%) were negative for COVID-19 and 96 (20.0%) were positive (Fig. 1). The clinical characteristics of the two groups are shown in Table 1. There was no significant difference in COVID-19 status between the male and female patients. The mean vitamin D level was 22.8 ± 12.7 ng/mL, and there was no significant difference in vitamin D level between the COVID-19 negative and positive groups (p = 0.870). In the positive group, 15 (15.6%) patients died, while 81 (84.4%) patients were alive at the end of the study. There was a significantly higher mortality rate among the patients with COVID-19 infection (p < 0.05).

The results of logistic regression analysis of risk factors for COVID-19 infection are shown in Table 2. There were no associations between COVID-19 infection and vitamin D level (OR = 1.00; 95% CI, 0.99-1.02; p = 0.687), age (OR = 1.01; 95% CI, 0.99-1.02; p = 0.349) or BMI (OR = 1.00; 95% CI, 1.00-1.01; p = 0.401) among the 96 positive patients. There was a visible clustering of deceased patients who were aged 60 years or older and had serum 25(OH)D levels below 20 ng/mL, suggesting a potential association between vitamin D deficiency, older age, and increased mortality risk (Fig. 2).

The results of univariate and multivariable logistic analyses of factors associated with COVID-19 mortality are shown in Table 4. In univariate analysis, COVID-19-related mortality was significantly associated with both age and vitamin D level. After adjusting for potential confounders in multivariable logistic regression, individuals with vitamin D deficiency had a significantly higher risk of mortality compared to those with sufficient vitamin D levels (OR = 10.68; 95% CI, 1.18-96.45; p = 0.035). Furthermore, individuals aged 65 to 74 years had a significantly higher risk of death compared to those aged 19 to 44 years (OR = 12.91; 95% CI, 1.29-128.83; p = 0.029).

4. DISCUSSION

This study explored the association between vitamin D status and COVID-19 infection and mortality among patients tested at a medical center in Taiwan. While vaccination remains the primary strategy for preventing COVID-19, the use of nutraceuticals to enhance immune function has also been proposed as a complementary approach.2 From this standpoint, the present study aimed to evaluate the potential impact of the antiviral properties of vitamin D on the risk of COVID-19 infection and related mortality.

Regarding the association between vitamin D status and COVID-19 infection, our results showed that vitamin D deficiency was not significantly associated with susceptibility to COVID-19, aligning with the results of previous studies.18,19 In addition, genome-wide association study data from the COVID-19 Host Genetics Initiative showed no genetic evidence that elevated vitamin D levels reduce the risk of COVID-19 infection, hospitalization, or progression to severe disease in the general population.19 Several other studies have also suggested that vitamin D is not significantly associated with the risk of COVID-19 infection, and that supplementation offers no clear protective benefit.20–22

Antioxidant agents, such as N-acetylcysteine, have also demonstrated beneficial immunomodulatory effects in COVID-19 patients, suggesting shared biological pathways with vitamin D’s anti-inflammatory actions.23 Beyond its well-established role in bone metabolism,13 vitamin D also plays a crucial role in modulating the immune response. Vitamin D status is influenced by factors such as sun exposure and skin color or ethnicity, which may impact susceptibility to COVID-19 infection. In addition, interindividual and interethnic variations in vitamin D-binding proteins may affect vitamin D bioavailability, and COVID-19 mortality rates have been negatively correlated with mean population vitamin D levels.15,24,25 These findings underscore the complexity of the relationship between vitamin D and COVID-19, emphasizing the need for further research to clarify these interactions and guide public health interventions.

Regarding the association between vitamin D status and COVID-19-related mortality, our results showed that vitamin D deficiency was significantly associated with an increased risk of death. This is consistent with previous studies,18,22,26 suggesting that supplementation may help reduce the risk of COVID-19-related mortality.27 COVID-19 has caused millions of infections and deaths worldwide, especially among older adults. De Smet et al26 reported that vitamin D deficiency was correlated with an increased risk of COVID-19 mortality, particularly among older patients. The results of our study support the association between vitamin D deficiency and an increased risk of COVID-19-related mortality in older adults. It is important to note that this study was conducted at a single medical center in Taiwan, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Future research involving more diverse populations and healthcare settings is needed to validate our results.

Several limitations warrant consideration in this study. First, vitamin D testing was self-funded, which may have introduced selection bias toward individuals of higher socioeconomic status or those suspected of having conditions such as osteoporosis, chronic kidney disease, intestinal malabsorption disorders, hyperparathyroidism, and similar diseases. Second, information on whether individuals were taking vitamin D supplements was not available, limiting the ability to account for the effects of supplementation. Third, the number of RT-PCR tests conducted per individual in the database varied; however, this study only analyzed data from the most recent positive RT-PCR result, defined as the last positive result recorded for each individual during the study period. Fourth, due to the diversity of vaccine types, complex vaccination regimens, and the intricate relationship between vaccination status and diagnosis, vaccination data were not incorporated into this analysis. Lastly, 15 of the 96 individuals infected with COVID-19 died, of whom 11 had vitamin D deficiency. Due to the small number of people in each age group, the 95% CI estimates were relatively wide.

Although previous retrospective studies have reported inconsistent findings regarding the association between vitamin D deficiency and COVID-19 infection and outcomes, our results demonstrated that vitamin D deficiency was not associated with an increased risk of infection but was significantly associated with a higher risk of mortality. The mean serum 25(OH)D level among the participants was 22.8 ng/mL, and approximately 46.0% of the participants had vitamin D deficiency. These findings suggest that vitamin D supplementation may improve clinical outcomes in patients with COVID-19. Given the high prevalence of deficiency and its potential impact on prognosis, further research is warranted to evaluate the use of vitamin D supplementation as a possible adjunctive preventive strategy for COVID-19. The integration of vitamin D monitoring into smart healthcare systems may complement future precision strategies for infectious disease prevention.28
