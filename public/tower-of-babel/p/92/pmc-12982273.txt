Tracking Contaminant Trends: PFAS, Metals and Metalloids in Indo-Pacific Bottlenose (Tursiops aduncus) and Common Dolphins (Delphinus delphis) from South Australia
Authors: Rebecca Souter, Damian Lettoof, Catherine Kemper, Lucy Woolford, Anne-Lise Chaber, Luciana Möller, Sam Gaylard, Ikuko Tomo, Mike Bossley, Gavin Stevenson, Anu Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPer- and polyfluorinated alkyl substances (PFAS), metals, and metalloids were analysed in liver of 42 Indo-Pacific bottlenose dolphins (Tursiops aduncus) and 28 common dolphins (Delphinus delphis) from South Australia (SA). Dolphins sampled between 1993 and 2015 (20 T. aduncus) were screened for eleven PFAS, while those sampled between 2021 and 2023 (22 T. aduncus and 28 D. delphis) were analysed for twenty-nine PFAS, and eighteen metals and metalloids. Twenty-one PFAS were identified overall, with PFOS comprising ~ 80% of ∑PFAS. Average ∑PFAS concentrations were similar among T. aduncus and D. delphis during 2021–2023. ∑PFAS was significantly higher in Adelaide Dolphin Sanctuary (ADS) T. aduncus (0.4–5.2 compared to < 2.7 mg/kg ww elsewhere). A decreasing trend in ∑PFAS, primarily driven by PFOS, was observed over time. A preliminary risk assessment based on tentative critical concentrations and hazard quotients indicated a reduced potential risk of adverse effects from PFOS exposure over time. Reductions may be due to regulatory efforts. ADS T. aduncus also exhibited significantly higher lead (0.13–1.9 mg/kg dw) and tin (0.15–1.1 mg/kg dw). In other SA regions, a low Selenium: Mercury ratio in 50% of T. aduncus, alongside Hg concentrations up to 2350 mg/kg dw, highlights a toxicity concern. This study is unique in being the first in Australia to report changes in hepatic PFAS in dolphins over time. It underscores the importance of contaminant load reassessments to understand bioaccumulation. Findings will help to understand contaminant threats to human, wildlife, and environmental health.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00244-025-01179-w.

Introduction

Persistent organic pollutants (POPs), metals, and metalloids are often introduced into the ocean through human activities. The ocean acts as a sink for contaminants and these increase in concentration in the tissues of organisms over time–a process known as bioaccumulation. Many contaminants are bioavailable and persistent, biomagnifying in higher trophic species such as dolphins (Lavery et al. 2008; Stockin et al. 2021). Some of these contaminants are known to reach toxic concentrations in mammals, with adverse effects on immune, endocrine, reproductive, and nervous system functions (Ankley et al. 2021; Desforges et al. 2016; Kakuschke et al. 2005; López-Berenguer et al. 2020a; Naidu et al. 2021; Page-Karjian et al. 2020).

Per- and polyfluorinated alkyl substances (PFAS) are a class of synthetic aliphatic compounds, consisting of a carbon chain and multiple fluorine atoms (Buck et al. 2011; Carlson et al. 2022). There are thousands of PFAS compounds, classified by carbon and fluorine chain length, and functional group (e.g., perfluoroalkyl carboxylic acids (PFCAs) and perfluoroalkyl sulfonic acids (PFSAs). Additional categories include precursors or related compounds (Buck et al. 2011; Pizzorno 2024). These compounds are used in a variety of industrial and consumer processes due to heat resistance, chemical resistance, hydrophobia, and lipophobia (Buck et al. 2011). PFAS are highly stable and persistent in the environment, resulting in some having strong bioaccumulative properties (Kannan et al. 2001; Seyoum et al. 2020). Adverse health outcomes such as hepatotoxicity, endocrine disruption, and impaired immune function have been associated with PFAS exposure in humans (Di Nisio et al. 2019; Blake and Fenton 2020; Wang et al. 2022) and laboratory mice (Peden-Adams et al. 2007; Yahia et al. 2008; Long et al. 2013; Ehrlich et al. 2023). Studies of PFAS exposure in bottlenose dolphins (Tursiops spp.) have detected changes in immunological parameters and suggest PFAS elicits chronic immune stimulation and subsequent immune suppression (Fair et al. 2013; Soloff et al. 2017). Similarly, Kannan et al. (2006) found Perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA) and Perfluorooctane sulfonate (PFOS) were higher in sea otters that died from infectious disease, further supporting potential immunotoxic effects in marine mammals. PFOS and PFOA were phased out of production and importation in Australia starting in 2002 (Toms et al. 2014). PFOS, PFOA, Perfluorohexanesulfonic acid (PFHxS), and long-chain PFCAs, in addition to their salts and related compounds, have since been added to the Stockholm Convention on Persistent Organic Pollutants, regulating their use globally.

Metals and metalloids have been frequently reported in marine ecosystems (Stavros et al. 2011; Briffa et al. 2020). They can enter the environment through natural processes such as erosion, weathering, and forest fires, and are also introduced at amplified concentrations through anthropogenic activities including mining, vehicle emissions, industry, and stormwater runoff (Briffa et al. 2020). Metals (e.g., mercury, cadmium, chromium, lead, zinc, and silver) have known adverse health outcomes in dolphins such as immune suppression, endocrine disruption, and are neurodegenerative (Schaefer et al. 2011; Desforges et al. 2016; Davis et al. 2021).

The marine ecosystem of South Australia (SA) is made up of eight bioregions to encompass the varying ecosystem and habitat types (Edyvane 1999a, b). South Australia is the driest state in Australia, with annual evaporation exceeding annual precipitation. This climate system with low freshwater inflow gives rise to two unique hypersaline inverse or negative estuarine ecosystems (i.e., where seawater is concentrated due to removal of freshwater (Nunes et al. 1990)), Spencer Gulf (SG) and Gulf St. Vincent (GSV). The hypersalinity (seasonal salinity up to 48 parts per thousand in SG and 42 in GSV) observed in these inverse estuarine environments (particularly in the northern or upper gulfs) is the result of reduced oceanic flushing (Nunes et al. 1990; de Silva Samarasinghe 1998; Kämpf 2014). The localised accumulation of anthropogenic contaminants due to limited dispersal or dilution is a risk factor for these environmental systems. Indo-Pacific bottlenose dolphins (Tursiops aduncus) from the SA gulfs have previously been reported with high concentrations of persistent organic and inorganic pollutants, including organochlorines, metals (cadmium, lead, mercury, and zinc), and PFAS (Lavery et al. 2008; Lavery et al. 2009; Gaylard et al. 2017; Weijs et al. 2020).

The Port River Estuary, GSV, is the state’s major shipping port, receiving various anthropogenic discharges and urban runoff (Bossley et al. 2017). This ecosystem is part of the Adelaide Dolphin Sanctuary (ADS) established in 2005 to protect the resident population of approximately 30 T. aduncus. Population demographics of the ADS resident dolphin population has been studied for over 30 years (Steiner & Bossley 2008; Bossley et al. 2017), with recent population declines being reported (DEW 2022; Haigh et al. 2025). Marine mammals are important sentinels for ecosystem health (Bossart 2011). Apex trophic positions and long lifespans (approximate longevity of T. aduncus in SA is 32 years (Kemper et al. 2019a)) influence bioaccumulation of contaminants.

This study investigates hepatic PFAS concentrations in T. aduncus and common dolphins (Delphinus delphis) from different SA regions, and evaluates PFAS concentrations utilising data collected over two periods: 1993–2015 and 2021–2023. Additionally, hepatic metal (aluminium, cadmium, chromium, cobalt, copper, iron, lead, manganese, mercury, molybdenum, nickel, strontium, tin, vanadium, and zinc) and metalloid (selenium, arsenic, and antimony) concentrations were assessed in dolphins during 2021–2023 to update the status of inorganic pollutants in these species. This study may contribute to understanding the potential factors associated with the decline of resident ADS dolphins, highlighting contaminants of concern and providing critical insights to inform conservation strategies.

Materials and Methods

Sample Collection

Two datasets were utilized: dolphins collected during 1993–2015 by the South Australian Museum (SAM) and dolphins collected during 2021–2023 by the University of Adelaide (UofA) (Table S1). Deceased dolphins (beach cast, live stranding mortalities, incidental captures) underwent postmortem investigations at UofA or SAM. Samples obtained were approved for collection by the Department for Environment and Water (DEW), South Australia. Dolphins (excluding known resident T. aduncus from the ADS) were grouped by region according to their stranding locations. These locations were grouped based on SA marine bioregions (Fig. 1) (Edyvane 1999a).

The study region and dolphin stranding location from samples collected during 1993–2015 and 2021–2023 in South Australia. Bioregions are presented as different colours on the map

Animals of all decomposition scores were considered for collection during 1993–2015, but only animals in codes 2 and 3 (Geraci and Lounsbury 2005) were collected for examination during 2021–2023 (Table S2). Deceased dolphins were stored refrigerated or frozen until necropsy. Some specimens were frozen prior to transport to the necropsy laboratory. Post-mortem investigations were performed by veterinarians, pathologists, and zoologists, and involved extensive investigation into cause of death and life history.

During necropsies, liver samples from 20 T. aduncus were collected during 1993–2015 and from 22 T. aduncus and 28 D. delphis during 2021–2023. Samples were excised from dolphins using a clean stainless-steel blade and subsequently placed in a food grade polypropylene bag (1993–2015 dataset) or wrapped in aluminium foil cleaned with acetone (2021–2023 dataset) prior to storage at −20 °C. Representative tissue samples were collected and stored frozen or fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for ancillary diagnostics. Pathological findings for dolphins collected during 2021–2023 will be reported elsewhere.

Relative Age

Dolphins were placed into three relative age classes: neonate, immature and mature. Neonates were determined based on the presence of neonatal rostral vibrissae, fetal folds, absence of erupted teeth, and an open to recently healed umbilicus (Kemper et al. 2019b) Animals were considered aborted or stillborn if there was diffuse fetal atelectasis; these individuals were grouped in the neonate category. Sexual maturity was determined using the protocol described in Kemper et al. (2014, 2019a). Female dolphins were classified as sexually mature if there was gross evidence of one or more corpora in the ovaries. Males were considered sexually mature if there was histological evidence of active spermatogenesis and developed germinal layers within seminiferous tubules. Some males in the 1993–2015 dataset were assigned a sexual maturity status based on testis weight (Kemper et al. 2014).

Sample Preparation and Transport

Contaminant analysis was performed on 70 liver samples (28 D. delphis and 42 T. aduncus) from three relative age classes (Table 1). Twenty T. aduncus sampled during 1993–2015 were examined for PFAS and the remaining 50 animals sampled during 2021–2023 were examined for PFAS, metals, and metalloids. Two approximately 0.5 g portions of liver were subsampled from frozen samples using a clean scalpel blade and implementing laboratory space decontamination between samples. Samples were placed in sterile 5 mL polyethylene tubes and transported on dry ice to the National Measurement Institute (NMI), North Ryde, New South Wales, Australia.

Analysis of PFAS in Dolphin Liver Samples

Eleven PFAS during 1993–2015 and twenty-nine during 2021–2023 were analysed in dolphin liver (Table S3) by NMI using liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) (Wells et al. 2024). Analyses were performed in 2015 and 2023. Samples were prepared by homogenisation by manual slicing/mixing, sub sampled and 13C isotopically labelled extracted internal standards (EIS) were added. Samples underwent saponification by tumbling with alkaline Methanol (0.01N Potassium Hydroxide in Methanol, ChemSupply AR grade, and Merck LCMS grade respectively). The extracts were purified by activated carbon (Envi-CARB 120–400 Mesh SPE, 1 g, Supelco), eluted with Methanol, followed by centrifugal filtration and evaporation under nitrogen. 13C isotopically labelled standards were added to the sample before instrumental analysis to serve as recovery standards.

Qualitative/quantitative analysis for PFASs was performed with a high-performance liquid chromatograph/triple quadrupole mass spectrometer. In 2015 this was an Agilent 1100 coupled to SCIEX 4000 QTRAP operating in triple quad mode and in 2023 a SCIEX Exion coupled to SCIEX 6500 + QTRAP operating in triple quad mode was used. In both cases, target analytes were injected and eluted from a C18 column (Aquity BEH XBridge, 2.1 × 100 mm × 1.7 µm, 130 Å, Waters). Multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) of two characteristic transitions was performed for PFAS analysis where possible. Analyte identification was confirmed when target transitions are detected in both the monitored MRMs, within retention time windows established by 13C isotopically labelled EIS. Quantification was based on the use of the 13C labelled EIS and certified calibration standards, for PFHxS and PFOS the calibration standards contained branched and linear isomers, which were calculated separately and summed for reporting. All standards were sourced from Wellington Laboratories (Canada). All analyte and EIS details, including MRMs used are presented in Table S4.

Analysis of Metals and Metalloids in Dolphin Liver Samples

Eighteen elements were determined by NMI in 50 liver samples from dolphins collected during 2021–2023 (Table S7). Samples were homogenised and subsequently digested with 3 mL nitric acid and 1 mL hydrochloric acid by heating on a hot block at 95–100 °C for 2 h. Samples were then made up to a volume of 40 mL with high purity water prior to dilution and analysis using inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP-AES) (Agilent 5110) and inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) (Agilent 7900).

Quality Assurance/ Quality Control

Analysis methodology and instrumentation at NMI dictates the Limits of Reporting (LOR) for individual compounds. The LOR ranged between < 0.0005 and < 0.5 mg/kg dw and ww (Table S5–S7). Each PFAS laboratory batch included a reagent blank, a spiked reagent blank, duplicates, and spiked samples (if sample mass permitted) (Table S8, S9). PFAS results are corrected for EIS recoveries with an acceptable range of 50–150%. Recoveries for PFHxA in the 1993–2015 dataset (Table S10), and EtFOSA and EtFOSE in the 2021–2023 (Table S11) were outside the acceptable range in > 50% of samples and were therefore omitted from further analysis. For inorganic contaminants, blanks, spiked blank, and spiked samples were prepared for each sample batch analysed, with an acceptable recovery range of 75–120% (Table S12). For Li, recoveries ranged from 76 to 124%. Li was excluded from further analysis. Matrix Spike levels for Al, Co, Fe, Hg, Se, Sb, and Ze was less than 50% of some sample concentrations, therefore recovery could not be reported. There were no metals or metalloids detected in blanks above the LOR.

Preliminary Risk Assessment

A preliminary risk assessment to assess potential toxicological threats associated with hepatic PFOS, PFHxS, PFOA, PFNA and PFDA accumulation in T. aduncus and D. delphis was performed following methodology described for cetaceans (Liu et al. 2024). Tentative critical concentrations (TCCs) and hazard quotients (HQs) were estimated for dolphins using lowest observable adverse effect levels (LOAEL) associated with PFAS exposure derived from laboratory models (Seacat et al. 2003; Guo et al. 2019; Pfohl et al. 2020; National Toxicology Program 2022), with scaling factors considered for inter- and intraspecific variability (Sample et al. 1996). Calculations used to determine TCC and HQs, and associated values for individual animals are presented in Text S1 and Table S13.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses and visualisation were performed using R statistical software (RStudio version 2023.06.1 + 524) and Microsoft Office Excel (Version 69.17.1). Maps were created using QGIS 3.34.15-Prizren. For compounds that were identified below the LOR, half the LOR was used for statistical analysis (for example LOR < 0.1, 0.05 was used). Spearman correlational analysis was first performed to detect correlation between contaminants (Fig. S1). A probability (p) value was set at 0.05 for consideration of statistical significance.

Tursiops aduncus and D. delphis contaminant concentrations were analysed independently to accommodate biases due to differing distribution and diets. Generalised Linear Models (GLMs) were used to assess relationships among contaminant concentrations and biological traits (Tersago et al. 2004; Gundersen et al. 2017) using the lme4 and performance packages (Bates et al. 2015; Lüdecke et al. 2021. Due to the small sample size, not all variables could be included in a single model; therefore, two models for each contaminant were fitted for each species. One model had the contaminant as the response variable, with body length, sex, and an interaction between the two as the predictor variables—this was to assess patterns of bioaccumulation (increasing contaminant concentration with body size/ age), and sex-specific differences that could be explained by maternal transfer. Body length displayed high collinearity (VIF > 10) with sexual maturity; therefore, only body length was used for statistical analyses. The other model had the contaminant as the response variable, with region, sex, and an interaction between the two as the predictor variables—this was to assess patterns of location-driven contamination, and sex-specific differences.

Metals where more than 50% of samples with concentrations below the LOR were excluded from statistical analysis. This included V, Sb, Al, and Cr. Additionally, Mo and Ni were excluded as only 30 liver samples were analysed for each. Body length in male and female D. delphis was statistically similar (p = 0.33), permitting comparison of contaminant concentrations between sexes. In T. aduncus, body length in males was significantly higher than females (p = 0.04) in the animals analysed, so models assessing relationships between contaminants and body length in male and female T. aduncus may not be comparable.

Results and Discussion

Biological Information

Table 1 provides a summary of the size information for each relative age class of dolphins included in this study. Raw data are available in the Supplementary Material (Table S1).

During 1993–2015, 20 T. aduncus were sampled, of which eight were known residents of the ADS. Additionally, six dolphins from GSV, four from Eyre Bioregion (EY), and two from Murat Bioregion (MU) were collected. During 2021–2023, 22 T. aduncus were collected, of which five were known residents of the ADS. Additional dolphins were from GSV (n = 13) and SG (n = 4). Twenty-eight D. delphis were collected during 2021–2023, 21 from the GSV, three from the SG, and four from the Coorong Bioregion (CO).

Liver PFAS Concentrations

PFAS in Indo-Pacific Bottlenose Dolphins

Tursiops aduncus samples collected during 1993–2015 had the highest concentration of total PFAS when compared with T. aduncus and D. delphis from 2021 to 2023. In 1993–2015, eight PFAS (of 11 screened) were identified (Fig. 2). PFOS was the most predominant compound, with a mean concentration of 0.98 mg/kg ww (Fig. 2) and an average of 73.9% total PFAS composition across samples (Fig. 3a). In 2021–2023, twenty-one PFAS (of 29 screened) were identified (Fig. 2). Similarly, PFOS was the most frequently reported compound, with an average concentration of 0.16 mg/kg ww (Fig. 2), comprising 84.4% the composition of total PFAS (Fig. 3b). In both datasets, PFOS and total PFAS were highly correlated (r2 = 0.99–1). In 1993–2015, total PFAS was moderately to strongly correlated with total PFSA, long (PFOS) and short (PFHxS) chain PFSA, total PFCA and long chain PFCA (PFHpA, PFOA, PFNA, PFDA, PFUdA, PFDoA) (r2 > 0.81). Similarly in 2021–2023, total PFSA, long (PFHpS, PFOS, PFNS, PFDS) and short (PFPeS, PFHxS) chain PFSA, total PFCA and long chain PFCA (PFHpA, PFOA, PFNA, PFDA, PFUnDA, PFDoDA, PFTrDA, PFTeDA) were strongly correlated with total PFAS (r2 > 0.88). Short chain PFCA (PFHxA in 1993–2015 and PFPeA and PFHxA in 2021–2023) were weakly or not correlated with total PFAS (r2 < 0.57) (Fig S1). However, recoveries for PFHxA were outside the acceptable range in the majority of samples in the 1993–2015 dataset (Table S10) and both PFPeA and PFHxA were below the LOR in > 81% of samples analysed in 2021–2023 (Table S6). Due to the high correlations observed (Fig. S1) and potential similarities in mode of action (McCarthy et al. 2021), total PFAS was used for further analysis.

In both datasets, region was a significant predictor of total PFAS (X2 = 9.5–43.3, p < 0.01), with ADS dolphins showing significantly higher concentrations when compared to other regions. The ADS is located within metropolitan Adelaide, where 82% of the state’s total population resides (Australian Bureau of Statistics). The ADS resident dolphins inhabit the Port River Estuary, a region of intense urbanisation and a history of PFAS contamination (Gaylard et al. 2017). It is likely PFAS are concentrated there through wastewater, stormwater, landfill, urban and industrial landscapes, and through maritime activities (Gaylard et al. 2017; EPA 2022, 2023). Several industrial facilities along the Port River with identified groundwater contamination, such as the former Port Adelaide Wastewater Treatment Plant (PAWWTP) and fuel supply stations, which have previously used fluorinated foams, may have also contributed to contamination within the area. Gaylard et al. (2017) suggested stormwater wetlands and shipping terminals may be significant entry points for PFAS into the Port River.

There was a strong negative relationship with total PFAS and increasing body length for ADS T. aduncus (X2 = 9.7, p < 0.01) (Fig. S2). Four of the ADS dolphins from years 1993–2015 were neonates, so PFAS burdens were likely the result of maternal transfer. These neonates presented the highest hepatic concentrations of total PFAS (1.7–5.2 mg/kg ww compared to < 1.4 mg/kg ww). Since no ADS neonates were studied during 2021–2023, the dolphins that were sampled must have acquired PFAS from other sources such as diet (Gaylard et al. 2017) and excretory mechanisms likely influenced body burdens (Van de Vijver et al. 2004; Houde et al. 2006b; Hart et al. 2008; Gebbink et al. 2016). It is likely that the absence of ADS neonates during 2021–2023 resulted in a false negative effect of increasing body size on PFAS concentration in the model.

Within the ADS (n = 8 in 1993–2015, n = 5 in 2021–2023) and GSV (n = 6 in 1993–2015, n = 13 in 2021–2023), there was a significant negative trend in ∑8PFAS over time (X2 = 9.7, p < 0.01), with an estimate of −0.054 mg/kg annually (Fig. 4). This was characterised by a significant decrease in PFOS concentration (−0.054 mg/kg/ year, X2 = 10.1, p = 0.001).

There were not enough data to evaluate temporal changes in SG (n = 4, 2021–2023 only), CO (n = 0), MU (n = 2, 1993–2015 only), or EY (n = 4, 1993–2015 only) bioregions. In 2020, PFAS were banned for use in firefighting foams in SA and contaminated sites along the Port River have commenced remediation activities, predicted to reduce the entry of PFAS into the ADS (PFAS National Environmental Management Plan (NEMP 2.0); EPA 2022). Additional reductions may have occurred from phasing out PFOS and PFOA production and importation into Australia from 2002 (Toms et al. 2014) and the subsequent addition of PFOS, PFOA, PFHxS, long-chain PFCAs, and their salts and related compounds to the Stockholm Convention on POPs. Decreasing trends in PFAS have been observed in the Indian River Lagoon, Florida, and Charleston, South Carolina bottlenose dolphin populations (Lynch et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2021; Liu et al. 2024). However, regulatory restrictions on long-chain PFAS have possibly led to rising levels of short-chain PFAS alternatives (Liu et al. 2024). This was not observed in the present study (e.g., average PFHxS decreased in T. aduncus from 0.025 mg/kg ww in 1993–2015 to 0.005 mg/kg ww in 2021–2023). However, short chain compounds are assumed to have a reduced affinity for accumulation in biota (Buck et al. 2011; Bowman 2015) therefore, hepatic burdens may be non-representative of environmental contamination (Brendel et al. 2018). In the Pearl River Estuary (PRE), China, PFOS concentrations in marine mammals have increased over time despite restrictions, likely due to its high bioaccumulative potential or ongoing release (Giesy and Kannan 2001; Houde et al. 2006a; Guo et al. 2023).

Comparison of PFAS Concentrations Between Dolphin Species in SA and Elsewhere

Hepatic PFAS concentrations during 2021–2023 did not show significant differences between D. delphis and T. aduncus in this study. Although D. delphis is believed to range over a larger area than T. aduncus in SA, including outside the gulfs, it has been recorded utilizing GSV for extended periods (Filby et al. 2010; Zanardo et al. 2016). Most dolphins sampled in 2021–2023 were from the GSV (21/ 28 D. delphis, 18/ 23 T. aduncus). Factors likely influencing exposure and accumulation of PFAS in dolphins include diet, compound properties (e.g., carbon and fluorine chain length, ionic state, head group), species-specific metabolism and accumulation rates, environmental pH, and biological parameters such as age, sex, reproductive status, and geographical distribution (Lynch et al. 2019; Dickman and Aga 2022; Brake et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2023). Understanding the half-life of PFAS in dolphins, and the marine environment more broadly, is not well understood (Houde et al. 2006a).

Delphinus delphis mature females had the lowest total PFAS concentrations (0.03–0.06 mg/kg ww) compared to other age categories. Because these were parous females, they may have offloaded some of their PFAS burden. Comparatively lower PFAS in mature females has also been reported in other species including melon-headed whales (Peponocephala electra) and harbor porpoises (Phocoena phocoena) (Van de Vijver et al. 2004; Van de Vijver et al. 2007; Dorneles et al. 2008a; Hart et al. 2008).

Total PFAS concentrations in T. aduncus reported in this study were lower when compared to Tursiops spp. from other Australian locations (< 5.2 compared to < 19.5 mg/kg ww), but higher than some locations elsewhere. Foord et al. (2024a) recently reported mean concentrations of hepatic total PFAS at 9.98 and 4.33 mg/kg ww in the Port Phillip Bay and Gippsland Lakes, Victoria, respectively, with the highest concentration reported globally in a Tursiops sp. at 19.5 mg/kg ww. In the Swan River, Western Australia, concentrations of PFOS in T. aduncus have been reported up to 14.0 mg/kg ww, with a mean of 6.97 mg/kg ww (Gaylard et al. 2017). In Chinese waters, hepatic PFOS in the Indo-Pacific finless porpoises (Neophocaena phocaenoides) and Indo-Pacific humpback dolphins (Sousa chinensis) have been reported at levels ranging from 0.14 to 15.3 mg/kg ww (Lam et al. 2016; Wang et al. 2021; Guo et al. 2023). In the Mediteranean, hepatic PFOS in common bottlenose dolphins (Tursiops truncatus) ranged from 0.006 to 1.9 mg/kg ww (López-Berenguer et al. 2020b; Sciancalepore et al. 2021) and in the United Kingdom P. phocoena, concentrations up to 2.7 mg/kg ww have been reported (Androulakakis et al. 2022). For D. delphis, median total PFAS concentrations in the present study were 0.18 mg/kg ww (range 0.03–0.64 mg/kg ww). This is comparable with concentrations in D. delphis from the Mediterranean (mean 0.24, range 0.086–0.4, n = 2) (López-Berenguer et al. 2020b), yet higher than levels reported in New Zealand (median 0.034, range 0.01–0.11 mg/kg ww) (Stockin et al. 2021).

Preliminary Risk Assessment

Four T. aduncus from 1993–2015 (ADS = 3, GSV = 1) displayed PFOS HQ > 1 (Fig. 5), highlighting a potential risk for adverse effects associated with accumulation. No dolphins from 2021 to 2023 presented with high-risk concentrations, suggesting PFOS regulation efforts may have reduced risk to SA dolphins. All other compounds presented with TCCs below those associated with adverse effects (Table S13), and are therefore considered low risk. PFAS exposure and toxicity thresholds in dolphins remain unclear (Lam et al. 2016; Wang et al. 2021; Liu et al. 2024), and extrapolations between species are limited by species-specific physiological differences and environmental settings (i.e., controlled vs. uncontrolled environments) (Ehrlich et al. 2023). Continued regulation and the phasing out of all PFAS, along with repeated testing in the marine environment and its biota, would further reduce risks and help assess the effectiveness of contaminant management practices.

Hepatic Metal and Metalloid Concentrations

Concentrations of all analysed metals and selenium in this study are reported in Table S7 and results for metal GLMs are presented in Table S14. Metals where significant relationships were detected are reported and discussed below. In many cases, limited sample sizes make comparisons difficult to interpret.

Arsenic

A mature male T. aduncus from GSV exhibited a concentration of As higher than all other sampled T. aduncus (9.1 vs < 3.6 mg/kg dw), although this was not statistically significant. In D. delphis, a significant positive relationship between As and body length was strongest in mature females, suggesting bioaccumulation. Mature females had a significantly higher concentration (1.5–6.5 mg/kg dw) compared to mature males (0.75–1.3 mg/kg dw) and immature animals (0.51–2.8 mg/kg dw). This suggests that As was not transferred maternally, similar to findings of Kubota et al. (2005) in the Dall’s porpoise (Phocoenoides dalli). Arsenic has known bioaccumulative potential of which the main source to marine mammals is diet, particularly in species where cephalopods are a prey focus (Kubota et al. 2001; Kunito et al. 2008). Cephalopods are important prey for SA T. aduncus and D. delphis (Kemper and Gibbs 2001; Gibbs et al. 2011). Arsenic is introduced into the environment through natural (e.g., geological processes) and anthropogenic (e.g., agriculture, industry, and mining) processes (Kunito et al 2008; Gaylard et al. 2011). This is the first published report of As in SA dolphins and concentrations are within range of levels reported in these species elsewhere (Table 2).

Cadmium

Cd concentrations were highest in mature individuals from both species, although this relationship was only significant in D. delphis, where the relationship was strongest in females (X2 = 148, p < 0.01). Cd has been shown to bioaccumulate in dolphins (Lavery et al. 2008) but has not been shown to cross the placenta and transfer maternally (Polizzi et al. 2013). The Cd concentrations reported in this study are lower or within range of previous hepatic Cd levels reported in small cetaceans elsewhere (Kunito et al. 2004; Weijs et al. 2016; Mapunda et al. 2017; Lischka et al. 2021; Jarolimek et al. 2023; dos Santos Lima et al. 2024; Gosnell et al. 2024) and are lower than that previously reported in SA (EPA 2000; Lavery et al. 2008) (Table 2). In the present study, mean Cd concentrations in T. aduncus measured at 1.4 mg/kg dw with a maximum concentration in a mature male from GSV (12 mg/kg dw). Lavery et al. (2008) reported a maximum Cd concentration of 333.2 mg/kg dw in an individual from SG, most likely due to the Port Pirie lead and zinc smelter which released approximately 1900 and 1600 kg of Cd/year into the SG through water and atmospheric emissions, respectively (Corbin & Wade 2004; National Pollutant Inventory 2023). Limited data for SG in this study may have resulted in an underestimation of current Cd levels in SG dolphins. The source of Cd in the GSV is unclear as Cd prevalence has not previously been associated with terrestrial inputs such as wastewater and stormwater (Gaylard et al. 2011). However, Cd-contaminated sewage sludge, originating from the former PAWWTP, was identified in 2000, suggesting historical contamination from land-based leachate (EPA 2023). Other Cd sources, including geological or diet-related, are also possible (Dorneles et al. 2007; Gaylard et al. 2011).

Cobalt

In D. delphis, Co concentrations were significantly positively related with body length, suggesting bioaccumulation (X2 = 18.9, p < 0.01). Females displayed higher Co concentrations; however, this relationship was not consistent among regions which is likely the result of low sample sizes. Cobalt concentrations in the dolphins tested in this study are within the range of previous hepatic Co levels reported in odontocetes globally (Kunito et al. 2004; Lischka et al. 2021; Page et al. 2024) (Table 2). Co concentrations were detected in trace amounts, and it is an essential element, thus levels reported here are not considered problematic. This is the first published report of Co in SA dolphins.

Copper

In T. aduncus and D. delphis, Cu was significantly negatively associated (X2 = 5.1–8.6, p < 0.02) with body length. Cu concentration was highest in neonates (mean 214 mg/kg dw in T. aduncus compared to mean 67 mg/kg dw in mature males of the same species). Lavery et al. (2008) also found dolphins in tooth category 1 (corresponding to neonates and immature animals) to have higher hepatic Cu concentrations. Cu is an essential element and has important physiological roles. It is known to be transferred through lactation in cetaceans (Caon et al. 2008), where there is an increased capacity in young animals to store essential elements required for rapid growth (Kunito et al. 2004; Cáceres-Saez et al. 2016; Mapunda et al. 2017). Cu concentration typically decreases with age before reaching a plateau, reflecting maintenance by homeostasis (dos Santos Lima et al. 2024). No regional or sex differences in Cu concentrations were observed.

Lead

In D. delphis, Pb was highest in a male calf from GSV (2.8 vs. < 0.12 mg/kg dw). In T. aduncus, ADS residents had significantly higher Pb concentrations compared to T. aduncus from other regions (mean 0.7 mg/kg dw compared to < 0.1, X2 = 9.2, p = 0.01). These results differ from Lavery et al. (2008) where T. aduncus Pb concentrations were highest in the SG, likely due to the Port Pirie lead smelter which has been continuously inputting Pb into the environment since 1889 (25,000 and 69,000 kg water and atmospheric inputs in 2022/2023, respectively (National Pollutant Inventory 2023)) (Ward and Hutchings 1996). A small sample size and not testing lead levels in bone make comparisons to other studies difficult. Higher Pb levels observed in the ADS may be the result of leaded petroleum emission from the city of Adelaide (Lavery et al. 2008; Daley et al. 2018). The consumption of lead fishing weights or penetration by a lead bullet can contribute to higher Pb levels in individuals; however, no evidence of this was observed during post-mortem. Most specimens had Pb concentrations within the range of previous hepatic Pb levels reported in cetaceans elsewhere (Kunito et al. 2004; Weijs et al. 2016; Lischka et al. 2021; Jarolimek et al. 2023) and are lower than that previously reported in SA (EPA2000; Lavery et al. 2008) (Table 2). Pb toxicity associated with a hepatic concentration of 3.6 mg/kg ww has been observed in a captive T. truncatus previously (Shlosberg et al. 1997) (12 mg/kg dw assuming 70% hepatic moisture content (Yang & Miyazaki 2003)).

Mercury and Selenium

In male dolphins, a positive association was observed for Hg and body length, with the highest concentrations observed in mature individuals (180–2350 mg/kg dw, compared to 45–300 mg/kg dw in mature females). Hg is known to bioaccumulate with age in cetaceans (Durante et al. 2020) and a weaker association in females is likely due to maternal transfer (Caon et al. 2008).

Using the Se:Hg molar ratio (([Se]/78.96)/[Hg]/200.59)) (López-Berenguer et al. 2020a), 10.7% of D. delphis and 50% of T. aduncus sampled in the present study had a Se:Hg ratio of < 1, suggesting that these individuals are vulnerable to Hg toxicity (Briffa et al. 2020; Zhai et al. 2024). Species was a significant predictor of SeHg ratio (X2 = 15.2, p = < 0.01), regardless of region or sexual maturity, with T. aduncus displaying lower ratios compared to D delphis (Fig. 6a and c). Sexual maturity classifications (particularly neonate and immature categories) had a significant effect on SeHg ratio (X = 9.4, p = 0.02), with higher ratios in these groups compared to mature individuals. This relationship was consistent in both species, supporting bioaccumulation. Hg has been reported to cause oxidative damage to organs such as the brain, liver, and kidney, contributing to immune suppression, neurodegenerative disease, and reduced thyroid function (Schaefer et al. 2011; Reif et al. 2015; Desforges et al. 2016; Davis et al. 2021). In the marine environment, inorganic Hg undergoes methylation by sulphate-reducing bacteria. These processes form the organic form of Hg (MeHg), which is bioaccumulative, more toxic, and can cross the blood–brain-barrier (López-Berenguer et al. 2020a). Se demethylates MeHg and forms a SeHg complex (in addition to other metals such as Cd and Ag), neutralising toxic effects (Cáceres-Saez et al. 2015). A Se:Hg less than 1 suggests that all available Se is bound, physiological Se functions are inhibited, and individuals may be at risk to oxidative stress (López-Berenguer et al. 2020a).

The highest concentration of Hg was observed in a mature male T. aduncus from SG (2,350 mg/kg dw) (Fig. 6b). This is in the upper range of Hg concentrations reported globally (Jarolimek et al. 2023) and exceeds levels reported as having toxic effects in marine mammals (~ 200–1300 mg/kg dw based on 70% hepatic moisture content) (Wagemann and Muir 1984; Rawson et al. 1993; Bennet et al. 2001; Yang and Miyazaki 2003). This individual had a SeHg ratio of 0.6. SG has a known history of metal contamination (hepatic Hg reported at 7035 mg/kg dw in T. aduncus (Lavery et al. 2008)), possibly introduced from iron ore mines and steelworks, but also from natural sources (EPA 2000; Butterfield & Gaylard 2005; Lavery et al. 2008). Additionally, the Port Pirie lead and zinc smelter is a known source of Hg contamination (2.8 and 390 kg water and atmospheric emissions in 2022/2023, respectively (National Pollutant Inventory 2023). Only one mature individual was collected from this region, but previous studies have detected levels equal to and above what is currently reported (EPA 2000; Butterfield & Gaylard 2005; Lavery et al. 2008). To compare T. aduncus Hg concentrations with previous data within the GSV (Butterfield & Gaylard 2005), individuals with a body length of > 1.5 m were used (n = 9). Median concentrations in the present study (300 mg/kg dw) were less than median concentrations previously reported (4060 mg/kg dw). Hg contamination in this region is likely from a variety of sources such as industry and wastewater (Butterfield & Gaylard 2005; Lavery et al. 2008; National Pollutant Inventory 2023). The results of the present study suggest that Hg contamination may be decreasing in the GSV.

Tin

The ADS had significantly higher Sn concentrations (mean 0.9 compared to < 0.05 mg/kg dw elsewhere in SA, X2 = 23.2, p < 0.01). Among D. delphis, a male calf from SG had the highest Sn concentration (3 vs. < 0.24 mg/kg dw). Hepatic Sn has been shown to be representative of organotin burden (Dorneles et al. 2008b). Organotins, which are known endocrine disrupting chemicals (Turan 2021), are widely used in products such as biocides, wood preservatives, and as chemical catalysts or stabilisers (Fent 1996; Turan 2021). Organotin compounds were widely used in antifouling agents until 2003 (Protection of the Sea (Harmful Anti-fouling Systems) Act 2006). Organotin were identified in both sediment and bivalves of the Port River (Mortimer 2004). Further, imposex in marine snails (Lepsiella vinosa) has been identified in this region, possibly reflecting high levels of organotin (Nais et al. 1993). The reasons behind higher hepatic Sn in the SG D. delphis calf are unclear; however, it is possibly that it was vertically transferred (Yang and Miyazaki 2006; Yang et al. 2007). Although interaction with contamination hotspots (e.g., slipways) would be expected to reach high levels in biota (Mortimer 2004), it is possible that there is organotin contamination of areas not yet explored (Ward and Hutchings 1996; Robbins et al. 2017). The hepatic Sn concentrations in this study are comparable or lower to cetaceans elsewhere (Zhou et al. 2001; Dorneles et al. 2008b, 2020) (Table 2). Sn concentrations have not been explored previously in SA dolphins.

Zinc

Mean concentrations of Zn are highest in T. aduncus (Lavery et al. 2008). In D. delphis, significantly higher Zn concentrations were detected in the CO bioregion (X2 = 17.3, p < 0.01). A mature female from CO exhibited particularly high levels (550 mg/kg dw), exceeding those found in dolphins from other SA regions (40–310 mg/kg dw). While small sample sizes may have influenced the results, Zn concentrations in CO dolphins were, on average, higher compared to other regions (382.5 vs. < 190 mg/kg dw). The cause of higher Zn in this region is unknown. In the present study, hepatic Zn in some specimens lie within the upper levels of hepatic Zn reported in cetaceans elsewhere (Jarolimek et al. 2023; dos Santos Lima et al. 2024; Page et al. 2024); however, levels are lower than what has previously been reported in SA (Lavery et al. 2008) (Table 2).

Limitations and Future Recommendations

Interpreting information from specific contaminant concentrations in one tissue type has limitations. Contaminant testing is complicated by the wide range of contaminants in the marine environment, resulting in the accumulation of complex chemical mixtures (Reiter et al. 2023; Foord et al. 2024a, 2024b, 2025), possibly having interactive effects (Reiter et al. 2022). Some contaminants and their organic forms favour certain tissue types (e.g., Cd in the kidney and MeHg in the brain), so testing only hepatic samples may cause bias (Long et al. 1997; Kubota et al. 2005; Endo et al. 2006; Kunito et al. 2008; Polizzi et al. 2013; López-Berenguer et al. 2020a; Kooyomjian et al. 2022; Ortiz-Garcia et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2022, 2023; Zhai et al. 2024). Opportunistic sampling of dead individuals can introduce biases in samples. A larger sample size with better representation over geographic regions and years would improve the accuracy of current results. Our study methodology employed the use of neonate, immature, and mature to categorise individuals based on relative age. Body length does not reliably indicate age in years as individuals maintain a constant body length once they reach physical maturity (Kemper et al. 2014, 2019a). Approximating age in years are important considerations for future studies (Kemper et al. 2014; Barratclough et al. 2019). Stable isotope analysis of dolphin tissues, to better understand intra- and interspecific biological variability (e.g., diet and ecology), could enhance conclusions drawn from this study (Vianna-Gatts et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2024). Time series contaminant analyses of dolphins with known home ranges and life history could further provide valuable information for environmental managers.

Conclusions

Tursiops aduncus from the ADS, an active shipping port and industrial area, presented significantly higher PFAS, tin, and lead concentrations compared to dolphins in other SA study regions. When assessing PFAS concentrations in the ADS over time, a negative trend was observed, suggesting the relative success of remediation efforts and usage ban in SA. This was further reflected by a reduction in the number of individuals displaying PFOS TCCs greater than what has previously been associated with toxicity in mammals. Metal analysis revealed that 50% of T. aduncus and 10% of D. delphis in this study had selenium: mercury ratios below one, with some individuals exhibiting mercury concentrations exceeding those previously associated with toxic effects in dolphins. This study is unique in being the first study in Australia to report change in PFAS over time in the marine environment and reports the first published records of As, Co, and Sn in SA dolphins, offering an important reference for future studies. The importance of reassessing contaminant loads in marine biota to understand bioaccumulation rates is highlighted, providing knowledge to infer current threats to human, wildlife, and environmental health.
