Evaluating mental health integration in PrEP programs to reduce HIV incidence among US gay and bisexual men: a network modeling study
Authors: Udodirim N Onwubiko, Adrien Le Guillou, Allison T Chamberlain, David Benkeser, David P Holland, Stefan D Baral, Samuel M Jenness
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite advances in HIV prevention, achieving elimination in the United States (US) remains challenging, particularly among gay, bisexual, and other men who have sex with men (GBMSM). Psychosocial barriers, including stigma and mental health challenges, influence sexual behaviors and hinder engagement in HIV prevention and care, suggesting that integrated interventions may improve HIV-related service uptake and reduce HIV incidence.MethodsWe adapted a stochastic network model simulating HIV transmission dynamics in a southeastern US metro to include individual-level sexual stigma and mental health factors impacting HIV risk and service engagement. The model assessed the impact of integrating routine mental health screening and resilience-building mental health care into PrEP programs and evaluated intervention effects on HIV incidence over ten years.ResultsEnhancements in mental health services alone led to modest reductions in HIV incidence, averting 12% of new infections with maximum improvements in depression diagnosis and treatment initiation across the general GBMSM population. However, integrated strategies combining mental health care with expanded PrEP and ART coverage resulted in up to a 41% reduction in HIV incidence when targeted at all HIV-negative individuals within PrEP settings, reaching a final-year incidence rate of 0.52 per 100 person-years.ConclusionsIntegrating mental health care into PrEP programs is an important step but it is not sufficient to achieve substantial reductions in HIV incidence. Substantial declines require broader engagement in both PrEP and ART, with a coordinated approach that addresses mental health alongside these HIV prevention strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-15074-0.Keywords: Depression, Mental health, Sexual stigma, Integrated approach to HIV elimination, PrEP engagement, Network models

Introduction

HIV elimination in the United States (US) is an ambitious goal reflecting the remarkable progress made in HIV prevention and treatment over the past decade [1]. Achieving the HIV elimination goals outlined in the Ending the HIV Epidemic (EHE) plan for the US would represent a major public health milestone, potentially marking the end of a long-standing epidemic [1]. Biomedical advancements, particularly HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP), are central to the EHE’s “Prevent” pillar and hold substantial promise for reaching elimination targets if widely adopted [1]. However, despite recent increases in PrEP uptake [2], coverage remains below EHE targets [1], with notable racial disparities among priority populations like gay, bisexual and other men who have sex with men (GBMSM) and among individuals at extreme ages (younger and older adults) [2].

Barriers to PrEP uptake among GBMSM are well-documented and include provider-related challenges (e.g., lack of knowledgeable providers), patient-related factors (e.g., low health literacy, medical mistrust), and systemic issues (e.g., PrEP deserts) [3–8]. Psychosocial challenges, such as sexual stigma and mental health issues, have also been increasingly recognized as key contributors to the underuse of PrEP and other healthcare services [4, 9]. These factors not only directly hinder PrEP engagement but also exacerbate existing barriers, ultimately reducing its real-world impact and slowing the decline in HIV incidence among GBMSM [4–12]. Research further indicates that these psychosocial factors are interconnected [13, 14], suggesting that combined interventions targeting both stigma and mental health may offer an effective approach to improve PrEP utilization and decrease HIV incidence among GBMSM [15, 16].

Past efforts to address sexual stigma in HIV-related efforts have involved a range of interventions targeting various manifestations of stigma across populations and levels of influence. These interventions include structural initiatives aimed at reducing HIV- and sexual behavior-related stigma through anti-discriminatory policies and stigma-reducing approaches in HIV and sexual health services. However, policy protections vary in scope and are often insufficient to address deeply rooted societal stigma. Organizational efforts, such as media campaigns and workforce training, have worked to reduce sexual behavior bias in healthcare, workplaces, and faith-based institutions. Interpersonal interventions have focused on promoting tolerance, empathy, and awareness in relational contexts, while individual-level strategies have aimed to build resilience, self-advocacy skills, and economic empowerment for those affected by stigma (including anticipated, enacted, and internalized forms) [17–24]. These interventions have employed a variety of tools, including information-sharing, skill-building, and counseling [19, 21, 25], often combining these approaches to address stigma mechanisms across socioecological levels [19, 21, 26].

The effectiveness of these interventions in reducing sexual behavior stigma or mitigating its impacts on health service engagement has been mixed. A 2019 systematic review found that stigma-focused interventions were most effective when they incorporated psychological support, improved access to HIV care, or provided economic empowerment to those affected by stigma [26]. Community- or interpersonal-level interventions, or those relying solely on information-sharing, showed limited success unless paired with psychological or structural support for persons affected by stigma [26]. Addressing how stigma-focused interventions influence internalized homonegativity (also referred to as self-stigma) is especially critical, as self-acceptance and motivational activation are key to driving beneficial behavioral changes such as increased HIV screening, treatment adherence, and prevention persistence that are vital for effective HIV response [27, 28].

Psychological support-based stigma interventions have shown positive outcomes in mitigating stigma’s impact on HIV-related behaviors. For instance, a multi-city randomized trial in India with 608 HIV-negative adult GBMSM found that an intervention combining resilience-based counseling and voluntary HIV testing resulted in a 56% reduction in condomless anal sex at four months and a 72% reduction at twelve months [28]. Similarly, a 2022 meta-analysis examining stigma interventions among US GBMSM reported significant increases in HIV screening uptake when participants engaged with trained personnel or video materials promoting resilience and self-regulation [25, 29–31].

Given the promising impact of psychology-based interventions to mitigate stigma impacts, integrating mental health care into HIV prevention strategies is important. This is especially pertinent for populations such as GBMSM facing high levels of sexual stigma and high prevalence of mental health problems like depression and anxiety [32]. Building on this evidence, integrating mental health care into PrEP programs has gained support as a way to enhance PrEP engagement [32, 33]. This integration seeks to capitalize on the regular follow-up visits inherent to PrEP use to engage GBMSM in comprehensive healthcare, including primary and mental health services [34, 35]. An empirical study examining health screening prevalence among people using PrEP found that they were more likely to receive a range of screenings, including flu vaccinations, glucose testing, and tobacco screening than non-PrEP users [34]. However, while people using PrEP showed slightly higher rates of depression screening, the increase was modest (7%), indicating untapped potential for PrEP care to serve as a gateway for improving access to mental health care for this population with a high burden of both sexual stigma and mental health conditions [34].

Building on the potential of stigma and mental health interventions to improve HIV-related behaviors among affected populations, this study explores their broader impact on HIV incidence. While individual-level interventions show promise in increasing engagement with HIV screening and prevention, their population-level effects on HIV incidence reduction remain unclear. Using a stochastic network model of HIV transmission, this study estimates the impact of integrating resilience-building mental healthcare into PrEP programs and how this integration could contribute to achieving HIV reduction goals among US GBMSM.

Methods

Study Design. We employed a stochastic agent-based network model, in which agents represent individual simulated persons, to simulate HIV transmission dynamics among GBMSM in the Atlanta metropolitan statistical area, built in EpiModel software [36]. We modeled main, casual, and one-time sexual partnerships among Black, Hispanic, and White GBMSM, aged 15 to 65 years old (reflecting the eligibility criteria of the ARTnet study used for network parameterization and the period of primary HIV acquisition risk among GBMSM) in Atlanta [37]. Simulations began with an initial network of 10,000 GBMSM within an open population framework, accommodating new entrants (at sexual debut) and exits (from mortality or sexual activity cessation). All model scenarios were based on HIV risk behaviors and prevention practices in 2019, prior to COVID-19 disruptions [38]. The model code is publicly available on GitHub (https://github.com/EpiModel/StigmaDepressionHIV_real). At baseline, approximately 30% of HIV-negative GBMSM in the model were using PrEP, consistent with recent estimates of PrEP coverage among GBMSM in the Atlanta metropolitan area and with prior applications of this model platform [39].

Reference Model and Data Sources. We adapted a previously developed network model to simulate the HIV epidemic among GBMSM [36, 40]. The initial network was defined by the target population’s demographic attributes, with partnership dynamics (formation and dissolution) modeled using temporal exponential random graph models (TERGMs), and sexual risk behaviors (e.g., act frequency, positioning, condom use) predicted via generalized linear models fit to ARTnet study data [41]. ARTnet was a 2017–2019 online cross-sectional study of nearly 5,000 US GBMSM, collecting data on mixing patterns, partnership durations and other sexual network features across 16,000 partnerships [37]. These data are well-validated for parameterizing GBMSM sexual network models [42–44]. HIV, Neisseria gonorrhoeae (NG), and Chlamydia trachomatis (CT) transmission were simulated separately, accounting for disease progression, natural history, and recovery (for NG and CT), with bi-directional feedback between the epidemic and network structure. Parameters for transmission, diagnosis, and treatment were sourced from secondary and public health surveillance data and are detailed in Supplemental Table S1 [37, 44, 45].

Reference Model Modifications. The core network model was adapted to include sexual stigma and mental health experiences as individual-level attributes assigned to network agents (i.e., simulated individuals in the model). Agents were assigned to one of four sexual stigma classes based on patterns identified in a prior study [15]: (1) Diverse Sexual Stigma, representing broad experiences of stigma across multiple settings (12%), (2) Anticipated Healthcare Sexual Stigma, primarily experiencing anticipated stigma in healthcare settings (14%), (3) Family and General Social Sexual Stigma, reflecting experiences of enacted and perceived stigma in family and social settings (32%), and (4) Minimal Sexual Stigma, representing having few or no stigma experiences (42%). Mental health disorder, specifically major depressive disorder (MDD) status, was also assigned to network agents at model entry based on race, sexual stigma class, and HIV status [15]. HIV-negative agents without MDD at baseline who later acquired HIV were simulated to potentially develop MDD at rates that achieve 33% overall MDD prevalence among HIV-negative and 47% among HIV-positive agents [32].

Agents with MDD experienced major depressive episodes (MDE) of varying severity (mild to very severe), with wellness intervals influenced by current and prior adequate treatment [46–48]. MDEs lasting two or more weeks with role impairment or moderate-severe symptoms qualified individuals for screening and diagnosis, with probabilities of diagnosis based on HIV status (Living with HIV: 45%, Not living with HIV: 47%) [49]. Diagnosed individuals became eligible for MDD treatment, with race-specific uptake probabilities aligned to coverage targets (Black: 39.7%, Hispanic: 33.6%, White: 54%) [50].

Depression treatment followed three phases: acute (12 weeks), continuation (4–9 months), and maintenance (up to 2 years) [51]. Spontaneous remission for untreated MDE was modeled by symptom severity [52]. Those initiating treatment could achieve remission, drop out, complete maintenance, or exit the network via mortality or sexual activity cessation [52–54]. Suicide attempts and completions were modeled as possible outcomes of MDE, with attempts influenced by severity and role impairment, and completions based on attempts during the index MDE [55, 56].

Both sexual stigma and MDD impacted HIV acquisition and management via their influence on sexual behaviors and engagement with HIV services. MDE severity modified sexual activity rates, with moderate-severe symptoms linked to increased activity and very severe depression linked to decreased activity [57–59]. Likelihood of condomless anal sex also varied by MDE severity, peaking in partnerships involving moderately depressed individuals [57–59]. In addition to its impact on MDD, sexual stigma also directly influenced PrEP engagement [16, 60]. Parameters for sexual stigma and depression modifications described were informed by secondary literature and empirical analysis of the American Men’s Internet Survey (Supplemental Table S1) [61–63].

Intervention Models. Two intervention models expanded the reference framework. The first (expanded depression screening in PrEP model), incorporated routine depression screening using validated screening tools within the PrEP context. In variant A of this intervention model, all PrEP initiators were set to be eligible for screening regardless of MDD status, MDE status, or symptom severity (contrasting with the reference model, which limited screening to those with MDEs of at least two weeks, role impairment, or moderate-severe symptoms). Screening uptake probabilities, initially set to zero in the baseline scenario, were incrementally increased in counterfactual scenarios simulating enhanced screening uptake. In variant B, eligibility was extended to all HIV-negative individuals at the index time step, regardless of PrEP initiation.

The second intervention model introduced resilience-building depression treatment for individuals screening positive for depression within the PrEP context. This treatment supplemented standard care with counseling to address sexual stigma trauma and build resilience in those affected. Unlike other models, which offered a single pathway for depression care, this model featured two distinct pathways: the general pathway (for standard treatment) and the PrEP pathway (which included stigma-focused interventions). Individuals screened through the PrEP context could access care via either pathway, with uptake probabilities via the PrEP pathway incrementally increased in counterfactual scenarios. As in the expanded depression screening model, variant A extended this treatment only to PrEP initiators, while variant B made it available to all HIV-negative individuals screened for depression within the PrEP context.

Simulating Counterfactual Scenarios. To assess the impact of enhanced depression screening and treatment uptake on the HIV epidemic, we analyzed incremental improvements in these processes (single-step enhancements) across the reference and intervention models. Probabilities for depression diagnosis and treatment initiation among all GBMSM in the modeled population were progressively increased from baseline values (GBMSM living with HIV: 45%, GBMSM not living with HIV: 47%) to multiples of these values, including scenarios with 50% increases (e.g., base × 1.5) and full uptake, where all eligible individuals were diagnosed or treated. For counterfactual scenarios in the PrEP context, depression screening uptake probabilities increased from 0% (baseline) to 10%, 50%, and 100% (where all eligible individuals were screened), with similar increments applied to scenarios enhancing depression treatment initiation.

Second, we assessed the impact of jointly enhancing depression screening and treatment initiation on HIV-related outcomes. Counterfactual scenarios evaluated the effects of maximal improvements in screening uptake (100%) and treatment initiation (100% among diagnosed individuals), both individually and in combination. Additionally, we examined the combined impact of these enhancements alongside optimized HIV-related efforts, such as increased ART coverage among individuals with HIV and improved PrEP coverage among those without HIV.

Outcomes Assessment. The outcomes assessed included both HIV-related epidemiological metrics and mental health services engagement. For HIV epidemiology, we evaluated incidence rates (IR)—the number of new HIV infections per 100 person-years (PY) during the final intervention year—and cumulative incidence, which captured all new infections over the 10-year intervention period. We also estimated the number and percentage of infections averted (NIA and PIA) in each counterfactual scenario relative to the baseline scenario. For scenarios with maximal cascade step improvements, we also estimated the numbers needed to treat (NNT), indicating the number of GBMSM receiving minimally adequate depression treatment required to prevent one new HIV infection.

The reference model was calibrated using methods from prior studies and fitted to local HIV/STI surveillance data [36, 44]. A total of 512 simulations were run per counterfactual scenario, each over a 10-year intervention period, with outcomes summarized as medians and 95% simulation intervals (SI).

Results

Impacts of depression care enhancement on HIV incidence

In the baseline scenario of the reference model, 1,409 new HIV acquisitions occurred over the 10-year period, with a final-year HIV incidence rate (IR) of 1.00 per 100 person-years (PY) (95% SI: 0.93–1.07) (Table 1). Maximizing depression screening alone in the general population (up to 100% uptake) averted 1.3% of new infections, while maximizing MDD treatment initiations alone reduced infections by over 7%, lowering the final-year IR to 0.91 per 100 PY (95% SI: 0.84–0.97). Enhancing depression screening within PrEP settings alone (through implementation of routine depression screening i.e., intervention 1 models) minimally impacted HIV incidence (PIA: 0–0.4%), regardless of whether screening targeted PrEP initiators or all who tested HIV-negative (Supplemental Tables S2A & S2B). However, expanding access to depression treatment within PrEP settings reduced infections by 3% when focused on PrEP initiators and 7% when extended to all people without HIV, with final-year IRs of 0.95 (95% SI: 0.88–1.02) and 0.88 (95% SI: 0.82–0.95), respectively (Supplemental Table S2C & S2D).

Supplemental Tables 3A-3D highlight why enhancing depression care in PrEP settings had greater impact when focused on treatment uptake than screening uptake. Introducing routine screening in PrEP settings for initiators made approximately 12,500 individuals eligible for depression screening activities, regardless of their prior access to or history of depression screening in the general population. However, even with 100% uptake, only 1,731 individuals were identified with depression due to screening guidelines requiring symptoms within two weeks to meet screening criteria. Without concurrent improvements in treatment access, the annual number of treatment initiations rose only modestly from the baseline of 1,787 to 1,947, with minimal impact on HIV incidence. Similarly, targeting depression screening to all HIV-negative individuals at the time of HIV testing (intervention model 1B) without enhancing treatment linkage led to 2,136 treatment initiations, despite identifying 9,972 individuals with depression at maximum uptake. By contrast, improving treatment access and linkage within PrEP settings (with 50% routine screening uptake) had greater impact, yielding 2,358 treatment initiations when focused on PrEP initiators and 3,494 when extended to all HIV-negative individuals.

Impacts of depression care enhancement on mental health outcomes

In the baseline scenario of the reference model, median MDD diagnosis coverage was 57% (95% SI: 56%–57%), rising to 85% with maximum depression screening uptake in the general population (Supplemental Table S4A). The annual proportion of individuals with active depressive episodes requiring care (i.e., lasting over two weeks with at least moderate severity or role impairment) who were diagnosed and treated rose from 9% at baseline to 28% with a twofold improvement in depression treatment initiation in the general population and 46% with maximal treatment initiation. In contrast, single-step depression care enhancements within PrEP settings alone yielded smaller gains in these mental health outcomes: MDD diagnosis coverage peaked at 68% (intervention 1B model), and the proportion of immediate mental health needs met rose to 12.3% annually (intervention 2B model), both substantially lower than outcomes from general population-level enhancements (Supplemental Tables S4B-4E).

Impacts of joint optimizations of depression care, PrEP and ART coverage

Table 2 summarizes the outcomes of optimized scale-up scenarios applying maximum enhancements across mental health care cascade steps. In the reference model, enhancements targeted general population mental health services, while intervention models also integrated routine mental health services within PrEP settings alongside these changes in the general population. PIA rose from 1.5% with maximal depression screening alone to 12% in the reference model when both screening and treatment initiation were optimized. Adding a two-fold increase in PrEP uptake (to 42% coverage) further raised PIA to 26%, peaking at 38% (95% SI: 34%–41%) with concurrent ART coverage improvements (averaging 74% among those diagnosed with HIV). Additional depression screening within PrEP settings provided minimal PIA gains over the reference model, but combining screening and treatment initiation within these settings had greater impact: PIA rose to 14% when targeting PrEP initiators and 17% when targeting all HIV-negative individuals, peaking at 41% (95% SI: 38%–44%) with optimized depression care, PrEP, and ART. This scenario also achieved the lowest final-year HIV incidence rate (IR), reducing it to 0.52 (95% SI: 0.47–0.57) per 100 person-years compared to 0.57 (95% SI: 0.52–0.62) in the reference model (Fig. 1).

HIV incidence rates in final intervention year across models

Enhanced mental healthcare reduced downstream impacts of depression, including suicide attempts among individuals with MDD. Median suicide attempts declined from 2,308 in the baseline scenario to 1,326 with mental healthcare optimization alone in the reference model and to 973 with comprehensive optimization of mental healthcare, PrEP, and ART in models targeting HIV-negative individuals within PrEP context.

Table 2 also highlights the number of individuals needing minimally adequate depression treatment to avert one new HIV infection (NNT) across scenarios. In the reference model, NNT was lowest at 751 with optimized mental healthcare alone under maximal depression screening uptake in the general population. Expanding mental healthcare access to PrEP settings further reduced NNT, particularly with optimized screening and treatment in PrEP contexts. NNT dropped to 445 (41% reduction) with mental healthcare optimization alone and to 175 (77% reduction) when combined with enhanced PrEP and ART coverage.

Discussion

Sexual stigma and mental health are important contributors to HIV acquisition and service use among GBMSM, making the need to address these intertwined factors crucial to U.S. HIV elimination goals. This study examined the impact of addressing both factors by enhancing mental healthcare access for GBMSM in the general population and integrating resilience-building mental healthcare into PrEP programs for those most affected. Results indicate that while expanding mental health services in PrEP settings would improve engagement, achieving significant HIV incidence reductions would require wide-reaching, multi-level improvements across both depression and HIV care continuum.

Depression, often caused and exacerbated by sexual stigma, discrimination, and social isolation, is highly prevalent among GBMSM [32]. Beyond affecting well-being, depression is linked to increased sexual risk behaviors, poor care engagement, and elevated HIV acquisition risk [11, 58]. Enhancing mental healthcare for GBMSM is therefore crucial for both improving mental health outcomes and bolstering HIV prevention efforts [12, 34]. Our findings show that improving mental healthcare access, whether through general population enhancements or routine screening with depression treatment linkage, substantially improved depression diagnosis coverage, mental health needs met during active depressive episodes, and modestly reduced HIV incidence, averting up to 12% of new infections. However, realistically realizing this potential requires addressing barriers that impact mental healthcare uptake. Mental health stigma, which deters care-seeking, is particularly impactful in the absence of routine screening. When compounded by sexual stigma, whether anticipated discrimination or actual experiences of bias in healthcare settings, access to services may be further limited for some individuals [14]. To optimize mental healthcare and HIV prevention for GBMSM, interventions implemented must address both mental health and sexual stigma. Normalizing mental healthcare use through routine screening and fostering inclusive, stigma-free environments are key steps in improving mental and HIV services engagement and addressing the intersecting challenges GBMSM face.

Integrating mental health services into HIV prevention programs like PrEP has been hailed as a potentially efficient way to improve access to mental healthcare for GBMSM, with implementation of routine screening helping to identify individuals in need earlier while reducing stigma around seeking mental healthcare [12, 34, 35]. Our findings, however, caution that the effectiveness of this approach in supporting HIV prevention depends on the successful linkage of screened persons to depression treatment where needed and on reaching a broad GBMSM population within PrEP settings. To meaningfully impact HIV incidence, integration of mental healthcare into PrEP must focus on both increasing depression screening uptake and ensuring treatment initiation across a broad recipient base. Achieving this goal presents its own challenges including those pertaining to resource allocation and balancing healthcare priorities within limited budgets. Despite these challenges, our study suggests that integrating mental healthcare with PrEP can achieve similar modest HIV reduction outcomes as enhancing mental health services for the broader GBMSM population, though with less impact on mental health epidemiological measures among GBMSM population. While the reduction in HIV incidence using this approach may be modest, the improvements in NNT make this approach a cost-effective strategy for reducing HIV infections among GBMSM, which may also ease long-term demands on healthcare resources.

Several factors may account for the limited reduction in HIV incidence observed with the implementation of the individual-level interventions targeting mental health and sexual stigma among GBMSM evaluated in this study. First, sexual stigma’s effects on HIV prevention behaviors, including PrEP engagement, are complex [61, 64–66] and context-dependent [16]. For example, an empirical study examining the relationship between sexual stigma experiences and PrEP engagement revealed contrasting effects: PrEP uptake was higher among individuals predominantly experiencing family and social sexual stigma but lower among those facing anticipated healthcare-related sexual stigma [16]. This complexity complicates quantifying sexual stigma’s overall influence on PrEP engagement among GBMSM, as the net effect is likely small and depends on the shifting balance of potentially “facilitating” versus hindering stigma experiences within the population. Second, depression’s impact on health outcomes is also complex, as reflected in the literature. Empirical studies on its effect on PrEP engagement have yielded mixed results, with some showing significant associations and others no effect [11, 33, 67–70]. Depression also influences risk behaviors in nuanced ways that vary with symptom severity; moderate depression may increase HIV acquisition behaviors (e.g., sexual activity and inconsistent condom use), while severe depression may reduce these behaviors by limiting social interactions and sexual activity [57–59, 71]. Furthermore, the episodic nature of depression creates variability in its effects on risk behaviors and healthcare engagement, with symptoms being most pronounced during episodes and potentially absent between them. Quantifying this variability in empirical studies depends on the distribution of symptom severity and study duration, complicating accurate representation in mathematical models. Stigma, a complex construct, influences health through multiple risk factors [9, 14]. While our study focused on stigma’s impact on mental health, HIV testing, and PrEP uptake, it likely affects other unmodeled factors. Moreover, the impacts on the factors considered were relatively small, underscoring the need for further research to better understand stigma’s broader role in HIV prevention [61].

Enhancing mental healthcare access is crucial for addressing the multifaceted needs of GBMSM populations. However, our findings underscore its limitations in contributing to substantial reductions in HIV incidence among GBMSM. Improvements in mental healthcare, whether alone or combined with strategies to also address individual-level stigma in integrated healthcare settings, resulted in only modest HIV incidence reductions. Notably, these modest impacts were mainly observed when enhancements were provided at maximal levels, which may be difficult to achieve without substantial healthcare system overhauls and resource investment. This highlights that individual-level mental health and stigma interventions, by themselves, may not fully address the complex dynamics driving HIV transmission within GBMSM communities. More substantial reductions (PIA 41%) were only achieved by combining enhanced mental healthcare access with improved PrEP and ART coverage, compared to mental health interventions alone (maximum PIA 17%).

Extending mental health interventions to both HIV-negative and HIV-positive GBMSM further amplified their impact. Applying screening and treatment broadly led to greater reductions in HIV incidence and lower numbers needed to treat than targeting a single subgroup, highlighting the benefits of delivering HIV and mental health services inclusively across serostatus to promote equitable access for all GBMSM [72]. Integrating mental healthcare in this way alongside PrEP and ART programs can maximize both HIV prevention and mental health outcomes. Adopting a multifaceted, community-centered approach is therefore essential to meet the diverse needs of GBMSM populations and achieve meaningful HIV reductions.

The implications of these findings should also be considered in the context of persistent inequities in healthcare access within GBMSM populations. In the United States, access to mental health services and PrEP varies by race and ethnicity, age, socioeconomic status, and geographic region, with Black and Hispanic GBMSM and those living in the US South often facing greater barriers to culturally competent care [50]. Without intentional implementation strategies, expanding mental health and PrEP-related services may disproportionately benefit GBMSM populations already well connected to care, potentially perpetuating disparities in HIV outcomes. Efforts to implement the mental health interventions should therefore prioritize equitable delivery, including integration of such services in community-based settings, and reduction of structural barriers to care. Ensuring equitable access will be essential to achieving population-level reductions in HIV incidence across diverse GBMSM communities. These findings also take on additional urgency in the context of ongoing shifts in the HIV policy landscape, including contraction of federal funding for HIV prevention and care programs and evolving legal protections for sexual and gender minority populations. Such changes may further constrain access to mental health and HIV prevention services for GBMSM, reinforcing the importance of integrated, efficient service delivery models like those evaluated here.

Few studies have quantitatively assessed the impacts of mental health or stigma on HIV incidence using mathematical models, with most relying on compartmental models [73–76]. Our study, however, utilized the capabilities of agent-based models to capture the complex biobehavioral processes driving HIV transmission among GBMSM and evaluate how individual-level interventions addressing psychosocial challenges could influence population-level HIV incidence over time. However, several limitations should be considered. First, as with all mathematical modeling, these findings may not fully reflect the diversity and healthcare access patterns of all GBMSM communities. The model’s reliance on assumptions and available empirical data may not capture the full extent of real-world complexities and disparities. Additionally, the model does not account for social factors such as socio-economic disparities, community engagement, social support, or other forms of stigma, which can directly influence HIV risk behaviors, healthcare engagement, and interact with depression or sexual stigma to affect these outcomes. Another limitation is the modeling of sexual stigma. We used distinct patterns of stigma experiences identified in a prior study to represent cumulative sexual stigma across an individual’s lifecourse [16]. While this allows for individual-level representation, it does not capture the dynamic nature of sexual stigma in real life, including its timing, volume, or intensity over time. It may also fail to accurately reflect how different stigma constructs in various settings influence self-stigma, mental health, and care engagement among GBMSM. Lastly, our model focused solely on individual-level stigma, overlooking its complex manifestation at structural, institutional, community, and interpersonal levels. Models that incorporate multi-level stigma could offer a more comprehensive understanding of its impact on mental health, PrEP utilization, and the HIV epidemic.

This study highlights that while enhancing mental healthcare for GBMSM improves mental health outcomes and modestly reduces HIV incidence, achieving substantial population-level impact requires combining these interventions with optimized PrEP and ART coverage. Extending mental health services across both HIV-negative and HIV-positive populations, in ways that are stigma-informed and inclusive, amplifies their effect on HIV prevention while addressing the psychosocial needs of GBMSM. Ultimately, multifaceted, community-centered approaches that prioritize equitable access will be important in ensuring that all GBMSM benefit and for advancing meaningful progress toward HIV elimination in the United States.
