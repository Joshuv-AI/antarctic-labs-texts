Concomitant carcinoid heart disease and patent foramen ovale: a case report and review of literature
Authors: Nuno Cotrim, Ricardo Carvalheiro, Bárbara Teixeira, Tânia Mano, Lídia de Sousa, Mariana Saraiva, Vítor Martins
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCarcinoid Heart Disease (CHD) primarily affects the right heart valves, while left heart involvement is rare and often associated with a patent foramen ovale (PFO). Early identification of a PFO in CHD can be critical to patient outcomes.Case summaryA 61-year-old woman with metastatic neuroendocrine tumor presented with worsening breathlessness and hypoxemia. Imaging excluded pulmonary embolism and lung metastases. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed severe tricuspid regurgitation due to carcinoid valve involvement. Persistent hypoxemia prompted transesophageal echocardiography, which demonstrated a right-to-left shunt through a PFO. Background

This case report describes a 61-year-old woman with carcinoid heart disease associated with a right-to-left (R-L) shunt through a patent foramen ovale (PFO), resulting in hypoxemia refractory to oxygen therapy and potential involvement of the left-sided heart valves. The uniqueness of this case lies in the detailed echocardiographic documentation supporting the diagnosis and the clear, literature-based rationale explaining the patient’s clinical response to the implemented therapeutic strategies.

Case presentation

We present the case of a 61-year-old woman who initially sought gynecological consultation due to abdominal pain, significant unintentional weight loss (approximately 21 kg over the past year), and fatigue with minimal exertion. Her past medical history was unremarkable. Physical examination revealed a firm mass in the right iliac fossa. The diagnostic workup and staging—summarized in Table 1—were consistent with a primary gastrointestinal or gonadal neuroendocrine tumor (NET) with metastases to the liver, kidneys, peritoneum, and lymph nodes. At this stage, the patient was initiated on octreotide therapy, resulting in improvement of her carcinoid syndrome symptoms.

Diagnostic workup and oncological staging summary

A few months later, the patient presented to the emergency department with worsening fatigue and hypoxemia. On physical examination, her oxygen saturation was 79% on room air, improving only to 84% with 4 L/min oxygen via nasal cannula. Platypnea-orthodeoxia syndrome was excluded. Lung auscultation was unremarkable. A pansystolic murmur was noted at the left sternal border, along with jugular venous distension and prominent “v” waves, in the absence of peripheral edema. Resting electrocardiogram and chest X-ray revealed no significant abnormalities. Arterial blood gas analysis on room air demonstrated hypoxemia with mild respiratory alkalosis and an elevated alveolar-arterial (A–a) oxygen gradient: pH 7.486 (reference range: 7.35–7.45), pO₂ 41.6 mmHg (83–108), pCO₂ 20.7 mmHg (32–48), and A–a gradient 84.6 mmHg. Laboratory findings were notable for elevated hemoglobin and hematocrit levels (16.1 g/dL [12–15 g/dL] and 47.8% [36–46%], respectively), normal renal function, and an N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) level of 860 pg/mL. CT pulmonary angiography excluded pulmonary thromboembolism and intrathoracic metastases (Figure 1 and Video 1, Supplementary Material). Oxygen therapy was initiated but failed to improve oxygenation.

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated normal left ventricular size and systolic function, with diastolic flattening of the interventricular septum consistent with a D-shaped morphology. The left atrium was within normal size parameters; however, an aneurysmal interatrial septum with leftward bulging was observed. The right atrium was dilated (41 mL/m²). The right ventricle was normal in size and exhibited preserved systolic function. The tricuspid valve leaflets appeared thickened and hypomobile (“frozen leaflets”), with absent coaptation, resulting in severe, free-flowing tricuspid regurgitation—findings suggestive of carcinoid valve involvement. Pulmonary artery diameter and pulmonary acceleration time were within normal limits (Fig. 2; Videos 2 and 3, Supplementary Material).

Given the hypoxemia refractory to oxygen therapy, a right-to-left shunt was suspected. A transesophageal echocardiogram with bubble study confirmed the presence of a shunt, demonstrating the passage of bubbles from the right atrium to the left atrium through a patent foramen ovale (Video 4, Supplementary Material). The PFO had remained clinically silent until the onset of tricuspid valve disease. The presence of the PFO, in combination with a regurgitant jet directed toward the interatrial septum and elevated right atrial pressures, explained the patient’s refractory hypoxemia.

Both transthoracic and transesophageal studies also revealed thickening and hypomobility of the posterior leaflet of the mitral valve and of the right and left coronary cusps of the aortic valve. Although these findings had no significant functional impact, they were suggestive of left-sided heart valve involvement, likely facilitated by the R–L shunt (Video 3, Supplementary Material).

Due to worsening hypoxemia and the requirement for mechanical ventilation, right heart catheterization was performed. The procedure confirmed previous findings and excluded significant pulmonary hypertension. Notably, balloon inflation across the PFO resulted in a marked rise in diastolic pressures within the right heart chambers. The calculated pulmonary-to-systemic flow ratio (Qp: Qs) was 0.64 (Table 2).

A multidisciplinary team discussion was held, and given the rapid deterioration of the patient’s condition, it was decided to first proceed with percutaneous closure of the PFO, followed by surgical tricuspid valve replacement. The patient underwent percutaneous PFO closure under fluoroscopic guidance using a 25 mm Cardia PFO Occluder device, resulting in resolution of hypoxemia (Video 5, Supplementary Material). Arterial oxygen saturation improved from a pre-procedural value of 90–100% immediately post-procedure.

Despite the initial success, the patient developed cardiogenic shock due to volume overload and right ventricular failure, which led to secondary low-output left heart failure while awaiting tricuspid valve surgery. Following hemodynamic stabilization, she underwent surgical tricuspid valve replacement with implantation of a 27 mm EPIC bioprosthesis. The immediate postoperative period was uneventful. Unfortunately, the patient subsequently developed multiorgan failure and ultimately passed away (Figure 3).

Discussion

Carcinoid heart disease results from endocardial damage due to prolonged exposure to vasoactive substances—primarily serotonin—secreted by neuroendocrine tumors, most often originating in the gastrointestinal or respiratory tracts. Cardiac involvement and carcinoid syndrome usually occur in the presence of hepatic metastases, which permit these substances to bypass hepatic degradation and reach the systemic circulation. However, tumors in the gonads, lungs, or retroperitoneum may also cause carcinoid syndrome without liver metastases due to alternative venous drainage routes [1].

NETs are rare, with an incidence of 2.5 to 5 per 100,000 population. Approximately 30–40% of patients with NETs develop carcinoid syndrome, classically presenting with diarrhea, flushing, and bronchospasm. CHD occurs in 20–50% of those with carcinoid syndrome and typically affects the right-sided valves due to pulmonary degradation of vasoactive substances [2]. Left-heart involvement is uncommon and generally limited to patients with a patent foramen ovale, bronchial carcinoids, pulmonary metastases, or massive hormonal secretion overwhelming pulmonary clearance [3].

The pathogenesis of CHD involves serotonin-mediated activation of 5-HT₂B receptors on endocardial cells, leading to fibroblast proliferation, inflammation, and plaque-like deposition [4]. In our case, left-sided valve involvement was likely related to a right-to-left shunt through a PFO, allowing serotonin to bypass pulmonary metabolism. Nonetheless, given the patient’s high biomarker burden and extensive metastatic disease, we cannot exclude the possibility that direct systemic exposure to vasoactive substances contributed independently. Both mechanisms may have played a role.

Guidelines recommend screening for CHD in patients with carcinoid syndrome using transthoracic echocardiography and NT-proBNP as a biomarker (cutoff > 260 pg/mL), both of which were consistent with CHD in our patient [5]. A 24-hour urinary 5-HIAA > 300 µmol is also associated with a 2–3-fold increased risk of CHD or disease progression, as seen in this case.

Given the known prevalence of PFOs in the general population (up to 25%) [6], active screening for PFO in patients with CHD is endorsed by international guidelines and should be performed using agitated saline contrast echocardiography [2, 7]. In this patient, the coexistence of CHD and a previously silent PFO led to refractory hypoxemia and facilitated left-sided valvular damage.

Early screening and systematic evaluation for cardiac involvement in patients with NETs may enable better planning of care. Prompt identification of right-sided valvular dysfunction allows for optimized timing of medical therapy and referral for structural intervention. Routine echocardiographic screening in NET patients—especially those with elevated biomarkers—may help detect cardiac involvement before symptom onset, allowing for elective, single-stage interventions rather than emergency procedures in deteriorating patients.

In patients with symptomatic CHD and an expected survival of ≥ 12 months, surgical valve replacement is recommended [7]. Our patient met these criteria and was considered eligible for surgery. Although a two-stage approach was ultimately pursued—first closing the PFO percutaneously, followed by delayed tricuspid valve replacement—this strategy may have contributed to clinical decline. Literature suggests that a simultaneous surgical approach, addressing both PFO and valvular pathology, is preferable to avoid hemodynamic deterioration related to abrupt changes in intracardiac pressures and preload [8]. Isolated PFO closure in the presence of severe tricuspid regurgitation can increase right ventricular afterload, reduce left ventricular preload, and precipitate low-output heart failure—a sequence that occurred in this case.

At our institution, limited experience with complex CHD, combined with the patient’s rapidly declining condition, made simultaneous intervention unfeasible. In retrospect, earlier identification of the PFO and more aggressive early intervention—including evaluation for hybrid or percutaneous approaches—may have altered the outcome. Percutaneous tricuspid valve replacement was not available at the time, and while tricuspid edge-to-edge repair (TEER) may be considered in select cases, it is frequently limited by the fibrotic retraction and tethering typical of carcinoid valve disease, which impairs leaflet coaptation.

Bioprosthetic valve selection was guided by the risk of hemorrhagic complications from hepatic metastases, in line with current recommendations [9]. However, as interventional technologies evolve, future patients may benefit from percutaneous PFO closure combined with transcatheter valve replacement or hybrid strategies that can be tailored to hemodynamic status and disease burden.

Carcinoid crisis is another important perioperative consideration. It is well established that stressors such as surgery can provoke life-threatening crises via massive serotonin release. Intravenous octreotide infusion (50–100 µg/h), started at least two hours preoperatively and continued for 48 h postoperatively, remains the most effective preventive strategy [10]. In this case, the patient had been receiving monthly subcutaneous octreotide, but it is possible that the absence of perioperative intravenous octreotide contributed to the refractory hypotension and eventual multiorgan failure.

This case underscores the importance of early recognition, structured screening, and multidisciplinary collaboration in the management of CHD, particularly when complicated by a right-to-left shunt. It highlights the potential consequences of staged interventions in critically ill patients and reinforces the need for institutional preparedness and tailored strategies. Moving forward, institutions should develop protocols to identify and intervene earlier in high-risk patients and evaluate the feasibility of single-stage, multidisciplinary interventions to optimize outcomes (Figure 4).

Conclusion

In summary, transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) remains the imaging modality of choice for the diagnosis and follow-up of carcinoid heart disease (CHD). Evaluation should routinely include assessment for a patent foramen ovale (PFO), given its high prevalence in the general population and the significant clinical implications when coexisting with CHD.

From a therapeutic standpoint, simultaneous closure of the PFO and surgical replacement of the tricuspid valve is preferred over a staged approach, as it may prevent hemodynamic instability related to abrupt changes in intracardiac pressures. Additionally, during the perioperative period, carcinoid crisis can be triggered—manifesting as profound vasodilation and hypotension that may be difficult to distinguish from other causes. In this context, the use of periprocedural intravenous octreotide is essential to suppress serotonin release and effectively prevent these life-threatening events. Finally, in select patients with carcinoid heart disease and high surgical risk, emerging percutaneous tricuspid valve replacement may represent a viable alternative, warranting further evaluation in multidisciplinary cardio-oncology care.
