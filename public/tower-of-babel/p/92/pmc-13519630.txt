Nascent ADAM17 synthesis potentiates GPIbα cleavage in resting and stimulated stored platelets
Authors: Shayan Askari, Harriet Ghansah, Christopher D Mansi, Timothy J Stalker, Lawrence E Goldfinger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•Platelets synthesize nascent ADAM17 during storage, aligning with progressive cleavage of GPIbα.•Intact GPIbα surface concentration in resting and stimulated platelets is regulated by ADAM17 in a gene-dose–dependent manner.

Introduction

Platelet products or platelet concentrates are instrumental in transfusion medicine for various clinical indications including management of hemorrhage, surgeries including organ transplantation, cancer therapies, and management of bleeding due to inherited or acquired platelet disorders. Depending on the indication, stored platelet concentrates can be administered either prophylactically or therapeutically. Platelet storage has been historically challenging. Platelets are the only blood products that are stored at room temperature (RT), and they have a short shelf life of only 5 days in the United States, except under certain specialized conditions.1 Apheresis platelets that are subject to pathogen reduction technology treatments or large-volume delayed sampling can be stored for up to 7 days.2 The US Food and Drug Administration also issued an exception allowing cold storage of platelets under certain extenuating circumstances, when conventional platelets are not available or practical, for up to 14 days3; however, cold-stored platelets suffer from several drawbacks, including decreased recovery, survival rate, and in vivo function.4,5 Alternative storage conditions, including frozen and lyophilized platelets, are under active investigation, but maintenance of platelet functionality after the freeze-thaw-reconstitution cycle, transfusion efficiency, and posttransfusion efficacy to meet clinical needs remain to be clarified.6 The 5-day shelf life of RT-stored platelets is due to the risk of bacterial growth as well as to biochemical and structural changes, collectively known as platelet storage lesion (PSL), which result in overall decreased reactivity and posttransfusion recovery.7, 8, 9, 10 Mechanisms of PSL are not fully understood, and elucidation of the degradation mechanisms in stored platelets is needed, both to improve transfusion medicine practices and outcomes, and to further our understanding of degradation pathways of anucleate platelets. A functionally important but incompletely understood phenomenon of PSL is the shedding of adhesion glycoproteins (GP), namely GPIbα and GPVI, by cleavage of the GP ectodomains by platelet-resident metalloproteinases, that occurs progressively during RT platelet storage.11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16 Initial studies by Bergmeier et al, demonstrated that GPIbα, which is essential for normal hemostasis by binding to von Willebrand factor (VWF) and thrombin, is primarily cleaved by a disintegrin and metalloproteinase 17 (ADAM17) in platelets ex vivo and in vivo.17

The ectodomain of mature ADAM17, a type I transmembrane metalloproteinase, consists of a catalytic domain, a disintegrin domain, a membrane proximal domain, and a conserved ADAM17 interaction domain.18,19 Multiple regulatory mechanisms have been proposed for ADAM17, including inhibition by tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinase 3,20 and activation by phosphorylation of the cytoplasmic tail via p38 or extracellular signal-regulated kinase, and subsequent dedimerization.21,22 p38 inhibition prevents Adam17-mediated cleavage of GpIbα during 15-hour storage of murine platelets and improves posttransfusion recovery.23 Additionally, multiple factors, such as mitochondrial damage and inhibition of protein kinase A, cyclooxygenase, and calmodulin, lead to ADAM17-dependent cleavage of GPIbα.11,24, 25, 26 Whereas pathological conditions may increase GPIbα shedding by ADAM17, the presence of cleaved glycocalicin (soluble cleaved GPIbα ectodomain) in healthy human plasma indicates constitutive sheddase activity. However, precise mechanisms driving GPIbα shedding by ADAM17 in platelets during storage remain poorly understood.

Although the major substrates of ADAM17 have been identified, the specific contributions of ADAM17 to PSL have not been elucidated. Off-target effects of inhibitors and putative intracellular functions of ADAM17 have further confounded mechanistic studies. Altogether, the precise roles of ADAM17 in platelet biology, PSL, and physiological functions, including hemostasis and thrombosis, are still unclear. We recently demonstrated constitutive translation of a broad spectrum of proteins in circulating platelets driven by factors present in plasma.27 Despite lack of transcription by these anucleate cells, platelets inherit precursor and mature spliced mRNAs from their parent megakaryocytes, and contain splicing machinery, tRNAs, the full spectrum of ribosomal subunits, and a rudimentary endoplasmic reticulum, together capable of supporting synthesis of a broad range of proteins including transmembrane proteins. Nascent synthesis of several proteins has also been observed in platelets kept in storage (maintained in plasma), but ADAM17 was not among the proteins tested, and overall functional roles of translation in stored platelets remain unclear.28, 29, 30 In this study, we investigated putative nascent synthesis of ADAM17 and its contributions to GPIbα shedding and platelet function during RT storage.

Methods

Reagents

For a complete list of the reagents and consumables, refer to supplemental Tables 1 and 2.

Metabolic labeling and isolation of nascent polypeptides

For translation inhibition, platelets suspended in plasma were treated with either vehicle or 50 μg/mL puromycin, which mimics tRNA and inhibits protein synthesis, every day for 5 days. Bio-orthogonal noncanonical amino acid tagging was performed as previously described27 with synthetic methionine analog, azidohomoalanine (AHA), added at the final concentration of 3mM every day for 3 days. Platelets were then lysed, desalted, and treated with biotin alkyne in the presence of copper ions as a catalyst (click reaction). Later, platelet lysates were incubated with NeutrAvidin agarose resin (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA) overnight at 4°C to capture biotinylated proteins.

siRNA design and synthesis

ADAM17 mRNA, transcript variant 1 (National Center for Biotechnology Information reference sequence NM_003183.6), was scanned in siDirect version 2.1 (https://sidirect2.rnai.jp/) for identification of short inhibitory RNA (siRNA) target sites with 100% homology amongst all known transcript variants. 2′-O-methoxyethyl (2′-OMe) and locked nucleic acid (LNA) modification were incorporated to enhance stability in plasma. siRNAs were obtained as high-performance liquid chromatography–purified duplexes from Dharmacon (Lafayette, CO). Sequences are as follows, shown 5′ → 3′: ADAM17 siRNA guide strand: UAAAGAGAGAAUAUCGUAGUC. ADAM17 siRNA passenger strand: CUACGAUAUUCUCUCUUUAUU; control (scrambled, nontargeting for human mRNAs) siRNA guide strand: UCACAACCUCCUAGAAAGAGUAGA; passenger strand: TACUCUUCUAGGAGGUUGUGAUU (bold and italics, LNA; bold and underlined, 2′-OMe nucleotides).

Mice

Mice deficient in megakaryocyte/platelet Adam17 were generated by breeding Adam17tm1.2Bbl/J mice (The Jackson Laboratory, strain number 009597), containing loxP sites flanking Adam17 exons 2 (“floxed”), with C57Bl/6J platelet factor 4–Cre mice (The Jackson Laboratory, strain number 008535). Genotypes were determined from ear clippings taken at the time of weaning, typically at 3 to 5 weeks of age. Genotyping primers are as follows, shown 5′ → 3′: Cre forward, CCCATACAGCACACCTTTTG; Cre reverse, TGCACAGTCAGCAGGTT; Adam17 forward, TCC CCC AGC TAG ATT GTT TG; Adam17 reverse, AGG ACC CAG GTT CAG TTC CT. This study was carried out in strict accordance with the recommendations in the guide for the care and use of laboratory animals of the National Institutes of Health. All mouse protocols and procedures were approved by the institutional animal care and use committee of Thomas Jefferson University. No unexplained mortality was observed in these studies. All experiments complied with the ARRIVE (animal research: reporting of in vivo experiments) guidelines. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Additional methods are provided in the supplemental Methods.

Results

Platelets translate ADAM17 during RT storage

We previously demonstrated that circulating peripheral blood platelets undergo constitutive protein translation in vivo driven by growth factors in plasma, and that this process is essential for normal platelet function.27 We hypothesized that platelets ex vivo synthesize nascent ADAM17 during RT (25°C) storage in platelet-rich plasma (PRP), potentiating GPIbα ectodomain cleavage in stored platelets. To test this possibility, we treated freshly isolated platelets from healthy donors, suspended in autologous plasma, with either vehicle or translation inhibitor puromycin daily for 5 days of storage with gentle agitation, and sampled platelets every 24 hours for western blot analysis of ADAM17. In vehicle-treated samples, platelet ADAM17 expression showed a biphasic response with increased expression peaking at 48 hours of storage, whereas ADAM17 levels in inhibitor-treated samples were decreased by 37.5% as early as 24 hours and remained stably suppressed over the experimental time frame, suggesting that platelets translate nascent ADAM17 during storage (Figure 1A-B). In contrast, GPIbα levels, which declined over time in vehicle-treated stored platelets as expected, showed a similar decline, with partial amelioration over time in puromycin- or cycloheximide-treated stored platelets (Figure 1C), suggesting a lack of robust synthesis of nascent GPIbα under these storage conditions. The partial protection is likely due to concurrent inhibition of ADAM17 translation.

To confirm nascent synthesis of ADAM17 during storage, we took advantage of metabolic labeling of newly synthesized polypeptides. We treated freshly isolated PRP with AHA, a synthetic cell-permeable alkyne-reactive analog of methionine that can be charged onto Met tRNAs and incorporated into newly translated polypeptides27,31 with translation inhibitor cycloheximide or vehicle added fresh daily. We next used copper-catalyzed click chemistry to link biotin alkyne tags to AHA-containing nascent proteins in lysates of platelets collected at different times during storage. Next, samples were enriched for biotinylated proteins using neutravidin-agarose bead pulldown, and subjected to western blotting with ADAM17 antibodies27 (Figure 1D). A band corresponding to AHA-tagged ADAM17 was detected in lysates collected at 24 and 48 hours of storage, but this band was absent in cycloheximide-treated samples (Figure 1E). Together, these results demonstrate active synthesis of ADAM17 protein in human platelets stored under these conditions, resulting in increased ADAM17 levels in stored platelets.

Suppression of ADAM17 translation by siRNA prevents ectodomain cleavage of GPIbα during storage

To investigate the role of nascent synthesis of ADAM17 in GPIbα ectodomain cleavage in stored platelets, we designed a siRNA targeting all known transcript variants of human ADAM17 mRNA, as well as a control siRNA harboring a scrambled sequence with no predicted complementarity to human mRNAs. We previously showed that platelets internalize naked, unencapsulated small noncoding RNA duplexes with high efficiency in vitro.32 To protect the siRNAs from digestion by riboendonucleases present in plasma, we substituted LNA and 2′-OMe nucleic acids for multiple residues in the passenger strands and the 2-nucleotide overhang at the 3′ end of the guide strands. Coincubation of ADAM17 siRNA with stored PRP reduced ADAM17 expression by 38.8% in platelets as early as 24 hours, and ADAM17 remained suppressed under these conditions for the 5-day experimental time frame (Figure 2A-B). To assess effects of suppression of ADAM17 translation on GPIbα cleavage in stored platelets, we probed the same samples for GPIbα with antibodies that detect the GPIbα ectodomain region, which is removed by ADAM17 proteolysis. In samples treated with control siRNA, intact GPIbα was progressively decreased in platelets over time in storage, whereas full-length GPIbα was preserved in samples treated with ADAM17 siRNA (Figure 2C-D). Similarly, the postcleavage C-terminal remnant of GPIbα increased in control-treated platelets over time in storage, but this increase was blunted by ADAM17 siRNA (supplemental Figure 1). Thus, ADAM17 synthesis during RT storage contributes to cleavage of GPIbα in human platelets.

Binding of GPIbα to VWF is not altered by ADAM17 translation inhibition in stored platelets

The principal physiological consequence of ADAM17 proteolysis of the GPIbα ectodomain in stored platelets is reduced ability of platelets to anchor to the GPIbα substrate, VWF.33 To investigate effects of ADAM17 knockdown on the binding capabilities of platelets to VWF via GPIbα, we assessed ristocetin-induced platelet aggregometry/agglutination (RIPA) of stored platelets after PRP treatment with ADAM17 or control siRNA.34 RIPA of human platelets stored in plasma steadily decreased over time in storage in control siRNA-treated samples, as expected due to progressive cleavage of the GPIbα ectodomain. Surprisingly, RIPA profiles and maximal levels were similar in ADAM17 siRNA–treated samples as in control siRNA-treated samples, despite apparent protection of the GPIbα ectodomain by this ADAM17 siRNA (Figure 3A-B). It should be noted that under these experimental conditions, the RIPA assay reflects both GPIbα-mediated agglutination and integrin-mediated aggregation.35 To investigate effects of inhibiting ADAM17 translation on GPIbα binding to VWF under shear, we perfused PRP that had been treated with either control or ADAM17 siRNA and collected during different days of storage over VWF-coated plates in microfluidic chambers under arterial shear, and assessed platelet deposition by platelet fluorescence intensities. Similar to effects observed by RIPA, stored platelets from PRP treated with ADAM17 siRNA showed a similar level of binding to VWF-coated surfaces compared with control siRNA samples (Figure 3C-D), suggesting that siRNA suppression of ADAM17 in stored PRP is not sufficient to rescue platelet GPIbα binding to VWF under high shear.

Nascent ADAM17 primarily cleaves an internal pool of GPIbα

To investigate the mechanistic basis for unaltered RIPA despite preservation of full-length GPIbα in stored human platelets by ADAM17 siRNA knockdown, we assessed the surface expression of GPIbα in stored platelets by flow cytometry. Whereas surface intact GPIbα declined in stored platelets over time, to our surprise, the addition of ADAM17 siRNA to stored PRP did not rescue this progressive decrease in surface intact GPIbα (Figure 4A). Based on these results, we considered whether an internal pool of GPIbα located in the inner folds of the open canalicular system (OCS), not accessible to GPΙbα antibodies in intact, unstimulated platelets, may constitute a primary substrate for newly synthesized ADAM17. To test this possibility, we assessed intact GPIbα in fixed freshly isolated human platelets, with or without membrane permeabilization with detergent. Permeabilized platelets demonstrated higher mean fluorescence intensities for GPIbα under resting conditions, indicating the existence of an internal pool as suggested by previous studies (Figure 4B).36,37 Additionally, we observed increased levels of GPIIb and GPVI in permeabilized platelets compared with nonpermeabilized platelets (Figure 4B). We note that unlike GPIbα, surface GPIIb was not altered in resting stored platelets over time (supplemental Figure 2). Platelet agonist stimulation is known to promote externalization of the internal OCS membrane network, but agonist stimulation simultaneously triggers ADAM17 activity.13,38, 39, 40 Therefore, we considered effects of platelet agonist stimulation on surface GPIbα integrity, as a function of surface-accessible or internal ADAM17 and GPIbα. Washed, fresh platelets were treated with vehicle, KP-457 (a cell-permeable compound that inhibits ADAM17 activity with high specificity41), or non–cell-permeable 5G6 antibodies raised against the cleavable domain of human GPIbα, that protects surface GPIbα from proteolytic cleavage by ADAM17.42 Platelet stimulation by thrombin, or calcium ionophore (A23187), resulted in acute decreases in surface full-length GPIbα (Figure 4C), consistent with previous reports.13,38,43 KP-457 treatment protected GPIbα from ectodomain cleavage upon platelet stimulation as evidenced by maintenance of full-length surface GPIbα levels (Figure 4C), confirming that decreased intact surface GPIbα after thrombin stimulation results from induced ADAM17-mediated cleavage. In contrast, pretreatment with 5G6 antibodies, which do not penetrate to reach the OCS,44 only partially protected GPIbα from ectodomain cleavage after thrombin or A23187 stimulation (Figure 4C). This partial protection is likely due to the saturating concentration of 5G6 antibodies, which blocks the cleavage site upon stimulation, and rapid externalization of the OCS. Additionally, surface levels of GPIX and GPIIb increased or remained unchanged after stimulation with the various agonists, except in response to A23187, after which their levels decreased. This reduction likely reflects the nonphysiologic effects of A23187, resulting in shedding not only of GPIbα but also of GPIX and GPIIb. However, neither KP-457 nor 5G6 altered agonist-induced exposure of GPIX or GPIIb (supplemental Figure 3A-B). These results suggested that an internal pool of ADAM17 is responsible for the additional cleavage of GPIbα upon platelet stimulation.

Inhibition of ADAM17 translation does not protect GPIbα from ectodomain cleavage after agonist stimulation in stored platelets

Observing that ADAM17 inhibition protected GPIbα upon platelet stimulation, we considered effects of ADAM17 knockdown on GPIbα cleavage in stimulated platelets isolated during storage. We predicted that ADAM17 siRNA treatment would yield increased levels of surface GPIbα after platelet stimulation compared with control-treated platelets. Over 5 days of storage, surface intact GPIbα in resting platelets steadily declined, as predicted (Figures 5 and 3A). Stimulation with either thrombin or convulxin resulted in further decreased intact surface GPIbα in both control siRNA– and ADAM17 siRNA–treated platelets compared with baseline levels, each day of storage (Figure 5). No significant differences were evident between the 2 conditions, indicating that moderate suppression of ADAM17 by siRNA knockdown is not sufficient to prevent agonist-induced cleavage of GPIbα in stored platelets. Additionally, to investigate the consequences of ADAM17 pharmacological inhibition on total GPIbα, we treated platelets suspended in plasma with either vehicle or KP-457 on the first day of storage. Similar to the siRNA treatment, total platelet GPIbα expression decreased over time in vehicle-treated samples, whereas it remained protected in KP-457–treated samples (supplemental Figure 4A-B).

Internal GPIbα translocates to the platelet surface upon platelet stimulation, concurrent with ADAM17-mediated cleavage

To investigate the subcellular distribution of GPIbα in resting and stimulated platelets as a function of ADAM17 inhibition, we used confocal microscopic imaging of GPIbα with ectodomain-specific antibodies in treated, fixed platelets captured on poly-l-lysine substrates, with or without permeabilization before staining, coupled with profiling of signal intensities from these images across platelet radii as an indicator of the relative enrichment of GPIbα from platelet centers out to the peripheral plasma membrane. In unstimulated platelets, GPIbα appeared enriched as a ring at the platelet surface, and upon permeabilization an internal pool was visible (supplemental Figure 5A), increasing the overall fluorescence intensity of GPIbα ectodomain staining (supplemental Figure 5B). Thrombin stimulation yielded a moderate leftward shift of median peak enrichment of intact GPIbα in the radial distance from the center of nonpermeabilized platelets (supplemental Figure 5B), indicating lower relative enrichment of GPIbα at the plasma membrane, consistent with reduced full-length GPIbα on platelet surfaces with this treatment as assessed by flow cytometry (Figures 4 and 5). Intact GPIbα showed enhanced overall intensities, as well as a shift toward enrichment at the surface, in nonpermeabilized platelets treated simultaneously with thrombin and KP-457 (supplemental Figure 5B). Together, these results indicate that GPIbα translocates to the platelet surface upon thrombin stimulation, but GPIbα is simultaneously subject to ADAM17-mediated cleavage under these conditions.

Deletion of Adam17 increases surface expression of GpIbα in agonist-stimulated murine platelets in a gene-dose–dependent manner

To determine the roles of ADAM17 in platelet function and GPIbα maintenance in circulating peripheral blood platelets in vivo, we generated mice with megakaryocyte/platelet–specific deletion of Adam17 by crossing Adam17tm1.2Bbl/J (TACEfloxp) mice, harboring loxP sites flanking exon 2 of Adam17, with mice expressing Cre recombinase under the control of the platelet factor 4 promoter (supplemental Figure 6). We will refer to Cre-positive mice heterozygote and homozygote for Adam17 floxed as Adam17+/− and Adam17−/−, respectively. After multiple generations, Adam17−/− progeny showed normal Mendelian distributions without any gross abnormalities. Adam17−/− mice showed platelet counts, mean platelet volumes, RBC, hemoglobin, and white blood cell levels similar to those in Adam17+/+ mice (supplemental Figure 7). Peripheral blood platelets from Adam17+/− mice demonstrated higher baseline levels of intact surface GpIbα than platelets from Adam17+/+ control mice, and platelet surface GpIbα was elevated further in Adam17−/−, demonstrating control of GpIbα surface expression by platelet Adam17 in an inverse gene dose–dependent manner (Figure 6A). Upon stimulation with thrombin, intact GpIbα surface expression rapidly decreased in platelets from Adam17+/+ control mice, but intact GpIbα surface expression remained unchanged in Adam17+/− platelets after thrombin stimulation (Figure 6B). Interestingly, Adam17−/− platelets demonstrated increased intact surface GpIbα upon thrombin stimulation compared with Adam17+/+ and Adam17+/− platelets (Figure 6B), further confirming that the internal pool of GpIbα that is exposed to the surface after platelet stimulation is protected from ectodomain proteolysis in the absence of platelet Adam17. No differences were evident in surface levels of GpIIb, GpIX, GpV, or GpVI in resting platelets across genotypes (Figure 6C,E; supplemental Figure 8). GpIIb and GpIX, which are not subject to Adam17 cleavage, were increased to similar levels upon platelet stimulation regardless of Adam17 genotype (Figure 6D,F). Thus, Adam17 is required for constitutive and agonist-mediated cleavage of GpIbα in resting and stimulated murine platelets, respectively.

Adam17 is dispensable for normal hemostasis

To assess the role of platelet Adam17 in normal hemostasis, we measured time to initial stoppage of bleeding and the amount of blood loss after tail clip. We did not observe significant differences in time to primary hemostasis or blood loss between control and platelet Adam17-deleted animals (Figure 7), suggesting that platelet Adam17 is not essential for normal hemostasis, consistent with previous observations in chimeric mice harboring catalytically inactive Adam17 in hematopoietic cells.17

Discussion

The results of this study demonstrate that nascent synthesis of ADAM17 enhances GPIbα ectodomain shedding in resting and stimulated platelets stored in autologous plasma. Platelets synthesized nascent ADAM17 during storage, and siRNA-mediated suppression of ADAM17 translation protected total GPIbα from ectodomain cleavage. However, siRNA suppression of ADAM17 did not rescue the progressive decline in surface GPIbα or rescue reduced binding capacity of GPIbα to VWF over time, indicating that ADAM17 knockdown is not sufficient to prevent ectodomain cleavage of surface GPIbα during storage. Platelet stimulation yielded similar levels of GPIbα surface exposure each day during storage in both control siRNA– and ADAM17 siRNA–treated samples, indicating that baseline levels of ADAM17, reduced but not eliminated by the knockdown approach, are sufficient to result in acute cleavage of GPIbα upon platelet stimulation. We identified an internal pool of GPIbα by flow cytometry and confocal microscopy in permeabilized platelets, which was protected from cleavage by inhibition of platelet agonist-induced activation of ADAM17 with KP-457, a cell-permeable small molecule ADAM17 inhibitor, but not by cell-impermeable antibodies against the ADAM17 cleavage site in GPIbα, indicating that an internal pool of ADAM17 contributes to GPIbα cleavage upon platelet stimulation. Adam17-deleted murine platelets demonstrated increased basal levels of surface GpIbα compared with WT in an inverse Adam17 gene-dose–dependent manner, and platelet agonist-induced cleavage of GpIbα was also Adam17 gene-dose dependent, underscoring that ADAM17 is the principal mediator of GPIbα cleavage in platelets and further demonstrating that ADAM17 expression level is a critical mediator of GPIbα cleavage capacity in platelets. Collectively, these results reveal that an internal pool of ADAM17 is essential for the cleavage of GPIbα in resting and stimulated platelets during storage and in vivo.

Nascent synthesis of ADAM17 in stored platelets ex vivo, which had not been described previously, possibly due to the limitations of proteomic approaches in reliably detecting low-expressed proteins, represents a new checkpoint for molecular control of stored platelet function. We have recently shown that circulating platelets constitutively translate proteins in response to plasma growth factors in vivo,27 and others have reported nascent synthesis of highly expressed proteins such as Septin2, GPIIb, GPIIIa, and RhoGDI in stored platelets ex vivo.28, 29, 30 However, we did not detect GPIbα synthesis in stored platelets, pointing to ADAM17 synthesis in stored platelets as a mechanism of increased GPIbα cleavage in storage over time. Indeed, siRNA knockdown of ADAM17 preserved baseline levels of GPIbα indicating reduced cleavage in stored platelets, indicating that newly synthesized ADAM17 is a significant contributor to GPIbα cleavage in stored, unstimulated platelets over time.

Despite protection from GPIbα cleavage by ADAM17 knockdown in unstimulated stored platelets as evidenced by western blot, surface levels of GPIbα and VWF binding capacity were not altered in ADAM17 knockdown stored platelets compared with controls, implying differences in accessibility of surface and internalized GPIbα to ADAM17 cleavage. ADAM17 is reportedly present at significantly lower copy number than GPIbα, 670 vs 18 900 copies per human platelet, respectively.45 It has been reported that GPIbα is abundantly expressed in the luminal surface of the OCS.36,37 Limitations in staining the internal pool of GPIbα in intact nonpermeabilized platelets can be explained by the reticulated and convoluted nature of the OCS.46,47 The localization of ADAM17 in platelets had not been described in the literature until recently. Using biochemical assays and immuno-gold microscopy, Six et al, reported that ADAM17 is located intracellularly in platelets and proposed that it may reside in the dense tubular system.48 These authors also proposed that only maximally 2% of GPIbα is both surface exposed and a substrate for shedding,48 which is consistent with our observation of the lack of protection in surface GPIbα by ADAM17 knockdown in resting platelets. Differences in surface expression are likely obscured by western blot analysis. GPIbα loss during storage may also result in part from microvesiculation, although we did not observe substantial concomitant decreases in other GPs likely to be released in microvesicles such as GPIIb under these storage conditions.

Using cell-permeable ADAM17 inhibitors and non–cell-permeable antibodies against the ADAM17 cleavage site in GPIbα, we found that internally localized ADAM17 is primarily responsible for agonist-induced GPIbα cleavage. Given the rapid cleavage of GPIbα by ADAM17 and the fact that ADAM17 is an obligate transmembrane type I protein, it is conceivable that ADAM17 may access GPIbα by coenrichment in the OCS. Although we showed that inhibition of nascent ADAM17 synthesis protects total GPIbα, the activity of nascent ADAM17 was not directly measured in this study. Additionally, it is unclear what drives ADAM17’s constitutive activity. Reactive oxygen species,49 which have been proposed to play a role in glycoprotein shedding during storage, might contribute to the basal activity of ADAM17 during storage. Additionally, iRhom2 and Frmd8 have been shown to interact with ADAM17 in the endoplasmic reticulum,50, 51, 52 promoting enzymatic maturation and trafficking to the cell surface; however, the extent to which these proteins play a role in the maturation of nascent ADAM17 during storage remains to be elucidated. The distinct contexts of storage and agonist stimulation may also be reflected in distinct molecular and spatial regulation of ADAM17-mediated cleavage of GPIbα in each condition. However, the precise distribution of ADAM17 in resting and stimulated platelets warrants further investigation.

Consistent with our observations, previous studies demonstrated that murine platelets harboring inactive Adam17 have a higher level of baseline GpIbα and show better posttransfusion recovery after storage compared with WT platelets.17,23 The inverse Adam17 gene-dose–dependent increase in surface GpIbα and agonist-induced GpIbα cleavage we observed are particularly striking, as previous proteomics data estimate copy numbers of Adam17 to be only 157 per mouse platelet, compared with 46 154 estimated GpIbα molecules per mouse platelet.53 These results suggest that a single molecule of ADAM17 must access multiple molecules of GPIbα to mediate proteolysis on the scale we, and others, have observed both in agonist-stimulated and stored platelets, implying complex spatiotemporal regulation of this axis. The precise nature of the inverse gene dose effect of ADAM17 levels to GPIbα cleavage requires further investigation. Murine Adam17 has been reported to cleave GpVI upon mitochondrial damage54; however, Gardiner et al, demonstrated that human ADAM17 is not capable of cleaving recombinant GPVI.13 This discrepancy may reflect interspecies differences between mice and humans, either in the GPVI cleavage site itself or in the mechanistic response to mitochondrial injury. In this study, although the surface or total expression of GPVI was not quantified during storage, the GpVI surface level in Adam17−/− animals was comparable with that of the controls.

Our findings indicate that downregulation of nascent ADAM17 expression alone is insufficient to maintain the structural and functional integrity of GPIbα in stored platelets. However, targeted suppression of ADAM17 synthesis combined with modulation of other key contributors to PSL might have promising translational potential.
