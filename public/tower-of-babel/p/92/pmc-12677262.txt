Is ‘Normal Hearing’ Truly Normal? Temporal Processing and Speech Perception in Noise Abilities Amongst Relatively High Non-Occupational Noise-Exposed Young Adults
Authors: Ritik Srivastava, Swati Goyal, Jayashree Bhat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objective:Whilst occupational noise is known to affect suprathreshold auditory processing abilities, the concern regarding non-occupational noise exposure, such as recreational or environmental noise, is growing. This study investigates temporal auditory processing and speech perception in noise (SPiN) abilities amongst young adults with normal hearing but varying non-occupational noise exposureMethods:The participants were recruited between September 2024 and December 2024. A total of 64 college-going normal hearing participants were classified into low noise exposure (LNE, n = 32) and high noise exposure (HNE, n = 32) groups on the basis of their annual noise exposure levels. SPiN (signal-to-noise ratio [SNR] 50) was assessed by presenting nonsense consonant-vowel-consonant-vowel words. Gap detection thresholds (GDTs) for 500 ms broadband noise were assessed in quiet and background noise conditions (+10 and +5 dB SNR) by using an adaptive procedure.Results:The HNE group demonstrated significantly increased SNR 50 thresholds compared with the LNE group (P < 0.001). GDT showed increased thresholds in the HNE group across all testing conditions (quiet, +10 and +5 dB SNR). However, a prominent increase was observed for the +5 dB SNR condition. Two-way repeated measure analysis of variance revealed a significant main effect of stimulus condition (P < 0.001) and noise exposure (P < 0.001). The interaction effects between stimulus conditions and noise exposure were significant (P < 0.001).Conclusion:The study shows that frequent exposure to high-intensity non-occupational noise can adversely affect suprathreshold auditory processing. The findings underscore the need for increased awareness and prevention to reduce the long-term impact of noise on hearing.

INTRODUCTION

Noise-induced hearing loss (NIHL) remains one of the most common yet preventable causes of sensorineural hearing impairment worldwide.[1] Whilst occupational noise exposure has received the most attention, growing evidence highlights the substantial role of non-occupational or recreational noise in auditory health. Recreational noise from sources, such as personal listening devices (PLDs), live concerts, sporting events and transportation, contributes to the increase in global social noise exposure.[2] These sources frequently exceed the safe levels, often reaching over 90 dBA.[34] In Australia, most of the young adults are exposed to noise through leisure activities, such as going to nightclubs, sporting events and music concerts,[5] with similar trends in developing nations, including India,[67] driven by urbanisation, media access and lifestyle changes. In India, recreational noise now surpasses occupational exposure amongst adolescents and young adults due to urbanisation, media access and lifestyle shifts.[8] Despite the increasing non-occupational noise exposure, awareness and regulation remain limited, and the long-term auditory risks are often underestimated.

Similar to occupational noise, recreational noise can cause cochlear damage. However, such damage may occur before it becomes detectable via conventional audiometry, particularly affecting high frequencies and synaptic structures within the cochlea.[9] This early-stage damage, known as cochlear synaptopathy (CS) or ‘hidden hearing loss’, involves the loss of synapses between inner hair cells and auditory nerve fibres without affecting hearing thresholds.[10] This damage is often manifested as difficulty understanding speech in complex environments,[1112] along with tinnitus or hyperacusis,[4] even in individuals with normal audiograms.

Recreational noise exposure is nearly universal, but its intensity, duration and context vary widely. Some individuals encounter chronic, high-level noise through frequent concert attendance,[13] high-volume PLD use[14] or living in noisy urban areas.[67] Such variability underscores the necessity of adopting a more nuanced and stratified approach to research in this domain.

Noise can trigger cochlear neuron degeneration, even after hair cell recovery and normalised thresholds,[15] with up to 50% of synaptic connections lost following loud noise exposure.[16] This synaptic loss also occurs with aging,[17] whereas spiral ganglion cell degeneration may appear months or years later.[15] Notably, low spontaneous rate (low-SR) fibres, crucial for detecting brief signals in background noise, are especially vulnerable.[18] Consequently, neural desynchronisation compromises the encoding of sound features essential for speech-in-noise (SPiN) understanding.[1920] Therefore, temporal processing assessments may serve as more sensitive markers of noise-induced damage than threshold-based measures alone.

Temporal processing plays a critical role in auditory perception, particularly in complex listening environments. Gap detection threshold (GDT) assesses the ability to discriminate brief silent intervals between sounds, reflecting auditory resolution and neural synchrony.[1221] Studies on individuals with occupational noise exposure have shown significantly poorer temporal resolution and SPiN understanding than non-exposed groups.[22] Temporal processing abilities decline in the presence of competing noise, with GDT increasing as signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) decreases from +10 to −5 dB.[23] However, limited literature exists on the impact of non-occupational noise exposure on these abilities. GDT and SPiN have been found to be effective behavioural measures for detecting noise-induced auditory damage. One study found these tests to have the highest area under the receiver operating curve (ROC) curve amongst various behavioural and electrophysiological measures, indicating their efficiency.[24]

This study addresses these gaps by examining GDT and SPiN in individuals with varying levels of non-occupational noise exposure. By stratifying participants based on noise exposure levels, the study investigates whether increased non-occupational noise exposure leads to subtle deficits in temporal processing. This investigation is particularly relevant because increasing noise exposure amongst young adults may cause early signs of auditory dysfunction despite normal hearing thresholds.[25] It also highlights the need for early detection strategies that include suprathreshold auditory processing, which is crucial for listening skills.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A between-group comparison study design was employed, with annual noise exposure (ANE) as the grouping variable. The study was conducted in a sound-treated audiometric test room.[26]

Participants

Sample Size and Recruitment

A priori power analysis was conducted to estimate the required sample size. The sample size was calculated using the formula n = 2(zα + zβ)2σ2/d2, where zα = 1.96 at a 95% confidence level, zβ = 1.28 at 90% power, σ = combined standard deviation, d = mean difference. Here, d = 0.83 and σ = 1. With reference to the parent article,[21] 95% confidence level and 90% power, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 32 participants per group. A total of 64 normal hearing adults aged 18–29 years were recruited from the university campus.

The present study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee of K. S. Hegde Medical Academy. The approval number is [EC/NEW/INST/2022/KA/0174]. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants after explaining the nature and purpose of the study. A purposive sampling strategy was adopted for participant recruitment. Approval was obtained from the heads of the respective departments before starting the recruitment. An internal advertisement was circulated within the university, inviting volunteers to participate. Interested individuals were screened for eligibility on the basis of predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Recruitment continued from September 2024 to December 2024 until the required sample size (n = 32) was achieved. Demographic data were collected at the time of participant recruitment prior to the administration of any questionnaires or audiological assessments.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The inclusion criteria for the study required participants to have normal hearing sensitivity, defined as pure-tone thresholds of ≤15 dB HL across octave frequencies from 250 to 8000 Hz. Additionally, participants were required to have normal middle ear function with present ipsilateral and contralateral acoustic reflexes at 0.5, 1, and 2 kHz. The presence of transient evoked otoacoustic emissions (TEOAEs) was considered, with an SNR of ≥6 dB in at least three consecutive frequency bands. Furthermore, participants were included only if they showed no significant auditory processing difficulties, indicated by a score of ≤6 on the Screening Checklist for Auditory Processing in Adults (SCAP-A) screening tool. Individuals were excluded if they had a history of otological or neurological disorders or any self-reported hearing-related complaints.

Group Classification

Eligible participants were given the Noise Exposure Questionnaire (NEQ)[27] and subsequently categorised into two groups on the basis of their ANE scores. Participants with ANE values equal to or greater than 79 LAeq8760h were classified as being at the highest risk for developing NIHL. NEQ is a structured self-report tool designed to estimate an individual’s ANE from various recreational, occupational and environmental sources. It quantifies noise exposure in terms of intensity (in dB) and duration (in hours per year), allowing for an approximate calculation of cumulative noise risk over time. Participants completed the NEQ, reporting the frequency and duration of various noisy activities over the past year. For each activity, the number of hours per year (C) was determined, and the dose percentage (D) was calculated using the formula D = (C/T) × 100, where T is the maximum allowable exposure time, computed using representative mid sound levels based on the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH)-recommended 3 dB exchange rate. Doses from individual activities were summed, and the total was converted into an annual equivalent continuous sound level by using the formula LAeq8760h = 10 × log10(D/100) + 79. Routine activities were accounted for as the remaining hours in a year (8760 hours minus total episodic exposure).

An ANE value of 79 dBA or higher (LAeq8760h ≥ 79) was considered indicative of high risk for NIHL, based on extrapolated NIOSH occupational exposure limits. The ANE cutoff of 79 LAeq8760h used in NEQ is based on the established occupational noise exposure guidelines. Specifically, NIOSH sets an occupational noise exposure limit of 85 LAeq2000h. This standard was extrapolated to an annual equivalent exposure of 78.6 LAeq8760h.

Audiological Evaluation

Inclusion criteria were met through an audiological evaluation. Pure tone audiometry was carried out using a Piano-Inventis diagnostic audiometer (Inventis Srl, Padova, Italy) across octave frequencies from 250 to 8000 Hz, using calibrated Sennheiser HDA 200 circumaural headphones (Sennheiser Electronic GmbH & Co. KG, Wedemark, Germany). Hearing thresholds were established using the modified Hughson–Westlake procedure.[28] Impedance audiometry was performed using the Clarinet-Inventis system (Inventis Srl, Padova, Italy) to assess middle ear function and acoustic reflexes. Tympanometry used a 226 Hz probe tone, and acoustic reflex thresholds were measured ipsilaterally and contralaterally at 0.5, 1, and 2 kHz. TEOAEs were recorded using a calibrated ILO-V6 system (Otodynamics Ltd., Hatfield, UK). Click stimuli were presented at 80 dB SPL, and each recording included 260 sweeps. The SCAP-A,[29] a standardised self-administered tool, was utilised for identifying auditory processing difficulties in adults. It consists of 12 items rated on a 2-point scale, where individuals indicate the presence or absence of specific auditory processing challenges.

Experimental Procedure

SPiN: speech perception was assessed using Smriti–Shravan software.[30] Testing was performed on a Lenovo personal computer routed through a calibrated audiometer. Nonsense consonant-vowel-consonant-vowel words were presented at 90 dB SPL via Senheiser HDA 200 circumaural headphones. Participants were instructed to ignore background noise and repeat the heard words. A three-down, one-up adaptive procedure was used to determine the SNR 50, with six reversals recorded. The SNR 50 score was calculated by averaging the midpoints of the last four reversals.

Gap detection test (GDT): A GDT was conducted using a psychoacoustic software package developed by Sęk and Moore.[31] Testing was performed on a Lenovo personal computer routed through a calibrated audiometer, and the stimulus was delivered through Sennheiser HDA 200 circumaural headphones at 90 dB SPL. A three-alternative forced-choice paradigm was used, where participants heard three stimuli per trial: one stimulus had a gap, and two were without any gap. They were asked to identify the stimulus containing the gap. The stimuli consisted of a 500-ms broadband noise, with a 300-ms interstimulus interval, and an initial gap of 10 ms. Rise and fall times were set to 1 ms to reduce spectral splatter. Stimuli were presented only to the right ear at 90 dB SPL. A two-down, one-up adaptive tracking procedure was used until eight reversals were obtained. The mean of the last six reversals was considered the participant’s GDT. The test was conducted in three different conditions: quiet, 10 dB SNR and 5 dB SNR.

Statistical Analysis

Normality of data was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test, which indicated that the data were normally distributed (P > 0.05). An independent t-test was performed to determine any differences in SNR 50 and different stimulus conditions between the two groups. A two-way repeated-measure ANOVA was conducted to examine GDTs across different stimulus conditions (quiet, +10 dB SNR and +5 dB SNR), with group (low noise exposure [LNE] vs. high noise exposure [HNE] as the between-subject factor and stimulus condition as the within-subject factor. Mauchly’s test was used to check the assumption of sphericity, and when violations were detected, the Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using the Bonferroni correction to identify significant differences between stimulus conditions within each group. Effect sizes (Cohen’s d) were calculated for significant between-group differences to interpret the magnitude of effects. A post-hoc power analysis was conducted using G*Power (version 3.1) for the repeated-measure ANOVA. A significance level of α = 0.05 was considered for all statistical tests. All tables were created using Microsoft Office Word 2019. Statistical figures were generated using JASP software (version 0.19.10; Amsterdam, Netherlands), and final graphical enhancements and figure editing were performed using Canva.

Speech Perception in Noise

Descriptive statistics showed that the HNE group exhibited higher mean (mean [M] = −5.281, standard deviation [SD] = 1.020) SNR 50 values than the LNE group (M = −7.739, SD = 3.780), suggesting that individuals with greater noise exposure required a higher SNR to achieve 50% speech intelligibility. An independent t-test was performed to evaluate this difference statistically. The results revealed that the difference in SNR 50 thresholds between the two groups was statistically significant [t(62) = 3.550, P < 0.001].

Comparison of GDT in Quiet Condition and in the Presence of Background Noise

Descriptive analysis revealed that the HNE group exhibited a higher mean GDT threshold than the LNE group [Figure 3] for all three conditions. The best GDT scores were obtained for the quiet condition for both groups, and the poorest scores were obtained for the background noise condition, i.e., +5 dB SNR. Two-way repeated-measure ANOVA revealed a significant main effect for stimulus conditions [F(2) = 187.201, P < 0.001] and noise-exposure [F(2) = 68.908, P < 0.001]. A significant interaction effect was found between the stimulus conditions and noise exposure [F(2) = 37.609, P < 0.001], suggesting that the difference between the groups was not the same across the stimulus conditions. The finding also suggests that the differences across stimulus conditions were not the same between the groups. Mauchly’s test indicated that the assumption of sphericity was violated (P < 0.05). Greenhouse–Geisser corrected analysis revealed a statistically significant main effect (P < 0.001), indicating robust differences across conditions despite the violation of sphericity. Further Bonferroni post-hoc pairwise comparisons revealed a significant difference between the pairwise comparisons in both groups [Table 4]. The results showed that the +5 dB SNR condition had a larger difference in GDT between the LNE and HNE groups, supported by a greater effect size.[32] Furthermore, the independent t-test showed a significant difference between the LNE and HNE groups for quiet [t(62) = 5.676, P < 0.001, d = 1.419], +10 dB SNR [t(62) = 7.387, P < 0.001, d = 1.847], and +5 dB SNR [t(62) = 8.010, P < 0.001, d = 2.003] conditions. In accordance with the observed effect size (f = [0.26], α = 0.05, and a total sample size of 62, the power analysis yielded an actual power of 0.987. This finding indicated that the study had a very high likelihood (98.7%) of detecting a true effect, confirming that the sample size was sufficient to support the statistical conclusions drawn from the analysis.

DISCUSSION

The results suggest that individuals with high noise exposure demonstrated changes in SPiN and temporal resolution. The findings support the growing evidence for subclinical auditory processing disorders that are not detectable through standard audiometry. The use of a validated recall-based tool (NEQ) to estimate ANE strengthens the reliability of the exposure classification in this study. Although dosimetry was not used, a prior work by Reeb-Whitaker et al.[33] demonstrated a strong correlation (r = 0.77) between the recall-based questionnaire and dosimetry, with only a 2 dB average discrepancy. This finding supports the use of a recall task-based questionnaire in non-occupational noise exposure studies. These findings contribute to the understanding of how everyday noise exposure, often underestimated, can have meaningful implications for auditory health.

Speech Perception in Noise (SNR 50)

The findings showed that the HNE group had significantly higher SNR 50 values than the LNE group, indicating poorer SPiN. This result aligns with those of previous studies reporting degraded SPiN performance in noise-exposed individuals with normal audiograms.[1225] Similarly, people with high noise exposure, such as musicians, military personnel and frequent attendees of loud venues, often perform worse than low-exposure peers.[25]

These deficits likely stem from damage to low-spontaneous-rate (LSR), high-threshold auditory nerve fibres, which are crucial for encoding sounds in noisy environments. Kujawa and Liberman[17] demonstrated that these fibres are especially vulnerable to noise-induced neuropathy, leading to desynchronised neural firing. This disruption compromises the encoding of temporal cues, such as envelope and fine structure, which are essential for speech understanding in noise.[20] Thus, the present study reinforces the hypothesis that noise exposure contributes to speech processing difficulties in noise, warranting the use of more sensitive diagnostic tools in clinical audiology.

GDT in Quiet and Background Noise

The GDT performance showed increased thresholds in the HNE group across all conditions (quiet, +10 dB SNR and +5 dB SNR), with the most prominent increase at +5 dB SNR, indicated by a larger effect size. This finding suggests that background noise makes temporal gaps harder to detect, and the HNE group may have reduced temporal resolution. Both groups showed the highest GDT thresholds in the +5 dB SNR condition, highlighting the vulnerability of temporal resolution in challenging acoustic environments. The larger effect sizes in noise conditions suggest that GDT tasks in background noise could serve as an early behavioural marker for subclinical auditory damage related to noise exposure.

Additionally, significant interaction effects between group and condition indicated that the effect of noise exposure on temporal resolution varies with listening conditions. These findings align with those of a prior work showing that auditory temporal processing is sensitive to neural damage caused by noise, even when standard hearing tests appear normal.[3435]The trend of increasing GDT thresholds with decreasing SNR, especially the steeper decline observed in the HNE group, supports the sensitivity of temporal resolution to noise exposure. This pattern mirrors the findings of Saxena et al.,[23] who showed that GDT worsened as SNR decreased. These GDT deficits may reflect damage to LSR fibres, which are vital for encoding amplitude modulations in noisy environments. Damage to these fibres disrupts phase-locking and neural synchrony, both essential for detecting brief temporal gaps.[19] Furman et al.[18] reported fewer low SR fibres in noise-exposed guinea pigs. They reported that exposure to noise damages the high-threshold LSR ANFs, which affect the responses more at high intensities than at low intensities. This finding is supported by findings of reduced auditory brainstem response (ABR) wave I amplitudes at higher intensities in noise-exposed subjects.[10] Temporal resolution is essential for speech understanding because it helps detect phonemic changes and preserve rhythm.[36] Impairments in this domain are linked with worsened SPiN due to degraded processing of temporal cues.[19] Therefore, temporal processing measures may provide functional insights into everyday listening difficulties that are missed by standard audiometric testing. Whilst CS provides a compelling explanation for SPiN and GDT deficits, other plausible mechanisms must be considered. Central auditory dysfunction; age-related neural changes; and cognitive factors, such as attention or working memory, may influence performance.

Whilst the findings provide valuable insights, the study has some limitations. Firstly, noise exposure was measured using the self-report questionnaire (NEQ), which may be subject to recall bias. Objective methods, such as personal noise dosimetry, could offer more accurate exposure estimates. Secondly, the sample included university students only, thus limiting the generalisability of the results to other age groups and populations. Thirdly, extended high-frequency audiometry, which is more sensitive than conventional pure tone audiometry to early noise-induced changes, was not included. GDT testing was conducted in the right ear only, which is commonly preferred due to the right ear advantage, especially in right-handed individuals. However, it limits the assessment of ear-specific effects and is recognised as a study limitation. Finally, although age and sex were controlled for other potential factors, such as the type of headphones used, individual listening habits, including volume levels and duration, and previous exposure to loud noise were not examined in detail and should be considered in future research.

CONCLUSION

The findings demonstrated that frequent exposure to high-intensity non-occupational noise can negatively impact suprathreshold auditory processing in young adults with clinically normal hearing thresholds. Individuals with high noise exposure showed significantly poorer SPiN and higher gap detection thresholds, especially under challenging listening conditions. These deficits likely reflect early neural changes, including damage to LSR auditory nerve fibres, which are essential for encoding temporal cues. The study highlights the importance of assessing auditory function beyond pure-tone audiometry and suggests that temporal processing tasks in background noise may serve as sensitive behavioural markers for identifying subclinical auditory deficits associated with noise exposure. The findings underscore the need for increased awareness amongst young adults to mitigate the long-term effects of non-occupational noise exposure on auditory health.
