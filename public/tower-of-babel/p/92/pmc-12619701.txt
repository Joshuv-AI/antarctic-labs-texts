Late-onset hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis associated with monoallelic STX11 mutation in an adult: a case report and rationale for early allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation
Authors: Jan Mateusz Zaucha, Andrzej Mital, Michał Taszner, Agata Ogłoza, Jan Maciej Zaucha, Rafał Machowicz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLate-onset familial hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (fHLH) remains a diagnostic and therapeutic challenge, particularly in adult patients presenting without a clear family history. We report a fatal case of HLH in a previously healthy adult harboring a monoallelic STX11 mutation. The patient presented with progressive jaundice, persistent high-grade fever, and pancytopenia, and was unresponsive to HLH-2004 protocol therapy with etoposide and dexamethasone, as well as subsequent salvage regimens. Despite aggressive treatment, the disease remained refractory, ultimately resulting in multi-organ failure. This case, alongside a review of reported adult-onset fHLH cases, highlights the diagnostic value of early genetic testing in adults with unexplained HLH, especially after secondary causes have been ruled out. Our findings underscore the importance of timely consideration of underlying genetic predisposition, even in the absence of biallelic mutations or childhood onset. Given the poor response to conventional therapies, early referral for allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation should be prioritized in suspected or confirmed fHLH cases to improve survival outcomes.Keywords: Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, STX11 mutation, Adult-onset HLH, Allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation, R/R HLH

Introduction

Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) is a rare, life-threatening hyperinflammatory syndrome characterized by continuous, abnormal activation of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells and, as a result, inflammatory cytokine storm. Most common features include high persistent fever, blood cytopenias, hepatosplenomegaly, elevated aminotransferase activity, ferritin level, and coagulopathy, although the clinical picture might be heterogeneous [1]. Primary or familial HLH mainly occurs in children with an incidence of 1 per 800,000 per year, with geographic variation [2]. It is a consequence of predisposing inherited conditions caused by highly penetrant autosomal recessive genetic mutations involving Perforin (PRF1), MUNC 13–4 (UNC13D), MUNC 19–2 (STXBP2), and Syntaxin 11(STX11) genes (Table 1). In adults, HLH is usually secondary to improper immune activation caused by a variety of different causes, including infections, neoplasms, and autoimmune disorders [3]. According to an American study, including 16,136 patients, the most common primary conditions in adult HLH are malignancies (30.7%), infections (24.3%), autoimmune disorders (20.8%), immunodeficiency either iatrogenic caused by solid organ transplantation (4%) or congenital immunodeficiency syndromes (2.5%). The high in-hospital mortality was reported in older adults (> 60 years) and males. However, the worst in-hospital mortality rate was documented in association with congenital immunodeficiency syndromes [4]. Familial HLH associated with gene mutations in adults is extremely rare, not exceeding 5–7% of the incidence rate in USA and the place of allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation (allo-HCT) is not established as in children [5, 6]. The most common mutation associated with HLH in adults is the biallelic A91V mutation in PRF1 gene. Interestingly, the monoallelic STX-11 gene mutation was reported only in 7 cases of adult HLH to date [7]. Here, we describe a rare, late-onset presentation of HLH in an adult associated with a monoallelic STX11 mutation, illustrating challenges in diagnosis, management, and timing of curative therapy and discuss the role of all-HCT in the disease course.

Case report

A 35-year-old female was admitted to the Emergency Department on 05.2023 due to jaundice and fevers that started five days prior to the admission. Her past medical history was unremarkable - no history of chronic illnesses and no exposure to toxins and medications. She denied recent infections or prodromal symptoms. One year earlier, she gave birth to her second healthy child with no significant complications. On admission, her vital signs were stable. On physical examination, an enlarged spleen and liver were noted, as well as jaundice (bilirubin 11,38 mg/dl). Laboratory results revealed anemia (Hb 9,1 g/dl), thrombocytopenia (40 G/l), neutropenia (0,83 G/l), lymphopenia (0 G/l), elevated liver enzymes, hypogammaglobulinemia. Computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the abdomen confirmed an enlarged spleen (174 × 58 mm) and enlarged liver (Cranio-Caudal length 200 mm) with a large hemangioma (98 × 77 × 117 mm) in the left lobe and two smaller hemangiomas (22 × 22 mm and 14 × 14 mm) in the 7th segment of the liver. There were no other pathological findings on both tests.

Test for viral infections including CMV, EBV, HIV, TORCH, HSV 1 and 2, HCV, HBV, Influenza, and Sars-Cov2 were negative. An immunology panel encompassing dsDNA, SMA, AMA, LKM-1-Ab, BDA, nRNP/Sm-Ab were also negative. Tumor markers (CA 19.9, CA 15.3, CEA, CA 125, AFP) were within normal ranges.

A bone marrow biopsy showed lymphopenia and hemophagocytosis; flow cytometry excluded the presence of a clonal cell population. The laboratory tests characteristic for HLH showed highly elevated soluble IL-2R (sCD25) 17,324 U/l, ferritin level 597 ng/ml (Fig. 1a), fibrinogen level above 2,5 G/l, triglycerides 754 mg/dl. HLH was diagnosed with a probability score of 88–93%. The patient was started on HLH-2004 protocol. High fever resolved (Fig. 1b), followed by normalization of bilirubin levels during the next four weeks [Fig. 1a].

Unfortunately, she developed the Sars-Cov2 infection but responded favorably to treatment with nirmatrelvir + ritonavir with a complete resolution of symptoms. An intensive search for secondary causes of HLH, including positron emission tomography combined with computed tomography (PET/CT), liver biopsy, and autoimmune screening, was negative. The histopathological result of the liver biopsy was normal without hemophagocytosis. The response to treatment was established as a complete response as both clinical and laboratory markers improved, however, without recovery of her blood counts. She was discharged. Since no secondary cause of HLH was found, the patient was tested for familial causes of HLH (15 genes), which revealed the presence of the monoallelic STX-11 (c.616G > A) gene mutation. The STX11 c.616G > A variant detected in our patient is reported as variant of uncertain significance. Mutations in this gene account for familial HLH-4. The patient was offered an allo-HCT; however, initially she did not consent to it. Her stay at home was complicated by a sudden septic shock requiring hospitalization and broad-spectrum antibiotics with pressors. Subsequent chest CT confirmed pneumonia. A bronchofiberoscopy with bronchoalveolar lavage revealed Aspergillus and CMV with a high copy number of almost 600 000 UI/ml. CMV replication was also confirmed in the peripheral blood with a level of 150 000 UI/ml. CMV was resistant to the standard dose of ganciclovir but subsequently responded to second-line treatment with foscarnet. For aspergillus pneumonia, voriconazole was administered followed by Isavuconazol leading to the aspergillus eradication confirmed by a control CT as an empty lung cavity. The patient’s general condition improved, and etoposide without steroids was planned to be continued at increased intervals in the outpatient clinic. At that time the patient consented to allo-HCT and the search for an unrelated donor was started.

However, after five weeks, the laboratory results showed rapid progression of HLH with increasing levels of ferritin up to more than 500,000 ng/ml. The patient received salvage therapy consisting of Anakinra, Tocilizumab, cyclosporine, and eventually due to the lack of response liposomal doxorubicin with methylprednisolone (DEP protocol). Unfortunately, the patient did not respond and died because of diffuse alveolar hemorrhage and PRES syndrome in October 2023 (Fig. 2).

Discussion

Our patient exemplifies an extremely rare case of late-onset adult HLH caused most likely by a monoallelic STX-11 gene mutation that presented with untypical HLH symptoms (jaundice) and quickly became resistant to standard treatment. The diagnosis of HLH was based on the clinical symptoms and abnormal laboratory results with the presence of hemophagocytosis in the bone marrow. Although hepatic involvement in HLH can occur, isolated and pronounced hyperbilirubinemia is rare, and its pathogenesis in our patient remains unexplained. The etiology of the patients’ high bilirubin level, mainly conjugated, remains unclear since histopathology examination of the liver biopsy did not reveal any significant abnormalities. The clinical course of HLH in our case was very aggressive, but we were not able to find any predisposing factors. The patient developed COVID-19 infection at the time of reaching clinical response but responded well to nirmatrelvir + ritonavir treatment and no exacerbation of HLH symptoms was noted as described in other patients developing secondary HLH to SARS-COV-2 infection during COVID-19 pandemic.

The genetic tests for primary HLH were positive for the presence of a monoallelic Syntaxin 11 also known as STX-11 gene mutation. STX11 is a human gene that is an atypical member of the t-SNARE family - a member of the SNARE family. The primary role of those proteins is to mediate the fusion of vesicles with the target membrane. This mediates exocytosis as well as fusion of vesicles with membrane-bound compartments. Syntaxin-11 plays a vital role in T and NK cells and is involved in the fusion of lytic granules with the plasma membrane. The malfunction of this process causes defective degranulation and impaired cytolytic activity of T and NK cells. In a mice model, STX-11 deficient mice developed HLH but it was not fatal. Only when the mice experienced T-cell depletion the nonfatal disease did convert into fatal HLH [12]. Traditionally, a diagnosis of familial HLH requires the identification of biallelic loss-of-function mutations. Steen et al. suggest a potential digenic mode of inheritance of familial HLH in which single loss-of-function mutations in two different degranulation pathway genes cooperate to impair pathway activity [10]. In our patient only one allele was mutated. It is very probable that other factors contribute to the development of HLH in such carriers. In contrast to our patient HLH in 7 individuals described in literature, an STX-11 monoallelic mutation was mainly driven by an infection. Four of those patients achieved remission (57%) while 3 other patients did not respond to treatment and died [7].

Analysis of the clinical course of our case raises two questions. First - when genetic testing for familial causes of HLH should be performed? Based on this case, we recommend considering genetic testing if no primary cause of HLH in an adult patient was found. The second question - is there a role of allo-HCT in such cases? The decision for referring adult patients with late-onset of familial HLH for allo-HCT is not supported by firm data in the available literature. We referred our patient for allo-HCT because of the incomplete recovery of blood counts despite the resolution of main HLH symptoms. Genetic findings supported the diagnosis of familial HLH but were not dominant in the decision to use allo-HCT, which could have been made earlier. At the time of HLH relapse, we used anakinra, tocilizumab and steroids based on the published possible options of second-line therapies for relapsed/refractory (r/r) familial HLH (Table 2).

The course of our case, clearly suggests allo-HCT was the only curative option and the earlier diagnosis of congenital HLH could have accelerated the decision to perform allo-HCT both by the treating physicians and by the patient. Of the 249 pediatric patients treated with the HLH-94 therapy, 124 (50%) received allo-HCT, and a 3-year survival rate of 55% was reported [22, 23]. Our patient met the third indication for allo-HCT suggested by the HLH Steering Committee of the Histiocyte Society HSCT however it is a late indication (Table 3).

The poor outcome of patients with late-onset of familial HLH may suggest the necessity to offer allo-HCT as soon as the diagnosis of familial HLH is established. As a bridging to allo-HCT in r/r cases several drugs are being investigated such as ruxolitinib (clinicaltrials.gov identifiers NCT05762640, NCT06160791), zanubrutinib (NCT05320575) and cyclophosphamide (NCT05936086), Emapalumab (NCT02069899), Alemtuzumab (NCT02385110), GTP regimen (NCT06038422).

In summary, this is the first fully described case of a patient with late-onset, adult familial HLH type 4 (STX-11 monoallelic mutation). This case highlights the challenges in treating familial HLH in adults, the importance of early diagnosis, and the potential benefits of hematopoietic stem cell transplantation as a curative option. Expanding the indication for allo-HCT to include patients with a monoallelic mutation may be beneficial, as in some cases, early allo-HCT could be the only curative option.
