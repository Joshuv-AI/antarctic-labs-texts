Visit-level prediction of missed HIV appointments using machine learning and transformer-based models in Uganda
Authors: Alex Mirugwe, Solomon Ssevvume, Arthur G. Fitzmaurice, Jonathan Mpango, Alice Namale, Evelyn Akello, Paul Katongole, Simon Muhumuza, Naseef Mayanja, Paul Mbaka, Enos Sande, Kenneth Musenge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAchieving high retention of people living with HIV (PLHIV) in care remains a challenge in Uganda, despite substantial progress towards UNAIDS 95–95-95 targets. The purpose of this study was to apply and compare machine learning and deep learning models using de-identified longitudinal clinical data collected from HIV clinics in Uganda to predict clients at high risk of missing treatment appointments. Predictions were made at the visit level, and a missed appointment was defined as failure to attend a scheduled clinical visit within 28 days of the assigned return data in accordance with the PEPFAR definition of loss to follow-up.MethodsWe compared the performance of traditional machine learning models (i.e. commonly used non-sequential models such as Decision Tree, Random Forest, AdaBoost, and XGBoost) and the Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT) model, which is designed to model sequential and contextual information in longitudinal data. Feature importance using the Shapley additive exPlanations method was used to identify the most influential predictors. We also evaluated the impact of various sampling techniques, i.e. undersampling, oversampling, and synthetic minority oversampling, to address class imbalance and improve model performance. Model performance was evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and the Area Under the Curve-Receiver Operating Characteristic (AUC-ROC) metrics.ResultsThe study was based on a longitudinal dataset of 66,206 PLHIV who initiated HIV care during 2000–2023 in 86 health facilities. The data comprised 1,479,121 clinical visits, an average of 22 clinical visits per client; 158,266 (10.7%) missed appointments, and 49,588 (74.9%) of clients missed at least one appointment. Median (interquartile range [IQR]) age was 38.0 [29.0–47.0] years, and the majority (n = 43,132, 65.1%) were female. The BERT model achieved the highest performance in predicting missed appointments, with an AUC of 0.96 [CI: 0.94–0.97], precision of 89.0% [CI: 87.1%–90.9%], recall of 100% [CI: 100.0%–100.0%], and an F1-score of 94.0% [CI: 92.5%–95.5%] for the missed appointment class. In comparison, the best-performing traditional model, XGBoost with undersampling, achieved an AUC of 0.90 [CI: 0.87–0.92], precision of 33.2% [CI: 30.3%–36.1%], recall of 79.7% [CI: 77.2%–82.2%], and an F1-score of 46.8% [CI: 43.7%–49.9%] for the missed appointment class. Feature importance analysis showed that treatment adherence, visit frequency, treatment duration, and visits on the current regimen were the most influential predictors of appointment interruption.ConclusionIn this retrospective analysis, transformer-based sequential modeling showed promising performance for visit-level prediction of missed HIV appointments compared with the non-sequential models evaluated. These findings should be interpreted cautiously, as further temporal and external validation is required. If validated prospectively, such models may support EMR workflows by identifying clients at higher risk of missed visits, potentially enabling more targeted outreach and retention support in HIV care programs.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-026-03434-z.

Introduction

Uganda has made substantial progress toward the UNAIDS 95–95-95 targets. Recent estimates show that 94% of people living with HIV (PLHIV) know their HIV status, 92% are on antiretroviral therapy (ART), and 84% are virally suppressed [1]. Despite these gains, HIV remains a leading cause of mortality, with approximately 22,000 AIDS-related deaths reported annually. One persistent challenge is retention in HIV care, which directly affects sustained viral suppression and progress toward the third 95 target. Studies report that up to 21% of PLHIV disengage from care within the first year after ART initiation, with attrition rates ranging from 10% to 25% across settings [2–5]. Poor retention has been linked to reduced viral suppression and increased HIV transmission [6–8].

Current strategies to re-engage PLHIV who miss appointments rely largely on community health workers and phone-based follow-up. These approaches are resource-intensive and face challenges related to cost, confidentiality, and limited infrastructure, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. Proactive identification of clients at risk of missing appointments could enable earlier, more targeted interventions and reduce reliance on reactive tracing strategies.

Recent work has shown the potential of machine learning methods to predict retention-related outcomes in HIV care [9–13]. Maskew et al. applied traditional machine learning models to South African cohorts and reported moderate predictive performance for visit attendance and viral suppression [9]. Ramachandran et al. developed predictive models using electronic medical records and geospatial data in a U.S. HIV clinic, improving identification of patients at high risk of disengagement [10]. Similar approaches have been used in pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) programs to identify predictors of retention among key populations [14]. These studies demonstrate the utility of predictive modelling for targeting adherence support.

Most prior studies, though, relied on traditional machine learning models that treat clinical encounters as independent observations. This limits their ability to capture longitudinal and sequential patterns in care engagement [15, 16]. Transformer-based models, such as Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT), are designed to model sequences and contextual dependencies and are therefore better suited for longitudinal clinical data [17, 18]. In addition, many earlier studies did not explicitly address class imbalance, which can bias model performance toward majority outcomes.

While much of HIV outcomes research focuses on causal inference to understand the effects of interventions, this study takes a predictive approach. Our goal is to estimate the likelihood of missed clinical visits using existing data, not to determine why they occur. Predictive modeling supports risk stratification and targeted follow-up but does not imply that identified features are causal drivers of disengagement.

This study applies both traditional machine learning models and a transformer-based sequential model to predict visit-level missed appointments among PLHIV using routinely collected longitudinal data from HIV clinics in Uganda. We compare model performance under different sampling strategies and identify key predictors of missed appointments. Specifically, we address the following questions: i) How do traditional machine learning models compare with a BERT-based sequential model in predicting missed appointments using longitudinal clinical data? ii) How do different sampling techniques affect model performance in the presence of class imbalance? iii) Which model and sampling approach yields the best performance across standard evaluation metrics?

Study design and population

This was a retrospective study that applied machine learning and deep learning approaches to routine clinical data. We used de-identified treatment records of PLHIV collected through UgandaEMR, an electronic medical records (EMR) system implemented in over 1000 health facilities across Uganda.

The dataset consists of longitudinal records of PLHIV receiving ART services from facilities using UgandaEMR. We extracted data for 106,155 clients with 2,071,479 clinical visits from 99 facilities across 56 districts. Facility types included 12 general hospitals, 8 Health Centre IIs, 53 HCIIIs, 19 HCIVs, 2 national referral hospitals, 2 regional referral hospitals, and 2 specialized HIV clinics (e.g., TASO). These facilities represent those that had submitted their data to the centralized national data warehouse at the time of its initial implementation, and collectively cover all levels of Uganda’s health facility hierarchy across multiple districts, providing a diverse and representative cross-section of the country’s HIV service delivery network.

These data include clients who were active in care between 2000 and 2023. Available variables included demographics (e.g., age, sex, contact status), clinical indicators (e.g., WHO stage, TB status), treatment variables (e.g., regimen type, adherence, treatment duration), and structural factors (e.g., facility level, entry point). These variables were selected based on three complementary criteria: their clinical relevance to HIV retention and appointment adherence, their established programmatic significance within Uganda’s national HIV care guidelines, and their consistent identification as important predictors in prior literature on IIT and loss to follow-up in sub-Saharan Africa. This approach ensured that the predictor set was both empirically grounded and contextually appropriate for the Ugandan HIV care setting.

Of the 106,155 clients and 2,071,479 clinical visits initially extracted, we excluded 39,949 clients and 604,348 visits due to missing or invalid data, as detailed in Section “Inclusion and exclusion”. The final analytic dataset included 66,206 clients with 1,479,121 valid clinical visits across 86 facilities in 56 districts.

Outcome variable

The primary outcome was visit-level retention in care, defined as attending a scheduled clinic visit on or within 28 days of the documented next appointment date. This aligns with the PEPFAR MER 2.4 guidance, which classifies clients who miss a visit by more than 28 days as LTFU [19]. Each scheduled visit was treated as a prediction instance. The outcome was binary: “attended” or “missed” based on whether the client returned within the 28-day window. Of the 1,479,121 clinical visits in the final analytic dataset, 1,320,855 (89.3%) were classified as “attended” and 158,266 (10.7%) as “missed,” reflecting a substantial class imbalance that was addressed through the sampling techniques described in Section “Sampling”. The outcome definition and 28-day threshold were applied consistently across all facilities and years. Clinical visits occurring after a documented death or formal transfer out were excluded and not treated as missed. These cases were removed from both the outcome classification and model training. Unscheduled or emergency visits were also excluded and did not reset the scheduled return date.

We acknowledge the potential for misclassification due to undocumented (silent) transfers, which are not always recorded in the EMR. While clinical visits after formal transfer-out were excluded, some clients labeled as “missed” may have silently transferred to another facility. These limitations are further discussed in Section “Limitations and future work”.

Inclusion and exclusion

The study included PLHIV with at least one recorded clinical visit and well-defined data points across various variables, whose records were captured using UgandaEMR. PLHIV initiated into care between 2000 and 2023 were included in the study. We excluded clients with only one clinical visit because it was not possible to determine the outcome variable for those records, as outcome classification required at least two visits to establish whether a client returned within the expected timeframe.

We also excluded clinical visits missing the outcome variable due to the absence of a documented next scheduled return visit date. Clinical visits occurring after documented death or formal transfer out were excluded from the analysis. Only scheduled routine clinical visits with a valid next appointment date were eligible for outcome classification.

Records missing values on any of the variables retained for analysis were excluded, as these records lacked sufficient information for modeling and could not be reliably imputed without introducing bias. Variables with very low completeness rates, such as comorbidities, CD4 count, weight, and height, were eliminated from the analysis entirely before this step and therefore did not contribute to record exclusion. The variable selection and missing data handling procedures are described in detail in Section “Missing data”. Predictors and feature engineering

This study utilized 18 predictors, including both raw and desired/engineered variables, to develop more robust predictive models. The predictors included client demographics such as age and sex. Derived clinical visit patterns included the overall number of visits, the number of prior interruptions in treatment (IITs) a client has ever had, the number of IITs in the last 12 months, and the duration on treatment. Information on IITs was included to help the models capture historical treatment interruptions. All engineered features, including visit counts and treatment interruptions, were derived strictly from data available up to the prediction point to ensure that inputs reflected only information known at the time of prediction and to prevent data leakage. Treatment data variables included the current ART regimen, regimen line (categorized as first-line, second-line, or third-line based on national treatment guidelines), regimen changes, and the number of visits on the current regimen (i.e., the number of visits the patient has had while on their current regimen). Additionally, we included calculated variables such as the season, derived from the month of each return visit date. We categorized March, April, May, September, October, November, and December as the “rainy season” and January, February, June, July, and August as the “dry season” to investigate the effect of seasonality on clinical visit attendance, based on the National Meteorological Authority seasonal report.

To avoid any risk of information leakage, we applied all feature engineering after temporally splitting the dataset and assigning patients to either the training or test set. For each visit, features were computed only from data available up to the prediction point, defined as the most recent prior visit before the scheduled return date. No label-adjacent or future information was accessed in building input features.

Missing data

The level of completeness for the considered variables varied significantly, with some variables, such as age, sex, and entry point, having a 100% completeness rate, while others, like comorbidities, had a very low completeness rate of 1.5%. Variables with very low completeness, such as comorbidities, CD4 count, weight, and height, were eliminated from the analysis to avoid the bias that could be introduced by imputing these largely missing values. For the phone number variable, we transformed the data to indicate whether a contact was provided (yes) or not (no), replacing NA values with “no” under the assumption that the number was not provided.

For PLHIV missing an ART start date, we used the proxy of the first encounter date. Since most machine learning models except XGBoost cannot handle missing values directly, and because many of the variables were categorical, which could introduce bias if missing values were replaced with the majority class, we opted to remove all records with missing data [20]. This decision was supported by the negligible effect it had on the models’ performance. We also tested adding a missing category for selected variables, but this approach did not improve model performance and introduced sparsity, so it was not used.

Machine learning

We explored several machine learning algorithms to compare their performance in predicting whether PLHIV will honor their scheduled appointment date. Models evaluated were: Decision Tree (DT), Random Forest (RF), AdaBoost, and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost). All models were trained to predict the outcome of the next scheduled clinical visit at the visit level, ensuring a valid forward-facing prediction framework. To preserve the temporal structure of the data and prevent information leakage, we implemented a time-based split strategy at the encounter level. Specifically, all clinical visit records were first sorted in chronological order by visit date. The earliest 80% of encounters were assigned to the training set, and the most recent 20% to the testing set. To avoid information leakage across patient histories, we ensured that each PLHIV’s complete set of visits was assigned to only one partition [21]. If any of a patient’s visits fell into the test window, all of that patient’s visits were included in the test set. This ensured strict separation between training and test subjects while maintaining the real-world scenario of forecasting future outcomes using past data. This approach simulates a real-world scenario where models trained on historical data are applied prospectively to future visits and clients.

The prediction target was visit-level attendance, specifically whether a client would attend their next scheduled appointment within 28 days of the assigned date. This defines a short-term prediction horizon focused on individual appointment adherence, rather than long-term retention (e.g., 6- or 12-month follow-up).

While traditional machine learning models (e.g., Random Forest, XGBoost) were trained on static, tabular representations of individual visits, the BERT model was trained on temporally ordered sequences of patient visits with positional and contextual embeddings. This creates a representational asymmetry across modeling approaches. The comparison was designed to evaluate the added value of sequential modeling on real-world longitudinal clinical data, not to establish superiority under identical feature constraints.

Decision trees

Decision Trees are a machine learning method that repeatedly splits the dataset according to a criterion that maximizes the separation of the data, resulting in a tree-like structure [22]. This study used a decision tree classifier and optimized its performance using a GridSearchCV approach. Several parameters were explored through a grid search, including the criterion (“gini” and “entropy”), splitter (“best” and “random”), max depth (None, 10, and 20), min samples split (2, 5, and 10), and min samples leaf (1, 5, and 10). The GridSearchCV was configured to use 10-fold cross-validation and optimize for accuracy. The optimal parameters identified were: criterion = ‘gini’, max depth = None, min samples leaf = 1, min samples split = 2, and splitter = ‘best’. This configuration was used to build the final Decision Tree models on the original dataset, as well as models using undersampling, oversampling, and SMOTE techniques to address class imbalance.

Random Forest

Random Forest is an ensemble learning method that makes predictions by constructing multiple decision trees during training and outputting the class that is the mode of the classes or the mean prediction of the individual trees [23]. This method improves predictive accuracy and controls overfitting by averaging multiple decision trees, thereby reducing variance without significantly increasing bias. The Random Forest algorithm uses the principle of bagging, which combines the predictions of various base estimators built with different subsets of the dataset to improve generalizability [24].

In this study, the training procedure of the Random Forest model involved several steps to ensure optimal performance. We defined a parameter grid specifying the number of trees (100), maximum depth of each tree (10), minimum number of samples required to split a node (10), minimum number of samples required to be at a leaf node (4), and the number of features to consider for the best split (square root of the total number of features). The model training utilized grid search with 5-fold cross-validation to identify the best parameters. While an extensive grid search with multiple parameters was not performed due to the computational demands, the selected parameters were found to provide good performance.

AdaBoost

AdaBoost, or Adaptive Boosting, is another ensemble learning technique that aims to improve the performance of weak classifiers by combining them into a strong classifier. In this study, we employed AdaBoost with a decision tree as the base classifier. AdaBoost works by sequentially fitting the base classifier to the data, where each subsequent model attempts to correct the errors made by the previous ones [25]. The algorithm assigns higher weights to misclassified instances, forcing the model to focus on the harder-to-classify samples. This iterative process continues until a specified number of classifiers are added or the performance no longer improves.

Extreme Gradient Boosting

Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) is a highly efficient and scalable implementation of gradient boosting that has gained popularity for its performance in various machine learning competitions and practical applications [26]. The algorithm builds an ensemble of decision trees in a sequential manner, where each tree attempts to correct the errors made by the previous trees by minimizing a specified loss function using gradient descent optimization. XGBoost introduces several enhancements over traditional gradient boosting, such as regularization techniques to prevent overfitting, a tree learning algorithm for handling sparse data, and the ability to parallelize tree construction, significantly improving computational efficiency and scalability [26].

For this study, we utilized the XGBoost classifier with specific hyperparameters to optimize model performance. The parameters included a maximum tree depth of 6, a learning rate of 0.1, and 100 estimators. The model’s objective was set to binary logistic regression, suitable for binary classification tasks, and the booster type was set to use gradient-boosted trees. The XGBoost classifier was configured to use a single job for parallel processing, which banks on the algorithm’s ability to distribute computations across multiple processors. This approach not only accelerates the training process but also enhances the model’s ability to handle large datasets efficiently.

Bidirectional encoder representations from transformers

We chose the BERT because transform-based approaches are designed to model sequential patterns and capture contextual information from both directions, which is critical in this study, where each HIV client has a longitudinal history of appointments. Traditional machine learning models, such as those discussed in Section “Bidirectional encoder representations from transformers”, are designed to handle independent and identically distributed data, but struggle to capture the temporal dependencies within sequences of client visits [16]. In this work, we used a base pre-trained BERT model and fine-tuned it on the clinical dataset. BERT, on the other hand, leverages a transformer-based architecture that enables it to capture complex patterns and relationships within the sequential data by processing the entire sequence simultaneously [17]. This capability allows BERT to effectively learn patterns and trends from the sequence of past appointments, which is vital for accurately predicting future attendance. Studies have demonstrated the superiority of transformer-based models like BERT in modeling sequential data and capturing long-term dependencies compared to traditional methods [17, 18]. All predictions produced by the BERT model were made at the visit level, where sequences of prior visits for each client were used to predict the outcome of the next scheduled visit.

The model predicts the outcome of the next appointment based on the sequence of past appointments, making it particularly useful for time-series prediction. Unlike traditional machine learning models that treat each clinical visit as independent, BERT uses past information to predict future outcomes. The prediction of the next appointment’s outcome was based on a sliding window approach to create sequences of a fixed length. Each sequence was used to predict the outcome of the subsequent appointments. A sequence length of the last 10 appointments was selected, balancing the need to capture sufficient recent historical data with model complexity. Studies have shown that recent behaviors are more predictive of future actions, and focusing on the last 10 appointments allows the model to leverage current trends and variations in a client’s attendance history [9, 27]. This length is also computationally efficient, enabling effective training and evaluation without excessive noise or overfitting.

At the core of the BERT model are six Transformer encoder layers, each comprising a multi-head self-attention mechanism and a position-wise fully connected feed-forward network [17]. The self-attention mechanism is crucial as it enables the model to weigh the importance of each past appointment in the sequence, thereby capturing relevant temporal patterns and dependencies [18]. To further refine the encoded information, the output from the Transformer encoder was passed through a fully connected layer with 512 units, followed by a dropout layer with a dropout rate of 0.3 to mitigate overfitting. The final classification layer maps the processed features to class probabilities corresponding to the predicted outcome. A dynamic calculation of the number of attention heads in the multi-head self-attention mechanism ensures compatibility with the input dimension, enhancing the model’s learning efficiency [17].

Each clinical visit was represented as a structured token within a visit-level sequence derived from demographic, clinical, treatment-related, and structural predictors described in Section “Predictors and feature engineering”, in addition to prior appointment outcomes. Categorical variables were embedded as learnable vectors, and continuous variables were normalized before input. All sequences were sorted chronologically, and sequence position was encoded using learned positional embeddings. Visits were padded or truncated to a fixed length of 10. Padding masks were applied to ensure that the model ignored non-informative padded positions during self-attention computations. Irregular time gaps between visits were not explicitly modeled, but temporal order was strictly preserved.

The model was trained using a binary cross-entropy loss function with a batch size of 32. Training was conducted for up to 20 epochs using the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 2 × 10− 5 and a StepLR learning rate scheduler (step size = 1, gamma = 0.9). An early stopping strategy was applied based on validation loss with a patience of 3 epochs to prevent overfitting. Sampling

In normal circumstances, machine learning models are mainly evaluated using accuracy, precision, and recall score metrics [28]. However, in our study, we dealt with a class-imbalanced dataset, where the majority of clinical visits were attended (percent) compared to those missed (percent). This imbalance can make the standard metrics misleading, as the models may accurately predict clients who attended their appointments on time while poorly predicting missed appointments, which are critical for improving client retention in treatment.

To address this problem, we utilized sampling techniques including undersampling, oversampling, and the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE). These methods were employed to assess their impact on the performance of the models compared to the results achieved when trained on the imbalanced dataset. All sampling techniques were applied only to the training dataset, while the original class distribution was preserved in the test dataset for unbiased model evaluation

Undersampling

Undersampling is a technique used to reduce the number of samples in the majority class to address the class imbalance in the dataset [29, 30]. This can be achieved either randomly, through Random under-sampling (RUS), or using statistical knowledge, through informed undersampling. For this study, the RUS method was used with a sampling strategy that undersamples the majority class to 90% of the minority class size. RUS reduces instances of the majority class by randomly deleting them from the dataset, thus creating a balanced dataset for both the majority and minority classes. This approach helps reduce the bias towards the majority class, allowing the machine learning models to perform better on the minority class.

Oversampling

For oversampling, new samples are added to the minority class to balance the dataset. These methods can be categorized into random oversampling, where the existing minority samples are replicated to increase the size of a minority class, and synthetic oversampling, where artificial samples are generated for the minority class. These new samples add essential information to the minority class and prevent its instances from being misclassified [31]. Similar to the approach used in undersampling, we utilized a sampling strategy of 90%. This approach ensured that the models had sufficient data from the minority class, improving their ability to correctly classify minority class instances and reducing bias towards the majority class.

SMOTE

SMOTE is a sampling method that generates synthetic samples within the minority class to address class imbalance [32]. SMOTE operates by identifying the minority class boundary samples, then producing a number of new samples between the boundary samples and the neighbors, and finally adding these synthetic samples to the original dataset [33]. In this study, the minority class in the training dataset was increased by over 90%. A significant number of synthetic instances of the minority class were generated and added to the dataset, enhancing the models’ ability to learn from a more balanced distribution and improving the accuracy of predictions for the minority class.

Model validation

After training, the performance of all models was evaluated and compared to select the best-performing one for predicting missed appointment dates among HIV patients. The evaluation used confusion matrices to compute accuracy, precision, sensitivity, specificity, F1-score, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic (AUC-ROC), as these are the most widely used evaluation metrics for classification tasks in machine learning [34].

Given the imbalanced nature of the outcome, emphasis was placed on class-specific performance metrics, particularly sensitivity, specificity, precision, recall, and F1score for missed appointments, rather than accuracy alone. Evaluation metrics were computed on the held-out set using the default decision threshold of 0.5. Sensitivity and specificity for the best-performing model (BERT) are reported at this threshold, alongside precision.

To maintain temporal integrity and simulate real-time forecasting, patient-level splitting occurred before any sequence construction or feature engineering. Each patient’s entire visit history was confined to either the training or test set, ensuring no overlap of time periods or future information in the input data.

Implementation

The models were developed and evaluated using open-source Python libraries. Scikit-learn (version 1.6.1) was used for traditional machine learning algorithms [35], while PyTorch (version 2.6.0) was utilized for deep learning models, particularly the BERT model [17, 36]. Data cleaning and transformations were performed using Pandas (version 2.2.0) [37] to ensure the dataset was properly preprocessed for model training. SHAP (version 0.45.0) was used for feature importance analysis. A random seed of 42 was set across all models and data splitting procedures to ensure reproducibility of results. Prior to model training, categorical variables were encoded using label encoding, assigning a unique integer for each category. For the BERT model, categorical variables were instead represented as learnable embedding vectors, as described in Section “Bidirectional encoder representations from transformers”.

Results

The final dataset used in this study included 66,206 PLHIV with 1,479,121 clinical visits from 86 facilities. It is important to note that the feature importance results shown in Figure 5 reflect how the model uses available information to generate predictions, rather than indicating causal relationships. The SHAP-based importance scores describe the relative contribution of each feature to the model’s predictive behavior and should not be interpreted as evidence that these features are direct causes of appointment attendance or non-attendance. These scores may also be influenced by feature correlations, meaning that the importance attributed to one feature may partly reflect the contribution of correlated features rather than its independent predictive value. It is also worth noting that SHAP-based interpretability was applied to the XGBoost model only; given the architectural complexity of the BERT model, feature-level interpretation was not performed and remains an important area for future work. Instead, these results highlight patterns in the data that are informative for prediction within the observed clinical context.

Discussion

The results from our comparative analysis show that the BERT model performed better than the traditional machine learning models in predicting missed appointment treatment dates among HIV patients using longitudinal clinical data. The BERT model achieved an AUC score of 0.96, higher than the scores obtained by the Decision Tree, Random Forest, AdaBoost, and XGBoost models. This superior performance can be attributed to BERT’s ability to capture complex patterns and dependencies within sequential data, an important factor in time-series prediction tasks such as appointment attendance [38]. Traditional models like Decision Trees and Random Forests treat each instance independently, limiting their ability to leverage temporal information [15, 16]. In contrast, BERT’s transformer architecture allows it to consider the sequence of appointments holistically, enhancing its predictive accuracy and robustness [17, 18]. These results demonstrate that when machine learning and deep learning approaches are properly applied to routinely collected clinical and demographic data, they can correctly predict appointment attendance with high accuracy.

The BERT model exhibited very high precision when predicting PLHIV likely to honor their treatment schedules, indicating it is almost always correct in its positive predictions. Its recall was slightly lower, meaning some actual attended visits were missed, but the high F1-score showed a strong balance between the two. For missed appointments, the BERT model had high recall, correctly identifying all PLHIV likely to miss their treatment appointment schedules, though its precision was slightly lower, indicating some false positives. These results align with findings from Ramachandran et al. [10], who used predictive models in a U.S. HIV clinic and achieved a positive predictive value of 34.6% for the top 10% high-risk clients, and from Maskew et al. [9], who reported an AUC of 0.69 using random forest models in South Africa. Compared to these, BERT achieved higher precision (89.0% for missed visits), recall (100%), and AUC (0.96), suggesting that transformer-based models offer superior performance in handling sequential clinical data. Given the clinical priority of identifying patients at risk of missing appointments, Recall for the Missed class was adopted as the primary evaluation metric, since failing to detect a likely no-show carries greater operational and health consequences than a false alarm. Under this criterion, BERT achieved the highest performance with a Recall of 100% for missed appointments, correctly flagging every at-risk patient. This was further supported by its leading F1-Score for the Missed class (94.0%), confirming strong overall balance between sensitivity and precision. BERT was therefore selected as the best-performing model. This demonstrates the advantage of using temporal models like BERT over traditional approaches in predicting both attendance and risk of disengagement.

The impact of various sampling techniques on the performance of traditional machine learning models showed that undersampling generally resulted in a performance drop across all metrics for the Decision Tree, Random Forest, AdaBoost, and XGBoost models due to the loss of valuable information when the majority class is reduced. However, undersampling provided a balance in performance for the two classes, evident from the improved AUC scores for Random Forest and XGBoost. Conversely, oversampling and SMOTE helped maintain more stable performance by reducing bias towards the majority class while retaining sufficient samples for training. These sampling techniques effectively addressed the imbalance problem, yielding better prediction outcomes for both classes. The algorithms achieved high accuracy scores when trained on the original imbalanced dataset, which can be misleading due to the imbalance in outcome classes. Correctly predicting PLHIV who are likely to miss their appointments is important for improving retention in care. Therefore, although high performance on an imbalanced dataset may appear impressive, it does not necessarily reflect true predictive accuracy. The use of sampling techniques significantly improved the classification accuracy for both classes. Similar findings were reported by Cai et al. [39], who applied SMOTE to improve the performance of models predicting retention in HIV care, and Bero et al. [14], who found that oversampling improved the sensitivity of models identifying clients at risk of dropping out of PrEP programs. Based on our findings, if computational resources and expertise are limited, the XGBoost model with undersampling provides the most balanced performance among traditional machine learning models evaluated.

The feature importance analysis highlighted the critical role of historical treatment patterns in predicting adherence to appointments. In particular, interruptions in treatment and visit frequency emerged as dominant factors, showing that past engagement strongly influences future outcomes. These findings suggest that monitoring adherence history and visit consistency is central to identifying clients at risk of missing appointments. While demographic characteristics such as sex, age group, and seasonal factors contributed less, they still provide additional context for risk stratification. Overall, these results align with other studies showing that patient behavior and treatment history are stronger predictors of retention than demographic variables, emphasizing the value of personalized treatment support and closer monitoring of continuity in care [9, 10, 14].

This study’s comparison framework reflects a paradigm-level evaluation. Traditional models rely on tabular input with static features, while the BERT model leverages the full temporal structure of patient visit histories. As such, the performance differences reflect both architectural and input representation advantages inherent to transformer-based sequential models. The goal was not a head-to-head comparison on identical inputs, but rather to assess the gains from modeling visit sequences explicitly using modern deep learning approaches.

Limitations and future work

The results of this study should be interpreted with some limitations in mind. First, the dataset had substantial missing or undefined values, resulting in the exclusion of 39,949 (37.6%) clients and 604,348 (29.2%) clinical visits. This reduction in the analytic sample may introduce selection bias and limit the extent to which the findings can be generalized to the broader population of PLHIV in care. Additionally, factors influencing PLHIV clinical attendance, such as education level and community contextual factors, were not routinely captured in the UgandaEMR. Also, mislabeling of the outcome was another limitation, as health facilities sometimes did not update transfer-in and out statuses in the EMR promptly. This may have led to PLHIV who were transferred to another facility being incorrectly labeled as having missed a visit.

We did not explicitly account for the hierarchical structure of patients nested within health facilities. Although facility-level variables were included, unmeasured differences across sites, such as urban versus rural setting, staffing levels, or resource availability, may have introduced clustering effects not captured in our models. Future work could address this using multilevel or mixed-effects modeling. Since the models were trained and evaluated only on Uganda data, their generalizability to other national HIV care settings remains to be established. While the predictor variables used, including demographics, treatment history, and visit patterns, are routinely collected across many LMIC HIV programs, the models reflect Uganda-specific clinical workflows, appointment scheduling practices, and patient population characteristics. Differences in EMR systems, data completeness, facility infrastructure, and patient demographics across other LMIC settings may affect model transferability, and external validation in settings with different EMR platforms, such as OpenMRS or DHIS2-linked systems, would be a necessary prerequisite before broader adoption can be recommended.

The interpretability of complex models like BERT remains a challenge, as it is difficult to explain how and why these models arrive at their predictions, making them somewhat of a black box [40]. Finally, the BERT model does not account for irregular time intervals between clinical visits. It processes sequences in fixed order, assessing equal spacing between events, which may obscure time-dependent relations. Future work will focus on developing models that are both more interpretable and time-aware, incorporating approaches such as Time-BERT [41], transformer models with temporal decay [42], or recurrent models like GRU-D [43] to better capture irregular clinical patterns while improving the transparency of model predictions.

The exclusions shown in Fig. 1, particularly the removal of clients and visits due to incomplete data, may bias the analytic sample toward better-documented clients and facilities. This could lead to overestimation of model performance, as clients with poorer retention or lower data quality, who are often most at risk, may be underrepresented. These limitations should be considered when interpreting model results and assessing real-world deployment in settings with higher EMR missingness.

Study contributions and practical implications

Despite the mentioned limitations, our study offers several contributions. It is among the few to evaluate transformer models like BERT on clinical HIV data. The BERT model achieved higher performance than the traditional models regardless of the sampling technique used, suggesting potential benefits of sequential modeling for visit-level prediction. This analysis provides a comprehensive comparison between traditional machine learning models and deep learning approaches, demonstrating how sampling techniques impact the performance of these algorithms. This comparison serves as a foundation for other researchers interested in similar studies. The study also responds to the call by the Government of Uganda through the Ministry of Health to integrate cutting-edge technologies like artificial intelligence into clinical decision-support systems to improve care and treatment.

The strong predictive performance of the BERT model, particularly its perfect recall for missed appointments, suggests a meaningful opportunity to translate these findings into actionable clinical tools. By leveraging routinely collected EMR data without requiring additional data collection, the model could be integrated into existing systems such as UgandaEMR to generate automated visit-level risk scores ahead of each scheduled appointment. Patients flagged as high-risk could then be prioritized for targeted retention interventions, including phone call reminders, peer navigator follow-up, or community health worker outreach, directly strengthening the last mile of the HIV care cascade.

Realizing this potential, however, requires careful attention to implementation feasibility. The 86 facilities included in this study vary considerably in their technical infrastructure and data quality, both of which would influence model performance in real-world deployment. The computational demands of the BERT architecture, while manageable in a centralized server environment, may present challenges in facilities with limited connectivity. In such contexts, a lighter-weight model such as XGBoost may offer a pragmatic alternative, trading a degree of predictive performance for greater operational simplicity. Regardless of the model adopted, outputs must be translated into interpretable and actionable formats for frontline staff, as raw probability scores are unlikely to be meaningful without appropriate training and decision support tools.

Equally important are the ethical dimensions of deploying automated risk stratification in HIV care. Predictions must be used to extend additional support to flagged patients, not to create barriers to care or reinforce existing stigma. Regular auditing of model performance across sex, age, facility type, and other demographic strata would be essential to identify and mitigate any disparate impact over time. Successful deployment, therefore, requires alignment with clinical workflows, sufficient provider capacity to act on model outputs, clearly defined response protocols, and ongoing engagement with patients and communities.

If validated prospectively, predictive models such as this one may support UgandaEMR workflows by identifying clients at higher risk of missed appointments, potentially enabling more targeted follow-up and retention support. Future work should prioritize prospective validation, user-centered design with clinicians and health workers, and rigorous impact evaluation to ensure that these predictive tools translate into measurable improvements in retention and health outcomes.

Conclusion

This study highlights the potential of advanced machine learning and deep learning models, particularly transformer-based architectures like BERT, in predicting missed appointment dates among HIV patients. Our implementation framework enables clinicians and HIV caregivers to identify clients at the highest risk of dropping out of care and develop targeted interventions to improve retention. The results show that BERT significantly outperforms traditional machine learning models. This superior performance is attributed to BERT’s ability to effectively capture and utilize complex temporal patterns within sequential data, which is vital for time-series prediction tasks such as appointment adherence.

Even with various sampling techniques applied to traditional models to address class imbalance, they could not match the accuracy of the BERT model. These findings indicate that while techniques such as undersampling, oversampling, and SMOTE can improve the performance of traditional models, advanced architectures like BERT are better suited for handling complex, sequential clinical datasets. This insight is valuable for guiding future research and implementations aimed at optimizing HIV patient care through predictive modeling and proactive programmatic strategies.
