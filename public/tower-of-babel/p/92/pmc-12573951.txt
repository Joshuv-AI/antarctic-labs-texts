Associations between general and abdominal obesity and oral health behaviors: a cross-sectional study based on data from the Rafsanjan Cohort
Authors: Elahe Tahmasebi, Pouya Abedi, Parvin Khalili, Mahmood Salesi, Zahra Jamali, Samira Hajisadeghi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObesity poses a risk for numerous chronic diseases including those affecting oral health. The present study aimed to assess the possible associations between obesity and oral health behaviors among the Iranian adult population.MethodsThis cross-sectional study used data from the oral health branch of the Rafsanjan Cohort Study (RCS) launched in 2015. A total of 6745 out of 9991 individuals were included in the analysis. RCS data had been collected by oral examination, interviewing, and completing questionnaires about demographic characteristics, lifestyle habits, such as smoking and oral health habits (tooth brushing frequency, dental cleaning method, and dental visit), and anthropometric measurements. Body mass index (BMI) for general obesity and waist circumference (WC) for abdominal obesity were used. Multinomial logistic regression models were employed to assess the possible association between obesity status and oral health behavior.ResultsOf 6745 participants, 2869 (42.54%) were overweight, and 2123 (31.48%) and 3757 (55.70%) had general and abdominal obesity, respectively. General obesity was associated with poorer combined brushing and flossing habits (OR: 0.77, 0.61–0.97). Abdominal obesity showed associations, including reduced odds of combined brushing and flossing habits (OR: 0.68, 95% CI 0.55–0.85) and 19% less frequent daily tooth brushing (OR: 0.81, 0.68–0.96). Also, overweight showed associations, including increased combined brushing and flossing habits (OR: 1.35, 1.09–1.66) and 22% more frequent daily tooth brushing (OR: 1.22, 1.03–1.45). While abdominal obesity was not associated with the timing of the last dental visit, overweight and general obesity had higher odds of recent dental visits (OR: 1.14, 1.01–1.29 and OR: 1.17, 1.01–1.34, respectively). Furthermore, the rate of decayed, missing, and filled teeth (DMFT) was lower in overweight and general obesity (OR: 0.96, 0.95–0.98, OR: 0.95, 0.94–0.97, respectively) and slightly higher in abdominal obesity (OR: 1.04, 1.03–1.05) compared to the reference group.ConclusionThe present study highlights the association between abdominal obesity and oral health behaviors, as it was linked to lower tooth brushing frequency, poorer combined brushing and flossing habits, and higher DMFT rates. These findings emphasize incorporating WC in risk assessments and addressing shared risk factors to improve oral health outcomes.Keywords: Oral health branch of Rafsanjan cohort study, Oral health behavior, Obesity, Abdominal obesity, Iran, BMI, Brushing

Introduction

Oral and dental problems represent a considerable financial burden at both the individual and healthcare system levels. Despite available preventive measures, these issues remain prevalent worldwide [1–3]. Research has demonstrated that maintaining proper oral hygiene through practices such as regular tooth brushing, flossing, and dental checkups can effectively prevent tooth decay and periodontal diseases, contributing to improved oral health [3, 4]. Conversely, neglecting oral health behaviors, increases the risk of dental problems and reduces quality of life [5]. Oral health behaviors are closely linked to overall health behaviors [6] and oral health is essential for overall well-being, with tooth brushing serving as a key indicator of overall healthy living rather than solely a dental care practice [7, 8].

Several factors, including gender, socioeconomic status, education, obesity, physical activity, and dietary habits, influence both general and oral health behaviors [9–15]. Obesity, a growing global concern is associated with higher rates of periodontal disease, dental caries, and chronic conditions such as hypertension and diabetes, while also posing significant public health and financial challenges that affect overall and oral health [9, 16–18]. In Iran, obesity prevalence among older adults is 21.4%, reflecting the need for attention to this issue [19]. Evidence suggests that obesity and oral health behaviors share complex interrelations and possibly mediated by shared risk factors [17]. Unhealthy lifestyle habits linked to obesity, such as frequent snacking, sugar consumption, fast food preference, low physical activity, and irregular tooth brushing, highlight the complex relationship between weight status and oral health behaviors [9–11]. Some previous studies have highlighted the influence of weight status on oral health behaviors [10, 14]. Studies have emphasized the shared behavioral and causal mechanisms underpinning general and oral health, with patterns like dental attendance and other health habits often align [10, 14]. Socioeconomic status (SES) and education significantly influence health behaviors, including diet and oral hygiene practices, and mediates the relationship between obesity and oral health outcomes. Psychological distress and unequal access to cultural resources, such as health literacy, further widen these disparities [13, 20]. These findings provide an evidence-based hypothesis for examining oral health behaviors as outcomes influenced by obesity.

To the best of our knowledge, there is insufficient research investigating the correlation between obesity and oral health behaviors, particularly in relation to Iranian adults. Therefore, the primary objective of the present study was to investigate the associations between oral health behaviors—including tooth brushing frequency, dental cleaning methods, and dental visitation—and obesity, as defined by Body mass index (BMI) and waist circumference (WC) in a representative sample of Iranian adults aged 35 to 75 years, using data from the Rafsanjan Cohort Study (RCS).

Materials and methods

Study design and patient selection

The present study was a cross-sectional study that was conducted using data from oral health branch of the Rafsanjan Cohort Study (OHBRCS) in the baseline phase of RCS [21] a component of the Prospective Epidemiological Research Studies in Iran (PERSIAN) [22]. The RCS, conducted from August 2015 to December 2017, involved 10,000 participants aged 35–70 years in Rafsanjan, a city in southeastern Iran [23]. Individuals were randomly selected by systematic clustering. All participants of the RCS were also invited to refer to the OHBRCS. Finally, 8682 individuals of the RCS were included in the OHBRCS. After excluding subjects with both upper and lower removable complete dentures, 6745 individuals were included in the present study. The RCS and PERSIAN methodologies, data collection tools, sampling methods, and variables were detailed in previous cohort profile and methodology studies [21–23]. The present study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Baqiyatallah University of Medical Sciences in Tehran, Iran (IR.BMSU.REC.1401.097).

Data collection

Data was collected at the Rafsanjan cohort center from 2015 to 2017 through interviewer-administered questionnaires and clinical oral examinations conducted by trained examiners. These examiners participated in calibration workshops led by an experienced dentist, following the World Health Organization (WHO) Oral Health Surveys Basic Methods, to assess the decayed, missing, and filled teeth (DMFT) index in individuals. Both inter- and intra-examiner reliability were assessed during these workshops to ensure consistency and accuracy in data collection. Inter-examiner reliability was evaluated by comparing the results among different examiners, while intra-examiner reliability was assessed by having the same examiner repeat assessments under similar conditions.

Questionnaire

The PERSIAN Cohort Study utilized a comprehensive questionnaire comprising 482 items divided into three major sections: general, medical, and nutrition. These items were categorized into 22 sections and covered a wide range of demographic, socioeconomic, lifestyle, and health-related variables. Each part of the questionnaire was completed through interviews conducted by specially trained personnel, ensuring data accuracy and consistency. Importantly, the questionnaire was validated alongside the cohort study, providing reliable data for secondary analyses such as ours. For our study, we utilized relevant sections related to oral health, lifestyle behaviors, and socioeconomic factors to examine the associations of interest. The questionnaires included questions on demographic characteristics, such as age, gender and education level, cigarette and hookah smoking status, opium consumption, alcohol consumption, BMI, wealth score index (WSI), and oral health behaviors. To assess oral health behaviors, participants completed a questionnaire that included the following questions: ‘How often do you brush your teeth?’ with response options: ‘Never,’ ‘Once a month,’ ‘Two to three times a month,’ ‘Once a week,’ ‘Two to six times a week,’ ‘Once a day,’ or ‘More than once a day’; ‘Do you use dental floss? (Yes/No)’; and ‘When was your last dental visit?’ with options: ‘<6 months,’ ‘6–12 months,’ ‘1–2 years,’ ‘2–5 years,’ or ‘Never.’ For analysis, tooth brushing frequency was categorized into three groups: ‘≥once daily,’ ‘2–6 times per week,’ and ‘Never.’ Dental cleaning methods were grouped into ‘Never,’ ‘Only brushing,’ and ‘Brushing and flossing.’ Dental visit timing was dichotomized as ‘>1 year’ and ‘≤1 year.’ Due to the small number of individuals who used only dental floss, this group was merged into another category. Anthropometric characteristics were measured by trained health professionals at Rafsanjan cohort Center. BMI was computed as weight divided by height squared (kg/m2). Underweight or normal weight was categorized as BMI ≤ 24.9 kg/m2, overweight as BMI 25–29.9 kg/m2, and obesity as BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2. Following WHO guidelines, BMI values are consistent across age groups and genders. Waist measurements were also performed according to the definition of National Institutes of Health (NIH) and were measured the horizontal plane of the superior border of the iliac crest (WC-IC) [24]. Waist circumference ≥ 102 cm for men and ≥ 88 cm for women was defined as abdominal obesity based on National Cholesterol Education Panel –Adult Treatment Panel III (NCEP-ATP III) criteria [25].

Socioeconomic status (SES) was assessed using the Wealth Score Index (WSI), constructed based on data from the Rafsanjan Cohort Study. The WSI incorporated information on infrastructure facilities (e.g., drinking water source, sanitation), housing conditions (e.g., number of rooms, home ownership), durable asset ownership (e.g., dishwasher, car, television), and education level. WSI was categorized into four groups based on the 25th, 50th and 90th percentiles; low income (≤ − 0.6069), low-middle income (− 0.607 to 0.0349), middle-high income (0.035 to 1.169), and high income (≥ 1.170). Participants’ daily physical activity levels were quantified using metabolic equivalents (METs), which represent the relative metabolic cost of an activity. MET-hours per day for 24 h were calculated accordingly. These MET values were then categorized into three groups: low (≤ 35.299), moderate (35.3–40.325.3.325), and heavy (≥ 40.326), based on the 25th and 75th percentiles.

Data on lifestyle habits including cigarette smoking were coded as never, current and former smokers, and alcohol drinking and opium consumption were coded as yes (currently or formerly) and no (never). Participants who had smoked more than 100 cigarettes in their lifetime and were still smoking were classified as current smokers, while those who had quit smoking before the study were considered former smokers [22]. Alcohol consumption was defined as consuming approximately 350 mL of beer, 150 mL of wine, or 45 mL of liquor, at least once per week for 6 months. Opium consumption was defined as self-reported use of opium at least once per week for the 6 months before the study [22, 23]. The frequency difference between the total number and some of the covariates was related to missing data.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using Stata software version 14. Descriptive statistics, including means with standard deviations (SD) for continuous variables and frequencies (%) for categorical variables, were calculated. Between-group comparisons were performed using independent t-tests, ANOVA, and chi-square tests. Multinomial logistic regression models were employed to assess the relationships between overweight/obesity/abdominal obesity and oral health habits after adjusting for covariates. Also, Poisson model was employed to assess the relationships between overweight/obesity/abdominal obesity and DMFT after adjusting for covariates. Variables with a p-value < 0.25 were selected as confounders. A cutoff value of 0.25 is supported by some of the literature [26, 27]. The adjusted model included age (continuous variable), gender (male, female), education years (continuous variable), wealth score index (continuous variable), and the lifestyle variables included cigarette smoking (yes, no), alcohol consumption (yes, no) and opium consumption (yes, no), and physical activity level (continuous variable). All tests were two-sided, with a significance level set at p < 0.05.

Of the 6745 participants, 2869 (42.54%) were overweight, 2123 (31.48%) were obese and 1753 (25.99%) had normal weight. Discussion

The present study examined the association between obesity types (general and abdominal) and oral health behaviors in a sizable Iranian population. Our findings revealed that overweight individuals demonstrated better oral health behaviors, while general and abdominal obesity were associated with poorer behaviors to varying degrees. Although the link between obesity and oral diseases is not fully established, obesity’s association with other health behaviors suggests it may influence oral health outcomes [28]. Given that a high prevalence of obesity among older adults in Iran (21.4%) [19], exploring this relationship is crucial for targeted interventions.

In our study, general obesity (BMI ≥ 30) was not significantly associated with tooth brushing frequency compared to normal-weight individuals, though combined brushing and flossing behavior was notably lower (OR: 0.77, 95% CI: 0.61–0.97). General obesity was also linked to lower physical activity and education levels, factors often associated with poorer health behaviors. The lower DMFT rate observed in this group suggests systemic or metabolic factors may influence oral health outcomes beyond behavioral patterns. These findings underscore the complexity of the relationship between general obesity and oral health.

The relationship between obesity and oral health behaviors has been explored extensively, with varying findings across studies. For instance, Park et al. [29] highlighted dental visit frequency as an independent predictor of BMI, emphasizing the importance of oral health behaviors in overall health management. Similarly, Han et al. [30] and Östberg et al. [9] reported significant associations between general obesity and reduced dental visit frequency, suggesting potential barriers to dental care access among individuals with obesity. Prpić et al. [31], observed no significant correlation but noted worse dental health outcomes, such as tooth loss and carious lesions, in obese individuals. However, our findings showed higher odds of dental visits in the past year in individuals with general obesity (BMI ≥ 30) than those with normal BMI, a result in contrast with mentioned studies.

Our study did reveal poorer combined brushing and flossing behaviors in individuals with general obesity, aligning with Hujoel et al. [32], who reported a dose-dependent correlation between the absence of flossing and obesity. Additionally, Furuta et al. [33] demonstrated a longitudinal association between low tooth brushing frequency and the development of general obesity, while Nihtila et al. [10] found poorer dental behaviors, such as infrequent brushing and irregular dental visits, among overweight populations, particularly younger adults. Interestingly, our results contrasted with studies such as Franchini et al. [34] and Park et al. [35], which reported worse oral hygiene behaviors, including less frequent brushing, among overweight and obese individuals.

These discrepancies highlight the complexity of the relationship between obesity and oral health behaviors, which may be influenced by factors such as SES, education levels, cultural practices, and systemic barriers to care. Notably, SES was included as a confounding variable in our regression models, allowing us to account for its impact on the observed relationships. These findings highlight the need to address upstream factors like SES in public health interventions to mitigate disparities in both oral and general health outcomes. For example, the high prevalence of obesity in certain populations and its association with lower SES and education, as noted by other researchers [36], could exacerbate oral health disparities. These findings underscore the importance of addressing shared risk factors, such as poor hygiene habits and limited access to care, to improve oral and general health outcomes in individuals with obesity.

Our study revealed that abdominal obesity was associated with poorer oral health behaviors, including reduced tooth brushing frequency and lower odds of combined brushing and flossing. These results suggest a substantial gap in oral hygiene practices among individuals with abdominal obesity. However, no association was observed between abdominal obesity and the timing of the last dental visit, even after adjusting for confounders, contrasting with studies such as Östberg et al. [9], which found a significant association between waist circumference (WC) and irregular dental visits (OR: 2.48, 95% CI: 1.06–5.78). Similarly, other research [35] identified reduced dental check-ups in individuals with obesity, suggesting systemic or practical barriers to accessing dental care, which were not evident in our sample. They also emphasized the importance of oral hygiene practices in mitigating obesity-related risks, including abdominal obesity. The observed associations between reduced brushing and flossing behaviors in our study support this connection, highlighting potential areas for targeted health interventions. Additionally, socioeconomic disparities, including lower education levels, reduced physical activity, and lower wealth score index in the abdominal obesity group, may partially explain these behaviors, reflecting a broader interplay of systemic and lifestyle factors.

The findings of our study revealed a lower rate of DMFT in general obesity (BMI ≥ 30). Interestingly, abdominal obesity, was associated with higher rate of DMFT, indicating a potential localized effect of adiposity on oral health in line with Wood N et al. study [37]. The observed associations between obesity, oral health behaviors, and DMFT in the present study may partly be attributed to shared risk factors, such as high sugar consumption and limited physical activity, which are influenced by socioeconomic status (SES).

This evidence suggests that the choice of obesity measurements may affect the results of studies related to oral health. Measures of abdominal obesity, such as WC, provide insights independent of general obesity indicators like BMI. Incorporating these measures into clinical practice can improve the assessment of risk factors for poor oral health behaviors in adults, enabling more targeted preventive interventions. These may include promoting consistent oral hygiene as part of broader health promotion programs that address the interconnected nature of health-related behaviors [12].

The present study has a few limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes the establishment of causal relationships. Consequently, the directions of the observed associations remain unclear. Second, reliance on self-reported data and individuals’ memory for habits, introduces potential biases, particularly toward socially desirable behavior, Thirdly, certain factors such as dietary patterns, stress levels, and environmental influences were not evaluated, highlighting areas for future research. Despite the possibility of bias, prior cross-cultural research has suggested that health-related behaviors exhibit sufficient validity [38]. The advantages of the current study lie in its population-based design and the size of the sample utilized. Moreover, anthropometric measurements, was conducted by proficient personnel at a single center, potentially decreasing bias arising from diverse devices and personnel. The evidence-based recommendation for tooth brushing is twice daily; however, this cut-off could not be applied in our study due to the structure of the questionnaire and the distribution of responses. Instead, we used different brushing frequency categories to ensure meaningful statistical analysis, while this approach may have influenced the interpretation of our findings relative to clinical guidelines. The adjustments for key confounding variables, such as SES, lifestyle factors, and substance use, further strengthen the validity of these results. However, further longitudinal studies are necessary to disentangle the causal pathways and explore the biological mechanisms underlying these associations.

In light of the increasing elderly population in Iran, health policymakers should prioritize physical activity and mobility for older adults and ensure suitable conditions for their exercise. Obesity alone cannot exclusively predict oral health; various other factors, likely socio-economic, may have a more significant influence.

Conclusion

The present study highlights the distinct roles of general and abdominal obesity in influencing oral health behaviors. While general obesity (BMI ≥ 30) was not significantly associated with individual behaviors like tooth brushing frequency or dental visits, it was linked to poorer combined brushing and flossing habits. Abdominal obesity was associated with lower tooth brushing frequency, poorer combined brushing and flossing habits, and higher DMFT rate, emphasizing its clinical relevance. These findings underscore the importance of incorporating measures like BMI and WC in risk assessments and targeting shared risk factors, such as low education and reduced physical activity, to improve oral and general health outcomes. Future research should focus on causal pathways to support integrated strategies for improving both oral and general health outcomes.
