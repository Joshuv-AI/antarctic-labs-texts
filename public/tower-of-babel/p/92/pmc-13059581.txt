Physiological and transcriptomic analyses for assessing the effects of uniconazole on female ear development in maize (Zea Mays L.)
Authors: Lei Xu, Huiquan Wang, Xiangchuan Wang, JianJian Zhang, Vincent JEI, Daoquan Cheng, Yue Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe maize female ear is a key reproductive organ whose morphology directly determines yield. Uniconazole, a gibberellin (GA) biosynthesis inhibitor, is used to regulate ear development and improve yield. However, its mode of action on ear morphogenesis remains incompletely defined.ResultsWe foliar-applied uniconazole (25 mg L–1) at the 12-leaf stage and evaluated ear development using morphology, physiology, and transcriptomics. Uniconazole shortened ear length while significantly increasing ear diameter, cob cross-sectional area, and cob cell-wall thickness. The changes of female ear morphology were accompanied by alterations in hormone (IAA, GA, ABA, ZR) levels and carbohydrate (cellulose, hemicellulose, lignin) profiles. Transcriptome analysis revealed a widespread transcriptional reprogramming. This reprogramming was characterized by the down-regulation of putative growth-restricting receptor kinases (e.g., LRR, PERK) and the concerted up-regulation of master transcriptional regulators (NAC, MYB) of secondary cell-wall biosynthesis. In hormone signaling, the down-regulation of auxin-responsive genes and the up-regulation of DELLA repressors indicated the suppression of both IAA and GA signaling pathways. Concurrently, the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway was strongly activated, aligning with enhanced lignin deposition.ConclusionsUniconazole reshapes maize female ears into a shorter length and thicker diameter morphology. This is achieved through a coordinated molecular mechanism involving hormone rebalancing, repression of the GA-DELLA signaling pathway, and activation of the NAC/MYB-mediated secondary cell wall biosynthesis pathway. Our findings provide a mechanistic basis for the targeted use of uniconazole in maize cultivation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08218-1.Keywords: Uniconazole, Maize, Female ear, Hormone, Carbohydrate, Transcriptome, Molecular mechanism

Background

Maize is a globally important staple, feed, and industrial crop [1, 2]. Morphological traits of female ear (i.e., ear length, ear diameter, row number per ear, grain number per row, grain weight) correlate strongly with maize yield [3, 4]. The critical window spans from heading stage to two weeks after silking, when ear growth and differentiation are most sensitive [5]. Before silking, ear length, diameter, and dry matter increase slowly with limited morphological change [6–8]. From silking to two weeks after silking, ear length and diameter increase most rapidly [5]. The maize cob, a complex tubular structure, is curial for source-sink connectivity, channeling above-ground photosynthates (e.g., carbohydrates) to developing kernels [9]. The cob also serves as a temporary storage organ, to regulate nutrients absorption and transport [10]. At silking, the cob constitutes more than half of ear tissue, as its development shapes ear morphogenesis and dominates ear-level transcriptomic signatures [11, 12]. This morphogenetic window is accompanied by pronounced physiological and molecular shifts in the female ear [5, 12].

Plant growth is governed by both external and internal genetic factors and modulated by single and interacting hormone pathways [13]. Indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) regulates cell elongation, vascular differentiation, root architecture, and ear development [14–18]. Gibberellins (GA), synthesized mainly in young tissues such as root tip, promote cell elongation and vertical growth [19, 20]. GA perception triggers DELLA degradation, releasing transcription factors that activate GA-responsive genes [21]. Cytokinins (CTK) including trans-Zeatin (ZT), trans-Zeatin-riboside (ZR), Kinetin (KT) and 6-Benzylaminopurine (6-BA) in plants mainly promote cell division and cell expansion, plant differentiation to delay senescence [17, 22, 23]. Abscisic acid (ABA) often antagonizes growth-promoting hormones [24]. Recent work revises this view, showing that ABA can also promote several growth and developmental processes in intact plants [25].

Maize cobs are rich in structural carbohydrates with approximately 45% cellulose, 35% hemicellulose and 15% lignin [26]. Cellulose, the primary load-bearing component of plant cell walls, underpins both primary and secondary walls [27, 28]. Hemicellulose, the second most abundant polysaccharide, are major constituents of primary walls and differ structurally from cellulose [29, 30]. They cross-link with cellulose and lignin to strengthen the wall. Lignin, a complex aromatic polymer, is a major component of plant secondary cell wall [31]. It confers rigidity and hydrophobicity, supports xylem transport, and maintains upright growth [32]. Lignin derives from the phenylpropanoid pathway with phenylalanine as the precursor [33].

Uniconazole, a GA-biosynthesis inhibitor, reshapes plant architecture by reducing elongation and promoting radial growth via hormone rebalancing [34]. Reported effects include increased biomass, photosynthetic efficiency, structural carbohydrates (cellulose, hemicellulose, lignin), non-structural carbohydrates (starch, sucrose, glucose), and altered hormone levels [35–40]. However, most studies focus on leaves and stems of maize or other crops. The female ear, as a determinant reproductive organ with unique developmental patterns and sink strength, remains largely unexplored at the molecular level in response to uniconazole. A critical knowledge gap exists regarding how uniconazole orchestrates the morphological and physiological changes specifically in the maize ear.Transcriptome has been widely used to explore how uniconazole regulating plant growth. For example, in hemp, uniconazole altered genes in hormone signaling, starch/sucrose metabolism, and nitrogen metabolism [41]. In soybean roots, uniconazole and GA modulated genes in hormone signaling, phenylpropanoid, and cell-wall biosynthesis pathways [42, 43]. Consequently, the precise molecular mechanisms by which uniconazole modulates female ear architecture, and whether these mechanisms are distinct from those in vegetative tissues, are still elusive.

To bridge this gap, the present study was designed with the following specific objectives: (1) to characterize the morphological and physiological alterations induced by uniconazole in the maize female ear during the silking period; (2) to decipher the associated shifts in endogenous hormones and structural carbohydrates; and (3) to elucidate the underlying transcriptomic reprogramming, with a focus on hormone signaling and cell wall biosynthesis pathways. We hypothesize that uniconazole reshapes the ear from longitudinal elongation to radial growth by rebalancing hormone homeostasis and activating a specific transcriptional network governing secondary wall development. To test this, we integrated phenotypic, physiological, and transcriptomic analyses following foliar application of uniconazole at the 12-leaf stage.

Results

Effect of uniconazole on ear morphology and cob microstructure

The maize female ear grows rapidly around silking. Uniconazole treatment induced a clear shift in ear development pattern from longitudinal elongation to radial expansion. While ear length showed a consistent yet moderate reduction (by 5.61% at R2 and 6.14% at R3, respectively; Fig. 1E), a far more pronounced increase in ear diameter was observed (by 22.26%, 17.90%, and 13.49% at R1, R2, and R3, respectively; Fig. 1F).

Effect of uniconazole on endogenous hormone levels in the maize female ear

Plants growth development and morphogenesis are coordinated by multiple hormones. Uniconazole rebalanced ear hormone profiles during the critical window around silking (Fig. 3). Ear IAA increased from VT to R2 and declined from R2 to R3. Relative to the control, uniconazole decreased IAA by 48.17%, 30.06%, and 34.69% at VT, R2, and R3, respectively (Fig. 3A). GA followed a similar temporal pattern to IAA. Uniconazole reduced GA by 9.83–41.69% from VT to R3 (Fig. 3B). ABA decreased progressively from VT to R3. Uniconazole increased ABA by 4.97% at VT and 11.22% at R1 versus control (Fig. 3C). ZR increased steadily from VT to R3. Across all stages, uniconazole elevated ZR by 8.12%−117.50% (Fig. 3D). Overall, uniconazole decreased auxin and gibberellin, elevated cytokinin (ZR) throughout development, and transiently increased ABA early, consistent with reduced elongation and enhanced radial growt.

Effect of uniconazole on structural carbohydrate in the maize female ear

The cob develops rapidly around silking and is enriched in structural carbohydrates. Uniconazole altered cob structural-carbohydrate levels from VT to R3 (Fig. 4). Cob cellulose and hemicellulose increased from VT to R2 and declined from R2 to R3 stages (Fig. 4). Relative to the control, uniconazole increased cellulose by 15.04%, 7.92%, and 7.83% at VT, R1, and R2, respectively (Fig. 4A). Hemicellulose increased by 10.08% at R1 under uniconazole (Fig. 4B). Lignin increased progressively from VT to R3. Uniconazole increased lignin at all stages (Fig. 4C). Together, uniconazole elevated cob cellulose/hemicellulose and sustained lignin accumulation, aligning with thicker cell walls and greater ear diameter.

Correlation between structural carbohydrate accumulation and cob morphology

To substantiate the functional link between the biochemical changes and the observed morphological alterations, we performed Pearson correlation analysis between the levels of structural carbohydrates and key ear microstructure parameters. At R1 stage, cell wall thickness showed highly significant positive correlations with the content of structural carbohydrates (cellulose: r = 0.742; hemicellulose: r = 0.736; lignin: r = 0.772). This indicates that the thickening of the cell wall is closely coordinated with the concurrent accumulation of its primary chemical constituents. More strikingly, cob cross-sectional area was strongly and significantly correlated with cellulose and hemicellulose content at both the VT and R1 stages (VT: cellulose r = 0.968, p < 0.01; R1: cellulose r = 0.930, p < 0.01). Lignin content also showed a significant positive correlation with cob area, particularly at VT (r = 0.906, p < 0.05). These robust correlations provide quantitative evidence that the radial expansion of the cob is strongly coupled with the biosynthesis and deposition of cellulose, hemicellulose and lignin in the cell wall (Table 1).

RNA-seq quality metrics and quality and differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

To probe the molecular mechanism of uniconazole-mediated ear formation, samples were taken at VT stage for transcriptome sequencing. RNA-seq yielded 41.66 Gb of clean bases across libraries (6.59–7.01 Gb per library). Quality metrics were high (Q20 ≥ 97.91%, Q30 ≥ 93.72%), and > 86.71% of reads mapped to the maize reference genome (Table S1). These metrics indicate data suitable for downstream analyses. There were 1032 DEGs were up-regulated and 167 DEGs were down-regulated from the uniconazole and control groups through using the parameters of log2 FC ≥ 1 and FDR < 0.05.

Principal component analysis reveals profound transcriptional reprogramming

To visualize the overall effect of uniconazole on the ear transcriptome, we performed principal component analysis (PCA) on the normalized expression data. The first principal component (PC1), which explained 42.0% of the total variance, clearly separated all uniconazole-treated samples from the control samples (Fig. S1). The second component (PC2), explaining 20.8% of the variance, primarily captured variance among biological replicates within the same treatment group, demonstrating good experimental reproducibility. The stark segregation along PC1 indicates that uniconazole induced a consistent and genome-wide shift in gene expression.

GO and KEGG enrichment of DEGs under uniconazole

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed functional categories pivotal to the observed phenotypic alteration. The significant enrichment of terms such as “cell wall organization” (GO:0071555) and “cellulose synthase/hexosyltransferase activity” (GO:0016759) directly correlates with the measured increase in cellulose/hemicellulose and the thickening of cob cell walls. Similarly, the enrichment of “carbohydrate metabolic process” (GO:0005975) aligns with the broader reprogramming of carbon allocation towards structural components (Fig. 5A), KEGG pathway analysis further pinpointed the specific metabolic and signaling routes modulated by uniconazole (Fig. 5B). The most significant enrichment was in the “phenylpropanoid biosynthesis” pathway, which provides a direct transcriptional explanation for the substantial accumulation of lignin quantified in treated cobs. Crucially, the enrichment of the “plant hormone signal transduction” pathway resonates with our physiological measurements (Table S2). This enrichment was driven by the differential expression of key signaling components, including the down-regulation of auxin-responsive genes/transporters (e.g., ARFs) and the up-regulation of GA signaling repressors (e.g., DELLAs like RGL2 and GAI1).

DEGs involved in plant hormone signal transduction

Plant hormones coordinate growth and development. We identified DEGs in auxin (IAA) and gibberellin (GA) signaling pathways after uniconazole treatment. Auxin-related DEGs comprised two auxin-responsive genes, two auxin response factors (ARFs), and two auxin transporters which were all down-regulated (Fig. 6A). DEGs involved in phenylpropanoid and flavonoid biosynthesis

Metabolic synthesis of phenylpropanoid and flavonoids was key pathway for lignin synthesis in plant. Phenylpropanoid-related DEGs included four phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) genes, two 4-coumarate-CoA ligase (4CL) genes, one β-glucosidase, two cinnamyl alcohol dehydrogenase (CAD) genes, twenty-five peroxidase (POD) genes and caffeic acid 3-O-methyltransferase (COMT) gene; all were up-regulated under uniconazole (Fig. 6B).

Discussion

Female ear architecture correlates with yield [3]. In particular, ear diameter associates positively with rows per ear, kernels per row, and grain yield [44]. Plant growth regulators (PGRs) can remodel ear traits. Prior work shows complementary PGR effects: ethephon (0.56–0.84 kg ha⁻1) reduced ear elongation but increased ear diameter and harvest index [45]; and early uniconazole reduced internode length yet promoted ear size at silking [47]. Consistent with these reports, foliar uniconazole at the 12-leaf stage decreased ear length but increased ear diameter and cob cross-sectional area after silking.

Endogenous hormones coordinate growth and organ morphogenesis. Auxin (IAA) promotes ear development: exogenous IAA increased ear length and dry weight around silking [48], and auxin-pathway activation increased ear length, kernels per row, and yield [49]. Uniconazole can lower endogenous IAA; rising doses decreased IAA and reduced longitudinal growth in rapeseed [50]. Our observation of shorter ears under uniconazole is concordant and is consistent with reduced IAA levels. Gibberellins (GAs) promote cell elongation and longitudinal growth and interact with auxin pathways [51]. Uniconazole inhibits GA biosynthesis, lowering GA levels and limiting elongation; in maize, 25–70 mg L⁻1 uniconazole reduced GA and suppressed longitudinal growth. Likewise, exogenous GA increased plant height and internode length, whereas GA inhibitors (e.g., paclobutrazol) had the opposite effect [53]. In our study, uniconazole reduced GA in ears and decreased ear elongation, aligning with these trends. ABA changes should be understood as part of a coordinated hormonal reconfiguration, in which the substantial reductions in IAA and GA act in concert with the increase in ZR to establish a new hormonal milieu. Uniconazole increased endogenous ABA in rapeseed, soybean, and maize [42, 50, 52], and we likewise observed higher ABA in ears after treatment. CTK promote cell division, cell expansion and plant differentiation [17, 46]. Uniconazole elevates CKs and caused ZT/ZR increased in maize stems with greater lateral growth [54], and CKs rose with thicker stems in rapeseed [50]. Similarly, we observed larger ear diameter and cob cross-sectional area, consistent with higher ZR and enhanced lateral growth of ear tissues.

The auxin signaling pathway is governed by a regulatory module involving Aux/IAA repressors and downstream auxin-responsive genes, including Auxin Response Factors (ARFs) [55, 56]. In our data, uniconazole down-regulated auxin-responsive genes, particularly key ARFs which act as central transcriptional activators of auxin-dependent gene networks. This specific transcriptional change suggests a dampening of auxin signaling output in the ears. This attenuated transcriptional response aligns with, and may partially explain, the measured decrease in IAA levels and the associated reduction in longitudinal elongation. DELLA proteins are key repressors of GA signaling [57, 58]. In our study, the up-regulation of DELLA genes (RGL2, GAI1), coupled with the measured decrease in GA levels, supports a model of attenuated GA signaling in uniconazole-treated ears. While direct evidence for DELLA protein accumulation is not provided here, this coordinated change at the transcriptional and hormonal level is consistent with the observed inhibition of ear elongation, mirroring effects reported in other systems [41, 59].

At silking stage, more than half of ear tissue is cob [11]. Structural carbohydrate (cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin) dominate cob composition and strongly influence ear architecture [26, 60]. PGRs can alter structural-carbohydrate accumulation. For example, uniconazole increased stem lignin and internode thickness/plumpness in maize [52]. EDAH + DA-6 likewise increased hemicellulose (+ 6.93%), cellulose (+ 3.87%), and lignin (+ 30.21%) in basal stalks [61]. In our study, uniconazole increased ear diameter, cob cross-sectional area, and cob cell-wall thickness. These changes are consistent with elevated cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin in the cob. Phenylpropanoid metabolism underpins lignin biosynthesis, with phenylalanine as the precursor [62]. Uniconazole up-regulated lignin-pathway genes (PAL, 4CL, CAD, POD, HCT, COMT), consistent with increased lignin accumulation. Flavonoids, the secondary metabolites, abundant in vascular tissues, modulate plant physiology, ecology, growth, and development [43, 63, 64]. The flavonoid pathway branches from phenylpropanoid biosynthetic pathway, and 4-coumaroyl-CoA and produced upstream serves as the substrate [62]. In soybean roots, exogenous GA down-regulated flavonoid-pathway genes [43]. conversely, uniconazole (GA inhibitor) in our study up-regulated key flavonoid genes, aligning with reduced GA signaling.

PGRs modulate cell growth and cell wall deposition, thereby reshaping plant morphology. For example, uniconazole reduced stalk length but increased stalk diameter in rice [65]. These changes were associated with shorter cells, denser packing, and thicker cell walls. Similarly, in maize ears uniconazole increased cob cell-wall thickness, ear diameter, and cob cross-sectional area; histology indicated denser cells around silking. Transcriptomics and GO analysis identified LRR-RLK and PERK genes among the DEGs, both families implicated in cell-wall/plasma-membrane signaling and meristem regulation [66, 67]. In Arabidopsis, CLAVATA (CLV) LRR receptor kinases maintain stem-cell number in the shoot apical meristem [68, 69]. PERKs were first described in Brassica napus; they are induced in callus and expressed across vegetative and reproductive tissues [70, 71]. In our study, uniconazole down-regulated LRR-RLK and PERK transcripts in ears. Given their reported roles as negative regulators of cell expansion, their down-regulation may contribute to the observed shift towards radial growth and denser cell packing, rather than directly increasing cell division rates. NAC transcription factors orchestrate multiple processes, notably secondary cell-wall formation and stress responses. MYB TFs also regulate secondary cell wall synthesis like MYB52/54/69/103 impact lignin, xylan, and cellulose biosynthesis [72], and MYB58/63/85 activate lignin pathways [72, 73]. MYB26 controls secondary cell wall deposition in anthers [74]. All of them are positive regulatory factors of cell wall thickening in fiber cell. The NAC–MYB transcriptional network underlying SCW formation has been established in Arabidopsis thaliana [75]. Furthermore, uniconazole treatment triggered the coordinated up-regulation of a suite of NAC and MYB transcription factors. Given that members of these families are known to form a core transcriptional network activating SCW biosynthesis [72, 75], their induction provides a plausible molecular explanation for the observed enhancement in cell wall thickening and lignin accumulation in the cob.

Based on our integrated physiological and transcriptomic data, we propose a cohesive regulatory model for uniconazole action on the maize female ear (Fig. 8). The primary effect appears to be the inhibition of GA biosynthesis, which initiates a signaling cascade. The stabilization of DELLA repressors and the concomitant down-regulation of auxin signaling likely create a permissive condition for the activation of NAC and MYB transcription factors. These factors, in turn, function as master switches, coordinately up-regulating the biosynthetic pathways for lignin and polysaccharides, thereby drastically reinforcing the secondary cell wall. Concurrently, the elevated cytokinin (ZR) may promote cellular division and expansion in the radial direction. Together, these molecular and metabolic shifts coherently explain the observed morphological transition from an elongated to a shorter, thicker ear architecture with enhanced mechanical strength.

A proposed regulatory model for uniconazole-mediated remodeling of maize female ear

Conclusions

Here, we integrated physiology and transcriptomics to elucidate how uniconazole reshapes maize ear development. KEGG enrichment indicated modulation of hormone signaling, phenylpropanoid, and flavonoid pathways, concordant with measured shifts in hormones and structural carbohydrates. Uniconazole lowered IAA and GA, consistent with reduced ear elongation, and increased ZR, consistent with enhanced radial growth and larger ear diameter. Elevated cellulose, hemicellulose and lignin aligned with thicker cob cell walls and a larger cob cross-section. GO enrichment analysis further suggested that the morphological changes induced by uniconazole were associated with transcriptional reprogramming, including the down-regulation of genes like LRR and PERK (potential growth restrictors) and the up-regulation of NAC and MYB transcription factors (key regulators of secondary cell wall biosynthesis). Our results enhance the understanding of the regulation of maize female ear development by regulators. These findings provide a mechanistic basis for targeted uniconazole use to manage ear formation in maize.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and treatments

The maize hybrid Nendan 18 (Heilongjiang Academy of Agricultural Sciences) was used. The crop cycle was around 125 days from emergence to maturity, the effective accumulated temperature was around 2600–2700 °C, and plants produced around 17 visible leaves. The plant growth regulator was uniconazole (growth retardant and GA-biosynthesis inhibitor), provided by the Chemical Control Laboratory, Heilongjiang Bayi Agricultural University. Uniconazole is a low-toxicity growth retardant widely used in crop production.

Field experiments were conducted at the Daqing Branch experimental farm, Heilongjiang Academy of Agricultural Sciences. Maize was sown in early May at 75,000 plants per hactar and harvested in late September. Field management, including irrigation, fertilization, and weed control, was uniformly applied across all treatment and control plots and followed local conventional practice. The site soil was typical black soil, slightly alkaline. Soil physicochemical properties were measured pre-sowing (Table 4). The region has a temperate continental monsoon climate; seasonal temperature, rainfall, and sunshine hours were recorded (Table 5). Sprays were applied on calm, sunny afternoons with an air temperature between 20–25 °C and relative humidity above 60% at the 12-leaf stage with a carrier volume of 225 L ha⁻1. Each plot was around 62 m2 (8 rows × 12 m, 0.65 m row spacing). Plots were arranged in a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with three replicates. Sampling began around silking, between 08:00–10:00 a.m. each day.

Observation on the morphology and microstructure of female ear

The samples were taken around silking stage, and the female ears of maize were collected at the heading stage (VT), silking stage (R1), one week after silking stage (R2) and two weeks after silking stage (R3). During sampling, 10 plants per plot were measured for ear length, ear diameter, and cob diameter using a ruler and vernier caliper.

At R1, a 0.5–1.0 cm mid-ear segment was fixed in formaldehyde glacialacetic acid (FAA) for 24 h dehydrated through ethanol/xylene, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 µm. Sections were stained with toluidine blue and mounted with neutral balsam [76]. Thereafter, the sections were observed under the optical microscope (Nikon Eclipse E100) and photographed with the imaging system (Nikon DS-U3). Cob cross-sectional area and cell-wall thickness were quantified using 3DHISTECH software (Budapest, Hungary).

Hormone determination

At VT, R1, R2, and R3, 10 representative ears per plot were pooled as one biological replicate. Fresh sample (0.5 g) was homogenized in 2 mL of chilled 80% methanol at a solvent-to-tissue ratio of 4:1 (v/w). To monitor extraction efficiency, known amounts of internal standards ([D₆]-ABA) were added prior to homogenization. After centrifugation, the supernatant was evaporated under nitrogen and reconstituted to 3 mL. IAA, GA, ZR, and ABA were quantified by ELISA following the manufacturer’s instructions [77]. The ELISA kit was provided by the Chemical Control Center of China Agricultural University. According to the manufacturer’s specifications, the cross-reactivity of each kit with other major plant hormones was less than 0.1%, and the limits of detection (LOD) were 0.01 ng/mL for IAA, 0.05 ng/mL for GA, and similarly sensitive for ABA and ZR. The average recovery rates for all hormones, determined by standard addition experiments, ranged from 85 to 105%.

Structural carbohydrate determination

At VT, R1, R2, and R3, 10 ears per plot were pooled per biological replicate. The fresh samples were frozen in liquid nitrogen and then stored in the –80 ℃ refrigerator. After collection, structural carbohydrates were quantified. Cellulose and hemicellulose were measured following Hussain et al. [78]. Lignin was determined according to Hussain et al. [78] and Ahmad et al. [52].

RNA-seq analysis

RNA was isolated from ears at VT after foliar uniconazole or water (control) treatment. Three biological replicates per treatment were analyzed. The 0.5 g sample was fully ground in liquid nitrogen, and total RNA extracted (Plant RNA Purification Kit, Tiangen, Beijing, China). RNA purification and cDNA library preparation were described in previous research of our group [41]. Sequencing analysis was performed using a HiSeq 4,000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) for paired end with a read length of 150 bp. Data are available at NCBI under PRJNA1019878.

Fastp was used to filter the original data [79]. The raw reads were filtered by removing adapter sequences, the nitrogen content in any sequencing reads exceeded 10% of the base number and the low-quality (Q ≤ 20) bases contained in reads exceeded 50% of the bases number. Additionally, reads shorter than 50 bp after trimming were removed. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to assess the overall transcriptomic variation and sample heterogeneity. The analysis was conducted in R on the variance-stabilized transformed count data. Prior to PCA, genes with low expression (FPKM < 1 in more than 50% of the samples) were filtered out to reduce noise. The clean reads were aligned to the maize Mo17 reference genome (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/genome/GCA_003185045.1/) using HISAT2 software with default parameters. Htseq-count software was used to calculate the gene alignment and the fragments per kilobase of exon per million mapped (FPKM) was used to obtain the relative expression levels [80]. DESeq2 package was used for the differential expression analysis between uniconazole and control treatment [81], and the false discovery rate (FDR) was used to determine the threshold P-value in multiple tests [82]. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were characterized by a threshold log2 fold change (log2 FC) ≥ 1 and FDR < 0.05 [42]. DEGs were further annotated by gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment [83]. The adjusted P-value of significantly substantiated GO terms was less than 0.05. The statistical enrichment of DEGs was performed using the KEGG Orthology Based Annotation System (KOBAS) software in the Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways [84–87].

Quantitative real-time RT-PCR

To validate the RNA-seq data, 10 DEGs were assayed by Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT–PCR). One μg purified RNA was reverse-transcribed using FastKing RT Kit (Tiangen BioTek, Beijing, China). Actin gene was selected as the reference gene. Gene-specific primers were designed in Primer Premier 5 (Premier Biosoft, CA, USA). The PCR conditions were as follows: 95 ℃ for 30 s followed by 40 cycles of 95 ℃ for 15 s and 60 ℃ for 30 s. Target genes’ relative expression levels were analyzed by the 2−∆∆Ct method [88]. Three biological replicates and three technical replicates were used for all qRT-PCR assays.

Data analysis

Data were processed in Microsoft Excel 2010, and figures were prepared in Origin 2020 (OriginLab, USA). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 20.0 (IBM, USA). For morphological and physiological data, treatment effects were analyzed using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with the plot as the experimental unit. When a significant F-test (p < 0.05) was found, Duncan’s multiple range test was used for pairwise comparisons between the control and uniconazole treatment at each sampling stage. For transcriptomic data, differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 package with the False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction. For all analyses, differences were considered significant at p < 0.05 and highly significant at p < 0.01, denoted by single (*) and double asterisks (**), respectively.
