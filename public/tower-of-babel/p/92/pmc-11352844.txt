The embryonic zebrafish brain is exclusively colonized by pu.1-dependent and lymphatic-independent population of microglia
Authors: Tao Yu, Jiahao Chen, Yuexin Wang, Jin Xu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Microglia, the crucial immune cells inhabiting the central nervous system (CNS), perform a range of vital functions, encompassing immune defense and neuronal regulation. Microglia subsets with diverse functions and distinct developmental regulations have been identified recently. It is generally accepted that all microglia originate from hematopoiesis and depend on the myeloid transcription factor PU.1. However, a recent study reported the existence of mrc1+ microglia in zebrafish embryos, which are seemingly independent of Pu.1 and reliant on lymphatic vessels, sparking great interest in the possibility of lymphatic-originated microglia. To address this, we took advantage of a pu.1 knock-in zebrafish allele for a detailed investigation. Our results conclusively showed that almost all zebrafish embryonic microglia (~95% on average) express pu.1. Further, lineage tracing and mutant analysis revealed that these microglia neither emerged from nor depended on lymphatic vessels. In essence, our study refutes the presence of pu.1-independent but lymphatic-dependent microglia.

INTRODUCTION

Microglia are resident macrophages in the central nervous system (CNS) that play diverse functions in the regulation of CNS homeostasis and pathogenesis (1, 2). As the major type of immune cells in the brain, microglia actively surveil the environment by extending and retracting their process (3, 4). Upon detection of pathogens, danger signals, or other insults, microglia rapidly undergo phenotypical and morphological transitions from the surveillant state to the activated state. Depending on the type and extent of stimulus and context of the environment, activated microglia might exert different functions, including facilitating phagocytosis and elimination of invaded pathogen or cellular debris, helping resolve detrimental neuronal inflammation, and triggering a response from peripheral immune cells or even adaptive immune system (5). In addition to their major roles as scavengers, recent studies have revealed that microglia also actively participate in the regulation of synaptic pruning, modulation of neuronal activity, and even the behaviors of other glial cells (1, 6, 7). Moreover, the importance of microglial functional diversification is further exemplified by their involvement in the development and progression of neurodegenerative diseases, such as Alzheimer’s disease, as activation of microglia is considered to be a double-edged sword that exerts both protective and detrimental effects on the CNS (8, 9). Yet, how microglia balance these flexible properties and the mechanisms governing their functional diversification remain to be further elucidated.

The heterogeneity of microglia has long been proposed, in which different microglia subsets carry out distinct functions. It is now recognized that microglia heterogeneity arises from the orchestration of many factors that are both intrinsic and extrinsic (10). First, the CNS network comprises multiple cell types such as neurons, oligodendrocytes, astrocytes, and microglia, which manifest varied proportions and distinct subtypes across different CNS regions. As microglia consistently surveil their surrounding environment, their identities are heavily shaped by the sophisticated local microenvironment (11, 12). For example, the expression of lysosome protein CD68 in microglia was reported to show regional differences across CNS in rodent (13, 14) and microglia in white matter were found to exhibit higher expression activating markers such as ubiquitin-specific protease 18 than cortical microglia (15). Second, microglia in CNS might be ontogenically heterogeneous under certain conditions. In mice, although adult microglia are thought to arise from a single origin, i.e., the erythromyeloid progenitors generated in the extraembryonic yolk sac and self-renew throughout the lifespan (16–18), monocyte-derived peripheral macrophages or border-associated macrophages are capable of infiltrating the brain parenchyma after irradiation or in the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative diseases (2, 19). Different from mice, two differentially originated microglia populations, namely, the rostral blood island (RBI)–derived embryonic microglia and aorta-gonad-mesonephros (AGM)–derived adult microglia, exist in zebrafish (20). During development, RBI and AGM microglia show different kinetics and proliferation capabilities. They express different levels of csf1ra (21). Furthermore, in the adult zebrafish brain, another subset of microglia, the regulatory or ramified microglia, have also been identified. Although regulatory microglia express microglia markers such as mpeg1 and lcp1, they differ from the classic phagocytotic or amoeboid microglia in terms of morphology, distribution pattern, origin, developmental regulation, and phagocytic capabilities (22). Further single-cell RNA sequencing results revealed that these regulatory microglia are dendritic cells in nature (23). Therefore, the classification of microglia subtypes with more advanced technology or tools will not only facilitate our understanding of the heterogeneity and versatile functions of microglia but also help revisit previous results from previously unappreciated angles.

A recent report suggested that the embryonic zebrafish brain is also seeded by a population of mrc1a+ microglia, which show no reliance on the myeloid master regulator Pu.1 but appear to depend on lymphangiogenesis. Although these findings are interesting and raise a completely different scenario of microglia generation (24), they are based on evidence with certain limitations and thus need to be further validated.

In the present study, we used a pu.1 knock-in allele in combination with genetic evidence to demonstrate that embryonic microglia in zebrafish are exclusively derived from microglia precursors depending on the myeloid regulator Pu.1.

RESULTS

All the mrc1a+4C4+ microglia are pu.1+ cells

A substantial microglia population, referred to as lymphatic-dependent mrc1a+pu.1− microglia, was recently proposed in embryonic zebrafish (24). However, the Tg(pu.1:Gal4) and Tg(pu1:eos) lines used in previous study may not faithfully recapitulate the endogenous pu.1 expression pattern as the transgenes are controlled by a 4-kb pu.1 promoter (24, 25). To enhance the accuracy of the labeling of pu.1-expressing cells, we used the pu.1 knock-in allele pu.1KI-DsRed, which faithfully mimics the native pu.1 expression pattern (fig. S1) (26). To investigate whether mrc1a+pu.1− microglia existed in the developing zebrafish brain, we crossed pu.1KI-DsRed with Tg(mrc1a:egfp) reporter line (27) and collected larvae at 5, 7, and 15 days postfertilization (dpf) for green fluorescent protein (GFP), DsRed, and 4C4 immunostaining. Notably, our analysis showed that, across different stages, almost all of the 4C4+ microglia are virtually pu.1+ (~95.7, 97.4, and 94.9% in total 4C4+ microglia at 5, 7, and 15 dpf, respectively) (Fig. 1, A to C). In contrast, the labeling efficiency of 4C4+ microglia by mrc1a expression was ~77.9, 90.7, and 71.1% for 5, 7, and 15 dpf, respectively (Fig. 1, A, B, and D). All mrc1a+4C4+ microglia were pu.1+ at all observed stages (Fig. 1, A, B, and E). These results strongly suggest that, in zebrafish larvae, all microglia are virtually pu.1+, and no evidence indicates the existence of mrc1a+pu.1− microglia.

Microglia do not originate from mrc1a+pu.1− myeloid precursors

The previous study proposed that mrc1a+ cells in the brain appeared earlier than pu.1+ cells (24). To monitor the dynamics of potential mrc1a+pu.1− microglia precursors, we revisited the early-stage microglial development in pu.1KI-DsRed;Tg(mrc1a:egfp) embryos. At 36 hours postfertilization (hpf), no 4C4+ signals were observed in the head region (fig. S2, A to C), suggesting that no microglia exist at this stage. In contrast, we observed mrc1a+ vascular structure in the head region. This mrc1a+ vascular structure was blood vessels, as evidenced by the presence of globin-DsRedx+ blood cells within the vascular structures (fig. S2E). We also detected nonvascular mrc1a+ cells in the head region. These mrc1a+ cells are all pu.1+ (fig. S2B, white arrow) and constitute a subpopulation of myeloid lineages as they account for 46.4% of all pu.1+ cells (fig. S2D). By 48 hpf, pu.1+, 4C4+, and mrc1a+ cells began to emerge in the brain (Fig. 2, A and B). Notably, 100% of 4C4+ microglia were pu.1+ (Fig. 2C), whereas only ~35.6% of 4C4+ microglia are mrc1a+ (Fig. 2D). The percentage of mrc1a+4C4+ microglia increased to ~56.8% at 3 dpf (Fig. 2, A and D). Similar to later stages, all mrc1a+4C4+ microglia were pu.1+ (Fig. 2, A and E). These findings suggest that pu.1+ microglia precursors undergo stepwise development to express mrc1a+ and 4C4 and evolve into microglia.

Microglia are not derived from lyve1b+ lymphatic vessels

The previous study raised the possibility that lymphatic vessels might serve as a source for microglia development (24). To use appropriate reporter lines for lineage tracing, we first characterized the expression of conventional macrophage/microglia markers mfap4 and mpeg1 in 4C4+ brain microglia. Co-staining of GFP or DsRed and 4C4 antibodies in either Tg(mfap4:eGFP) or Tg(mpeg1:loxP-DsRedx-loxP-eGFP) embryos revealed that all of the 4C4+ microglia in the brain could be efficiently labeled by mfap4 or mpeg1 expression (fig. S3, A and B), suggesting that either reporter could be used as a readout to score microglia development. Thus, we used the Tg(lyve1b:CreER) transgenic fish, which labels lymphatic vessels as well as venous endothelia (28–30), in combination with the macrophage/microglia reporter fish Tg(mpeg1:loxP-DsRedx-loxP-eGFP) for lineage tracing (31). To activate CreER, the embryos were incubated with 4-hydroxytamoxifen (4-OHT) from 22 hpf to 5 dpf (Fig. 3A). Larvae at 5 dpf were collected to score microglia labeling. The results revealed that no GFP-labeled microglia were present in the brain of Tg(lyve1b:CreER;mpeg1:loxP-DsRedx-loxP-eGFP) larvae (Fig. 3B). The absence of labeling was not due to any limitation in Tg(lyve1b:CreER) in mediating recombination at the loxP sites, as efficient labeling of brain vessels was observed when similar tracing experiments were conducted in Tg(lyve1b:CreER;kdrl:loxP-DsRedx-loxP-eGFP) embryos/larvae (Fig. 3, A and C). To label microglia specifically, we took advantage of Tg(mfap4:CreER), which expresses CreER under macrophages/microglia specific mfap4 promoter (21, 32). When incubated with 4-OHT with a similar pipeline, the Tg(mfap4:CreER;mpeg1:loxP-DsRedx-loxP-GFP) transgenic embryos showed robust labeling of brain microglia (Fig. 3, A and B). Notably, the labeling efficiency in some embryos reaches more than 90% (average, 74.7%) (fig. S4), again making the existence of non–hematopoiesis-originated microglia to be unlikely. As a control, Tg(mfap4:CreER;kdrl:loxP-DsRedx-loxP-GFP) failed to label vessels. Together, these results suggest that microglia do not originate from lymphatic vessels (Fig. 3C).

Microglia development does not rely on lymphatic vessels but requires pu.1 function

While lymphatic vessels are not the source of microglia, they may still play pivotal roles in microglia development. Flt4 encodes the vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 3, which is crucial for lymphatic vessel development (33, 34). To explore the potential functions of lymphatic vessels in microglia development, we used the flt4ihb439 mutants. In our investigation, we first examined the development of lymphatic vessels in Tg(mrc1a:egfp;kdrl:mCherry), in which lymphatic vessels in the head region of 5 dpf embryos were clearly marked by mrc1a:GFPhigh signals (fig. S5A, white arrowheads), while blood vessels (kdrl:mCherry+) exhibited mrc1a:GFPlow signals (fig. S5A, yellow arrowheads). GFPhighmCherry− lymphatic vessels and GFPlowmCherry+ blood vessels developed parallelly in optic tectum (fig. S5A and movie S1). We found that GFPhigh signals associated with lymphatic vessels were entirely absent in flt4ihb439 mutants, whereas GFPlow signals from blood vessels remained intact (fig. S5B, left), suggesting that the development of lymphatic vessels was defective in flt4ihb439 mutants. We then examined microglia by 4C4 antibody staining in siblings and flt4ihb439 mutants under Tg(mrc1a:egfp) background. Quantification of 4C4+ or mrc1a+4C4+ microglia revealed no discernible difference between the siblings and flt4ihb439 mutants (Fig. 4, A and B). To further strengthen our findings, we used the ccbe1sa1069 mutant (28). Ccbe1 is required for the processing and maturation of Vegfc; thus, its mutant displays defective brain lymphangiogenesis but has normal brain vasculature (28). Our results revealed that mrc1a:GFPhigh lymphatic vessels were completely absent, whereas mrc1a:GFPlow blood vessels remained intact (fig. S5B, middle). Again, quantification of 4C4+ or mrc1a+4C4+ microglia between siblings and ccbe1sa1069 mutants revealed no difference (Fig. 4, C and D). Collectively, these data strongly suggest that lymphatic vessels are dispensable for microglia development.

Previous studies have established the essential role of Pu.1 in microglia development (35). However, the proposed mrc1a+pu.1− microglia suggest the existence of a pu.1-independent microglial population (24). To investigate this possibility, we examined microglia development through mrc1a:GFP and 4C4 antibody co-staining in pu.1∆839 mutants, which carry an amorphic pu.1 allele (35). In contrast to their siblings, where ~20 4C4+ microglia were present, few 4C4+ or mrc1a+4C4+ microglia were observed in pu.1∆839 mutants at 5 dpf (Fig. 4, E and F). Lymphatic vessels in pu.1∆839 mutants remained normal, as evidenced by the presence of mrc1a:GFPhigh signals (fig. S5B, right). We also occasionally observed 4C4+ cells in 5-dpf pu.1Δ839-null mutants (Fig. 4F and fig. S6A). Given that zebrafish harbors a paralogue of pu.1, namely, the spi-b gene that cooperates with pu.1 in the regulation of late-stage AGM-derived microglia development (35), we speculate that the occasional appearance of 4C4+ microglia in pu.1Δ839-null mutants belongs to these pu.1- and spi-b–dependent microglia. When Pu.1 and Spi-b are both defective, 4C4+ microglia are completely absent in pu.1Δ839/Δ839;spi-bΔ232/Δ232 double mutants (fig. S6, B and C). Collectively, our results suggest that all the microglia in the developing zebrafish brain are virtually Pu.1 dependent. This conclusion aligns with our findings that microglia are primarily pu.1+ (Figs. 1 and 2) and negates the existence of pu.1-independent mrc1a+pu.1− microglia.

DISCUSSION

Microglial diversity has sparked interest, leading to the identification of microglia with distinct origins and functions. Amid this complexity, the conventional perspective posits that all microglia originate from myeloid cells and rely on the pivotal transcription factor PU.1. However, a recent study by Green et al. has presented a completely different scenario of microglia development. Their research suggests the presence of a substantial population of mrc1a+pu.1− microglia, which, crucially, are considered to be Pu.1 independent and lymphatic dependent, hinting at a lymphatic origin of microglia (24). It is worth noting that these conclusions, while intriguing, are based on evidence that carries certain limitations.

In the previous study, Tg(pu.1:Gal4) and Tg(pu1:eos) transgenic lines were used to identify pu.1+ microglia. Their results indicated that only ~36.5% of 4C4+ microglia were pu.1+ at 5 dpf. In contrast, roughly 82.99% of 4C4+ microglia were mrc1a+. However, the expression of the transgene was governed by a 4-kb pu.1 promoter, which might have omitted certain regulatory elements and thus failed to fully replicate the endogenous pu.1 expression (25). To address this limitation, we used a pu.1 knock-in allele, pu.1KI-DsRed, in which DsRed was inserted into the pu.1 locus, ensuring a faithful representation of endogenous pu.1 expression. Our pu.1 knock-in transgenic fish consistently demonstrated that almost all of the 4C4+ microglia are virtually pu.1+ at all stages that we examined. For comparison, we recorded that 77.9% of 4C4+ microglia are mrc1a+ at 5 dpf, a percentage consistent with the findings from the previous study. All mrc1a+ microglia were unequivocally pu.1+, but not vice versa. These results underscore the superior microglial labeling achieved with our pu.1 knock-in transgenic line compared to the previous promoter-based pu.1 transgene. They further indicate that the majority, if not all, microglia are fundamentally pu.1+ and the existence of mrc1a+pu.1− microglia is highly improbable.

A notable finding in the previous study revolves around the reliance of mrc1a+ microglia on lymphangiogenesis. This conclusion was substantiated by several pivotal experiments, which are potentially problematic. Interference with lymphatic development, whether through chemical inhibitors or flt4 single guide RNA (sgRNA), led to a reduction in the number of mrc1a+ microglia. In contrast, when microglia were hindered by either pu.1 sgRNA or Csf1r inhibitor, it primarily affected mrc1a−pu.1+ microglia, with limited impact on mrc1a+pu.1− microglia. These outcomes raised the intriguing prospect that these atypical mrc1a+ microglia could potentially originate from lymphatic vessels, an overlooked source of microglia. However, chemical inhibition can be plagued by pleiotropic effects and unintended consequences, while transient sgRNA blocking may introduce off-target effects and results in incomplete gene disruption. To circumvent these shortcomings, we used genetic tools known for their stability and reproducibility. Initially, we conducted lineage tracing using the lymphatic transgenic fish Tg(lyve1b:CreER). The results clearly demonstrated that microglia do not originate from lymphatic vessels. Subsequently, we leveraged flt4ihb439, ccbe1sa1069, and pu.1∆839 mutants, which provided compelling evidence that disrupting lymphatic vessels did not affect mrc1a+ microglia, whereas pu.1 knockout led to the complete elimination of the microglia population. These findings underscore the critical importance of using reliable genetic tools and serve as a reminder to exercise caution when dealing with chemical inhibition and transient sgRNA blocking in biological research.

While our data does not support the concept of lymphatic-derived or lymphatic-dependent microglia, the potential for vascular structures within the brain to generate microglia remains an intriguing question. The runx1 gene plays a crucial role in endothelial-to-hematopoietic transition (EHT) (36). To date, there have been no reports indicating the expression of runx1 in the blood or lymphatic vessels of CNS. Hence, the existence of microglia derived from CNS blood or lymphatic vessels lacks substantial evidence. Intriguingly, ectopic expression of runx1 in the dorsal aorta has been shown to initiate the EHT process (37). Whether similar ectopic expression could artificially induce microglia from CNS blood or lymphatic vessels remains to be examined. If successful, these microglia could be examined for their functional heterogeneity compared to traditional microglia. During development, the zebrafish brain generates massive apoptotic neurons, which, on one hand, drive the brain colonization of microglia, while, on the other hand, trigger microglia efferocytosis and induce the expression of microglia signature genes such as apoeb and slc7a7 (38, 39). As Mrc1a (mannose receptor C-type 1a) is a membrane-localized receptor involving in endocytosis, the comparison of our current study with that of Green et al. (24) suggests that the heterogeneous expression of mrc1a in microglia might be functionally shaped by local brain environment (possibly microglia efferocytosis), rather than predetermined by their ontogenies. This concept is further supported by the observation that the two microglia populations, i.e., RBI-derived embryonic microglia and AGM-derived adult microglia in zebrafish, while manifest distinct turnover rates and lifespan, display no discernible functional heterogeneity (20, 21). Thus, given the notable differences in microglia gene expression across various CNS regions, it is plausible that the environmental cues, rather than their origins, predominantly contribute to microglia heterogeneity.

We also noticed that 4C4+pu.1− cells were occasionally detected in the brain for wild-type larvae larger than 5 dpf. These cells might be attributed to the incomplete labeling of pu.1+ cells of our knock-in line at late stages, considering the absence of 4C4+pu.1− cells in pu.1Δ839/Δ839;spi-bΔ232/Δ232 double mutants. Alternatively, this could be a result of the incomplete antibody staining caused by poor antibody penetration in large fish larvae instead of a pu.1-independent microglia population.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Zebrafish husbandry

Zebrafish were maintained according to the standard protocol. Tg(mrc1a:egfp) (27), pu.1KI-DsRed (also named as pu.1CKO) (26), pu.1KI-eGFP (also known as pu.1KI) (26), Tg(globin:loxP-DsRedx-loxP-GFP) (40), Tg(kdrl:Hsa.HRAS-mCherry)s896Tg [shortened as Tg(kdrl:mCherry)] (41), Tg(mfap4:eGFP) (42), Tg(mpeg1:LGFP) (42), Tg(mfap4:CreER)hkz036Tg (21), Tg(mpeg1:LRLG) short for Tg(mpeg1:loxP-DsRedxloxP-eGFP)hkz015Tg (42), Tg(lyve1b:CreERT2)cq25 (28), Tg(kdrl:LRLG) short for Tg(kdrl:loxP-DsRedx-loxP-eGFP) (43), flt4ihb439 (ordered from China zebrafish Resource Center, National Aquatic Biological Resource Center, no. CZ1389; http://www.zfish.cn/meetingResource), pu.1Δ839 (35), spi-bΔ232 (35), and ccbe1sa1019 (28) were used in this study. All animal procedures and experiments were conducted according to the guidelines of the South China University of Technology (SCUT) and Shenzhen Peking University–The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology Medical Center (SPHMC). All the animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of SCUT and SPHMC.

Generation of pu.1KI-DsRed

In brief, the donor plasmid containing Loxp-flanked P2A-pu.1-exon4-6-eGFP cassette and the P2A-Dsred sequence was integrated into intron 3 of pu.1 locus via nonhomologous end-joining method to generate the pu.1KI-eGFP fish. Then, pu.1KI-eGFP embryos were injected with Cre mRNA to excise the Loxp-flanked P2A-pu.1-exon4-6-eGFP cassette at one-cell stage. The resulted embryos were raised to adult and outcrossed with wild-type fish to generate the stable pu.1KI-DsRed fish (26). In pu.1KI-DsRed fish, the proper splicing of the splicing-acceptor site SpA2 and joining of the P2A-DsRed with exon 3 allow the expression of DsRed under the control of the endogenous regulatory elements of pu.1 gene.

Immunohistochemistry

Embryos and larvae were raised to desired stages and fixed in 4% fresh paraformaldehyde at 4°C overnight followed by dehydration with 100% methanol at −20°C overnight. After rehydration with 1× Phosphate Buffered Saline with Tween-20 (PBST), samples were permeabilized by proteinase K treatment (10 μg/ml; EO0492, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 5 min (36 hpf), 10 min (48 hpf), 15 min (3 dpf), 45 min (5dpf), 60 min (7 dpf), or 90 min (15 dpf) at room temperature followed by acetone for 7 min at −20°C. After washing three times with 1× PBST (10 min each), samples were blocked by 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at room temperature for 1 hour and incubated with primary antibody at 4°C overnight. Then, samples were incubated with secondary antibody at 4°C overnight after washing at least six times with 1× PBST (5 min each). The primary antibodies used in this study include 4C4 (mouse, Seiger, Becker and Becker Laboratories) (44), goat anti-GFP (Abcam, ab6658), rabbit anti-DsRed (Clontech, 632496), rabbit anti-mCherry (MBL, PM005), and rabbit anti-Lcp1 (45). The secondary antibodies used in this study include donkey anti-goat immunoglobulin G (IgG) Alexa Fluor Plus 488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A-32814), donkey anti-rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor 555 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A-31572), donkey anti-mouse IgG Alexa Fluor 647 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A-31571), and donkey anti-mouse IgG Alexa Fluor 555 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A-31570). All the primary and secondary antibodies were used in 1:400 dilution.

RNAscope smFISH and immunostaining

RNAscope single molecule fluorescent in-situ hybridization (smFISH) was conducted with the RNAscope Multiplex Fluorescent Reagent Kit [323100, Advanced Cell Diagnostics (ACD)] according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The Dr-spi1b-C2 (1095851-C2, ACD) probe was diluted 1:50 for hybridization. After in situ hybridization, embryos were incubated in the blocking buffer (5% FBS in PBST) at room temperature for 1 hour. GFP and 4C4 antibodies were diluted together in the blocking buffer for the primary antibody staining at 4°C overnight. Then, the samples were incubated with secondary antibodies at 4°C overnight after washing at least six times with 1× PBST. The secondary antibodies used were donkey anti-goat IgG Alexa Fluor Plus 488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A-32814) and donkey anti-mouse IgG Alexa Fluor 647 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A-31571).

Lineage tracing

Tg(mfap4:CreER) or Tg(lyve1b:CreER) fish was mated with Tg(mpeg1:loxP-DsRedx-loxP-GFP) or Tg(kdrl:loxP-DsRedx-loxP-GFP) reporter, respectively. Embryos were dechorionated at 22 hpf and treated with 5 nM 4-OHT until 5 dpf at 28.5°C. Then, the 5-dpf larvae were collected to assess the percentage of GFP+ microglia in brain by confocal imaging.

Imaging

Live larvae or fixed samples were anesthetized with 3-amino-benzoic acid ester (A5040, Supelco), mounted in 1% low-melting-point agarose with the right position in glass-bottomed 35-mm petri dishes. Images were captured by Zeiss LSM880 or LSM800 confocal microscope with Plan-Apochromat 10×/0.45 M27 and Plan-Apochromat 20×/0.8 M27 objectives and processed with ZEN 2.5 software (maximum z-projection).

Quantification and statistical analysis

All the statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.3.1. Unpaired Student’s t tests were used to calculate the P value for pairwise comparisons. Two-tailed P values are used for all t tests.
