A Comprehensive Overview on Alopecia Areata with Review on Advances in Genetics and Treatment
Authors: Sudip Das, Apeksha Singh, SK Shahriar Ahmed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Alopecia areata (AA) is a chronic autoimmune disorder characterised by sudden, non-scarring hair loss due to the immune system mistakenly attacking hair follicles. It affects approximately 2% of the population and can manifest in various patterns, ranging from small patches to total loss of scalp and body hair. While the exact cause remains unknown, genetic predisposition and environmental triggers play a significant role in its development. Hair follicles in affected individuals become arrested in later stages of the hair growth cycle, leading to premature transition to the catagen or telogen phase and impaired regrowth. Though not life-threatening, AA has profound psychological and emotional effects, impacting self-esteem and quality of life. Various treatment options, including corticosteroids, immunotherapy, and emerging biologic therapies, aim to manage symptoms and promote hair regrowth, but no definitive cure exists. Ongoing research continues to explore the genetic and immunological mechanisms underlying the condition to develop more effective treatments. Awareness and support for individuals with AA remain crucial in helping them cope with the physical and emotional challenges associated with the disorder. This article provides a comprehensive overview of the genetics, causes, and treatment options for AA.

Introduction

Alopecia areata (AA) was first described by Celsius between 14 and 37 BC, while the term “alopecia areata” was introduced by Sauvages.[1] In 1843, Hebra challenged the fungal aetiology theory proposed by Willan and Gruby, highlighting its inaccuracies. AA is a chronic autoimmune disorder where the immune system targets hair follicles, nails, and, in some cases, the pigment epithelium of the retina. Affecting approximately 2% of the population, the condition results in hair loss without permanently damaging the follicles. The root cause lies in the immune system’s mistaken attack on these structures, influenced by genetic predispositions and environmental triggers. AA is now widely recognised as an autoimmune disease with a genetic foundation. In individuals with AA, hair follicles become arrested in stages III or IV of the hair growth cycle, prematurely transitioning to the catagen or telogen phase. This disruption leads to sudden hair loss and impaired hair regrowth.[2] This article provides a comprehensive overview of AA.

Pathogenesis

In healthy individuals, hair follicles are immune-privileged, protecting them from autoimmunity. In AA, this privilege is disrupted, leading to immune attacks on melanocyte- and keratinocyte-related autoantigens, driven by Th1 (IL-2, TNF, IL-12) and Th17 (IL-17, IL-17E) cytokines, which correlate with disease activity.[3]

Genetics of AA: Gene expression studies in AA consistently show upregulation of interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) response genes (e.g. CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCL11) and downregulation of hair keratin (KRT) and keratin-associated protein (KRTAP) genes. IFN-γ, a key Th1 cytokine, is widely recognised as a major driver of AA pathogenesis, supported by evidence from gene expression studies, immunohistology, Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) loci, and cytokine profiling in AA patients.[4]

A genetic predisposition is evident, with polymorphisms in PTPN22, FAS, FASL, CTLA4, and IL2RA identified as potential contributors. Triggers include viral infections (e.g. EBV, hepatitis B/C, swine flu) and vaccines (e.g. influenza, hepatitis, coronavirus).[5]

Common and Rare Genetic Risk Variants

GWAS on AA have identified 14 susceptibility loci, highlighting both the polygenic nature of the condition and its autoimmune foundation.[67] Most of the 14 loci identified are associated with immune responses, with the major histocompatibility complex showing the strongest link.[6] So far, only a limited number of genetic studies have explored the role of rare variants in the risk of developing AA. Rare risk variants in AA have been identified. Notably, the authors reported reduced expression of KRT82 in the skin and hair follicles of patients with AA.[8] In addition to the rare single-nucleotide variants in KRT82 identified through whole-exome sequencing, genome-wide SNP array data, along with findings from other studies, suggest that large, rare structural variants, such as copy number variants, may also contribute to the aetiology of AA.

Association

AA is associated with numerous comorbidities, including autoimmune thyroid disease, atopic conditions, metabolic syndrome, Helicobacter pylori infection, lupus erythematosus, iron deficiency anaemia, and vitamin D deficiency. A recent large-scale study involving nearly 70,000 patients identified the most common comorbidities as hyperlipidaemia (22.4%), hypertension (21.8%), thyroid disease (13%), contact dermatitis (11%), and depression and anxiety disorders (each at 9%).[9]

Autoimmune thyroid disease is commonly reported, with an odds ratio (OR) of 1.66 and a prevalence of 13.9%, while thyroid dysfunction has an OR of 4.36 (prevalence: 12.5%). Other notable comorbidities include atopic dermatitis (OR: 2.36; prevalence: 9.6%), metabolic syndrome (OR: 3.68; prevalence: 37.3%), and vitamin D deficiency (OR: 4.61; prevalence: 65.4%).[10]

Psychiatric conditions, particularly anxiety and depression, are also more prevalent in individuals with AA. Anxiety, in particular, shows a significant 2.5-fold increase in risk compared to control groups, with paediatric patients especially vulnerable (51% meeting criteria for an anxiety disorder). Emotional stress has been implicated as a trigger for AA, potentially through mechanisms involving elevated catecholamines and increased expression of corticotropin-releasing hormone receptors around hair follicles.[11]

Current Treatments

There is a pressing need for new therapies for AA, as most existing treatments offer limited effectiveness, particularly in cases involving extensive hair loss.

Topical therapy

Topical corticosteroids are the first-line treatment for patchy AA, especially in children, due to their painless application and safety. While effective for some, with studies showing up to 70% regrowth after 3 months, their benefits are limited and may cause side effects such as folliculitis.[12]

Intralesional corticosteroids: Intralesional corticosteroids, particularly triamcinolone acetonide (5–10 mg/mL), are the first-line treatment for localised AA affecting < 50% of the scalp. Injections are administered every 4–6 weeks in 0.1 mL doses spaced 1 cm apart. Hydrocortisone acetate (25 mg/mL) is another commonly used option.

Topical contact sensitisers: DPCP is preferred over mutagenic agents such as DNCB. It induces contact dermatitis to modulate T cell activity, with complete regrowth reported in 62.5% of patients. Side effects include dermatitis, lymphadenopathy, and rare systemic effects.[13]

Anthralin: Effective for AA with anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive properties. Applied at 0.5%–1% concentrations, it can cause skin irritation and staining. Studies report complete regrowth in 25% of patients and good regrowth in 39.5%.[13]

Minoxidil: Minoxidil, a direct-acting arteriolar vasodilator, promotes hair growth by increasing intracellular calcium and upregulating ATP synthase, which enhances stem cell differentiation. The 5% solution is applied twice daily (up to 25 drops) and shows initial regrowth within 3 months, requiring continued use for optimal results. Effective in 81% of patchy AA cases, minoxidil offers limited benefit for severe forms such as alopecia totalis or universalis. Side effects include headache, dermatitis, and reversible hypertrichosis.[13]

Topical tacrolimus

Topical tacrolimus, a calcineurin inhibitor that suppresses cytokines such as IL-2, IFN-γ, and TNF-α, has shown limited effectiveness in treating AA. While animal studies indicated potential benefits, human trials, such as a study by Price et al.,[14] reported no terminal hair growth after 24 weeks of 0.1% tacrolimus ointment applied twice daily.

Topical retinoids

Topical retinoids such as tretinoin and bexarotene have shown mixed results in AA, with skin irritation being a common side effect. A half-head trial by Talpur et al.[15] using 1% bexarotene gel twice daily for 6 months demonstrated variable outcomes.

Prostaglandin analogues

Prostaglandin analogues, such as latanoprost and bimatoprost, have been used to treat AA affecting the eyelashes, but the results have been unsatisfactory.[15]

Systemic Therapy

Systemic steroids can halt the progression of AA, but their use is often limited due to poor results, high relapse rates, and significant side effects. The suggested dosage ranges 0.5–1 mg/kg/day for adults and 0.1–1 mg/kg/day for children, with treatment lasting 1–6 months.[16] High-dose pulsed corticosteroid treatments, including oral mini pulse therapy (OMP), have shown better regrowth in some cases, with a 71.43% regrowth rate at a dose of 5 mg/day for 2 consecutive days.

Novel Therapies

Oral janus kinase inhibitors for alopecia areata

Currently, six Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors have demonstrated success in treating AA: Tofacitinib, Ruxolitinib, Baricitinib, CTP-543, PF-06651600, and PF-06700841. These are oral medications that target the JAK-signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) pathway.[17]

The involvement of the JAK-STAT pathway in AA and the reversal of AA with JAK inhibitors were first demonstrated in mice in 2014. These findings underpin the use of JAK inhibitors for AA treatment. In AA, JAK-STAT inhibition disrupts the positive feedback loop between hair follicle cells and cytotoxic CD8+ NKG2D+ T cells, which is critical to the disease process. Key genes in this pathway related to hair growth include STAT5A/B, STAT3, JAK1, JAK3, and Socs2/3. These genes are highly expressed during the catagen and telogen phases of hair growth but are suppressed in the early anagen phase.[17]

JAK inhibitors are convenient oral medications with proven efficacy and safety in large-scale studies for conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis and psoriatic arthritis. Although they are selective, they are not specific to a single JAK, allowing them to impact multiple immunologic pathways. For AA, recommended dosages are as follows: tofacitinib, 5 mg twice daily (BID); ruxolitinib, 15–20 mg BID; and baricitinib, 4 mg daily.[1819]

A study by Khan et al. reported a 72.4% response rate among patients treated with oral JAK inhibitors, with 45.7% classified as good responders and 21.4% as partial responders. The average time to initial hair regrowth was 2.2 ± 6.7 months, while complete regrowth occurred within 6.7 ± 2.2 months.[20]

Despite the excitement surrounding JAK inhibitors in AA, potential risks should not be overlooked. Common side effects include nausea, headaches, anaemia, high cholesterol, and an increased risk of infections, including herpes virus reactivation. Additionally, there are reports of relapse after discontinuing treatment, and the reactivation of tuberculosis has been documented with baricitinib and tofacitinib. Rare but serious risks include blood clots, emphasising the importance of careful patient monitoring during therapy.

Topical JAK inhibitors

The two primary topical JAK inhibitors that have demonstrated some success in promoting eyelash and eyebrow regrowth are tofacitinib 2% ointment and ruxolitinib 0.6% cream. Notably, Craiglow reported a case of successful treatment of localised AA using a topical tofacitinib 2% solution.[21]

Dupilumab

Dupilumab is a biologic medication that targets interleukin-4 (IL-4) and interleukin-13 (IL-13), key cytokines involved in inflammatory responses. It is administered via subcutaneous injection every 2 weeks.[21]

Effectiveness in AA

Research on dupilumab’s role in treating AA is still emerging. One study reported significant improvement in alopecia totalis following treatment with dupilumab. However, other reports have documented cases of AA developing shortly after initiating dupilumab for eczema.[22] These conflicting outcomes highlight the need for further clinical trials to evaluate its efficacy and safety for AA.

Safety and tolerability

Dupilumab is generally well tolerated, with minimal reported side effects. Its safety profile makes it an attractive option for further investigation in managing AA.

Apremilast

PDE4 inhibitors reduce inflammation but show mixed efficacy in AA, with side effects such as diarrhoea, nausea, headache, fatigue, and weight loss.[5]

Abatacept

Abatacept is a biologic medication and a fusion protein composed of cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated antigen 4 (CTLA-4) linked to part of an immunoglobulin. It works as an immunomodulator by inhibiting the activation of T cells, which play a critical role in autoimmune and inflammatory conditions.[23]

Mechanism of action[24]

Abatacept binds to CD80/CD86 on antigen-presenting cells, preventing them from delivering the costimulatory signal required for T cell activation. This reduces the inflammatory response by dampening the activity of overactive T cells.

Conclusion

PRP is a promising treatment for AA, particularly in early or less severe cases. However, its effectiveness can vary, and patients with chronic or extensive disease may require complementary or alternative therapies. Ongoing studies aim to refine protocols and better understand PRP’s role in AA management.

Ustekinumab

Ustekinumab is an immunomodulatory medication that reduces inflammation by inhibiting the activity of specific cytokines, interleukin-12 (IL-12) and interleukin-23 (IL-23), which are key drivers of inflammatory processes. While primarily used for treating psoriasis and Crohn’s disease, a small case series has suggested its potential efficacy in promoting hair regrowth in patients with moderate-to-severe AA.[27]

Administration

Ustekinumab is administered as a subcutaneous injection into the stomach, thighs, or upper outer arms. The treatment schedule includes an initial injection followed by a second dose at week 4, after which it is given every 12 weeks.

Adverse effects

Like other immunosuppressive therapies, ustekinumab can increase the risk of serious infections. Common side effects include injection site reactions, fatigue, headaches, and sinusitis. Proper patient monitoring is essential to mitigate these risks.

Fexofenadine[28]

AA: Research suggests that it may have additional immunomodulatory effects in AA patients:

Reduction of IFN-γ (Interferon Gamma): This cytokine, produced by T cells, is associated with inflammation and immune activation in autoimmune conditions such as AA. Fexofenadine may reduce IFN-γ production, thereby reducing inflammation.

Decrease in ICAM-1 expression: Intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1) is expressed on epithelial cells and is involved in the recruitment of immune cells. Fexofenadine’s ability to reduce ICAM-1 expression may contribute to its beneficial effects.

Lowering Substance P levels: Substance P is a neuropeptide implicated in inflammatory and autoimmune responses, including those seen in AA. Fexofenadine appears to reduce Substance P levels, further contributing to its anti-inflammatory properties.

Vitamin D

Vitamin D deficiency is linked to AA. A lack of 1,25-dihydroxy vitamin D3 receptor (VDR) expression impairs hair follicle growth.

Mechanisms involved:

Reduced VDR expression: Leads to decreased Wnt/β-catenin signalling, essential for hair follicle proliferation and differentiation.

Immune privilege maintenance: Vitamin D helps maintain immune privilege in hair follicles by reducing IFN-γ levels, downregulating ligands for NKG2D and CXCR3, inhibiting the JAK/STAT pathway, and reducing oxidative stress.

Calcipotriol: A Vitamin D analogue shown to promote hair regrowth in AA.[29]

Oral zinc sulphate

Enhances hair regrowth, potentially through immune modulation. A study showed zinc deficiency in AA patients.[30]

Statins

They work synergistically to reduce C-reactive protein (CRP), which is associated with inflammation. Studies have shown variable results regarding their effectiveness in treating AA.[35]

Emerging and future therapies include stem cell therapies that stimulate Wnt/β-catenin signalling and protect hair follicle immune privilege, with phase II trials evaluating stem cell educator therapy. BNZ-1 blocks IL-2, IL-15, and IL-9 signalling and is in preparation for a phase II trial. Fractional photothermolysis involves controlled dermal injury to stimulate hair regrowth, currently being tested in combination with PRP or triamcinolone acetonide. Tralokinumab is an anti-IL-13 monoclonal antibody with results pending from clinical trials. Gene therapy uses siRNAs targeting T-box 21 and other pathways to reduce autoimmunity. Microbiome dysbiosis—an imbalance in the microbial communities within the body—has been observed in patients with AA. Notably, some studies have reported cases where patients with AA experienced hair regrowth after undergoing faecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) for Clostridium difficile infection. This highlights a potential link between gut microbiota and the pathogenesis of AA. Novel targets in inflammation focus on blocking NKG2D-activating ligands or halting T cell activity to modulate the immune response.[3637]

In conclusion, AA is a complex autoimmune condition that causes unpredictable hair loss, impacting both physical appearance and emotional well-being. While its exact cause remains unclear, genetic and environmental factors play a significant role in its development. Although there is no definitive cure, various treatment options, including medications, immunotherapy, and lifestyle adjustments, can help manage symptoms and promote hair regrowth. Ongoing research continues to provide hope for more effective therapies in the future. Raising awareness and offering emotional support are essential in helping individuals cope with the psychological impact of the condition, empowering them to lead confident and fulfilling lives.
