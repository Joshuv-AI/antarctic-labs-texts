Learning to share nicely: Leveraging the Nobel Prize-winning Shapley value to enhance collaboration and cooperation in healthcare systems
Authors: Mohammed A. Mohammed, Neil Walton, Ozayr Mohammed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Healthcare systems are facing mounting financial pressures, often intensified by structural fragmentation that fosters ʻself-serving profit centres’, fuels a ʻmedical arms race’, and leads to a ʻtragedy of the commons’. As these systems shift toward more integrated and collaborative models, the need for fair, efficient and transparent cost-sharing mechanisms becomes increasingly urgent. The Nobel Prize-winning Shapley value, derived from cooperative game theory, offers a principled approach to allocating costs and benefits based on each party’s contribution. This method not only promotes equity and accountability, but also encourages sustainable collaboration toward shared objectives. Given the complexity of healthcare decision-making, the Shapley value represents a promising approach that merits further exploration and evaluation in real-world settings.

Systems thinking and cooperative strategies in healthcare

Systems thinking emphasises that any system must have a clear aim and that its components should cooperate effectively to achieve this aim.1 For instance, one of the most complex systems that we know is the human body, characterised by a high degree of interdependence and cooperation. When a toothache compels us to visit the dentist, it’s our feet that take us there.

Healthcare systems similarly operate with a shared purpose, often articulated through the ʻQuadruple Aim’2 – enhancing patient care, improving population health, reducing per capita healthcare costs, and the joy of work for staff. Although the potential benefits of cooperation – such as risk sharing, cost savings, reduced duplication and improved care coordination – are widely recognised, several barriers impede collaboration.3, 4, 5 These include institutional inertia, misaligned incentives, market pressures and, critically, disputes over how to distribute costs and benefits fairly. For instance, if cost savings or additional income result from a cooperative venture, determining a fair distribution often proves contentious.

So, in practice, cooperation among healthcare organisations – such as primary, secondary, tertiary and community/social care providers – is often lacking, because individual entities often act as ʻselfish profit centres’, prioritising their own interests over the system’s collective good. This behaviour contributes to inefficiencies such as the ʻmedical arms race’ and the ʻtragedy of the commons’, harming patients, payers and society.6 To counteract this, approaches that foster cooperation are essential. We demonstrate how the Shapley value,7, 8 a Nobel Prize-winning concept from game theory, ensures fair allocation of costs/benefits to stakeholders, fostering collaboration and reducing conflicts by way of a simple illustrative example.

An illustrative example

Consider three hospitals (A, B, C) that transport blood samples to a common testing facility along the same route. If each hospital pays individually, their respective costs are £70, £90 and £240, totalling £400. By cooperating, they reduce the total cost to £240 – the price of the longest journey. However, the challenge lies in deciding how to share this reduced expense.

Several methods might be proposed:1.Equal split: Dividing the cost equally, each hospital pays £80 (£240 ÷ 3). However, hospital A would reject this, as its individual cost rises from £70 to £80.2.Proportional split: Each hospital pays a share proportional to its initial cost. Calculations yield contributions of £42, £52 and £144 for A, B and C, respectively. Yet, hospitals A and B could form their own coalition, paying £90 together and leaving C to shoulder the full £240 cost alone.3.Financial capability: Costs are distributed based on financial capacity, relying on goodwill and negotiation. Each of these methods has significant drawbacks, undermining cooperation and risking the dissolution of the coalition while increasing costs for all participants (the tragedy of the commons).

The Shapley value: a fair solution

In the 1950s, Nobel laureate Lloyd Shapley introduced the Shapley value, a solution from cooperative game theory designed to distribute the value of collaboration fairly among participants.7 The Shapley value considers each participant’s marginal contribution to the overall savings, ensuring a transparent and equitable allocation of resources.

So, by collaborating through the Shapley value, each hospital contributes less than it would on its own while collectively driving down costs. This incentivises cooperation, while also generating substantial savings for the entire healthcare system. It’s a win–win solution!

A more complicated scenario

We now consider a more complicated scenario where hospital A has three advanced CT scanners, hospital B has two, and hospital C does not have any. Hospitals A and B currently operate their scanners at a utilisation of 85%. Hospital C finds that it will require 60% utilisation of a scanner. Hospital C has the choice to purchase a new CT scanner for £10,000,000 or share access to the CT scanners in hospitals A and B. Hospital A has 3 × 100% − 3 × 85% = 45% spare capacity across its three scanners, and hospital B has 30% (2 × 100% − 2 × 85%) spare capacity across its two scanners. We assume that each percentage point of scanner utilisation costs £4,000. Finally, let us assume that each machine requires maintenance at a cost of £100,000 per year.

The problem appears somewhat complex at first glance. However, it is simpler than it may seem. We can work out the values when hospitals A, B and C decide to collaborate. When we consider the hospitals jointly, they effectively act as a ʻsingle’ hospital with five scanners running. In this case, it is beneficial for hospitals to collaborate. Again, we must distribute costs. A calculation similar to the above gives the solution through the Shapley value: the respective costs are £0.953 M, £0.513 M and £0.973 M to hospitals A, B and C, and all participants save over £333 K by sharing. Discussion

At its core, the Shapley value offers a principled way of ʻsharing nicely’, allocating costs or benefits across organisations in proportion to their contribution to the collective outcome. However, the Shapley value, or indeed any form of cooperation, is not without limitations. It is essential to acknowledge these in cooperative healthcare systems.

Providers are often assessed individually through performance targets and public league tables. While these provide accountability, they may inadvertently discourage cooperation.10 However, this tension between collaboration and accountability is increasingly recognised in NHS policy.11 Governance structures and mitigations are developed to address this, such as pooled funds within the Better Care Fund framework.12 We further note that the Shapley framework is not restricted to monetary values; while we illustrate financial costs in examples, the value attributed to coalitions could equally represent improvements in quality of care, reductions in waiting times, or other system-level performance metrics.

Healthcare providers operate across multiple services with overlapping collaborative arrangements.13, 14, 15, 16, 17 While Shapley values can be calculated for a specific activity – such as joint procurement – providers typically participate in multiple, overlapping collaborations. An allocation that is fair within one service area may interact with others, increasing calculation complexity. In practice, Shapley-based allocations are best applied within clearly defined service boundaries, rather than assumed to scale automatically across all activities. While health organisations likely collaborate implicitly without clear rules, Shapley values can provide a transparent and quantifiable basis for structuring cooperative arrangements.

Policy and governance constraints can further restrict the ability of organisations to cooperate9, 10, 11, 12 (and adopt the Shapley value), while the data required to support such calculations may be incomplete or contested. Still, analogous allocation principles have been operationalised in highly regulated environments such as spectrum auctions,18 where complex rules must operate within strict legal and governance frameworks. Further, the increasing digitisation of healthcare data, standardised costing frameworks and system-level reporting requirements can reduce these barriers, making more structured approaches to sharing more feasible over time.19

Beyond these non-technical considerations20, 21 technical barriers can also exist. As indicated above, the number of calculations increases rapidly as more participants are added. Practical approximations and computational tools, such as Monte Carlo methods, can substantially reduce this complexity, as demonstrated by Lightfoot6 and by our accompanying online application.9 In some cases, individual partners may perceive that they contribute more than they receive relative to acting alone or within a smaller coalition. In technical terms, the Shapley value may fall outside the core – the set of allocations that all participants would prefer to the alternative of leaving the group. This is more likely when the overall gains from cooperation are limited, or contributions are highly uneven, and, if unaddressed, such perceptions may undermine trust and cooperation, particularly in systems where organisational incentives are poorly aligned with collective efficiency.

Conclusion

Potential benefits of sharing have been observed in multiple health settings, such as shared corporate functions, sharing clinical pathways22 and multi-trust procurement.23 Furthermore, cooperation is increasingly seen as a signal of good governance.24

We posit that the Shapley value remains underexplored with a strong potential to influence future healthcare.6 Given the complexity of real-world decision-making and the absence of established mechanisms for multi-site resource sharing, we propose pilot studies that use the Shapley value as a starting point for discussion rather than a rigid rule.

As healthcare systems transition toward integrated care models, the need for fair and efficient cost-sharing mechanisms is paramount. The Nobel Prize-winning Shapley value offers a novel approach that merits further exploration and evaluation in the real world.
