Development of high-throughput analytical methods for characterization and quantitation of rAAV genome integrity
Authors: Xushan Wang, Zhaohui Yang, Andrew Pla, Mary Ng, Brandon Zhuang, Shreya Ahuja, Clarissa Tjoanda, Vedud Purde, Sabine Wenzel, Brian Marshall, Kaixiang Huang, Brandon Lee Doyle, Meng John Zhao, Christopher A Moreland, Jorge Francisco Haller, Garrett Daniels, Stuart Nelson, Beverly A Heinz, Sarah M Richer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecombinant AAV (rAAV) vectors are a leading viral vector for gene therapy. Viral genome (Vg) titer is the primary method to determine potency of rAAV and dosing in preclinical/clinical studies. However, the rAAV genome comprises a heterogeneous population. These particles not only contain the intact genome but also include numerous truncated species, which likely lack functionality and may induce adverse effects. Consequently, the Vg titer does not accurately reflect the integrity of the rAAV genome. Currently, there is no reliable quantitative method available. In this study, we demonstrate that there is a disconnect between Vg titer and the activity of rAAV by using multiple vectors and high-throughput imaging assays. Importantly, we have developed a novel, high-throughput RNA-DNA hybrid capture-multiplex meso scale discovery (MSD) method for characterizing the integrity of the rAAV genome. This method quantifies the intact versus truncated genomes of both the plus and minus strands individually with high sensitivity and specificity. The integrity data generated by our novel method exhibits a strong correlation with the activity of the rAAV. We anticipate that our new method will significantly improve preclinical/clinical studies, enhance vector design, and increase delivery efficiency. Furthermore, this method can be used to characterize and quantitate RNA and DNA in various fields.Keywords: rAAV, gene therapy, high content imaging screening, RNA-DNA hybrid capture, genome integrity, multiplex, meso scale discovery, MSD, eGFP vectors, and transduction efficiency

Introduction

Adeno-associated virus (AAV) has emerged as a predominant viral vector for gene therapy. One of the most important features of recombinant AAV (rAAV) vectors is the capability of mediating long-term gene expression.1 After administration, rAAV particles enter the cells, uncoat and release their genome in the nucleus. The uncoated rAAV genomes assemble into circular monomers, further progressing into concatemers, and form chromatin-like structures, which are associated with stable, long-term transgene expression.2,3 The AAV inverted terminal repeats (ITRs) located at both 3′ and 5′ ends are the only cis elements required for rAAV rescue, replication, and packaging,4 and play a critical role in the formation of circular monomers and concatemers.5,6 Only full length rAAV genomes with ITRs at both 5′ and 3′ ends assemble into circular monomers and concatemers. Any truncated rAAV genomes lacking ITRs are quickly degraded.7 However, like other parvoviruses, AAV DNA replication and packaging are error prone, and packaged AAV genomes are heterogeneous within AAV particles. Some AAV particles within the population contain the intact genome, but there are also many truncated species, typically termed “partial” or “defective interfering (DI)” particles, which typically lack part of the AAV genome and/or one ITR.8,9 During rAAV production, multiple mechanisms contribute to truncations of AAV genomes including AAV genome rescue, replication, packaging, harvest, purification, and even storage conditions.10,11,12,13,14,15 These mechanisms are also impacted by vector sequence, vector size, and method of production.11,16,17

Viral genome titer (Vg titer), as determined by qPCR or digital PCR, has been widely used to dose rAAV in preclinical and clinical studies.18,19 However, the current PCR-based Vg titer methods do not distinguish between intact and truncated rAAV genomes, making correlation between titer and accurate potency difficult. Accurate quantitation of intact and truncated rAAV genomes is one of the most critical quality attributes in rAAV gene therapy. Multiple methods have been developed to tackle this challenge, such as alkaline agarose gel electrophoresis,20 analytical ultracentrifugation (AUC),21 capillary electrophoresis (CE),22 charge detection mass spectrometry (CD-MS),20,23 anion-exchange HPLC assay (AEX),24 cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM),25 long-read next generation DNA sequencing (NGS),16,26 and multiplex digital PCR.27,28,29 NGS methods such as PacBio single molecule real-time (PacBio SMRT) and Oxford Nanopore sequencing, along with multiplex digital PCR, are among the most promising techniques currently available. However, despite their potential, these methods embody some major setbacks. For instance, PacBio SMRT has high material requirements, complex data analysis,27 and necessitates double-strand DNA as the template for sequencing.30 The rAAV genome, with its heterogeneous nature and hairpin structure of the ITR,9 is not an ideal template for PacBio SMRT sequencing and demands extensive DNA repair processes during library preparation, including using the ITR 3′ end as a primer for new DNA synthesis31 and trimming of 3′ overhangs.16 These steps significantly alter the integrity of the DNA samples. Oxford Nanopore technology is capable of sequencing single-strand DNA templates; however, it demonstrates a significant preference for double-strand templates, producing a substantially higher number of reads for the latter.32 Both PacBio SMRT and Oxford Nanopore show size and sequence biases in DNA fragment sequencing,33,34,35 and their accuracy in quantifying the rAAV genome integrity remains uncertain.22,26,29,36 Multiplex digital PCR has significant considerations, such as low amplification efficiency with amplicons close to the ITR,37 formation of full-length-like sequences through annealing two partial genomes, and breakdown of full-length genomes during the heating procedure.36 Most importantly, plus and minus strands of rAAV genomes are not always equally packaged into AAV particles. Current multiplex digital PCR methods cannot quantitate the plus and minus strands separately. Therefore, it is urgent for the rAAV-mediated gene therapy field to develop reliable and innovative analytical methods to accurately determine virus potency and quantitate intact versus truncated rAAV genomes. It is not only required for accurately projecting a safe and effective dose for preclinical and clinical studies, but also for improving the vector design and production systems.

In this paper, we describe a series of rAAV eGFP vectors with a wide range of potency and efficacy to evaluate analytical methods. Using these vectors, we developed a high-throughput, high-content imaging screening method that is able to accurately quantitate the potency of rAAV eGFP vectors and provide a large size of data to evaluate multiple data analysis methods. The data clearly demonstrate that there is a disconnect between Vg titer and the activity of rAAV virus. Most importantly, we have developed a novel high-throughput RNA-DNA hybrid capture-multiplex meso scale discovery (MSD) method. This method allows for the precise and thorough characterization of both plus and minus strand rAAV genome integrity, and quantitation of intact versus truncated rAAV genomes in rAAV products through a straightforward and quick sample preparation procedure. This method will not only be able to provide accurate dose projection for preclinical and clinical studies but also has potential to identify the mechanisms causing truncation and providing solutions to modify the rAAV vector or processes of virus production, improve rAAV product quality, and increase efficacy of gene delivery with potential reduction of side effects on patients.

Results

Development of the high-content screening assay and evaluation of models of data analyses

Thirteen eGFP vectors (Figure 1A) of varying lengths were engineered by incorporating stuffer DNA at the 3′ end. This was done with the intent to develop and evaluate new analytical methods and investigate how the size of rAAV influences its packaging efficiency and transduction capabilities. rAAVs were produced by using the baculovirus/Sf9 system and packaged into AAV serotype 9 (AAV9). Each vector successfully generated a virus with titers measured by droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) (Figure S1). Vectors with smaller genome sizes had greater yields, but when the genome exceeded 4.05 Kb, the yield significantly decreased. The natural AAV genome is 4.7 Kb, while some evidence suggests rAAV can package genomes up to 5.1–5.3 Kb.38 Remarkably, even the 6.0 Kb vector managed to produce a virus, though its yield was three times lower than that of the most productive vector (3.05 Kb).

(A) The design of 13 eGFP vectors with increasing size of a stuffer DNA. (B) The images of the eGFP (top) and nuclei staining (bottom) of SH SY5Y cells, which were infected by 13 rAAV eGFP vectors at the same amount of rAAV virus based on the Vg titer.

To assess the effectiveness of the rAAV eGFP vectors, we adjusted the virus titers to an equivalent concentration based on the Vg titer and infected undifferentiated SH SY5Y cells on a 384-well plate, beginning with 4.0e11 Vg/mL and employing a serial dilution with 1.6-fold decrements across 20 to 21 doses. We examined the infected cells using the Opera Phenix high-content imaging screening system, utilizing a confocal lens at 10× magnification to observe the entire well area across nine fields, as well as at 20× with a water immersion lens to view around 30% to 40% of the entire well area with five fields. To assess the variation and data reliability of different vectors and analytical methods, all rAAV vectors were also tested in HEK293T cells with two different time courses and repeated in SH SY5Y cells. Figure 1B illustrates a snapshot of one field from column 4 in the 384-well plate, where SH SY5Y cells were infected with rAAV virus at 2.5e11 Vg/mL or 2.3e5 Vg/cell. The cell count, inferred from nuclei staining, was consistent across various vectors. Notably, the number of eGFP-positive cells and the intensity of eGFP varied significantly based on the size of the vectors. Smaller vectors were associated with a higher number of eGFP-positive cells and greater intensity. A considerable decrease in the signal was noted once the vector size reached 4.5 Kb, and the expression levels of eGFP were minimal when sizes exceeded 4.8 Kb. The activity dramatically dropped even though rAAV should be able to package up to 5.1 or 5.3 Kb. It is not clear why the 5.5 and 6.0 Kb vectors had activity. It is suggested that a very small portion of particles packaged up to 6.0 Kb or had some internal deletion that made it fit within the 5.3 Kb limit. The experiments repeated in HEK293T cells resulted in similar results (Figure S2).

The data demonstrated that these 13 vectors had a wide range of activity and could be valuable to evaluate the high-throughput imaging and data analysis tools thoroughly. We designed multiple experiments to test this (Figure S3). Images captured underwent analysis in Columbus software by quantitating real nuclei count, eGFP positive cell numbers, intensity mean, and intensity sum. A total of 24 sets of 384-well data were generated. The data was first analyzed by a four-parameter curve fitting. Figures 2A–2F presents data from SH SY5Y cells infected with rAAV eGFP viruses for 4 days. The percentage of cells positive for eGFP demonstrated that vectors with smaller sizes showed greater activity (Figure 2A). The calculated EC50 (half maximal effective concentration) value was lower (shifted to the left) for these smaller-sized entities, indicating increased potency. However, numerous vectors failed to achieve 100% infection at even the highest tested viral concentration, making comparisons between different vectors challenging. Analysis of intensity mean and intensity sum were more challenging with the four-parameter curve fit (Figure 2B). The intensity mean and intensity sum showed a rise in intensity correlating with increased viral concentrations. However, it could not reach a plateau, making determining the upper asymptote difficult. We explored the possibility of using three-parameter curve fitting by fixing the top. While the maximum percentage of eGFP positive cells can be established at 100%, the maximum intensity level remains unknown, complicating cross-vector activity comparisons. A method to navigate this challenge is fixing the top to the highest intensity determined on the plate, depicted in Figures 2C and 2D, thereby enabling calculation of EC50 values across different vectors. Also, the vectors differ in both potency and efficacy. Consequently, comparing vectors solely based on EC50 may be somewhat inadequate because it does not incorporate the aspect of efficacy. To strengthen our analysis, we introduced a novel approach to analysis by plotting intensity mean against the virus concentration on a linear scale and evaluating the area under the curve.39 We utilize the area under the curve as an indicator of viral activity (Figures 2E and 2F). Because the vector with the smallest size displayed greater activity most of the time, we normalized the activity of larger vectors to a percentage of the 3.05 Kb vector. All datasets were evaluated by three data analytical methods: four-parameter curve fitting, three-parameter (fixed top) curve fitting, and area under the curve. Pearson correlations revealed that the EC50 values varied across different experiments and time courses using the same vector. Additionally, the EC50s did not correlate with vector sizes when a four-parameter curve fitting method was applied. However, the EC50 from three-parameter (fixed top) curve fitting and relative activity from area under the curve analyses were very stable among different experiments and time courses (Tables S1 and S2). The Pearson correlations were very high among different experiments and time courses (Table S2).40 The agreement or minimum significant ratio (MSR) was determined by the Bland-Altman plots and had a value less than three within two experiments in SH SY5Y cells and even between SH SY5Y and HEK293T cell line (Figures 2G and 2H).41,42 The % area under the curve of individual vectors related to the 3.05 Kb vector was also very consistent among different experiments and time courses. Selecting between intensity mean or intensity sum made no difference. The data from 20× magnification had less variation than 10× magnification for % eGFP positive cells within different experiments (Table S2), so the intensity mean at 20× magnification was further used to determine the activity of eGFP vectors and evaluate different analytical methods. It is worth noting that the % eGFP positive cells and intensity were quite different among the analyses, especially with the area under the curve. The smaller vectors showed saturation with the % eGFP positive cells. However, the intensity had a linear relationship with all vectors. The % eGFP positive cell parameter only reflected a positive or negative condition, not the strength. The intensity reflected the strength of gene expression. The % GFP positive cell measurement can be used as an indicator of infectivity. However, the intensity may be a better indicator for the transduction efficiency. EC50 data are summarized in Table S1. All data has been presented in Table S2.

As an orthogonal approach, we confirmed the imaging results by flow cytometry and analyzed by the parallel line method.43 The data showed that the high-content screening method has a very strong correlation to the flow cytometry method, even with a different data analysis method (Figures 2I and 2J).

We performed a correlation analysis of vector activity (determined by the area under the curve with intensity mean) with vector size using Pearson’s correlation analysis. It showed a very strong correlation (Figure 2K). However, there was an inflection point at around 4.7–4.8 Kb. There is a near perfect linear correlation between vector size and activity after excluding the vectors larger than 4.9 Kb (Figure 2L).

Development of RNA-DNA hybrid capture-multiplex MSD method

To investigate the integrity of encapsidated rAAV genomes and quantify intact versus truncated species, a method capable of characterizing single-stranded plus and minus rAAV genomes thoroughly and separately is required. There are two reliable methods to quantify ssDNA: branched DNA44 and RNA-DNA hybrid capture techniques.45,46 We combined the RNA-DNA hybrid capture with MSD’s electrochemiluminescence detection technology to develop a novel method to quantify intact versus truncated AAV ssDNA. Initially, we tested using S9.6 antibody or streptavidin to capture the hybrids of RNA probe with target DNA (Figure 3A). Figures S5A and S5B demonstrated that these methods could detect plasmid DNA over a wide range with strong signals. With these methods, the hybridization of RNA probe with target DNA was performed in a separate plate. Next, we tested the idea by coating the RNA probe on the MSD plate and using it to capture the target DNA through hybridization directly in the MSD plate (Figure 3A). Figure 3C illustrates that five biotin conjugated RNA probes coated on a streptavidin plate were able to capture and quantify the denatured plasmid DNA over a wide range. The background signal in wells where the probe was omitted was very low. The RNA oligos were titrated, and the data indicated that the signal was stable over a wide range for most of the tested probes from 0.5 to 33.3 nM (Figures S5C and S5D).

To comprehensively study rAAV genome integrity, it is essential to specifically target multiple regions of the single strand rAAV genome, ideally using a multiplex format. Figure 3B illustrates our RNA-DNA hybrid capture-multiplex MSD method strategy. Initially, we tested the MSD U-Plex (multiplex MSD assay) under single-plex hybridization conditions at 65°C; however, the signal was significantly low. We hypothesized that the high hybridization temperature may have affected the U-plex linker affinity. By lowering the hybridization temperature from 65°C to 42°C, multiple RNA probes effectively detected DNA with both a broad range and high sensitivity in the same well. We then assessed potential interference among multiple probes by testing four probe/linker pairs individually or in various combinations. Figures 3D–3G show signals at four distinct locations with corresponding probe/linker pairs. Figure 3D presents the signal at position 1 (linker-1) with RNA probe A. It is evident that wells plated only with probe A exhibited a signal, and the presence of other probes did not affect the signal of probe A. Wells coated with probes other than probe A also did not generate any signal. Similar results were observed for the three other linkers and probes, confirming the specificity of the signal and the absence of interference between different probes (Figures 3E–3G).

We successfully integrated RNA-DNA hybrid capture with MSD technology to quantify DNA in a multiplex format. Figure 3H illustrates seven probes coated in the same well, each capable of detecting plasmid DNA over a range extending up to three logs. Over 100 RNA probes targeting sequences from multiple vectors containing various genes of interest and promoters were assessed, with approximately 90% of the probes demonstrating strong signals (data not shown). However, approximately 10% exhibited low signals (data not shown), primarily in AU-rich sequences. Probes that are susceptible to forming hairpin or dimer structures may also display reduced signals.

Additionally, we generated three short RNA probes of 20, 40, and 60 nucleotides based on the sequence of M-3, and examined the effect of probe length on the signal. The data indicated that the 60-nucleotide probe produced a signal comparable to the original 80-nucleotide probe. In contrast, the 40-nucleotide probe showed a weaker signal, and the 20-nucleotide probe had a very faint signal (Figure S6). All RNA probes are listed in Table S2 and Figure S4.

The data indicated that rAAV viruses have a good linear range across most of the dilutions tested.

We applied the RNA-DNA hybrid capture-multiplex MSD method to determine the percentage (%) of intact AAV genome in all 13 rAAV eGFP vectors used in this study. Figure 5A shows how the plus and minus strand rAAV genome is generally packaged and the % intact rAAV genome is mathematically determined. The probes (P3′ end and M3′ end) targeting the 3′ end of the plus and minus determine the total copy number of the rAAV genome and the probes (P5′ end and M5′ end) targeting the 5′ end of the plus and minus strand quantitate the intact rAAV genomes. rAAV viruses from 13 constructs were tested two to three times with the average % intact being determined for each vector. The same Vg titer was used to infect HEK293 or SH SY5Y cells, and the relative activity was vastly different among different vectors. Figures 5B and 5C show that there is a very strong correlation between relative activity or infectivity with % intact rAAV genome within the two different cell lines by Pearson’s correlation analyses.40 We also tested rAAV samples harboring various genes with different promoters. Our method adequately quantitated the rAAV genome integrity in these samples (data not shown).

Discussion

rAAV is emerging as a leading choice for viral vector-mediated gene therapy due to its ability to transduce various cell types and tissues, coupled with its sustained gene expression and relatively safe profile. Vg titer is the most widely used parameter for dosing rAAV in both preclinical and clinical studies. However, rAAV viruses are highly heterogeneous populations, and Vg titer cannot accurately reflect viral genome integrity. Thus, innovative analytical methods are urgently needed to characterize and accurately quantify rAAV genome integrity in the AAV gene therapy field. At the beginning of method developments, we noticed a lack of vectors with varying activity levels to properly evaluate new methodologies, test data analysis tools, and establish meaningful statistical correlations among different analytical methods and functional data. Accordingly, we developed a series of vectors with a wide range of potency and efficacy, as illustrated in Figures 1 and S2. Both SH SY5Y and HEK293T cell lines demonstrated significant differences in eGFP intensity across different vector sizes, with smaller vectors showing greater activity. This enabled comprehensive evaluation of our high-throughput high-content imaging screening system. Using this system, we generated 24 sets of 384-well data (Figure S3), then evaluated four-parameter curve fitting and explored the feasibility of three-parameter curve fitting by fixing the top to the assay plate’s maximum value. Our findings showed that four-parameter curve fitting was unsuitable for analyzing rAAV activity in the functional assays, particularly given intensity data that never reached a plateau. Notably, even when the maximum varied under different conditions, fixing the top in three-parameter curve fitting yielded consistent EC50 values across measurements, experiments, and cell lines for the same vector. The analysis by the Bland-Altman plot (Figures 2G and 2H) shows that EC50 values were reproducible between two experiments with SH SY5Y cells and were comparably consistent between SH SY5Y and HEK293T cells. Three-parameter curve fitting proved reliable for analyzing potency data and determining EC50 values. However, because EC50 measures potency rather than efficacy, we introduced the area under the curve to assess vector activity relative to concentration response. Area under the curve provides a single metric incorporating both potency and efficacy, making it ideal when EC50 and Emax values are challenging to determine. Our data indicated that area under the curve effectively captures relative vector activity, encompassing both potency and efficacy. Intensity mean and sum metrics provided similar results; thus, analyzing either one suffices. Magnifications of 10× and 20× yielded consistent intensity results, though 20× was superior for % positive cell readout, likely due to higher resolution and sensitivity. For correlation analyses with other analytical methods, we used % eGFP positive cell and intensity mean data from 20× magnification. Independent validation using orthogonal flow cytometry, performed by a separate research group, confirmed the reliability of our imaging assay and data analysis methods, including the three-parameter curve fitting and area under the curve approaches. Our studies showcased how a series of vectors, combined with high-throughput assays and rigorous data analysis methods, can refine rAAV activity evaluation. While we utilized eGFP vectors in this study, the high-throughput, high-content imaging screening system is adaptable for non-eGFP vectors through antibody staining or targeting vector DNA/mRNA.

Functional data highlighted that Vg titer is not a reliable measure of rAAV product potency, as our imaging assay using identical Vg titers for 13 vectors revealed significant potency discrepancies. This discrepancy suggests that rAAV genome integrity plays a crucial role. Published research indicates that intact (full-length) genomes with ITRs at both ends are necessary for stable circular episomal structures in the nucleus, which is essential for sustained transgene expression.7 Our surprise finding of a 30-fold activity difference between the smallest (3.05 Kb) and largest (6.0 Kb) vectors underscores that full-length genome formation, including the eGFP expression cassette and two ITRs, is critical. The single-strand rAAV genome is packaged into viral particles from 3′ to 5′ ends via rep protein ATPase-driven positive process.12 Initial fast packaging within 2 Kb is followed by a slower phase, potentially increasing truncation rates for larger vectors and resulting in activity loss. Vg titer methods do not accurately capture such truncations, emphasizing the need to determine rAAV genome integrity accurately for rAAV-mediated gene therapy. Biophysical methods such as AUC, AEX, cryo-EM, and CD-MS partially address genome integrity but are semi-quantitative. These methods are unable to decipher rAAV genome compositions and heterogeneous populations. Notably, ITR sequences, at only 145 bp, remain challenging for these methods to distinguish between truncated and intact genomes due to limited resolution. Emerging methods such as NGS (Long-Read Nanopore and PacBio SMRT sequencing) and multiplex digital PCR show promise but present hurdles such as NGS’s complex library preparation. The PacBio SMRT sequencing method needs double-stranded DNA as its template. The single strand rAAV genome presents multiple conformations and is not a linear, open-ended molecule but rather forms T-shape hairpin structures at the 3′ end and 5′ end through the ITRs. Although dsDNA could be made through annealing the plus and minus strands, most of the double strand products could be partially due to the heterogeneous nature of rAAV genomes as multiple configurations could form (Figure S7). During library preparation and template repair, the 3′ end ITR acts as a primer to repair partial double-stranded samples and generate complete double-stranded DNA templates. Oversized single strands (3′ end overhang) that cannot be repaired are cleaved. This process changes the nature of the rAAV genome and affects its integrity. On the Oxford Nanopore sequencing platform, two primary library preparation methods are used: ligase-based and transposase-based methods. The ligase-based method requires blunt-end double-stranded DNA as the template and involves new DNA synthesis.35 The transposase-based method can sequence single-strand DNA templates but shows a strong bias toward double-strand DNA templates and involves DNA fragmentation. Both PacBio SMRT and oxford Nanopore sequencing methods require complex data analysis and have biases related to DNA size and sequence. Both platforms prefer longer DNA fragments (1,500 vs. 200 bp),33 and GC content35 impacts the performance of these platforms. These biases further complicate quantitation. NGS might be able to profile the rAAV genome such as validating vector sequences and identifying species and hotspots of rAAV genome truncations, but accurate quantitation of rAAV genome integrity could be challenging due to the need for library preparation and potential biases. Multiplex digital PCR faces challenges in sample lysis, the amplification efficiency of amplicons near the ITRs and data analysis. Our internal data indicated that the amplicons within 900 bp range of the ITRs only had 40% to 80% amplification efficiency of expected preventing accurate quantitation of rAAV integrity (Pla et al., unpublished data). Another issue is that rAAV plus and minus strands are not always equally packaged into rAAV particles. Currently, digital PCR is unable to distinguish the plus vs. minus strand rAAV genomes. When developing analytical methods for studying rAAV genome integrity, it is essential to consider that DNA replication occurs from 5′ to 3′ through DNA synthesis, whereas rAAV genome packaging involves translocation of the DNA replicate intermediates into the AAV capsid from 3′ to 5′.12 To analyze rAAV genome integrity effectively, a method capable of accurately, separately, and comprehensively quantifying both the plus and minus strands from the 3′ to the 5′ end is required. Traditional single strand specific radiolabeled probes with dot blot or Southern blot approaches offer low throughput and semi-quantitative results.14,17 By integrating these principles with new technologies such as anti-RNA-DNA antibody hybrid capture and MSD, we have developed a novel RNA-DNA hybrid capture-multiplex MSD method to quantify DNA with superior accuracy and sensitivity. Figure 3A illustrates that this innovative method is straightforward, as it eliminates the need for DNA extraction, annealing, repairing, ligation, or amplification. Additionally, the fragmentation of DNA caused by the heating process theoretically does not affect its quantitation. Figures 3C and S5 demonstrate the method’s effectiveness across a wide dose range using various RNA probes. Further progression to multiplex MSD allowed successful quantitation of 10 targets in the same well in a 96-well plate format. Figure 4 highlights the method’s precision in detecting individual rAAV genome plus and minus strands separately, confirming rAAV genome packaging patterns: the package is 3′ end to 5′ end and has increased truncations on the 5′ end. At the same time, spike-in plasmid DNA showed consistent copy numbers with different RNA probes. The percentage of intact rAAV genome (Figure 5) by the high throughput RNA-DNA hybrid capture-multiplex MSD strongly correlated with activity determined using high-throughput imaging assays across cell lines and conditions. Data in Figure 6 indicate that purification can reduce empty particles but does little for improving rAAV genome integrity, suggesting partial particle removal limitations. These studies demonstrated that the RNA-DNA hybrid capture-multiplex MSD method can accurately characterize rAAV genome and determine the integrity in AAV products.

rAAV genome truncation can happen at every level of AAV production as described in the introduction. However, the truncation could be classified into two categories that are random and vector sequence specific hotspot mediated truncations. The hotspot-mediated truncations could happen via two major mechanisms. First, hairpin-like structures11 and/or terminal resolution-like site (trs-like)47 can cause the truncation of DNA replicating intermediates during rAAV genome replication. Second, special secondary structures of the single genome prevents its packaging into the pre-formed rAAV capsid particle.14 The integrity of the ITR significantly affects the integrity of the rAAV genome.13 Figure 6B shows the % intact between plus and minus strand was not equal. It indicates the hotspot-mediated truncation, but it could not distinguish the replication vs. package level. Preliminary data from Figure 4 shows that the signal quickly dropped between the last two probes near the 5′ end of the plus strand, and the signal was increased with the second probe near the 3′ end of the minus strand with all four vectors tested. This finding indicates hairpin-mediated rAAV genome truncation during DNA replication as reported in literature since we used the same promoter and had a similar sequence at this region.11 However, additional comprehensive studies with more probes combining with DNA sequencing methods at this region are needed to identify the hotspot. Since the % intact between minus and plus strands are quite different, there are opportunities for improving the vector design.

The single plex with three formats and multiplex RNA-DNA hybrid-capture MSD methods described here have applications that extend beyond the AAV gene therapy field. These methods could be future modified such as using DNA probes to capture DNA sample and detect signal by RNA probes, or RNA probes to capture RNA and detect them by DNA probes. Which could increase the specificity or be used to study integrity of targets. It is useful for characterizing and quantifying DNA and RNA, particularly oligonucleotides, in both in vitro and in vivo samples, aiding RNA and DNA-based therapies.

One interesting outcome was identifying the correlation of vector size vs. functional data from the imaging assay, which had a clear reflection point at 4.7–4.8 Kb—the wild-type AAV size. Excluding vectors over 4.9 Kb yielded a surprisingly excellent linear relationship between vector size and activity. It clearly illustrated that the AAV biological design of the package size of AAV is 4.7 Kb. This is another aspect that supports the reliability of imaging assay and data analyses methods. Any vectors over 4.7 Kb would be packaged through a different mechanism at a lower rate. It provides an important clue into designing vectors with high transduction efficiency.

It should be noted that designing probes for both ITRs is not feasible due to their similarity, positioning the probes close to the 5′ end ITR may slightly overestimate intact rAAV genome percentages. It will be interesting to explore how the virus production systems, SF9 vs. HEK293, impact the rAAV genome integrity. The heterogeneity and integrity of rAAV genomes are more complex than previously understood. Figure S7 only illustrates this complexity under normal conditions. Full and partial double strand rAAV genomes with a palindromic structure or snapbacks9,48 produced by regular single-strand AAV vectors and especially self-complementary AAV vectors present significant challenges for the development of analytical methods, including our novel approach. This may require further development. While NGS, multiplex digital PCR, and other techniques such as AUC and alkaline gel cannot accurately quantify the integrity of the rAAV genome, they remain useful for profiling its heterogeneity. By combining these methods, we can better understand the mechanisms causing rAAV truncation and improve vector design. Future investigations also require establishing the correlation between % intact vs. transduction efficiency in vivo. Any new method must stand the test of time, and we are looking forward to these methods being tested broadly, applying improvements, and driving rAAV gene therapy forward.

In conclusion, we developed vectors with varied activities useful for analytical method advancements, high-throughput high-content imaging screening for accurate rAAV activity and potency assessment and confirmed the suitability of top-fixed three-parameter curve fitting and the area under the curve for functional data analyses. Our innovative high-throughput RNA-DNA hybrid capture multiplex MSD method effectively characterizes and measures rAAV genome integrity with straightforward sample preparation. This represents a crucial advancement in enhancing AAV design and production, thereby ensuring the development of safe and effective products.

Materials and methods

Baculovirus process

AAV9 vectors containing eGFP gene with different sizes of stuffer DNA were produced using the baculovirus production system. The AAV genome was cloned into a shuttle plasmid, which was used to generate a recombinant baculovirus by transformation of DH10Bac-competent cells and transfection of the purified Bacmid DNA into Sf9 cells. Sf9 cells were co-infected with the recombinant baculoviruses containing AAV-eGFP genome(s) and AAV2rep/AAV9 cap inserts. Three days post infection, Sf9 cells were harvested and lysed in Sf9 lysis buffer (0.5% V/V TX-100; Sigma-Aldrich, 33 mM Tris-base, 2 mM MgCl2, and 200 mM NaCl) containing benzonase (50 U/mL; Millipore). Genomic DNA was digested by incubation at 37°C for 1 h, after which cell debris was removed by centrifugation. Full AAV vector particles in the cleared lysates were purified by AAV9 affinity ligand resin (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Vg titer was determined by ddPCR with amplicon in eGFP and Woodchuck Hepatitis Virus Posttranscriptional Regulatory Element (WPRE) regions. Vg titer from WPRE was used for dosing in all following studies.

Cell culture

HEK293T cell line was obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) (Manassas, VA). SH SY5Y cell line was licensed from MilliporeSigma. SH SY5Y cells were maintained in T75 Flask in Cell culture media (DMEM/F12, Gibco, 11330-032; supplemented with 10% heat inactivated fetal bovine serum, Gibco, 10082-147 and 20 mM HEPES, Gibco, 15630-080) in a humidified incubator at 37°C and 5% CO2. 384-well CellCarrier ultra microplates (Revvity, currently named PhenoPlate 384-well microplate) were used for the imaging assay. For the experiments with HEK293T cells, 384-well CellCarrier ultra microplates were coated with 25 μL of 50 μg/mL Poly-L-Lysine (MilliporeSigma) in DPBS with Ca2+ and Mg2+ overnight at 4°C. The coated plates were rinsed with 50 μL/well DMEM/F12 once. HEK293T cells were thawed from liquid nitrogen, suspended in cell culture media, seeded in the Poly-L-Lysine coated plates at 8,000 cells/well and incubated in a humidified incubator at 37°C and 5% CO2 overnight. For the experiments with SH SY5Y cells, SH SY5Y cells were detached from the T75 flask and resuspended in cell culture media, seeded in the 384-well CellCarrier ultra microplates (without coating) at 12,000 cells/well and incubated in a humidified incubator at 37°C and 5% CO2 overnight. Viruses were prediluted to the same concentration, and then 1.6-fold serial dilutions were performed from column 3 to 22 or 23 to generate a 20–21 dose-response curve in a 384-well plate with the cell culture media. The cell culture plates were changed with 20 μL/well fresh cell culture media, 20 μL media from the virus dilution preparation plate was added, and then incubated in a humidified incubator at 37°C and 5% CO2 for 24 h. On the second day, the SH SY5Y cells were changed into 60 μL/well fresh cell culture media and incubated in a humidified incubator at 37°C and 5% CO2 for another 3 days. For the HEK293T cells, cells were changed into 60 μL/well fresh cell culture media containing 0.1 μg/mL Hoechst 33342 to stain the nuclei (minimum Hoechst 33342 was added to avoid toxicity effect). The images were acquired using the Opera Phenix High Content Screening System in confocal mode with the objectives 10× air NA 0.3 and 20× water NA 1.0. The fluorophores were detected with the following excitation and emission (Ex/Em) wavelengths: Hoechst 33342 (405/435–480) and eGFP (488/500–550). After image acquisition, HEK293T cells were incubated in a humidified incubator at 37°C and 5% CO2 for another 3 days. On virus infection day 4, for the HEK293T cells, the images were re-acquired as the same protocol described previously. For the SH SY5Y cells, cells were added 1 μg/mL Hoechst 33342. The image acquisition was performed as the same protocol for HEK293T cells.

All raw fluorescence measurements were analyzed using the PerkinElmer Columbus Image Analysis Software (recently renamed Revvity Signals Image Artist). Individual cell nuclei were identified using the Hoechst 33342 channel. The background signal of eGFP was determined by the no virus infection wells (negative control wells), then eGFP positive cells were determined based on threshold intensity above background. % eGFP positive cells was calculated by the positive cell number divided by the nuclei count determined from Hoechst 33342. eGFP intensity mean and sum in the whole-cell cytoplasm and nuclei masks were quantitated. The entire well eGFP sum pixel intensity was also normalized by the nuclear count. The average intensity background of the eGFP intensity mean and sum was determined across all negative control wells, respectively. All eGFP intensity mean and sum values per well was subtracted to the respective average intensity background. % positive cell numbers and background subtracted intensity mean and sum were used for the curve-fitting analysis.

Curve-fitting analysis

For each vector, a single curve was fit to data that were normalized as described previously. All values were initially fit to a four-parameter logistic equation using GraphPad software (v.10). However, some vectors had low potency and efficacy. Only the initial rising part of the curve was measurable. The top never reaches a plateau. We explored the three-parameter curve fitting. With the data of % positive cell, since every vector theoretically would be able to reach 100% if virus was dosed high enough, so the top was fixed to 100%. With the intensity data, all vectors could not reach a plateau. The maximum effect could not be defined. We fixed the top to the highest value in the plate. We wanted to use our datasets to evaluate if this method is practical. To access the agreement of different methods, the MSR was determined by Bland-Altman plots. We also introduced the area under the curve to compare the activity among different vectors by plotting the % positive cells or intensity against the concentration of the virus at linear scale. The area under the curve was determined for each vector. The percentage relative to the vector 3.05KB was determined by the individual area under the curve divided by the area under the curve of vector 3.05KB.

RNA-DNA hybrid capture-multiplex MSD

Biotin conjugated RNA oligos with 20–80 nucleotide length were synthesized by Integration DNA Technologies. Sequences of all biotin conjugated RNA Oligos are listed in Table S2. Buffer: 2× hybridization buffer: 1 M BES, 0.5 M sodium acetate, 0.2% Tween 20, 2.5 mM EDTA, 10% dextran sulfate, and 0.025% sodium azide; PBST: PBS + 0.1% Tween 20; PBSTE: DPBS (Hyclone, SH30028.02) + 0.1% Tween 20 + 5mM EDTA (pH 8); and PBSTEG: PBSTE + 5% goat serum. Anti RNA-DNA antibody (Anti-DNA-RNA Hybrid, clone S9.6, MillporeSigma, MABE1095); Diluent 100 (MSD, R50AA-4); Sulfo-Tag Streptavidin, 500 μg/mL (MSD R32AD-1); Sulfo-Tag Goat antimouse antibody, 500 μg/mL (MSD, R32AC-1); MSD Read Buffer T (4×) with Surfactant (MSD, R92TC-3); MSD Gold read buffer B (MSD, R60AM-2); MSD Multi-Array 96-well plate (MSD, L15XB-3); MSD GOLD 96-well Streptavidin SECTOR Plate (MSD, L15SA-1); U-Plex Development Pack, 6-assay, Sector plate (MSD, K15231N-2), U-Plex Development Pack, 7-assay, Sector plate (MSD, K15232N-2); and U-Plex Development Pack, 10-assay, Sector plate (MSD, K15235N-2).

HC2 CT-ID DNA test v.2.0 (QIAGEN 5135-1220) kit was used to confirm the concept of RNA-DNA hybrid capture method for quantitating rAAV genome by replacing the RNA probe in the kit with 80-nucleotide length RNA oligos following the protocol in the kit.

Three strategies were used to develop the MSD based RNA-DNA capture method at the beginning (Figure 3A).

Preparation of DNA

Dilute plasmid DNA with PCR dilution buffer (1× TE + 0.01% Tween 80) and transfer 100 μL well into a PCR plate and add 50 μL of denature reagent (from the HC2 CT-ID DNA test kit), seal and vortex the plate, quickly spin down and incubate at 65°C (water bath) for 45 min, then cool down on ice and quickly spin down.

Strategy one

Use coated anti RNA-DNA antibody S9.6 to capture the RNA-DNA hybrid, then detect by Streptavidin conjugated Sulfo-Tag.

Coat the assay plate: Incubate the MSD Multi-Array 96-well plate with100 μL/well Anti RNA-DNA antibody S9.6 in DPBS with 1:500 dilution at 4°C overnight. On the second day, wash the plate with PBST four times, and then block the plate with PBSTEG for 2 h.

Hybridization of RNA oligo probe and DNA: dilute the biotin-RNA oligo probe with hybridization buffer and mix very well, the final concentration was 3.3 nM, dispense into a PCR plate at 50 μL/well and then add 50 μL denatured plasmid DNA, seal and vortex, spin down and incubate at 65°C for 1 h. Rinse the assay plate with PBSTE once, transfer the 100 μL annealed DNA/RNA hybrid into the assay plate and incubate at room temperature (RT) for 1 h. Wash plate with PBST four times, incubate with Sulfo-Tag Streptavidin in PBSTEG (12 mL of PBSTEG + 24 μL of Sulfo-Tag Streptavidin) at RT for 1 h. Wash four times with PBST and rinse the assay plate with 200 μL of PBSTE once, decant the PBSTE and add 150 μL/well of 2× MSD Read Buffer T and load onto MSD QuickPlex 120 mm to read.

Strategy two

Use streptavidin coated plate to capture the Biotin-RNA/DNA hybrid, then detect by anti RNA-DNA antibody in conjunction with Sulfo-Tag Goat anti-mouse antibody.

Block one MSD Gold 96-well Streptavidin SECTOR Plate with 200 μL/well PBSTEG for 30 min at room temperature, then rinse the plate with 200 μL/well PBSTE once. Prepare the hybridization of RNA oligo probe and DNA as described previously and transfer the RNA-DNA hybrid into the assay plate and incubate at RT for 1 h. Wash with PBST four times, and incubate with anti-RNA-DNA antibody S9.6 (12 mL PBSTEG + 24 μL of anti-DNA-RNA antibody, 100 μL/well) in PBSTEG for 1 h. Wash with PBST four times, and incubate with Sulfo-Tag anti-mouse antibody in PBSTEG (12 mL PBSTEG + 24 μL of Sulfo-Tag goat anti-mouse antibody, 100 μL/well) for 1 h. Wash four times with PBST, rinse the assay plate with 200 μL of PBSTE once, decant the PBSTE and add 150 μL of MSD GOLD Read Buffer B to each well. Read the plate on MSD QuickPlex 120 mm immediately.

Strategy three

Coat RNA oligo to capture DNA by hybridization in the assay plate, then detect by anti RNA-DNA antibody in conjunction with Sulfo-Tag Goat anti-mouse antibody.

Dilute biotin-RNA oligo probe with PCR diluent to 3.3 nM, add 50 μL/well to the MSD GOLD 96-well Streptavidin SECTOR Plate and coat for 1 h at room temperature. Wash the plate with PBSTE three times, add 50 μL of hybridization buffer and 50 μL denatured plasmid DNA, and incubate at 65°C for 1 h. Wash with PBSTE three times, add anti RNA-DNA antibody 100 μL/well (12 mL PBSTEG + 24 μL of antibody), and incubate at RT for 1 h. Wash with PBST three times, and incubate with anti-mouse antibody in PBSTEG (12 mL PBSTEG + 24 μL of Sulfo-Tag goat anti-mouse antibody, 100 μL/well) for 1 h. Wash four times with PBST and add 150 μL of MSD GOLD Read Buffer B to each well. Read the plate on MSD QuickPlex 120 mm immediately.

High throughput RNA-DNA hybrid capture-multiplex MSD method

Coat U-Plex MSD plate with RNA oligos

Dilute 4 μL of 10 μM probe into 200 μL Diluent 100 buffer (200 nM, final 80 nM) and mix it very well. Add 300 μL linker and mix it very well and incubate at RT (slightly shake) for 30 min, and then add 200 μL stop buffer and incubate at RT for another 30 min. Combine all ten probe-linker pairs together and mix them very well, plate 50 μL/well into the MSD U-Plex plate, and incubate at RT for 1 h.

DNA standard and virus preparation

Dilute plasmid DNA standard and virus with PCR diluent buffer. Transfer 50 μL/well of 1 N NaOH to a PCR plate and add 100 μL of diluted DNA or virus and mix about 4 times by pipetting, incubate at 95°C for 30 min, then quickly cool to 4°C and hold until to next step.

Hybridization and capture

Wash coated assay plate with 500 μL PBST (overflow) four times using the plate washer, add 50 μL of 2× hybridization buffer to the coated assay plate, transfer 50 μL of denatured sample to the coated assay plate, seal the plate very well, vortex thoroughly, spin down at 2,000 rpm for 1 min, and incubate at 42°C for 90 min on a lid heated Microplate Vortexer (Benchmark, Incu-Mixer MP H6004) with shaking speed at 600 rpm.

Detection

Wash assay plate four times with 500 μL PBST, and incubate with 50 μL anti-RNA-DNA antibody S9.6 at 1:750 dilution in PBSTEG for 1 h at RT. Wash assay plate four times with 500 μL PBST, and incubate with 50 μL Sulfo-Tag Goat anti mouse antibody at 1:500 dilution in PBSTEG for 1 h at RT. Wash assay plate four times with 500 μL PBST. Rinse once with 200 μL PBSTE, dump the PBSTE, add 100 μL MSD Gold read buffer B, and read the plate on an MSD QuickPlex SQ.

Data analyses

Use MSD Discovery Workbench to analyze the data, plot standard curve with samples, and copy the data into an Excel sheet. Check the linearity compatibility, only the points in the linear range and the % of lowest/highest = or >70% will be used to determine the average concentration of test samples.

Flow cytometry

For the flow cytometry analysis, HEK293T cells were seeded at 55,000 cells per well in a 48-well plate (Corning). The following day, cells were transduced with the virus at specified MOIs. Two days post-infection, cells were detached using TrypLE (Gibco) and stained with DAPI (Sigma D9542) at a 1:10,000 dilution to assess cell viability. Fluorescence was analyzed on the Cytek Northern Lights flow cytometer, incorporating appropriate single stain controls for unmixing. DAPI fluorescence was detected using the violet laser at an excitation wavelength of 405 nm with a 473/15 bandpass filter, while eGFP was detected via the blue laser at 488 nm excitation and a 525/17 bandpass filter. eGFP-positive cells were gated off live, non-transduced cells. Parallel line analysis was conducted to determine relative potency by correlating the viral load with the percentage of eGFP-positive cells, constraining it to the average slope.
