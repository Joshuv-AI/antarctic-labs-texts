Development of a paediatric asthma shared decision-making tool: intervention design and evaluation
Authors: Casper E. W. Gijsen, Carolien van Rossem, Regina The, Jean W. M. Muris, Marieke W. P. van Horck, Edward Dompeling
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAsthma is the most common chronic disease in children, yet adherence to treatment remains poor due to barriers in communication and self-management. Shared decision-making (SDM) can improve adherence, but no decision aid currently exists for paediatric asthma. This gap limits children’s engagement in treatment decisions, highlighting the need for a tailored patient decision aid. The aim was to develop a decision aid to support SDM between children with asthma, their parents, and healthcare providers (HCPs), followed by an evaluation of its acceptability and usability.MethodsA user-centred, iterative co-design approach was used, involving children aged 6–18 years with physician-diagnosed asthma, their parents, and healthcare providers (including paediatricians, asthma nurses, and general practitioners). An initial needs assessment identified key challenges in asthma-related decision-making. These insights informed eight structured co-creation sessions focusing on identifying decision points, exploring preferences, and shaping content and format of the decision aid. Usability and acceptability were tested with end-users, and the final version was independently reviewed against the IPDAS checklist by two researchers to assess quality and completeness.ResultsA needs assessment (n = 37) with HCPs, patients, and caregivers identified substantial information gaps, with many patients and parents unaware of treatment options or potential side effects. HCPs emphasised the potential value of a decision aid in improving information delivery and encouraging SDM. In eight co-creation sessions, a multidisciplinary group (n = 18) collaborated to develop and refine two complementary decision aids. Feedback highlighted improved clarity, age-appropriate design, and relevance for real-life consultations. In the final phases, both decision aids were tested for acceptability and usability, showing high user satisfaction with minor revisions made (n = 32). A quality assessment was conducted by two independent reviewers. Both tools met all 12 IPDAS criteria. These results confirm their quality and suitability for implementation in practice.ConclusionThe decision aids were well received by patients, parents and HCPs and met IPDAS criteria. They address critical unmet needs in paediatric asthma care by supporting informed decision-making. These tools have the potential to improve the quality of clinical consultations and promote more patient-centred care in the treatment of childhood asthma.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-025-03235-w.

Background

Asthma remains the most common chronic disease among children, significantly impacting their quality of life and daily activities. Achieving good asthma control is crucial to minimise these effects, as uncontrolled asthma can compromise lung development and long-term well-being [1]. Despite the availability of effective treatments, adherence remains a significant challenge. Barriers such as parental perceptions of disease severity, concerns about medication side effects, and limited symptom recognition and self-management often hinder optimal asthma management. In addition, children themselves report barriers such as forgetfulness, embarrassment about using inhalers in public, lack of understanding of the long-term benefits of treatment, and inconsistent routines [2]. These child-reported barriers often hinder optimal asthma management [3].

Shared decision-making (SDM) is an evidence-based approach and shows potential to improve adherence and asthma outcomes by actively involving children and their families in treatment decisions [4, 5]. SDM has been associated with improved asthma control, fewer exacerbations, better quality of life, and enhanced communication between families and healthcare professionals [6–9]. Patient decision aids (PtDAs) support SDM by helping patients understand options, clarify values, and make informed choices [10]. Digital tools and portals, such as eAAPs and MyAsthma, have shown promising effects in paediatric asthma care by embedding SDM into routine clinical workflows [6, 8].

While PtDAs are available in fields such as oncology, cardiology, and diabetes most asthma PtDAs focus on adults, with very limited tools available for paediatric patients, particularly in the Dutch context [11–14]. This gap restricts the ability of children and families to engage in meaningful discussions with healthcare providers. Moreover, existing tools rarely address the developmental and communication needs of children. To address this gap, a child-specific asthma PtDA is needed one that supports shared decision-making by empowering children to express preferences, improving communication with HCPs, and helping families navigate treatment options [15, 16]. The International Patient Decision Aid Standards (IPDAS), a set of internationally recognized standards for developing patient decision aids, was applied to assess the final product’s clarity, balance, and evidence-based content, ensuring its quality and usability [17].

The aim of this study was to develop a PtDA for children with asthma and evaluate its acceptability and usability among children, parents, and healthcare professionals. This PtDA was designed to improve understanding of treatment options, enhance communication, and empower families to take an active role in care decisions. By addressing a key gap in paediatric asthma management, this initiative may reduce the burden of asthma in children’s daily lives and serve as a model for improving patient engagement in other chronic disease contexts. The user-centred development process ensures relevance and transferability to broader healthcare settings.

Needs assessment (Phase A)

This phase was part of a broader published needs assessment focused on challenges in paediatric asthma care [18]. Details are summarised here, and full findings are available in the cited publication.

Design and data collection

Semi-structured interviews were conducted to understand the decision-making and informational needs of patients and HCPs. Interviews, led by trained researchers (CG/CR), followed a topic guide and explored decision experiences, treatment preferences, and unmet needs. All interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and thematically analysed by two independent researchers. Data collection continued until thematic saturation was reached.

Participants and inclusion criteria

Patients were eligible if they were under 18 years of age, received asthma care, and were able to participate in Dutch-language interviews. Healthcare professionals (HCPs) were eligible if they were involved in paediatric asthma care, proficient in Dutch, and willing to participate. Eligible HCPs included paediatric pulmonologists, general paediatricians, asthma nurses, and general practitioners.

Patients were recruited via referrals from the steering group and snowball sampling. HCPs were recruited through purposive and snowball sampling from both academic and non-academic hospitals, as well as general practices.

In total, 37 participants were included: 15 paediatric asthma patients (aged 6–17) and 22 HCPs. Oral informed consent was obtained from all participants (METC 2023–3635).

Co-creation sessions (Phase B)

Eight co-creation sessions were held over a 20-month period, facilitated by a researcher (CR) and a PtDA development specialist (RT, ZorgKeuzeLab (www.zorgkeuzelab.nl)). Sessions took place both online and in person and focused on the PtDA’s content, structure, language, implementation strategy, and design.

Participants

Eight of the 37 Phase A participants took part in Phase B, including seven HCPs and one parent of a child with asthma. Additionally, the co-creation team involved one patient representative, two national patient representatives from the Lung Foundation Netherlands, and two others members were parents of a child with asthma. The team also involve two HCP’s where also parents of children with asthma, contributing a patient-centered perspective and actively relaying feedback to and from their respective communities. While the number of direct patient participants was limited, this structured involvement of patient representatives helped ensure that the broader patient voice was reflected.

Activities and process

Detailed field notes and audio recordings were used to capture session content. Sessions included structured feedback rounds, prioritisation of content, iterative prototyping, and joint review of language and visuals. Findings from Phase A directly informed session content. Adjustments were made to ensure age-appropriate communication, simplified wording, and alignment with evidence-based care. A steering group, composed of project leads, HCPs, and one patient representative, reviewed major design decisions.

Acceptability and usability testing (Phase C)

The acceptability and usability testing phase aimed to assess how effectively the PtDA met the needs of both healthcare professionals (HCPs), paediatric patients, and their parents, ensuring the tool was practical, user-friendly, and suitable for clinical integration.

Participants and recruitment

In total, 32 participants were involved in Phase C, including 24 HCPs, 8 children with asthma. Of these, 7 HCPs and 0 children had previously participated in Phase A or B.

Parent and child involvement

While children directly interacted with the PtDA, parents played a crucial role in reflecting on accessibility, relevance, and age-appropriateness. This dual perspective enriched understanding of how the PtDA is used in practice by families. In reporting, we have clearly distinguished feedback from children and parents where applicable.

Quality assessment using IPDAS criteria (Phase D)

Compatibility with IPDAS criteria

Final PtDA was systematically evaluated the 12 minimum criteria set by the International Patient Decision Aids Standards (IPDAS). This evaluation was conducted by two authors (CG, CR) and assessed key aspects such as clarity, balanced presentation, evidence base, and accessibility (appendix 1).

The IPDAS framework includes six qualifying standards (required to classify a tool as a decision aid) and six certification standards, aimed at reducing bias and promoting transparency. The PtDA fulfilled all minimum criteria, confirming that it meets international standards for transparency, usability, and credibility. This evaluation further supported the PtDA’s ongoing refinement and its suitability for clinical implementation and meaningful patient engagement.

Data analysis and iterative refinement

The data analysis approach described below was applied consistently across all phases of development to ensure continuity and depth of insight throughout the development process. All interviews with HCPs and patients were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analysed through an iterative thematic approach [19]. Feedback from both interviews and think-aloud sessions was mapped to key themes such as user-friendliness, clarity, relevance, communication, and alignment with clinical workflows. Analyses were conducted by two researchers (CR and CG) and reviewed by the broader research team (including RT and AT) to ensure interpretive validity. Although no validated SDM measures were used, participant insights addressed core SDM constructs such as value clarification, understanding of options, and involvement in decision-making, providing meaningful input for refinement. These findings directly informed iterative improvements to the PtDA, ensuring it remained both scientifically rigorous and accessible for children, families, and HCPs. The co-creation process served as a continuous feedback loop to ensure alignment with IPDAS criteria.

Co-creation

The development of the PtDAs was guided by a multidisciplinary steering group comprising 18 members, including paediatric pulmonologists, general practitioners, nursing specialists, representatives from the Lung Alliance Netherlands (LAN), parents and physician-researchers (Table 1). Eight sessions, held between November 2022 and July 2024, each lasting approximately three hours, provided the foundation for co-creation (Fig. 2).

Consensus-building and approach

Consensus was achieved through iterative feedback sessions where disagreements were discussed, and decisions were weighed based on their relevance to patients and feasibility in practice. This collaborative process ensured that the PtDAs would address real-world needs and align with the clinical workflow.

Session 1: Validating the needs assessment and defining scope

In the first session, the steering group reviewed and validated the findings from the needs assessment. These findings highlighted a broad range of decision-making needs among children, parents, and healthcare professionals. It became clear that the scope and goals derived from the needs assessment could not be adequately addressed within a single decision aid. As a result, the group collectively decided to develop two separate PtDAs, each targeting a distinct phase of asthma care. This division allowed for better alignment with the identified decision points and ensured that the tools would respond more effectively to the specific needs of users.

Evaluation PtDA (pre-consultation): Supports families in preparing for consultations by reflecting on symptoms, triggers, and personal goals. This structured preparation helps clarify what families want to discuss, enabling HCPs to tailor the visit accordingly (Figs. 3 and 4). Medication PtDA (in consultation): Provides clear, accessible information on treatment options during the clinical encounter. It facilitates informed conversations about preferences and supports shared decisions about medication use, helping address common issues such as poor adherence and confusion about treatment rationale (Figs. 5 and 6)

Session 2: Refining decision points and integration into the care pathway

In the second session, the identified decision points were further refined and positioned within the paediatric asthma care pathway. Together with an implementation specialist, the team and steering group determined the most appropriate moments within the care process to introduce each PtDA. Recognizing the limitations of the existing pathway, they initiated the development of a revised care pathway in which the decision aids would be central components, ensuring effective integration and use.

Session 3–7: Finalizing and refining the PtDAs

Sessions 3 through 7 focused on ensuring the PtDAs were effective, accessible, and aligned with clinical guidelines:

1. Scope alignment:

Each iteration of the PtDAs was reviewed and reflected back to the steering group to ensure continued alignment with the defined scope and decision-making structure. Feedback was systematically collected and incorporated as part of an iterative refinement process.

2. Content development:

Medical content was developed in accordance with the Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) guidelines [28], ensuring consistency with current clinical standards. This alignment helped safeguard the scientific validity and clinical relevance of the information presented in the PtDAs.

3. Patient-friendliness:

Language and visuals were iteratively refined to enhance clarity and accessibility, especially for children aged 6–12. Complex medical terminology was simplified, and supportive visual elements were added to aid understanding. These adaptations were informed by usability testing and guided by the steering group to ensure the content was appropriate for all user groups. Input from both the steering group and the KinderAdviesRaad (children’s advisory board) played a direct role in these refinements. For example, children recommended alternative wording for abstract terms such as “lifestyle,” which were adopted in the final version of the PtDA.

4. Supporting materials:

Additional tools were developed to support implementation, including consultation leaflets for patients and clear distribution strategies. In addition, training modules were created to help healthcare professionals effectively integrate the PtDAs into routine clinical practice.

Session 8: Finalizing the decision aid and preparing for implementation

The final session made adjustments to the PtDAs based on feedback received throughout the process. The group reviewed the complete aid for accuracy and user-friendliness and developed an implementation plan, including training strategies for HCP and monitoring the use of PtDAs in practice.

Survey feedback

The survey for both PtDAs was completed by 16 HCPs.

For the Evaluation Decision aid, 87.5% expressed willingness to use it during paediatric asthma follow-ups. 81.3% found the information clear and valuable for improving patient-provider communication, though 18.8% expressed concerns about potentially overwhelming patients with information.

For the Medication Decision Aid, opinions were more varied: 62.5% of HCPs viewed it positively, with no negative feedback. However, 25% felt the information could be seen as directive, though it was still considered essential for addressing an existing information gap.

Semi-structured interviews with healthcare professionals

We conducted in-depth interviews with 13 healthcare professionals (5 paediatric pulmonologists, 3 asthma nurses, 3 general practitioners, 2 practice nurses). The aim was to gain qualitative insights into the perceived utility, clarity, and integration of the PtDAs in practice.

The evaluation decision aid was particularly appreciated for its ability to prompt patients and caregivers to reflect on their symptoms, progress, and treatment goals prior to consultations. Healthcare providers noted that these preparatory steps facilitated more focused and productive discussions during follow-ups. While some professionals highlighted that younger children might require additional parental support to engage with the tool effectively, its overall potential to activate patients and caregivers in their care was widely acknowledged.

The medication decision aid was commended for its clear structure and concise yet comprehensive presentation of treatment options, enabling more collaborative discussions during consultations. Many professionals noted that sections like “What can you do yourself?” effectively supported patient education and fostered active engagement. However, concerns were raised about the substantial amount of information presented, with one provider describing it as “necessary but potentially overwhelming”. However, patients did not experience this because they have control to access what is relevant to them. The PtDAs can improve coordination between primary and secondary care by providing standardized information and preparation. Based on this feedback, revisions were made, including making the Medication Decision Aid less directive, better aligning it with user needs.

Integration and refinement

Findings from all three methods were jointly analysed and discussed in the project team. While the survey provided overall acceptability and usability ratings, the think-aloud and interview data allowed us to contextualize and interpret these ratings, identifying concrete areas for improvement. In response to the concern about directive language in the Medication PtDA, adjustments were made to tone and phrasing to improve neutrality and user control.

Quality assessment

Based on this iterative testing and refinement process, both PtDAs were confirmed to meet the IPDAS criteria for quality, balance, and usability. Their integration into the newly developed care pathway supports shared decision-making throughout the asthma trajectory during both preparation and consultation phases. These tools enhance communication, support self-management, and contribute to more collaborative, informed care for children with asthma and their families.

Discussion

Summary of results

This paper outlines the development of PtDAs addressing critical challenges in paediatric asthma care. The Evaluation Decision Aid helps users identify triggers, symptoms, and potential lifestyle modifications before consultations, fostering proactive preparation. The Medication Decision Aid guides families through treatment options, enhancing understanding and encouraging reflection on preferences. Together, these tools bridge gaps in communication: patients often struggle to articulate concerns, while HCPs may miss misunderstandings. By fostering structured, collaborative discussions, the PtDAs empower children, enhance parental input, and improve coordination between primary and secondary care settings.

To our knowledge, these are the first online PtDAs designed for the treatment of childhood asthma. Effective asthma management requires multifactorial approaches, involving patient adherence, trigger recognition, and HCP adherence to guidelines [20, 21]. Standardized recommendations often overshadow personalized decision-making, limiting opportunities for collaboration. Our PtDAs address these gaps by providing clear, evidence-based information, promoting open dialogue, and encouraging shared reflection on treatment options and lifestyle considerations [4]. This approach fosters medication adherence, better asthma control, and long-term management success.

A key strength of the PtDAs is their ability to actively involve children in SDM [22, 23]. Paediatric care is often dependent on parental decision-making, but these tools ensure that children’s questions, preferences, concerns, and experiences are an integral part of the process [23–25]. Empowering children improves satisfaction with care, strengthens trust between families and HCPs, and enhances the quality of care. By facilitating preparation at home, the PtDAs facilitate more efficient, goal-oriented consultations that align with family expectations and streamline workflows for HCPs [23, 25, 26]. Evidence shows that involving children, even at young ages, contributes to personalized treatment plans, better adherence, and improved asthma control [15, 23, 25, 27].

The needs assessment highlighted the critical relevance of a structured tool to support SDM in paediatric asthma care. Although HCPs often believed they were practicing SDM, their approach frequently centered around obtaining informed consent rather than fostering genuine collaborative decision-making, particularly when children were involved. Parents and children commonly reported feeling insufficiently engaged in the decision-making process, while HCPs acknowledged the need for a more structured approach to SDM. The PtDAs address this gap by empowering patients and their families to make informed choices when multiple treatment options are available [11, 27]. Evidence consistently supports the positive impact of active SDM engagement on health outcomes, further underscoring the importance of this approach [28].

In addition to gaps in SDM, clinician-related factors often influence disease management, including failure to follow guidelines, incorrect patient assessments, and inappropriate selection of treatments or inhaler devices [20, 29]. The PtDAs were specifically designed to address these challenges by emphasizing continuous education and trigger recognition while correcting common misconceptions about asthma medication that persist among parents and patients [30]. By providing clear, evidence-based information and promoting open dialogue between HCPs, parents, and children, the PtDAs enable families to make informed decisions aligned with their preferences. This approach not only improves medication adherence and trigger identification but also enhances overall asthma control, ultimately leading to better health outcomes [3].

The study revealed several challenges, particularly in implementing the PtDAs and integrating SDM into clinical practice. Defining the scope and determining how to fit the PtDAs into the care pathway were key challenges, leading to the decision to create two separate PtDAs: one for asthma evaluation and the other for medication choices. To address this, the steering group revised the care pathway for seamless PtDA integration. Implementation is essential but serves to support SDM and patient engagement, not as an end goal. Organizational barriers, such as misaligned workflows and insufficient training, are common obstacles in decision aid implementation [31, 32]. Similar challenges have been seen in other studies, emphasizing the need for comprehensive strategies. To bridge the knowledge gap, training programs for HCPs were developed to ensure effective PtDA use. Combining PtDAs with SDM training aims to improve the quality and inclusivity of paediatric asthma care [33]. These efforts are expected to address key barriers, but further research is needed to evaluate their long-term impact on practice and patient outcomes.

Study limitations

Despite its strengths, the study has limitations. First, to ensure buy-in for post-development implementation, there were somewhat more HCPs and families involved in the PtDAs’ development from the Netherlands. This may limit generalizability; however, it ensures strong regional alignment and, since both PtDAs meet the IPDAS criteria, facilitates broader implementation. Second, patients were slightly underrepresented in the larger group, and self-selection of particularly engaged individuals may have introduced some bias. A further potential limitation is that some participants were involved across multiple phases of product development, which may lead to overcommitment in the product, reducing their ability to recognize its flaws or areas for improvement. Nevertheless, national patient representatives and parents were actively involved in each phase of the study, ensuring that their perspectives were taken into account. Finally, while the PtDAs were designed to be user-friendly and accessible to children as young as six, the literacy demands both for children and parents may still pose a barrier in some cases. Future research should explore the tools’ applicability across diverse populations, assess comprehension among different age groups, and formally evaluate their impact on shared decision-making using validated instruments to measure outcomes such as SDM scores, changes in intentions, and actual health behaviours like treatment adherence.

Validity of the study

The study’s validity was strengthened by a mixed-methods approach, incorporating interviews, surveys, and think-aloud sessions to capture diverse perspectives. Adhering to the IPDAS standards, the PtDAs were developed through an iterative co-creation process involving children, parents, and HCPs, ensuring they address user needs and gaps in childhood asthma care [17]. Feedback-driven iterations enhanced usability, scientific rigor, and alignment with clinical workflows. Independent evaluation by uninvolved HCPs reinforced objectivity and quality. By bridging information gaps, the PtDAs enable better patient understanding, guide HCPs in addressing concerns, and support consistent, effective shared decision-making to improve outcomes and reduce healthcare utilization [11, 34, 35].

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study successfully developed two childhood asthma decision aids that fill a crucial gap in care by actively involving children in their own health management. By involving children in SDM, the PtDAs have the potential to improve both quality of care and asthma management outcomes. It also promotes better collaboration between healthcare professionals, patients, and families, leading to more informed treatment decisions, which will improve adherence to treatment and result in better asthma control and quality of life.

Future research should assess the long-term impact and contextual applicability of the PtDAs, including their effect on shared decision-making, intentions, and health behaviours such as adherence. Efforts should also aim to optimize the tool for younger children and integrate it into routine care.
