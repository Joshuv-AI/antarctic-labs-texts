Community perspectives and recommendations to reduce epilepsy stigma and enhance effective management of epilepsy in Uganda: A qualitative study
Authors: Angelina Kakooza Mwesige, Mayanja M Kajumba, Paula N Njeru, Noeline Nakasujja, Juliet Nabwire, Mark Kaddumukasa, Martin N Kaddumukasa, Deborah Koltai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:The Intersectoral Global Action Plan (IGAP) for Epilepsy and other Neurological Disorders aims to improve access to quality care, including diagnosis, treatment, and support services for people with epilepsy (PWE). Adolescents with epilepsy (AWE) comprise a highly vulnerable group, with limited access to treatment, higher seizure-related risks, and poor quality of life. However, there are hardly any systematic descriptions of the community perspectives on how the care and treatment of epilepsy can be improved in this group.Purpose:The study aimed to establish the primary ideas within the community of proposed interventions that can improve the care and treatment of AWE in Uganda.Methods:A qualitative study, using semi structured focus group and in-depth interview guides, explored the perspectives of 128 participants enrolled from Wakiso district, Uganda. Sample included AWE (n = 22); Caregivers of AWE (n = 22); Health workers (n = 24); Teachers (n = 19); Local leaders (n = 14); Religious leaders (n = 6); Traditional healers (n = 5); Village health team members (n = 10) and other community members (n = 6). Using Dedoose software, thematic analysis ascertained commonly occurring themes about perceptions and recommendations for epilepsy care.Results:All community groups emphasized the profound stigmatization of AWE/PWE. Four major themes summarized the proposed areas to impact care and well-being: education of AWE/PWE and their families; development of a broad epilepsy community sensitization campaign, restoration and strengthening of the epilepsy healthcare infrastructure, and government commitment to epilepsy health service provision.Conclusions:The community recommendations prioritized as key in improving the care for PWE/AWE in Uganda, aligns with the IGAP. Their adoption is critical in ensuring effective management of epilepsy in Uganda.Keywords: Epilepsy, Community perspectives, Management, Stigma, IGAP, Uganda

Introduction

The epilepsies are a clinically heterogeneous group of neurological disorders, affecting over 70 million people globally, with approximately 80% residing in resource-poor areas [1,2]. With approximately 5 million new cases every year, epilepsy accounts for more than 0.5% of the global burden of disease and affects people of all ages, races, social classes, and geographical locations [3-5]. A nationwide population study reported that the epilepsy burden in Uganda is high, estimated at 16.9 per 1000 [6], with the highest burden seen among the 20–39 year age group. Young people, especially those living in rural areas of Uganda, carry a heavier burden of epilepsy [7,8]. Generally, a significant proportion of the African population, particularly in the rural areas, lacks fundamental knowledge about epilepsy, its causes, and management; hence hindering effective management of the disease [9]. This state of low epilepsy awareness encompasses both the general public and healthcare professionals and plays a major role in driving delayed diagnosis, limited access to healthcare, inadequate treatment and social stigma [10].

Although epilepsy is a highly treatable condition, with approximately 70% of people with epilepsy [11] achieving seizure control, the proportion of individuals diagnosed with epilepsy and correctly using anti-seizure medication (ASM) in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) remains low at 25% [12]. In Uganda, close to one-third of PWE do not receive the appropriate medication [13], opting for traditional or pastoral/faith healers, which delays their reaching biomedical care by an average of two years [14]. The poor health infrastructure, frequent ASM stockouts at government units, prohibitive ASM costs on the private market, coupled with almost widespread discrimination and stigma associated with epilepsy, culminate in high levels of otherwise avoidable disability, mortality, social exclusion, economic disadvantage, and negative mental health outcomes in PWE [15].

Unlike the overwhelming majority of other diseases, epilepsy is often associated with stigma, prejudice, and discriminatory practices, which negatively impact people’s everyday lives in important areas, such as access to education, employment, marriage, and social integration. Globally, the human rights violations, stigma, and discrimination often pose more challenges to PWE than the seizures themselves [16,17].

Epilepsy related stigma and discrimination are fueled in part by century-old misconceptions, with the three prevalent misconceptions revolving around people with epilepsy being contagious, possessed by demons, or bewitched, and punished by ancestral spirits or gods [1,18]. The stigma of epilepsy can hinder PWE from seeking treatment and consequently affect their quality of life, social inclusion and lower their self-esteem [19,20].

Findings from a study conducted in rural southwestern Uganda [21], noted that perceived stigma among children and adolescents was higher among older children and those who had never attended school, as well as those with epilepsy-related injuries or comorbid chronic illnesses. Among the PWE, the adolescents are particularly vulnerable to stigma because of their mental and emotional fragility, and are consequently more susceptible to its harmful effects, including impaired self-esteem, peer relations, cognitions, social abilities, mood [22], engagement in age- appropriate activities [23], and school attendance [21]. Stigma results in adolescents with epilepsy (AWE) being more secretive and ashamed, and contributes to the high prevalence of adjustment problems and mood disorders [24]. Not surprisingly, the levels of depression and anxiety among people with epilepsy are extremely high [25,26]. AWE are often behind in their academic progression or not in school at all [27], often as a result of being expelled once their illness is discovered [18]. Adults with epilepsy are underemployed relative to age and gender matched controls [27].

Adolescence is an important transitional phase of growth and development between childhood and adulthood. It is a sensitive time when cognitive and social development allow for greater perception of negative attitudes [28,29], while simultaneously instigating the adolescent’s desire for greater self-reliance, and less dependence on parental protection. A number of studies of epilepsy in Africa have focused on young children and adults to examine the epilepsy seizure burden [30], risk factors [30], clinical features [31], and policy implications [32], but there are fewer studies describing the methods of improving the care and treatment of epilepsy in this vulnerable adolescent population. To the best of our knowledge, this is one of the first studies focused on understanding both adolescents and their communities, and their thoughts about ways of improving epilepsy care for adolescents with epilepsy (AWE).

Kajumba et al. [33] (this issue) put forth explanatory models of epilepsy among AWE learned from this study. The purpose of this analysis, then, was to elucidate the primary ideas for interventions to impact the care and wellbeing of AWE and their communities as they navigate the challenges of the illness. Specifically, we summarize the ideas that were generated by the adolescents, their families, and key members of their communities to improve the care and treatment of adolescents with epilepsy.

Methods

Ethical statement

The study was approved by the TASO Research and Ethics Committee (TASO-2022-102), Uganda National Council of Science and Technology (UNCST HS2421ES) and Duke Health Institutional Review Board (Pro00110377).

Study area

All participants were recruited from Wakiso District in central Uganda. Wakiso is the most populous district in Uganda that partly encircles Kampala, Uganda’s capital city and has a population estimated at 3.3 million (“National Population and Housing Census 2024,” n.d.).

The majority of the population in Wakiso are Baganda, a Bantuspeaking group, who form one of the largest tribes in Uganda, with their primary language being Luganda (local language of the study area). The population is mostly engaged in peri-urban agriculture activities, particularly crop cultivation, livestock rearing, fisheries, and beekeeping.

This location was chosen due to its large population density and because our previous work showed a high prevalence of AWE (Kakooza-Mwesige et al. [7]). Wakiso also offers the advantage of having both rural communities similar to those in more remote areas of the country, as well as rapidly expanding urban communities, as seen in other urban centers like Kampala.

Study population and data collection techniques

The data collection methods included focus group discussions (FGDs) and in depth interviews (IDIs). All FGD and IDI participants were known residents of the study area. They were mobilized by community members identified by the study research team and were considered 'information-rich cases' [34], as they were in a good position to discuss the impact of stigma on the highly vulnerable adolescent with epilepsy (AWE) population, and also expose the key drivers of stigma in the community. Each FGD with 4–8 participants, lasted 45–60 min, and was conducted in the environs of Wakiso Health Centre IV. Both the interviewers and the note-takers had a social scientist background and were experienced in conducting FGDs and indepth interviews. They fluently spoke both English and Luganda, the local most widely spoken language in Wakiso.

A total of 128 people were enrolled, and these were AWE, their caregivers, teachers, religious leaders, traditional healers, village health team members, health workers, and a diverse group of local leaders, as shown in Fig. 1.

Instruments

We used a short video depicting three vignettes of an adolescent experiencing a generalized tonic clonic seizure in different life circumstances, in one preparing to sweep the courtyard, in another digging in the garden and the other coming from collecting water from the well. These different scenarios were to ensure that all the participants were aware of the condition being referred to during the FGDs and IDIs. The video was collaboratively developed by the authors and graduates from the School of Performing Arts, Makerere University, reviewed for clarity and accuracy by a third party, and subsequently revised.

The FGD and IDI guides were designed by 3 research team members (AKM, DK, MMK) who spanned the disciplines of psychology, neuropsychology, and neurology. Construction of the guides utilized questions previously piloted by our research team, with revisions designed to foster discussion of proposed health literacy content and methods of sensitization not previously explored. Questions were modified by deliberation until consensus was reached to ensure clarity through an iterative review process.

The FGDs were conducted using the FGD guide which focused on questions designed to elicit information about how the individual or groups experience epilepsy, its causation treatment, stigma and discrimination. Specifically, questions prompted discussion on: (1) Individual, family, and community knowledge about epilepsy; (2) Beliefs about causation, treatment, contagiousness, and curability; (3) The best way to respond or deal with a PWE; (4) Family, teacher, peer, and community treatment of the AWE; (5) What activities a PWE can or cannot participate in or rights they should have; (6) Drivers of stigmatization; and 7) What might be needed to provide better care for AWE or other ideas to improve the lives of AWE. IDI participants were selected because, in their community roles, they interact closely with AWE and their caregivers when seeking expertise or treatment for seizures. They included health care workers, pastoral leaders, and/or traditional healers. All the data were tape recorded and transcribed verbatim for thematic analysis. Sessions conducted in Luganda were later translated into English by the interviewers.

Analysis

Data analysis used both deductive analysis of predefined categories and inductive analysis, which allowed for the identification of additional key drivers of stigma. Dedoose software was utilized for grouping, coding, and cleaning of emergent themes. Three professionals with expertise in thematic analysis collectively reviewed and discussed the preliminary themes to reach a shared understanding grounded in the research perspectives and then developed the codebook.

To ensure reliability, two team members independently coded a subset of 16 transcripts, equally distributed across participant categories, totalling 827 passages (Kappa = 0.75). After achieving an acceptable reliability score, the full team of 3 coded all transcripts. All excerpts for each code were reviewed, and relevant quotes were extracted from the excerpts to support the identified themes. An additional validity check was then done by having two senior members of the research team (AKM and DK) review the fidelity of the identified themes and quotes from the transcripts.

Results

General characteristics of the study population

The study population of 128 mainly comprised females 82(64%). Data on age was available for 93% of the sample, and showed a median age of 39 years (IQR range: 30–50). One third 40 (31.3%) were in formal employment, with the others owning their own businesses, being peasant farmers or non-employed.

Participants reactions to the informational video

Study participants recognized the imminent vulnerability of a person experiencing a seizure, and the need to swiftly remove them from danger, offer them a safe haven, ensure privacy, prevent suffocation during the seizure, and provide other forms of first aid.

“If someone gets an attack, he or she might be in a bad position, and if not urgently helped, he or she may suffocate. He or she needs to be positioned properly.. The person really needs to be moved and put in a more comfortable place”

(Religious leader, IDI)

“…In most cases, they usually fall down, so I think it would be good to first of all cover them up, and then also support their head and put it in a better position. In most cases they fall face-down, and it would be good to change their faces to look up…”

(Teacher, IDI)

Thematic analysis

Several themes related to perceptions and recommendations for epilepsy care emerged from the thematic analysis, each of which is described in detail below.

Fostering reinforced and focused epilepsy sensitization programs

Many participants spoke of the need to design and implement community sensitization programs and policies to protect PWE. There was almost universal consensus that there was a need to reinforce and pay closer attention to sensitization programs on raising awareness about epilepsy identification and appropriate care seeking to improve outcomes for PWE.

“We need to go to the community to sensitize people about this disease so that when they see someone has an attack [epileptic seizure], they know how to help them and where they can take such a person [for treatment].”

(Health Worker, FGD).

Participants proposed that such programs begin at the grassroots to combat stigma as well as misconceptions about causation and treatment.

“So, we need to start the sensitization right from home; they shouldn’t discriminate against PWE because they have a mental challenge, which is made worse by other people’s conduct.”

(Health Worker, FGD)

Suggested channels of sensitization and target audiences

While participants had ideas about pathways of programs and sometimes specified who might be the best authorities to provide sensitization, not surprisingly, the primary suggested audience for sensitization was the community. There were different community groups that were earmarked to carry out this process and others that needed to be sensitized.

The families of persons with epilepsy.

The education of the patients and their family members was emphasized, with special attention to the need to involve fathers of children with epilepsy, who often lack emotional involvement, and who offer limited social support or parental supervision of the children. Obtaining the help from the government and courts of law in such situations was recommended.

“Government should emphasize sensitization and counseling to parents who have such children, especially the fathers because they abandon such responsibilities.. The law should hold such fathers accountable because a child is not only a mother’s responsibility; it's the man’s responsibility too, and that’s their blood [offspring].”

(Local leader, FGD)

Health workers and the ministry of health.

The health workers acknowledged that they were key in leading the community sensitization campaigns to clear the misconceptions about epilepsy causation and treatment.

“We as health workers should sensitize people in the communities about epilepsy so that they know that epilepsy can be healed [controlled] and that it is not contagious. They should also know that these patients, once healed [with controlled seizures], they can be productive and become important people in the future, like a president.”

(Health Worker, FGD)

Other members of the community further suggested ways in which these activities could be propagated by the health workers.

“I think that the Ministry of Health itself should lead this campaign. They can use TV stations, radios, and other media outlets to inform the people.”

(Traditional Healer, IDI)

“…They should use those speakers [public address systems] daily to teach people about the existence of epilepsy. They [people] usually listen to those speakers.”

(Health Worker, FGD)

School children.

Utilizing students in schools to sensitize their parents and fellow peers was another avenue proposed for sensitization.

“We can sensitize the communities using schools in that the children from these schools will spread out the information to their parents, who can spread it out to their friends..”

(Health Worker, FGD)

Researchers.

The researchers were also suggested as important in community sensitization to not only carry out their assigned work but also use the data collection period as an opportunity to educate the public.

“Researchers would help us to learn more because as them [researching] and us in the neighborhood we have less knowledge about it.”

(Teacher, FGD)

Witchdoctor’s shrine.

Traditional healers, and specifically those referred to as “witchdoctors”, were not spared as a group in need of sensitization, with participants noting that some patients come to harm in their care when they could have been effectively treated in biomedical care.

“To get these witch doctors also to sensitize them because it's very easy for this patient to die from their place.”

(Health Worker, IDI)

Places of worship.

In Uganda, much of the population attends formal religious services. Not surprisingly, targeting the various places of worship as another avenue of community sensitization to dispel misconceptions was also suggested

“Go to churches, because the truth is that in churches, these children [with epilepsy] sit on the floor. I am an elder in the church, and I am telling you that we make them sit down on the floor; we separate them from the normal children. So, you have to come and teach in churches and mosques because people believe in being infected when in close contact with the sick [PWE]..”

(Community Member, FGD)

Essential content of an epilepsy health literacy program

There was considerable agreement that better health literacy would improve outcomes for PWE. Building literacy involved empowering community members to effectively assist when a PWE suffers a seizure, demystifying the illness and correcting misperceptions, and establishing a clear referral system for affected persons. Participants also alluded to educating people so that they can normalize situations as a way of fighting stigma. The proposed literacy program was based on ensuring that all these fundamental concerns were addressed and packaged in a seven element program. The key focus areas of the seven-element program involve a comprehensive approach that addresses multiple aspects of improving the holistic care of PWE and the details of the Epilepsy Health Literacy program proposed are further elaborated.

Information about epilepsy as a treatable medical illness.

Some participants expressed in a general way that they, or the community, need to better understand the illness.

“…If the people understand the disease and someone has an idea like ‘I have the medicine or I know of a doctor’, we can be helped for the future generation “

(Local leader, FGD)

“… As medical people, you need to educate the public about how these people can be treated in case someone gets a seizure. Educate the public about this disease, educate the schools, the teachers and all, on how to support these epileptic people”

(Teacher, IDI)

Training on how to care for the person having a seizure.

Participants expressed a yearning for more information about how to manage situations when a person has a seizure so that the PWE could avoid injury or exploitation. The desire for clear information on how to immediately support PWE in crisis was expressed as a key aspect of the epilepsy literacy program.

“if we were educated or we get people to help teach us on how to control it in case it happened, we can be of help to them”

(Teacher, FGD)

“I wouldn’t know what to do and I would really have to be trained on how to support them in case of a seizure. And if I am trained, then I can support that person.”

(Teacher, IDI)

“…But most people are now[not] aware, even us leaders, we are not aware about this condition. We don’t know that such and such a facility provides services for this condition, we need to be sensitized about ways of handling this condition…”

(Local leader, FGD)

Information on precautionary measures to take in the care of the person with epilepsy.

In addition to the need for support of PWE who are having a seizure, the need to address sources of potential injury or stress in the environment was acknowledged, emphasizing the importance of safety and comfort.

“ …they shouldn't avoid the person and they should help the person each time he or she gets seizures and they should not lose sight of him or her. The other family was negligent with uncle Kaloli who went to the kitchen, got an attack and fell in a saucepan of beans and down into the fire”

(Religious leader, IDI)

“…I advise people to be careful because they can easily see the conditions of an epileptic person and quickly react or respond by for example taking them inside the house, distancing them from fire places since fire is dangerous to them as they can easily fall in [and get burnt]“

(Teacher, FGD).

Information on how to combat stigma and discrimination and promote social inclusion towards people with epilepsy.

Many participants expressed compassion, and the need to reduce discrimination and stigma. These comments reflected awareness of how isolated PWE are and how harshly they are regarded and treated. There was pervasive discrimination and stigma practiced by different types of people, including family members, community members, and even healthcare workers, was noted.

“……. Don't discriminate them because you should put your foot into their shoes [be empathic] and say, if it was me who was looked at [or treated] that way”

(Teacher, IDI)

“…society should stop marginalizing those people because they are part of us. I know they are part of us…”

(Teacher, IDI)

“Remember some of the health workers had that stigma. So we would find at times these patients would miss out on treatment until one [particular health officer] leaves the night offs and comes to find and attend to them……..The rest of the health officers would not attend to PWE, yet the medicine was present in the store.”

(Health Worker, FGD)

Information on dispelling the myths and negative perceptions about epilepsy and its cure.

Some specifically noted the need to broadly convey that epilepsy is a biomedical illness and not a supernatural phenomena or witchcraft, and specifically that it is not contagious. Participants also noted the need to clarify that epilepsy is not curable, but rather must be managed as a chronic illness.

“…there is a saying that when you touch a person with epilepsy, his saliva contains the sickness. That is also a myth but however am not sure if it's true. You would as well explain more for us…”

(Teacher, FGD)

“…I would like to advise them to treat me just like they treat their children because epilepsy is not contagious…”

(AWE, IDI)

“Since they say it's hereditary, there is that gene, which is transferred from one person to another. I think it's not curable unless you are dead. But we would request the health professionals to educate us more about it.”

(Teacher, FGD)

Information on treatment options, referral, resources and fostering supportive environments for people with epilepsy and their families.

Some also expressed wanting information about the treatment of illness and where to send PWE for effective treatment.

“…by the time I leave here I will get answers to my questions of can it be treated, can it be cured, and which hospitals can one go to for treatment…..”

(Local leader, FGD)

“Those health workers that do not know about epilepsy need to learn about it so that we can make good referrals”.

(Health Worker, FGD)

Some participants also noted that educating PWE and their families about epilepsy treatment would enable them be in control of their illness and refrain from solitude during their care.

“…sensitize and create awareness and make them understand their situations better. This makes them master their sickness…”

(Religious leader, IDI)

“..PWE should be taught that they are not alone and they should be taught that they are normal people just that they have some situation which happens to them… “

(Teacher, IDI).

Information on the importance of medication adherence in epilepsy management.

Notably, several participants highlighted the importance of medication adherence, which is critical to avoid seizures and the development of treatment resistance.

……”They should be with their medications all the time and also take the dosage without missing any”

(Religious leader, IDI)

“… Secondly, if the patient is an adult, it would be good to also first counsel them and help them understand that their condition is an illness just like any other and also advise them to comply with their medication [guidelines].”

(VHT member, IDI)

Access to anti-seizure medication

Several participants highlighted the need for consistent free access to anti-seizure medications, and in cases of out of pocket purchases to make the drug prices affordable, as well as the need to educate people about the efficacy of medication treatment. It is worth noting that access to medications was emphasized by patients, local leaders, caregivers, traditional healers and healthcare workers, reflecting the deleterious outcomes of medication supply interruption.

“Medicine shouldn’t be sold because people don’t have money. However, if they [Ministry of Health] decide to sell it, let it be affordable even to the person deep down in the village. But if possible it should be free because, after all, the patients are not many”.

(Local leader, FGD)

“I think what can be done is to make sure that there is medication for this illness. Just like there is medicine for illnesses like malaria, there should be medicine for this illness for all people just like it was provided to me and I got healed.”

(AWE, IDI)

“I call upon the government to avail medicine for epilepsy in the health facilities so that a person can get medicine and take the way you see HIV.”

(Traditional Healer, IDI)

“I once saw a patient that was buying herbal medicines from a drug shop to treat epilepsy but we started giving them real medicine for epilepsy and after sometime, these patients were surprised by the great performance of these drugs in that one of them made a comment saying,” Who knew that so and so who had epilepsy would heal completely, get married and even give birth to children!”.

(Health Worker, FGD)

The health workers reiterated that sometimes the lack of available medications stemmed from the higher authorities not considering their particular health units as priority to be supplied with the required medications.

“The health center IIs do not receive mental health medication therefore, there is need to let the drug stores like National Medical Stores (NMS) know that there are people in these health centers that require mental health drugs and Folic acid supplements.”

(Health Worker, FGD)

“But, our facilities are isolated in that you find grade two health centers do not even have those epilepsy drugs”.

(Health Worker, FGD)

However even when these drugs are made available at the different units, some PWE and their families are often unaware that this service exists and need to be informed. Furthermore, for those who are started on medications, often need to be reminded to remain adherent to avoid the consequent complications.

“Another thing is to remind them to come and pick the drugs from the hospital so that they pick the right medication. We have realized the medicines are available there, and we should remind them to be consistent with taking their medication for a long time because this is a chronic illness. Some patients take this epilepsy medication but at a certain point they get tired, and stop taking it. Some of them, once they stop getting attacks or frequent fits, they stop taking the medication and they end up relapsing. So, we need to put more effort on health education…”

(Health Worker, FGD)

Barriers to accessing medicine, beyond availability, were also acknowledged and included the cost of transportation to the health units incurred by PWE and the cost of the medicines themselves.

“Another thing is transport. We try as much as possible to tell them to come back for checkups and medication but due to lack of transport, they do not come.. So they have to first dig in order to get the transport money they need. Therefore, they end up missing the drugs; hence get the epilepsy attacks [seizures]. In addition to that, some of them come from so far. Due to this distance, they fail to come to the facilities and some even send their fellow epileptic friends to pick for them their drugs.”

(Health Worker, FGD)

“Sometimes you find that what the health center can afford is just phenytoin and carbamazepine yet you want to use sodium valproate which is expensive. Our patients cannot afford it because it is UGX 1000 (USD ≅ 0.3, per tablet) and yet they also have to buy food.”

(Health Worker, FGD)

“…one day I went to Bulondo (health unit) where I normally get drugs but it was out of stock. But I had also received drugs from here so I decided to come here. However, I did not have transport and I footed [walked] all the way from Baka (home village). I had to move with the child, and that day I got extremely tired. We had nothing to eat on the way, so we suffered a lot.”

(Caregiver, FGD)

Responsibility and authority

Interestingly several participants emphasized the role of government in delivering such health education literacy programs, the sensitization campaigns and improved access to medications. The Ministry of Health was earmarked as a key player in leading the health educative talks, with emphasis made on sensitization and counselling of parents especially fathers of children living with epilepsy. Utilization of the Local Councils (LCs) (LC is a form of local elected government within the districts of Uganda, that plays a critical role in governance, service delivery, and community development) with the help of the government was another suggested approach, as well as researchers working with government to carry out community outreach interventions, such as going to schools to talk to the teachers and creation of educative programs on the local TV stations. These observations noted the national reach the government has, and the power to shape normative behavioral standards through programs and policies.

“The Ministry of Health should demystify or clearly come out and talk about this disease and all issues related to it. It is because of ignorance that people behave the way they are behaving”

(Local leader, FGD).

“It is the awareness part that needs to be emphasized and also availing treatment for epilepsy at government facilities, and people should know that this is a place where epileptic patients receive drugs from, in the same way as it is for HIV.”

(Teacher, IDI)

“Some people are ignorant about it. They may say it's witchcraft where they end up slaughtering goats, and cows, yet it's a disease, which is scientific I am appealing to the government to come over and help us get knowledge about that disease, and how it can be reduced or eliminated in our country, Uganda”

(Teacher, IDI).

Some participants also suggested infrastructure and organizational development to strengthen epilepsy care through referral systems, specialized identification, dedicated follow up programs, concern for their social needs through setting up saving support groups and the role of faith communities.

“Let us allow the experts to follow up. We could have a follow-up program at every subcounty health facility. There should be a doctor who manages cases and follows up on quarterly basis..”

(Health Worker, FGD)

“My recommendation to the Ministry of Health would be to empower these lower-grade health facilities because all cases start from us before reaching the main facilities like Mulago (National Specialists Referral Hospital) and Butabika (National Referral Mental Hospital).

(Health Worker, FGD)

Another recommendation is integration [of epilepsy care] when they are planning [for health service provision]. The people who have HIV are screened and helped early. So, I think if we can integrate this [epilepsy care] into outpatient departments (OPDs) with streamlined screening, I think we can identify these cases because the people have this condition, but we don’t screen for it.”

(Health Worker, FGD)

“So, the government should help us and show us that we can have them as our friends, and I am calling out church leaders not to neglect them when they come to church. People should be aware and give them space because that attack [seizure] can come anytime, and church members should recognize such people. Thank you.”

(Community Member, FGD)

Discussion

This study aimed to expound on the key viewpoints recommended by the AWE, their families, and key members of their communities that can impact the care and well-being of AWE and their communities as they cope with the multiple challenges of living with epilepsy. We noted that the community recognized that epilepsy was a complex condition with several interconnected causal attributions, as was also depicted in the explanatory models illustrated in Kajumba et al [33] (in this issue). Aware of the complexity of challenges, the community suggested comprehensive solutions that integrate various strategies that would be necessary to achieve more effective and sustainable epilepsy care outcomes in Uganda.

Across the many community groups that were engaged in the study, there were concordant views regarding the need to sensitize the community, with the ultimate goal of raising awareness about epilepsy and improving the social and health outcomes for AWE. Suggested channels of sensitization included family members, school children, witchdoctors, health workers and the Ministry of Health, researchers and places of worship. A seven-pronged Epilepsy Health Literacy program can be proposed given the areas of educational needs suggested. They also emphasized the need for free, consistent access to anti-seizure medications, with all programs spearheaded by the Ugandan government. Education of PWE/AWE and their families

Our participants recognized that increased public awareness of epilepsy, with factual medical information and recommendations for care, was indispensable to effecting change for AWE. Raising public awareness about epilepsy through sensitization programs aligns with the Intersectoral Global Action Plan (IGAP) for Epilepsy and other Neurological Disorders, approved by the World Health Assembly [35,36]. IGAP aims to improve access to quality care, including diagnosis, treatment, and support services, for people with epilepsy. The foundation of this initiative began in 1997 [37] with the World Health Organization, the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE), and the International Bureau of Epilepsy(IBE) partnering on the “Out of the Shadows” campaign to reduce the treatment gap, economic burden, and stigma associated with epilepsy. The campaign targeted a number of regionally focused programs including raising general awareness and understanding of epilepsy [38].

Family members were among the primary suggested target audience and channels of sensitization, with a special focus upon fathers who oftentimes abandon the family if a child has epilepsy. In the African context, gender norms and cultural expectations often create barriers to men's participation in health matters, because societal beliefs link healthcare to traditional female roles. The role fathers/male guardians play as the key breadwinners of the family is integral in shaping family health outcomes primarily by providing financial stability which ensures access to essential resources like healthcare, nutrition, and education [80].

Our finding of rejection in the care of PWE by the fathers is similar to research done by O’Toole et al. [39], but dissimilar to that of Akay et al. (2011) who reported more violence and rejection by the mothers of PWE [40]. Epilepsy not only affects the PWE but also has profound consequences for the entire family [41]. Stress manifesting as anxiety or depression in the family can arise from the fear of the child’s death or injury following the seizure as well as parental feelings of helplessness and inadequacy during the child’s seizure [42,43]. Having a child with epilepsy can affect the relationship patterns in the family, as parents find it difficult to attend to the needs of other family members and often miss out on important events in their life. Furthermore, families are also stigmatized, with the child with epilepsy impacting the parents’employability, social standing, and broad acceptance in the community [44].

The epilepsy literature highlights that families of PWE are also noted to experience more communication and parenting challenges [41,45,46]. The reasons for this are many, and might include the additional stress that both parents need to manage, and the fear of social stigma [39]. Furthermore, poor coping parenting styles of parents of PWE contribute to a higher incidence of behavioral problems in families, with the child’s behavioural problems aggravating the fragile family environment [46-48]. In cases where the father is absent, the sole responsibility of the care of the child rests on the mother, impacting the child's care and the family's overall well-being. It is therefore of crucial importance to empower parents of PWE by exploring the use of family-to-family education sessions to learn about epilepsy and share experiences. Targeted culturally sensitive interventions to reduce parental stress can be prioritized in this population, with education about the illness and its comorbidities, coupled with education regarding parenting styles that promote the whole family’s well-being. There is also need to devise culturally sensitive ways to engage fathers/male guardians about the societal gender norms, cultural beliefs, and misconceptions about care for PWE. This requires employing a multifaceted approach that includes public health education, and community-based interventions that encourage fathers to become active participants in the care of their children with epilepsy so as to address these barriers.

Another proposed sensitization channel involved school-age children, as they can be excellent peer educators as well as change agents at the household level [49-51]. While there are few studies in low resource settings, findings from research done in Western countries shows that educational interventions are particularly effective with school children and their teachers. One Czech study [52] demonstrated reduced stigma following use of a video and story, while a cluster randomized Canadian trial [53], showed a significant increase in epilepsy knowledge and positive attitudes after the intervention. In Uganda, as part of the draft school health policy [54], all staff in any educational institution shall have sensitization/training on health issues. However, it is not clear if or to what extent such training has included epilepsy. Although schools have been identified as significant settings for care and support in the response to the HIV/AIDS pandemic [55], and Malaria control[56], for examples, there is currently no research on how these potential channels could be utilized for sensitization in epilepsy care in Ugandan schools. Possible sensitization tools in schools might include the use of electronic media-radio, television, 'edutainment' approaches, using music, dance, and drama to deliver prevention messages; print media – pull outs, magazines, supplements; School debates; Facilitated interactive sessions during adolescent club activities; Youth camps; Text messaging and the Internet. There is a need to explore the suitability, feasibility, and approach required to take on such epilepsy sensitization projects among school children in Uganda.

Advocacy of a broad epilepsy community sensitization campaign

The proposed seven components of the Epilepsy Health Literacy program aim to enhance care for AWE by addressing multiple key focus areas. One of these elements included fostering supportive environments for PWE and their families. Unfortunately, there are few support groups in Uganda, though the work of the Epilepsy Support Association Uganda [57] and Purple Bench Initiative are notable [58]. Support groups provide a unique opportunity for PWE to connect with others who understand the complexities of living with the disease, they offer a valuable source of information, emotional support, practical advice, and a sense of community. In addition, by virtue of the group dynamics, they are able to amplify their voices in awareness campaigns, educational events, and advocacy efforts to promote understanding and support for those living with epilepsy [59]. Support groups are not only effective, but also easy and inexpensive/cheap to implement in poor or low- income community settings [60]. There is a need to advocate for the creation of more of these support groups across the country.

Kajumba and colleagues [33] (this issue) illustrated that the community most often holds pluralistic assumptions, whereby biological, social, and/or spiritual factors can interact in the causation, manifestation, and treatment of epilepsy. This confirms the need for broad community education about the biological causation and treatability of the illness. A notable finding from our study was the importance of involving traditional healers and faith leaders in sensitization efforts. They play a crucial role in shaping perceptions of epilepsy, influencing how people with epilepsy are viewed and treated within the community, potentially leading to stigma and marginalization unless their role is positively leveraged. This dearth of knowledge regarding epilepsy is not limited to Uganda alone. It is noteworthy that a significant proportion of the African population, particularly in rural areas, lacks fundamental biomedical knowledge about epilepsy, its causes, and management, which contributes to delayed diagnosis and impedes successful disease management. Studies from Southern Africa [61], West Africa [62], Central Africa [63] and East Africa, in Uganda [14,64] all illustrate the various misconceptions about the causes of epilepsy and the appropriate care-seeking pathway. The limitation in epilepsy literacy encompasses not only the general public but also includes healthcare professionals, cumulatively resulting in restricted access to healthcare, and inadequate treatment, which then further fuels social stigma.

The pervasive nature of the prevailing stigmatizing and negative attitudes towards PWE noted in this study is complex and varied, influenced by cultural, traditional, and religious factors that stem from beliefs and perceptions about persons with disabilities in Uganda [65] This stigmatization may lead not only to rejection, neglect, denial of access to basic services, abuse, and marginalization of PWE but also to explicit exclusion from community activities, self-stigmatization, and family stigmatization, resulting in social withdrawal or hiding of these individuals.

As depicted in the explanatory models of epilepsy in Uganda [33] (in this issue) it is often believed that PWE can transmit their seizures through touch (biological contagion); passing over the area where someone has experienced a seizure (spiritual contagion), or showing amusement at a PWE during a seizure (socio-spiritual contagion). Irrespective of the explanatory model of epilepsy adopted, belief in the spiritual causation of epilepsy is the major driver determining the traditional or pastoral healing option over biological medical care [14]. This is a critical misconception that must be addressed. Other critical health literacy areas highlighted in our findings include training caregivers on proper seizure management, addressing environmental risks, and emphasizing medication adherence, all of which can improve outcomes while respecting cultural beliefs and values.

Changing these perceptions requires collective and individual efforts of all stakeholders to promote and protect the rights of PWE. Whereas Uganda's Constitution explicitly prohibits discrimination based on disability, and the Persons with Disabilities Act, 2020 further reinforces this stance, there is a need for an effective strategy for change that is well operationalized, measurable, and enforceable. Building blocks will involve addressing negative attitudes and misconceptions among leaders everywhere, including health workers and service providers, politicians, administrators, and religious/cultural leaders. This might best be accomplished through a multipronged community epilepsy awareness program, in order to shift the mindsets of the community at large. The removal of stigmatizing attitudes and behaviors will require significant education and investment, but would be the foundation of inclusivity and participation of PWE and those with disabilities in general in community life. Therefore, this recommendation of a broadbased education program should particularly be targeted towards those who directly impact the lives of PWE/AWE and who serve as important role models (e.g., teachers, faith leaders, healthcare workers, village health team members, local council members, and elders/community leaders).

Refurbishment of the Uganda epilepsy health care infrastructure

Our results indicated that stigma and misconceptions about epilepsy can be found, even among healthcare workers in Uganda. This highlights critical gaps in provider education, which is catastrophic not only as they should be the source of life-changing medical care but also because they are professional examples in the community. Improved knowledge of epilepsy, its treatment, and its psychosocial effects among healthcare workers as a result of brief, focused training is possible, as shown by Arinda and colleagues [66]. While this study showed significant gains in epilepsy knowledge, it did not evaluate the impact of improved knowledge on clinical practice strategies. Others have also recommended [67] that to ensure that PWE and families have access to high-quality epilepsy care, health care workers should augment their knowledge base and skill sets in early diagnosis, treatment, and support of PWE. In addition, our results showed that health care workers could strengthen public health approaches through disseminating educational materials and developing innovative educational tools and technologies. All these avenues align with the IGAP, which emphasizes the need for enhanced education and training for healthcare workers to improve epilepsy care [68], but require time and resources to accomplish.

Another key finding was the perception of the community on the need for free and consistent ASMs. This resonates with Strategic Objective 2 in the IGAP which states “Provide effective, timely and responsive diagnosis, treatment and care”. In line with IGAP advocacy for improved epilepsy care, ILAE and IBE jointly proposed a 90%–80%–70% target-oriented strategy, which requires that at least 90% of people with epilepsy worldwide are appropriately diagnosed, at least 80% are on appropriate and affordable treatment/s, and at least 70% of those treated attain complete seizure remission without experiencing side effects [69]. Approximately 70% of PWE can have good seizure control with treatment, however the epilepsy treatment gap prevalent in many countries hinders this possibility. All groups who participated in this study noted inconsistent or absent medications as an immense barrier to care. PWE in Uganda encounter a large epilepsy treatment gap that ranges between (66–79%) [8,70] depending on the population assessed. Based on findings of a study that examined the epilepsy treatment practices within three of the largest Ugandan public referral hospitals, it was noted that medication usage varied between sites and carbamazepine use made up for more than half of all prescribed ASMs across all sites and age groups [71]. The Uganda Essential Medicines List (EML) includes carbamazepine, diazepam, lorazepam, midazolam, phenobarbital, phenytoin, ethosuximide and valproic acid [72]. Often these drugs are not available at the health units where they are needed for adequate seizure control, even though they are relatively inexpensive [73]. Achievement of the 90%–80%–70% target-oriented strategy remains difficult for the Ugandan health care system to attain without support from stakeholders, but are ideal key performance indicators.

Government commitment to epilepsy health service provision

The community respectfully and consistently called upon the Ministry of Health (MoH-‘government’) of Uganda to deliver the proposed health education literacy programs, conduct the sensitization campaigns and ensure improved access to anti-seizure medications. As Uganda strives towards progressive realization of rights of persons with disabilities, the following four strategic interventions by the government of Uganda, in support of health service provision for people with epilepsy are likely to make a difference in enhancing effective management of epilepsy. Such investment would support not only the quality of life of PWE in Uganda, but also return many to occupational, social, and academic functioning.

Support for epilepsy public sensitization campaigns

The Ugandan health system has exhibited success in public sensitization campaigns to change sexual behaviours and prevent HIV/AIDS [74]. The HIV/AIDS example was reiterated by several participants as a benchmark that could be utilized to model the sensitization campaigns for epilepsy. Other examples included campaigns to increase the uptake of immunization and control immunizable diseases among children [75]. Despite this progress, it has struggled to pivot to the support of now prevalent noncommunicable diseases such as epilepsy.

Among the contributory factors is the complexity of public sensitization campaigns, characterized by the involvement of a multiplicity of stakeholders and independent support groups. These stakeholders are mainly civil society organizations or charities operating with donor funds alongside government programmes, but running totally independent programmes. These often lead to duplication of efforts, the creation of multiple mixed messages which may in turn cause confusion than clarity. The MoH is in a unique position to identify and understand the relevant stakeholder groups, and equip them with redesigned standard messages that communicate in a manner that gives adequate time for the population to understand. In the creation of epilepsy public health campaigns, the government could possibly partner with local healthcare providers and Non-Governmental Organizations and Civil Society Organizations of Persons with Epilepsy, as well as with professional organizations (e.g ILAE) and/or layperson organizations (e.g IBE), commercial entities, universities, research institutions and industry. Such partnerships would require commitment of dedicated budgets to design and implement such campaigns in a coordinated fashion, maximizing use of each stakeholder group skillset and resources. The government could also establish epilepsy hotlines for PWE, their families, and anyone seeking information on the topic to provide a confidential space to discuss concerns, receive support, and access accurate information about epilepsy.

Support for improvement in epilepsy healthcare infrastructure

In line with IGAP strategic objective 1, the government of Uganda could raise policy prioritization and strengthen governance by including epilepsy service provision in national health plans and budgets and improving the epilepsy healthcare infrastructure. Of the highest priority would be the assurance of a consistent supply of anti-seizure medications (ASMs) with alternate avenues for its distribution at primary and follow-up treatment sites and the expansion of the access of supportive diagnostic equipment like portable EEG machines [76].

In addition to resolving medication stock-outs and possibly reassessing the type of medications available at the various facilities, the shortage of epilepsy specialists might be partially offset through task shifting and sharing [77], with targeted training of primary care health workers on epilepsy diagnosis and management to enhance knowledge and skills among these providers, who are more accessible than specialists. Training, coupled with a strong referral system, can minimize fragmentation within the healthcare system that can lead to gaps in continuity of care.

The empowerment of the lower level health facilities to enable them handle management of PWE was raised as a recommendation. Oftentimes these services are limited to the urban cities and at the general and regional referral hospitals (RRH). Whereas the need to promote comprehensive epilepsy care services at these hospitals is also crucial there is an urgent need to provide the necessary support to those lower level health facilities with weak mechanisms to screen, refer and track epilepsy patients by strengthening the identification and epilepsy care referral system [14]. Construction and rehabilitation of dedicated epilepsy clinics in both urban and rural areas to provide specialized care, minimize patient referral and associated burdens, and to alleviate the patient load on other facilities is recommended [76].

Support for health financing and resource allocation for PWE

As previously recorded participants emphasized the need for consistent free access to anti-seizure medications which is a great challenge to realize in this resource-limited country of Uganda. The care for a PWE confers a large financial burden, with substantial costs related to purchasing medication, which is required when there are drug stockouts at the health units or when a medication specific to a type of epilepsy is needed that is not stocked locally. Additional costs are shouldered from transportation to obtain health care, long term therapy, and neurodiagnostic investigations like electroencephalogram (EEG) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) that are needed for diagnosis and treatment planning. Other indirect and often ignored costs include the indirect trauma and other costs to the caregivers, most often relegated to family members, usually mothers or other female relatives, as well as the opportunity costs of income foregone from incapacity by the PWE and caregivers. These direct and indirect costs have been identified as the largest barrier to reaching epilepsy care among PWE in Uganda [78]. Family members or social networks often try to meet these needs informally, but oftentimes this approach is insufficient. The resulting unmet needs of PWE can exacerbate their symptoms and other health-related conditions, reduce functioning, restrict participation, limit activities, increase dependency on others for assistance, and decrease overall quality of life [79]. These challenges call for a multifaceted approach by the government of Uganda that involves increasing government funding for epilepsy and other neurological diseases, improving the Ministry of Health’s resource allocation efficiency, strengthening community-based interventions, and exploring sustainable financing mechanisms like health insurance and the utilization of social protection programs [1].

Support for the enforcement of disability policies and social protection programs

Despite Uganda taking steps to incorporate disability inclusion within its legislative framework, the social protection mechanisms for persons with disabilities, that includes those with epilepsy, are weak and existing laws and policies are not enforced to their full potential. The government of Uganda, through the Ministry of Gender, Labor, and Social Development actively promotes disability inclusion with emphasis on livelihood development for persons with disabilities. Uganda established a social protection system which has evolved since its inception in 2010, reflecting the government's commitment to addressing poverty, vulnerability, and inequality within its population. Central to this effort is the National Social Protection Policy (NSPP), introduced in 2015, which serves as a guiding framework for various interventions aimed at safeguarding the well-being of Ugandans. For example, 10% of the “Parish Development Model” fund is ring-fenced for persons with disabilities. Similarly, other wealth creation Government led initiatives like “Emyooga fund”provide loans to communities to boost wealth and job creation by providing financial support to specialized enterprise groups.

Regrettably, a large implementation gap remains in these social protection schemes in Uganda. They are not disability sensitive and have weak enforcement for effective inclusion of persons with disabilities or social protection services. These schemes do not cater for extra costs relating to disability, yet parents and families have limited capacity to cope with the financial effects of moderate and severe disability. Furthermore, there is inadequate coordination of delivery of social protection at national and district local governments including non-state actors leading to duplication of services and inefficiencies. To ameliorate this situation, the government needs to invest in scaling up disability inclusion within the social protection system and strengthen the coordination networks so as to enhance resilience for persons with disabilities including PWE.

It is commendable that the Ugandan government has undertaken several legislative and policy measures to protect and strengthen the rights of persons with disabilities, however, while some progress has been made in some areas, equal access to education, employment, health, and social services remains elusive. Should the government of Uganda actively partner with the National Council for Persons with Disabilities to monitor and enforce implementation of these protections and promote cooperation within disability programs, these goals would be achievable.

Limitations

The community perceptions and recommendations described here originated from information gathered through focus group discussions and in-depth interviews with participants’ with and without epilepsy or having had a close experience with someone having epilepsy, in only one district, Wakiso in Uganda. Therefore, these findings may not generalize to all settings, but likely are applicable to other similar low resource settings where a PWE resides.

Every effort was made to ensure that there was a good balance in the characteristics of the interviewers such as age, sex, level of experience and to build rapport with the participants. However, we cannot rule out that some unknown factors may have influenced the range of responses we obtained.

The study did not formally assess the level of epilepsy knowledge of the study participants or expertise of the health workers in diagnosing and managing epilepsy which may have influenced the responses of the participants. Notably, because basic epilepsy care training is not included in the teacher training curriculum therefore they may have not been familiar with these issues. There may have also been some social desirability bias in the responses to the questions since the participants were aware that this was a research study.

Conclusion

The community’s perspectives highlight invaluable insights into ways to enhance effective epilepsy management in Uganda. These approaches fall under four broad categories, or pillars, which include (1) Education of PWE/AWE their families about epilepsy, its treatments and the importance of medical adherence; (2) Broad community sensitization through public awareness campaigns to reduce stigma and facilitate biomedical treatment and inclusion, with particular attention to involving community leaders, teachers and healthcare providers, and policy makers; (3) Refurbishment of the Uganda epilepsy health care infrastructure, including more hands-on training of primary care health workers, strengthening the identification and epilepsy care referral system, and provision of consistent ASMs with alternate avenues for their distribution; (4) Government commitment to facilitate sensitization on epilepsy, coordinate epilepsy health education/sensitization campaigns among local and national government institutions, improve epilepsy health care infrastructure, strengthen and enforce legal frameworks for effective inclusion of PWE in social protection services, provide for ring-fenced budget allocations earmarked for PWE in related programs to responsible government institutions, and create a national partnership with the local Ugandan ILAE and IBE chapters led by the Ministry of Gender, Labour and Social Development (MoGLSD) to ensure that “no one with epilepsy in Uganda is left behind”. Overall, effective epilepsy management requires a holistic approach that targets several stakeholders, including PWE, families, the wider community of local leaders and people of influence, and government, as well as integration of numerous support systems, with the ultimate goal of addressing the challenges faced by PWE and fostering increased access to affordable and sustainable epilepsy care. The consideration of the PWE’s perspectives is an essential step in the creation of meaningful and effective intervention strategies and in empowering the epilepsy community towards improved acceptance, uptake of epilepsy care, and quality of life.
