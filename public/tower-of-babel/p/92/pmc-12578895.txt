Familial and genetic relationships of major depressive disorders and adiposity markers in the community
Authors: Alexandre Berney, Giorgio Pistis, Marie-Pierre F Strippoli, Enrique Castelao, Setareh Ranjbar, Peter Vollenweider, Julien Vaucher, Pedro Marques-Vidal, Zoltán Kutalik, Aurélie Lasserre, Carole Clair, Armin von Gunten, Kathleen R Merikangas, Jennifer Glaus, Caroline L Vandeleur, Martin Preisig
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe mechanisms underlying the association between major depressive disorders (MDD) and obesity are still only partially understood. We aimed to explore these mechanisms using two approaches: 1) studying the parent-offspring transmission patterns of MDD and its subtypes and of the adiposity markers BMI and waist circumference, and 2) assessing the associations of parental polygenic risk scores (PRS) for MDD and BMI with MDD and adiposity markers in parents and offspring. Data on parents (N = 467) and their 18 to 35-year-old offspring (N = 592; mean age: 26.5 (s.d. 4.4) years) were derived from a community study conducted in an urban area. Psychiatric DSM-IV disorders including MDD and its atypical and melancholic subtypes were assessed using semi-structured diagnostic interviews. Phenotypic analyses provided evidence for a strong parent-child transmission of BMI (β = 0.33 kg/m2, 95% Confidence Intervals (CI): 0.24;0.42) and waist circumference (β = 0.28 cm, 0.19;0.38), but not for MDD or its subtypes or a cross-transmission of MDD and adiposity markers. Parental PRS for MDD were not only associated with MDD and its subtypes in parents, but also with BMI and waist circumference levels in parents (β = 0.49 kg/m2, 0.11;0.86; β = 2.00 cm, 1.05;3.00, respectively) and offspring (β = 0.54 kg/m2, 0.16;0.91; β = 1.27 cm, 1.17;2.37, respectively), whereas parental PRS for BMI were only associated with adiposity marker levels in parents and offspring. In conclusion, our findings relying on PRS suggest that the associations between atypical MDD and elevated adiposity marker levels may at least be partially attributable to common genetic liability. In contrast, the absence of parent-child transmission of MDD or its subtypes as well as the cross-transmission of MDD and adiposity markers according to our phenotypic analyses could be attributable to the young age of our offspring cohort.Subject terms: Scientific community, Pathogenesis

Introduction

Meta-analytical findings support associations between major depressive disorders (MDD) and obesity [1, 2], which are both major public health challenges [3, 4]. Recent meta-analyses of cross-sectional [5] and longitudinal studies [6] suggest that within MDD particularly the atypical (generally characterized by symptoms of increase in appetite or weight) rather than the melancholic subtype is associated with elevated body mass index (BMI) or obesity. The mechanisms underlying these associations are still poorly understood [7]. It has been suggested that depression could increase vulnerability to elevated weight gain or the converse [1, 7, 8]. Alternatively, depression and overweight/adiposity could share etiological factors [1, 7, 8]. A classic strategy to determine the underlying mechanism relies on the study of the co-aggregation of two disorders within families. According to this approach, if disorder A predisposes to disorder B (e.g. if we assume that depression predisposes to adiposity) we would expect to find a statistically significant association between disorder A (i.e. depression) in parents and disorder B (i.e. adiposity) in relatives [9]. However, this association would disappear after adjusting for disorder A (i.e. depression) in relatives, given that the rate of disorder B (i.e. adiposity) would only be elevated among the relatives who were previously affected with the causal disorder A (i.e. depression in our example). In contrast, if the two disorders share common etiological factors, we would expect to also find cross-aggregation, i.e. a significant association between disorder A (i.e. depression) in parents and disorder B (i.e. adiposity) in offspring after adjusting for disorder A (i.e. depression) in relatives. Although a series of studies and meta-analyses have consistently documented the specific familial aggregation of MDD [10] and its atypical subtype [11] as well as adiposity and BMI [12], the co-aggregation between depression and adiposity or BMI levels, has rarely been established. One of the rare studies focusing on this topic was the NIMH Family Study of Affective Spectrum Disorders. Relying on a large community-based sample enriched for participants with mood disorders, semi-structed diagnostic interviews and partially measured BMI data, the results of this study suggested a causal relationship rather than a common diathesis [13]. This causal relationship was essentially restricted to the atypical depression subtype driving the increase in overweight. The large multigenerational population-based Lifelines Cohort study conducted in the North of the Netherlands, in which more than 100'000 first-degree relatives were interviewed, supported co-aggregation of depression and BMI or adiposity [14]. However, the analyses did not assess for comorbid adiposity in probands or relatives with depression or comorbid depression in probands or relatives with adiposity, thereby not allowing for conclusions regarding the existence of cross-aggregation.

More recently common genetic liability between depression and BMI or adiposity was studied using polygenic risk scores (PRS). Indeed, PRS derived from very large data sets are available for both BMI [15] and MDD [16]. Several studies relying on PRS supported genetic overlap between depression and BMI or adiposity as summarized in the article of Wang et al. [14]. A recent study applying Mendelian Randomization (MR) provided evidence for a bidirectional causal relationship of adiposity with MDD [17]. However, the Dutch NESDA study suggested that the association between the PRS for BMI and MDD is essentially restricted to a major depression subtype characterized by atypical symptoms of increase in appetite or weight [18].

Using a population-based sample, we aimed to explore the mechanisms underlying the association between MDD and adiposity by 1) studying the parent-offspring transmission patterns of MDD, BMI and waist circumference, and 2) assessing the associations of parental PRS for MDD and BMI with MDD and adiposity markers in parents and offspring. Our semi-structured diagnostic interview also allowed us to dichotomize MDD into atypical and others and to study the co-aggregation between this MDD subtype and adiposity markers.

Methods

Participants

The data of the present paper stemmed from CoLaus|PsyCoLaus [19, 20] a prospective cohort study initially including 6 734 randomly selected 35 to 75-year-old residents of the city of Lausanne according to the civil register. The study was designed to explore associations between mental disorders and cardiovascular risk factors or diseases. Participants underwent physical and psychiatric investigations at the baseline and already completed three follow-up evaluations. Within the whole cohort, psychiatric information could be collected on 5 144 participants (76%). During follow-up 3, which took place from 2018 to 2021, and follow-up 4, which started in 2022 and is ongoing, we systematically invited the 18 to 35-year-old offspring of participants to also complete the physical and psychiatric evaluations. A total of 640 offspring took part in these two evaluations. Among them 48 offspring were excluded because their parent met criteria for bipolar, schizoaffective, psychotic or eating disorder, because these disorders were also likely to be associated with elevated adiposity markers. This resulted in a final sample of 592 offspring within 467 families. There were 357 families with one offspring, 96 with two offspring and 14 with three and more offspring.

Procedures

Psychiatric measures

Both parents and offspring were interviewed by masters-level psychologists who had completed intensive training over at least a one-month period. Training included supervision of videotaped interviews by clinically experienced senior psychologists. Interviewers were blind to the disease status of the family members in any given family. Information on mental disorders including mood [21], alcohol and drug use disorders [22] was collected using the French version [21] of the Diagnostic Interview for Genetic Studies (DIGS) [23], which has revealed excellent inter-rater reliability, and slightly lower reliability for the 6-week test-retest [21, 22]. The DIGS was completed with the Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD) and PTSD sections of of the French version [24] of the anxiety section of the Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia-Lifetime and Anxiety disorders (SADS-LA) [25] and the brief phobia chapter of the DIGS was replaced by the corresponding more extensive chapters of the SADS-LA which elicited detailed information relating to the DSM-IV criteria for agoraphobia with or without panic attacks, social and specific phobias. These sections revealed excellent inter-rater and fair-to-good test-retest reliability [24, 26]. Psychiatric lifetime diagnoses were assigned according to the DSM-IV. MDD was dichotomized according to the lifetime history of episodes with atypical features according to the DSM-IV specifiers (i.e. mood reactivity and two or more of the following symptoms: increase in appetite (weight gain not included), hypersomnia, leaden paralysis, long-standing pattern of interpersonal rejection sensitivity) into those with and without such episodes across the lifespan.

Other covariates

Socio-demographic characteristics in parents were assessed with the DIGS: sex, age and marital status and the socio-economic status (SES) of the family according to the parental report, defined according to the Hollingshead scale [27]. Subjects were considered to be physically inactive if they reported doing physical activity less than once a week. Smoking status was defined as current, former or never.

Somatic measures

Body weight and height were measured by trained study nurses with participants standing without shoes in light indoor clothes. Weight was measured after fasting for 8 h. Waist circumference was determined using a non-stretchable tape over the unclothed abdomen at the mid-point between the lowest rib and the iliac crest, and the average of two measurements was used for analyses.

Polygenic risk scores calculations in parents

Genotyping, quality control and imputation

Nuclear DNA was extracted from whole blood of 6 043 participants from European descent (89.7% of the entire sample) at the baseline evaluation of the study. Genome-wide genotyping was performed using the Affymetrix 500 K SNP array and the Affymetrix Axiom SNP array. Genotypes were called using BRLMM (http://www.affymetrix.com/support/technical/whitepapers/brlmm_whitepap). Genome-wide genetic ancestry was inferred using SNP weights version 2.1.3 [28] and individuals who did not segregate with European samples were excluded. Duplicate individuals, and first- and second-degree relatives, were identified and then removed by computing estimates pair-wise genomic kinship coefficients, using KING [29]. Subjects were excluded from the analysis in case of inconsistency between sex and genetic data, a genotype call rate of less than 90%, resulting in 5 809 samples. Quality control for SNPs was performed using the following exclusion criteria: monomorphic (or with minor allele frequency (MAF) < 1%), call rates of less than 90%, deviation from the Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) (p < 1×10−6). Phased haplotypes were generated using SHAPEIT2 [30, 31]. Imputation was performed using minimac3 [32] and the Haplotype Reference Consortium (HRC version r1.1) [33] hosted on the Michigan Imputation Server [32]. We used imputed allele dosages for all SNPs to avoid genotyping missingness. A MAF > 1% and an imputation quality Rsq > 0.3 were required for the inclusion of the variant into further analyses. A dummy binary variable was created to correct for a possible batch effect due to the use of the two genotyping arrays. Polygenic risk scores were derived using the “standard weighted allele” method implemented in PRSice-2 [34] using standardized effect sizes from large genome-wide association studies (GWAS) on BMI [15] and MDD [16] that included only individuals of European descent. Linkage disequilibrium (LD) clumping was performed to retain only data for independent SNPs (r2 < 0.01, 1 Mb window, minimum allele frequency (MAF) > 1%). Finally, we applied the GWAS p-value thresholds (pt) of <1.0 and ≤ 0.05 for BMI and MDD respectively, as suggested in the literature [15, 35].

Among the 467 parents included in the present paper, the PRS for BMI and MDD could be computed for 397 of them. The included parents had 502 offspring.

Data analysis

For parents, data from the baseline evaluation were used. Descriptive statistics of the sample characteristics were provided as mean and (standard deviation) for continuous variables and number of observation (percentages) for categorical variables, respectively. The differences of these characteristics between parental MDD subtypes were tested using chi-square tests or ANOVA as appropriate.

Intra-individual associations of lifetime MDD status (any MDD or subtypes) with BMI and waist circumference in parents were assessed using linear models, whereas intra-individual associations among these variables in offspring were assessed using linear mixed models. Given the high correlation between BMI and waist circumference (r = 0.81, p < 0.0001 in parents, and r = 0.68, p < 0.0001 in offspring), separate models were run to test the associations between BMI and waist circumference. Associations of parental variables (MDD status or adiposity markers as independent variables) with continuous (adiposity markers as dependent variables) or categorical variables in offspring (MDD status) were assessed using linear mixed models and generalized linear mixed models with logistic and multinomial logistic links, respectively. All models were adjusted for sex, age, anxiety and substance use (alcohol/drug abuse/dependence) disorders, smoking status (current/former/none) and physical inactivity status (yes/no) in both parents and offspring as well as SES of the family. In addition, models which involved offspring were adjusted for intra-familial correlations (potentially more than one offspring per family) as a random intercept.

The associations of BMI, waist circumference and lifetime MDD status (any MDD or subtypes) in parents with the parental standardized PRS for MDD and BMI were assessed using generalized linear regression models (linear, logistic and multinomial links, respectively) with the same adjustments as for the respective phenotypic analyses plus an additional adjustment for a batch effect. For offspring, the same association with the parental standardized PRS for MDD and BMI were assessed using generalized linear mixed models (linear, logistic and multinomial links, respectively), with the same adjustments as for the respective phenotypic analysis plus an additional adjustment for a batch effect. The models jointly included the two scores.

In case of a significant association between parental PRS for MDD and adiposity marker levels in parents or offspring, we ran additional models with adjustments for MDD phenotypes, in order to determine whether MDD phenotypes were potential mediators in the association between the parental PRS for MDD and adiposity marker levels. Similarly, in case of a significant association between parental PRS for BMI and MDD diagnoses in parents or offspring, additional models with adjustments for adiposity marker levels were planned. For all analyses, the statistical significance level was set at p < 0.05 (two-sided). Although analyses with adiposity markers were run separately for BMI and waist circumference, these analyses were not adjusted for multiple comparisons given the high correlation (r = 0.81 in parents, r = 0.76 in offspring) between the two variables. All statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Analysis System, version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Characteristics of parents and offspring

A total of 205 out of the 467 parents (43.9%) reported any lifetime MDD, 39 (8.4%) lifetime MDD with at least one atypical episode. The mean age of parents was 47.4 (s.d. 6.6) years. A proportion of 51.4% were women. The mean BMI and waist circumference were 24.8 (s.d. 4.0) kg/m2 and 86.4 (s.d.) 11.9 cm, respectively.

The description of the parent and offspring samples in function of MDD status in parents is provided in Table 1. Parents differed by MDD diagnostic status for sex, marital status and the occurrence of lifetime anxiety disorders. Regarding BMI and waist circumference, the unadjusted ANOVA analyses revealed no association of MDD status with adiposity markers. Applying linear regression models adjusted for sex, age, smoking status, inactivity as well as anxiety and substance use disorders (results not shown in Table 1) any MDD was still not associated with BMI (β = 0.7 kg/m2, 95% Confidence intervals (C.I.) −0.1-1.4, p = 0.0856) or waist circumference (β = 1.8 cm, 95% C.I. −0.1-3.8, p = 0.0634). However, there were significant associations between atypical MDD and both an increased BMI (β = 1.3 kg/m2, 95% C.I. 0.0-2.7, p = 0.0484) and waist circumference (β = 4.6 cm, 95% C.I. 1.2-8.0, p = 0.0086), but not between other MDD and BMI (β = 0.5 kg/m2, 95% C.I. −0.3-1.3, p = 0.2287) or waist circumference (β = 1.1 cm, 95% C.I. −0.9-3.2, p = 0.2717).

Associations of parental PRS scores with MDD status or adiposity marker levels in parents and offspring

Table 5 shows the associations of the parental PRS for MDD and BMI with these phenotypes and waist circumference in parents and offspring. According to these models, the parental PRS for BMI was strongly associated with the measured BMI and waist circumference in both parents and offspring, but not with the MDD phenotypes. The parental PRS for MDD was associated with MDD phenotypes in parents only, but not in offspring. However, this PRS also revealed associations with measured BMI and waist circumference in both parents and offspring. Indeed, every 1 s.d. increase in MDD-PRS in parents was associated with an increase in BMI of 0.49 kg/m2 and of 2.00 cm in waist circumference in parents as well as with an increase in BMI of 0.54 kg/m2 and of 1.27 cm in waist circumference in offspring. After additional adjustment for atypical MDD in parents, the size of the associations between the parental PRS for MDD and parental BMI (β = 0.43 kg/m2, 95% C.I. 0.05-0.80, p = 0.0263) or parental waist circumference (β = 1.82 cm, 95% C.I. 0.86-2.79, p = 0.0002) only marginally decreased and remained significant. Similarly, adjustment for atypical MDD in offspring only very marginally increased the size of the associations between the parental PRS for MDD and BMI (β = 0.55 kg/m2, 95% C.I. 0.17-0.93, p = 0.0044) or waist circumference (β = 1.32 cm, 95% C.I. 0.22-2.42, p = 0.0185) in offspring.

Discussion

Relying on semi-structured diagnostic interviews and measured adiposity markers, we conducted one of the rare studies that used both the phenotypic classical parent-offspring and the PRS approaches to assess potential shared liability between MDD and adiposity in a community sample. Our most saliant findings were: 1) the association between the atypical MDD subtype and adiposity markers was confirmed in parents, but not found in their offspring; 2) adiposity markers but not MDD or its subtypes showed significant parent-offspring transmission; 3) adiposity markers and MDD or its subtypes did not provide evidence for cross-transmission; 4) parental PRS for BMI was associated with the corresponding phenotype in both parents and offspring, but not with MDD or its subtypes; and 5) PRS for MDD was associated with MDD and its subtypes in parents only, but also with adiposity markers in both parents and offspring.

Intra-individual associations of MDD and its subtypes with adiposity markers in parents but not in offspring

Although the parental sample confirmed the association between the atypical but not the other MDD subtype and adiposity markers, we could not replicate this association in offspring. This lack of association in the offspring sample may be explained by the relatively young mean age of 26.5 years of this sample. Indeed, research that supported the associations between the atypical depression subtype and elevated BMI or adiposity relied on participants aged between 30 and 50 years [36–45]. Given recent meta-analytical evidence suggesting that the median age of onset of mood disorders including MDD is around 30 years [46, 47], it is likely that the majority of our offspring who will ultimately develop this phenotype were still unaffected at the time of their evaluation. This may be particularly true for the atypical subtype, which according to previous data emerges later than the other MDD subtypes [48]. Moreover, given evidence from previous analyses supporting the persistence of an elevated weight increase in participants with atypical MDD [42], the difference in adiposity markers between those who already met criteria for atypical MDD in the present study and other participants may reach the level of statistical significance only after a longer observation period.

Parent-child transmission of adiposity markers but not MDD and its subtypes

Our data confirmed a strong parent-child transmission of adiposity marker levels, which corroborates the findings of numerous previous studies [12]. In contrast, our data did not provide evidence for the transmission of MDD or its subtypes, although the negative association between parental atypical MDD and other MDD in offspring, which corroborates a previous finding of the NIMH Family Study of Affective Spectrum Disorders [11], still may suggest a certain specificity of the familial aggregation of MDD subtypes. The absence of parent-child transmission of MDD and its subtypes in our study is again likely to be explained by the relatively young age of the offspring. Indeed, research that consistently documented familial aggregation of MDD [10] or its subtypes [11], typically assessed first-degree relatives that were much older than our offspring. For instance, in the study of Lamers et al. [11], which supported the familial aggregation of atypical MDD, the mean age of these relatives was 42.3 years. The observed parent-offspring transmission of adiposity markers but not MDD in our relatively young offspring could be attributable to an earlier development of weight-related markers as compared to MDD phenotypes. However, this needs to be confirmed in future follow-up assessments of this cohort or similar studies including older offspring.

Absence of evidence for cross-transmission of MDD and its subtypes and adiposity markers

Considering the absence of parent-child transmission of MDD and its subtypes most likely due to the young age of the offspring, we were also unlikely to find an association between parental adiposity marker levels and MDD phenotypes in offspring. Indeed, the family studies that documented associations of adiposity markers in probands with MDD [14] or uniquely with its atypical subtype [13] generally assessed first-degree family members with a mean age of more than 40 years. Similarly, our data also did not provide evidence for associations between parental MDD or its subtypes and adiposity marker levels in offspring, which contrasts with the recent findings of the NIMH Family Study of Affective Spectrum Disorders and the Lifelines Cohort study conducted in North of Netherlands, which however assessed older first-degree relatives. Moreover, in the NIMH Family Study of Affective Spectrum Disorders the observed association of the atypical subtype of MDD in probands with BMI/overweight in their adult relatives disappeared after adjusting for lifestyle factors, suggesting a causal relationship [13], whereas in the very large Lifelines Cohort study conducted in the North of the Netherlands the established effect size for the recurrence risk of 1.10 for adiposity among first-degree relatives of depressed persons was of only a small effect size [14].

Associations of PRS for MDD and BMI with MDD and adiposity phenotypes in parents and offspring

As expected, parental PRS for MDD and BMI were associated with the respective phenotypes in parents. The parental PRS for BMI was also associated with adiposity marker levels in offspring, which is in line with the observed parent-offspring transmission of these phenotypes. In contrast, the parental PRS for MDD was only associated with MDD diagnoses in parents but not in offspring, which is also consistent with the observed absence of parent-offspring transmission of these phenotypes most probably due to the young offspring age. We also found significant associations between the parental PRS for MDD with adiposity marker levels in parents, whereas the parental PRS for BMI was not associated with MDD or its subtypes. Likewise, although we did not find an association between MDD or its subtypes in parents and adiposity markers in their offspring on the phenotypic level, the parental PRS for MDD was also significantly associated with adiposity markers levels in offspring. The associations of the parental PRS for MDD with adiposity marker levels in parents and offspring were not mediated by the atypical MDD phenotype, suggesting that the comorbidity of MDD and particularly its atypical subtype with adiposity could be explained by common genetic liability, predisposing to both depression and adiposity rather than by a causal relationship. However, as the two mechanisms are not exclusive, it is possible that a causal relationship, as suggested by the NIMH Family Study of Affective Spectrum [13] and a recent MR study [17], will emerge at subsequent follow-up evaluations, when a larger number of offspring likely present with MDD phenotypes. The absence of a phenotypic association between parental MDD and adiposity marker levels in offspring despite significant associations of these markers with the parental PRS for MDD is likely to be explained by environmental or behavioral factors such as stress, diet or other factors non assessed in our study with opposite effects on the development of parental MDD and adiposity in offspring, which thereby blurred the phenotypic association that would have been expected according to the genetic analysis.

Implications

Our phenotypic data provide further support to the well-established associations between the atypical MDD subtype and adiposity in middle-aged people, whereas these associations may not yet be manifest in young adults. Our results relying on both the classical phenotypic parent-offspring and the PRS approaches also provided additional insight into the mechanisms underlying these associations as well as on the risk of weight gain in offspring of parents with MDD. Interestingly, PRS analyses suggested shared genetic liability, whereas the absence of cross-transmission between MDD and adiposity phenotypes did not support the implications of shared etiological factors underpinning the comorbidity between MDD or its subtypes and adiposity. These apparently contradictory findings could be best explained by environmental or behavioral factors that blurred the phenotypic but not the genetic association. On the clinical level, this common genetic diathesis of MDD and adiposity would expose the offspring of parents with MDD to an increased risk of weight gain even if they are not affected by MDD themselves. Studies following the offspring of parents with MDD beyond the peak age of onset of MDD will be critical to inform whether, in addition to shared genes, which should ultimately lead to cross-aggregation between the two conditions, the increasing emergence of MDD with increasing age will further predispose these offspring to an elevated risk of adiposity through behaviors or drug treatment related to depression (causal mechanism).

Limitations

The findings of this study should be placed into the context of several limitations. First, despite a relatively large overall sample size, the proportion of parents and offspring who reported having experienced the atypical subtype of MDD was still small, involving the risk of false negative findings. Second, our offspring were still relatively young and were likely to not yet present the final MDD phenotype, which increased the risk of negative findings in analyses with this outcome in offspring. Third, the role of the co-parent, which is important to understand environmental and genetic influences on offspring outcomes, could not be assessed in this sample. Fourth, only a minority of offspring were already genotyped which impeded us from conducting analyses linking PRS in offspring with their MDD and adiposity phenotypes. Fifth, the inclusion of parents from European ancestry affects the generalizability of the results of our genetic analyses.

Conclusion

Although our phenotypic data provide further support to the consistently reported association between the atypical MDD subtype and adiposity in middle-aged people, they also suggest that these associations may not yet be manifest in young adults. This association between atypical MDD and adiposity could at least partially be attributable to common genetic liability, which is also likely to expose the offspring of parents with MDD to an increased risk of weight gain, regardless of whether they are affected by MDD themselves. Future follow-up assessments of this cohort or similar studies including older offspring are needed.
