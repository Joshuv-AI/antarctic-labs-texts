Isolation and characterization of probiotic candidates with antimicrobial and barrier-protective properties
Authors: Jiddu Joseph, Sanya Boby, Zhaoyan Zhu, Shankumar Mooyottu, Poonam Gopika Vinayamohan, Abraham Joseph Pellissery, Yihang Li, Muhammed Shafeekh Muyyarikkandy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) in pathogens such as multidrug-resistant Salmonella Typhimurium and extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) Escherichia coli poses a growing threat to human, animal, and environmental health, yet effective non-antibiotic mitigation strategies remain limited. Here, we report the isolation and comprehensive characterization of three lactic acid bacteria from homemade cottage cheese, including Leuconostoc pseudomesenteroides MLS3, Lactococcus lactis MLS12, and Leuconostoc lactis MLS22 as next-generation probiotic candidates targeting these AMR pathogens. Whole-genome sequencing confirmed compact genomes (1.7–2.5 Mb) free of transferable resistance determinants and enriched in secondary metabolite biosynthetic clusters. All three strains survived gastrointestinal challenge conditions (≥ 6 log CFU/mL at pH 1.5, 2% bile salts, and 100 µg/mL lysozyme), exhibited strong mucosal adhesion parameters (auto-aggregation 30–33%, surface hydrophobicity 20–49%), and co-aggregated efficiently with both pathogens (27–64%). Live co-cultures and cell-free supernatants significantly inhibited S. Typhimurium and ESBL E. coli within 24 h, with electron microscopy revealing probiotic-induced structural disruption of pathogen cell integrity. Probiotic pretreatment extended Caenorhabditis elegans lifespan under Salmonella challenge (P < 0.001), and cell-free supernatants attenuated LPS-induced epithelial barrier permeability in chicken enteroids (P < 0.05) without affecting baseline barrier function. Together, these findings demonstrate that MLS3, MLS12, and MLS22 combine genomic safety, gastrointestinal resilience, direct antimicrobial potency, and epithelial barrier protection, supporting their potential as effective probiotics for AMR-associated applications in food safety and gut health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54375-6.

Introduction

The global rise in infectious diseases, alongside escalating antimicrobial resistance (AMR), represents a critical public health challenge1. The Global Antimicrobial Resistance and Use Surveillance System (GLASS) 2025 reported that one in six laboratory-confirmed bacterial infections worldwide in 2023 were resistant to standard antibiotic treatments. Moreover, the resistance increased across more than 40% of monitored pathogen-antibiotic combinations between 2018 and 20232. Of particular concern are multidrug-resistant (MDR) Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium and extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) Escherichia coli, which exhibit high-level resistance to critically important antibiotics, causing substantial treatment failures and excess mortality worldwide3. Identifying effective alternatives to conventional antibiotics, whether through direct substitution or potentiation of existing agents, represents a priority research objective, and probiotics have emerged as a compelling strategy in this context4. While traditional probiotics belonging to the Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium genera dominate the market, emerging evidence highlights the potential of alternative genera, particularly Leuconostoc and Lactococcus isolated from fermented dairy products5,6. Such probiotic strains frequently exhibit superior tolerance to gastrointestinal stresses, stronger pathogen-competitive exclusion, and secretion of bioactive metabolites capable of reinforcing epithelial barrier function relative to conventional probiotic strains. Moreover, they demonstrate lower risks of horizontal antibiotic resistance gene transfer, which is a safety concern documented in some traditional probiotic genera7,8. In addition, these genera have demonstrated health benefits across human and animal contexts, including roles in managing metabolic conditions such as diabetes and in exerting anti-cancer activity9,10.

Despite the availability of numerous commercial probiotics with demonstrated health benefits, a critical gap persists, which is a lack of rigorous, integrated workflows that combine comprehensive genomic and phenotypic data with mechanistically informative functional assays11. Thorough probiotic characterization is therefore essential as a prerequisite for fully understanding microbial safety and functional potential. Whole-genome sequencing, when coupled with advanced annotation, comparative genomics, and phylogenetic analysis, enables resolution of evolutionary relationships, core and accessory gene content, secondary metabolite biosynthetic clusters, and mobile genetic elements, collectively informing both safety assessment and mechanistic prediction. Additionally, the presence or absence of specific beneficial genes, such as those encoding bacteriocins and carotenoid biosynthetic enzymes, may further confer strain-specific therapeutic properties12,13. These genomic insights directly bridge to phenotypic characterization, enabling evaluation of key functional attributes. These include gastrointestinal survival, mucosal adhesion, direct antimicrobial activity, probiotic-induced structural disruption of the pathogen, host-protective capacity, and preservation of epithelial barrier integrity under inflammatory stress5,14. Notably, the physicochemical stressors inherent to fermented food environments serve as natural selective forces that enrich for strains possessing precisely these resilience and bioactive traits. Such strains are therefore compelling candidates for next-generation probiotics with functional attributes superior to those of conventionally selected strains15–17. Among fermented dairy matrices, cottage cheese represents a particularly rich and underexplored reservoir, as its low-pH, high-osmotic-pressure environment imposes selective pressure that naturally favors lactic acid bacteria with inherent gastrointestinal resilience and bioactive potential18,19. This study, therefore, aimed to isolate and comprehensively characterize lactic acid bacteria from cottage cheese through an integrated genomic, in vitro, and in vivo analytical framework. We hypothesized that strains recovered from our homemade cottage cheese would possess genomic safety profiles, gastrointestinal resilience, and anti-pathogen activities required of next-generation probiotics targeting multidrug-resistant S. Typhimurium (MDR) and ESBL E. coli.

Methods

Isolation and identification of probiotic strains

Probiotic strains were isolated from homemade cottage cheese samples, which were stored at 4 °C until processing. Samples were directly streaked in triplicate onto De Man, Rogosa and Sharpe (MRS) agar (Oxoid, Basingstoke, UK) and incubated overnight at 37 °C under both aerobic and anaerobic conditions (GasPak™ EZ, Puerto Rico, ≤ 1% O2, > 13% CO2). From each plate, 1–2 colonies with lactic acid bacteria-like morphology (white, creamy, pinpoint) were randomly selected, yielding a total of 30 primary isolates. Colonies exhibiting the morphology were picked, propagated overnight in MRS broth at 37 °C, and purified by three consecutive subcultures. Isolates were characterized by Gram staining, cell morphology, and catalase reaction20. Gram-positive, coccus-shaped, catalase-negative strains were selected and cryopreserved in MRS broth containing 25% glycerol at -80 °C. Three representatives exhibiting distinct MALDI-TOF and genomic profiles were subsequently selected for detailed characterization. Each isolate was assigned a unique identifier (MLS followed by a number). This approach enabled reliable isolation and cryopreservation of candidate probiotic strains for downstream characterization.

Species identification

Isolates were initially identified by matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) using a Bruker Microflex LT instrument and MALDI Biotyper software (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany) at the Bronson Animal Disease Diagnostic Laboratory, University of Florida21. Scores ≥ 2.0 were considered high-confidence species matches, while scores below this threshold were assigned as a tentative identification. Species-level identification was subsequently confirmed by whole-genome sequencing.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

The probiotic isolates were submitted to the Bronson Animal Disease Diagnostic Laboratory (Florida Department of Agriculture & Consumer Services, Kissimmee, FL, USA) for antimicrobial susceptibility testing (Accession No. K2510701). Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs, µg/mL) were determined by the Clinical Laboratory and Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines22.

Whole genome sequencing

Pure bacterial cultures grown on MRS agar were submitted to SeqCenter (Pittsburgh, PA), for whole-genome sequencing. DNA extraction was performed using the ZymoBIOMICS™ DNA Miniprep Kit2 (Zymo Research Group, CA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Final DNA concentrations were determined using a Qubit 3 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA). Illumina sequencing libraries were prepared using the tagmentation-based and PCR-based Illumina DNA Prep kit and custom IDT 10 bp unique dual indices (UDI) with a target insert size of 280 bp. No additional DNA fragmentation or size selection steps were performed. Illumina sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus sequencer in one or more multiplexed shared-flow-cell runs, producing 2 × 151 bp paired-end reads. Demultiplexing, quality control, and adapter trimming were performed with bcl-convert1 (v4.2.4). Short-read assembly was performed using Unicycler 1. Assembly statistics were recorded with QUAST2. Whole-genome sequencing depth was calculated as the mean fold-coverage of reads mapped to the reference sequences using the breseq pipeline, with a minimum average threshold of 20x required for high-confidence variant calling across all probiotic strains. The genotypic features comparison and gene density analysis were performed in R.4.3.323 .

Genotypic characterization

Assembled genomes for Leuconostoc pseudomesenteroides (MLS3), Lactococcus lactis (MLS12) and Leuconostoc lactis (MLS22) were submitted to the comprehensive genome analysis service at PATRIC24 using BV-BRC 3.49.1. Genomes were annotated using the RAST25 toolkit (RASTtk) and assigned a unique genome identifier. All genomes were classified within the super kingdom Bacteria and annotated using genetic code 11. A subsystem is a set of proteins that together implement a specific biological process or structural complex, and PATRIC annotation includes an analysis of the subsystems unique to each genome. The Genome Annotation Service in BV-BRC employs two complementary AMR gene detection algorithms26. First, a k-mer-based method that queries a curated collection of representative AMR gene sequence variants and assigns functional annotation, resistance mechanism, and drug class. Second, a BLAT-based alignment method that identifies AMR genes through sequence similarity to reference resistance gene databases, capturing additional targets not resolved by k-mer matching alone. The closest reference and representative genomes were identified by Mash/MinHash27. PATRIC global protein families (PGFams)28 were selected from these genomes to determine the phylogenetic placement of this genome. The protein sequences from these families were aligned with MUSCLE29 , and the nucleotides for each of those sequences were mapped to the protein alignment. The joint set of amino acid and nucleotide alignments was concatenated into a data matrix, and RaxML30 was used to analyze this matrix, with fast bootstrapping employed to generate the support values in the tree. Further, the annotated genes were analyzed for the biosynthetic gene clusters using antiSMASH v831, core genome alignments were constructed using Roary v3.13.032. Additionally, concatenated core-gene phylogeny including representative lactic acid bacteria and non-LAB reference species (Escherichia coli and Bacillus subtilis) was inferred with IQTREE v3.0.133 and visualized using iTOL v6.9.134 .

Phenotypic characterization

Growth kinetics

Growth kinetics of the bacteria were analyzed as previously described, with minor modifications35. A single colony of each probiotic strain streaked and grown on MRS agar (Oxoid, UK) was inoculated into the MRS broth and incubated overnight at 37 °C. The strains were subcultured by transferring 1 mL of the broth, previously cultured at 37 °C, into a sterile culture tube containing 9 mL of sterile MRS broth. The culture was incubated overnight at 37 °C. The growth of the strains was monitored using two complementary methods: optical density (OD) measurements and viable colony counts. For OD600 measurements, aliquots of the culture were transferred into a clear-bottom 96-well plate (Fisher brand, MA), and absorbance readings were taken at each designated time point (0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 16, and 24 h) using SpectraMax M (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA). Simultaneously, viable counts were determined by plating serial dilutions onto MRS agar plates. Serial dilutions were prepared by adding 900 µL of diluent, phosphate-buffered saline (Fisher Bioreagents, MA), into a series of sterile tubes and transferring 100 µL from the previous dilution sequentially. For each dilution, 10 µL aliquots were plated in triplicate on two MRS agar plates labeled accordingly for the isolates. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, after which colonies were counted at the appropriate dilution level.

Auto-aggregation and co-aggregation

Auto-aggregation and co-aggregation tests were conducted as previously described, with minor modifications36. Auto-aggregation was assessed as an indirect indicator of intestinal colonization potential. Cells grown on MRS agar were washed twice and suspended in phosphate-buffered saline (Fisher Bioreagents, MA) (10⁶ CFU/mL). Suspensions were mixed by vortexing for 20 s, then transferred to 96-well plates (100 µl) and incubated at room temperature for 4 h to determine auto-aggregation. At 0 and 4 h, the OD600 value was measured at 600 nm. The following formula was used to calculate auto-aggregation of strain (%): [(A0 – At) /A0] × 100, where At and A0 denote the absorbance at 4 h and absorbance at 0 h, respectively. OD600 readings were blank corrected using PBS as a reference. For co-aggregation assays, the cell suspensions of the probiotics and pathogenic strains [S. Typhimurium ATCC 13311 (10⁶ CFU/mL) and E. coli ATCC BAA 196 (10⁶ CFU/mL)] were washed twice and then vortexed for 20 s after being suspended in PBS. The control wells were prepared using either the probiotic strain or the pathogenic strain alone or in combination. After incubation for 4 h, the absorbance of the suspension was measured at OD600. The following formula was used to calculate the coaggregation of a strain:{[(Ax + Ay)/2] – A mix / (Ax + Ay)/2} × 100, where Ax and Ay denote the bacterial suspension in the control wells, and A mix indicates the mixed bacterial suspension at 4 h.

Lysozyme tolerance test

Lysozyme tolerance was assessed as previously described, with minor modifications37. Overnight probiotic cultures (~ 8.5-9 log CFU/mL) were inoculated into MRS broth in the presence and absence of 100 µg/ml lysozyme and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. To test the tolerance, the bacterial count in the culture was enumerated by dilution and plating technique on MRS agar plates at 0, 2,4,6 and 24 h36.

Cell surface hydrophobicity

Probiotics in the exponential growth phase were centrifuged (10,000 × g, 5 min). The resulting pellet was washed twice in PBS, re-suspended in 3 ml of 0.1 mol/l KNO3 and the absorbance at 660 nm was measured (A0). One milliliter of xylene was added and the suspension was incubated at room temperature for 10 min. The two-phase system was formed before being mixed by vortexing for 2 min. The aqueous, water, and xylene phases were separated by incubating at room temperature for 20 min. Following this, the aqueous phase was carefully removed, and the absorbance at 660 nm was recorded (A1). The percentage of the cell surface hydrophobicity was calculated as (1 − A1 / A0) × 10038 .

Bile salt tolerance

Bile salt tolerance was assessed by growing each probiotic strain overnight in MRS broth. Further, the cell suspensions adjusted to 10⁶ CFU/mL were inoculated into 10 mL of fresh MRS media containing 0.3% (physiological) and 2% (high) of bile salts (Oxoid, UK). The broths were incubated for 6 h and cell viability was determined by serial dilution and plating onto MRS agar after 0, 3, and 6 h incubation36.

Acid tolerance

Acid tolerance was assessed as previously described, with minor modifications39,40. MRS broth was adjusted with 1 N HCl (Fisher Scientific, MA) and 1 N NaOH (Fisher Scientific, MA) at pH levels 1.5, 2 and 4. Freshly prepared probiotic cultures (~ 6 log CFU/mL) were inoculated into pH-adjusted or unadjusted MRS broth (control). The tubes were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. The pH of each tube was measured at 0, 4, and 6 h and remained stable (within ± 0.1 pH units of the starting value). After 24 h of incubation, the pH of each tube was measured to estimate the pH content. The cultures were plated in MRS agar after serial dilution to different concentrations at 0, 4, 6, and 24 h. The plates were incubated overnight, and bacterial colonies were enumerated to determine the concentration in CFU/mL.

Hemolytic activity

Probiotic isolates were streaked on the surface of Columbia blood agar plates (Oxoid, UK) supplemented with 5% sheep blood. After 48 h of incubation at 30 °C, the plates were examined for hemolytic reaction41. Experiments were performed in triplicate, and results were interpreted as the green zones around colonies as α-hemolysis, clear zones around colonies as β-hemolysis, and no zones around colonies as γ-hemolysis on Columbia blood agar plates.

Antibacterial activity

Co-culture assay

The antibacterial activity of probiotic strains against S. Typhimurium and ESBL-producing E. coli was evaluated using a co-culture assay38. Individual probiotic strains (~ 8 log CFU/mL) were co-cultured with S. Typhimurium ATCC 13,311, ESBL E. coli BAA 196 (~ 6–7 log CFU/mL) in broth containing equal quantities (5 ml) of MRS and Lauria-Bertani (LB) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. This was to facilitate the simultaneous growth of both populations. Baseline growth kinetics for each strain were established in monoculture using the same 1:1 medium, serving as the primary control for all competitive interaction assays. The surviving Salmonella and E. coli population in the co-culture was enumerated at different time points (0, 2, 4,8,16, 24 h) following serial dilution and plating on MacConkey agar (Oxoid, UK) plates42. All experiments were performed in triplicate with two replicates per treatment.

Antibacterial activity with cell free supernatant

Overnight cultures of the probiotics in MRS broth were centrifuged (12000 rpm for 10 min), and the supernatant was passed through 0.22 μm filters to get the undiluted (100%) cell-free supernatant. Overnight cultures of S. Typhimurium (ATCC 13311) and ESBL E. coli (BAA 196) were used to estimate the antibacterial activities of cell-free supernatants. Briefly, 100 µl of pathogen (~ 10⁶ CFU/ mL) was used in combination with 100 µl of cell-free supernatant (25 µl each + 25 µl filtered MRS broth) with controls (pathogens or supernatants). Prior to incubation, the pH was neutralized to 7 using 1 N NaOH (Fisher Scientific, MA). A kinetic assay was conducted at OD600 for 24 h to evaluate the growth parameters of the pathogens alone in the presence or absence of cell-free supernatant (CFS)43.

Caenorhabditis elegans solid killing assay

The host protective effect of the probiotic strains was evaluated using a solid killing assay as described previously, with minor modifications44. Twenty-five synchronized L4-stage hermaphrodites of wild-type Caenorhabditis elegans N2 Bristol strain (Caenorhabditis Genetics Center, University of Minnesota) were transferred to nematode growth medium (NGM, RPI, IL) plates seeded with each probiotic strain (MLS3, MLS12, or MLS22) at approximately 10⁹ CFU/plate and incubated overnight at 20 °C for probiotic pretreatment. Pretreated worms were washed and transferred to fresh NGM plates seeded with the pathogen S. Typhimurium ATCC 13,311 (10⁶ CFU/plate). Control groups consisted of (i) worms pretreated overnight with each probiotic and then transferred to standard food source E. coli OP50, and (ii) worms without probiotic pretreatment transferred directly to S. Typhimurium or E. coli OP50 plates. Each condition was established in three independent biological replicates (total n = 75 worms per condition). Plates were incubated at 15 °C, and worms were scored daily as alive or dead by gentle touch-provoked movement using a platinum wire picker with transfer to fresh plates every 2 days to prevent confounding by progeny.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The three probiotics and pathogens were co-cultured for 4 h before collecting them for SEM sample processing. Biological samples for SEM were prepared using a hexamethyldisilazane (HMDS) protocol45 conducted in a fume hood with appropriate personal protective equipment. Silicon wafers or coverslips were treated with 10% poly-L-lysine (Innoprot, Spain) for 5 min, rinsed with 0.22 μm filtered water, dried (optionally in an oven), and cooled prior to use. Samples were fixed in 2% glutaraldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Cat# 16020) for a minimum of 1 h (or overnight at 4 °C), followed by three 5-10-minute rinses in 0.22 μm filtered, sample-compatible buffer. Dehydration was performed through an ethanol (EtOH) series (25%, 50%, 75%, 95%, and two 100% 200-proof EtOH steps, 10–15 min each), followed by HMDS/EtOH mixtures (1:1 and 2:1, 3–30 min each) and 100% HMDS for 3–30 min Samples were air-dried after removing the final HMDS, mounted onto SEM stubs, and coated with platinum or another metal. Throughout the process, samples were kept hydrated to prevent drying artifacts, with incubation times extended for thicker samples to ensure proper penetration. After sample processing, the samples were mounted on aluminum stubs and coated with platinum using a Leica ACE600 sputter coater. Imaging was performed on an Apreo VolumeScope™ SEM. The samples were imaged at 2 kV and 13pa.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses for phenotypic assays were performed in GraphPad Prism v10.1. Data are presented as mean ± SEM unless otherwise indicated. Normality and homogeneity of variance were checked using Shapiro-Wilk and Levene’s tests, and data were log-transformed where necessary. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Growth kinetics data were analyzed by fitting an exponential growth model. Auto-aggregation, co-aggregation, and cell surface hydrophobicity results were presented descriptively as mean ± SEM without specific inferential testing. Lysozyme tolerance was evaluated using a two-way mixed effects model ANOVA with Šídák post-hoc to compare cultures in the presence versus absence of lysozyme at different time points. Bile salt tolerance and acid tolerance were both assessed by one-way ANOVA to compare different concentrations/levels and time points. Hemolytic activity and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were evaluated qualitatively, with no statistical analysis performed. Antibacterial activity in the co-culture assay was analyzed by two-way repeated measures ANOVA with time and log CFU/mL as factors to compare pathogen survival in co-culture versus monoculture controls with Tukey’s post hoc analysis. Antibacterial activity with CFS was evaluated using one-way ANOVA to compare different treatments. Survival data from the Caenorhabditis elegans solid killing assay were analyzed using Kaplan-Meier survival curves compared by the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. Barrier integrity in the chicken enteroid model (FD4 uptake) was assessed by two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test for comparisons between probiotic treatments, LPS challenge, and controls.

Results

The isolated bacterial strains were Gram-positive cocci and catalase-negative

Following isolation, morphological and biochemical screening revealed that all 30 isolates were Gram-positive and catalase-negative. For systematic identification, isolates were designated with the prefix ‘MLS’ followed by a numerical identifier. Based on these preliminary screens, three representative isolates, MLS3, MLS12, and MLS22, were selected for comprehensive characterization. Microscopic analysis showed that MLS3 and MLS22 exhibited cocci or short-rod morphology arranged in pairs or chains. In contrast, MLS12 consisted of spherical or ovoid cocci in pairs or short chains and demonstrated robust growth under anaerobic conditions. The morphological features are shown in Fig. 1. The MALDI TOF analysis showed MLS 3 as Leuconostoc pseudomesenteroides with a log confidence score of 1.825, MLS12 as Lactococcus lactis with a log confidence score of 2.3 and MLS22 as Leuconostoc lactis with log confidence score of 2.03. For MLS3, the MALDI-TOF score of 1.825 fell below the high-confidence threshold of 2.0 and was therefore treated as a tentative genus-level assignment. Definitive species identification as Leuconostoc pseudomesenteroides was established by whole-genome sequencing, which served as the primary identification method for all three isolates. Additionally, the in vitro antimicrobial susceptibility showed that the MIC profiles for the strains do not harbor concerning phenotypic antimicrobial resistance (Supplementary file S1).

Genotypic features of the probiotic isolates

According to the QUAST 2 assembly matrix, the contig counts for MLS3, MLS12 and MLS22 were 58, 73, 20 and with total genome length of 2,130,395, 2,474,864 and 1,717,900 bp, respectively (Fig. 2A). Whole-genome sequencing depth exceeded the 20x quality threshold for all isolates, with average chromosomal coverage of 31.2x (MLS3), 53.6x (MLS12), and 140.8x (MLS22). Additionally, the GC content and N50 of the isolates were 38.93% and 154,272 (MLS3), 34.78% and 271,066 (MLS12), 44% and 456,360 (MLS22). For MLS12, the peak gene density reaches around 10 genes per 10 kb window near the origin, and declines sharply and fluctuates between 0 and 25 genes per kb window beyond 1e + 05 bp (100,000 bp). This indicates a gene-rich region at the sequence start, followed by a sparser distribution downstream. For MLS3, peak gene density reaches around 24 genes per 10 kb window near the start (0–600000 bp). Density exhibits minor variations afterward between 10 and > 20 genes per 10 kb window beyond 1e + 05 bp (100,000 bp). This suggests a high concentration of genes at the genome origin, possibly indicating a gene-rich region, with sparser gene distribution toward the terminus. Moreover, for MLS22, the peak gene density is higher, around 26 genes per 10 kb window near the start. The density remains relatively stable with minor fluctuations between 10 and 26 genes per 10 kb window across the genome (Fig. 2B, C, D).

The number of coding sequences in the annotated MLS3 genomes was 2,222, along with 51 tRNAs and 3 rRNAs. For MLS12, the CDS was 2519 with 55 tRNA and 5 rRNA. Additionally, for MLS22, the number of coding sequence was comparatively less than the other two isolates of about 1696 and 49, 3 tRNA and rRNA respectively (Fig. 2E). Further, the comprehensive genome analysis service at PATRIC annotations showed almost a similar number of hypothetical proteins for MLS3 (653) and MLS12 (603), and a lower number for MLS22 (367). MLS12 showed the highest number of proteins with functional assignments and cross-genus family assignments, which were comparable to MLS3. However, MLS22 showed the lowest number of proteins assigned (Fig. 2F).

Subsystem annotations helped to identify specialty genes

To elucidate the characteristics of the gene products of the annotated genomes, subsystem categories were used. Eleven sub-systems were used to classify the annotated genomes across the three probiotic isolates. As anticipated, the highest number of genes was found to be involved in bacterial metabolism, with around 347 genes in MLS3, 425 in MLS12, and 317 in MLS22 (Fig. 3A-C). Subsequently, categories including protein processing, stress response, virulence, and energy were showing more than 50 annotated genes across all three probiotic genomes. Genes coding for regulation and cell signaling were comparatively sparse among the three probiotic genomes (< 5). Additionally, specialty genes primarily associated with transport, drug target and antibiotic resistance, were annotated among MLS3 (26), MLS12 (119), and for MLS22 (28) (Supplementary file S1-1). Although k-mer-based AMR detection by PATRIC provided genes associated with antibiotic resistance, no corresponding AMR phenotypes were detected among the three isolates. Furthermore, metabolic pathways were annotated across the three genomes. Dominant pathways identified include carbohydrate metabolism, lipid metabolism, amino acid metabolism, as well as metabolism of cofactors and vitamins. The pathways involving the synthesis of secondary metabolites exhibited promising functional attributes across the three probiotics. Moreover, pathways for flavonoid, carotenoid, zeatin, and stilbene biosynthesis were present among all three genomes (Supplementary file S2-1,2). Conversely, metabolite pathways for limonene, pinene and diterpenoid were absent among MLS22. Another fact was that the presence of a steroid called brassinosteroid biosynthetic gene cluster was detected in MLS3 and was absent in MLS12 and MLS22 (Supplementary file S2-2). However, the functional identity of this cluster in a bacterial context remains to be experimentally confirmed.

A core-gene maximum likelihood phylogeny placed MLS12 within the Lactococcus lactis clade, clustering with reference dairy L. lactis genomes, whereas MLS3 and MLS22 grouped within distinct Leuconostoc lineages. Inclusion of E. coli and Bacillus subtilis as outgroup references provided a broad taxonomic context and confirmed that all three isolates fall within the lactic acid bacteria.

Phenotypic features exhibited prominent probiotic properties

To evaluate the functional attributes of the isolates, multiple physiological and probiotic parameters were characterized. Growth kinetics were established over a 24 h period by monitoring optical density (OD₆₀₀) and viable cell counts (CFU/mL) (Supplementary file S2- 3). Furthermore, auto-aggregation assays were performed to assess the potential of the isolates for intestinal colonization. Results showed that L. lactis showed slightly higher auto-aggregating (%) capacity (33.16 ± 1.83) while MLS3 had the lowest (30.03 ± 1.00) (Table 1). Additionally, the co-aggregating ability of the probiotics with pathogens was estimated to mock pathogen exclusion and inhibition of pathogen growth using S. Typhimurium ATCC 13,311 and ESBL E. coli ATCC BAA 196. Notably, MLS12 was showing the highest coaggregation percentage with S. To determine the survivability of the isolates within the host, their resilience to various gastrointestinal barriers was examined. Initially, the isolates were screened for lysozyme resistance (Fig. 4A-C). The results demonstrated that all three isolates showed lysozyme tolerance (around 9–10 log CFU/ mL) over the period of 24 h. Furthermore, we estimated the acid tolerance of the isolates for pH 1.5, pH 2 and pH 4 at different time points. All three isolates were able to survive up to 6 h at all pH levels. However, MLS22 maintained viability of around 3–4 log up to 24 h even at pH 1.5 (Fig. 4D-F). The pH after 24 h was further dipped to 1.26 from 1.5, 1.82 from 2 to 3.62 from 4. In addition to acid tolerance, the probiotics were evaluated for their tolerance to bile salts in the intestine to estimate their stability. Despite the significant differences in tolerance at time points 3 h and 6 h for 0.3% (physiological) and 2% (extreme) of bile salt concentrations, all the probiotics managed to survive up to 8 log CFU/ mL. This shows the potential of the isolates to survive even in higher bile salt environments (Fig. 4G-L). Moreover, none of the isolates showed β-hemolytic activity, which is complete hemolysis.

In vitro antibacterial activity estimation showed probiotics’ effect against ESBL E. coli and S. Typhimurium

Subsequent to the assessment of probiotic attributes, the antibacterial efficacy of the strains was investigated to determine their capacity for pathogen inhibition. Therefore, we estimated the in vitro antibacterial activity of the isolates against S. Typhimurium ATCC 13,311 and ESBL E. coli ATCC BAA 196. When live probiotics were co-cultured along with these pathogens, interesting characteristics were observed. After 4 h of incubation, all the co-cultured pathogens exhibited a reduction in growth compared to probiotics in the co-culture, with a value between 6 and 7 Log CFU/ mL. Furthermore, these levels were significantly lower than the pathogen growth observed in controls without probiotics (Fig. 5A- F). All three probiotics arrested the growth of Salmonella after 16 h of incubation. However, in the case of E. coli, the MLS12 only resulted in complete arrest of growth after 24 h of incubation.

In vivo antibacterial activity estimation using C. elegans survivability

The safety of the isolates and their impact on host longevity were evaluated using a Caenorhabditis elegans model (Fig. 6A-C). The strains MLS3 and MLS12 demonstrated non-toxic profiles with survival rates closely mirroring the standard E. coli OP50 control, maintaining host viability for approximately 18–19 days. Conversely, MLS22 exhibited a distinct safety profile, in which the group treated with the isolate showed a significant reduction in longevity compared to the OP50 control, reaching total mortality by day 19. Regarding protective efficacy, worms pre-colonized with MLS3 or MLS12 and subsequently challenged with Salmonella exhibited survival kinetics comparable to their respective probiotic controls (p < 0.001), extending host longevity until day 16–17. However, for MLS22, while probiotic pretreatment provided a survival buffer between days 14 and 15, the group ultimately reached total mortality (0% survival) by day 15, coinciding with the pathogen-only control. Collectively, these results demonstrate that while all isolates offer varying degrees of protection, MLS3 and MLS12 possess superior in vivo capacity in the C. elegans model to enhance host lifespan during pathogenic challenge.

Scanning electron microscopy revealed minor structural changes

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed the distinct morphological characteristics of the probiotic isolates and pathogenic strains. The probiotics appeared as oval or coccoid shapes, while rod-shaped was observed for ESBL E. coli and S. Typhimurium with visible flagella (Fig. 7A-E). The growth of the bacteria was monitored during 0 and 4 h of the experiment. The pathogens were grown up to 7 log CFU/ mL in combination with the probiotics, which were grown up to ~ 9 log CFU/mL, yielding a 100-fold reduction in growth. Additionally, the SEM images revealed loss of structural integrity of the pathogens and clumping together in most combinations. For instance, Fig. 7F and I (MLS3 + E. coli and MLS12 + S. Typhimurium) illustrates significant clumping as well as structural changes, including the loss of flagella.

Barrier-beneficial effects of the cell-free supernatant in chicken enteroids with inflammatory challenge

To test whether probiotic factors benefit the epithelium, we assessed FD4 permeability in chicken enteroids. As expected, 24 h LPS challenge increased FD4 leakage versus unchallenged controls (p < 0.05), confirming barrier disruption. In the absence of LPS, conditioned media (CM) from MLS-3, MLS-12, or MLS-22 (1% or 10% v/v) did not alter FD4 permeability. By contrast, CM pretreatment significantly reduced LPS-induced FD4 leakage for all three strains (p < 0.05 vs. LPS alone), indicating preservation of barrier integrity (Fig. 8A–C). Within the tested range, responses were dose-independent (1% vs. 10% CM, not significant).

Discussion

Traditional fermented dairy products remain a notable reservoir for discovering novel microbes with therapeutic potential. In this study, we successfully recovered three promising probiotic candidates, MLS3, MLS12, and MLS22, from cottage cheese. While MLS12 was isolated under strict anaerobic conditions using GasPaks, MLS3 and MLS22 were recovered through aerobic incubation. Despite these differing primary isolation environments, all three strains belong to genera characterized as facultatively anaerobic. Of particular interest, certain Leuconostoc species have previously demonstrated enhanced physiological features during aerobic respiration47. The isolates were identified as Gram-positive cocci or ovoid cocci, occurring in chains or pairs, consistent with the characteristic morphology of probiotic lactic acid bacteria48,49. Beyond morphological characterization, species-level identification using modern platforms is essential in terms of understanding the probiotic potential. MALDI-TOF MS-based bacterial identification offers a robust methodology that can be integrated with whole genome sequencing data to confirm the isolate identity50. In the present study, MALDI-TOF log scores (> 1.8) provided preliminary taxonomic assignments, with definitive identification achieved through the integration of MALDI-TOF and WGS data.

The genotypic characterization revealed that MLS22 exhibited the fewest contigs (20) and total length (~ 1.7 Mb), suggesting a contiguous assembly compared to MLS3 (58 contigs, ~ 2.1 Mb) and MLS12 (73 contigs, ~ 2.5 Mb). The reduced contig count in MLS22 along with its higher (L50 = 2), indicates a more complete and less fragmented genome assembly relative to MLS3 (L50 = 4) and MLS12 (L50 = 3). This could imply better sequencing or assembly quality for MLS22, potentially facilitating more accurate downstream analyses. Furthermore, the GC content ranged from 35 to 45% among the isolates, representing a significant genomic characteristic. Previous genomic analyses among the Leuconostoc and Lactococcus genera report an average GC content of approximately 37% and 34%, respectively51,52. Additionally, the length of the bacterial genomes is highly variable. The probiotics in this study exhibited a genome length ranging from 1.7 Mb to 2.4 Mb, consistent with some recent studies reporting lengths ranging from 1 Mb to 2.5 Mb47. Such variations in GC content may reflect evolutionary adaptations, as GC composition is known to influence gene expression and protein53. The lower GC content in MLS12, combined with its higher number of contigs, may suggest a more complex or less streamlined genome, potentially affecting its metabolic or environmental adaptability compared to MLS2254.

Gene density analysis highlighted distinct patterns of genomic organization across the three strains. While MLS3 and MLS12 exhibited gene densities ranging from 10 to 25 genes per 10 kb with periodic fluctuations, MLS22 maintained a relatively stable gene density throughout the genome. These patterns indicate that the three genomes differ in how coding sequences are distributed or show insights for expressed genes critical for core functions such as metabolism or stress response55. MLS12 displays a constant gene density near the start, with relatively abrupt peak distribution around the halfway point of the genome. This more uniform gene distribution in MLS12 may indicate a more balanced genomic organization, which might be contributing to functional stability under varying conditions. Furthermore, the annotation data provide insights into the functional potential of these strains. MLS12 possessed the highest number of coding sequences (CDS) (2519), followed by MLS3 (2222) and MLS22 (1696). The elevated CDS count in MLS12, along with its elevated number of tRNA (55) and rRNA (5) genes compared to MLS3 (51 tRNA, 3 rRNA) and MLS22 (49 tRNA, 3 rRNA), suggests a greater coding capacity and potentially enhanced translational efficiency. This could confer an advantage for diverse metabolic capabilities56. On the other hand, MLS22’s lower CDS count may indicate a more compact genome, possibly specialized for specific functions57,58. The PATRIC annotation results reveal that MLS3 and MLS12 have a comparable number of hypothetical proteins (653 and 603, respectively), while MLS22 has fewer (367). Hypothetical proteins that lack characterized functions may represent novel or strain-specific genes that warrant further investigation, which are previously observed6. MLS12 stands out with the highest number of proteins with functional and cross-genus family assignments, and representation of known functional protein families compared to MLS3 and MLS22. This could indicate that MLS12 has a broader metabolic or adaptive capacity, potentially making it more effective in diverse probiotic applications. MLS22, with the lowest number of assigned proteins, may have more specialized functions similar to research published previously59.

The results for sub-subsystem-based annotations revealed the isolated characteristics, with metabolism as the most dominant subsystem across MLS3, MLS12, and MLS22. This is consistent with their expected roles in nutrient acquisition and growth. The comparatively high representation of stress response and protein processing genes indicates their potential adaptation to dynamic environments such as the gastrointestinal tract, while the limited number of genes involved in regulation and signaling reflects the uniform genomic makeup typical of lactic acid bacteria. Similar observations were included in some previous research focusing on probiotic characterization7,60. The annotation of specialty genes, particularly those associated with transport and putative antibiotic resistance, underscores the genetic diversity across isolates, although the absence of phenotypic resistance suggests that these traits may not be functionally expressed under the tested conditions61,62. The presence of secondary metabolite biosynthesis pathways, including flavonoid, carotenoid, zeatin, and stilbene synthesis, highlights the probiotic strains’ potential contribution to host health through antioxidant and immunomodulatory effects63,64. Moreover, the identification in MLS3 of a putative biosynthetic gene cluster with homology to brassinosteroid pathway components, which are absent in MLS12 and MLS22, showed strain-specific relevance. However, the biological importance of this cluster warrants experimental follow-up, including heterologous expression or targeted gene deletion, before any functional claims can be made65. From the phylogenetic analysis, MLS12 clustering was more closely related to lactic acid bacteria, while MLS3 and MLS22 displayed greater distance, reflecting lineage-specific evolutionary pathways66,67. Furthermore, the absence of genotypic and phenotypic antimicrobial resistance was notable. The relationships between genotypic AMR annotations and phenotypic susceptibility profiles are a well-documented phenomenon in probiotic LAB, where putative resistance genes frequently represent intrinsic chromosomal elements involved in non-resistance functions such as metabolite efflux or cell wall biosynthesis, rather than acquired mobile resistance determinants. The absence of plasmid-borne or transposon-associated resistance genes in the assemblies, combined with phenotypic susceptibility across all tested antibiotic classes, supports the conclusion that these annotations do not represent transferable resistance risks7. Conclusively, these findings suggest that while the three isolates share core probiotic features, their distinct genomic attributes may confer differential functional advantages pertaining to probiotic applications.

The phenotypic characterization revealed more intriguing observations that can be correlated with genotypic characteristics. MLS12 exhibited the highest auto-aggregation capacity, suggesting superior colonization potential, whereas MLS3 showed the lowest capacity, indicating weaker self-association. Similar auto-aggregation patterns were observed for Leuconostoc and Lactococcus, previously showing a percentage between 20 and 6068,69. Co-aggregation assays further emphasized the strain-specific differences, with MLS12 displaying the strongest interaction with S. Typhimurium and MLS3 showing the lowest with E. coli. These results indicate that MLS12 may play an important role in pathogen exclusion through aggregation mechanisms. In addition, cell surface hydrophobicity assays showed that MLS12 possessed higher hydrophobicity than the other isolates, supporting its strong adhesion potential to gastric mucosa. However, there was a wide variation in the cell surface hydrophobicity values reported among the Lactococcus genus70. Equally important were the stress tolerance assays, which are critical for probiotic survival in the host environment. All isolates displayed high lysozyme tolerance, maintaining viable counts over 24 h, indicating their resistance to enzymatic degradation in the oral cavity and gastrointestinal tract. Acid tolerance assays demonstrated the ability of all strains to survive at pH 1.5, 2, and 4 for up to 6 h, with MLS22 showing exceptional resistance by maintaining viability at pH 1.5, 2 for about 24 h. This highlights MLS22 as particularly robust against gastric acidity. Moreover, acid production might be the reason for the slight dip in pH after 24 h. Bile salt tolerance results further confirmed that all isolates were able to withstand both low and high concentrations, maintaining viable counts close to 8 log CFU/mL, reflecting strong adaptability to intestinal conditions. Similar significant tolerance against lysozyme and bile acids was observed among probiotics isolated from a variety of sources, including breast milk, edible snails and pineapple silages71–73. Overall, all three isolates possess desirable probiotic characteristics.

The antibacterial activity assays revealed that all three isolates were effective in reducing the growth of S. Typhimurium and ESBL E. coli, highlighting their potential pathogen-inhibitory effects. In co-culture experiments, pathogen growth was consistently reduced within 4 h, and complete inhibition of Salmonella was achieved by all isolates after 16 h. For E. coli, only MLS12 achieved full growth arrest, but this required 24 h of incubation, suggesting isolate-specific differences in antibacterial potency. While the 1:1 MRS: LB medium is non-standard, monoculture growth controls confirmed that both probiotics and pathogens maintained viable and reproducible growth rates in this composite environment. This ensures that the observed inhibitory effects in co-culture were the result of interspecies competition rather than a simple lack of nutritional support for the pathogenic strains. Similar complete inhibition of Salmonella and ESBL E. coli was previously reported by scientists either in liquid culture or with Caco-2 cells74–76. These findings indicate that while all isolates can restrict pathogen growth, MLS12 demonstrates stronger and more sustained inhibitory activity, particularly against E. coli. Further analysis using CFS suggested that secreted metabolites (not characterized) contribute significantly to the antibacterial effects. Both MLS3 and MLS12, and MLS22 CFS displayed marked inhibitory activity. The minor variations demonstrate that the mode of inhibition may vary between strains. The combination of all three supernatants resulted in the strongest antibacterial effect, as reflected by the minimal area under the growth curve, indicating synergistic activity when used as a cocktail. The relative contribution of pH-mediated acidification versus secreted bioactive metabolites to the observed antibacterial activity remains to be formally evaluated in future studies, as probiotic CFS can inhibit pathogens through both mechanisms. Similar effects of the probiotic CFS were reported against various pathogens, including ESBL E. coli, Klebsiella and Pseudomonas74,77,78. In vivo validation using C. elegans survivability assays corroborated these findings, demonstrating improved host survival following probiotic pretreatment, specifically for MLS3 and MLS12. This protective effect demonstrates that the isolates not only limit pathogen growth in vitro but also enhance host responses in vivo. Probiotics have shown significant effects on the life span of hosts and even showed neuroprotective effects from previous studies71,78. MLS22 displayed comparatively lower C. elegans survivability despite its strong in vitro characteristics. This discrepancy may reflect species-specific limitations in gut colonization efficiency or host immune interactions that are not fully captured by in vitro assays and warrants further investigation in mammalian in vivo models. Further, the scanning electron microscopy revealed structural changes in the pathogen that were specifically evident in the case of the length of the pathogen and decreased production of flagella in the presence of probiotics. Although previous studies did not examine these specific pathogen strains, SEM evidence has shown that probiotics can reduce biofilm formation on glass slides78. A key value proposition for next-generation probiotics (and their secreted “postbiotic” factors) is enhancement of epithelial barrier integrity. Apical-out chicken enteroids provide a robust, reductionist system that retains all major epithelial lineages and recapitulates transport, barrier, and injury responses, making them well-suited for mechanistic evaluation of barrier modulation. Consistent with prior work, LPS challenge increased FD4 uptake, indicating barrier disruption. CFS from MLS3, MLS12, and MLS22 did not alter FD4 uptake under homeostatic conditions, suggesting little direct tonic effect on tight junction permeability at baseline. Brief pretreatment with each CFS significantly attenuated LPS-induced permeability, and this protection appeared dose-independent within 1–10% (v/v). These data align with previous reports that Lactobacillus-derived CFS protects the barrier function of the epithelia from LPS stress79. These findings imply a preconditioning effect that enhances epithelial resilience to inflammatory stress rather than constitutively tightening junctions. The underlying mechanisms remain to be defined. Candidates include (i) dampening of TLR4–NF-κB signaling, (ii) preservation or redistribution of tight-junction proteins (e.g., ZO-1, occludin, claudins), (iii) reinforcement of the mucus/glycocalyx layer, and/or (iv) mitigation of oxidative stress. Because the activity resides in CFS, small molecules or secreted peptides/metabolites are likely mediators46,80.

Collectively, these data demonstrate that all three strains exhibit potent antibacterial activity and beneficial effects across in vitro and surrogate host models, despite strain-specific variations in potency and environmental resilience. However, the precise mechanisms and metabolites responsible for the observed CFS activity and barrier protection remain to be elucidated.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we present a comprehensive genotypic and phenotypic characterization of three novel probiotic strains, Leuconostoc pseudomesenteroides MLS3, Lactococcus lactis MLS12, and Leuconostoc lactis MLS22, which are isolated from cottage cheese. Through integrated, genomic, and functional analyses, we demonstrate that these strains exhibit key probiotic traits. This includes potent antibacterial activity against clinically relevant pathogens (S. Typhimurium and ESBL-producing E. coli), the absence of transferable antimicrobial resistance determinants, and the ability to enhance host intestinal barrier integrity. CFS from all three strains significantly attenuated LPS-induced epithelial permeability in chicken enteroids without compromising barrier function under homeostatic conditions within the tested concentration range (1 and 10% v/v). While MLS3 and MLS12 demonstrated robust host-protective capacity in the C. elegans model, MLS22 showed comparatively reduced in vivo efficacy despite strong in vitro performance, warranting further investigation of its colonization dynamics in mammalian systems. Collectively, these findings establish MLS3, MLS12, and MLS22 as promising probiotic candidates with strong potential for applications in food safety and gut health. Future studies will focus on comprehensive metabolite profiling, detailed mechanistic investigations, and validation in mammalian models to fully assess their safety, efficacy, and optimal use.
