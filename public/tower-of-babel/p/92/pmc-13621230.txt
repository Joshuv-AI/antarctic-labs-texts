Steroid Injection in Patients With Benign Vocal Fold Lesions: A Meta‐Analysis and Meta‐Regression
Authors: Yousef M. Al‐Shammari, Bisher Albesher, Sarah M. Murad, Dhari Altaher, Nourah H. AlGhurair, Ahmad S. Alawadhi, Hasan Alshemari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aims to assess the efficacy and safety of steroid injections in patients with benign vocal fold lesions.Data SourcesPubMed, Scopus, and Embase were searched from January 2000 through December 2025 using predefined terms for benign vocal fold pathology and steroid injection.Review MethodsWe conducted a PRISMA‐guided systematic review and meta‐analysis of studies evaluating steroid injections for benign vocal fold lesions. Study selection and data extraction were performed independently by two reviewers. Study quality was assessed using NIH (cohort studies), JBI (case series), and RoB 2 (RCTs). Random‐effects meta‐analysis (DerSimonian‐Laird) was performed in OpenMeta[Analyst], with Freeman‐Tukey double arcsine transformation for proportional outcomes.ResultsFourteen studies were included. Posttreatment VHI pooled mean was 17.585 (95% CI 10.61‐24.55; I 2 = 97%), while VHI change was not statistically significant (−6.948; 95% CI −16.06 to 2.16; I 2 = 98%), indicating substantial between‐study heterogeneity and limited certainty in pooled VHI effect estimates. MPT improved, with post‐treatment pooled mean 13.24 seconds (95% CI 12.54‐13.94; I 2 = 73%) and pooled change 1.81 seconds (95% CI 1.10‐2.52; I 2 = 71%). Complications were uncommon (1.5%; I 2 = 0%). Overall improvement was 92%, recurrence 15.2%, complete remission 42.5%, partial remission 39.6%, and no response 9.4%.ConclusionSteroid injection was associated with high clinical improvement and low complication rates. However, VHI demonstrated substantial heterogeneity and inconsistent statistical significance across studies, limiting the robustness and generalizability of pooled quality‐of‐life estimates. These findings support steroid injection as a minimally invasive option in selected patients but highlight the need for standardized protocols and higher‐quality comparative trials to better define its effect on patient‐reported voice outcomes.

Benign vocal fold lesions (BVFLs) such as chorditis, vocal nodules, vocal polyps, Reinke's edema (RE), and vocal scars can cause voice changes, including reduced loudness and pitch range. 1 The histologic characteristics of BVFLs include inflammation, increased vessel wall thickness, edema, thickening of the basement membrane of Reinke's space, and elevation of the free edge of vocal cords. 2 Although pathological examination reveals subtle differences between these lesions, most present as phonotrauma, chronic inflammation, and subsequent remodeling of the Reinke's space. 3 , 4

Benign lesions of the vocal folds, including nodules, polyps, and RE, are multifactorial in pathogenesis, though phonotrauma due to voice overuse or misuse is an important precipitating factor. 5 Chronic smoking is known to be strongly linked to RE; it also predisposes to polyps and other lesions. 6 Laryngopharyngeal reflux is believed to make the mucosa vulnerable to injury from lesions. 7 Vocational voice demand and gastroesophageal reflux disease are also known risk factors. 7

Vocal fold steroid injection (VFSI) is an established office‐based treatment that has been used for decades in the management of inflammatory and benign laryngeal conditions. 8 , 9 Compared with systemic routes of administration, intralesional steroid injection has the advantage of higher primary site concentration and lower rates of adverse effects. 10 Intralesional steroid injection was suggested to play a role in the treatment of inflammatory laryngeal diseases like sarcoidosis, systemic lupus erythematosus, and Wegener's granulomatosis. 9 Microlaryngeal surgery is used to treat benign vocal fold pathologies, excising nodules, polyps, and cysts with the aid of a microscope. 11 It helps to increase the quality of voice and wave but poses risks to scarring or dysphonia. 11 Because of the variations in outcome, type of lesion, and injection protocol, there arises a need for systematic investigation on the efficacy and safety of VFSI for BVFLs, particularly as an option to or supplement of voice therapy and surgical procedures. The aim of our study was to assess the efficacy, safety, and effects of voice therapy after steroid injection for the treatment of BVFLs.

Methods

This systematic review and meta‐analysis were performed and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) statement.

Data Sources and Search Strategy

A comprehensive search was performed in PubMed, Scopus, and Embase from January 2000 through December 2025 to identify studies evaluating steroid injection for BVFLs. The strategy combined terms related to steroid injections and vocal fold lesions. Full search strategy available in Supplemental Table S1, available online. Search syntax was tailored to each database. In addition, the reference lists of eligible articles and relevant reviews were screened to identify additional studies.

Eligibility Criteria

Studies were selected if they assessed the efficacy of steroid injection for the management of benign vocal fold disorders. Potential benign vocal fold disorders included vocal nodules, vocal polyps, vocal cord edema (Reinke's), vocal cords cysts, mucosal retention cysts, fibrovascular lesions, vocal cord scarring, and vocal cords inflammation. The treatment under review involved steroid injections with any type of steroid using an office‐based or percutaneous approach under local anesthesia. The types of steroids involved include triamcinolone acetonide, dexamethasone, and methylprednisolone. Studies were included whether patients received a single injection or one or more injections as clinically indicated.

Eligible studies were required to report at least 1 relevant clinical outcome, such as patient‐reported voice improvement, acoustic or objective voice measures, changes in lesion size, rates of improvement or recurrence, or procedure‐related complications. Only randomized controlled trials, prospective or retrospective cohort studies, and case series with sufficient extractable outcome data were included. Studies published between January 2000 and December 2025 were eligible for inclusion. Case reports, narrative or systematic reviews, editorials, animal studies, and conference abstracts without fully accessible data were excluded. In addition, studies in which outcomes of intralesional steroid injections could not be separated from other concurrent interventions were also excluded. Only studies published in the English language were considered eligible for inclusion.

Study Selection

All retrieved records were imported into a reference‐management workflow and duplicates were removed. Screening was performed in 2 steps: (1) title/abstract screening, followed by (2) full‐text assessment for final eligibility. Two reviewers screened studies independently at both stages, and reasons for exclusion at the full‐text stage were documented. Disagreements were resolved by discussion, with input from a senior reviewer when needed.

Data Extraction

Two reviewers independently extracted data using a standardized, piloted form. Extracted items included study characteristics (first author, year, country/region, design), participant characteristics (sample size, age, and sex distribution when reported), lesion type, steroid regimen (eg, dexamethasone, triamcinolone, or combinations), injection approach, and follow‐up duration. Outcomes were extracted as reported, including posttreatment values and/or pre‐post change when available (eg, Voice Handicap Index [VHI], Maximum Phonation Time [MPT], acoustic measures), along with clinical outcomes such as remission/improvement, recurrence, and complications. Any discrepancies were resolved by consensus.

Quality Assessment

Methodological quality and risk of bias were assessed independently by 2 reviewers using design‐appropriate instruments. Cohort studies were evaluated using the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Quality Assessment Tool for Cohort Studies, case series were assessed with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklist for case series, and randomized trials were appraised using RoB 2. Studies were categorized according to each tool's guidance (eg, overall judgment such as good/fair/poor for NIH/JBI and domain‐based judgments for RoB‐2), and disagreements were resolved through discussion with senior adjudication if required.

Statistical Analysis

All quantitative analyses were performed using OpenMeta [Analyst] software. Pooled estimates were generated using a random‐effects model based on the DerSimonian‐Laird method. For continuous outcomes, pooled effects were summarized as mean differences (MDs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For proportional outcomes, pooled proportions were calculated after applying the Freeman‐Tukey double arcsine transformation to stabilize variances. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the I 2 statistic. Sensitivity analyses, including leave‐one‐out analyses, were conducted to evaluate the robustness of pooled estimates. Alongside the primary meta‐analysis, a meta‐regression analysis using random effects model was conducted to find the reasons behind between‐study heterogeneity and to test the effect of study‐specific variables on treatment outcomes. The variables analyzed included the publication year, the average age of patients, the duration of follow‐up, and the number of males and females in each sample. These variables were chosen based on their presumed clinical importance to treatment outcomes for patients receiving steroids injections to treat BVFLs. Coefficients with 95% CIs along with corresponding P‐values were determined. A two‐sided P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Search Strategy

A comprehensive search of 4 electronic databases (PubMed, Embase, Scopus) identified 130 records after duplicate removal. Titles, abstracts, and full texts were then screened according to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Ultimately, 14 studies met the eligibility criteria and were included in the final analysis (see Figure 1).

Quality Assessment

Twelve of our included studies were evaluated using the NIH Quality Assessment Tool for Cohort Studies. All the studies were determined to be of good quality. Full details about the quality assessment process available in Supplemental Table S2, available online. One case series study was evaluated using JBI tool and the remaining one RCT study was evaluated using ROB2. All the studies were determined to be of good quality. Full details are provided in Supplemental Table S3, available online and Supplemental Figure S1, available online.

Meta‐Analysis

VHI

Seven studies reported the VHI poststeroid injection outcome. The VHI ranges from 0 to 120, with lower scores indicating better voice‐related quality of life; scores below 20 are generally considered within the normal or minimal impairment range. Our analysis showed that VHI poststeroid injection was significantly low with pooled mean (17.585, 95% CI (10.61, 24.55), P < .001), however, this estimate was accompanied by substantial heterogeneity (I 2 = 97%), indicating considerable variability across studies and limiting the interpretability of the pooled effect size. Figure 2A shows leave‐one‐out meta‐analysis was conducted and showed that no single studies affect heterogeneity. Subgroup meta‐analysis was conducted for the country and type of steroid. In Korea, pooled VHI post steroid injection was significant with pooled mean (17.814, 95% CI (3.33, 32.29), P < .001) (see Supplemental Figure S2, available online). In Taiwan, was significantly lower than Korea with pooled mean (12.985, 95% CI (10.79, 15.17), P < .001) (see Supplemental Figure S3, available online).

(A) Meta‐analysis of the VHI posttreatment. (B) Meta‐analysis of the VHI change. VHI, Voice Handicap Index.

Patients received triamcinolone significantly have higher pooled mean of VHI than mixture of dexamethasone and triamcinolone, with pooled mean (19.652, 95% CI (7.01, 32.29), P < .001) and (14.93, 95% CI (9.835, 20.024), P < .001), respectively (see Supplemental Figures S4 and S5, available online).

None of the examined study‐level covariates significantly explained the between‐study variation in posttreatment VHI. Year of publication (coefficient 0.664, P = .27), follow‐up duration (0.319, P = .282), and sex distribution (males: 0.497, P = .532; females: −0.048, P = .748) were not associated with the posttreatment VHI outcome, with confidence intervals crossing zero for each factor. Age demonstrated the largest estimated effect (coefficient 1.56) and approached statistical significance (P = .074), suggesting a possible trend toward higher post‐treatment VHI with increasing age (see Supplemental Table S4, available online).

Eight studies reported the VHI change outcome. Our analysis showed that the change in VHI from pretreatment to posttreatment was insignificant with pooled mean (−6.948, 95% CI (−16.06, 2.16), P = .135), and was associated with substantial heterogeneity (I 2 = 98%), further limiting the certainty of this estimate. Figure 2B shows leave one out meta‐analysis was conducted and showed that no single study affects heterogeneity. Subgroup meta‐analysis was conducted for the country and type of steroid. In Korea, pooled VHI change was insignificant (−14.15, 95% CI (−37.53, 9.23), P = .234) (see Supplemental Figure S6, available online). However, in Taiwan, the pooled VHI change was significant (−9.67, 95% CI (−11.60, −7.738), P < .001) with zero heterogeneity (I 2 = 0%) (see Supplemental Figure S7, available online). Patients received mixture of dexamethasone and triamcinolone have high significant change (−8.51, 95% CI (−11.75, −5.26), P < .001) (see Supplemental Figure S8, available online). However, patients on triamcinolone alone have insignificant change in VHI (−6.42, 95% CI (−23.97, 11.12), P = 435) (see Supplemental Figure S9, available online).

Among the evaluated covariates, only the number of females was significantly associated with VHI change (coefficient 0.453, 95% CI 0.138‐0.768, P = .005), indicating that studies with a higher female count tended to report a larger VHI change. In contrast, year of publication showed a positive but nonsignificant trend (1.802, CI −0.356 to 3.960, P = .102), while age (−1.048, P = .574), follow‐up duration (−0.472, P = .269), and male count (−0.088, P = .956) were not associated with VHI change (see Supplemental Table S5, available online).

MPT

Six studies reported the MPT posttreatment outcome. MPT in healthy adults typically ranges from approximately 15 to 25 seconds, depending on age and sex, with lower values suggesting reduced phonatory efficiency. Our analysis showed that MPT post steroid injection was significantly high with pooled mean (13.24, 95% CI (12.54, 13.94), P < .001), with moderate heterogeneity (I 2 = 73%). Figure 3A shows leave‐one‐out meta‐analysis was conducted and showed that no single studies affect heterogeneity. Subgroup meta‐analysis was conducted for the country and type of steroid. In Korea, pooled MPT post steroid injection was significant with pooled mean (12.93, 95% CI (12.45, 13.42), P < .001), with low heterogeneity (I 2 = 37%) (see Supplemental Figure S10, available online). In Taiwan, was also significant with pooled mean (12.78, 95% CI (11.87, 13.69), P < .001), with zero heterogeneity (I 2 = 0%) (see Supplemental Figure S11, available online). Patients on triamcinolone only have significant high pooled mean (13.42, 95% CI (12.5, 14.3), P < .001) (see Supplemental Figure S12, available online).

(A) Meta‐analysis of the MPT posttreatment. (B) Meta‐analysis of the change in MPT. MPT, Maximum Phonation Time.

Year of publication (coefficient 0.123, P = .132), follow‐up duration (−0.026, P = .139), age (coefficient 0.091, P = .708), and sex (males: −0.002, P = .991; females: 0.040, P = .358) were not associated with posttreatment MPT (see Supplemental Table S6, available online).

Nine studies reported the change in MPT. Our analysis showed that the change in the MPT was significantly high with pooled mean (1.81, 95% CI (1.10, 2.52), P < .001), with moderate heterogeneity (I 2 = 71%). Figure 3B shows leave‐one‐out meta‐analysis was conducted and showed that no single studies affect heterogeneity. Subgroup meta‐analysis was conducted for the country and type of steroid. In Japan and Taiwan, the pooled change in MPT was significantly high with zero heterogeneity (I 2 = 0%), (2.39, 95% CI (1.81, 2.96), P < .001) and (2.2, 95% CI (1.29, 3.122), P < .001), respectively (see Supplemental Figures S13 and S14, available online). However, the change was insignificant in Korea, with pooled mean (1.13, 95% CI (−0.11, 2.38), P = .07) (see Supplemental Figure S15, available online). Patients on triamcinolone only have significant high pooled mean (1.74, 95% CI (0.865, 2.62), P < .001) (see Supplemental Figure S16, available online).

None of the evaluated covariates significantly explained the variability in MPT change across studies. Year of publication (coefficient −0.037, P = .501), follow‐up duration (−0.039, P = .313), age (−0.132, P = .149), and sex (males: −0.012, P = .750; females: 0.000, P = .991) (see Supplemental Table S7, available online).

Noise‐to‐Harmonic Ratio (NHR)

Four studies reported the NHR posttreatment outcome. Our analysis showed that NHR post steroid injection was significantly low with pooled mean (6.09, 95% CI (5.58, 6.6), P < .001), with high heterogeneity (I 2 = 99%). Figure 4A shows leave‐one‐out meta‐analysis was conducted and showed that no single studies affect heterogeneity. Subgroup meta‐analysis was conducted for the country and type of steroid. In Korea the pooled change in NHR was significantly high (16.181, 95% CI (0.527, 31.834), P < .001) and low in Taiwan (0.119, 95% CI (0.08, 0.159), P < .001) (see Supplemental Figures S17 and S18, available online). Patients on triamcinolone only have insignificant moderate pooled mean (10.83, 95% CI (−1.107, 22.76), P = .07) (see Supplemental Figure S19, available online). Neither year of publication nor follow‐up duration was associated with post‐treatment NHR (see Supplemental Table S8, available online).

(A) Meta‐analysis of the NHR posttreatment. (B) Meta‐analysis of the change in NHR. NHR, Noise‐to‐Harmonic Ratio.

Four studies reported the NHR change outcome. Our analysis showed that the change in NHR was insignificant with pooled mean (0.044, 95% CI (−0.161, 0.250), P = 654), with high heterogeneity (I 2 = 89%). Figure 4B shows leave‐one‐out meta‐analysis was conducted and showed that no single studies affect heterogeneity. Subgroup meta‐analysis was conducted for the country and type of steroid. The pooled change in NHR was insignificant among the studies from Korea (−1.88, 95% CI (−10.21, 6.45), P = 658) and in Taiwan (−0.054, 95% CI (−0.120, 0.011), P = 105) (see Supplemental Figures S20 and S21, available online). Patients on triamcinolone only have insignificant pooled mean (0.261, 95% CI (−1.624, 2.147), P = .786) (see Supplemental Figure S22, available online). Follow up period was significant in the meta‐regression analysis (see Supplemental Table S9, available online).

Complications

Twelve studies reported the complications. Our analysis showed significant low pooled proportion of complications among patients (1.5%, (95% CI; 0.006, 0.027), P < .001), with zero heterogeneity (I 2 = 0%) (see Figure 5A). Subgroup meta‐analysis was conducted for the country and type of steroid. In Taiwan, Korea and Japan, the analysis showed significant low pooled proportion of complications (1%, (95% CI; 0.00, 0.032), P < .001), (3.1%, (95% CI; 0.001, 0.086), P < .001), and (0.8%, (95% CI; 0.00, 0.03), P < .001), respectively (see Supplemental Figures S23‐25, available online). Patients on triamcinolone only also have low pooled proportion of complications (1.8%, (95% CI; 0.004, 0.037), P < .001), with low heterogeneity (I 2 = 25%) (see Supplemental Figure S26, available online).

(A) Pooled proportional meta‐analysis for complications. (B) Pooled proportional meta‐analysis for improvement. (C) Pooled proportional meta‐analysis for recurrence.

None of the examined covariates significantly moderate the complication rate across studies. Year of publication (coefficient −0.002, P = .614), age (coefficient 0.000, P = .928), follow‐up duration (−0.001, P = .727), sex (males: −0.003, CI −0.006 to 0.001, P = .192; females: approximately 0.000, P = .353) (see Supplemental Table S10, available online).

Improvements

Seven studies reported the improvement outcome (partial and complete remission). Our analysis showed significant high pooled proportion of improvement among patients (92%, (95% CI; 0.818, 0.985), P < .001), with high heterogeneity (I 2 = 87%). Figure 5B shows leave‐one‐out meta‐analysis was conducted and showed that no single study affect heterogeneity. Subgroup meta‐analysis was conducted for the country and type of steroid. In Japan and Korea, the analysis showed significant high pooled proportion of improvement (99.1%, (95% CI; 0.963, 1.00), P < .001) and (82.7%, (95% CI; 0.566, 0.984), P < .001), respectively (see Supplemental Figures S27 and S28, available online). Patients on triamcinolone only also have high pooled proportion of improvement (93.2%, (95% CI; 0.822, 0.995), P < .001) (see Supplemental Figure S29, available online).

Longer follow‐up was associated with lower observed improvement (coefficient −0.015, 95% CI −0.020 to −0.009, P < .001), indicating that improvement estimates tended to decline as follow‐up increased. In addition, studies with a greater number of male participants reported higher improvement (coefficient 0.029, 95% CI 0.005‐0.054, P = .019). By contrast, year of publication (0.009, P = .308), age (−0.001, P = .947), and female sample size (0.002, P = .382) were not significant moderators (see Supplemental Table S11, available online).

Recurrence

Six studies reported recurrence rates. Our analysis showed significant low recurrence rate (15.2%, (95% CI; 0.066, 0.264), P < .001), with high heterogeneity (I 2 = 79%). Figure 5C shows leave one out meta‐analysis was conducted and showed that no single study affects heterogeneity. Subgroup meta‐analysis was conducted for the country and type of steroid. In Taiwan and Korea, the analysis showed significant low pooled proportion of recurrence (4.2%, (95% CI; 0.00, 0.14), P = .03) and (14.8%, (95% CI; 0.026, 0.338), P < .001), respectively (see Figures S30 and S31, available online). Patients on triamcinolone only also have low pooled proportion of recurrence (18.8%, (95% CI; 0.095, 0.301), P < 0.001) (see Supplemental Figure S32, available online).

Studies with a higher number of female participants reported lower recurrence (coefficient −0.003, 95% CI − 0.004 to −0.001, P = .006). In contrast, year of publication (−0.017, P = .101), age (0.017, P = .068), follow‐up duration (0.001, P = .857), and males (−0.010, P = .089) were not significant moderators (see Supplemental Table S12, available online).

Complete Remission

Four studies reported the complete remission outcome. Our analysis showed significant complete remission rate (42.5%, (95% CI; 0.348, 0.504), P < .001), with low heterogeneity (I 2 = 42%) (see Figure 6A). Subgroup meta‐analysis was conducted for the country. In Korea, the pooled complete remission was significant (42.2%, (95% CI; 0.340, 0.466), P < .001), with low heterogeneity (I 2 = 14%) (see Supplemental Figure S33, available online).

(A) Pooled proportional meta‐analysis for complete remission. (B) Pooled proportional meta‐analysis for partial remission. (C) Pooled proportional meta‐analysis for no response.

None of the assessed moderators significantly influenced the complete remission rate. Year of publication had a near‐null effect (coefficient 0.001, 95% CI −0.025 to 0.026, P = .961), and neither age (0.007, CI −0.013 to 0.028, P = .478) nor follow‐up duration (0.001, CI −0.004 to 0.007, P = .609) was associated with complete remission (see Supplemental Table S13, available online).

Partial Remission

Four studies reported the partial remission outcome. Our analysis showed significant partial remission rate (39.6%, (95% CI; 0.266, 0.526), P < .001), with high heterogeneity (I 2 = 80%) (see Figure 6B). Leave‐one‐out meta‐analysis was conducted and showed that no single study affects heterogeneity. Subgroup meta‐analysis was conducted for the country. In Korea, the pooled partial remission was significant (41.3%, (95% CI; 0.268, 0.567), P < .001), with high heterogeneity (I 2 = 84%) (see Supplemental Figure S34, available online).

Year of publication showed a significant negative relationship (coefficient −0.035, 95% CI −0.056 to −0.013, P = .002), indicating that more recently published studies tended to report lower partial remission rates. Age was also inversely associated with partial remission (coefficient −0.029, CI −0.048 to −0.009, P = .004), suggesting reduced partial remission with increasing patient age. In addition, longer follow‐up was linked to lower partial remission (coefficient −0.010, CI −0.015 to −0.004, P < .001) (see Supplemental Table S14, available online).

No Response

Four studies reported the no response outcome. Our analysis showed significant no response rate (9.4%, (95% CI; 0.051, 0.148), P < .001), with low heterogeneity (I 2 = 44%) (see Figure 6C). Subgroup meta‐analysis was conducted for the country. In Korea, the pooled no response rate was significant (9.6%, (95% CI; 0.045, 0.162), P < .001), with moderate heterogeneity (I 2 = 63%) (see Supplemental Figure S35, available online).

No response was not significantly associated with any of the examined moderators. Although the direction of effect suggested slightly lower no‐response rates with more recent publication year (coefficient −0.018, 95% CI −0.041 to 0.006, P = .139) and with longer follow‐up (−0.004, CI −0.010 to 0.002, P = .170). Age showed a near‐null association (−0.001, CI −0.021 to 0.020, P = .954) (see Supplemental Table S15, available online).

Discussion

Across fourteen studies including approximately 1000 patients, steroid injection was associated with improved patient‐reported voice outcomes and objective phonatory performance. Posttreatment VHI values were significantly lower overall. Measures of phonatory efficiency showed a clearer pattern: maximum phonation time improved after treatment, and pooled analyses suggested a high probability of symptomatic improvement in most patients. Importantly, adverse events were uncommon, with a low pooled complication proportion and minimal between‐study variability, supporting the procedure's overall tolerability. Recurrence occurred in a minority of cases but estimates differed across cohorts and tended to be less favorable with longer follow‐up, suggesting that durability may depend on lesion characteristics, vocal demands, and ongoing exposure to phono‐traumatic behaviors.

Across contemporary treatment paradigms for BVFLs, steroid injection has been positioned as a minimally invasive option that may bridge conservative therapy and phonosurgery. Standard pathways often emphasize behavioral intervention first, particularly for phonotraumatic lesions, because structured voice therapy can improve function and, in selected patients, avert surgery. 25 , 26 When surgery is required, phonosurgical principles prioritize preservation of the layered microstructure, yet recurrence remains clinically relevant and may be mitigated by postoperative voice therapy. 27 Our pooled findings support steroid injection as a pragmatic office‐based intervention with measurable benefits in key voice outcomes and an encouraging safety profile.

Regarding voice outcomes, our meta‐analysis showed significantly lower post‐treatment VHI, although heterogeneity was high, and the pooled change in VHI did not reach statistical significance overall. This pattern is consistent with earlier reports showing that patient‐reported outcomes improve after percutaneous or intralesional steroid injection, yet effect size can vary with baseline severity, lesion composition, and follow‐up timing. 23 , 24 The MPT findings were more cohesive in our analysis: both post‐treatment MPT and MPT change improved with only moderate heterogeneity. This aligns with the physiologic rationale that reducing inflammatory edema and improving pliability can enhance glottal efficiency and sustain phonation, a pattern described in clinical series and outcome studies of VFSI. 16 , 24 In contrast, acoustic measures were inconsistent and highly heterogeneous in our pooled analyses, with non‐significant pooled change estimates. This mirrors broader voice literature in which acoustic parameters can be sensitive to recording conditions, analytic platforms, and patient effort, making them less stable across cohorts than patient‐reported scales and aerodynamic measures. 1 , 24

From the standpoint of clinical effectiveness (improvement, remission, recurrence), our pooled improvement proportion was high, while recurrence remained relatively low but heterogeneous across studies. Prior work similarly suggests that many patients experience meaningful symptomatic improvement after steroid injection, including professional voice users, although durability may be influenced by vocal load and behavioral exposures. 17 , 23 Meta‐regression signal concerning longer duration of follow‐up and observed improvement helps to validate the clinical plausible hypothesis that improvement might wane with passage of time, thereby underlining the relevance of cautioning the patients about the potential efficacy of the steroid injection procedure but without persisting or uniform curative effects. These results are more encouraging than the recurrences documented in the literature on BVFL surgery. 28 , 29

The AAO‐HNSF recommends timely laryngoscopy when dysphonia does not resolve and promotes appropriate use of voice therapy and surgery while discouraging unnecessary pharmacologic treatment. 30 , 31 These broad dysphonia guidelines do not provide detailed, lesion‐specific algorithms for office‐based steroid injections; management of BVFLs remains heterogeneous in practice, reflecting limited high‐quality comparative evidence and variability in access to specialized voice care. 32 , 33

Future Directions and Recommendations

Future directions should focus on generating higher‐certainty evidence and improving clinical standardization for steroid injections in BVFLs. Well‐designed, multicenter randomized trials are needed to compare steroid injection against optimized conservative care (structured voice therapy and management of contributing factors such as reflux and phonotraumatic behaviors) and against surgery when indicated. These studies should use clearly defined lesion phenotypes and consistent diagnostic criteria, because response is likely to differ across nodules, polyps, cysts, RE, and scar‐related pathology. Protocol transparency is essential: future reports should specify the injection route (intralesional vs percutaneous), steroid formulation and dose, use of local anesthesia, number of sessions, and postprocedure voice rest recommendations. Follow‐up must be sufficiently long to characterize durability and recurrence, as outcomes may evolve beyond early post‐treatment improvement.

From a practical standpoint, steroid injection can be recommended as a minimally invasive option within a stepwise pathway, particularly for patients seeking to avoid surgery, those with high vocal demands, or those with lesions where inflammation is thought to be a prominent driver of dysphonia. Clinicians must be careful in generalizing the results to individual lesions since, at present, there are no lesion‐specific results available; the studies are based on mixed lesions. Clinicians should counsel patients to experience meaningful improvement, yet complete remission is not universal and recurrence can occur. Routine integration with voice therapy and risk‐factor modification is advisable to maximize durability.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several important strengths. To the best of our knowledge, this represents one of the most comprehensive quantitative syntheses that assess the efficacy and safety of steroid injections in the management of BVFLs. A widely directed literature search involving multiple databases allowed for the inclusion of studies emanating from diverse geographic regions and clinical settings, thereby enhancing the generalizability of the findings. We assessed a wide range of clinically relevant outcomes, including patient‐reported measures, objective phonatory parameters, recurrence, and complications, which allowed for a balanced assessment of both efficacy and safety. Sensitivity analyses, subgroup analyses, and meta‐regression were also conducted to investigate heterogeneity and possible effect modifiers, further providing insight beyond pooled point estimates.

There are several limitations that must be recognized: most of the included studies were observational in design, with only one randomized controlled trial. This limits causal inference and increases the susceptibility to selection bias and unmeasured confounding. Another weakness of the current review is the presence of high levels of statistical heterogeneity in various critical outcomes. There is a high level of inconsistency noted in patient‐rated outcomes and acoustic parameters, specifically VHI (I 2 = 97%‐98%), NHR (I 2 = 99%), change in MPT (I 2 = 71%), and partial remission (I 2 = 80%). Heterogeneity may have been influenced by differences in the baseline disease condition, lesion type, administration technique, steroids used, voice training, and duration of follow‐ups among included studies. Notably, despite undertaking subgroup analysis and meta‐regression, there were still unidentified factors contributing to the heterogeneity. In addition, the use of a single‐arm design in the included studies could have led to greater levels of heterogeneity, considering that the lack of a comparison group increases susceptibility to disparities within the participant populations and study procedures. Consequently, pooled results from heterogeneous effects, especially VHI and other subjective variables, should be regarded descriptively rather than being considered exact estimates of effects. Analyses were based on aggregate study‐level data, restricting lesion‐specific or patient‐level subgroup analyses. Also, while the nature of lesion (such as nodules, polyps, RE, cysts, scarring, and chorditis) is clinically significant, most of the studies analyzed in this review do not provide separate outcomes for each subtype of lesion, and the data used came mostly from heterogeneous lesions; consequently, there was no basis for conducting a lesion‐specific subgroup meta‐analysis. Future research should provide lesion‐specific results to allow for comparison of the two approaches. Finally, follow‐up durations varied widely, and longer follow‐up was associated with attenuated improvement, indicating that long‐term durability remains incompletely defined.

Conclusion

Injections of steroids in patients with BVFLs seem to provide significant improvement in voice outcomes with minimal complications, as well as an acceptable risk of recurrence. Though there seems to be variation in their responsiveness, there still seems to be sufficient evidence for their use in patients as a minimally invasive office procedure that can be included in stepwise management in appropriately chosen patients. However, these findings should be interpreted in the context of heterogeneous and predominantly mixed‐lesion study populations, limiting lesion‐specific conclusions. More robust studies are still required.
