Role of E. coli acid resistance systems in proton motive force formation during fermentation
Authors: Heghine Gevorgyan, Tamara Abaghyan, Stella Hakobyan, Sophie Brameyer, Kirsten Jung, Karen Trchounian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe regulation of intracellular pH (pHin) and the maintenance of a proton motive force (Δp) are vital processes for cellular viability. Escherichia coli employs different amino acid decarboxylase acid resistance (AR) systems: specifically, Gad (glutamate-dependent), Cad (lysine-dependent), and Adi (arginine-dependent) systems for pHin and pHex regulation under acidic conditions. This study elucidated the pH-dependent roles of these AR systems in the formation and regulation of the Δp components (membrane potential [ΔΨ] and transmembrane proton gradient [ΔpH]) and proton flux rate (JH+) during fermentation at different levels of acid stress: (no stress [pH 7.6], mild acid stress [pH 6.5], moderate acid stress [pH 5.8], and sublethal acid stress [pH 5.4]). The regulatory role of AR systems in Δp and pH homeostasis demonstrated significant special contingent at levels of acid stress. Moreover, AR systems affected the Δp components and the proton flux rate from the cytoplasm to the external environment. Under no acid stress, the Gad system played a role in the regulation of ΔpH by maintaining a higher extracellular pH (pHex). The Adi system demonstrated significance in balancing of Δp components, and the observed compensatory variation in balancing both ΔΨ and ΔpH in ΔadiY mutant emphasized the critical role of Adi in maintaining Δp stability under mild acid stress, pH 6.5. The Cad system was important in regulating ΔpH under moderate acid stress levels by influencing pHin. Under sublethal acid stress, all AR systems were crucially involved in ΔpH regulation and JH+. Thus, our data show that each AR system possesses a crucial and distinct role in Δp formation that is highly dependent on the acid stress level. Moreover, the role of one AR system was, in most cases, not fully compensated by the others, underscoring the importance of these AR systems individually in pH homeostasis in E. coli during fermentation.

Why it matters

Cells living in acidic environments must preserve energy to prevent excessive proton influx that can disturb cytoplasmic pH homeostasis. This study shows how Escherichia coli adjusts fundamental bioenergetic parameters: proton motive force, proton efflux, and proton conductance, across increasing acid stress levels. The importance of acid resistance systems in the regulation of proton motive force was elucidated under fermentative conditions. Our findings reveal that individual acid resistance systems contribute in distinct and specialized ways to proton motive force formation, and that these contributions are related to the acid stress level. By understanding how energy conservation and proton flux are balanced under acid stress conditions, this work provides relevant insight into pH-dependent bioenergetics applicable to diverse cells and environments.

Introduction

The maintenance of a stable internal environment, or homeostasis, is a fundamental requirement for the survival and proliferation of all living cells, particularly in bacteria facing fluctuating external conditions, such as external pH. For Escherichia coli, a major physiological challenge arises in environments rich in fermentable carbon sources, such as the mammalian gut.1,2 Here, fermentation of carbon substrates led to the production of organic acids, such as acetate, lactate, formate, and succinate, acidifying extracellular pH. To counteract acidification in the intracellular environment and survive acid challenges, E. coli activates multiple acid resistance (AR) systems that act both to maintain cytoplasmic pH and alter transmembrane proton movements.3,4 The best-characterized inducible systems are the glutamate-dependent AR2 (Gad, GDAR), the arginine-dependent AR3 (Adi, ADAR), and the lysine-dependent AR4 (Cad, LDAR) systems. Each amino acid-dependent system couples a cytoplasmic amino acid decarboxylase (GadA/B, AdiA, CadA, respectively) that consumes a cytoplasmic proton to an inner-membrane antiporter that imports substrate (amino acid) and exports the decarboxylation product: a net reaction that removes intracellular net H+. Among these systems, Gad exhibits higher efficiency compared with Adi and Cad in highly acidic conditions. The structure and molecular mechanisms of action of these systems have been elucidated.5,6,7

Regulatory proteins control the induction and activity of these systems in response to external pH. During fermentative growth conditions, at least three AR mechanisms are operative, which rely on the activity of amino acid-dependent decarboxylase/antiporter systems, glutamate-, arginine- and lysine-dependent systems.2,8 The regulatory network of the Gad system is highly complex and involves more than 10 regulatory elements, whereas the Adi and Cad systems are each regulated by a single transcriptional regulator, AdiY and CadC, respectively.9,10,11 GadE is a transcriptional activator and controls the transcription of genes involved in the maintenance of pH homeostasis, including the principal Gad acid resistance system. The gadE gene is expressed in a heterogeneous mode, and consequently a subpopulation of the cells shows an increased expression of its regulon and resistance to acid stress. A gadE knockout mutant affects the cellular permeability and viability.11,12 AdiY acts as a positive transcriptional regulator of the genes encoding the arginine decarboxylase Adi system.13 CadC is a membrane-associated pH sensor/transcription activator that, together with LysP and other inputs, induces the cadBA operon under moderately acidic, lysine-rich conditions.6

The antiporters AdiC and CadB are electrogenic as positively charged amines that are transported across the membrane to the external environment.4,14 Proton consumption in the cytoplasm tends to reduce ΔpH (making the intracellular environment less acidic), while antiporters can be electrogenic and thereby change membrane potential (ΔΨ). The antiport of glutamate and gamma-aminobutyric acid by GadC is electrogenic with a net charge of +1 transported across the membrane at an acidic external pH <2.5.2 The antiport of arginine and agmatine via the Adi system, as well as lysine and cadaverine via the Cad system, is also electrogenic at an acidic pH. Thus, the AR systems contribute to the cells’ electrical potential (ΔΨ). Acid-tolerant microbes are generally equipped with less permeable membranes to reduce the entry of protons into the cells.15 However, the mechanism that will describe how these systems influence the formation of the proton motive force (Δp) value is not understood yet.

While the necessity of these AR systems for survival under extreme acid stress (pH < 4.0) is well established, their differential and potentially redundant contributions to Δp and pHin regulation across the transition from neutral (pH 7.6) to sublethal acidic conditions (pH 5.4) are less understood. Moreover, the structural characteristics and functions of AR systems as a H+-consuming system in the bacterial membrane are well-described. However, the mechanisms and their importance in the formation of bioenergetic parameters are not well-known. Besides, most studies have focused on respiratory metabolism, while comparatively little information is available regarding fermentation conditions. Thus, the main aim of this research is to characterize the involvement and differential contributions of the major AR systems (Gad, Cad, Adi) in the regulation of pH homeostasis and Δp in E. coli across a wide range of acid stress levels (pH 7.6 to 5.4) during fermentation. Understanding the mechanisms mentioned above will provide insights into how bacteria tackle acidic environments, aiding in applications spanning from biotechnology to food safety.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains, growth conditions, and specific growth rate

The E. coli strains used in this study are described in Table 1. Bacteria were grown in lysogenic broth (LB) medium (10 g L-1 tryptone, 5 g L-1 yeast extract, 10 g L-1 NaCl) with the addition of 4 g L-1 glucose. Cells were grown at 37°C for 3.5 h under fermentative conditions. Cells were cultivated in 2-L flasks. The initial pH of the medium was measured using a pH meter equipped with a selective pH electrode (HI1131, Hanna Instruments, Portugal) and adjusted to four test values: pH 7.6, pH 6.5, pH 5.8 and pH 5.4 using 0.1 M HCl prior to inoculation. No further pH adjustments or buffering were performed during the 3.5-h growth period.11 The medium was filled in glass vessels, and oxygen was bubbled out from the medium and exchanged to nitrogen and further autoclaved, as described.18

Plasmids used in this study

To construct a marker-less in-frame deletion of gadE in E. coli MG1655, firstly, the suicide plasmid pNPTS138-R6KT-ΔgadE was generated as previously described in Brameyer et al.10 Briefly, flanking regions of each 600 bp upstream and downstream of gadE were amplified by PCR from E. coli MG1655 genomic DNA using appropriate primer pairs. After purification, the PCR products were assembled into the PCR linearized pNPTS138-R6KT plasmid via Gibson assembly.22 The resulting plasmid, pNPTS138-R6KT-ΔgadE, was verified by sequence analysis. To generate the deletion of gadE in E. coli MG1655, at first, the suicide plasmidpNPTS138-R6KT-ΔgadE was introduced into E. coli MG1655 by conjugative mating using E. coli WM3064 as a donor in LB medium containing 2,3-diaminopropionicacid (DAP). Single-crossover integration mutants were selected on LB plates containing kanamycin but lacking DAP. Single colonies were grown for a day without antibiotics and plated onto LB plates containing 10% (wt/vol) sucrose but lacking NaCl to select for plasmid excision. Kanamycin-sensitive colonies were checked for targeted deletion by colony PCR using primers bracketing the site of the insertion and deletion of gadE, which was verified by sequencing, resulting in the strain E. coli MG1655ΔgadE.

To construct a marker-less in-frame deletion of cadC in E. coli MG1655, firstly, the suicide plasmid pNPTS138-R6KT-ΔcadC was generated as described above using the flanking regions of each 600 bp upstream and downstream of cadC amplified by PCR from E. coli MG1655 genomic DNA using appropriate primer pairs. Secondly, the suicide plasmid pNPTS138-R6KT-cadC was introduced into E. coli MG1655 by conjugative mating using E. coli WM3064 as a donor in LB medium containing DAP as described above. Deletion of cadC was verified by colony PCR and sequencing, resulting in the strain E. coli MG1655ΔcadC.

To generate the triple deletion strain containing the marker-less in-frame deletion of gadE, cadC, and adiY, at first, the suicide plasmid pNPTS138-R6KT-ΔcadC was introduced into E. coli MG1655ΔadiY by conjugative mating using E.coli WM3064 as a donor in LB medium containing DAP as described above. Deletion of cadC was verified by colony PCR and sequencing, resulting in the strain E. coli MG1655ΔcadCΔadiY. In the next step, deletion of gadE was achieved using the suicide plasmid pNPTS138-R6KT-ΔgadE. This plasmid was introduced into E. coli MG1655ΔcadCΔadiY by conjugative mating using E. coli WM3064 as a donor in LB medium containing DAP as described above. Deletion of gadE was verified by colony PCR and sequencing, resulting in the strain E. coli MG1655ΔcadCΔadiYΔgadE.

Measurement of H+ flux rate

The total rate of H+ flux (JH+) by whole cells was quantified using an established electrochemical method designed to monitor dynamic changes in the external proton concentration.23,24 The kinetics of H+ concentration changes in the experimental medium were measured. JH+ represents the rate of total proton flux (efflux direction) to the external environment. It characterizes the net movement of protons across the membrane and serves as an integrative parameter reflecting both direction of proton transport (efflux and influx). JH+ measurements were conducted in a buffer solution (experimental media) adjusted to the specific pH values corresponding to the initial growth media (pH 7.6, 6.5, 5.8, and 5.4). Experimental assay composition was 150 mM Tris-phosphate, including 0.4 mM MgSO4, 1 mM NaCl, and 1 mM KCl with supplementation of 10 mM each of glutamic acid, lysine, and arginine.

After supplementation of the bacterial suspension, glucose was added in the assays with the same concentration as in the initial growth medium (4 g L-1). Bacterial cells were normalized to OD600 of 0.35–0.40 following a 50-fold dilution, corresponding to approximately 0.8–1.0 × 1010 cells. Proton concentration changes were continuously registered in the external medium via ion meter (HI 5222, Hanna Instruments, USA) and a selective pH electrode (HI1131 electrode, Hanna Instruments, USA).18,25 The electrode was calibrated by titration of the experimental medium with a known concentration of 0.01 M HCl. The calculated JH+ values were expressed in mM min−1 per 109 cells.

ΔpH and ΔΨ

Transmembrane proton gradient (ΔpH) was calculated as the difference between intracellular pH (pHin) and extracellular pH (pHex).18,26 pHex was measured using a pH meter equipped with a selective pH electrode (HI1131, Hanna Instruments, USA). pHin was determined using a fluorescent dye 9-aminoacridine (9-AA).27,28 100 mM Tris-HCl buffer with a pH range of 4.5–8.5 was used. Bacterial cells were normalized to OD600 of 0.20–0.22, corresponding to approximately 0.95–1.00 × 108 cells. The quenching of 9-AA fluorescence was measured using a spectrofluorometer (Cary Eclipse, Agilent Technologies, USA) at an excitation wavelength of 339 nm and an emission wavelength of 460 nm.

The membrane potential (ΔΨ, inside negative) was determined by measuring the steady-state distribution of the tetraphenylphosphonium cation (TPP+) between the bacterial cytoplasm and the external medium.28,29 150 mM Tris-HCl buffer containing 1 μM TPP+ was used. Bacterial cells were normalized to OD600 of 0.35–0.40 following a 50-fold dilution, corresponding to approximately 0.8–1.0 × 1010 cells. The pH of the buffer was maintained at the same value as the bacteria’s growth medium. The alterations in TPP+ concentration in the external medium were assessed using a TPP+-selective electrode. The absorption of TPP+ on the bacterial cell surface was determined for boiled (during 3 min) cells.

Proton motive force and proton conductance

Δp was determined by combining the measured components ΔΨ and ΔpH according to the Nernst equation equivalent ΔμH+/F = ΔΨ − ZΔpH (negative value in mV), where Z is RT/F equal to 61.1 mV at 37°C.26 Proton conductance of the membrane (CMH+) was calculated by the following formula: CMH+ = JH+/Δp as described.18,30 CMH+ was expressed in nM min-1 mV-1 per 109 cells.28

Results

Effect of acid stress on E. coli MG1655 proton motive force components

This study was designed to understand the role of AR systems in the regulation of the Δp and cellular pH homeostasis at different levels of acid stress during fermentation. To characterize this role, we measured the formation of Δp and the JH+ at four distinct stress levels, whereby the starting pH of the growth medium was set as follows: pH 7.6 (no acid stress), pH 6.5 (mild acid stress), pH 5.8 (moderate acid stress), and pH 5.4 (sublethal acid stress). JH+ represents the rate of total proton concentration in the external environment and net outcome of several processes, not a single mechanism. Certainly, proton flux might occur during physiological processes; however, we measured total proton flux rate changes (efflux direction) as this is the prevailing process during anaerobic fermentation. Obviously, it is a composite parameter integrating both membrane-associated proton movements and metabolic contributions. In this manuscript, JH+ was investigated to understand how AR systems are involved in the total JH+, as these systems physiologically are important for the proton efflux and regulation of intracellular pH.

The bacterial growth rate (μ) of E. coli K-12 MG1655 wild type (WT) was determined to be 0.52 h-1 under no acid stress condition. The cultivation of E. coli under fermentative conditions at all tested conditions resulted in additional acidification of the growth medium due to the generation and accumulation of organic acids (e.g., acetate, lactate, succinate, formate) (Table 3).31 Under the no stress condition, pHex decreased from 7.6 to 6.5 after 3.5 h of growth, and pHin was determined to be pH 6.42 (Figure 1). The ΔpH (defined as pHin − pHex) was near zero. ΔΨ was −105 mV, yielding a Δp of −103 mV. Both the JH+ and CMH+ were maximal among the tested conditions under no stress condition. JH+ was 3.68 mM min−1, while CMH+ reached 35.7 nM min-1 mV-1 (Figure 1).

Under mild acid stress (pH 6.5), μ was 0.47 h-1, pHex decreased from 6.5 to pH 5.4 after 3.5 h of growth, while pHin was determined to be pH 6.4. ΔpH reached 1.02 units (−63 mV), and ΔΨ was −94 mV, yielding a Δp of −157 mV. Compared with no stress conditions, both the JH+ and CMH+ decreased markedly. JH+ was 2.10 mM min-1, and CMH+ reached 13.4 nM min-1 mV-1 (Figure 1).

Under moderate acid stress (pH 5.8), μ was 0.22 h-1, pHex decreased from 5.8 to 5.0 after 3.5 h of growth, while pHin was determined to be pH 6.6. This led to the higher ΔpH reaching 1.62 units (−100 mV) and ΔΨ with −109 mV. Δp was −209 mV, and JH+ and CMH+ demonstrated 1.04 mM min-1 and 5.0 nM min-1 mV-1, respectively (Figure 1).

Under sublethal acid stress (pH 5.4), μ was 0.16 h-1, pHex decreased only to 5.1, but pHin dropped significantly by approximately 0.29 units compared with the other conditions. As a consequence, ΔpH declined to 0.99 units (−61 mV). The ΔΨ was −114 mV resulting Δp with −175 mV. No further reduction in the JH+ and CMH+ was observed compared with moderate acid stress. JH+ was 1.02 mM min-1, and CMH+ reached 5.8 nM min-1 mV-1 (Figure 1).

Thus, during fermentation, E. coli primarily regulates the Δp through the chemical gradient—ΔpH—while the electrical potential—ΔΨ—remains relatively stable across different levels of acid stress. Therewith, JH+ and CMH+ decreased with increasing acid stress, indicating that the cells actively limit energetically non favorable proton efflux to conserve energy and maintain intracellular pH within viable ranges. Moreover, the decrease in JH+ at pH 5.8 and pH 5.4 compared with those exposed to pH 7.6 and pH 6.5 might be a result of the several processed typical for acidic environment. Bacteria reduce specific growth rate and the decrease of external pH during the 3.5 h growth was low, compared with pH 7.6 and pH 6.5 (Table 1), suggesting that metabolic activity and FOF1-ATPase activity might also be decreased.18,20

Altogether, these results reveal a coordinated and dynamic regulation of ΔpH, proton flux, and pHin that enables E. coli to adaptively sustain metabolic activity and viability under progressively acidic conditions.

Effects of AR system mutations on formation of proton motive force components

No acid stress

Under nonstressed fermentative conditions, WT cells maintained pHin at 6.42 units (Figure 2A). pHin was not changed in the ΔgadE mutant. It is suggested that loss of one AR system can be partially compensated by the remaining systems; particularly, Cad and Adi systems can still function in ΔgadE mutant and contribute regulation of pHin.10 pHex was decreased by approximately 0.2 units, compared with WT. This resulted in positive ΔpH (∼0.1 units). The ΔΨ increased marginally by about −13 mV, causing a net increase in Δp by −21 mV, compared with WT (Figure 1B). Although JH+ remained unchanged, extracellular acidification persisted, indicating that the H+ consuming glutamate decarboxylases GadA and GadB is missing; consequently, fermentative acid accumulation might dominate pHex changes.4,8,32 The CMH+ showed no difference from WT, suggesting the primary defect is the lack of the Gad pathway’s biochemical function (proton consumption), rather any secondary effect on general membrane integrity or proton leak at pH 7.6.

Neither the pHin nor the pHex was significantly altered in the ΔcadC or ΔadiY single mutants (Figure 2A). However, JH+ was increased in both strains: by ∼25% and ∼20%, respectively, in ΔcadC and ΔadiY (Figure 2C). CMH+ was significantly higher in the ΔcadC mutant (approximately 35 nM min−1 mV-1), suggesting a potential increase in membrane proton conductance via leakier membrane or upregulated proton efflux pathways.33,34

The triple ΔcadCΔadiYΔgadE mutant exhibited a more alkaline pHin (by ∼0.19 units) and an acidified pHex (by ∼0.54 units), resulting in a substantially higher ΔpH (0.69 units, 42 mV) (Figures 2A and 2B). The JH+ was significantly increased (by ∼40%), and Δp increased by ∼53 mV. Since the CMH+ remained unchanged, the increased JH+ suggests an activation of other, energy-efficient proton-exporting systems to compensate for the loss of the three major proton sink pathways.

Mild acid stress

ΔgadE and ΔcadC mutants regulated intracellular pHs via different ways under mild acid stress (Figure 3A). The ΔgadE strain demonstrated a decrease in both JH+ (by ∼30%) and CMH+ compared with WT, while other parameters were unaffected (Figure 3). This suggests that at this mild acidic pH, the Gad system implements an energy-efficient proton flux. The ΔcadC mutant regulated pHin; however, it lost the regulation of pHex, lowering it by ∼0.22 units. This led to an increase in ΔpH, which was the primary cause of the increased Δp (Figure 3B). The decrease in CMH+ (by ∼20%) suggests that proton flux occurs via actively regulated transport systems (Figure 3C).

The ΔadiY mutant showed an increase in pHin (∼0.33 units) and ΔpH (∼0.25 units, ∼15 mV), compared with WT, highlighting the importance of the Adi system under mild acid stress (Figures 3A and 3B). A compensatory minor change in ΔΨ (∼16 mV) resulted in an unchanged Δp. Decreased JH+ and CMH+ suggest that other Δp-regulating systems may be implementing intracellular proton neutralization more efficiently (Figure 3C).

In triple ΔcadCΔadiYΔgadE mutant only ΔΨ increased by ∼16 mV, resulting in a ∼19 mV increase in Δp (Figure 3B). The CMH+ was decreased. This hyperpolarization (ΔΨ increase) combined with reduced CMH+ compensates for the loss of multiple decarboxylase/antiporter pathways.

Moderate acid stress

At this moderately acidic pH, all mutant strains exhibited higher JH+ and CMH+ compared with the WT strain (Figure 4C). JH+ was significantly increased in ΔgadE (∼45%), ΔcadC (∼30%), ΔadiY (∼65%), and ΔcadCΔadiYΔgadE (∼60%). Other parameters were not changed in ΔgadE and ΔadiY, compared with WT. cadC showed acidified pHin (∼0.5 units) and pHex (∼0.24 units), compared with WT, with a decreased ΔpH (∼0.26 units, ∼16 mV), indicating the importance of the Cad system at pH 5.8 (Figure 4A and 4B). The triple mutant also showed a reduced pHin (∼0.3 units) and ΔpH (∼0.22 units, ∼13 mV). The universally higher CMH+ across all mutants suggests that all AR systems investigated are significantly involved in efficient proton transduction mechanisms at this mild acidic stress.

Sublethal acid stress

At this acidic pH, all mutant strains showed an increase in pHin: ΔgadE (by ∼0.22 units), ΔcadC (by ∼0.32 units), ΔadiY (by ∼0.28 units), and ΔcadCΔadiYΔgadE (by ∼0.60 units) compared with the WT, while pHex remained largely unchanged (Figure 5A). This resulted in an increased ΔpH in all mutants. Both JH+ and CMH+ were increased in all single mutants (Figure 5C). This finding supports the hypothesis that the absence of a specific AR system (a significant proton sink) leads to an increase in the general proton conductance or “leakiness” of the cell membrane, which allows for a higher total outflux of protons, driving the pHin to become more alkaline. Changes in ΔpH led to a significant increase in Δp only in the ΔcadC mutant (∼23 mV) and the triple mutant (∼44 mV) (Figure 5B).

Discussion

Growth under acidic conditions serves as a sensitive indicator of the ability of E. coli to exhibit adaptive stress responses to low extracellular pH.3,35 Analysis of μ in the absence of acid stress and under conditions of increased acid stress revealed a clear, stress-dependent effect of pHex on the growth of E. coli. Under nonstress conditions, cells exhibited the highest μ, which then gradually decreased, indicating that metabolism and energy were primarily directed toward coping with stress rather than toward biomass production.35 The acidification of pHex also decreased at 3.5 h of the fermentative growth, which is due to bacterial adaptation and a decrease in μ with increasing acid stress.

The maintenance of a stable pHin is a critical feature of bacterial physiology, commonly observed across a wide range of external pH conditions.2,26,33 Under respiratory conditions in the logarithmic phase, E. coli demonstrates remarkable pHin constancy, varying only from pH 7.2 to pH 7.8 across an extensive pH range of 5.5 to 9.0.36 Our investigation under fermentative conditions revealed that the pHin was slightly more acidic, spanning 6.13 to 6.42 within the pHex range of 5.4 to 7.6 during the early logarithmic phase. This slightly lower pHin during fermentation is consistent with previous reports, which cited values between 6.6 and 6.8 after 3 h of growth at pHex 5.5 and 6.5 using mixed carbon sources.20,37

These findings confirm that E. coli maintains pHin within a narrow, physiological range, exhibiting small variation in response to changes in pHex, largely independent of the growth conditions (respiration versus fermentation). Although homeostatic mechanisms are challenged under extreme pH, the accumulation of metabolically generated protons within the cytoplasm necessitates their active neutralization or efflux. This process influences the pHex and consequently modulates the ΔpH (pHin − pHex). It is important to mention that in our study, pHex was decreased at 3.5 h of the growth, as during fermentation, organic acids are generating, which caused the reduce in pHex, and they have their own role in the formation of Δp.31 The greatest absolute pH drop was observed under no acid stress and mild acid stress conditions, when the initial pH values are higher (7.6 and 6.5), and the low change in pHex encountered by cells grown under moderate and sublethal acid stress condition (initial pH 5.8 and pH 5.4). It is suggested that bacteria regulate metabolic pathways to less generation of organic acids for avoiding more acidification in pHex.18,20

The ΔpH was observed to be near zero under no acid stress condition, aligning with prior data indicating a minimal ΔpH (where pHin > pHex) around neutral growth pH.33 Consistent with established literature,18,28,37 the ΔpH increased progressively as growth pH was reduced from 7.6 to 5.8. Notably, a decline in ΔpH was observed under sublethal acidic stress condition (pH 5.4) compared with the moderate acid stress condition (pH 5.8), although the value remained elevated relative to mild acid stress condition (pH 6.5). This suggests a mechanistic shift in ΔpH regulation under sublethal acidic stress condition. At this acidity, previously identified mechanisms, including ion exchange, FOF1-ATPase mediated proton pumping, or the formate hydrogen lyase (FHL) system regulating H+ and H2 cycling, are potentially differentially deployed.18 Under fermentative conditions, the FOF1-ATPase operates in the ATP hydrolyzing mode pumping out H+ from the cytoplasm and contributing generation of Δp. Under acidic conditions proton FOF1-ATPase significantly contributes to the formation of both electrical and chemical components of Δp.18 FHL complexes consist of two components: formate dehydrogenase and hydrogenase 3 (FHL-1) or hydrogenase 4 (FHL-2): formate dehydrogenase disproportionates formate into CO2 and H+, and then hydrogenase generates H2 from H+. This effectively “neutralizes” formate produced during fermentation.20,38 In addition, ion exchange systems play a crucial role in regulating ΔpH under acidic conditions. Especially, it is suggested that Kup system operates as a K+/H+ symporter under acid condition. Moreover, influx of these ions might compensate some H+ efflux through the proton pump and with fermentation acids, and (or) movements of other ions across the membrane.39 Furthermore, the cells may alter the ratio of fermentative end products to influence and regulate the ΔpH.20,29,34,37

ΔΨ in WT exhibited no significant variation across the tested acid stress levels. This observation suggests that the Δp is predominantly regulated by the chemical component (ΔpH) during fermentation. Consequently, the ΔpH displayed a dependence on pHex: a gradual increase from no acid stress to moderate acid stress condition followed by a decrease under sublethal acid stress condition while remaining higher than the value determined under mild acid condition. This trend aligns with the concept that decreasing pHex transiently increases both the pH gradient and the resulting Δp.40 The co-regulation of ΔpH and pHin is evident, as reductions in ΔpH are known to impair E. coli’s ability to maintain pHin.41 Thus, the lower Δp and relatively low pHin observed under sublethal acid stress condition are likely intercorrelated adaptive responses aimed at maintaining cellular viability during acidic challenges.

The total JH+ was observed to decrease from 3.68 mM min−1 to 1.02 mM min−1 along with increasing acid stress. While previous studies under respiratory conditions showed that decreased pHex increased proton influx,40 our data during fermentation indicate that E. coli regulates Δp by limiting the accumulated proton pool within the cytoplasm, particularly when pHex was severely low.

To quantify the efficiency of proton translocation across the cytoplasmic membrane via the electrochemical gradient, the CMH+ was calculated. During fermentation, the generation and export of organic acids are expected to drive proton efflux, contributing to Δp dissipation.31,42 Protons may permeate the cytoplasmic membrane through specific channels, transporters, or nonspecific leaks within the lipid bilayer. Passive mechanisms of pH homeostasis, such as changes in membrane permeability and surface charges, are proposed to support active regulation by delaying proton entry or loss.33 Furthermore, adaptation to low pH involves modifications in inner and outer membrane composition to restrict proton uptake and accelerate extrusion.43

Our results demonstrated that in the WT strain, CMH+ was decreased from 35.7 nM min−1 mV-1 to 5.8 nM min−1 mV-1 in correspondence to the increasing acid stress level. This finding is consistent with values reported for Streptococcus faecalis during respiration.44 The decrease in CMH+ suggests a critical regulatory response: when external conditions are acidic and the ΔpH component of Δp is high, the cell must actively control proton influx. Lowering CMH+ represents an energy-conserving strategy, minimizing nonproductive proton leakage and increasing the efficiency of proton transduction to maintain viability, a known strategy for survival in acidic environments.34

Mutants deficient in specific AR exhibited altered homeostatic parameters, suggesting distinct roles for each system across the pH range. The Gad system appears crucial for regulating ΔpH and Δp by maintaining pHex under no acid stress conditions. Its importance lies in regulating proton transport and pH homeostasis during fermentation. Given that CMH+ was unchanged in the ΔgadE mutant, its function is likely not associated with membrane integrity; this is supported by the cytoplasmic localization and conformational change of GadB at different pH.9,45

The absence of Cad and Adi systems increased JH+, underscoring their general importance in pH homeostasis during acid production under fermentation conditions. CMH+ was increased in ΔcadC; thus, the absence of the Cad system leads to unregulated proton leakage, suggesting the system is vital for maintaining membrane integrity and proton homeostasis. The compensatory role of the Gad system in Δp under no acid stress condition was evident in the ΔcadC and ΔadiY mutants, suggesting potential regulatory crosstalk.10

The ΔcadCΔadiYΔgadE triple mutant (lacking the main regulators of the three major AR systems) showed an interrupted ΔpH regulation component, even under nonacid stress conditions. It is suggested that bacteria activate compensatory systems to preserve cytosolic pH: the cells therefore appear to export more protons actively (or otherwise route protons out), which raises pHin (fewer protons remaining inside) and increases ΔpH and consequently Δp (because ΔΨ remained stable). It is suggested that the H+-ATPase, which has high activity at slightly alkaline pH, in the case of a lack of three AR systems, operates more vigorously to pump out protons. On the other hand, the FHL complex might operate to neutralize protons to H2, increasing pHin.25,46,47

Under mild acid stress condition, the Adi system demonstrated a significant role in ΔpH homeostasis. Its absence (ΔadiY mutant) led to a decrease in JH+ and an increase in pHin and ΔpH. Moreover, a decrease in ΔΨ highlighted the importance of Adi in membrane potential regulation at this stress level. Crucially, the increase in ΔpH compensated for the decrease in ΔΨ, resulting in an unchanged Δp. The Adi system, known to be induced at low acidic pH under anaerobic condition. The Adi system is induced under anaerobic conditions at low acidic pH values,7,48 with AdiC responding to pH changes between pH 5.5 to 6.5.49 In our conditions, pH was decreased from startingand AdiC responds to pH changes within the range of 5.4-6.5. pH 6.5 to pH 5.4 during the growth, and Thus, the importance of Adi system in the regulation of Δp was evident under mild acid stress condition.

The ΔgadE and ΔcadC mutants maintained pHin via distinct, energy-conserving proton transport processes (CMH+ was decreased in both strains) under mild acid stress. The ΔgadE mutant achieved effective energy transduction by reducing JH+, while the ΔcadC mutant achieved it by increasing Δp via an enhanced ΔpH. These results suggest that when Gad or Cad are nonfunctional, compensatory mechanisms efficiently transport protons, impacting various components of CMH+.

The triple mutant exhibited an increased ΔΨ ∼16 mV, which translated to an increased Δp value and simultaneous decrease in CMH+. The increase in ΔΨ did not affect the proton gradient or flux rate, suggesting that this variation in membrane potential may be coupled to the flux of other ions (K+, Na+, Cl−, etc.) in a controlled, energetic process, especially when CMH+ was decreased and H+ translocation occurred under control of energetic processes.30,50,51,52

Under moderate acid stress condition, the Cad system was paramount in pHin regulation. The cadC mutant displayed a significant drop in pHin (∼0.50 units) and a decrease in ΔpH (∼0.26 units, ∼16 mV) compared with WT. This aligns with prior reports that expression of the E. coli cadBA operon, encoding functions required for the conversion of lysine to cadaverine and for cadaverine excretion, requires at least two extracellular signals: low pH (optimum pH for CadA induction is pH 5.8) and a high concentration of lysine.17,53 Our results have shown that during fermentation, the Cad system is important for the regulation of ΔpH; moreover, its role in proton export occurred via efficient energy transduction, as CMH+ was increased in the ΔcadC mutant, suggesting protons outflux via leakier membrane (JH+ was increased by ∼30%, compared with WT).

The ΔgadE and ΔadiY mutants showed no change in Δp or its components under moderate acid stress level. Moreover, regulation of pHin was implemented, increasing JH+. In the triple mutant strain, both pHin and ΔpH were decreased, which is a result of the absence of the Cad system, as the other two systems did not affect the regulation of pHin under moderate acid stress conditions. Thus, the significant acidification of pHin in ΔcadC at this stress level highlights the critical and nonredundant role of the Cad system in maintaining pH homeostasis under moderately acidic conditions.

The most striking finding was under sublethal acid stress level, where all single mutants showed simultaneous increases in pHin, ΔpH, JH+, and CMH+. This indicates that the failure of any one major AR system under sublethal acid stress compromises overall membrane integrity and/or regulation, resulting in a nonspecific variation in CMH+. The cell responds by aggressively increasing its total JH+, via this heightened conductance to elevate pHin, resulting in a higher pHin and ΔpH. This high CMH+ signifies a transition from tightly regulated proton transport to a state of “leakier” membrane, an emergency response to prevent acidification. It has been previously shown that in response to low pH, both inner and outer membrane composition undergo significant modification, including differential synthesis of transporters, efflux pumps, and fatty acids. These adaptations restrict proton influx and enhance proton extrusion.43 In the single mutants, where one of the AR systems is not active, the elevated CMH+ suggest a shift from tightly regulated proton transport to a state of “leakier” membrane. Moreover, the Gad AR system has been shown to contribute to a “reversed ΔΨ,” limiting proton uptake. During fermentation, organic acids are produced, which additionally affect the intracellular pH.33 During acid stress, the removal of acids and, accordingly, protons becomes a primary task for the cell to maintain viability. In mutants lacking AR systems, this regulated control may be compromised, and the observed increase in CMH+ was detected. Such increased CMH+ may arise from alterations in both lipid and protein components, consistent with previous studies demonstrating that both phospholipids and membrane proteins are key determinants of proton conductance.54 This high-conductance state represents a failure in an efficient acid adaptation mechanism, leaving the cell in a vulnerable and energetically costly situation. The massive increase in pHin, ΔpH, and Δp in the triple mutant, coupled with high CMH+, underscores the extreme activation of all available defense mechanisms. The large Δp increase (by ∼44 mV, compared with WT) highlights that even without the three major AR systems, E. coli possesses robust, ΔpH-driven systems to survive sublethal acid stress.

Thus, under sublethal acid stress, the mutants are less able to restrict uncontrolled proton outflux. This forces the cell to expend significant extra energy to maintain a viable internal pH (resulting in the observed pHin changes) by constantly pumping protons out. Thus, bacteria included AR systems in the regulation of Δp at different pH values during fermentation, when organic acids are generated, and under sublethal acid stress, all AR systems are modulating to regulate pHin and generate Δp and save viability.

Our future work will investigate the precise mechanisms by which AR systems regulate pHin at different pH values, including the measurement of ionic flux rates (K+, Na+, Cl−), FOF1-ATPase activity, and associated metabolic and energetic fluxes.

Conclusion

In this work, the role of E. coli AR systems (Gad, Cad, and Adi) in the formation and regulation of Δp and JH+ was investigated depending on the bacterial growth pH during fermentation. Our findings establish that the AR systems exhibit highly specialized and distinct roles in Δp formation, which are dependent on the acid stress level. E. coli sustains pHin within a physiological range, largely independent of the growth conditions. In WT, Δp is generally regulated by the chemical component (ΔpH) during fermentation, and ΔΨ exhibited no crucial variation under acid stress levels tested. The decrement in CMH+ with increasing acidity suggests a key regulatory response to control proton flux via efficient energy transduction.

The Gad system has a crucial role in regulating ΔpH and Δp by maintaining pHex under no acid stress condition. This system is involved in the proton efflux via efficient energy transduction, and its function is likely not associated with membrane integrity. Under mild acid stress, the Adi system demonstrated a significant role in ΔpH homeostasis. A key observation was that the increase in ΔpH in ΔadiY mutant compensated for the decrease in ΔΨ, maintaining unchanged Δp. The increase in ΔΨ in the ΔcadCΔadiYΔgadE triple mutant, which did not affect ΔpH and JH+, suggests that this variation may be coupled to the flux of other ions. Under moderate acid stress, Cad system was essential for the regulation of ΔpH, and its function in the efflux of protons occurred via efficient energy transduction. The significant finding was the simultaneous increases in pHin, ΔpH, JH+, and CMH+ in all single mutants, when bacteria were grown under sublethal acid stress condition. It is suggested that AR systems do not function redundantly; rather, they serve distinct, pH-optimized roles in Δp regulation. Their collective presence is indispensable for efficient pH homeostasis, particularly under severe fermentative acid conditions.
