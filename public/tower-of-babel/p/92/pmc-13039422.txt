B7-H3-mediated cis-inhibition of EGFR by a tumor-selective bispecific antibody enhances anti-tumor efficacy and minimizes toxicities
Authors: Jian Guan, Tiongsun Chia, Bin Li, Tianyu Zhu, Zhengguang Liao, Junjie Deng, Fenggen Fu, Weiwei Wu, Chengtao Liu, Ya Liu, Ninghuan Li, Lili Yue, Lei Cao, Jia Lu, Mengjia Zhu, Xiaomin Ling, Huilin Zheng, Shuming Lin, Li Li, Shuaixiang Zhou, Kaijie He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Therapeutic targeting of epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) in solid tumors faces significant limitations due to on-target/off-tumor toxicities, underscoring the urgent need for tumor-selective anti-EGFR therapies. Comprehensive bioinformatics and histopathological analyses identify marked upregulation of B7-H3 across EGFR-positive malignancies, contrasting with its minimal expression in healthy tissues. Leveraging an unbiased functional screen of bispecific antibodies (bsAbs) combining diverse B7-H3 and EGFR binders, we develop IBI334, a EGFR/B7-H3 bsAb exhibiting exceptional tumor selectivity. In preclinical models, IBI334 outperforms conventional EGFR antibodies by demonstrating superior EGFR occupancy, enhanced ligand-blocking efficacy, accelerated receptor degradation, and potent suppression of downstream EGFR signaling. Mechanistic studies demonstrate B7-H3-mediated cis-inhibition. The human B7-H3 extracellular domain (ECD) in complex with anti-B7-H3 Fab is resolved by cryo-EM, revealing critical residues for the antibody-B7-H3 interaction. IBI334 demonstrates robust antitumor activity in vitro and in vivo across EGFR-driven tumor models and synergized effectively with KRAS inhibitors. Toxicological evaluations in non-human primates reveals a favorable safety profile, with no EGFR-related adverse effects observed at doses up to 120 mg/kg over 4 weeks. Supported by these preclinical findings, IBI334 has advanced to a phase 1 clinical trial (NCT05774873) for advanced/metastatic solid tumors.

Introduction

Epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) activating mutations and amplifications are common oncogenic drivers in multiple cancers, including colorectal, gastric, breast, non-small cell lung, head and neck squamous cell and pancreatic cancers and diffuse intrinsic pontine glioma and cholangiocarcinoma1,2. Many EGFR-targeted therapies, such as monoclonal antibodies (e.g., Cetuximab3,4, Panitumumab5, Necitumumab6, Nimotuzumab7), bispecific antibody (e.g., Amivantamab8) and small molecule tyrosine kinase inhibitors (e.g., Gefitinib9, Afatinib10, Osimertinib11), have received approval for treating EGFR-positive tumors. Despite the clinical benefits of EGFR inhibitors, they are associated with various dose-limiting adverse events such as acneiform eruption, severe gastrointestinal (GI) toxicities, and rare but severe skin toxicities (e.g., toxic epidermal necrolysis), which not only significantly compromise patient quality of life but also limit the maximal therapeutic potential of the drugs12–14.

B7 Homolog 3 (B7-H3) is a single-pass transmembrane protein that is minimally expressed in normal tissues but is upregulated in several malignancies including lung, prostate, breast, pancreatic, colorectal, cutaneous squamous cell, and esophageal squamous cell carcinoma15,16. Elevated B7-H3 expression correlates with immune evasion, metastasis, disease progression and recurrence, poor prognosis, and resistance to therapy17–23. Human B7-H3 has two isoforms, 2Ig-B7-H3 (IgV-IgC domains similar to murine B7-H3) and 4Ig-B7-H3 (duplicated IgV-IgC domains)24, and the human 4Ig-B7-H3 isoform (referred to as “B7-H3” hereafter for simplicity) is the predominant form in human tumors25,26. B7-H3 is a promising therapeutic target, with various B7-H3-targeted therapies under clinical development, including monoclonal antibodies, bispecific/trispecific antibodies, antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs), CAR-T cell therapies and radionuclide-drug conjugates (RDCs)23,27.

In this work, we conduct bioinformatics analysis and immunohistochemistry staining to show strong co-expression of EGFR and B7-H3 in multiple solid tumours, but not in normal tissues. Then we design and screen B7-H3/EGFR bispecific antibodies to selectively augment EGFR signal suppression in B7-H3 positive tumors. The preclinical characterization of the lead bispecific antibody, IBI334, is comprehensively described in this report, encompassing its design rationale, mechanisms of action, structural analysis, anti-tumor efficacy, and toxicity profiles in animal studies.

Results

B7-H3 and EGFR are co-expressed in various tumors

To assess B7-H3 and EGFR co-expression across different cancer types, we analyzed RNA expression data of both targets from the TCGA database using linear regression. Statistically significant co-expression was observed in lung, head and neck, pancreatic, esophageal, thyroid, and kidney cancers (Fig. 1a). This co-expression pattern persisted when analyzing all cancer types collectively from TCGA, as well as in various cancer cell lines (Fig. 1b, c). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining confirmed the co-expression of both targets in lung squamous carcinoma, but not in normal lung or skin (Fig. 1d, e). Notably, B7-H3 was highly expressed in lung squamous carcinoma patient samples with minimal expression in normal tissues (Fig. 1d, and Supplementary Fig. 1a–c); whereas EGFR, but not B7-H3, was expressed in normal skin tissues (Fig. 1d, and Supplementary Fig. 1d). Immunofluorescent staining demonstrated co-localization of EGFR with B7-H3 on the same cancer cells (Fig. 1f). 20G5/Zalu balances antitumor efficacy and safety

To enhance EGFR inhibition while minimising on-target toxicities, we assembled half-antibodies from five anti-EGFR clones with varying inhibition potencies (Supplementary Fig. 2a–d, and Supplementary Table 1) and 11 anti-B7-H3 clones differing in affinity, binding epitopes and ADCC activities (Supplementary Fig. 2e–h, Supplementary Table 2) into the “1 + 1” asymmetric IgG-like bsAbs using Innobody platform. These bsAbs were then extensively screened using unbiased functional bioassays across both cancerous and normal cell lines (Fig. 2a). The degree of optimization was assessed based on the efficacy and safety parameters. A B7-H3/EGFR clone demonstrating strong in vitro anti-tumor effects, indicated by a lower IC50 value, received a higher efficacy score. To identify the optimal EGFR binder, three distinct B7-H3 clones (20G5, 5C2, and 19A2) with varying epitopes on Ig domains, and different ADCC activities were evaluated against five EGFR binders in bsAb format (Supplementary Fig. 2i–k, and Supplementary Table 1–2). Among these, the bsAb combining anti-B7-H3 clone 20G5 and anti-EGFR clone Zalutumumab (20G5/Zalu) emerged as the most promising candidate based on efficacy and safety parameters (Fig. 2b, and Supplementary Fig. 2l), demonstrating the most potent anti-tumor efficacy (Supplementary Fig. 2m), reduced skin cytotoxicity compared to Zalutumumab (Fig. 2c, and Supplementary Fig. 2n, o) and superior ADCC activity (Supplementary Fig. 3a-d).

Mechanistically, 20G5/Zalu showed much stronger binding activity to EGFR than Zalutumumab on B7-H3-positive cancer cells as measured by both binding competition and receptor occupancy assays (Fig. 2d-e, Supplementary Fig. 3e-g). This resulted in better internalization of EGFR, and a more potent and sustained suppression of the downstream ERK phosphorylation than Zalutumumab (Fig. 2f–i, and Supplementary Fig. 3h–j), even in the presence of EGFR ligands, EGF and TNF-α (Fig. 2i, j). In addition, growth inhibition of IBI334 on SK-MES1 and PC-9 cells co-cultured with CHO-S cells overexpressing B7-H3 (B7-H3OE) (trans-inhibition) showed minimal increase in efficacy (Supplementary Fig. 3k, l), indicating that the anti-tumor effects of IBI334 are primarily cis-mediated. To further delineate the mechanisms of cis versus trans binding, we established a sparse plating model by culturing SK-MES-1wt and SK-MES1-B7-H3OE cells at low density to minimize cell-cell contacts, thereby isolating cis-interaction effects (Fig. 2k; and Supplementary Fig. 3m). Western blot analysis showed that 20G5/Zalu exerted strong suppression of EGFR phosphorylation (pEGFR) in SK-MES-1-B7-H3OE cells, whereas only marginal inhibition was observed in SK-MES-1wt cells (Fig. 2l). Importantly, the control antibody null/Zalu (lacking the anti-B7-H3 arm) exhibited minimal pEGFR inhibition in B7-H3OE cells (Fig. 2m), demonstrating the essential role of B7-H3 targeting for effective EGFR signal blockade. Fluorescence microscopy analysis of pEGFR levels revealed stronger suppression in 20G5/Zalu-treated B7-H3OE single cells (cis-inhibition) compared to wild-type (WT) cells contacting B7-H3OE neighbors (trans-inhibition) (Fig. 2n). The untreated control groups showed comparable baseline pEGFR signals between B7-H3OE and WT cells. These data collectively suggest that 20G5/Zalu-mediated inhibition is primarily cis-mediated.

20G5/Zalu showed binding preference to the IgC1 of human B7-H3

To further elucidate the binding mechanism of 20G5/Zalu, we mapped the epitope (s) of 20G5 Fab (fragment-antigen binding) on the redundant IgC domains of human B7-H3. Structure prediction28 of human 2Ig-B7-H3 indicated that Q179, Q199, D159 and T160 are solvent-exposed residues (Supplementary Fig. 4d) that can be accessed by 20G5. We generated CHO cell lines overexpressing either wild-type or mutant 2Ig-B7-H3 containing mouse back mutations (Q179K, Q199R, or D159T + T160M). In contrast to Q199R or D159T + T160M, only Q179K mutation significantly reduced the binding activity of 20G5, indicating Q179 as the essential residue in the epitope on 2Ig-B7-H3 (IgC) (Fig. 3b). As human B7-H3 (4Ig-B7-H3) contains two redundant IgC domains (IgV1-IgC1-IgV2-IgC2), to investigate whether 20G5 binds equally to the two epitopes containing either Q179 (IgC1) or its counterpart Q397 (IgC2), we created NCI-H292 cell lines overexpressing human B7-H3 with Q179K, Q397K, or Q179K + Q397K mutations. Interestingly, Q179K single mutation only partially weakened 20G5 binding, indicating a second epitope on IgC2 which can compensate for the disruption of epitope on IgC1 (Fig. 3c, d). In contrast, Q397K single mutation had minimal impact on the binding of 20G5/null, suggesting the epitope containing Q397 on IgC2 is dispensable if 20G5 has access to the epitope on IgC1. Moreover, Q179K + Q397K double mutations synergized to nearly eliminate 20G5 binding. Consistently, the NCI-H292 cell line expressing B7-H3Q179K+Q397K exhibited the weakest tumor growth inhibition by 20G5/Zalu compared to cells expressing B7-H3Q179K, B7-H3Q397K, or B7-H3WT, while cytotoxicity was only marginally affected in B7-H3Q397K expressing H292 cells (Fig. 3e). These results confirm that 20G5 can bind to either epitope on IgC1 and IgC2 of 4Ig-B7-H3, but with a binding preference to the Q179-containing epitope in the IgC1 domain.

Cryo-EM analysis of 20G5 (Fab’)2 and human B7-H3 complex

Human B7-H3 has four extracellular immunoglobulin domains arranged in an IgV1-IgC1-IgV2-IgC2 configuration (Supplementary Fig. 4e). The presence of a long, flexible loop between the IgC1 and IgV2 domains has made it difficult to resolve the complete structure of the B7-H3 ECD using cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM). Since 20G5 Fab recognizes both epitopes on the IgC1 and IgC2 domains, bivalent 20G5 (Fab’)2 binding to B7-H3 with high avidity can stabilize the 4Ig-B7-H3 ECD.

Given the possibility of different binding valences of 20G5 F(ab’)2 to 4Ig-B7-H3, the study identified two complexes: the bivalent 20G5 F(ab’)2 and human B7-H3 (4Ig-B7-H3) complex at a resolution of 3.59 Å (Supplementary Fig. 4f, g), and the monovalent 20G5 Fab and B7-H3 (IgC) complex at a resolution of 2.98 Å (Fig. 3f-g, Supplementary Fig. 4f-i). Unfortunately, the density of (Fab’)2 and the B7-H3 complex is insufficient for further interpretation.

The IgC domain of B7-H3 is characterized by a typical β-sandwich structure formed by ABED and CFG sheets. The 20G5 Fab engages all three CDRs to bind the IgC domain, primarily interacting with the CFG β-sheets through electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonds, and hydrophobic interactions (Fig. 3g). The epitope residues from the CD loop (Q179-P185) are deeply embedded between the VH and VL domains of 20G5 Fab, where they establish hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions with the side chains of HCDR1-3 and LCDR3. Notably, F177 aligns with aromatic residues W103 (HCDR3) and Y31 (LCDR1), while R223 engages in an electrostatic interaction with D49 in LCDR2. This structural insight prompted us to engineer single-point mutations in 2Ig-B7-H3 (Fig. 3h), pinpointing F177, Q179, D180, Q182, V184, P185, and R223 as crucial residues for the binding of 20G5 Fab. These interactions between the epitope and antigen are key to the high specificity of 20G5 Fab for the IgC domain of B7-H3.

Low fucosylation of 20G5/Zalu enhances ADCC effects

For therapeutic enhancement, the fragment crystallizable (Fc) region of the 20G5/Zalu was afucosylated using GlymaxX® technology, forming the lead molecule IBI334. This modification enhances its affinity to human RcγRIIIa (CD16A) and antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) effects, making IBI334 more effective than IBI334-IgG1, which has a standard IgG1 Fc (as shown in Supplementary Fig. 5a and Supplementary Table 4). Both versions of the molecule inhibit EGFR-mediated growth similarly, which is expected since the modification affects only the Fc region (Supplementary Fig. 5b). In an in vivo non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) model using NCI-H1975 cells (with EGFR L858R and T790M mutations) implanted in nude mice, IBI334 showed limited anti-tumor efficacy (Supplementary Fig. 5c). However, when these cells were implanted in NOG mice reconstituted with human NK cells, IBI334 demonstrated significant efficacy, highlighting its potent ADCC effects in vivo (Supplementary Fig. 5d).

It was previously reported that EGFR mutated NSCLC patients treated with Osimertinib usually developed resistance due to the additional EGFRC797S mutation. Preclinical and early clinical studies have indicated synergistic effects between EGFR inhibitors and KRAS inhibitors30–33. This prompted us to investigate whether IBI334 could synergize with KRAS inhibitors. Indeed, IBI334 displayed strong synergy with AMG510, a KRASG12C inhibitor, in NCI-H358 cells harboring the KRASG12C mutation, both in vitro and in vivo (Fig. 5f–h, Supplementary Fig. 7b). IBI334 showed good pharmacokinetics and safety profiles in cynomolgus monkey

The pharmacokinetics (PK) of IBI334 was assessed in cynomolgus monkeys following a single intravenous administration at doses of 3, 10, or 30 mg/kg (Fig. 6a, Supplementary Table 5). Within this dose range, IBI334 exhibited a non-linear PK with slightly more than dose-proportional increase of drug exposure (mean AUClast values were 3430 ± 686, 17100 ± 1840, and 66400 ± 9810 h*µg/mL for 3, 10 and 30 mg/kg, respectively), and higher clearance at low dose, possibly due to target-mediated drug disposition (TMDD) effect34. The average terminal half-life (T1/2) of IBI334 was ~3 days, which support weekly or longer dosing interval. To assess the toxicokinetics (TK) and safety profile of IBI334, cynomolgus monkeys of both sexes received intravenous doses of 30, 60, and 120 mg/kg of IBI334 weekly for a total of five doses, adhering to good laboratory practice (GLP) conditions (Fig. 6b). The mean serum exposure to IBI334 increased proportionally with the dose across the range from 30 to120 mg/kg. After the first and fourth doses, there were no significant differences in serum IBI334 exposure between sexes, and no significant accumulation (accumulation factor <1.56 ± 0.29) was detected (Fig. 6c, Supplementary Table 6).

Throughout the dosing period, there were no deaths, moribund states, or other significant toxic symptoms observed in any of the monkey groups. At 2 hours post-first dose, serum IL-6 increase was noted in most monkeys that received IBI334, with highest average increase observed at 60 mg/kg group (7.2 ~ 242.9 pg/mL), but the increase was transient and returned to normal 24 h after dosing (Fig. 6d). There were no significant changes in ALT and AST levels (Fig. 6e). Other blood biochemical parameters remained within normal ranges across all groups. Histopathological evaluations revealed mild increase in sinusoidal cells in the liver, moderate perivascular inflammation at the injection site, minimal to mild interstitial mononuclear cell infiltration in the kidneys, and minimal to mild mucosal mononuclear cell infiltration in the stomach for all IBI334-treated groups (Fig. 6f, Supplementary Table 7). Overall, IBI334 demonstrated a favorable TK and safety profile.

Discussions

In this study, we present a comprehensive preclinical development strategy for the B7-H3/EGFR bispecific antibody IBI334. The rational drug design process initiated with co-expression screening-based target selection, followed by systematic evaluation of efficacy and safety parameters through optimized screening methodologies. The resultant molecule demonstrated: (1) potent antitumor activity across preclinical models; (2) favorable safety profile through tumor-selective targeting; and (3) synergistic potential with existing EGFR-targeted therapies.

Structurally, IBI334 adopts an asymmetric “1 + 1” IgG-like configuration, comprising a moderate-affinity anti-EGFR Fab with ligand-blocking capability, and a high-affinity anti-B7-H3 Fab targeting IgC1 domain. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that the monovalent EGFR antibody (null/Zalu) alone or in combination with the monovalent B7-H3 antibody (20G5/null) only minimally inhibit EGFR and its downstream pathways, leading to limited tumor-killing efficacy. These findings align with our design rationale, confirming that a single-arm moderate EGFR blocker could not adequately suppress EGFR signaling. This aspect is crucial for IBI334 to reduce toxicities in organs with low B7-H3 expression, such as skin, gastrointestinal tract, and lungs, and to overcome the dose-limiting toxicities (DLTs) commonly associated with EGFR antibodies.

Despite exhibiting modest single-arm activity, IBI334 showed remarkable activity in EGFR blocking and anti-tumor efficacy across various tumor models compared to traditional bivalent EGFR antibodies. Given that B7-H3 does not directly engage in EGFR or related oncogenic pathways, and the lack of tumor cell killing activity by B7-H3 antibodies, we propose that IBI334 leverages its unique bispecific configuration to create a synergistic mechanism. This mechanism significantly enhances EGFR blocking by utilizing the B7-H3 binding arm, which is unattainable through the monotherapies or combination of anti-EGFR and anti-B7-H3 antibodies. The high-affinity B7-H3 arm of IBI334 firmly anchors the molecule to the cell surface, allowing the EGFR arm to efficiently locate and bind to nearby EGFR receptors on the same cell surface in cis position. This configuration facilitates sustained EGFR suppression, contributing to its potent anti-tumor effects.

The mechanism of action of IBI334 is characterized by three distinctive features: (1) Enhanced binding specificity. Considering the critical role of EGFR in normal epithelial tissues, increasing the affinity of anti-EGFR antibodies does not necessarily translate into enhanced efficacy. Instead, high affinity EGFR blockers are always associated with target-related adverse effects in clinical trials, thereby failing to expand the therapeutic window35–37. In contrast, given that B7-H3 exhibits differential expression in tumors versus normal tissues and is not involved in key physiological functions, we can confidently employ a strategy to boost the affinity of anti-B7-H3 in IBI334 (KD = 0.27 nM, ~12 times higher than its EGFR arm) (Supplementary Table 3). This approach allows for selective targeting of B7-H3-positive tumor cells to achieve cis-inhibition of EGFR, while preserving the physiological function of EGFR in non-tumor tissues. The unique asymmetric and imbalanced affinity design of IBI334 demonstrated superior tumor cell binding, increased EGFR receptor occupancy, and enhanced biological activity compared to Zalutumumab and Cetuximab, thereby providing a preclinical proof-of-concept for designing EGFR super blockers without modifying the EGFR binding affinity. (2) Enhanced blockade efficiency. The robust cis-inhibition of EGFR, driven by the high-affinity anti-B7-H3 arm of IBI334, leads to a potent and sustained suppression of EGFR phosphorylation and downstream signaling pathways, whether in the presence or absence of EGFR ligands. Additionally, IBI334 prompts rapid and enduring degradation of EGFR in both cancer cells and xenograft tumors, likely initiated by antibody-induced receptor endocytosis. This mechanism may partially elucidate the superior effectiveness of IBI334 in tumors resistant to EGFR-TKI, potentially through the degradation of mutated EGFR proteins and/or disruption of EGFR scaffold functions and subsequent oncogenic signaling in tumors. Notably, IBI334 facilitates significantly greater EGFR internalization and degradation compared to anti-EGFR antibodies lacking B7-H3 assistance, such as monovalent null/Zalu or bivalent Zalutumumab. This suggests that the B7-H3 arm of IBI334 not only boosts EGFR binding but also markedly enhances EGFR internalization via a yet-to-be-identified mechanism, meriting further investigation. (3) Enhanced synergistic effect. The IBI334 molecule is designed to achieve optimal synergistic functionality by requiring the concurrent binding of its EGFR and B7H3 arms to their respective antigens on the same cell surface. This synergy is compromised if only one arm is engaged or if the arms bind to different cells in trans. This has been confirmed through various in vitro and in vivo models. Additionally, IBI334 leverages both kinase-dependent (ligand blocking) and kinase-independent (EGFR degradation and ADCC functions) mechanisms. This dual approach allows IBI334 to more effectively trigger tumor cell death, especially when used in combination with other EGFR pathway inhibitors, resulting in synergistic anti-tumor efficacy. This combination strategy is employed to overcome resistance to single-agent and to provoke a more profound tumor response.

The structural constraints of the bispecific antibodies, such as bispecific format, molecular length, Fab elbow angle and hinge flexibility etc., can limit its functionality38,39. Despite testing various anti-B7-H3 binders with comparably high affinities, only a select few that target specific domains and epitopes effectively synergize with the EGFR binder to create a highly functional EGFR-blocking bispecific molecule. To delve deeper into how the binding characteristics of the B7-H3 binder influence the bispecific’s functionality, we mapped the primary binding epitopes of anti-B7-H3 clone 20G5 and analyzed the cryo-EM structure of the 20G5(Fab’)2/hB7-H3 complex. This analysis pinpointed the precise amino acid epitopes where 20G5 binds to the IgC domains of B7-H3, highlighting the critical residues and intermolecular interactions that bolster the binding affinity. However, due to the sequence similarities between the IgC1 and IgC2 domains (Supplementary Fig. 4j), it was challenging to determine from the structural data alone which domain 20G5 prefers. By mutating key residues in these domains (Q179K in IgC1 and Q397K in IgC2) and assessing their impact on binding, we inferred that 20G5 Fab predominantly targets the IgC1 domain of hB7-H3. Integrating this information with previous structural simulations of anti-EGFR antibodies40–42, we modeled how the IBI334 molecule potently inhibits EGFR by B7-H3 guided ligand blockade (Fig. 3i).

Our model suggests that IBI334 leverages the high binding affinity of 20G5 to preferentially recognize and bind to the IgC1 domain of hB7-H3 on the cell membrane. The slow dissociation rate of 20G5 Fab allows IBI334 to remain bound to B7-H3, prolonging its presence on the cell surface and increasing the likelihood of the other arm engaging nearby EGFR receptors. Structural insights into another bispecific IgG1 (Zenocutuzumab) indicate that one Fab arm binding to a distal membrane epitope of the first target can provide the necessary spatial distance to freely orient the other Fab arm to interact with the adjacent second target43. Similarly, 20G5 Fab binds to the IgC1 domain of B7-H3 at a location distal from the cell membrane. This positioning provides the EGFR Fab arm with optimal flexibility to recognize and inhibit EGFR in its two distinct activation states39,40: (1) by directly competing with receptor-bound ligands for interaction with domain III in the extended configuration of EGFR; or (2) by binding to and maintaining the ligand-free EGFR in its tethered (inactive) conformation. This allows IBI334 to effectively compete with the high local concentration of EGFR ligands within the tumor microenvironment, thereby continuously suppressing EGFR signaling. Moreover, the high expression of surface B7-H3 antigens helps to accumulate IBI334 on the cell membrane, which in turn increases the local concentration and potency of EGFR blockade. Given that IBI334 requires a 1:1 stoichiometry binding to both antigens to maximize its biological function, the absolute number and relative density of B7-H3 and EGFR on the cell membrane are expected to greatly impact IBI334’s anti-tumor efficacy. Further exploration in subsequent biomarker studies is necessary to validate these hypotheses and determine how the expression levels of B7-H3 and EGFR could guide patient stratification and predict treatment efficacy.

In conclusion, IBI334 represents a next-generation bispecific antibody that redefines EGFR targeting through: (1) tumor-selective pathway inhibition; (2) multi-mechanistic receptor suppression; (3) synergistic spatial regulation. These preclinical findings support its ongoing phase I clinical evaluation (NCT05774873) in EGFR-driven malignancies.

Cell lines and reagents

Cancer cell lines were sourced from various institutions: YBio (PC9), Cobioer (TE-1, SK-MES-1, NCI-H322, NCI-H1650, CCK-81, HT-55, NCI-H508, LS180, COLO680, NCI-H358, HCT116, COLO205, NCI-H2122, NCI-H1993, SW-48, AsPC-1, NCI-H820, SCC-9), Cell lines service (HaCat), Cell Bank/Stem Cell Bank of Chinese Academy of Sciences (NCI-H292, SW480), and the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; A431, NCI-H1975, NCI-H1703, NCI-H2030, NCI-H520, SW-1573, NCI-H441, Calu-6, HCC4006, NCI-H1792, FaDu, LoVo), Meisen CTCC (HCC2279). These cell lines were maintained under manufacturers’ recommended conditions in growth media supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Hyclone, SH30406.05) and Penicillin-Streptomycin antibiotics (100 U/ml penicillin and 100 ug/ml streptomycin) (Gibco, 15140-122) at 37 oC in a 5% CO2 humidified incubator.

Genetically modified stable cell lines, including PC9-B7-H3, SK-MES-1-B7-H3, SK-MES-1-B7-H3-GFP, NCI-H322SVD (EGFRexon20ins-S768_D770dup), SK-MES-1LTC (EGFR-L858R/T790M/C797S), NCI-H1975LTC, and NCI-H292-B7-H3 mutants, were generated using the LV-MAX™ Lentiviral Production System (Gibco, A35684CN). Following lentiviral transduction, these cells were selected for 2 weeks in media containing 1 μg/ml puromycin (Invitrogen, A11138-02). Human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from healthy donors were procured from TPCS and All Cells. The small molecule inhibitors employed in this study included Osimertinib (MCE, HY-15772), AMG510 (MCE, HY-114277), MRTX133 (MCE, HY-134813), and LY3009120 (Macklin, L873098).

Protein expression and production

The sequences of the variable regions from the heavy and light chains of Panitumumab (CAS NO., 339177-26-3), Cetuximab (CAS NO., 205923-56-4), Necitumumab (CAS NO., 906805-06-9), Zalutumumab (Patent, WO2002100348A), Nimotuzumab (CAS NO., 828933-61-3), MGA271 (CAS NO., 1353485-38-7), M30 (INN, PL126), 8H9 (CAS NO., 1895083-75-6) and internally generated B7-H3 monoclonal antibodies (5C2, 20G5, 38G9, 19A2, 6C2, 1C5, 20E4, 7B6; PCT patent CN2021/140449) were cloned into the pcDNA3.1 vector (Invitrogen, V790-20). This vector includes the Fc region of wild-type human IgG1 for monoclonal antibodies or the Fc region with S364R + D399K or K370S + K409D + Y349T substitutions from the Innobody platform (WO Patent2022143912A1) for bispecific antibodies. The plasmids were synthesized by Genewiz, and the antibodies were produced using the ExpiCHO Expression System (Invitrogen, A29133). GlymaxX technology is used to produce afucosylated antibodies44. Briefly, antibodies were expressed in Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells overexpressing GDP-6-deoxy-D-lyxo-4-hexulose reductase (RMD), a prokaryotic enzyme that deflects an intermediate in the de novo synthesis of fucose to a dead-end product, and the resulting antibodies are afucosylated.

Antibodies

For the FACS internalization assay, AF647-anti-human EGFR antibody (BD, 563577) was used. For IHC, multiplexed immunofluorescence (mIF), and western blotting (WB), the following antibodies were utilized: EGFR (CST, 4267), pEGFR (CST, 3777), B7-H3 (CST, 14508), AKT (CST, 4691), pAKT (CST, 4060), ERK (CST, 4695), pERK (CST, 4370), S6 (CST, 2217S), pS6 (CST, 2211S), GAPDH (CST, 2218), Pan CK (Gene Tech, GM351514), and Ki67 (CST, 9027S).

Affinity and epitope binning measurement

The binding affinity and kinetics were assessed using biolayer interferometry (BLI) on a Fortebio Octet RED96. The monoclonal antibodies for B7-H3, EGFR, and the bispecific antibody for B7-H3/EGFR were immobilized on AH sensors (Sartorius, 18-5060) at a concentration of 100 nM for 100 seconds. The analytes, 2Ig-B7-H3 (Sino Biological, 29849-H08H), 4Ig-B7-H3 (Sino Biological, 11188-H08H), and EGFR (Acro Biosystems, EGR-H5222) were prepared at 100 nM, associated with the antibody for 100 seconds, and then dissociated in buffer for 120 seconds. The affinity data was processed using Fortebio data analysis software (Version10.0), employing a 1:1 binding model.

Epitope binning for the B7-H3 mAbs were conducted on the Octet Red96 using an in-tandem format. B7-H3 (Sino Biological, 29849-H08H) was immobilized onto HIS1K biosensors (Sartorius, 18-5120) at an approximate density of 0.5 nm. The biosensors were first saturated with the primary antibody (20G5), followed by the introduction of a secondary B7-H3 mAb. The detection of a significant binding signal from the secondary antibody indicated that its binding epitope is distinct from that of 20G5.

Cell growth inhibition assay

Growth inhibition assays were performed by seeding 1000–2000 cells per well into 96-well plates in 2D (Nest, 701001) or 3D (Corning, CLS7007-24EA) culture system. On the same day, the cells were treated with either proteins or small molecule inhibitors. After a 5-day treatment period, cell viability was assessed using the CellTiter-Glo assay (Promega, G7572) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. The percentage of surviving cells was calculated using the formula: % of surviving cells= (Luminescence_test/ Luminescence_blank) x 100%, where blank is IgG1 control.

Drug synergy between IBI334 and small-molecule inhibitors was evaluated using SynergyFinder+ algorithm according to the instruction45. Briefly, cell inhibition was quantified using the CellTiter-Glo assay and experimental data were processed through a web-based SynergyFinder+ algorithm (http://www.synergyfinderplus.org) to obtain the synergy score. Synergy scores were interpreted as follow:

< −10: Antagonistic interaction

−10 to 10: Additive interaction

> 10: Synergistic interaction

Cytotoxicity ADCC assay

For the ADCC reporter assay, Jurkat T cells engineered to express FcγRIIIa (reporter cells) were obtained from Promega (Catalog No. G7102). 1.5 × 105 reporter cells and 1.5 ×104 tumor cells were co-cultured in 96-well culture plates using RPMI1640 Medium (Gibco, Catalog No.22400-071) supplemented with 4% low IgG FBS (Invitrogen, Catalog No.1921005PJ). The co-culture was incubated for 20 h at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Subsequently, the signals were quantified using the Bio-Glo Luciferase Assay System (Promega, Catalog No. G7940) and measured with the Spectra Max I3 (Molecular Devices) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The ADCC effect was calculated using the formula: Fold change in relative to IgG= (Luminescence_test/Luminescence_blank), where blank is IgG1 control.

For the ADCC assay involving human PBMCs (huPBMC),7.5 × 105 huPBMCs and 1.5 × 104 tumor cells were co-cultured in 96-well culture plates using CTS media (Gibco, Catalog No.0870112DK). This setup was incubated for 6–8 h at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels in the co-culture supernatants were quantified using the LDH kit (Roche, Catalog No.4744926001). The percentage of cytotoxicity (LDH) was calculated following the guidelines provided in the manufacturer’s protocol. Cytotoxicity was quantified by measuring LDH release using the formula: % cytotoxicity = [(OD490_ test − OD490_ blank) / (OD490_ max tumor − OD490_ blank tumor)] × 100, in which “blank” represents PBMC and tumor cells co-culture with IgG1 control, “max tumor” is the tumor cells completely lysed and “blank tumor” is the tumor cell with IgG1 treatment.

FACS internalization assay

NCI-H358 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight. The following day, the cells were treated with PBS, null/Zalu, Zalutumumab, and 20G5/ZalubsAb, and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for various durations. Cells were harvested at specific time points (4 h, 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h) and stained with AF647-anti-human EGFR antibody (BD, 563577). The mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of EGFR on the cell surface was measured using a FACSymphony™ A3 flow cytometer. The percentage of EGFR internalization (% EGFR internalization) was calculated using the following formula: EGFR internalization % = (1 - EGFR_test/EGFR_control) × 100%, where EGFR_control represents the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of NCI-H358 cells stained with AF647-anti-human antibodies following treatment with PBS alone.

EGFR occupancy assay

Zalutumumab was labeled using the AF647 antibody labeling kit (Invitrogen, A20186) to prepare AF647-labeled Zalutumumab (AF647-Zalu). Tumor cells were treated with either Zalutumumab or the 20G5/Zalu at 4 °C for 1 h, washed with FACS buffer (PBS containing 2% FBS), and followed by an additional 1-h incubation with AF647-Zalu. Subsequently, the cells were washed with FACS buffer (PBS containing 2% FBS) and analyzed using the FACSymphony™ A3 (BD). Cells that were not treated and stained solely with the AF647-Zalu antibody served as the negative control (representing 0% EGFR occupancy and 100% AF647-Zalu staining). Receptor occupancy (RO%) is calculated using the formula: RO% = 100% - X, where 5 represents the percentage of cells gated within the population showing an AF647 signal below that of the negative control.

Western blotting

Cells were lysed using RIPA buffer (Thermo, 89900) supplemented with both phosphatase inhibitors (Abcam, ab201112) and a protease inhibitor (Roche, 11836170001). Following centrifugation at 13,523xg for 15 minutes at 4 °C, the supernatants were collected, and protein concentrations were determined using the BCA Protein Assay (Beyotime, P0012). Equal amounts of protein were resolved by SDS-PAGE (Thermo, NP0321Box) and subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes using the Invitrogen Trans-Blot system (Invitrogen, IBCOT2). Immunoblotting was conducted using primary antibodies and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. The membranes were then treated with Western HRP Substrate (Beyotime, P0018AM) and the signals were visualized with the Chemi-DocMP Imaging System (BIO-RAD).

Fluorescence microscopy images of phosphorylated EGFR

SK-MES-1 cells (25,000 cells) and SK-MES-1-B7-H3OE - GFP cells (25,000 cells) were co-seeded onto chamber slides (Beyotime, FSL108) and cultured overnight. Cells were then treated with 20G5/Zalu bsAb or untreatment for 4 hours. Co-cultured cells were fixed and permeabilized according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Invitrogen, 00-5523-00). Phosphorylation of EGFR was detected using a phospho-EGF receptor antibody (CST, 3777S) followed by an anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Biolegend, 406414). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI before mounting with anti-fade mounting medium. Images were acquired using an EVOS M7000 high-resolution fluorescence imaging system (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Cryo-EM complex and structure determination

Preparation of the 4Ig-B7-H3-20G5 F(ab’)2 complex. The 4Ig-B7-H3 and 20G5 F(ab’)2 proteins were mixed in a molar ratio of 1:1.2 and incubated at 4 °C overnight. Subsequently, the complex underwent further purification via size exclusion chromatography using a Superdex200 Increase10/300GL column (GE Healthcare) with a running buffer composed of 25 mM Tris-HCl (pH7.5) and 150 mM NaCl. The fractions containing the complex were collected and concentrated to a final concentration of 0.92 mg/mL for cryo-EM sample preparation.

Cryo-EM sample preparation and data acquisition. A droplet of the protein sample was placed onto a glow-discharged Cu holey carbon film grid (Quantifoil), treated for 60 seconds at 15 mA current using a PELCO easiGlow™ Glow Discharge system. The grid was blotted for 4.0 seconds with a blot force of 0 at 4 °C and 100% humidity, then plunge-frozen in liquid ethane using a Vitrobot MarkIV. Cryo-EM datasets were acquired using a 300 kV Titan Krios electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) equipped with a Falcon IV direct electron detector (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) in counting mode. Movies were automatically recorded using EPU software at a magnification of 165 K, yielding a physical pixel size of 0.743 Å and a total dose of 55 e-/Å^2. A total of 5017 movies were collected with a defocus range from −1.0 µm to −2.4 µm, and the slit width of the Selectris X energy filter (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was set at 10 eV.

Image Processing. Image processing was conducted using cryoSPARC v4.4.0, unless specified otherwise. The 5,017 dose-fractionated movies underwent patch motion correction using cryoSPARC’s native tools. Patch CTF estimation, 2D classification, heterogeneous refinement, and homogeneous refinements were also performed in cryoSPARC. A total of 1,520,193 particles were automatically picked using the blob picker and extracted with 2×2 binning (220-pixel box size, 1.486 Å per pixel). After two rounds of 2D classification to select high-quality particles, the remaining 538,193 particles underwent ab-initio reconstruction. These selected particles were then subjected to heterogeneous refinement across five volumes. The refined coordinates facilitated re-centering and re-extraction of unbinned particles (0.743 Å per pixel, 440-pixel box size). Particles of the B7-H3-20G5 Fab complex underwent further homogeneous refinements, resulting in a final density map at a resolution of 2.98 Å. Concurrently, particles of the 4Ig-B7-H3-20G5 F(ab’)2 complex were refined homogeneously, achieving a final density map at a resolution of 3.59 Å.

Immunohistochemistry and multiplex immunohistochemical staining

Tissue microarrays from both LUSC and normal tissues, as well as paraffin sections from xenograft models, were deparaffinized and stained using the Ventana Discovery Ultra automated slide stainer (Roche). Following the initial deparaffinization, heat-induced epitope retrieval (HIER) was conducted in DISCOVERY CC1 (Roche, 950-500) at100 °C for 40 minutes. This was succeeded by multiple rounds of staining, which included antigen retrieval, blocking, application of the primary antibody, HRP-coupled secondary antibody, and hematoxylin.

For multiplex immunohistochemical staining, immunofluorescent signals were captured using the DISCOVERY FAM Kit, DISCOVERY Cy5 Kit, DISCOVERY Rhodamine6G Kit, and QD DAPI (Roche). Imaging was performed on the Leica Aperio Versa8 Automated Imaging System, and the images were analyzed using the Aperio Image Scope Software Versa12.3.3 (Leica Biosystems).

In vivo xenograft model efficacy

For in vivo efficacy and safety evaluations, female Balb/C nude mice and female NOG mice (PF grade, 16–20 g) were procured from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. and were housed under SPF conditions with a controlled temperature of 22 ± 2 °C, relative humidity of 50 ± 5%, and a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle for efficacy and safety evaluations. Various cancer cell lines including SK-MES-1 (4 ×106 cells/mouse), NCI-H292 (4 ×106 cells/mouse), NCI-H1975 (5 ×106 cells/mouse), SK-MES-1LTC (3 ×10^6 cells/mouse), NCI-H508 (5 ×106 cells/mouse), NCI-H358 (5 ×106 cells/mouse), and NCI-H322SVD (5 ×106 cells/mouse) were implanted into female Balb/C nude mice or NOG mice to evaluate anti-tumor efficacy. The safety of Zalutumumab and IBI334 was assessed in the A431 model by implanting A431 cells (3 ×106 cells/mouse) into female Balb/C nude mice. Furthermore, the combined antitumor activity of IBI334 and small-molecule inhibitors was tested in vivo using NCI-H1975 LTC (5 × 106 cells/mouse) and NCI-H358 (4 × 106 cells/mouse) implanted in either female Balb/C nude mice or NOG mice to evaluate the efficacy of IBI334 and small inhibitors combination therapy.

Tumor-bearing mice were monitored, and when tumor volumes reached ~120–220 mm3, the animals were randomly assigned to treatment groups to receive either IBI334 or benchmark. For all efficacy studies, body weight and tumor dimensions (length and width) were measured bi-weekly. Tumor volumes were calculated using the formula: (width)2 × length/2. Mice were humanely euthanized when tumor volumes exceeded 2000 mm3 or when body weight loss surpassed 20%.

Cynomolgus monkey pharmacokinetics (PK) study

Cynomolgus monkey were obtained from Guangxi Xiongsen Primate Laboratory Animal Breeding Development Co., Ltd., which is certified with a production license (No. SCXK(Gui) 2021-0004) and a quality certificate (No. 0002997). West China- Cynomolgus monkey were housed under animal house with a controlled temperature of 22 ± 2 °C, relative humidity of 60 ± 20%, and a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle for PK and GLP toxicity study.

For the experimental procedure of PK study, the eighteen cynomolgus monkeys were randomly assigned into three groups, each consisting of six monkeys, balanced for gender. Each group received a single intravenous injection of IBI334 at dosages of 3, 10, and 30 mg/kg, respectively. Blood samples were collected at specified time points before and after the treatment of IBI334, specifically at 1, 2, 4, 8, 24, 72, 120, 168, 240, 336, 504, 672, 840, and 1008 hours post-treatment.

The serum concentration of IBI334 was quantified using an ELISA method. Human EGFR Protein (Acro, EGR-H522a) served as the coating antigen, samples containing IBI334 were placed on the ELISA plate and incubated. The detection involved a primary antibody specific to 20G5 of IBI334 (prepared in house), which bound to the antigen-antibody complexes on the plate. This was followed by the addition of a secondary antibody (Jackson Immuno Research, AB_10015289). After washing away unbound secondary antibodies, a substrate solution was added. Detection was performed using the Varioskan LUX (Thermo), and pharmacokinetic parameters were subsequently analyzed using Phoenix WinNonlin V8.2 software.

Cynomolgus monkey GLP toxicity study

In this study, forty cynomolgus monkeys were recruited and divided into four groups: a control group and three treatment groups receiving IBI334 at dosages of 30, 60, and 120 mg/kg, respectively. Each group comprised ten monkeys with an equal distribution of genders. The treatment of intravenous injections occurred weekly over a four-week period, totaling five doses, followed by a four-week recovery phase. The onset of dosing was defined as Day 1, and the commencement of the recovery phase was the day following the final dose.

The study encompassed various assessments including clinical observations, hematology, blood chemistry, cytokine profiling, immune response analysis, histopathology, and toxicokinetic (TK) analysis. Throughout the dosing and recovery phases, the general condition of the monkeys was monitored daily, with weekly measurements of body weight and food consumption. Urinalysis and ophthalmic examinations were conducted at the end of the dosing and recovery periods. Neurological assessments were conducted, electrocardiograms (ECGs) were obtained, and respiratory rate and blood pressure were measured at several time points: prior to treatment, and at 2, 24, 72, 120, and 168 h post-initial treatment, one day post the third and fifth treatments, and at the end of the recovery phase. Hematology using ADVIA 2120i Hematology System (Siemens), blood biochemistry using Cobas 8000 Modular Analyzer (Roche), and lymphocyte subsets using LSRFortessaTM flow cytometer (BD) were analyzed one day after the third and fifth treatment and at the end of the recovery phase. Cytokine levels (IL-2, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α, IFN-γ) were measured at 2 and 24 h following the initial treatment, 2 h post the fifth treatment, and at the end of the recovery phase.

Blood samples were drawn from the IBI334 treatment group at prior to treatment, the first and fourth treatments, and at 1 minute, 2, 8, 24, 48, 72, 96, and 168 h post IBI334 treatment to measure IBI334 concentrations and analyze toxicokinetic parameters. Following the dosing period, six monkeys from each group were selected for histopathology analysis; After the recovery phase, four more were chosen from each group for euthanasia and subsequent post-mortem and histopathological analysis.

Statistical and reproducibility

GraphPad Prism10.0 software was utilized for graph generation and statistical analysis. The results from in vitro studies, pharmacokinetics (PK) study, and GLP toxicity study were presented as mean ± SD, while the in vivo xenograft efficacy results were expressed as mean ± SEM. Linear regression module was used for correlation analysis. Statistical significance was assessed using one-way or two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc tests for continuous variables. P values ≥ 0.05 is denoted as non-significance. All immunoblots and core data shown are at least two independent experiments. For in vivo efficacy and monkey studies, animals were randomly assigned to different groups. The investigators who performed the experiments were blinded to the group assignments during data collection and/or analysis to reduce bias.
