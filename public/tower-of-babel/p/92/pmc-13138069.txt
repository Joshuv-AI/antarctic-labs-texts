Mediating mechanisms and urban diversity: How environmental risk perception shapes recycling behavior in China’s megacities
Authors: Chunyu Yang, Zhishan Wang, Xiaonan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryMandatory recycling policies in China’s megacities have had varying degrees of effectiveness, revealing a persistent gap between policy goals and actual public participation. Using a “perception-to-action” framework integrating environmental risk perception into the theory of planned behavior, this study quantitatively evaluated the psychological drivers of 4,983 urban residents. The findings demonstrate that the psychological mechanism driving recycling is not universal, with this heterogeneity being caused by two important factors: (1) an “infrastructure ceiling effect,” where the influence of perceived behavioral control on behavior diminishes as urban infrastructure matures; and (2) “demographic divergence,” where age, education, and income systematically alter the dominant psychological pathways from risk perception to action. Ultimately, effective urban recycling governance requires an abandonment of one-size-fits-all campaigns and should, instead, adopt “precision governance” that precisely targets social norms while adapting to the life cycle of urban infrastructure and the distinct psychological profiles of its residents.Subject areas: Environmental health, environmental management, human geography, psychology

Introduction

Globally, waste-management crises in urban centers are intensifying, prompting governments to implement ambitious recycling policies.1 However, the persistent chasm between policy mandates and public uptake, a phenomenon observed in various regions (from European municipalities to Asian megacities), often challenges these top-down initiatives.2,3 This global policy-action gap shows that the core challenge transcends mere infrastructure, existing in the psychological domain: decoding the mechanisms that translate public awareness into sustained behavior.4,5 To decode this puzzle, we turned to the theory of planned behavior (TPB), a framework that provides a foundation for explaining pro-environmental actions.6,7 While TPB effectively explains how attitudes, social norms, and perceived control shape intention, it provides less insight into an important upstream question: why do these beliefs form in the first place? This well-documented limitation, often termed “cognitive parsimony,”8,9 creates a critical gap in the construction of comprehensive behavioral models. We argue that this gap can be filled by a powerful but often under-specified cognitive driver: environmental risk perception (ERP), that is, an individual’s subjective judgment of environmental threats.10 A substantial body of international evidence has confirmed that higher ERP strongly catalyzes various pro-environmental behaviors.11,12 However, the exact psychological pathways through which such perception translates into specific actions, such as recycling, remain insufficiently addressed.

While studies have extensively explored the drivers of waste recycling, the literature often provides a fragmented picture, focusing on specific interventions, such as incentives,4 motivational framing,5 or distinct program designs, as confirmed by systematic reviews.13,14 Within this landscape, critical theoretical ambiguity persists regarding the psychological mechanisms linking ERP to behavior. Although calls for integrated models are increasing, few studies have systematically determined how ERP simultaneously influences all three core pathways of TPB: environmental attitude (EA), subjective norms (SNs), and perceived behavioral control (PBC) within a single model. This, it remains unclear how ERP differentially influences these pathways and which channel is most potent in converting risk perception into recycling action.

Compounding this theoretical gap is one that is methodological. Most studies have promoted a “one-size-fits-all” model, which overlooks the major influence of diverse socioeconomic conditions and policy contexts on environmental psychology. The importance of context, particularly social norms and capital, has been strongly emphasized,15 and based on cross-national studies, common determinants have revealed significant variations among cities, underscoring the need for deeper contextual analyses.16 This leaves a critical gap: understanding how these mediating psychological pathways vary across different urban and demographic landscapes.

To address these gaps, this study developed and empirically validated an integrated “perception-to-action” model within the context of China’s megacities. This setting provided a critical “natural laboratory” for observing these dynamics under the high-stakes conditions of intense urbanization and sweeping policy reforms. This study contributed to the literature in three main aspects. First, moving beyond models focused on single factors such as norms15 or external motivators,4,5 we systematically modeled ERP as a key cognitive antecedent shaping recycling behavior (RB) through the three distinct TPB mediators, providing a more comprehensive theoretical linkage. Second, using a large-scale dataset (N = 4,983) from seven cities, we empirically dissected and quantified the relative potency of the three mediating pathways, uncovering the dominant psychological channel for intervention. Most importantly, our multi-city comparative design directly challenged the “one-size-fits-all” assumption. Building on calls to contextualize RB,16 we used multi-group analysis to determine how these psychological mechanisms vary across cities and demographic segments. Our results yield context-specific policy insights into China’s urban governance and offer a validated psychological framework with strong implications for other rapidly urbanizing emerging economies. Crucially, our findings provide an important, evidence-based counterpoint to the universalist models often developed in Western contexts, highlighting the necessity of culturally and structurally situated research.

Theoretical framework and hypotheses

ERP as a catalyst for action

Wasteful behavior poses considerable environmental, economic, and social challenges, and a systematic review of the field has confirmed the urgency of understanding and intervening in such behavior.14 As global waste pollution escalates, household waste sorting has become a key policy priority, making the understanding of the psychological drivers of this behavior essential.17 A primary psychological driver is ERP, defined as an individual’s subjective appraisal of the severity of environmental threats.10 To fully understand its motivational power, we integrated insights from two complementary theoretical perspectives.

First, the value-belief-norm (VBN) theory posits that personal values and worldviews shape beliefs, which, in turn, drive behavior.18 From this perspective, ERP is not a monolithic construct; it is often filtered through different value orientations, manifesting as egoistic (risks to personal health), social-altruistic (risks to one’s community), and biospheric (risks to ecosystems) concerns.19 Acknowledging this richness, we conceptualized ERP as a multifaceted cognitive assessment of these distinct threats. A recent study in the waste-sorting context strongly corroborated this framework, demonstrating that cultural worldviews influence behavior precisely through the mediating pathway of ERP.20

Second, while the VBN theory explains the origin and content of environmental concerns, protection motivation theory (PMT) explains the action-oriented mechanisms. According to PMT, when individuals perceive a significant threat (threat appraisal)—be it egoistic, social, or biospheric—they become motivated to adopt protective behaviors.21 In this context, recycling serves as a quintessential protective behavior against the multifaceted threats encapsulated by ERP. The direct positive link between individuals’ perceptions of environmental risks and their engagement in pro-environmental actions such as waste sorting is, thus, strongly supported by both theory and recent empirical evidence.20 Building on this strong foundation, we propose the following hypothesis:

H1: ERP positively influences RB.

Integrating ERP as a core antecedent to TPB constructs

Building on the premise that a more causally complete model is required, this study formally integrated ERP as a core antecedent to the constructs of TPB.22 As established, the standard TPB model, although powerful, explains the immediate drivers of intention but not their origin—a limitation often termed “cognitive parsimony”.8 This proposition is firmly rooted in established meta-theories of pro-environmental action, which support the notion that broad value orientations and threat appraisals (the VBN and PMT domain) exist causally before the formation of specific behavioral beliefs (the TPB domain).23,24 This theoretical sequencing has recently found direct empirical validation in waste sorting contexts. For instance, Cai et al.20 demonstrated that ERP functions as a critical intermediary that translates general environmental concerns into specific behavioral intentions. Therefore, by positioning ERP as the direct precursor to EA, SNs, and PBC, a “perception-to-action” cascade is formalized. This integrated model bridges an individual’s broad risk assessments with the immediate psychological drivers of recycling, providing a more causally complete explanation for how pro-environmental behavior forms.

ERP and EA

EA reflects an individual’s favorable or unfavorable evaluation of recycling, which is fundamentally shaped by beliefs, a core tenet of the VBN theory.18 A primary belief in this context is ERP—the subjective assessment of threats from pollution and waste accumulation.25,26 When ERP is high, the negative impacts of environmental degradation become more salient and personally relevant, thus framing recycling as a necessary and valuable protective solution. This mechanism, in which perceived risks drive positive attitudes toward mitigation behaviors, is a robust finding in behavioral science. For instance, just as perceived risks in other domains (e.g., greenwashing and product harm) shape consumer attitudes,27,28,29 the perceived risk of environmental harm logically fosters a more positive evaluation of recycling. Therefore, a heightened sense of environmental threat is expected to cultivate a stronger and more favorable attitude toward recycling.

H2a: ERP positively influences EA.

ERP and SN

ERP influences extend beyond individual cognition to social dynamics, strongly shaping SNs. When environmental issues are framed as large-scale collective threats, they create a sense of shared vulnerability and responsibility.19 This process aligns with the stimulus-organism-response model, where ERP functions as a potent ecological-threat stimulus that heightens an individual’s sensitivity to social cues related to appropriate collective responses.30,31 This heightened sensitivity is further amplified by protection motivation (PMT), which posits that a high-risk appraisal motivates adherence to group norms as a collective coping strategy.21 Consequently, an individual with high ERP is more attuned to both observing others’ recycling (descriptive norms) and believing their community expects such behavior (injunctive norms). This theoretical pathway is empirically supported in waste management contexts: elevated ERP is correlated with stronger community norm internalization,32,33 reinforces eco-centric group identities,3,34 and is particularly effective when recycling is socially validated as a risk-mitigation response.35 Therefore, a heightened perception of shared environmental risk is expected to amplify the perceived social pressure to recycle.

H2b: ERP positively influences SN.

ERP and PBC

PBC reflects an individual’s perceived ease or difficulty in performing a behavior, encompassing beliefs regarding capabilities and external factors.22 An overwhelming ERP concerning abstract, large-scale threats can induce “learned helplessness,” a psychological state in which personal actions feel futile, thus diminishing PBC.36 Conversely, the “empowerment pathway” is has been theorized to dominate in the context of recycling. This pathway is grounded in PMT, where a heightened threat appraisal (ERP) catalyzes a proactive coping assessment that encourages individuals to overcome perceived barriers.21 The decisive factor is the nature of the behavior itself; recycling is a concrete, highly actionable, individual-level response serving as a tangible antidote to the paralysis induced by abstract risks, allowing individuals to channel their risk awareness into a demonstrable sense of control. This empowerment logic is empirically substantiated by the finding that a higher risk perception correlates with a greater perceived ability to enact specific protective behaviors and adopt sustainable practices.37,38,39 Thus, regarding the specific and tangible RB, the empowering effect of ERP was hypothesized to outweigh the potential for helplessness, leading to a net positive influence on PBC.

H2c: ERP positively influences PBC.

Mediating role of TPB constructs in the “perception-to-action” cascade

Having established how ERP shapes EA, SNs, and PBC (H2a–H2c), we propose that these core TPB constructs mediate the relationship between ERP and RB. This mediation framework details the “perception-to-action” cascade, explaining how a general awareness of environmental threats can be translated into specific behavior via three theoretically distinct pathways. This model integrates insights from VBN, PMT, and TPB, suggesting that perceptions influence internal cognitive states, subsequently driving behavioral responses.

Cognitive-affective pathway (via EA)

As argued for H2a, a high ERP fosters a more positive EA toward recycling. According to TPB, favorable attitudes are direct and potent predictors of both behavioral intention and subsequent behavior.22,24 Therefore, we posit that ERP’s influence on RB is channeled through the enhanced positive attitude it cultivates.

Social-contextual pathway (via SNs)

As argued for H2b, a high ERP, perceived as a collective threat, strengthens SNs. Within the TPB framework, this heightened perceived social pressure is a key driver of behavioral conformity, as individuals are motivated to comply with the expectations of significant others.40 ERP is, thus, expected to impact RB indirectly by amplifying these SNs.

Self-efficacy pathway (via PBC)

As argued for H2c, a high ERP enhances an individual’s PBC for recycling. TPB posits that a stronger sense of perceived control directly facilitates behavioral engagement by reducing perceived barriers and increasing one’s confidence in their capabilities.22 Consequently, we hypothesize that ERP influences RB by enhancing the sense of perceived control over the action.

Based on these pathways, we hypothesize that the influence of ERP on RB occurs through the following key TPB constructs.

H3a: EA mediates the relationship between ERP and RB;

H3b: SNs mediate the relationship between ERP and RB;

H3c: PBC mediates the relationship between ERP and RB.

Furthermore, we recognize that alternative frameworks, notably social practice theory (SPT), may provide a complementary perspective by placing these individual cognitive processes within broader systems of shared meanings, materials, and routines. However, an investigation of the complex interactions between mediators, or the complete integration of SPT, represents a valuable but distinct research objective that falls outside the scope of this quantitative study. Our study aimed to first provide a robust test of the primary psychological model, thereby paving the way for future research to explore these more complex, multi-level dynamics.

Moderating role of urban and demographic characteristics

The ERP-RB mechanism operates within contextual ecosystems where urban environments and individual characteristics systematically moderate its efficacy.40 We posit that these factors do not simply influence individual behavioral antecedents in isolation but, rather, alter the efficacy of the entire “perception-to-action” psychological pathway.3,41,42 This study hypothesizes a comprehensive moderating effect that will be tested using multi-group structural equation modeling (SEM) to compare the strength of the entire set of path coefficients across different groups.

Moderating role of urban context

Urban environments are heterogeneous in recycling infrastructure, policy enforcement, and information dissemination, critically shaping residents’ RB.43 This process is theorized to be multi-faceted, simultaneously amplifying certain pathways while potentially attenuating others. Specifically, a context rich in public service announcements and visible community engagement is more likely to amplify attitudinal and normative pathways. In addition, such an environment would constantly reinforced the value of recycling, making the behavior appear more impactful, and thus strengthening the link between risk awareness and a positive EA.44 Concurrently, it fosters strong local social norms for waste classification,32,41 heightening the salience of SNs by making pro-environmental actions more socially expected and visible.3 However, the influence on PBC is more nuanced. On the one hand, convenient facilities and supportive policies directly reduce practical barriers that would logically enhance an individual’s sense of control over the task.33,34 However, this strongly enabling environment could also create a “contextual substitution effect”; when recycling becomes universally easy, the unique motivational push from an individual’s personal risk perception (ERP) onto their sense of control (PBC) may become less critical and thus diminish. Despite this potential substitution in the control pathway, the powerful amplification of the attitude and norm pathways is expected to result in a more efficient psychological system overall. Therefore, we hypothesize that the net effect of a supportive context is positive, enhancing the mechanism’s total effectiveness in converting risk perception into action.

H4a: A supportive urban context (improved infrastructure and policy support) enhances the overall effectiveness of the psychological mechanism linking ERP to RB through the paths of H1, H2, and H3.

Moderating role of demographics

Beyond the urban context, individual demographic characteristics are posited to moderate underlying “perception-to-action” pathways.40 Serving as proxies for life experiences, cognitive resources, and value orientations,39,45 these factors systematically determine how an individual processes environmental risks, interprets social cues, and assesses their capacity to act, thereby moderating the efficacy of the entire mechanism.

Higher education is strongly linked to greater environmental knowledge and awareness.42,46 This heightened capacity is expected to amplify the attitudinal pathway, as educated individuals can more effectively translate abstract ERP into robust and reasoned EA. Moreover, education is often correlated with higher social capital and a higher sensitivity to community and institutional expectations.47,48 This strengthens the normative pathway, as educated individuals become more adept at interpreting and internalizing social cues, thus more strongly connecting their risk perception to SNs. Finally, the practical knowledge education provides improves the ability of individuals to understand and navigate complex recycling systems, directly boosting PBC. In summary, given that higher education equips individuals to more effectively convert risk perception into motivation across all three psychological channels, H4b is presented: The overall strength of the psychological mechanism linking ERP to RB (encompassing H1, H2, and H3) is significantly stronger for individuals with higher levels of education.

Environmental concern, habits, and responsiveness to social influences significantly vary among age cohorts exhibit.49,50 These cohort-specific differences serve as powerful contextual filters, determining how individuals perceive environmental risks, respond to social influences, and assess their own capabilities.51 For instance, younger and older generations are exposed to different information channels, belong to distinct social reference groups, and have varying levels of cognitive flexibility and established behavioral routines.52,53 Such fundamental differences in the context of life suggest that the psychological pathways converting ERP into RB are unlikely to be uniform across age groups. Rather, the relative importance and interplay of the attitudinal (EA), normative (SNs), and control-based (PBC) pathways for younger individuals are expected to be structured differently than those for older individuals. This suggests that, while the core components of the “perception-to-action” mechanism exist, their configuration and overall efficacy are not age-invariant; therefore, it is hypothesized that age significantly moderates the structure of the entire psychological model:

H4c: The composition and overall strength of the psychological mechanism linking ERP to RB (encompassing H1, H2, and H3) differ significantly between younger and older individuals.

Income level is a major determinant of the capacity of an individual to engage in pro-environmental action, primarily by providing resources and mitigating daily stressors that could otherwise inhibit such behaviors.54 Therefore, it is hypothesized to function as a systemic moderator, enhancing the overall efficacy of the “perception-to-action” mechanism. Specifically, higher income provides individuals with greater cognitive, social, and material resources, freeing up the “mental bandwidth” required to translate EA into consistent action, amplify the influence of pro-environmental SNs often present in higher-income communities,43 and directly lower the practical barriers that impede recycling, thus bolstering PBC. While income may not significantly influence core environmental values,55 the resources afforded by higher income create more fertile ground for the entire psychological process to operate. By simultaneously reducing constraints and enhancing capabilities, higher income is expected to strengthen the conversion of ERP into RB across all mediating pathways: H4d: The overall strength of the psychological mechanism linking ERP to RB (encompassing H1, H2, and H3) is significantly stronger for individuals with higher income levels.

To conclusively demonstrate the model’s structural validity, we conducted a robustness check using two smaller, random sub-samples (sample 1 = 517, sample 2 = 493). Mediation effect analysis

To precisely quantify how ERP translates into RB, a mediation analysis was conducted using the bias-corrected percentile bootstrap method (5,000 resamples) in AMOS 28.0. The results in Table 3 confirm our core proposition that TPB constructs act as powerful mediators. The total indirect effect was substantial and significant (β = 0.241, 95% CI [0.251, 0.390]), accounting for nearly 60% (59.8%) of the total influence of ERP on RB. This provides strong validation for our overarching “perception-to-action” framework. Notably, the significant direct effect of ERP on RB remained (β = 0.162, 95% CI [0.151, 0.273], suggesting that risk perception also directly triggers behavior, independent of measured cognitive and social pathways. First, the cognitive-affective pathway acting via EA showed a significant indirect effect of 0.074 (95% CI [0.066, 0.132]), accounting for 18.22% of the total effect, thus supporting H3a. In summary, our analysis results clearly illustrate the psychological cascade from risk to action. While all three TPB pathways are active, the mechanism is most strongly influenced by social influence (SNs), followed by the cultivation of personal conviction (EA), with an enhanced sense of self-efficacy (PBC) playing a smaller, tertiary role. This hierarchy strongly suggests that, in China’s current urban context, the “social signal” (what others expect) generated by risk perception is a far more potent behavioral driver than the “attitudinal signal” (what I believe) or the “capacity signal” (what I can do).

Urban context: “Infrastructure ceiling effect” and policy imprints

Regarding regional disparities, the overall effects of H1, H2, and H3 were stronger in developed regions than in developing regions, thus supporting H4a. Moreover, a critical exception emerged for PBC. In both the Jingjin and Huguang regions, the direct path from PBC to RB was not significant (β = 0.026, p = 0.531; β = 0.039, p = 0.146, respectively). Consequently, the indirect path from ERP through PBC to RB was also nonsignificant. This disparity suggests that an “infrastructure ceiling effect” exists in highly urbanized regions with mature recycling systems (Jingjin and Huguang). The convenience of facilities may have lowered the barrier to participation, rendering individual capabilities (PBC) less predictive. This finding that, in environments where recycling is highly convenient and accessible, an individual’s perceived capability stops being a major bottleneck, is a critical insight. The barrier has been removed by the system, rendering PBC less predictive of behavior.

Conversely, these paths remained significant for the Chuanyu region (βPBC→RB = 0.163∗∗∗; βIndirect = 0.053∗∗∗). In the developing Chuanyu region, personal control and capability remained critical bottlenecks for behavior, and the “bottleneck effect” existed in emerging cities. Regarding other paths, the Jingjin region showed the highest direct sensitivity of ERP on RB (β = 0.312∗∗∗), while the Huguang region exhibited the strongest conversion of risk perception into attitudes and norms (βERP→EA = 0.362∗∗∗; βERP→SN = 0.459∗∗∗), reflecting a more cognitively driven response in early-adopter cities. This suggests that its policies have successfully cultivated a powerful, norm-driven recycling culture. Furthermore, this confirms that, in developing systems, personal agency and the perceived ability to overcome logistical hurdles remain critical drivers of recycling.

Education level: “Normative vs. cognitive” processing divide

The overall effects of H1, H2, and H3 were significantly stronger for individuals with higher than for those with lower education levels, supporting H4b. Educational background also yielded two distinct psychological routes to recycling. On the one hand, in the Normative-Heuristic route, individuals with education below a bachelor’s degree exhibited higher sensitivity to the path SN→RB than those with a bachelor’s degree or higher (β = 0.532∗∗∗ vs. β = 0.524∗∗∗); although the difference was small, the overall pattern was clear. More importantly, the direct ERP→RB link was much stronger for this group (β = 0.258∗∗∗ vs. β = 0.173∗∗∗), reflecting a more direct, “fast-thinking” or heuristic processing style, in which behavior is driven more by immediate risk signals and prevailing social expectations. On the other hand, in the cognitive-systematic route, those with a bachelor’s degree or higher exhibited much stronger pathways from ERP to all three mediators (EA, SN, and PBC). For instance, the ERP→EA path was nearly double in strength (β = 0.327∗∗∗ vs. β = 0.181∗∗∗). This indicates a more deliberative, “slow-thinking” or systematic processing style, where abstract risk, before guiding action, is first translated into personal attitudes, social understanding, and a sense of capability.

Age: “Duty vs. value-internalization” generational divide

A clear generational divide emerged in the psychological triggers for recycling, thus supporting H4c. Specifically, those with younger respondents exhibited much stronger pathways from ERP through EA and PBC to RB. An exception emerged regarding SNs, though the difference was small (β = 0.193∗∗∗ vs. β = 0.194∗∗∗). In the “duty and habit” pathway, older residents (>40 age group) displayed a stronger direct ERP→RB path (β = 0.244∗∗∗ vs. β = 0.171∗∗∗) and a stronger link between SN and RB (β = 0.541∗∗∗ vs. β = 0.509∗∗∗). This profile suggests that, for the older generation, recycling is driven more by a sense of established social duty, habit, and a direct response to perceived authority or risk. Conversely, in the “value and efficacy” pathway, younger residents (<40 age group) reflected a much stronger path from ERP to EA (β = 0.293∗∗∗ vs. β = 0.225∗∗∗) and a significantly stronger influence of PBC on RB (β = 0.113∗∗∗ vs. β = 0.051∗). This indicates that younger generations are more likely to internalize environmental risk into their personal value systems (attitudes) and are more motivated by a sense of personal agency and self-efficacy.

Income: Interplay of normative pressure and cognitive resources

Income level also significantly moderated the model, with the overall effects of H1, H2, and H3 being stronger for individuals with higher levels than for those with lower income levels, supporting H4d. These results indicate that individuals with higher income are more likely to internalize risk into personal attitudes and values. In the norm-sensitive pathway, respondents with lower income showed a stronger reliance on the direct ERP→RB path and, most notably, a stronger link from SN to RB, compared to those in the higher-income group (β = 0.547∗∗∗ vs. β = 0.509∗∗∗). This may reflect a greater sensitivity to community-level norms and public campaigns, which often serve as primary sources of information and motivation. Contrastingly, in the Structured-Processing pathway, higher-income individuals exhibited much stronger paths from ERP to all three TPB mediators (EA, SN, and PBC). For example, the ERP→SN path was more than 2.5 times stronger (β = 0.527∗∗∗ vs. β = 0.199∗∗∗). This suggests that higher income, which is potentially correlated with greater access to information and cognitive resources, facilitates a more structured and comprehensive process of translating abstract risk into a full suite of attitudes, perceived norms, and capabilities.

Discussion

Theoretical contributions

This study contributes to the literature on pro-environmental behavior by assessing the psychological mechanisms through which ERP influences RB in China’s diverse megacities. Our analysis revealed a robust “perception-to-action” cascade in which ERP acts as a critical upstream catalyst for TPB constructs. More importantly, we found significant heterogeneity in these pathways, highlighting a novel “infrastructure ceiling effect” and profound demographic divides that contextualize and challenge universalist models of environmental action. Our principal contributions are 3-fold.

First, our findings empirically validate the VBN theory that in the recycling context, ERP is a potent antecedent to the TPB framework, providing large-scale, multi-city evidence. This is consistent with and expands on recent studies that explored ERP’s mediating role within specific cultural worldviews.20 By demonstrating that risk appraisal is a direct driver of physical waste-sorting—not just financial willingness as shown in some studies3—our study solidifies the “risk-as-catalyst” premise suggested by PMT.21 Thus, we provide a more causally-complete model that addresses the well-documented “attitude-behavior gap” by identifying a key origin of TPB core constructs.40

Second, the consistent dominance of the social-contextual pathway (via SNs) across most segments reinforces the strength of social influence in collectivist cultures.3,43 Our model, however, adds an important layer, the salient, shared perception of environmental risk that most effectively activates these potent social norms. This finding is further nuanced by our moderation analysis showing that higher education significantly amplifies the activation of this normative channel (the ERP→SN path), suggesting that educated individuals more effectively translate abstract risk perception into a concrete sense of social obligation.23

Third, and perhaps our most important theoretical contribution, is the identification of context-dependent mechanisms that challenge the uniform application of the TPB model. We introduced the concept of an “infrastructure ceiling effect” to structurally explain the surprisingly weak role of PBC in the aggregate model. While previous studies have often attributed such weakness to speculative factors such as a lack of system transparency,34 our multi-group analysis offers data-driven evidence. In developing urban regions such as Chuanyu, where recycling is a newer mandate, PBC acts as a critical “gatekeeper” to action.28 Conversely, in mature regions, the ubiquity of facilities lowers the capability barrier to a point where the predictive power of PBC diminishes, introducing a critical, lifecycle-based boundary condition to the TPB model, confirming that, as urban infrastructure matures, the primary psychological driver shifts from “capability” (Can I do it?) to “motivation” and “social conformity” (Should I do it, and are others doing it?).

Our findings also reveal a substantial “demographic divergence” in psychological pathways. We identified a distinct cohort divide in which the behavior of older individuals was driven by a direct, visceral response to risk—consistent with PMT’s emphasis on personal salience50—while younger individuals relied on a self-efficacy pathway, leveraging digital literacy and a solutions-oriented mindset to overcome barriers.51,52 Similarly, income moderated the mechanism, shifting the driver from relying on direct risk signals and prevailing community norms for lower-income groups—a pattern consistent with facing more immediate environmental justice concerns,54 to “social status signaling” for higher-income groups, whose pro-environmental behavior was more intertwined with internalized values and social identity.55

Collectively, these findings not only add moderators to a model but also paint a more dynamic and socially embedded picture of environmental behavior, prompting a shift away from universalist assumptions toward context-contingent theories of pro-environmental action.

Practical and policy implications: Toward precision governance

Our findings offer actionable insights for policymakers, advocating for a shift from generic public management to a “precision governance” approach rooted in the “perception-to-action” framework. This approach requires the tailoring of strategies to specific urban contexts and demographic drivers.

First, strategically, governance should embrace “health-centric risk communication.” Considering the foundational role of ERP as an upstream catalyst, environmental communication should pivot from abstract warnings to tangible local health outcomes. Messaging should explicitly link improper waste disposal to community health risks, such as worsened air quality from waste incineration, noise, and sanitation issues resulting from overflowing bins and the degradation of local green spaces, framing recycling not simply as an environmental duty but as a crucial protective health measure. This strategy would directly leverage the strong risk-perception pathways identified in our model, making environmental threats more immediate and personally salient.

Second, intervention design should be based on a dynamic, “lifecycle-based policy roadmap” acknowledging the “infrastructure ceiling effect.” In developing urban regions (e.g., Chuanyu), where PBC is a critical bottleneck, policies should prioritize “hardware optimization and capability building.” Moreover, focus should be placed on improving physical accessibility, increasing the density of recycling bins, simplifying sorting guidelines using clear visual aids, and launching digital tools (e.g., mini-programs) to build resident confidence and reduce perceived difficulty. For mature urban regions (e.g., Shanghai and Guangzhou), where the infrastructure ceiling has been reached and the influence of PBC is attenuated, resources should shift from physical facilities to “software reinforcement and normative guidance.” Efforts should be directed toward strengthening EA and SNs through community-based interventions such as publicizing community recycling rates, celebrating “recycling champion” households, and leveraging local opinion leaders and social networks to foster a strong, visible norm of participation.

Third, communication and engagement must be classified to target the specific psychological levers of different demographics. For younger and highly educated individuals, our data reveal that the normative (SNs) and cognitive-affective (EA) pathways are the primary channels through which risk perception is converted into action; therefore, interventions for this group should be focused on social proof and value alignment. This would include the use of “normative framing” in campaigns, deployment of gamified mobile apps that can track personal and collective impact, and leveraging of their desire to act as social exemplars. Furthermore, while the overall self-efficacy pathway was less dominant, the direct link between PBC and behavior was notably stronger for the younger cohort, suggesting that empowerment, which makes recycling feel easy and effective, remains a critical strategy for this group.

For older and lower-income individuals, for whom direct risk perception and community norms are more influential, interventions should emphasize community solidarity and direct health benefits. This would involve mobilizing trusted community leaders (e.g., building managers and respected elders) to harness social norms and design communication that directly connects recycling to the well-being of their families and immediate neighborhood.

Overall, by adopting these data-driven, multi-pronged approach—specifically combining health-centric communication, a lifecycle-based infrastructure strategy, and segmented demographic engagement—local governments can shift from one-size-fits-all campaigns and ensure that the allocation of administrative resources occurs according to the specific psychological or structural levers most likely to yield sustained behavioral changes in a given context.

This study used robust empirical validation for a “perception-to-action” framework, establishing ERP as a critical upstream catalyst that activates the TPB mechanism. Our results demonstrate that ERP directly influences RB and operates through three distinct psychological channels, namely the cognitive-affective (EA), social-contextual (SNs), and self-efficacy (PBC) pathways. Beyond verifying these general relationships, a pivotal contribution of this work lies in the clarification of the “infrastructure ceiling effect” and the “cohort divide.” It was found that the potency of these pathways is not uniform but is structurally moderated by the maturity of urban infrastructure, shifting from a “capability-driven” model (high dependence on PBC) in developing regions to a “norm-driven” model in mature regions. This finding challenges the “one-size-fits-all” assumption made in previous studies on environmental governance, highlighting the need for context-specific policy adaptations.

Limitations of the study

The findings should be interpreted within the context of some methodological limitations. First, regarding measurement validity, while the average variance extracted (AVE) for the selected constructs fell slightly below the conventional threshold of 0.5, the composite reliability (CR) values remained robust across all factors. This suggests that, while convergent validity warrants refinement in future scale development, CR values remained robust, indicating that the internal consistency of the constructs is sound. While future studies should aim to refine these scales to further improve the convergent validity, we are confident that the current measurement model provides a reliable foundation for the core findings of this study. Second, our operationalization of ERP, while intentionally focused on salient urban-life indicators (noise, air, and sanitation) to maximize psychological proximity, did not capture the broader dimensions of environmental risk, such as concerns regarding climate change or the long-term impacts of waste accumulation. This constitutes a deliberate theoretical trade-off, grounded in psychological distance theory: we prioritized measuring experiential, proximal risk rather than abstract, distal risk perception. Future research may build on our model by incorporating these broader ERP dimensions to determine whether the “perception-to-action” cascade holds for more abstract threats.

To advance this field, future studies should address these gaps through three avenues. First, longitudinal designs may be used to track the evolution of the dominance of psychological drivers (e.g., from PBC to SN) as a region transitions from a “developing” to a “mature” infrastructure stage. Second, by integrating objective behavioral data, such as smart bin logs or third-party audits, self-reports can be validated and the evidence base can be strengthened. Finally, to help verify whether the “infrastructure ceiling effect” is a universal phenomenon or specific to the rapid urbanization context of China, the geographical scope should be expanded to include diverse cultural and socioeconomic settings, which would reveal the cross-cultural validity of the “perception-to-action” framework and foster a more globally informed science of pro-environmental behavior.

Experimental model and study participant details

Ethics statement

This study was conducted in strict accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The research protocol, including the survey questionnaire and informed consent procedures, received formal approval from the Ethics Committee of the Department of Psychology at Nanjing University (Approval No. NJUPSY202304001). All participants provided electronic informed consent before beginning the survey, acknowledging the study's purpose, the voluntary nature of their participation, and the confidentiality of their anonymized data. To ensure privacy, no personally identifiable information was collected.

Study design and participants

This study employed a large-scale, cross-sectional survey design to investigate the psychological drivers of recycling behavior (RB) among urban residents in China. The data were obtained from the “Green Lifestyle Survey of Residents in Megacities,” an original survey conducted between June 2023 and January 2024. The survey targeted adult residents across seven of China's most influential megacities (Beijing, Tianjin, Shanghai, Shenzhen, Guangzhou, Chengdu, and Chongqing), selected to represent a wide range of economic, policy, and cultural contexts.

To ensure sample representativeness, a multi-stage sampling strategy was implemented. First, a Probability Proportional to Size (PPS) approach was used to allocate sample quotas to each city based on official population data. Second, within each city, demographic quotas for gender and age were established based on the latest national census data. The final valid sample consisted of 4,983 participants, including 50.03% male and 49.97% female participants. The sociodemographic profile of the sample is detailed in Table S1.

Data collection procedures

Data were collected by a leading professional survey firm in China via its extensive, pre-screened national online panel. Participants received a small monetary compensation for their time. A rigorous data quality-control process was applied to the initial 5,188 collected responses. This involved the exclusion of entries with illogical answers, excessively short completion times (<5 minutes), and duplicate IP addresses, resulting in the final analytical sample of 4,983.

Influence of gender on study results

The potential influence of gender on the proposed psychological pathways was systematically assessed using a multi-group structural equation modeling (SEM) analysis. As detailed in the “Moderating Effects” subsection of the Results, the analysis revealed no significant differences in the overall model structure or path coefficients between male and female subsamples. This finding indicates that the theoretical model explaining recycling behavior holds robustly across genders within the studied urban contexts.

Method details

To ensure the psychometric soundness of the measurement scales, a rigorous validation process was undertaken, which included in-depth interviews with 45 urban residents, expert review, and a pilot study (N=185). The internal consistency of all the multi-item scales was confirmed using Cronbach's alpha, with all coefficients exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.80.

Independent variable

The operationalization of ERP was guided by a deliberate two-stage process to ensure both theoretical grounding and contextual validity. First, our measurement strategy is firmly grounded in psychological distance theory. This principle, rooted in a foundational psychometric paradigm that established risk as a subjective construct,10 posits that risks perceived as psychologically “close,” in terms of time, space, and personal relevance, are much more potent drivers of individual behavior, compared to distant, abstract concerns such as those related to global climate change. A recent study confirmed that reduced psychological distance significantly amplifies risk perception and protective behavior.58 Therefore, we made the strategic decision to focus on proximal, experiential risks, so as to measure the component of ERP that is most likely to bridge the well-documented “Perception-to-Action” gap for daily actions such as recycling. Second, this theoretically driven decision was validated through a preliminary qualitative investigation. A grounded theory analysis of interviews with urban residents confirmed that noise, air quality, and the state of public greenness/sanitation were indeed the most salient and frequently cited dimensions through which they experienced and appraised immediate environmental threats in their daily lives. This two-stage approach, combining an overarching theory with context-specific evidence, is strongly supported by current research frontiers. Our focus on a “tri-exposure” of local stressors aligns with advanced public-health methodologies, such as that of Liu et al.,59 who emphasized capturing individual-level, proximal exposures to accurately assess health impacts. Furthermore, the core logic of our model, that is, that specific proximal risk perceptions drive corresponding behaviors, is validated by recent findings, such as those of Wang et al.,60 who demonstrated a direct path from noise risk perception to noise mitigation intentions. Based on this multi-layered evidence, ERP was operationalized as a composite, three-item construct reflecting these core experiential risks. Responses were measured on a five-point Likert scale, where respondents rated the severity of each issue (1 = “very non-severe”, 5 = “very severe”). The final ERP score was calculated by summing the scores across the three items, with higher scores indicating a greater perception of environmental risk.

Mediating variables

Following the TPB theoretical framework, this study examined three core mediating constructs. EA captured an individual's positive or negative evaluation of performing RBs. SNs measured the perceived social pressure from significant others (e.g., family and community) to recycle. Finally, PBC was used to assess an individual's confidence in their ability to overcome potential barriers and perform these behaviors. The measurement items for these constructs were adapted from well-established scales in the TPB and environmental psychology literature.26 Through an expert consultation process, all items were carefully tailored to the characteristics of urban recycling in China to ensure contextual relevance. Additionally, the responses for all three variables were recorded on a Likert scale, ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”).

Dependent variable

The study's dependent variable, RB, was measured as a self-reported behavioral index, a common approach in environmental behavior research when direct observation is not feasible. The scale assessed the frequency of four specific, high-impact recycling actions: (1) household waste sorting, (2) the recycling of used items (e.g., clothes and electronics), (3) the separation of food waste, and (4) the recycling of delivery packaging (e.g., cartons and plastic). Respondents reported the frequency of each behavior on a scale ranging from 1 (“never”) to 5 (“several times a day”). The specific measurement items for all variables, along with their corresponding sources, validity indices (e.g., factor loadings), and reliability scores (Cronbach's alpha), are detailed in Table S2.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics and frequency distributions for demographic characteristics were calculated using IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0. Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) was conducted using IBM SPSS AMOS 28.0 to test the hypothesized relationships.

A Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was performed to assess the psychometric properties of the measurement scales.

Measurement model evaluation

Before testing the hypotheses, we rigorously assessed the measurement model’s reliability and validity. First, reliability was evaluated using Cronbach’s α and Composite Reliability (CR). As shown in Table S2, the factor loadings for all indicators ranged from 0.556 to 0.765, exceeding the acceptable limit. Cronbach’s α values ranged from 0.626 to 0.783, and CR values ranged from 0.629 to 0.785. All values exceeded the recommended threshold of 0.60,61 indicating satisfactory internal consistency for all constructs.

Subsequently, the convergent validity was evaluated using the AVE. We noted that the AVE values for certain constructs ranged between 0.36 and 0.48, slightly below the conventional threshold of 0.50. However, according to Fornell and Larcker,62 if the AVE is less than 0.50 but the CR is higher than 0.60, the convergent validity of the construct can still be considered adequate. As displayed in Table S3, the convergent validity of the measurement scales was supported despite the lower AVE values. Overall, the convergent validity was deemed adequate for the purpose of the study, and the model demonstrated a good overall fit.

Structural model analysis

Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) with maximum likelihood estimation was used to test the overall model and the hypothesized relationships. Model fit was evaluated against established criteria: χ2/df<5, CFI>0.90, TLI>0.90, and RMSEA<0.08. To test the mediating effects (H3a-c), we employed a bootstrapping procedure with 5,000 resamples to generate bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A significant indirect effect was indicated if the CI did not contain zero. To test the moderating effects of urban context and demographics (H4a-d), a multi-group SEM analysis comparing path coefficients across different subgroups was conducted. Statistical significance was set at p< 0.05.
