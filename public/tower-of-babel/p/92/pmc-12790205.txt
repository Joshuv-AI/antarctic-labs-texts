Ischemic Complications in a Patient With Spontaneous Echo Contrast Undergoing Percutaneous Left Atrial Appendage Closure
Authors: Weronika Byliniak, Maria Pręgowska, Patrycjusz Stokłosa, Jarosław Skowroński, Barbara Miłosz, Gary S. Mintz, Adam Witkowski, Jerzy Pręgowski
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLeft atrial appendage closure (LAAC) is a safe procedure. However, clinically silent brain ischemic lesions occur in 30% of patients, and device-related thrombosis (DRT) in up to 8%.Case SummaryThe current case describes an 87-year-old female who underwent LAAC because of bleeding complications related to anticoagulation therapy. A Watchman FLX (Boston Scientific) was successfully implanted. Periprocedural transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) revealed spontaneous echo contrast in the left atrium. At 30-day follow-up TEE, a large DRT was detected, and oral anticoagulation was restarted. Serial TEEs performed during extended follow-up showed gradual resolution of the thrombus. The patient also underwent serial periprocedural magnetic resonance that documented occurrence of 5 new brain ischemic lesions.Take-Home MessageThis case shows that spontaneous echo contrast present in the left atrium can potentially result in increased risk of thrombotic complications after LAAC such as new brain ischemic lesions or DRT.

Past Medical History

Past medical history included coronary artery bypass grafting 12 years ago, percutaneous coronary intervention of the right coronary and left anterior descending artery 7 years ago, and transcatheter aortic valve implantation 1 year prior to the scheduled LAAC procedure. Transcatheter aortic valve implantation was complicated by high-grade atrioventricular block that required pacemaker implantation. Other comorbidities were arterial hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and peripheral artery disease. The patient suffered from gastrointestinal bleeding of unknown origin that was likely related to long-term apixaban treatment (2.5 mg bid).

Differential Diagnosis

The CHA2DS2-VASc score calculated for the patient was 7. However, taking into account her bleeding tendency, anticoagulation was stopped, and LAAC was scheduled.

Investigations

Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) performed prior to LAAC revealed favorable left atrial appendage (LAA) anatomy and excluded evident thrombus (Figure 1A). Management

LAAC was performed under general anesthesia via right femoral vein access. The LAA was closed with a 27-mm Watchman FLX (Boston Scientific) with adequate compression of 15%, no detectable leaks, and shoulder protrusion of up to 5 mm. However, due to anatomical challenges, 7 partial device recaptures and repositions were required to achieve a satisfactory result. Periprocedural echocardiography documented spontaneous echo contrast (SEC) in the left atrium throughout the procedure, but without definite features of thrombus (Video 1, Figure 1B). The activated clotting time measured within 5 minutes after transseptal puncture was 245 seconds, and the total dose of unfractionated heparin administered was 8,500 U.

Outcomes and Follow-Up

In-hospital follow-up was uncomplicated except for diffuse access-site hematoma without underlying vascular complications. No new clinically overt neurological symptoms developed. However, brain MR performed as a part of the research protocol on the second day after the procedure revealed 5 clinically silent ischemic brain lesions with a total volume of 378 mL (Figures 2D to 2F). The patient was discharged home on day 4 in good clinical condition on dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT). On routine 30-day follow-up, the patient was in good clinical condition, without any new adverse events. TEE revealed a stable Watchman FLX position with adequate compression and without any leaks, but with device-related thrombosis (DRT) (Video 2, Figure 1C). A large thrombus was detected on the surface of the device, extending towards the pulmonary vein ridge. Its dimensions were 21 mm in length and 10 mm in thickness. The third brain MR did not show any new ischemic lesions, while those present on day 2 had partially resolved (Figures 2G to 2I). DAPT was stopped, and apixaban (2.5 mg bid) was initiated. Additional visits were scheduled, and 3 serial TEE examinations performed within 4 months revealed gradual thrombus resolution until its complete disappearance (Video 3, Figure 1D). The patient remained asymptomatic throughout the follow-up period. After the final TEE, apixaban was stopped, and the patient was prescribed a single antiplatelet therapy (75 mg acetylsalicylic acid [ASA]).

Discussion

It has been shown that LAAC, although very safe, may lead to the occurrence of new brain ischemic lesions in around 30% of patients.2 Most of the lesions are silent, and their clinical significance remains uncertain.3 The potential causes of new ischemic lesions include fresh thrombi formation, tissue debris, and air embolism.2,4 Recently, the device manufacturer (Boston Scientific) issued a letter highlighting the importance of appropriate device preparation to avoid air embolism, especially in patients who undergo LAAC under deep or conscious sedation. The letter was noticed by the United States Food and Drug Administration.4 Another well-known complication of LAAC is DRT that occurs in up to 8% of patients who undergo serial TEE imaging.5 DRT may be related to the increased risk of ischemic events.6 Factors associated with DRT include a larger LAA, history of stroke, vascular disease, lower left ventricular ejection fraction,7 presence of SEC in the left atrium, history of venous thromboembolism, LAA emptying velocity ≤20 cm/s, and deep device implantation.8 In those patients, an additional TEE at 6 months post procedure and intensified antithrombotic therapy should be considered.7,8 Although SEC, which is a risk factor for DRT, was observed in our patient, we decided to initiate DAPT instead of oral anticoagulation (OAC) because we were concerned about bleeding complications in an 87-year-old fragile patient.

The present case demonstrated 2 thrombotic complications of LAAC—silent ischemic brain lesions and DRT—both potentially related to a prothrombotic state as evidenced by SEC in the left atrium, which has been observed in 10% of patients undergoing LAAC.9 Wang et al. reported the correlation between SEC and occurrence of DRT at follow-up TEE.9 However, Wang et al. did not find any relation between SEC and periprocedural stroke or transient ischemic attack, while our patient was diagnosed with 5 new brain ischemic lesions. This may be explained by the fact that the majority of new ischemic brain lesions are clinically silent. Further studies focused on procedural aspects of the LAAC procedure using serial MR brain imaging are warranted.

There have been several treatment regimens proposed after LAAC including OAC therapy with low-dose ASA for 45 days, DAPT for up to 6 months, and ASA for life.10 The strategy depends on the balance of bleeding and ischemic risks. The optimal therapy is currently being tested in clinical trials. Our case report may suggest that in patients with SEC in the left atrium, a more aggressive strategy shortly after the procedure is better than DAPT. Some studies show that new OAC therapies are superior to warfarin in terms of post-LAAC treatment, and DAPT may be ineffective in preventing DRT in high-risk patients.9 Moreover, it is possible that in the elderly patients with numerous comorbidities and especially with limited life expectancy, a conservative strategy (ie, anticoagulation discontinuation irrespective of the CHA2DS2-VASc score) rather than an LAAC intervention is better.

Conclusions

This case highlights the possible association of SEC in the left atrium with ischemic complications related to LAAC.
