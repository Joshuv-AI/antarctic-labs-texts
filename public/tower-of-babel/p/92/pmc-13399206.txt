ASEMBLE technique: A novel approach for bronchial blocker placement in paediatric patients
Authors: Srinivasan Ramachandran, Sekar Loganathan, Savitri Velayudhan
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Lung isolation during surgery in the paediatric population is challenging due to small airway dimensions, limited availability of ultrathin bronchoscopes, and the risk of desaturation during placement of the bronchial blocker (BB).[12] There is a need for a technique that allows rapid placement of a BB and early resumption of ventilation in paediatric patients. We describe a novel technique, ASEMBLE: Assembly-guided Sequential Extraluminal Bronchial Blocker Placement, to overcome the above shortcomings. We used this technique in some cases. Table 1 describes the individual cases with demographic details, proposed surgery, bronchoscope dimensions, and the side of isolation. All patients underwent pre-operative anaesthesia checkup and followed standard fasting guidelines. The technique is described as below. An appropriate size Microcuff® endotracheal tube (ETT) was mounted on the insertion cord of a 2.8 mm bronchoscope, and a 5 Fr Arndt endobronchial blocker (AEB) wire was looped near the tip of the bronchoscope (Assembly unit, Figure 1a). After standard anaesthesia induction and mask ventilation for 3–4 minutes after administering a non-depolarising muscle relaxant, an anaesthesiologist performed direct laryngoscopy and inserted the assembly unit into the glottis under direct vision. A second anaesthesiologist simultaneously directed the unit towards the intended main bronchus using bronchoscope controls [Figure 1b]. Loop was released in the bronchus, and the depth of marking was noted at the angle of the mouth and stabilised. The bronchoscope was withdrawn into the trachea, and the ETT was railroaded over it, confirming ETT placement on withdrawal (sequential placement AEB ETT) [Figure 1c]. Ventilation was resumed promptly. Subsequently, the bronchoscope was reintroduced to reconfirm the AEB position, and the blocker cuff was inflated under vision and lung isolation was ensured. The ETT and blocker were secured separately at the angle of the mouth.

Paediatric lung isolation remains challenging, especially in infants and small children. Various techniques, such as endobronchial intubation and BBs, have been used according to the child’s age and airway dimensions. Double-lumen ETTs and Univent tubes are not suitable for them. BBs are increasingly preferred because they can be used with conventional ETTs and provide effective lung separation with fibreoptic guidance.[34] Stephenson et al.[5] have described extraluminal BB placement in a series of 26 patients. During laryngoscopy, the BB was placed first, followed by endotracheal intubation, and later, the BB was positioned in the desired bronchus with fibreoptic bronchoscope guidance. Ventilation during BB positioning was done through a multi-port adapter. It is technically challenging to loop the insertion cord to manipulate the BB inside the trachea in the technique described by Stephenson et al. In contrast, in our technique, BB was placed in the desired bronchus, followed by rail-roading of ETT. This allows quick BB placement in the desired bronchus. Direct laryngoscopy enables rapid access to the trachea and advancement of the blocker to the desired bronchus with bronchoscopy guidance. Mounting the ETT over the bronchoscope insertion cord allows immediate sequential tracheal intubation after blocker deployment, enabling early resumption of ventilation, reducing the risk of desaturation, and avoiding repeated laryngoscopy attempts. This technique also minimises the chance of blocker displacement by allowing ETT advancement under continuous bronchoscopic visualisation. During the procedure, should desaturation or any technical difficulty occur, the ETT can be rail-roaded over the bronchoscope and ventilation can be resumed. There should be good coordination and communication between the proceduralists throughout the procedure. Migration of the BB is a possibility during ETT advancement if not held at its depth.

ASEMBLE is thus a promising technique, providing a controlled, potentially safe approach for lung isolation in paediatric patients.
